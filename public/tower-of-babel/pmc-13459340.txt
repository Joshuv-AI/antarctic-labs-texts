Primary care physicians’ understanding and counseling practices of sexually transmitted diseases: a cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Norah Mohammed AlZamil, Ebtesam Almajed, Badriyah AlDejain, Norah Alqntash, Ghadah Aldayel, Sahar Khaytan, Doaa Aljasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a significant global health burden. In Saudi Arabia, limited awareness, stigma, and inconsistent provider competence impede effective prevention and counseling. This study aimed to assess primary care physicians’ (PCPs) competence and physician perceived barriers (attitudes) in STI prevention and counseling across Saudi primary care.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from July 2024 to July 2025 among PCPs in Saudi Arabia. Data were collected using a validated, self-administered electronic questionnaire through social media and official email lists. The survey collected sociodemographic data, STI-related practices (including risk assessment, counseling, and testing), and perceived barriers to care. Practices were scored as continuous variables using a 5-point Likert scale (Never to Always), while perceived barriers were assessed across five domains using a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) and subsequently dichotomized into ‘high’ or ‘low’ based on the median score. Descriptive statistics summarized responses, and non-parametric tests evaluated group differences. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with higher practice scores, while logisitc regression identified predictors of high perceived barriers. Both models included age, gender, years of experience, practice sector, and number of STI patient consultations as independant variables and confounders.ResultsA total of 133 physicians participated (55% male, 45% female); most were aged 36–45 years (40%) and practicing in primary healthcare centers (38%) or governmental hospitals (35%). Training in STI counseling was most often obtained during residency (38%), though 12% reported no formal training. Clinical exposure was limited, with 51% managing 1–5 STI cases in the past six months. Mean scores were 2.59 for risk assessment, 3.76 for counseling, 3.75 for testing, and 3.64 for attitudes. Multivariable analysis revealed that the absence of recent clinical exposure to STI cases (managing zero cases in the past 6 months) was significantly associated with a lower risk assessment (β = -0.888, p = 0.0031), testing (β = -0.951, p = 0.0024), and attitude scores (β = -0.281, p = 0.0319). Counseling scores were significantly higher among physicians with 16–20 years’ experience (β = 1.333, p 0.0108).ConclusionPCPs in Saudi Arabia demonstrate moderate STI-related competence, with gaps in risk assessment and pervasive barriers to counseling. Targeted continuing education, structured training in sexual health, and standardized screening protocols are needed to enhance STI prevention and care in primary care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03372-9.Keywords: Sexually transmitted infections, Primary care physicians, Counseling practices, Barriers, Attitudes, Saudi Arabia

Background

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) continue to pose a significant global health burden. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 374 million new curable STI infections occurred in 2020 among individuals aged 15–49 years, equating to over one million new infections every day [1]. These infections contribute not only to immediate morbidity but also to long-term complications such as infertility, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and increased HIV susceptibility [2].

International research highlights persistent barriers to integrating STI prevention and counseling into primary care. A systematic review across clinical settings reported that programs in urgent care, university clinics, and primary care often faced time constraints, insufficient provider training, and limited-service capacity [3]. In the United States, primary care-based screening for chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and HIV declined sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic, with only partial recovery thereafter [4]. Similarly, a German national survey found that 43% of general practitioners felt inadequately trained for STI counseling, and many preferred referral to specialists [5]. Collectively, these findings suggest that even in well-resourced health systems, STI counseling and screening are not consistently integrated into routine primary care due to systemic, organizational, and training-related barriers.

In Saudi Arabia, studies consistently show limited awareness of STI transmission, symptoms, and prevention among youth and women, with misconceptions remaining widespread [6–8]. This sociocultural context poses challenges for primary care physicians, who must address stigma and inadequate baseline knowledge during consultations. An observational study in Jeddah found that physician competence varied by syndrome: while 72% prescribed correctly for urethral discharge, only 21% managed vaginal discharge in pregnant women appropriately, though 76% of patients overall received treatment aligned with national guidelines [9]. More recently, a cross-sectional study in the western region reported that 43% of PHC physicians rated themselves as moderately competent, with 41.9% citing barriers such as a lack of cultural sensitivity, insufficient training, and discomfort in counseling adolescents or patients of the opposite gender [10]. Collectively, these findings suggest that both inadequate patient awareness and gaps in provider competence and confidence may hinder effective STI prevention and counseling within Saudi primary care.

Despite growing evidence, important gaps remain. STI counseling in primary care is underutilized, constrained by limited consultation time, inadequate training, and sociocultural sensitivities. In Saudi Arabia, most research has focused on patient awareness or specific clinical practices, with little attention to physicians’ perceptions, barriers, and confidence in delivering counseling. This fragmented evidence leaves uncertainty about how primary care physicians approach STI prevention in practice. In Saudi Arabia, factors such as limited awareness, social stigma, and variability in provider competence hinder the effectiveness of prevention and counseling. This study aimed to evaluate the competence, attitudes, and perceived barriers of primary care physicians (PCPs) in delivering STI prevention and counseling within primary care settings in Saudi Arabia. This study aims to assess physicians’ knowledge, counseling practices, and perceived barriers, generating evidence to guide training, improve communication, and strengthen STI care in Saudi primary healthcare.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted between July 8, 2024, and July 27, 2025, among primary care physicians (PCPs) practicing in Saudi Arabia. Participants were recruited through non-probability convenience sampling. Eligible participants were physicians working in primary care with at least two years of clinical experience who consented to participate and completed the survey. Physicians from other specialties, those with less than two years of experience, or those who declined consent or submitted incomplete responses were excluded. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 119 participants, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a p-value of 0.5. It is important to note that the term “primary care physicians” in this study refers broadly to physicians engaged in first-contact and continuing care, regardless of their specific institutional setting. Participants were recruited from a range of healthcare sectors, including governmental hospitals, academic or university hospitals, private hospitals, and primary healthcare centers, reflecting the diverse practice environments in which primary care is delivered in Saudi Arabia.

Study settings

This study was conducted among primary care physicians practicing across the five main administrative regions of Saudi Arabia: Central, Western, Eastern, Northern, and Southern. These regions include the country’s major healthcare hubs and represent the broad geographic distribution of primary care services. Participants were recruited from different practice sectors, including governmental hospitals, academic or university hospitals, private hospitals, and primary healthcare centers. Although these administrative regions include both urban and rural areas, the survey did not collect a separate variable distinguishing urban versus rural practice setting.

Ethics statement & informed consent

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University, College of Medicine (IRB Log Number: 23-1031; IRB Registration Number with KACST: HAP-01-R-059). All participants were informed of the study objectives, procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained prior to survey completion.

Study survey and data collection

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed in English and distributed electronically via REDCap. The survey link was shared through social media platforms (X, WhatsApp, and Telegram) and was also distributed via official email lists by the Health Research Center at the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS). The study’s objectives were provided at the beginning, and consent was obtained prior to participation.

The instrument comprised three sections: the first section gathered sociodemographic and professional information, including age, gender, years of clinical experience, professional rank, practice sector, region, predominant patient demographic, and clinical exposure to STI cases and counseling. The second section, adapted from a validated tool by Montaño et al. [11] with cultural modifications, assessed STI related medical care across three domains; risk assessment, prevention counseling, and STI testing practices, each measured by a set of items rated on a five-point Likert scale from “Never” to “Always.” The third section, developed by the study authors, examined perceived barriers across five domains: physician knowledge, age and gender differences, patient stigma and discrimination, cost and access to services, and fear of legal or social consequences rated on a five-point Likert scale from “Strongly Disagree“ to “Strongly Agree“. For the scoring of the perceived barriers domain, a median cut-off point approach was utilized [12]. Participants scoring below the median were categorized as having low perceived barriers, while those scoring at or above the median were categorized as having higher perceived barriers.

The questionnaire underwent content validation by family medicine clinical experts and was pilot-tested among 10 primary care physicians to ensure clarity, cultural appropriateness, and relevance. The conscensus was positive and minor modifications were made based on pilot feedback. Following data collection, the internal consistency of each survey domain was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Reliability analysis was conducted for the risk assessment practices, STI prevention counseling practices, STI test offering practices, and perceived barriers domains. A Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable internal consistency. Because the survey link was distributed through open electronic platforms and mailing lists without a defined sampling frame, the total number of physicians who received or viewed the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a precise response rate could not be calculated. However, to provide context regarding the potential representativeness of the sample, national workforce data indicate that approximately 10,896 physicians were practicing across specialties in Saudi Arabia in 2023, according to reports from the Saudi Ministry of Health [13]. Although the exact denominator could not be established, this information provides a general estimate of the broader physician population from which participants may have been drawn.

To support the ethical standards followed, the complete version of the questionnaire, including all adapted and author-developed items, is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Statistical analysis and data management

Data were exported from REDCap into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using JMP version 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participant characteristics and survey responses. Associations between ordinal variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation. Normality was assessed, and data were found to be normally distributed. Therefore, for group comparisons, independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with higher perceived barriers among primary care physicians. Additionally, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with risk assessment practices, STI counseling practices, STI testing behaviors, and physician attitude scores.

Results

A total of 133 physicians participated in the study. Internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.747 to 0.875 across all survey domains, indicating acceptable to good reliability (Table 1). The demographic and professional characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 2. Male physicians comprised 55% (n = 73), while female physicians represented 45% (n = 60). Most respondents were aged 36–45 years (40%), followed by those aged 28–35 years (31%). In terms of professional experience, the most significant proportion had 11–15 years of experience (28%), while 25% had 2–5 years of experience. Participants worked across a range of healthcare settings, with primary healthcare centers (38%) and governmental hospitals (35%) being the most common practice environment. The majority of participants were from the central region (42%). Regarding training in counseling for sexually transmitted infections (STIs), 38% (n = 79) received training during postgraduate or residency programs, 30% (n = 62) during medical school, 20% (n = 41) through continuing medical education courses, and 12% (n = 25) reported no formal training. In terms of clinical exposure, 51% (n = 68) had diagnosed or treated 1–5 patients with STIs in the previous six months, 24% (n = 32) reported no such cases, 16% (n = 21) managed 6–10 cases, and 9% (n = 12) had seen 11 or more cases.

Risk assessment scores did not differ significantly by gender, age, years of experience, professional rank, practice sector, or training background. However, physicians who managed a greater number of STI cases reported significantly higher risk assessment scores (p = 0.006). STI counseling scores were significantly elevated among physicians aged over 55 years (5.11 ± 1.16, p = 0.0001) and those with 16–20 years of professional experience (5.64 ± 1.53, p = 0.0003). The practice sector and the absence of formal STI training were also associated with significant differences in counseling scores (p = 0.0323 and p = 0.0174, respectively).

Regarding STI testing practices, scores increased significantly with the number of STI patients managed (p = 0.0231), although no other demographic or professional characteristics showed significant associations. Physician attitude scores were significantly higher among those with more years of experience (p = 0.017) and those managing a greater number of STI cases (p = 0.0118). Higher levels of perceived barriers were most frequently reported by physicians working in academic or university hospitals (68%, p = 0.0429), while no other significant differences were observed across demographic or professional subgroups.

Multivariable linear regression analysis identified several significant factors associated with physicians’ scores in risk assessment, STI counseling, STI testing, and overall attitude toward STI care (Table 4). Physicians who had not diagnosed or treated any STI patients within the preceding six months demonstrated significantly lower risk assessment scores compared to those who had managed 11 or more cases (β = -0.888, 95% CI: -1.472 to -0.304, p = 0.0031). Age and gender were not significantly associated with risk assessment scores in the adjusted model. Regarding STI counseling, physicians with 16–20 years of professional experience had significantly higher counseling scores compared to those with 21 years or more of experience (β = 1.333, 95% CI: 0.314 to 2.353, p = 0.0108). Interestingly, those who reported having no specific training in STD counseling also had significantly higher scores than their counterparts with formal training (β = 0.594, 95% CI: 0.030 to 1.159, p = 0.0391). No significant associations were observed between counseling scores and physicians’ gender or age groups.

In terms of STI testing, physicians with no recent experience in managing STI patients had significantly lower testing scores than those who had managed 11 or more cases (β = -0.951, 95% CI: -1.598 to -0.305, p = 0.0024). No significant associations were found between testing scores and other demographic or professional variables, including gender, age, or years of experience. Physician attitude scores were significantly lower among younger physicians aged 28–35 years compared to those older than 55 years (β = -0.362, 95% CI: -0.722 to -0.003, p = 0.0481), and among those with 11–15 years of experience compared to ≥ 21 years (β = -0.354, 95% CI: -0.635 to -0.073, p = 0.0137). Additionally, not managing any STI patients in the previous six months was associated with lower attitude scores (β = -0.281, 95% CI: -0.538 to -0.024, p = 0.0319), while managing 6–10 cases was positively associated with higher scores (β = 0.329, 95% CI: 0.047 to 0.610, p = 0.0224). Gender and other practice-related factors did not show significant associations.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with physicians’ likelihood of reporting higher perceived barriers to STI management and counseling (Table 5). Using physicians aged 28–35 years as the reference group, the results revealed that physicians aged 46–55 years had significantly lower odds of perceiving higher barriers (OR = 0.328, 95% CI: 0.112–0.960, p = 0.0421), as did physicians older than 55 years (OR = 0.124, 95% CI: 0.021–0.710, p = 0.0190), suggesting that older physicians may experience fewer perceived barriers in delivering STI-related care.

Discussion

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a significant global health challenge [1]. A substantial proportion of STIs are asymptomatic, such as gonorrhea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis, which complicate timely diagnosis and treatment [14]. In this context, primary care physicians (PCPs) serve as the frontline for STI prevention, diagnosis, counseling, and management. As international health systems increasingly emphasize the integration of preventive counseling into primary care, this study provides valuable insights into the knowledge, practices, and barriers faced by PCPs in Saudi Arabia.

On a five-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always,” the mean risk assessment score of 2.59 corresponds to practices performed between “rarely” and “sometimes,” indicating inconsistent implementation of sexual risk evaluation in routine primary care. In contrast, the mean scores for STI counseling (3.76) and STI testing (3.75) fall within the “sometimes” to “often” range, reflecting relatively more frequent engagement in these activities. However, the observed standard deviations suggest notable variability in practice patterns among physicians. Furthermore, physicians with recent experience managing STI cases consistently demonstrated better risk assessment, testing, and attitude scores, highlighting the role of clinical exposure in enhancing quality of practice. This observation aligns with prior evidence; Kamb et al. highlighted the positive influence of clinical exposure on effective counseling [15]. This is further supported by an interventional study involving 110 clinicians, in which participation in standardized STD/HIV training programs led to significant improvements in knowledge, clinical skills, and practice behaviors compared with pre-training assessments [16]. Similarly, a nationally representative U.S. survey found that clinicians with longer professional experience and greater STI care exposure were more consistent in sexual behavior assessments and preventive counseling [17]. Collectively, these findings highlight the significant impact of both experience and patient volume on shaping physician competence and practice. However, the relationship between patient volume and counseling quality is complex. Although greater patient loads may provide opportunities to refine counseling skills, resource-constrained environments with high workloads and limited staffing can reduce the consistency and quality of care. In a qualitative study exploring healthcare providers’ perspectives on managing STIs in Kenya, healthcare providers emphasized that while integrating additional STI services is a shared goal, it is as important to have adequate structural capacity and workforce to maintain service quality [18]. These studies align with our results, which indicate that longer professional experience and older age are associated with more positive counseling attitudes.

Interestingly, physicians who reported no formal STI counseling training demonstrated higher counseling scores than those with formal training. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. Because outcomes were self-reported and the study design was cross-sectional, the higher scores may reflect greater self-confidence, experiential learning, or differences in case exposure rather than a true lack of benefit from structured training. Additionally, the study did not assess the quality, duration, or recency of prior training, which may influence its effectiveness.

Within Saudi Arabia, AlShamlan et al. reported that limited training and clinical confidence remain prevalent, with nearly one-quarter of family medicine physicians reporting poor or no perceived competency in managing sexual health issues, and over 40% reporting only moderate competency [19]. International evidence similarly demonstrates that higher STD knowledge, whether acquired through structured training or other learning modalities, is associated with improved screening and counseling behaviors [20]. Moreover, interventional studies have shown that targeted educational programs, including interactive workshops and multifaceted training strategies, can significantly improve STI-related knowledge and practice.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while experiential learning may influence counseling confidence, the effectiveness of training likely depends on its structure, reinforcement, and practical components rather than its mere presence.

Although perceived barriers were dichotomized as “high” or “low” for regression analysis, examination of the underlying domains indicates that challenges were multifaceted. Sociocultural factors, particularly patient stigma, fear of social or legal consequences, and discomfort in discussing sexual health, represented prominent concerns. Structural barriers, including limited consultation time, resource constraints, and access to STI-related services, were also commonly reported. These findings suggest that barriers to STI counseling are shaped by both systemic limitations and contextual cultural sensitivities within primary care practice. Older physicians, specifically those aged 46–55 years and those over 55 years, were less likely to perceive high barriers compared to the youngest group (28–35 years), suggesting generational differences in attitudes or adaptability. Practice setting also played a role, with physicians outside academic institutions reporting fewer barriers. Consistent with our findings, Martin et al. emphasized that barriers to STI services often stem from broader structural and health system weaknesses, such as inadequate infrastructure, limited resources, and workforce shortages, highlighting the critical role of systemic capacity in shaping the effectiveness of care delivery [19]. Similar patterns were described by Rizg et al., who identified gender differences, personal discomfort, and attitudes as barriers in addressing sexual health issues among Saudi PCPs and noted a negative correlation between perceived competence and reported barriers [10]. Furthermore, Alansari et al. documented systemic barriers including time constraints, patient reluctance, and lack of standardized screening protocols [20]. These findings align with our results and underscore the multifaceted nature of STI-related service barriers.

Overall, our study highlights deficiencies in STI-related clinical preparedness among Saudi PCPs, consistent with previous reports. Almugti et al. identified knowledge gaps in syndromic STD management among PCPs, recommending targeted continuing medical education (CME) initiatives [9]. Similarly, El-Tholoth et al. documented limited awareness of STD transmission and prevention among Saudi youth [6]. Internationally, PCPs have also been shown to underperform in sexual health counseling; in the U.S., discussions on STD prevention occurred in less than 1% of primary care visits [11]. However, our participants reported comparatively higher STI testing rates than those described in international literature, which may reflect heightened awareness or a perceived greater need within the Saudi healthcare context.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits any causal inferences regarding the relationship between physician characteristics and their counseling or testing practices. Second, the use of a non-probability convenience sampling technique may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to all primary care physicians in Saudi Arabia. Additionally, the Recruitment of PCPs proved challenging, mainly due to low response rates and difficulties in direct physician engagement. Nonetheless, the involvement of the Health Research Center at the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties substantially improved data collection. Finally, although the sample size is sufficient for statistical analysis, it is relatively small. Future research should employ larger, multi-center studies with probability-based sampling techniques to enhance the representativeness of findings across diverse regions and healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia. Qualitative investigations that explore cultural perceptions and barriers in greater depth would provide valuable insights into the contextual challenges of STI counseling. Moreover, future research should also explore patient perspectives to corroborate physician-reported practices with patient experiences, thereby facilitating the development of patient-centered, culturally appropriate strategies for improving STI prevention and management in primary care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings highlight both strengths and gaps in STI-related practices among PCPs in Saudi Arabia, showing moderate competence in managing STIs; however, deficiencies still exist in risk assessment, and significant barriers hinder effective counseling. While experience and clinical exposure improve counseling and testing, systemic barriers such as stigma, patient concerns, limited access to services, and inadequate formal training remain major challenges. The findings emphasize the need for targeted continuing professional education, structured sexual health training, and standardized screening protocols to improve STI prevention, counseling, and management in Saudi Arabia.
