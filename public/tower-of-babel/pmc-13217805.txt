Development and validation of the Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ) from the physiotherapy student’s perception
Authors: Giovanni Sellitto, Ilaria Ruotolo, Manuela Deodato, Roberto Gatti, Emanuele Amadio, Giovanni Galeoto, Marco Trucco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical traineeships represent a core component of undergraduate physiotherapy education, contributing substantially to the development of professional competencies. Despite their central role, psychometrically validated instruments specifically designed to evaluate students’ perceptions of placement quality remain limited. This study aimed to develop and evaluate the measurement properties of the Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ).MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted using an online survey administered to undergraduate physiotherapy students from six Italian universities. The TEQ was developed through a structured content validation process involving expert review and cognitive interviews with students. Structural validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Internal consistency, test–retest reliability, construct validity, and feasibility (including floor and ceiling effects) were evaluated.ResultsA total of 436 students participated. EFA supported a predominantly unidimensional structure, justifying computation of the TEQ total score. The instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.932) and strong test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.916). Construct validity was confirmed through strong correlations with a validated comparator instrument assessing clinical supervisor competencies (r = 0.874, p < 0.001). Floor effects were negligible, whereas ceiling effects were substantial, particularly at the subdomain level. Completion time was under ten minutes.ConclusionThe TEQ demonstrated satisfactory structural validity, excellent reliability, and robust construct validity. Its brevity and feasibility support its use as a practical instrument for evaluating clinical placement quality in undergraduate physiotherapy education. Further research should explore cross-cultural validity and responsiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09179-7.

Background

Clinical education is a cornerstone of undergraduate physiotherapy training, playing a crucial role in achieving the educational objectives outlined in the national Professional Profile (Ministerial Decree No. 741/94) and in adhering to the ethical principles set forth by the Code of Ethics of the Italian Association of Physiotherapy (A.I.F.I.) [1, 2]. Through practical experience, students acquire specific competencies, develop clinical reasoning, and cultivate critical thinking—skills that are essential for professional practice [3].

The quality of learning and competency development during clinical training is strongly influenced by the organizational and relational characteristics of the educational environment [4, 5]. A setting with effective interpersonal relationships, strong leadership, and appropriate supervision by clinical supervisors enhances not only knowledge acquisition but also students’ ability to adapt to complex clinical environments [6]. Students’ satisfaction with clinical placements represents a key indicator of training effectiveness and educational goals [7]. However, evidence from low-resource settings—defined as contexts with limited educational, organizational, or supervisory resources available to support clinical training—suggests lower satisfaction levels, highlighting disparities and the need for robust evaluation tools [8].

Exposure to diverse clinical environments provide students with the opportunity to apply theoretical knowledge in real-world situations, stimulating critical thinking and professional development [9]. These learning opportunities, combined with adaptability and proactive engagement, contribute to the formation of solid professional competencies [10]. Therefore, student satisfaction, linked to the learning environment and the supervisory relationship, should be considered a core parameter in evaluating and improving the educational experience [11, 12].

The role of the clinical tutor (i.e., the clinical supervisor responsible for supporting students during placement), as defined by the Italian Ministerial Decree of February 19, 2009, is pivotal in bridging theoretical instruction with clinical practice [13]. Clinical supervisors provide continuous guidance, structured feedback, and qualified supervision, thereby facilitating the integration of academic professional learning [14, 15]. Despite the centrality of this role, the competencies required for effective tutoring remain partially unexplored in health professions especially in physiotherapy education [16]. A national Australian survey demonstrated that clinical educators often assume their role due to availability or institutional expectations, without specific training or proven competence, and highlighted the lack of validated tools to assess their qualifications and support needs [17].

Existing literature highlights the importance of supervisors’ interpersonal skills and accessibility, yet further research is needed to identify and assess core tutoring competencies [18, 19]. In Spain, psychometrically sound tools have been developed to measure physiotherapy students’ satisfaction with teaching resources, but they are limited to classroom settings and do not address clinical placements [20]. In nursing, the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher (CLES + T) scale demonstrated good test–retest reliability, though it is specific to that discipline and not transferable to physiotherapy education [21]. Similarly, in midwifery, key factors of student satisfaction include supervision quality and interpersonal dynamics, reinforcing the need to foster reflective learning [14]. In physiotherapy, Frontani et al. validated a questionnaire focused on supervisors’ communicative, relational, and pedagogical competencies [22], but it does not evaluate the overall quality of clinical placements, confirming the need for instruments tailored to the multidimensional nature of physiotherapy training.

This study therefore aimed to validate the Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ), developed to comprehensively assess physiotherapy students’ perceptions of clinical placement quality. By evaluating its reliability and validity, we seek to offer a standardized instrument to support quality assurance and inform educational strategies aligned with international standards and evolving clinical demands.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter cross-sectional study evaluated the measurement properties of the newly developed TEQ, designed to assess physiotherapy students’ perceptions of clinical placement quality. It included two phases: content development and validation, followed by psychometric properties assessment. Ethics approval was obtained from the Sapienza University Ethics Committee (CERT_1954CE99AF5), and all participants gave informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Methodological procedures were developed and reported in line with the COSMIN 2.0 guidelines for studies on the measurement properties of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [23].

Study population and recruitment

Participants were undergraduate physiotherapy students (academic year 2024–2025) from six Italian universities. Eligible students were in any year of study and fluent in Italian. Recruitment was via email through academic coordinators, using a secure Google Forms®. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated.

The study involved students from a broad cohort of Italian academic institutions—including Sapienza University of Rome; UniCamillus – Saint Camillus International University of Health and Medical Sciences; Campus Bio-Medico University of Rome; University of Bari “Aldo Moro”; University of Turin and University of Trieste.

Questionnaire development

Conceptual foundation and item generation

The TEQ was developed using a structured, theory-driven process to ensure alignment between the instrument and the underlying construct of perceived quality in clinical placements. Conceptually, the TEQ was developed as a reflective PROM, based on the assumption that students’ perceptions of their clinical experience are shaped by the interplay between organizational features, the quality of supervision, and the interpersonal dynamics with supervisors. The item pool was derived through iterative discussions among an expert panel consisting of academic physiotherapy faculty, clinical educators, and experienced field-based supervisors. This intradisciplinary panel reviewed existing instruments, educational frameworks, and curricular standards to identify key domains. The three primary conceptual dimensions identified were: (1) the support and availability of the clinical site supervisor, (2) the pedagogical and relational role of the internship guide, and (3) the overall structure and perceived educational value of the internship.

Content validity evaluation procedures

In accordance with the COSMIN guidelines for evaluating content validity, both qualitative and quantitative methods were applied to assess the relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility of the TEQ items [24]. The evaluation process was conducted across two phases: expert review and cognitive debriefing with students. These procedures were carried out involving experts and students affiliated with Sapienza University of Rome and the University of Turin.

First, a panel of five experienced professionals in physiotherapy education—including academic course directors and clinical supervisors—were recruited to evaluate each TEQ item. Reviewers assessed relevance (i.e., whether the item accurately captured an essential aspect of the construct), comprehensiveness (i.e., whether the item set as a whole fully covered the construct of interest), and comprehensibility (i.e., whether the item wording was clear and easily understandable by the target population). They were asked to rate each item using a 4-point ordinal scale ranging from 1 (not relevant/not clear) to 4 (highly relevant/highly clear) and to provide qualitative feedback on problematic wording, content overlap, or conceptual omissions.

Simultaneously, cognitive interviews were conducted with a stratified sample of 15 undergraduate physiotherapy students, with representation from each academic year. Students completed the draft questionnaire and subsequently participated in individual cognitive debriefing interviews guided by a semi-structured interview guide developed for this study (Additional file 1).

These students were asked to complete the draft questionnaire and then participate in individual feedback sessions, in which they discussed their interpretations of each item, identified any ambiguous or confusing wording, and suggested possible refinements. The aim of this phase was to evaluate face validity and to ensure semantic alignment between item content and the linguistic-cognitive capabilities of the intended population, in line with recommended approaches for cognitive interviewing and content validation of PROMs [24].

Following data collection, the research team convened a structured consensus meeting to synthesize findings. Items that received a mean score below 3.0 on any dimension were critically reviewed. Revisions were made to enhance clarity, reduce redundancy, and improve the conceptual mapping between items and the three hypothesized dimensions of the TEQ. Particular attention was paid to ensuring that response options and the recall period were appropriate and meaningful for the clinical training context. Where necessary, terminology was simplified or aligned with language commonly used in physiotherapy education.

The revised version of the questionnaire was then redistributed to the expert panel for a second round of review. Final consensus was reached on item content, with all revised items receiving acceptable ratings for relevance and clarity. No items were removed at this stage, as the panel considered the instrument to be sufficiently comprehensive and well-balanced across the three intended domains.

Outcome measures

Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ)

The Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ) is a self-administered instrument specifically developed to assess physiotherapy students’ perceptions of the quality of their experiences during their clinical placements. The full English version of the instrument is provided in Additional file 2.

The TEQ comprises 10 items distributed across three conceptual domains: clinical site supervisor (4 items), internship guide (3 items), and overall internship experience (3 items). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = definitely not, 1 = more no than yes, 2 = more yes than no, 3 = definitely yes), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate more favorable evaluations of the clinical learning environment. The TEQ was conceptualized as a reflective measure covering three content domains (clinical site supervisor, internship guide, and internship experience). Dimensionality was empirically evaluated through structural validity analyses to determine whether it is appropriate to compute a total score by summing item responses. Based on the evidence of a dominant general factor, the TEQ total score was considered the primary score for subsequent analyses, while domain-level scores were retained to support interpretability.

The domain titled “clinical site supervisor” explores the organizational role and welcoming behavior of the staff within the clinical facility, assessing the clarity of expectations, support with logistical or procedural challenges, and efforts to promote active and reflective learning. The “internship guide” domain refers to the student’s direct supervisory figure, assessing their availability, ability to promote autonomy and responsibility, and capacity to create a supportive learning climate. The third domain, “internship experience,” encompasses broader structural and environmental factors, such as the adequacy of the physical and organizational setting, the coherence of feedback and evaluation processes, and students’ overall satisfaction.

The final item of the TEQ includes an open comment field in which students are invited to provide qualitative reflections or suggestions. The entire questionnaire was designed to be completed in under ten minutes, making it highly practical for large-scale academic use and routine program evaluations.

Questionnaire on communicative, relational, and pedagogical competencies of clinical tutors

To evaluate the construct validity of the TEQ, the study used as comparator a 27-item validated instrument designed to measure students’ evaluations of the communicative, relational, and pedagogical competencies of clinical supervisors in physiotherapy education. Developed by Frontani et al., this tool is structured into three macro-areas aligned with key supervisor competencies: (1) communication skills, including listening, stimulating critical thinking, and dialoguing constructively with students; (2) relational skills, such as establishing trust, offering constructive feedback, and supporting students’ professional identity development; and (3) pedagogical skills, including the ability to plan individualized learning objectives, promote progressive responsibility, and facilitate reflective learning [22].

Data collection procedures

Timing and mode of administration

The data collection phase was structured to ensure rigorous and standardized administration of the questionnaires across all participating institutions. Data were collected electronically using Google Forms®, a secure, GDPR-compliant platform. Students were invited to complete the online questionnaires at two time points, corresponding to distinct phases of the data collection protocol.

The first administration (T0) was scheduled within 48 h following the conclusion of each participant’s most recent two-month clinical placement. At this time point, students were asked to complete both the TEQ and the Questionnaire on Communicative, Relational, and Pedagogical Competencies of Clinical Tutors. This strategy aimed to capture students’ perceptions while their experiences were still recent, minimizing potential recall bias [25].

The second administration (T1) was conducted seven days after the initial submission and included only the TEQ. This time-lagged reassessment was designed to evaluate the test–retest reliability of the instrument. To avoid conditioning participant responses or introducing measurement bias, no information was provided at T0 regarding the planned follow-up administration.

Participation was voluntary and anonymous. No identifying personal data were collected. To enable the secure matching of T0 and T1 responses while preserving confidentiality, each participant generated a pseudonymized identification code composed of the first two letters of the mother’s name, the first letter of the father’s name, and the day of birth. This code was used exclusively to pair responses across time points and was not accessible to faculty or course coordinators.

Sample size

Sample size determination was informed by the psychometric objectives of the study, specifically the evaluation of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and construct validity of the TEQ.

For the assessment of internal consistency, Bonett’s formula was applied to estimate the minimum number of participants required to detect a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80, assuming a 95% confidence interval, a margin of error of ± 0.05, and 90% statistical power [26]. Under these conditions, the required sample size was calculated to be at least 169 participants.

To evaluate test–retest reliability, the sample size was determined based on the detection of an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.80 against a minimally acceptable threshold of 0.60, with an alpha level of 0.05 and 90% power. Assuming two repeated measurements per participant, the number of observations needed was 264, corresponding to a minimum of 132 participants.

Regarding construct validity, a moderate correlation (r = 0.40) between the TEQ and the comparator instrument was hypothesized. Based on COSMIN recommendations for hypothesis testing and under the same statistical assumptions (α = 0.05, power = 0.90), the minimum required sample size was 24 participants.

To account for potential attrition and ensure robust psychometric estimations, a recruitment target of 150 participants was established across all participating sites.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30.

Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the sample and to summarize the distribution of TEQ responses. Specifically, absolute and relative frequencies (n, %) were used to describe categorical variables such as university affiliation and year of study, providing an overview of the academic composition of the sample.

Item-level responses were examined to assess the variability and distribution of answers across the three TEQ domains. In addition, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to investigate potential differences in TEQ scores across academic years (first, second, and third year). Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated prior to each test. For each domain and the total score, mean values and standard deviations were reported, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Feasibility and score distribution was explored by examining response distributions and calculating floor and ceiling effects for each TEQ item, each subdomain score, and the total score. Floor and ceiling effects were defined as the proportion of respondents achieving the minimum and maximum possible scores, respectively. To enhance interpretability and comparability, floor/ceiling estimates were based on observed frequency distributions for the original response categories (items scored 0–3) and for summed subdomain and total scores.

Structural validity was examined to determine whether TEQ scores adequately reflected the dimensionality of the underlying construct and whether computation of a total score was justified. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal component extraction on the covariance matrix with listwise deletion. Sampling adequacy and factorability were evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The number of components to retain was determined according to the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1) and visual inspection of the scree plot [27]. When only one component satisfied retention criteria, rotation was not applied. Component loadings and communalities were examined to evaluate the strength of the association between each item and the retained component and the proportion of item variance explained. Loadings ≥ 0.40 were considered meaningful. Communality values were interpreted as follows: < 0.30 = low, 0.30–0.50 = acceptable, > 0.50 = good. Component loadings and communalities are presented from the scale-modified solution to facilitate interpretability.

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients calculated for the TEQ total score and for each domain: clinical site supervisor, internship guide, and internship experience. In accordance with psychometric conventions, values ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable, while values ≥ 0.80 indicated good internal consistency. To ensure each item contributed meaningfully to its respective domain, corrected item-total correlations were also computed. Internal consistency findings were interpreted in light of the structural validity results supporting a dominant general factor.

Test–retest reliability was examined to assess the temporal stability of the instrument over a one-week interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, applied to both the total TEQ score and individual subscale scores. ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.50 = poor; 0.50–0.75 = moderate; 0.75–0.90 = good; > 0.90 = excellent. All estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Construct validity was evaluated through hypothesis testing, based on theoretical expectations of moderate to strong correlations between the TEQ and a previously validated comparator instrument—the Questionnaire on Communicative, Relational, and Pedagogical Competencies of Clinical Tutors. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between each TEQ subscale and the corresponding subscales and total score of the comparator. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (r = 0.10–0.29), moderate (r = 0.30–0.49), or strong (r ≥ 0.50), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed). This analysis was designed to confirm convergent validity by demonstrating that higher TEQ scores corresponded with higher evaluations of clinical supervisor competencies.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 436 undergraduate physiotherapy students participated in the study, representing six Italian universities. The sample included students from all three academic years, with the majority enrolled in the second year (n = 221; 50.7%). The institutional distribution was led by Sapienza University of Rome (n = 137; 31.4%), followed by the University of Turin, the University of Bari “Aldo Moro”, Campus Bio-Medico University of Rome, University of Trieste, and UniCamillus. Structural validity

Preliminary analyses indicated that the data met the assumptions required for factor analysis. Sampling adequacy was excellent (KMO = 0.933), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ²(45) = 2904.788, p < 0.001), indicating sufficient inter-item correlations. EFA supported a predominantly unidimensional structure of the TEQ. Only one component exhibited an eigenvalue greater than 1 (3.606), accounting for 62.33% of the total variance. The scree plot showed a clear inflection after the first component, further supporting retention of a single dimension (Fig. 1). Component loadings were consistently moderate to high across items. In the scale-modified solution, loadings ranged from 0.690 to 0.848. All loadings exceeded the predefined threshold for meaningful interpretation. Communality values ranged from 0.476 to 0.719, indicating acceptable-to-good proportions of item variance explained by the retained component. These findings support computation of the TEQ total score for subsequent analyses (Table 3).

In addition to examining the latent structure of the TEQ, the distribution of observed scores was evaluated to assess feasibility and interpretability, specifically the presence of floor and ceiling effects.

Feasibility and score distribution

Score distributions were skewed toward higher values. For the TEQ total score (range 0–30), the floor effect (score = 0) was 0.46% (2/436), while the ceiling effect (score = 30) was 18.35% (80/436). At the subdomain level, floor effects were consistently negligible (0.92–1.15%), whereas ceiling effects were substantial, particularly for Clinical Site Supervisor (31.88%, 139/436) and Internship Guide (37.61%, 164/436), and were also notable for Internship Experience (28.44%, 124/436). At the item level (0–3 response options), floor effects were low across all items (range: 2.06–3.67% selecting the lowest category), whereas ceiling effects were pronounced (range: 41.06–58.94% selecting the highest category), indicating limited discrimination among respondents reporting very positive traineeship evaluations. Detailed floor and ceiling estimates for the TEQ total score, each domain, and individual items are reported in Additional File 3.

Internal Consistency

The TEQ demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the total score (Cronbach’s α = 0.932). Subscale-specific coefficients also exceeded the conventional threshold for good internal consistency, with values of α = 0.885 for Clinical Site Supervisor, α = 0.860 for Internship Guide, and α = 0.805 for Internship Experience. All items showed satisfactory corrected item-total correlations, confirming that each item contributed meaningfully to its respective subscale. These results are presented in Table 4.

Test–retest reliability

Test–retest reliability was assessed in a subgroup of 135 students who completed the TEQ at two time points, seven days apart. The instrument demonstrated excellent temporal stability. The ICC for the total score was 0.916 (95% CI: 0.882–0.940), with similarly high ICCs for each subdomain: Clinical Site Supervisor (ICC = 0.898), Internship Guide (ICC = 0.838), and Internship Experience (ICC = 0.894). The mean scores at the two time points were closely aligned, confirming consistency of responses over time. Complete test–retest data are reported in Table 5.

Construct validity

Construct validity was evaluated by correlating TEQ scores with those obtained from the validated Questionnaire on Communicative, Relational, and Pedagogical Competencies of Clinical Tutors. As hypothesized, strong and statistically significant associations were found. The TEQ total score correlated highly with the total score of the comparator instrument (r = 0.874, p < 0.001), confirming convergent validity. Correlations between TEQ subscales and corresponding dimensions of the comparator ranged from r = 0.728 to r = 0.826 for Clinical Site Supervisor, r = 0.731 to r = 0.779 for Internship Guide, and r = 0.728 to r = 0.773 for Internship Experience. These findings support the alignment between the constructs measured by the TEQ and the established domains of clinical supervision quality. Table 6 shows the full correlation matrix.

Discussion

This study evaluated the measurement properties of the Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ), a newly developed questionnaire designed to assess physiotherapy students’ perceptions of clinical placement quality. The results indicated that the TEQ is a valid and reliable tool across its three conceptual domains—Clinical Site Supervisor, Internship Guide, and Internship Experience. High internal consistency, strong test–retest reliability, and significant correlations with Questionnaire on Communicative, Relational, and Pedagogical Competencies of Clinical Tutors, confirm its utility within undergraduate physiotherapy education.

A key finding is that structural validity analyses supported a dominant general factor underlying the 10 TEQ items. This evidence justifies the computation of the TEQ total score as the primary indicator of perceived placement quality. Although the questionnaire was designed to ensure content coverage across three domains, the observed dimensionality suggests that these domains represent closely related facets of a broader construct rather than fully distinct latent dimensions.

The analysis of score distributions revealed negligible floor effects alongside substantial ceiling effects, particularly at the subdomain level. This pattern indicates that a large proportion of students reported highly positive perceptions of their traineeship experience. While this finding is consistent with generally favorable evaluations of clinical placements, it also suggests reduced discrimination at the upper end of the scale. From a measurement perspective, pronounced ceiling effects may limit the TEQ’s ability to differentiate among respondents with very positive experiences or to detect incremental improvements in already high-quality placements [28].

Within clinical education contexts, this distributional tendency may partly reflect the evaluative nature of traineeships, where student ratings are often positively skewed. Factors such as social desirability, gratitude toward supervisors, or placement within well-structured clinical environments may contribute to this effect [29, 30]. Future research should determine whether similar distributional characteristics are observed across different educational systems and cultural contexts. Additionally, refinement of response categories or the inclusion of items targeting higher levels of perceived placement quality may enhance the instrument’s discriminative capacity.

The excellent internal consistency observed for the overall scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.932) and for each subscale aligns with findings from previous studies on similar tools in health professions education. The high Cronbach’s alpha should be interpreted alongside the structural validity findings supporting a dominant general factor. As noted by Frontani et al., their instrument assessing communicative, relational, and pedagogical competencies of physiotherapy supervisors reached a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96, reinforcing the strength of multidimensional instruments for clinical education contexts [22]. Similarly, comparable internal consistency levels have been documented for the Clinical Placement Quality Survey–Student (CPQS-S), developed by Jones et al. for allied health students, and for the midwifery student evaluation of practice (MidSTEP) tool validated by Griffiths et al. [31].

The TEQ also demonstrated strong measurement stability, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) exceeding 0.83 across all subscales and a total score ICC surpassing 0.91. These values reflect the test–retest reliability observed in tools such as the Clinical Learning Environment Comparison Survey (CLECS) questionnaire and the Turkish version of the MidSTEP tool, both of which confirmed robust stability over brief retesting intervals [32, 33]. The one-week time frame adopted here follows COSMIN recommendations for PROMs, offering a reasonable balance between recall precision and stability.

Construct validity was further reinforced by strong, statistically significant correlations with the comparator instrument developed by Frontani et al., which focuses on supervisor competencies. These findings align with Clinical Placement Quality Survey-Educator (CPQS-E) validation and Irish supervision studies supporting a multifactorial model of clinical education environments [34, 35]. As indicated by these authors, clinical education environments are best understood as multifactorial constructs, integrating both interpersonal relationships and structural-organizational components—an approach that underpins the TEQ design.

In contrast to earlier studies reporting greater satisfaction among last term students—such as those by Weerasekara et al. and Pallant et al., which used the CLES + T and the Practice Environment Scale (PES) in midwifery and nursing education—this study found no significant differences in TEQ scores across academic years [36, 37]. This consistent perception of placement quality among physiotherapy students may reflect the standardized organization of internships and structured supervisory models within Italian undergraduate education. Unlike settings where satisfaction increases with seniority, the TEQ reveals a homogeneous experience, possibly due to supervisor training, regulated placements, and institutional oversight. Barrett et al. similarly noted that uniform supervision and consistent placement structures across institutions promote comparable learning experiences regardless of academic progression [35].

From an international perspective, the TEQ shares conceptual similarities with several instruments developed for allied health education, including the CPQS-S, the evaluation model by Frantz et al. for physiotherapy students, and placement readiness assessments by Nehyba et al., which emphasize alignment between learning activities and student needs [31, 38, 39]. The TEQ is tailored to the Italian context, reflecting national regulations and the pedagogical role of supervisors. At the same time, the underlying constructs it assesses—such as supervision quality, relational support, and the perceived educational value of the placement—may also be relevant across other educational systems. Entry-level physiotherapy education, however, differs substantially across countries, particularly between undergraduate and graduate-entry models, with potential implications for how students interpret placement quality items. As emphasized in several recent studies, local adaptation and cross-cultural validation are essential to ensure contextual relevance and psychometric soundness across diverse educational settings [40–42].

The TEQ’s reflective structure, concise item set, and short completion time (< 10 min) make it particularly feasible for routine integration into academic monitoring and quality assurance cycles. In keeping with recommendations by Riahi et al., the inclusion of an open-ended comment section enhances the depth of student responses and facilitates a mixed-methods approach to evaluation [32]. As further highlighted by Judd et al., qualitative feedback is essential to uncover nuances in supervision and learning [43].

In conclusion, the TEQ represents a psychometrically robust and contextually grounded instrument for assessing the quality of clinical placements in Italian physiotherapy education. It addresses a significant gap in validated tools tailored to the specific needs of physiotherapy training and offers educators and curriculum planners a structured means of monitoring and enhancing the clinical learning environment. Its use may support ongoing improvement of internships and supervision, aligning with national standards and international educational priorities.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Although the sample size was adequate for all planned psychometric analyses and included students from six Italian universities, the results reflect a single national educational context and an undergraduate training model. Therefore, generalizability to other countries, institutional systems, or graduate-entry physiotherapy programs may be limited. Differences in curriculum structure, timing of clinical placements, and supervisory frameworks may influence how students interpret TEQ items. Future studies should therefore examine the cross-cultural validity, measurement invariance, and applicability of the TEQ across diverse educational settings.

The use of a self-administered online questionnaire may have introduced response-related biases, including social desirability and individual variability in item interpretation. Additionally, although the timing of questionnaire administration was designed to minimize recall bias, subjective evaluations of clinical experiences remain inherently susceptible to contextual influences.

While construct validity was supported through correlations with a validated comparator instrument, the study did not include external performance indicators such as academic achievement, clinical competence assessments, or observational measures. The inclusion of such variables could further strengthen evidence regarding criterion and predictive validity.

Finally, although qualitative comments were collected through an open-ended item, these responses were not formally analyzed in the present study. Future mixed-methods investigations, including thematic analysis of qualitative feedback, may provide deeper insights into the experiential dimensions of clinical placement quality.

Conclusion

The Traineeship Evaluation Questionnaire (TEQ) demonstrated satisfactory structural validity, excellent internal consistency, strong test–retest reliability, and robust construct validity in a large multicenter sample of undergraduate physiotherapy students. These findings support its use as a reliable and valid instrument for assessing students’ perceptions of clinical placement quality.

In practical use, the TEQ total score should be considered the primary summary measure for the overall evaluation of clinical placement quality, in line with the evidence supporting a dominant general factor. Subdomain scores may still assist interpretation of specific aspects of the traineeship experience; however, they should be interpreted with caution, particularly in light of the ceiling effects observed at the subdomain level.

By capturing multiple dimensions of the traineeship experience within a concise and practical format, the TEQ provides educators and academic institutions with a structured tool to inform quality assurance processes and guide evidence-based educational improvements.

As clinical education continues to evolve within increasingly complex healthcare systems, the availability of psychometrically sound evaluation instruments is essential. Future research should focus on cross-cultural validation, responsiveness, and the applicability of the TEQ across different educational models, as well as its integration within broader programmatic assessment frameworks.
