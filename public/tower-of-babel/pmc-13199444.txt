Porosity optimization and Fe2O3 functionalization synergistically enhance nitrate adsorption on bamboo biochar
Authors: Wen Jia, Lei Zhang, Yan Chen, Hong Xu, Jing Wang, Fan Yang, LiuYan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating issue of nitrate-nitrogen (NO3−–N) pollution in water bodies demands advanced remediation solutions. This study developed engineered iron-modified biochar (FeB) via a sequential acid-washing and FeCl3-impregnation strategy to enhance NO3−–N adsorption. Comprehensive characterization (SEM, FTIR, XRD, and XPS) confirmed that the modification successfully optimized the pore structure, enriched oxygen-containing functional groups, and loaded active Fe2O3 particles. FeB exhibited a reversed positive surface charge (zeta potential = + 7.58 mV), favoring anion capture. Adsorption isotherms were well-described by the Langmuir model, revealing a maximum adsorption capacity of 6829.74 mg/kg, which is more than three times that of pristine biochar. Kinetic studies indicated that the process followed the pseudo-second-order model, suggesting a chemisorption-dominated mechanism. Detailed mechanistic analysis demonstrated that nitrate removal was synergistically governed by electrostatic attraction, ligand exchange (forming inner-sphere complexes), and physical pore filling. Crucially, FeB achieved high adsorption efficiency under neutral pH (7.0) with a low dosage (1.0 g/L) and exhibited excellent structural stability after adsorption. This work provides a robust, scalable adsorbent for mitigating aquatic NO3−–N contamination, showing significant potential for practical applications in wastewater treatment and constructed wetlands.Keywords: Iron-modified biochar, Nitrate-nitrogen adsorption, Structure optimization, Ligand exchange, Adsorption mechanismSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Nitrate-nitrogen (NO3⁻-N) typically constitutes more than 76% of the total dissolved nitrogen in surface waters. Concentrations above 0.2 mg/L readily promote eutrophication and can precipitate harmful algal blooms and hypoxic events. In drinking water, elevated NO3--N has been epidemiologically linked to higher incidences of diabetes mellitus, methemoglobinaemia, gastric carcinoma, and thyroid disorders1,2, Tracy A.3. Accordingly, the World Health Organization sets a guideline value of 10 mg L⁻1 for NO3⁻-N in potable supplies. Nonetheless, intensive fertiliser application and insufficiently treated municipal and industrial effluents have driven persistent increases in ambient NO3⁻-N, rendering nitrate pollution a critical global water-quality issue. Recent nationwide surveillance in China found that 7.83% of rivers exceeded the statutory limit of 45 mg L-1, with concentrations surpassing 90 mg L-1 in the Yangtze Estuary, Mudan River, and Haihe River. Effective reduction of aquatic NO3⁻-N loads is therefore essential to the sustainable stewardship of water resources.

Available technologies for NO3⁻-N abatement span physical, chemical, and biological domains, and include adsorption, electrocatalytic reduction, membrane separation, nanomaterial-assisted processes, and biological denitrification. Among these options, adsorption is widely adopted as a preliminary treatment because it combines operational simplicity with low energy demand and consistently high removal efficiencies. Biochar—produced from the pyrolytic conversion of renewable biomass—has emerged as a particularly promising adsorbent owing to its ready availability, low production cost, substantial sorption capacity, and overall environmental compatibility4.

Biochar’s utility extends well beyond nitrate control to the immobilisation of trace metals in aquatic environments. However, the practical application of pristine biochar is often constrained by its relatively limited specific surface area and insufficient abundance of surface functional groups, which can restrict its adsorption capacity and kinetics for contaminants. Integration of biochar with gibberellic acid-treated rhizobacteria, for instance, elevated crop water-use efficiency while concurrently lowering the dissolved concentrations of Cd, Pb and Zn in irrigation return flows5. Column studies have likewise documented a 64% decline in aqueous Cd when rice-husk biochar was applied at 5% w/w under alternating wet–dry conditions, highlighting its dual value for water purification and soil remediation. Performance is further enhanced by physicochemical modification: acid or alkali activation and high-temperature treatment routinely expand the specific surface area to > 800 m2 g-1, create abundant micro-/mesopores, and enrich surface O- and N-functional group6. Through modification, biochar can exhibit significantly improved specific surface area, increased pore structure abundance, and enhanced adsorption capacities, which are crucial for the effective treatment of wastewater contaminants. For example, a study by Goswami et al.7 found that biochar modified at higher temperatures shows a significant increase in surface area, resulting in improved adsorption capabilities. This modification helps in more effective removal of dyes and other pollutants from wastewater. These findings align with the results of a study by Godwin et al. (2019), which enhances its surface area and porosity, making it more effective in adsorbing heavy metals from aqueous solutions. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that tailored biochar is a versatile, low-carbon material capable of simultaneously attenuating nutrients and heavy metals, thereby supporting integrated, sustainable strategies for water-quality management.

Despite extensive domestic and international work on biochar’s ability to sequester heavy metals and organic contaminants, comparatively few investigations have evaluated its affinity for oxygen-bearing anions; in particular, the adsorption behaviour of NO3⁻-N remains largely unexplored8.

Bamboo-derived biochar has attracted widespread attention as a valorised waste product that combines high porosity, large specific surface area and a rich inventory of surface hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. These features translate into superior sorptive performance for dissolved metals (e.g., Cu2+, Pb2+, Cd2+) and organic micropollutants (dyes, pharmaceuticals) relative to biochars produced from other agricultural residues9. Chemical activation—particularly acid treatment—further enlarges the accessible surface area and introduces additional functional moieties, thereby broadening the spectrum of contaminants that can be sequestered10. Li et al.11 further expands the theoretical framework by introducing an anion-synergism mechanism for enhanced metal adsorption. In their work, aminopropyltriethoxysilane-functionalized biochar grafted on MXene demonstrated highly selective Pb2⁺ removal from complex wastewater matrices. The synergistic interaction between surface amine groups and co-existing anions such as NO3⁻ and Cl⁻ was found to significantly enhance Pb2+ binding via electrostatic attraction and complexation, even in the presence of competing ions . This evidence highlights the potential of biochar interfaces to leverage anion co-effects, offering a valuable conceptual link to FeB’s interaction with anionic species like NO3⁻ in this study.

Specifically, the sequential treatment of acid washing followed by iron loading adopted in this study offers a distinct advantage in material design. Acid washing effectively removes impurities to unblock pores, thereby enhancing the specific surface area and porosity for physical adsorption. Subsequent iron loading further optimizes the interfacial chemistry by introducing abundant oxygen-containing functional groups and active iron sites, which significantly improve reactivity compared to pristine biochar12. Unlike complex reduction methods such as nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) synthesis, this two-step modification is more controllable and scalable13. Moreover, the iron-loaded matrix promotes a dual mechanism of physical adsorption and chemical reduction, rendering it particularly effective for targeted nitrate removal14. By optimizing both the structural framework and adsorption mechanisms, this approach provides a robust foundation for practical environmental applications.

Although functionalized biochars have shown promise, a critical bottleneck restricts their practical engineering application. Many existing iron-modified adsorbents achieve optimal nitrate removal only under highly acidic conditions (pH 2.0–4.0) or require substantial dosages (> 2 g/L) to overcome electrostatic repulsion. Such acidic requirements are incompatible with subsequent biological denitrification processes, which necessitate a neutral environment. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop engineered biochars that maintain high anion adsorption capacity under neutral conditions to facilitate integrated water treatment strategies. This study addresses this gap by optimizing the synergy between porosity and iron functionalization to achieve efficient nitrate capture at neutral pH with reduced material dosage.

In this study, pristine bamboo-derived biochar was activated and impregnated with ferric ions to fabricate iron-modified biochar (FeB). Notably, FeB exhibited a maximum nitrate adsorption capacity of 4060.63 mg kg-1, which is 3.9 times greater than that of the unmodified biochar. The adsorption kinetics were best described by the pseudo-second-order model, indicating a chemisorption-dominated process. Comprehensive characterization (Scanning electron microscopy, SEM; Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, FTIR; X-ray diffraction, XRD; X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, XPS) confirmed successful iron modification and elucidated the associated mechanisms, predominantly involving electrostatic attraction and ligand exchange. This work not only identifies FeB as an efficient, low-cost adsorbent with strong potential as a substrate filler for enhanced nitrogen removal in constructed wetlands, but also provides deeper mechanistic insights into biochar-based remediation.

Materials and methods

Materials

The bamboo biochar used in this study was supplied by Hangzhou Lin’an Yaoshi Charcoal Industry Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China), produced via pyrolysis at 600 °C. The raw biochar had an average particle diameter of approximately 5 mm, a bulk density of 0.75 g cm-3, and a specific surface area of 2.5 × 108 m2 m-3. All chemical reagents, including hydrochloric acid (HCl), ferric chloride (FeCl3), potassium nitrate (KNO3), and potassium bromide (KBr), were of analytical grade and used with deionized water. Sandy soil was collected from the riverbank adjacent to the High-Tech Research Institute of Nanjing University in Changzhou. The soil was air-dried naturally indoors and subsequently sieved through a 1 mm mesh (No. 18) prior to use.

Preparation of iron-modified biochar

For acid activation, the bamboo biochar was immersed in 1 mol L-1 HCl at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v) for 2 h, followed by repeated rinsing with de-ionised water to neutral pH. A sub-sample of the acid-treated biochar was subsequently impregnated with 1 mol L-1 FeCl3 (solid-to-liquid ratio 1:12.5; Fe/C mass ratio 0.7) for 2 h and washed to neutrality9. The biochar only activated by HCl was referred to as unmodified biochar (biochar), while the biochar modified by FeCl3 was referred to as FeB in this study. Finally, both biochar and FeB were dried in an oven at 80 °C until constant weight was achieved.

Characterization analysis of iron-modified biochar

SEM analysis: biochar and FeB were thoroughly ground into powder and gold-coated using ion sputtering, and then observed analyzed their surface morphological features using a scanning electron microscope (SU8000, Japan Hitachi).

XRD analysis: a suitable amount of ground Fe-biochar was dried at 120 ℃ for 12 h to prepare the sample. Subsequently, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using an XRD instrument (X'TRA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under scanning conditions of 2θ from 10° to 65° at a scanning rate of 2°/min for sample phase analysis.

FTIR analysis: biochar and FeB were individually grounded after mixed with KBr (spectroscopic grade) at a ratio of 1:100 (w/w), and then pressed into pellets and analyzed for their functional group types using an FTIR instrument (NEXUS870, NICOLET, USA) with a resolution of 0.09 cm-1, a wavenumber accuracy of 0.01 cm−1, and a range of absorption peaks from 400 to 4000 cm-1.

XPS Analysis: the surface elemental contents and functional group types of biochar and FeB were determined using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy instrument (PHI5000 VersaProbe, ULVAC-PHI, Japan).

Analysis of basic chemical characteristics of iron-modified biochar

The pH of each material was measured after suspending 1 g of biochar or FeB in 5 mL deionised water (solid-to-liquid ratio = 1:5, w/v) using a multi-parameter meter (HQ30D, Hach, USA). Total C, H and N were quantified with a CHN-O-Rapid elemental analyser (Heraeus, Germany); O, P and Fe contents were obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (PHI-5000 VersaProbe, ULVAC-PHI, Japan). Zeta potential was determined on a ZetaPALS analyser (Brookhaven, USA) after dispersing the sorbent in deionised water (1:150, w/v) and sonicating for 30 min at 25 °C. Exchangeable NH4⁺-N, NO3⁻-N, NO2⁻-N and dissolved inorganic phosphate were extracted with 0.5 M K2SO4 (1:10, w/v) and quantified colorimetrically on a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (V-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Study on the adsorption performance of nitrate by iron-modified biochar

Adsorption kinetics experiments: solutions of KNO3 with initial NO3--N concentrations of 20 mg/L and 100 mg/L were prepared using deionized water to study the NO3--N adsorption performance of biochar and FeB. Three sets of experiments were conducted: (a) biochar Group, 100 mL KNO3 solution + 0.1 g biochar; (b) FeB Group, 100 mL KNO3 solution + 0.1 g FeB; (c) Soil Group, 100 mL KNO3 solution + 0.1 g soil, this experimental group primarily served as a control group to provide background parameters. The experiments were carried out in 300 mL conical flasks, with the pH value adjusted to 7.0, then placed on a shaker (25℃, 150 rpm) to shake for a certain period under the constant-temperature . Afterward, the water samples were taken out immediately and filtered through the aqueous membrane filter (0.45 μm). Finally, the NO3--N concentrations in the filtrate was determined using a ultraviolet and visible spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan). Sampling times were 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 13, and 24 h, and three replicates were set for each experiment.

Adsorption thermodynamic experiments: 1 g of samples was added to 100 mL of KNO3 solutions with initial NO3--N concentrations of 0, 2, 5, 10, 50, 100, and 150 mg/L. The mixtures were placed in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and shaken at 200 rpm for 24 h at 25℃. The initial pH of the solutions was adjusted to 7.0. After 24 h, each mixture was filtered through ash-free filter paper, and 2 mL of the filtrate was further filtered using a 0.45 μm syringe filter. The NO3--N concentration in the filtrate was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

Results and discussion

Surface morphological characteristics of iron-modified biochar

Scanning-electron microscopy (Fig. 1) and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectra (Fig. 2) demonstrated marked alterations in the architecture of the FeB compared with the biochar. Biochar displayed an irregular, plate-like framework that was predominated by micropores and exhibits only sporadic meso-/macropores, giving rise to a relatively smooth external surface. In FeB, however, the amounts of meso- and macropores had increased significantly, and the surface was studded with numerous fine particulates identified by elemental mapping as ferric (hydr)oxide phases. These deposits form a discontinuous, membrane-like coating that roughened the biochar surface while locally occluding pores15. The concurrent enlargement of pore size distribution and establishment of iron-rich surface domains should enhance the accessibility of adsorption sites and introduce additional sorption pathways (e.g., inner-sphere complexation), thereby improving the material’s affinity for nitrate16.

The results elemental spectrum (Fig. 2) showed that FeB was primarily composed of carbon (C) and oxygen (O) elements, with atomic percents of 88.16% and 10.49%, respectively. This was attributed to the further decomposition of organic components of biochar during the process of pyrolysis carbonization, leading to the formation of numerous aromatic compounds and oxygen-containing functional groups (such as -COOH and -OH). After acid washing and iron modification, the elemental contents of Mg, Al, and Ca in FeB had fallen below the detection threshold. Additionally, the weight percentage of Fe in FeB (1.65%) and its atomic percentage (0.38%) were significantly higher than those in biochar (0.55% and 0.13%), respectively (Table 1). These were consistent with the results of elemental composition analysis, indicating the successful loading of iron on the surface and within the pores of FeB.

After HCl activation and iron modification, the pH value of FeB was close to neutral (7.42), significantly lower than that of biochar (8.84), which is more favorable for denitrifying microbial growth and metabolism17. Similar to biochar, the primary elemental component of FeB was still carbon (C), accounting for 55.58%. FeB had significantly higher contents of oxygen (O) (21.85%) and iron (Fe) (1.31%) than biochar, indicating the iron oxides were presented on the surface of FeB. However, the nitrogen (N, 0.34%) and phosphorus (P, undetectable) contents in FeB were lower than those in biochar, potentially suggesting more available sites for the adsorption of N and P from wastewater, though this hypothesis requires experimental validation. Zeta-potential measurements revealed a reversal in surface charge: FeB carried a net positive charge of + 7.58 mV, whereas biochar was negatively charged (–13.9 mV). The positively charged surface favors electrostatic attraction of oxygen-bearing anions such as NO3- and PO43-, thereby improving anion uptake. Iron modification notably improved surface hydrophilicity, effectively reducing the inherent hydrophobicity of pristine biochar. Increased hydrophilicity facilitates bacterial adhesion to the adsorbent surface18. Meanwhile, based on the compositions of bacterial cell membranes, bacteria exhibited hydrophilicity, while biochar was generally hydrophobic, making bacterial attachment challenging. Iron-loading modification effectively reduced the hydrophobic nature of the biochar surface, thus enhancing microbial attachment19,20. Furthermore, the soluble N and P concentrations extracted from FeB and biochar exhibited significant differences. FeB had significantly higher soluble NH4+-N (1.55 mg/kg) and NO3--N (4.12 mg/kg) compared to biochar, dissolved inorganic phosphorus (DIP) content was significantly lower (undetectable) than biochar, and NO2--N content was negligible 21–23.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy probes the vibrational and rotational modes of molecular bonds, thereby revealing the nature and abundance of surface functional groups. During pyrolytic conversion of lignocellulosic biomass to biochar, polysaccharides (cellulose and hemicellulose) undergo progressive thermal depolymerisation, while high-temperature reactions volatilise a spectrum of liquid and gaseous products. Concurrently, aromatic domains derived from lignin experience partial demethoxylation and cleavage of aliphatic side chains, modifying or eliminating many native oxygen-containing functionalities. FTIR thus provides a useful fingerprint of these thermally induced transformations and the residual functional moieties that govern the sorptive behaviour of the resulting biochar 24–26. As a result, organic compounds such as methanol, phenols, and acetic acid were abundant , while fatty alkyls and lipid carbonyl groups were relatively reduced in biochar.

Absorptions in the 1750–1200 cm-1 region arise chiefly from C = O, C = C, C–N and N–H vibrations. The band at 1693 cm-1 corresponds to conjugated C = O and aromatic C = C stretching, whereas the band at 1560 cm-1 is attributable to N–H deformation and hydrogen-bonded –OH stretching. In this study, there were no significant differences in the absorption bands of FeB and biochar at 1693 and 1560 cm-1 (Fig. 3). It indicated that the loading of iron did not change the main structure of the carbon surface.

FTIR spectra of biochar and Fe-modified biochar (FeB).

However, FeB exhibited markedly stronger absorption bands at 1029, 790 and 465 cm-1 than the biochar. The peak at 1029 cm-1 reflects asymmetric stretching of C–O–C and C–C bonds in residual polysaccharide fragments27,28. Signals at 790 and 465 cm-1 are characteristic of Si–O–Si bending, indicative of silicate minerals intrinsic to the biochar matrix19,20. The heightened intensity of these bands after iron impregnation pointed to an increased abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups and a greater exposure of mineral phases, modifications that were expected to enhance the material’s affinity for NO3--N29. Therefore, the primary functional groups on the surface of FeB include -OH, C-N, -C = O, C = C, N–H, -C–C-, -C–O–C-, and -C≡O. The relative intensities of most absorption peaks were higher in FeB than in biochar, indicating a significant increase in the ability of functional groups on the surface of FeB to absorb infrared light30,31. According to the basic principles of Lambert–Beer’s law, these suggested that the content of each functional group on the surface of FeB had significantly increased after modified by loaded iron.

In addition to the bands shared with the parent biochar, FeB displayed several diagnostic absorptions at 3630, 1081, 694, 642 and 540 cm-1. The weak band at 3630 cm-1 is assigned to O–H stretching of carboxylic or phenolic groups, confirming the retention of oxygenated functionalities on the modified surface. The 1081 cm-1 band corresponds to Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching, indicative of residual silicate minerals32,33. Crucially, the bands at 694, 642 and 540 cm-1 arise from Fe–O vibrations characteristic of iron-oxide phases, unequivocally demonstrating the successful deposition of iron oxides onto the biochar matrix34. These results were consistent with the SEM analysis results.

XRD analysis

The crystal structure and phase composition of the materials were analyzed by XRD, as shown in Fig. 4. The pristine biochar exhibited a single broad diffraction hump centered at approximately 2θ = 24°, corresponding to the (002) plane of the amorphous carbon structure (turbostratic carbon), indicating its low crystallinity. In significant contrast, the XRD pattern of FeB showed a series of distinct and sharp diffraction peaks superimposed on the amorphous carbon background, indicating the successful formation of crystalline iron species35. FeB exhibited seven strong diffraction peaks between 18° and 65°. An extremely strong peak was detected at 2θ = 33.34°, indicating the presence of a large amount of Fe(III) oxidized crystals on the surface of FeB. Subsequent smaller peaks suggested the presence of a small amount of Fe–C, Fe–N, Fe, and FeO crystals on the surface of FeB.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum of the iron-modified biochar (FeB).

By comparing with the standard data card (JCPDS No.33–0664), it was found that the peak positions of various diffraction peaks coincide most closely with those of hematite (Fe2O3). This revealed that the main component of the iron-containing particles on the surface of FeB was Fe2O336. Liu et al.37 used Fe(NO3)3 for the modification of activated carbon and found through XRD analysis that there was a solid film structure of Fe2O3 on its surface. However, the intensity of its Fe2O3 diffraction peaks was significantly lower than the results of this study, suggesting that FeB had a higher crystalline structure and was loaded with more Fe2O3 on the surface. This implied that FeB had a stronger ability to adsorb pollutants and higher application value in water purification38. In addition, there were also many SiO2 diffraction peaks in the spectrum, which were because FeB contained a certain amount of silicon element.

XPS analysis

To further investigate the influence of FeCl3 modification on the surface element content and functional group types of FeB, XPS analyses were conducted on both biochar and FeB. After X-ray irradiation, the samples could absorb a certain amount of energy, and the electrons on certain orbitals of molecules or atoms on their surface would be in an excited state and leave the samples surface, causing the molecules or atoms on the sample surface to form ionic states by ionization. Therefore, the binding energy (EB) of electrons was an important characteristic parameter in XPS spectrum analysis.

The XPS characterization full spectra of biochar and FeB (Fig. 5a, b) showed that the FeB had significant peaks at EB of 285 eV, 530 eV, and 990 eV, corresponding to the characteristic peaks of C1s, O1s, and Na1s, respectively. This indicated that compared to biochar, the main element compositions on the surface of FeB were not significantly altered. Among all peaks, the intensities of C1s peak and O1s peak were highest, indicating that the main atomic composition on FeB surface was carbon atoms, while there were also a large number of oxygen atoms which can form various functional groups by bonding with carbon atoms. The Na1s peak of FeB was relatively weak, indicating the presence of trace amounts of the metal element sodium on its surface and interior. However, compared to biochar, more sodium was exposed to the surface of FeB39.

XPS characterizations of biochar (a) and Fe-modified biochar (FeB, b).

Surface element percentages (Table 3) showed that compared to biochar, the proportion of carbon atoms on FeB surface decreased (75.27%), while the proportion of oxygen atoms increased significantly (21.85%). These demonstrated that the C = C bonds in the surface structure of FeB were opened up, causing more oxygen atoms to be fixed on its surface in various forms40,41.

DFT calculations identified that functional groups like -OH, -COOH, and -C = O on the modified biochar could act as binding sites for NO3- in water bodies. Liu et al.49 showed that oxygen-containing functional groups, including -OH and -COOH, enhanced the adsorption of nitrogen oxides (NO) and facilitated the removal of anionic pollutants like NO3-. Particularly, metal-modified biochar, particularly iron-modified biochar, exhibited the enhanced adsorption properties due to the presence of metal sites created by the incorporation of iron. Huang et al.50 and Zhang et al.51 revealed that iron modification increased biochar’s adsorption capacity by introducing additional active sites, which interacted strongly with NO3- through electrostatic and coordination bonds. These underscored the significance of surface functionalization in improving pollutant removal efficiency.

In this study, the XPS analysis of FeB and biochar revealed that the modification opened up C = C bonds, allowing for more oxygen functional groups (-OH, -C = O, -C–O–C-, -C≡O) on the FeB surface. Moreover, the specific structures, including Fe2O3, lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancies, were found on its surface. These indicated that the active sites on the surface of FeB increased, which could enhance the NO3- adsorption capacity through electrostatic attraction and ligand exchange36,52.

Nitrogen adsorption performance of iron-modified biochar

The adsorption process of adsorbates on the surface of porous adsorbents primarily includes: (1) Diffusion of the adsorbate from the solution to the water film layer on the adsorbent surface; (2) Overcoming liquid film resistance to reach the adsorbent surface; (3) Diffusion of the adsorbate from the external particle surface to the internal particle surface. Throughout the adsorption process, Intragranular diffusion is the limiting step that restricts the adsorption rate. In the adsorption system with an initial NO3--N concentration of 20 mg/L, the NO3--N adsorption kinetics curves of biochar, FeB, and soil were shown in Fig. 7, with the corresponding kinetic model fitting parameters as shown in Table 4. As the adsorption time increased, the NO3--N adsorption capacity of biochar, FeB, and soil gradually increased. Within the initial 6 h, the adsorption capacity exhibited a rapid increase, while from 6 to 24 h, the rate of increase gradually slowed down until it approached equilibrium. The results indicated that during the first 6 h of adsorption, the main process involved the rapid diffusion of adsorbate to the adsorbent surface, driven by the maximum adsorption dynamic gradient formed by high concentration adsorbent at the solid–liquid interface. After 6 h, the primary location of adsorption gradually shifted from the external particle surface to the internal particle surface. As the adsorbates entered the internal pores of the adsorbent, its concentration gradually decreased, leading to a weakening of adsorption kinetics. This resulted in a gradual reduction in adsorption capacity, ultimately reaching adsorption equilibrium.

Comparatively, FeB exhibited the highest adsorption efficiency for NO3--N, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 4060.63 mg/kg, which was 3.9 times that of biochar. Both pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models fit the adsorption dynamic process of NO3--N on biochar well (R2 > 0.95), indicating that the NO3--N adsorption on biochar was accomplished through a combination of physical and chemical processes. However, when fitting the adsorption dynamic process of NO3--N on FeB, it was found that the pseudo-second-order kinetic equation provided the best fit among the three kinetic equations (R2 > 0.99), with the theoretical adsorption capacity closely matching the experimental values. Thus, the NO3--N adsorption process on FeB was more accurately described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model53,54, which indicated that chemical adsorption predominantly governed the adsorption process. These results were in agreement with the findings of Zhang et al. (2023). Consequently, it was evident that the chemical adsorption capacity of biochar was significantly enhanced after modification with FeCl3, leading to a notable improvement in NO3--N adsorption efficiency at equilibrium. In contrast, soil exhibited the weakest NO3--N adsorption capacity, with a maximum adsorption capacity of only 632.15 mg/kg. It was shown that the deep nitrogen removal from wastewater could not be achieved using traditional land treatment systems. All three kinetic models fit the adsorption dynamics well (R2 = 0.99), suggesting that NO3--N adsorption of soil was collectively accomplished through a series of reaction mechanisms, including physical diffusion, surface activation, and deactivation, with significant variations in activation energy.

To further investigate the adsorption potential of the materials, the initial NO3--N concentration was increased to 100 mg/L. Three kinetic models were used to fit the NO3--N adsorption kinetics of biochar, FeB, and soil (Fig. 8), respectively, and the corresponding kinetic model fitting parameters were shown in Table 5. The study found that the fitting results of the three kinetic models for the adsorption of NO3--N on biochar, FeB, and soil were consistent with the results obtained at an initial NO3--N concentration of 20 mg/L, indicating that the NO3--N concentration did not significantly affect the adsorption mechanism of the three materials. The NO3--N adsorption capacity of FeB increased with increasing NO3--N concentration, with a maximum NO3--N adsorption capacity of 4787.44 mg/kg, which was 2.4 times that of biochar. This suggested that FeB had a higher NO3--N adsorption potential. Several studies had suggested that in low-pH environments, the presence of a large amount of H+ could increase the number of positive charges on the surface of biochar, thereby enhancing its adsorption of anios55. Zhang et al.56 modified reed and bulrush biochar with loaded iron, achieving maximum NO3--N adsorption capacities of 2.70 and 5.80 mg/g at a material dosage of 2.00 g/L and pH 3.0, respectively. Zhi et al.57 modified peanut shell biochar with Fe3+ and achieved a maximum NO3--N adsorption capacity of 6.30 mg/g at pH 2.0. However, a low-pH environment was highly unfavorable for denitrification. In this study, favorable NO3--N adsorption results were achieved at a neutral pH of 7.0 and with a FeB dosage of only 1.00 g/L, indicating that FeB had high practical value in the treatment of wastewater dominated by NO3--N.

Previous studies have demonstrated that Fe-modified biochars can achieve high adsorption capacities, but typically under acidic conditions (pH 2.0–4.0) and at higher dosages. For instance, Mood et al.58 reported that an iron–nitrogen co-modified biochar achieved a maximum NO3⁻-N adsorption of 20.67 mg/g at pH 3, attributing the improvement to the synergy between Fe2O3 loading and nitrogen-containing functional groups. Similarly, Zhang et al.36 found that acid-treated Fe-based biochar significantly enhanced surface area and electrostatic interactions, with a maximum NO3⁻-N uptake of 9.35 mg/g, due to enriched oxygen-containing groups and a more positive surface potential. Li et al. (2019) further confirmed that FeCl3 modification increased the surface zeta potential and nitrate adsorption to 32.33 mg/g, with minimal influence from pH or temperature, making it more versatile. Kuang et al.59 also reported that FeCl3-modified reed biochar achieved a theoretical NO3⁻-N adsorption capacity of 272.02 mg/g, emphasizing the synergistic role of Fe2+/Fe3+ ions with hydroxyl and amine groups on the biochar surface. In comparison to recent composite systems, the FeB material developed in this study demonstrates unique functionalization and performance advantages. For instance, Bukhari et al.60 synthesized a hybrid material by modifying Shorea faguetiana-derived biochar with Fe2O3 nanoparticles and MXene nanosheets, achieving maximum removal efficiencies of 99.92% for methylene blue (MB) and 93.67% for Pb2+ under acidic conditions (pH 2.0–4.5) and relatively high dosages (2 g/L) within 90 min. The excellent removal performance was attributed to the synergistic effect of Fe2O3 surface reactivity and MXene’s high conductivity, which promoted rapid charge transfer and strong π–π interactions.

In contrast to these studies, which required low pH or high material dosages, the FeB composite developed in this study demonstrated robust nitrate adsorption under neutral pH (7.0) with a low dosage of only 1.00 g/L, achieving a capacity of approximately 4.79 mg/g (4787.44 mg/kg). This finding aligns with the work of Han et al.61, who reported that agro-waste-derived Fe-biochar could remove 85–91% of NO3⁻-N at neutral pH and 1.0 g/L dosage, achieving a Langmuir maximum capacity of 1.96 mg/g. These results highlight FeB’s superior performance in environmentally relevant conditions, offering high adsorption efficiency, operational simplicity, and better compatibility with biological denitrification processes, thus enhancing its practical value for real-world wastewater treatment applications.

As shown in Fig. 9 and Table 6, both Langmuir and Freundlich models describe the NO3⁻–N adsorption behavior of biochar and FeB well, but FeB consistently exhibits higher fitting coefficients and adsorption capacities, indicating a fundamentally enhanced adsorption mechanism after iron modification. The Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity (qm) of FeB reached 6829.7 mg kg-1, which is more than three times that of pristine biochar (2117.2 mg kg-1), demonstrating a substantial increase in effective adsorption sites and surface affinity. Horizontally, this performance is comparable to or exceeds many reported Fe-modified biochars that often require acidic conditions or higher dosages, such as Fe/N co-modified biochar achieving 20.67 mg g-1 at pH 358 and FeCl3-modified reed biochar reported by Kuang et al.59 Vertically, the stronger Langmuir fitting for FeB (R2 = 0.973) combined with pseudo-second-order kinetic behavior indicates that nitrate adsorption is dominated by monolayer chemisorption rather than simple physical diffusion, which is consistent with Fe-induced surface functionalization, increased positive surface potential, and inner-sphere complexation mechanisms reported in previous studies36,62. Importantly, achieving high adsorption capacity under neutral pH and low dosage conditions highlights the practical advantage of FeB for real wastewater treatment and supports its applicability as an efficient adsorption material or functional wetland substrate.

Adsorption mechanisms

Morphological changes and stability analysis

To elucidate the nitrate adsorption mechanism of FeB and evaluate its structural stability after adsorption, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on both biochar and FeB after nitrate adsorption (Fig. 10). Compared with the samples before adsorption (Fig. 1), the surfaces of both nitrate-loaded biochar and FeB exhibited noticeable changes, with the alterations being more pronounced for FeB. A relatively greater amount of nitrate ions successfully entered and occupied the internal pore channels of FeB, leading to a smoother surface with less distinct pore boundaries, and some pores appeared to be filled or covered by nitrate. Furthermore, it was observed that more nitrate accumulated around the Fe2O3 particles loaded on the FeB surface. These results indicate that the unique pore structure of FeB and the presence of Fe2O3 particles synergistically enhanced the nitrate adsorption process, providing visual evidence for the pore-filling mechanism in nitrate adsorption by FeB. Notably, despite the significant changes in surface morphology, the carbon skeleton of FeB remained intact after nitrate adsorption, and the loaded Fe2O3 particles remained firmly attached without obvious dissolution or detachment. This demonstrates that FeB possesses good reusability and ecological safety for practical water remediation applications.

Surface morphological characteristics of biochar (a, c) and iron-modified biochar (FeB; b, d) after nitrate adsorption as assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

However, physical pore filling alone cannot fully account for the high adsorption capacity and the chemisorption nature indicated by the pseudo-second-order kinetics (R2 > 0.99). Combined with the characterization results in "Chemical characteristics of iron-modified biochar" Section, the abundant hydroxyl groups (-OH) and oxygen vacancies identified by FTIR and XPS (Figs. 3, 6) provide critical active sites for ligand exchange. According to previous studies36,52, nitrate ions act as Lewis bases and can displace surface hydroxyls or coordinate with Fe centers to form stable inner-sphere surface complexes (Fe–O-NO2). This ligand exchange mechanism works synergistically with the observed physical entrapment to ensure the efficient and stable removal of nitrate.

Adsorption mechanisms

Integrated characterization, adsorption kinetics, and thermodynamic analyses collectively revealed that the enhanced nitrate adsorption by FeB was synergistically governed by ligand exchange, electrostatic interaction, and physical diffusion (Fig. 11).

Mechanism of enhanced nitrate adsorption by iron-modified biochar (FeB). This schematic diagram was created by the authors using Microsoft PowerPoint (Microsoft 365, https://www.microsoft.com/microsoft-365/powerpoint).

First, chemical interactions played a pivotal role in the enhanced adsorption process. The FeB surface was abundant in Fe2O3 particles, which undergo hydrolysis in aqueous environments to generate a substantial number of iron-hydroxyl (Fe-OH) groups. As Lewis bases, nitrate anions could displace the -OH groups from Fe-OH and coordinate with iron centers via oxygen atoms, forming inner-sphere surface complexes (monodentate Fe-ONO2 and/or bidentate Fe-O2NO). These complexes represented the primary speciation through which nitrate was firmly immobilized on the FeB surface. Second, the highly positive surface charge of FeB (zeta potential = + 7.58 mV) exerted strong electrostatic attraction toward nitrate anions in the aqueous phase. The adsorbed nitrate on the positively charged Fe2O3 particles formed outer-sphere surface complexes, albeit with relatively weaker binding affinity. Furthermore, the increased proportion of mesopores and macropores on the FeB surface facilitateed the rapid diffusion of more nitrate ions into the internal pore channels, thereby enhancing the accessibility of nitrate to the active binding sites.

Practical applicability in real wastewater

Recent studies have demonstrated that FeB exhibits significant efficacy in enhancing nitrogen and phosphorus removal, particularly in real wastewater environments where competing anions are present. For instance, Zhang et al.63 reported that FeB substantially improved PO43- removal from industrial wastewater containing coexisting anions such as SO42-, Cl-, and HCO3-. Similarly, Li et al.11 found that, despite competition from coexisting anions (SO42-, Cl-, and organic acid anions), FeB outperformed conventional biochar and unmodified carbonaceous adsorbents in adsorbing PO43- via ligand exchange and inner-sphere complexation with Fe–O functional groups. Furthermore, Xin et al. (2024) demonstrated that Fe(II)/Fe(III)-modified biochar maintained high NO3- adsorption capacity in wastewater systems containing PO43-, SO42-, Cl-, and HCO3-/CO32-. These findings suggested that the FeB developed in this study possessed robust interfacial chemical properties, enabling effective treatment of real wastewater and, in particular, significantly enhancing its application potential in constructed wetlands. Future work will focus on validating the long-term performance and stability of FeB in pilot-scale constructed wetland systems.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed an engineered FeB via a sequential acid-washing and FeCl3-impregnation strategy. The modification significantly optimized the pore structure and enriched the surface with oxygen-containing functional groups and active α-Fe2O3 particles. FeB exhibited a reversed positive zeta potential (+ 7.58 mV), facilitating the capture of anionic nitrate. Adsorption experiments revealed that FeB achieved a maximum theoretical adsorption capacity of 6829.74 mg/kg based on the Langmuir model, which is over three times that of pristine biochar. The adsorption kinetics followed a pseudo-second-order model (R2 > 0.99), indicating a chemisorption-dominated process.Mechanistic analysis confirmed that nitrate removal was driven by the synergistic effects of electrostatic attraction, ligand exchange (forming inner-sphere complexes), and physical pore filling. Importantly, FeB demonstrated excellent removal efficiency under neutral pH (7.0) with a low dosage (1.0 g/L) and maintained robust structural stability after adsorption. These findings establish FeB as a highly efficient, stable, and practical adsorbent for nitrate mitigation in wastewater treatment systems and constructed wetlands.
