Changes of hip fracture in older patients before and after the COVID-19 pandemic: a retrospective multicentre study in Japan
Authors: Shinya Tanaka, Yusuke Osawa, Yasuhiko Takegami, Nobuyuki Okui, Ken-ichi Yamauchi, Yoshiki Aoki, Shiro Imagama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on healthcare systems and the general population. However, it remains unclear to what extent these major societal changes have had an impact on the management of hip fractures in older people in Japan. Therefore, we investigated the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the number of patients with hip fractures, their characteristics, and their perioperative management as a retrospective multicentre study.MethodsWe included 1894 patients aged ≥ 65 years who underwent surgery for hip fracture at three hospitals between January 2019–December 2021. Patients were classified according to the time of injury; patients treated between January–December 2019, January–December 2020, and January–December 2021 were divided into the pre-COVID-19 group, early COVID-19 group, and late COVID-19 group, respectively. We compared age, sex, body mass index, preadmission residence, surgical procedure, length of hospital stay, waiting time for surgery, in-hospital complications, and in-hospital death.ResultsOur findings suggested that the early COVID-19 and late COVID-19 groups showed a 6.8% and 7.5% reduction in the number of HF patients, respectively, compared to the pre-COVID-19 group. Waiting days for surgery, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality or complication rates did not significantly change before and after the pandemic. However, infection was increased in the early COVID-19 group regarding the subgroup of complications.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic altered the characteristics of hip fractures in older individuals.

Introduction

The first case of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection was reported in Wuhan, China, in late 2019, and subsequently led to a global pandemic. The highly contagious nature of COVID-19 has resulted in significant lifestyle changes among the general population. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, studies have documented decreased levels of physical and social activity, deterioration in dietary habits, and increased incidence of depression in older individuals [1–5].

The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on health systems. Particularly in the early stages of the outbreak, healthcare facilities were overwhelmed and struggled to provide effective medical care to patients [6]. As the number of infected people increased, COVID-19-specific wards were established, leading to a shortage of health workers owing to staff redeployment and depression [7, 8]. In addition, the depletion of medical resources became a significant problem, leading to restrictions on elective surgery and reductions in outpatient services [9].

Hip fracture (HF) remains a major traumatic event in the older individuals and is associated with reduced life expectancy. Early surgical intervention in HF patients has been shown to have the following benefits: improved likelihood of return to independent living, reduced risk of pressure ulcer development, shorter hospital stay and improved patient survival [10, 11]. Rehabilitation should begin as soon as possible after HF surgery to reduce functional decline and complications. However, the changes in lifestyle and healthcare systems due to the COVID-19 pandemic may have also affected the management of hip fracture patients, raising concerns that conventional treatment approaches may have been disrupted.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on HF patients in Japan. Specifically, we aimed to investigate (1) the number of HF patients, (2) characteristics of HF patients, and (3) perioperative survival, complication rates, and management of HF patients.

Patients and methods

Study setting and design

The study was approved by the institutional review board, and it conforms to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. This is a retrospective multicentre study conducted at three hospitals in the Tokai region of Japan. We included 1894 patients with HF, aged ≥ 65 years, who underwent surgery at our hospital between January 2019 and December 2021. HF included femoral neck and trochanteric fractures. The three hospitals have a common treatment strategy for HF: both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, surgery has been performed as early as possible in compliance with the Japanese guidelines for femoral neck/trochanter fractures.

Patients were divided into the following three groups according to the time of injury; pre-COVID-19 (between January and December 2019), early COVID-19 (between January and December 2020), and late COVID-19 (between January and December 2021). Considering the seasonal variation in the incidence of hip fractures, the periods for each of the three groups were aligned with the January to December period [12]. According to the reports by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW, https://covid19.mhlw.go.jp, cited January 1, 2024) and other previous reports, the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth waves of infection were observed between March–May 2020, July–August 2020, November 2020–January 2021, April–May 2021, and July–September 2021, respectively. Therefore, early COVID-19 group corresponds to the time from the first to the first half of the third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas late COVID-19 group corresponds to the time from the second half of the third wave to the fifth wave. The COVID-19 vaccination programme in Japan was rolled out sequentially from February 2021, corresponding to the late COVID-19 phase. COVID-19 admission screening was conducted at the three hospitals in May 2020, December 2020, and February 2021. The screening identified three positive cases. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many health facilities closed wards owing to cluster outbreaks; however, no wards were closed in our hospitals during this period.

Characteristics and outcomes of HF

We compared the number of HF cases, age, sex, body mass index (BMI), pre-admission residence, length of hospitalisation, surgical procedure, length of wait for surgery, in-hospital complications, and in-hospital death by groups. The pre-admission residence was classified as either living at home or living in a nursing home or hospital. Surgical procedures were divided into osteosynthesis and arthroplasty. Complications were classified by the organ involved, based on previous reports. Additionally, DVT/PE and infection were included as separate classifications. Infection was defined as infections excluding COVID-19, such as aspiration pneumonia and urinary tract infections. If a patient had more than one complication during the hospitalisation period, they were counted separately according to this classification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed for each factor using one-way ANOVA and the chi-square test for continuous and categorical variables with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test, respectively. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.52 (Saitama Medical Centre, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).

Results

The pre-COVID-19, early COVID-19, and late COVID-19 groups included 663, 618, and 613 cases, respectively, suggesting that the early COVID-19 and late COVID-19 groups showed a 6.8% and 7.5% reduction in the number of HF patients, respectively, compared to the pre-COVID-19 group (Fig. 1). No statistically significant differences were found in the number of HF patients per month (55.3 ± 7.6 in the pre-COVID-19 group, 51.5 ± 10.2 in the early COVID-19 group, and 51.1 ± 10.3 in the late COVID-19 group; p = 0.498). The mean ages were 84.2 ± 7.6, 84.9 ± 7.3, and 85.0 ± 7.7 years in the pre-COVID-19, early COVID-19, and late COVID-19 groups, respectively. Patient age tended to be higher in the early and late COVID-19 groups than in the pre-COVID-19 group, but there were no significant differences (p = 0.082). The preadmission residence status in the pre-COVID-19 group included 470 patients (70.9%) at home and 166 patients (25.0%) in nursing homes or hospitals. In the early COVID-19 group, 407 patients (65.9%) were at home and 190 patients (30.7%) were in nursing homes or hospitals. In the late COVID-19 group, 419 patients (68.4%) were at home and 177 patients (28.9%) were in nursing homes or hospitals. Compared to the pre-COVID-19 group, there was a trend towards more injuries in nursing homes or hospitals in the early and late COVID-19 groups, but no significant difference was observed (p = 0.081). The length of hospital stay was 26.6 ± 13.6, 25.0 ± 12.0, and 25.6 ± 12.9 days in pre-COVID-19, early COVID-19, and late COVID-19 groups, respectively (p = 0.152). The waiting time for surgery was 2.0 ± 3.1, 1.8 ± 2.9, and 1.8 ± 2.7 days in pre-COVID-19, early COVID-19, and late COVID-19 groups, with no significant differences between the groups (p = 0.372). In-hospital complications occurred in 93 patients (14.0%) in pre-COVID-19 group, 90 (14.6%) in early COVID-19 group, and 69 (11.3%) in late COVID-19 group. However, there was a significant increase in infection in the early COVID-19 group compared to the late COVID-19 group (pre-COVID-19 group: 48 patients (7.2%), early COVID-19 group: 57 patients (9.2%), late COVID-19 group: 33 patients (5.4%), p = 0.035; Table 2). Discussion

We investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the number of HF patients, patient characteristics, and perioperative management. The incidence of HF decreased after the COVID-19 pandemic. Length of hospital stay and waiting time for surgery remained unchanged after the pandemic. Complications of infection increased in the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic but did not increase in the late stages.

We lack consensus on how the COVID-19 pandemic affects the incidence of HF [13–28] (Table 4). O'Driscoll et al. reported an increase in the incidence of HF after the COVID-19 pandemic, which they attributed to a decline in activities of daily living (ADL) associated with prolonged refraining from going out [29]. However, the incidence of HF reportedly decreased after the COVID-19 pandemic. Notably, Mataloni et al. reported a reduced probability of falls with the reduced frequency of outings among older people [30]. In contrast, Okike et al. attributed this decrease to the influence of family members living together longer on account of telecommuting [31]. In Japan, Minato et al. reported a decrease in the incidence of fragility fractures among the elderly during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic [32]. Similarly, in our study, we observed a reduction in the number of HF patients during the first to fifth waves. It may be suggested that the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated self-isolation in Japan persisted long into the late phase of the pandemic. Home quarantine during the COVID-19 pandemic may affect trauma in older and younger individuals and may impact the characteristics of HF patients. Notably, Lim et al. reported that the impact of home quarantining reduced sports- and road-traffic-related injuries and increased the incidence of injuries at home [33]. Concordantly, Bhat et al. [34] and Louise et al. [22] reported increased injuries at homes and nursing homes, respectively. Slullitel et al. also reported an increase in the number of patients with high comorbidity and low ADL after the COVID-19 pandemic [35]. In Japan, Bekki et al. reported a decrease in the number of trauma patients injured outdoors compared to those in the previous year, after the COVID-19 pandemic [36]. Our study found a trend indicating a decrease in injuries among patients living at home after the COVID-19 pandemic. Refraining from going outside may have reduced the number of home residents going outside, which may have reduced the number of outdoor falls among the elderly.

The effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on perioperative mortality and complications remains controversial. Boukebous et al. reported increased mortality in HF patients after the COVID-19 pandemic [14], which was attributed to the increased frailty among patients owing to staying indoors, the shortage of geriatricians, and the increase in waiting times for surgery. Golinelli et al. reported that delaying surgery after injury was associated with increased mortality [23]. Jawad and Zhao et al. also reported delays in the timing of medical attention and increased perioperative complications such as DVT [37, 38]. In contrast, reduced mortality has also been reported in HF patients following the COVID-19 pandemic. Lachnish et al. attributed the reduced mortality to early surgical interventions and adequate rehabilitation during the COVID-19 pandemic, as was done before the pandemic [39], whereas Cintean et al. attributed it to reduced waiting time for HF surgery owing to factors such as cancellation of planned surgeries [40]. Kanazawa et al. reported that during the COVID-19 pandemic, inpatient rehabilitation hours increased due to a decrease in outpatient rehabilitation [41]. Our study found no increase in in-hospital mortality or complication rates after the COVID-19 pandemic. This may be attributed to the lack of changes in the waiting time for surgery before and after the COVID-19 pandemic at our hospital. The absence of an increase in waiting time for surgery indicates that emergency surgeries can still be performed within the same timeline as before the pandemic, although postponement of scheduled surgeries has been considered.

However, regarding the subgroup of complications, there was an increase in infection complications during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. Limited data are available on trends in hospital-acquired infectious complications in the post-COVID-19 pandemic. While improved infection control measures, such as increased use of masks and hand hygiene practices by healthcare workers, may have contributed to a reduction in certain hospital-acquired infections, some studies have paradoxically reported an increase in antibiotic use since the COVID-19 pandemic [42–46]. This apparent paradox may be partly explained by the high rate of false-negative COVID-19 test results early in the pandemic, which may have led to misdiagnosis and inappropriate management of some cases as bacterial infections [47, 48]. In addition, increased clinical attention to fever may have led to the detection and treatment of infections that may have previously gone unnoticed. Consequently, the observed decrease in cases of fever of unknown origin may reflect this increased diagnostic scrutiny rather than a true change in the underlying characteristics of HF patients. However, the reasons for the reported increase in infectious complications in the early postoperative period of HF management remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic varies by region and country. The COVID-19 pandemic has led to a significant impact on people's activities due to lockdown and self-isolation. However, there are various reports on the number of HF patients and perioperative complication and mortality rates, which could vary depending on several factors, including the time of observation, government policies on COVID-19, differences between rural and urban areas, local health systems, and the timing of widespread vaccination. Second, in-hospital mortality was assessed over a short period during the hospitalisation. As the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality is currently controversial, this factor should be assessed over longer periods to draw conclusive results. However, there have been few multicentre studies in Japan investigating the number of HF patients, patient characteristics, and changes in the post-hospitalisation course after the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, our study may be useful for understanding HF during the COVID-19 pandemic.

In conclusion, the number of HF patients decreased after the COVID-19 pandemic. Number of days waiting for surgery, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality or complication rates did not change significantly before and after the pandemic. Patients tended to be older, institutionalized, and hospitalized.
