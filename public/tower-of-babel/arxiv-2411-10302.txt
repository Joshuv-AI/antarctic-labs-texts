Selected topics from the theory of intersections of balls
Károly Bezdek, Zsolt Lángi, and Márton Naszódi

Abstract
. In this survey, we discuss volumetric and combinatorial results concerning (mostly finite) intersections or unions of balls (mostly of equal radii) in the d d -dimensional real vector space, mostly equipped with the Euclidean norm. Our first topic is the Kneser–Poulsen Conjecture , according to which if a finite number of balls are rearranged so that the pairwise distances of the centers increase, then the volume of the union (resp., intersection) increases (resp., decreases). Next, we discuss Blaschke–Santaló-type inequalities, and reverse isoperimetric inequalities for convex sets in Euclidean d d -space obtained as intersections of (possibly infinitely many) balls of radius r r , which we call r r -ball bodies . We present some results on 1 1 -ball bodies (also called ball-bodies or spindle convex sets ) in the plane, with special attention paid to their approximation by the spindle convex hull of a finite subset. A ball-polyhedron is a ball-body obtained as the intersection of finitely many unit balls in Euclidean d d -space. We consider the combinatorial structure of their faces, and volumetric properties of ball-polyhedra obtained from choosing the centers of the balls randomly.

Introduction

Most of our discussion takes place in Euclidean
d
-space,
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Intersections of balls appear in the study of diameter graphs (Borsuk-type problems), sets of constant width or convex bodies with a
C^{2+}
boundary, and thus, they are studied
in combinatorial geometry, differential geometry and classical convexity.

The goal of this survey is to collect recent advances in the topic not covered in earlier works cataloging progress, most notably in

[

,
75
,
18
,
19
,
15
,
78
]
. For further reading on the topics covered in this survey, we recommend
[

]
for interested readers.

Our first topic (Section
2
) is the
Kneser–Poulsen Conjecture
, according to which if a finite number of unit balls are rearranged so that the pairwise distances of the centers increase, then the volume of the union (resp., intersection) increases (resp., decreases).

Section

d
-space obtained as intersections of (possibly infinitely many) unit balls, which we call

.
In particular, we discuss Blaschke–Santaló type, and isoperimetric inequalities.

Section

covers spindle convex sets in the plane, with special attention paid to their approximation by the spindle convex hull of a finite subset.

A

is a convex body obtained as the intersection of finitely many unit balls in Euclidean
d
-space,
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}

1.1.
Notation

Let
\|\cdot\|
denote the standard Euclidean norm of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{d}
. So, if
{\bf p}_{i},{\bf p}_{j}
are two points in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
, then
\|{\bf p}_{i}-{\bf p}_{j}\|
denotes the Euclidean distance between them. It is convenient to denote a (finite) point configuration consisting of points
{\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,{\bf p}_{N}
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
by
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
. Now, if
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
and
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
are two configurations of
N
points in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
such that for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
the inequality
\|{\bf q}_{i}-{\bf q}_{j}\|\leq\|{\bf p}_{i}-{\bf p}_{j}\|
holds, then we say that
{\bf q}
is a
contraction
of
{\bf p}
.

Let
{\rm V}_{d}\left(\cdot\right)
denote the
d
-dimensional volume (Lebesgue measure) in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Let
\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p},r]
denote the (closed)
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball centered at a point
{\bf p}
with radius
r\geq 0
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
. In particular, let
\omega_{d}:={\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p},1]\right)=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})}
. For simplicity, we set
{\rm V}_{d}\left(\emptyset\right)=0
.

2.
From the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture to intersections of balls

2.1.
The Kneser–Poulsen conjecture

In 1954, E. T. Poulsen
[
90
]
and in 1955, M. Kneser
[
72
]
independently conjectured the following inequality for the case when
r_{1}=\dots=r_{N}
. Hence, following
[
70
]
, we attribute the following conjecture to Kneser and Poulsen.

Conjecture 2.1
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is a contraction of
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},

\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
, then

(1)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)\geq{\rm V}_{d}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf q}_{i},r_{i}]\right)

holds for all
r_{1}>0,r_{2}>0,\dots,r_{N}>0
.

It is customary to attribute also the following closely related conjecture to Kneser and Poulsen.

Conjecture 2.2
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},

\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is a contraction of
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},

\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
, then

(2)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf q}_{i},r_{i}]\right).

holds for all
r_{1}>0,r_{2}>0,\dots,r_{N}>0
.

We note the following in connection with (
2
). First, in 1979, Klee
[
71
]
asked whether (
2
) holds for
d=2
. Then in 1987, Gromov
[
65
]
published a proof of (
2
) for all
N\leq d+1
and
d>1
, and conjectured that his result extends to spherical
d
-space
\mathbb{S}^{d}
(resp., hyperbolic
d
-space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
) for all
d>1
. Finally, in 1991, Klee and Wagon
[
70
]
asked whether (
2
) holds for all
d>1
. In any case, following the tradition, we call Conjecture
2.1
(resp., Conjecture
2.2
) the
Kneser–Poulsen conjecture
for unions of balls (resp., intersections of balls).

2.2.
The Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for continuous contractions

If
{\bf q}
is a contraction of
{\bf p}
, then there may or may not be a continuous motion
{\bf p}(t)=({\bf p}_{1}(t),{\bf p}_{2}(t),\dots,{\bf p}_{N}(t))
, with
{\bf p}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{E}^{d}
for all
0\leq t\leq 1
and
1\leq i\leq N
such that
{\bf p}(0)={\bf p}
and
{\bf p}(1)={\bf q}
, and
\|{\bf p}_{i}(t)-{\bf p}_{j}(t)\|
is monotone decreasing for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
. When there is such a motion, we say that
{\bf q}
is a
continuous contraction
of
{\bf p}
. For example, a contraction that fixes the vertices of a
d
-dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
and maps an exterior point lying sufficiently far from the vertices onto an interior point of the simplex cannot be represented as a continuous contraction in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
. By representing the union (resp., intersection) of balls as a tiling of nearest (resp., farthest) point truncated Voronoi cells, Csikós
[
37
]
proved a volume formula for unions (resp., intersections) of balls, from which the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture follows for continuous contractions in a straightforward way.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is a continuous contraction of
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},

\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d>1
, then (
1
) and (
2
) hold for all
r_{1}>0,r_{2}>0,\dots,r_{N}>0
.

We note that the planar case of Theorem
2.3
has been proved independently in
[
27
]
,
[
36
]
,
[
32
]
, and
[
10
]
. Moreover, Csikós
[
39
]
managed to generalize his volume formula to configurations of balls called
flowers
which are sets obtained from balls with the help of operations
\cap
and
\cup
. This work extends to hyperbolic as well as spherical space. In particular, the union (resp., intersection) Kneser–Poulsen conjecture is true for continuous contractions in hyperbolic and spherical spaces. However, one cannot expect the Kneser–Poulsen conjectures to be true in spaces of non-constant curvature as shown in
[
38
]
. On the other hand, Csikós
[
40
]
has succeeded in proving a Schläfli-type formula for polytopes with curved faces lying in pseudo-Riemannian Einstein manifolds, which can be used to provide another proof of Theorem
2.3
(for more details see
[
40
]
).

2.3.
The Kneser–Poulsen conjecture in the plane

In
[
12
]
, the first named author and Connelly proved Conjecture
2.1
as well as Conjecture
2.2
in the Euclidean plane. Thus, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is a contraction of
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},

\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{2}
, then

{\rm V}_{2}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{2}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)\geq{\rm V}_{2}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{2}[{\bf q}_{i},r_{i}]\right)

and

{\rm V}_{2}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{2}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)\leq{\rm V}_{2}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{2}[{\bf q}_{i},r_{i}]\right)

hold for all
r_{1}>0,r_{2}>0,\dots,r_{N}>0
.

In fact, the paper
[
12
]
contains a proof of an extension of the above theorem to flowers as well. In what follows we give an outline of the three-step proof published in
[
12
]
by phrasing it through a sequence of theorems each being higher-dimensional. Voronoi cells play an essential role in the proofs of Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Consider
N
moving closed
d
-dimensional balls
\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i}(t),r_{i}]
with
1\leq i\leq N,0\leq t\leq 1
in
\mathbb{E}^{d},d\geq 2
. If
F_{i}(t)
is the contribution of the
i
th ball to the boundary of the union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i}(t),r_{i}]
(resp., of the intersection
\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i}(t),r_{i}]
), then

\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{r_{i}}\ {\rm svol}_{d-1}\left(F_{i}(t)\right)

decreases (resp., increases) in t under any analytic contraction
{\bf p}(t)
of the center points, where
0\leq t\leq 1
and
{\rm svol}_{d-1}(\cdot)
refers to the relevant
(d-1)
-dimensional surface volume.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let the centers of the closed
d
-dimensional balls
\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]
,
1\leq i\leq N
lie in a
(d-2)
-dimensional affine subspace
L
of
\mathbb{E}^{d},d\geq 3
. If
F_{i}
stands for the contribution of the
i
th ball to the boundary of the union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]
(resp., of the intersection
\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]
), then

{\rm V}_{d-2}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d-2}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{r_{i}}\>{\rm svol}_{d-1}(F_{i})

\left(resp.,\ {\rm V}_{d-2}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d-2}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{r_{i}}\>{\rm svol}_{d-1}(F_{i})\right),

where
\mathbf{B}^{d-2}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]=\mathbf{B}^{d}[{\bf p}_{i},r_{i}]\cap L,1\leq i\leq N
.

Theorem 2.7
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is a contraction of
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,

{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{d},d\geq 1
, then there is an analytic contraction
\mathbf{p}(t)=(\mathbf{p}_{1}(t),\dots,

\mathbf{p}_{N}(t)),0\leq t\leq 1
in
\mathbb{E}^{2d}
such that
\mathbf{p}(0)=\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}(1)=\mathbf{q}
.

As for
d=2
one has
d+2=2d
therefore clearly, Theorems
2.5
,
2.6
, and
2.7
imply Theorem
2.4
. Next, we mention two corollaries obtained from the above outlined proof and published in
[
12
]
. (For more details see
[
12
]
.)

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
and
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
be two point configurations in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
such that
{\bf q}
is a contraction of
{\bf p}
via the piecewise-analytic contraction
\mathbf{p}(t)=(\mathbf{p}_{1}(t),\dots,

\mathbf{p}_{N}(t)),0\leq t\leq 1
in
\mathbb{E}^{d+2}
such that
\mathbf{p}(0)=\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}(1)=\mathbf{q}
. Then the conclusions of Conjecture
2.1
as well as Conjecture
2.2
hold in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
.

The following generalizes a result of Gromov in
[
65
]
, who proved it in the case
N\leq d+1
.

Corollary 2.9
.

If
{\bf q}=({\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N})
is an arbitrary contraction of

{\bf p}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\dots,{\bf p}_{N})
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
and
N\leq d+3
, then both Conjecture
2.1
and Conjecture
2.2
hold.

Applying results on central sets of unions of finitely many balls, Gorbovickis (
[
64
]
) has proved the following analogues of the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(resp.,
\mathbb{H}^{2}
).

Theorem 2.10
.

If the union of a finite set of closed disks in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(resp.,
\mathbb{H}^{2}
) has a simply connected interior, then the area of the union of these disks cannot increase after any contractive rearrangement.

The following statement published by Gorbovickis in
[
64
]
, is a straightforward corollary of Theorem
2.10
.

Corollary 2.11
.

(i) If the intersection of a finite set of closed disks in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is connected, then the area of the intersection of these disks cannot decrease after any contractive rearrangement.

(ii) The area of the intersection of finitely many closed disks having radii at most
\frac{\pi}{2}
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
cannot decrease after any contractive rearrangement.

It is surprising that part (ii) of Corollary
2.11
can be extended to
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
d>2
for a specific radius of balls (i.e., spherical caps). Namely, the first named author and Connelly

[
13
]
proved the following analogue of the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.12
.

If a finite set of closed spherical caps of (angular) radius
\frac{\pi}{2}
(i.e., of
closed hemispheres) in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
d>1
is rearranged
so that the (spherical) distance between each pair of centers does not increase, then
the (spherical) d-dimensional volume of the intersection does not decrease and the
(spherical) d-dimensional volume of the union does not increase.

Finally, we mention the following recent result of Csikós and Horváth (
[
35
]
) that extends part (ii) of Corollary
2.11
to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by applying the method of Gorbovickis (
[
64
]
) to co-central sets, which one can regard as natural duals of central sets. For properties of central (resp., co-central) sets of compact sets, which might be of independent interest, see the relevant sections of
[
64
]
(resp., Csikós and Horváth
[
35
]
).

Theorem 2.13
.

The area of the intersection of finitely many closed disks in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
cannot decrease after any contractive rearrangement.

2.4.
Kneser–Poulsen-type results in Minkowski spaces

Let
\mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body, i.e., a compact convex set with nonempty interior symmetric about the origin
\mathbf{o}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{K}}
denote the norm generated by
\mathbf{K}
, which is defined by
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{K}}:=\min\{\lambda\geq 0\ |\ \mathbf{x}\in\lambda\mathbf{K}\}
for
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Furthermore, let us denote
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{K}}
by
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
and call it the
Minkowski space of dimension
d
generated by

\mathbf{K}
. We write
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x},r]:=\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{K}
for
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r>0
and call any such set a (closed)
ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
, where
+
refers to vector addition extended to subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in the usual way (called the Minkowski sum). Next, recall the following definitions from
[
21
]
.

Definition 2.14
.

For
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r>0
let

X_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}:=\bigcup\{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x},r]\ |\ \mathbf{x}\in X\}\ ({\rm resp.,}\ X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}:=\bigcap\{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x},r]\ |\ \mathbf{x}\in X\})

denote the
r
-ball neighbourhood of
X
(resp.,
r
-ball body generated by
X
) in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
. If
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a finite set, then we call

X_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}
(resp.,
X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}
) the
r
-ball molecule (resp.,
r
-ball polyhedron) generated by
X
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
.
We simplify our notations when
\mathbf{K}
is a Euclidean ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as follows: for a set
X\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
and
r>0
, let

X_{r}:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]\ \text{(resp.,}\ X^{r}:=\bigcap_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]).

Following
[
21
]
, we say that a (labeled) point set
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a
uniform contraction
of a (labeled) point set
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
separating value

\lambda>0
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
if

\|\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{j}\|_{\mathbf{K}}\leq\lambda\leq\|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{\mathbf{K}}\ {\rm holds}\ {\rm for}\ {\rm all}\ 1\leq i<j\leq N.

Finally, the (labeled) point set
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a
uniform contraction
of the (labeled) point set
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
if there exists a
\lambda>0
such that
Q
is a uniform contraction of
P
with separating value
\lambda
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
. The following theorem of
[
21
]
is an analogue of the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for unions of balls under uniform contractions in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
. (For an earlier and weaker Euclidean version of this theorem we refer the interested reader to
[
17
]
.)

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be an
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
If
r>0,d>1
,
N\geq 2^{d}
, and
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a uniform contraction of
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
, then

(3)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(Q_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}\right).

As the proof of Theorem
2.15
in
[
21
]
is a short one and might be of independent interest, we recall it here. The details are as follows. Let
\lambda>0
such that
Q
is a uniform contraction of
P
with separating value
\lambda
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
.
As (
3
) holds trivially for
0<r\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}
therefore we may assume that
0<\frac{\lambda}{2}<r
. Recall that for a bounded set
\emptyset\neq X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

the diameter
{\rm diam}_{\mathbf{K}}(X)
of
X
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
is defined by
{\rm diam}_{\mathbf{K}}(X):=\sup\{\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\|_{\mathbf{K}}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in X\}
. Clearly,

(4)

{\rm diam}_{\mathbf{K}}(Q_{r}^{\mathbf{K}})={\rm diam}_{\mathbf{K}}(Q)+2r\leq\lambda+2r.

Thus, the isodiametric inequality in Minkowski spaces (Theorem 11.2.1 in
[
31
]
) and (
4
) imply that

(5)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(Q_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)\leq\left(r+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right).

For the next estimate recall that the
volumetric radius relative to
\mathbf{K}
of a nonempty compact set
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is denoted by
r_{\mathbf{K}}(A)
and it is defined by
{\rm V}_{d}\left(r_{\mathbf{K}}(A)\mathbf{K}\right)=\left(r_{\mathbf{K}}(A)\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right):={\rm V}_{d}\left(A\right)
. Using this concept one can derive the following inequality from the Brunn–Minkowski inequality in a rather straightforward way (Theorem 9.1.1 in
[
31
]
):

(6)

r_{\mathbf{K}}(A_{\epsilon}^{\mathbf{K}})\geq r_{\mathbf{K}}(A)+\epsilon,

which holds for any
\epsilon>0
. As
\{\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}\left[\mathbf{p}_{i},\frac{\lambda}{2}\right]\ |\ 1\leq i\leq N\}
is a packing in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
therefore
r_{\mathbf{K}}\left(P_{\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)=N^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{\lambda}{2}
. Combining this observation with (
6
) yields

(7)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{r}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\left(P_{\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)_{r-\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)\geq\left(N^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{\lambda}{2}+\left(r-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)=

\left(r+\left(N^{\frac{1}{d}}-1\right)\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right).

Finally, as
N\geq 2^{d}
therefore
N^{\frac{1}{d}}-1\geq 1
and Theorem
2.15
follows from (
5
) and (
7
) in a straightforward way.

Thus, Corollary
2.9
and Theorem
2.15
prove Conjecture
2.1
for uniform contractions of sufficiently many congruent balls.

Corollary 2.16
.

Let
r>0,d>2
, and let either
1\leq N\leq d+3
or
N\geq 2^{d}
. If
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a uniform contraction of
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then

(8)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(Q_{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{r}\right).

Recall from
[
99
]
that the compact convex set
\emptyset\neq{A^{\prime}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a
summand
of the compact convex set
\emptyset\neq{A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if there exists a compact convex set
\emptyset\neq{A^{\prime\prime}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
{A^{\prime}}+{A^{\prime\prime}}={A}
. Furthermore, the convex body
\mathbf{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a
generating set
if any nonempty intersection of translates of
\mathbf{B}
is a summand of
\mathbf{B}
. In particular, we say that
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
possesses a generating unit ball
if
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{o},1]=\mathbf{K}
is a generating set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Here we recall the following statements only. Two-dimensional convex bodies are generating sets. Euclidean balls are generating sets as well and the system of generating sets is stable under non-degenerate linear maps and under direct sums. We refer the interested reader for additional properties of generating sets to the recent paper
[
30
]
and the references mentioned there. The following theorem of
[
21
]
is an analogue of the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for intersections of balls under uniform contractions in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
.

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
r>0,d>1
,
N\geq 3^{d}
, and let the
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body
\mathbf{K}
be a generating set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a uniform contraction of
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
, then

(9)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(Q_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right).

In what follows we prove Theorem
2.17
based on
[
21
]
. As a Blaschke-Santaló-type inequality we are going to use Theorem
2.18
, which might be of independent interest. In order to state it we need to recall the following concepts. Let
\emptyset\neq A\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
be a compact convex set, and
1\leq k\leq d
. We denote the
k
-th intrinsic volume
of
A
by
{\rm V}_{k}\left(A\right)
. It is well known that
{\rm V}_{d}\left(A\right)
is the
d
-dimensional
volume of
A
,
2{\rm V}_{d-1}\left(A\right)
is the surface area of
A
, and
\frac{2\omega_{d-1}}{d\omega_{d}}{\rm V}_{1}\left(A\right)
is equal to the mean width of
A
. We set
{\rm V}_{k}\left(\emptyset\right):=0
for
1\leq k\leq d
. Next, let
\mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For a set
\emptyset\neq X\subset\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x},r]
with
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r>0
let
{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(X):=\inf\{R>0\ |\ X\subseteq B_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime},R]\ {\rm with}\ \mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}\leq r
. We call
{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(X)
the
circumradius
of
X
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
and we call
{\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}(X):=\bigcap\{B_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r]\ |\ X\subseteq B_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime},r]\ {\rm with}\ \mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}

the
r
-ball convex hull
of
X
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
. Finally, recall the following easy extension of Lemma 5 from
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
to
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
in
[
20
]
:

(10)

X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}=\left({\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}(X)\right)^{r}.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
r>0,d>1
,
1\leq k\leq d
, and let the
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body
\mathbf{K}
be a generating set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If
\emptyset\neq A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a compact set with
{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(A)\leq r
and
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a ball of
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
such that
{\rm V}_{k}\left({\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}A\right)={\rm V}_{k}\left(B\right)
, then
{\rm V}_{k}\left(A_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(B_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)
.

Proof.

We start by recalling Lemma 8 from
[
21
]
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
d>1
,
r>0
, and let
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
possess a generating unit ball. If
X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\neq\emptyset
, then

(11)

X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}-{\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}(X)=B_{\mathbf{K}}[\mathbf{o},r].

Clearly, the Brunn-Minkowski inequality for intrinsic volumes (
[
63
]
, Eq. (74)) combined with Lemma
2.19
implies

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
r>0,d>1
,
1\leq k\leq d
, and let
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
possess a generating unit ball. If
X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\neq\emptyset
, then

(12)

{\rm V}_{k}\left(X_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}+{\rm V}_{k}\left({\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}(X)\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq r{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}.

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
2.18
. By assumption
A_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\neq\emptyset
. For the estimates that follow, let the
k-th intrinsic volumetric radius (relative to
\mathbf{K}
) of
A
be denoted by
r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A)
and defined by
{\rm V}_{k}\left(r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A)\mathbf{K}\right)=\left(r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A)\right)^{k}{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{K}\right):={\rm V}_{k}\left({\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}A\right)
. Notice that
r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A)\mathbf{K}=B
. Thus, Corollary
2.20
and homogeneity
(of degree k) of k-th intrinsic volume imply in a straightforward way that

{\rm V}_{k}\left(A_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)\leq\left[r{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}-{\rm V}_{k}\left({\rm conv}_{r,\mathbf{K}}(A)\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right]^{k}=\left[r{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}-{\rm V}_{k}\left(r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A)\mathbf{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right]^{k}

=(r-r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A))^{k}{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)={\rm V}_{k}\left((r-r_{k,\mathbf{K}}(A))\mathbf{K}\right)={\rm V}_{k}\left(B_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right),

finishing the proof of Theorem
2.18
.
∎

Based on Theorem
2.18
we can give a short proof for Theorem
2.17
as follows. If
r\leq{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(P)
, then clearly
V_{d}(P_{\mathbf{K}}^{r})=0\leq V_{d}(Q_{\mathbf{K}}^{r})
, finishing the proof of Theorem
2.17
in this case. Hence, for the rest we may assume that
{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(P)<r
. By assumption there exists
\lambda>0
such that
Q
is a uniform contraction of
P
with separating value
\lambda
in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{\mathbf{K}}
. Then
\emptyset\neq P_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}=\left(P_{\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)_{\mathbf{K}}^{r+\frac{\lambda}{2}}
with
{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)=N\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(N^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{K}\right)
and so, Theorem
2.18
for
k=d
and (
10
) imply that

{\rm V}_{d}\left(P_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\left(P_{\frac{\lambda}{2}}^{\mathbf{K}}\right)_{\mathbf{K}}^{r+\frac{\lambda}{2}}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(\left(N^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{K}\right)_{\mathbf{K}}^{r+\frac{\lambda}{2}}\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\left(r+\frac{\lambda}{2}-N^{\frac{1}{d}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\mathbf{K}\right)

(13)

=\left(r-(N^{\frac{1}{d}}-1)\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right).

Next, recall that
Q=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
N\geq 3^{d}
such that
\|\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{j}\|_{\mathbf{K}}\leq\lambda
holds for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
. Thus, Bohnenblust’s theorem (Theorem 11.1.3 in
[
31
]
) yields
{\rm cr}_{\mathbf{K}}(Q)\leq\frac{d}{d+1}{\rm diam}_{\mathbf{K}}(Q)\leq\frac{d}{d+1}\lambda
, from which it is easy to derive that

(14)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(Q_{\mathbf{K}}^{r}\right)\geq\left(r-\frac{d}{d+1}\lambda\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right).

Finally, observe that
N\geq 3^{d}
implies

\left(r-(N^{\frac{1}{d}}-1)\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)\leq\left(r-\lambda\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)<\left(r-\frac{d}{d+1}\lambda\right)^{d}{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)
. This inequality combined with (
13
) and (
14
) completes the proof of Theorem
2.17
.

The following theorem of
[
17
]
strengthens Theorem
2.17
using isoperimetric properties of balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. (See also
[
23
]
for a Blaschke–Santaló-type inequality on intrinsic volumes of

-ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, which can be used to prove Theorem

in a somewhat different way.)

Theorem 2.21
.

Let
r>0
,
d>1
,
1\leq k\leq d
, and let
N\geq(1+\sqrt{2})^{d}
. If
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a uniform contraction of
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then

(15)

{\rm V}_{k}\left(P^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(Q^{r}\right).

This leads to the following volumetric question on
r
-ball bodies, which was raised in
[
17
]
under the belief that the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for intersections of congruent balls can be sharpened.

Problem 2.22
.

Let
r>0
,
d>1
,
1\leq k\leq d-1
, and let
N>1
. If
Q:=\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a contraction of

is still open in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. In fact, Alexander

conjectured that under any contraction of the center points of a finite family of unit disks in the plane, the perimeter of the intersection does not decrease. For partial results on

we refer the interested reader to

. We note that the higher dimensional (

) analogue of Alexander’s conjecture (that is, Problem

for

3.
More on volumetric properties of intersections of congruent balls

3.1.
Blaschke–Santaló-type inequalities for
r
-ball bodies

Recall that according to Definition
2.14
, for a set
X\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
and
r>0
, the
r
-ball body generated by
X
is
X^{r}=\bigcap_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]
. We note that either
X^{r}=\emptyset
, or
X^{r}
is a point, or
{\rm int}(X^{r})\neq\emptyset
. Perhaps not surprisingly,
r
-ball bodies of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
have already been investigated in a number of papers however, under various names such as “überkonvexe Menge” (
[
81
]
), “
r
-convex domain” (
[
49
]
), “spindle convex set” (
[
14
]
,
[
75
]
), “ball convex set” (
[
76
]
), “hyperconvex set” (
[
55
]
), and “
r
-dual set” (
[
20
]
). Next, we recall that the
r
-ball convex hull

{\rm conv}_{r}X
of
\emptyset\neq X\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
for
d>1
and
r>0
, is defined by

{\rm conv}_{r}X:=\bigcap\{\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]\ |\ X\subseteq\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]\}.

Moreover, let
{\rm conv}_{r}{\mathbb{E}}^{d}:={\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and
{\rm conv}_{r}\emptyset:=\emptyset
. Thus, we say that
X\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is
r
-ball convex
if
X={\rm conv}_{r}X
. Furthermore, we recall from
[
14
]
the following concepts. If
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\leq 2r
for some
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then let the
(closed)
r
-spindle

[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]_{r}
be defined by
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]_{r}:={\rm conv}_{r}\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}
. If
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|>2r
for some
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then let
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]_{r}:={\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. So, we say that
X\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is
r
-spindle convex
if for any
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in X
we have that
[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}]_{r}\subseteq X
. Finally, recall that if
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]
contains the set
\emptyset\neq X\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
with
\mathbf{y}\in{\rm bd}(X)\cap{\rm bd}(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r])
, then we say that
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{x},r]
supports
X
at the point
\mathbf{y}
.
When
r
is omitted, it is taken as
r=1
. The following statement from
[
14
]
shows the equivalence of the concepts introduced.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
r>0
,
d>1
, and
\emptyset\neq\mathbf{A}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
be a compact (linearly) convex set. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)
\mathbf{A}
is an
r
-ball body.

(2)
\mathbf{A}
is
r
-ball convex.

(3)
\mathbf{A}
is
r
-spindle convex.

(4) For every boundary point of
\mathbf{A}
there exits a closed ball of radius
r
that supports
\mathbf{A}
at that point.

The way
r
-ball bodies have been introduced in Definition
2.14
suggests to investigate the map
\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbf{A}^{r}
that assigns to an arbitrary
r
-ball body
\mathbf{A}
another
r
-ball body namely,
\mathbf{A}^{r}
. This map has been investigated in
[
11
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
55
]
,
[
20
]
, and based on the properties derived there we call this map
r
-duality
. The following statement highlights some remarkable properties of
r
-duality, which with the exception of (6) and (7) are analogues of the corresponding properties of the classical polarity map. If we write
X\vee X_{1}:={\rm conv}_{r}\left(X\cup X_{1}\right)
for sets
X,X_{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and
\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d}
for the
set of
r
-ball bodies
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then we observe that
(\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d},\cap,\vee)
is a lattice.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
X,X_{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
are arbitrary sets and
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}_{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d}
are arbitrary
r
-ball bodies for
d>1
and
r>0
, then we have the following:

(1)
\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)^{r}=\mathbf{A}
.

(2)
\emptyset\neq X\subseteq X_{1}
implies
X_{1}^{r}\subseteq X^{r}
.

(3)
X^{r}=({\rm conv}_{r}X)^{r}
.

(4)
(X\cup X_{1})^{r}=X^{r}\cap X_{1}^{r}
and
\left(\mathbf{A}\vee\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)^{r}=\mathbf{A}^{r}\cap\mathbf{A}_{1}^{r}
.

(5)
\left(\mathbf{A}\cap\mathbf{A}_{1}\right)^{r}=\mathbf{A}^{r}\vee\mathbf{A}_{1}^{r}
.

(6)
\mathbf{A}+(-\mathbf{A}^{r})=\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r]
and
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{r}
is a body of constant width
2r
provided that
\mathbf{A}\neq\emptyset
. In particular, the sum of the minimal width of
\mathbf{A}
and the diameter of
\mathbf{A}^{r}
is equal to
2r
.

(7) The largest ball contained in
\mathbf{A}
and the smallest ball that contains
\mathbf{A}^{r}
are concentric and the sum of their radii is equal to
r
.

According to (1), (2), (4), and (5) of Proposition
3.2
the
r
-duality
\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbf{A}^{r}
is an order reversing involution that interchanges the lattice operations.

Next, recall the theorem of Gao, Hug, and Schneider
[
62
]
stating that for any convex body of given volume in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
the volume of the spherical polar body is maximal if the convex body is a ball. In 2018, this theorem was extended by the first named author
[
20
]
to
r
-ball bodies of spaces of constant curvature. While the spherical and hyperbolic versions can be proved using two-point symmetrization, and that method works for the Euclidean case as well, there is another and simpler way to prove the following statement. If
A\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
is a compact set of volume
{\rm V}_{d}\left(A\right)>0
and
r>0
and
\mathbf{B}\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is a ball with
{\rm V}_{d}\left(A\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)
, then
{\rm V}_{d}\left(A^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{B}^{r}\right)
. In fact,
[
23
]
shows that similar inequalities hold for intrinsic volumes (see also
[
25
]
). Thus, we have the following Blaschke–Santaló-type inequality for
r
-ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d>1
,
r>0
, and
1\leq k\leq d
. If
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d}
,
d>1
is an
r
-ball body of volume
{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)>0
and
\mathbf{B}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is a ball with
{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)
, then

(17)

{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{r}\right)

holds with equality if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is congruent (i.e., isometric) to
\mathbf{B}
.

For the convenience of the reader we recall here the short proof of Theorem
3.3
from
[
25
]
. Let
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r-R]
and
\mathbf{B}^{r}=\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},R]
for
0<R<r
. The Brunn–Minkowski inequality for intrinsic volumes (
[
63
]
, Eq. (74)) and (6) of Proposition
3.2
imply

(18)

{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}+{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}+{\rm V}_{k}\left(-\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}+(-\mathbf{A}^{r})\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r]\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}

with equality if and only if (
\mathbf{A}
and
-\mathbf{A}^{r}
are homothetic, i.e.,)
\mathbf{A}
is congruent to
\mathbf{B}
. Finally, (
18
), the isoperimetric inequality for intrinsic volumes stating that among convex bodies of given volume the balls have the smallest
k
-th intrinsic volume (
[
99
]
, Section 7.4), and the homogeneity (of degree
k
) of
k
-th intrinsic volume imply

{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r]\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}-{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r]\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}-{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},r-R]\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}

={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},R]\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}},

with
{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}
if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is congruent to
\mathbf{B}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

In
[
25
]
, the following corollary has been derived from Theorem
3.3
, which for
k=d
has been proved independently by Fodor, Kurusa, and Vígh
[

.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
d>1
,
r>0
,
1\leq k\leq d
, and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d}
be an

-ball body. Set
P_{k}(\mathbf{A}):={\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}\right){\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)
. Then

(19)

P_{k}(\mathbf{A})\leq P_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[\mathbf{o},\frac{r}{2}\right]\right)

with equality if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is congruent to
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{o},\frac{r}{2}]
.

Indeed, Corollary
3.4
follows from Theorem
3.3
by the homogeneity (of degree
k
) of
k
-th intrinsic volume, and the observation that
f(x)=x^{k}(r-x)^{k}
,
0\leq x\leq r
has a unique maximum value at
x=\frac{r}{2}
for any
d>1
,
1\leq k\leq d
, and
r>0
.

) to prove the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for uniform contractions of intersections of sufficiently many congruent balls. We close this section with the following complementary question to Theorem

for all

-ball bodies
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}_{d}
of given volume
v={\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
.

Remark 3.6
.

Problem
3.5
for
d=2
was solved in
[
25
]
as follows. Let
0<v<\pi r^{2}
. Then the minimum of
{\rm V}_{1}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)
(resp.,
{\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{A}^{r}\right)
) for all
r
-disk domains
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}^{r}_{2}
of given area
v={\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
is attained only for
r
-lenses, which are intersections of two disks of radius
r
.

3.2.
Reverse isoperimetric problems for
r
-ball bodies

The classical isoperimetric inequality (
[
95
]
) implies in a straightforward way that among
r
-ball bodies of given volume the ball has the smallest surface area in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. On the other hand, Borisenko (
[
33
]
) conjectured that among
r
-ball bodies of given
d
-dimensional volume in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
the
r
-lens, which is the intersection of two balls of radius
r
, is the unique maximizer of surface area.
Borisenko’s conjecture
was proved for
d=2
by Borisenko and Drach in
[
28
]
and very recently for
d=3
by Drach and Tatarko in
[
43
]
. Thus, we have the following reverse isoperimetric theorem for
r
-ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
for
d=2,3
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
r>0
. Among
r
-ball bodies (resp.,
r
-disk domains) of given volume (resp., area) in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
(resp.,
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
), the
r
-lens is the only one whose surface area (resp., perimeter) is maximal.

As an extension of Borisenko’s conjecture, the first named author (
[
24
]
) suggested to investigate the following reverse isoperimetric problems for
r
ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, which seem to be open except for
k=d-1
with
d=2,3
.

Problem 3.8
.

Let
0<k<d
and
r>0
. Among
r
-ball bodies of given
d
-dimensional volume in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
find the one(s), whose
k
-th intrinsic volume is the largest possible.

The recent paper
[
22
]
proves the following - closely related - reverse inradius inequality on the
d
-dimensional volume of
r
-ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Then its
inradius

r_{in}(\mathbf{K})
(resp.,
circumradius

r_{cr}(\mathbf{K})
) is the radius of the largest (resp., smallest) ball contained in
\mathbf{K}
(resp., containing
\mathbf{K}
). In particular, we use the notation
L_{r,\rho,d}
for the
r
-lens whose inradius is
\rho
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, where
r\geq\rho>0
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
r>r_{0}>0
,
N>1,d>1
, and let
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
with
r_{cr}(P)=r_{0}
. Then

(20)

{\rm V}_{d}\left(P^{r}\right)\leq{\rm V}_{d}\left(L_{r,r-r_{0},d}\right).

Remark 3.10
.

We note that
r_{in}(P^{r})=r-r_{0}
in Theorem
3.9
. Thus, the proof of Theorem
3.9
in
[
22
]
implies the somewhat stronger statement that among
r
-ball bodies of given inradius in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, the
r
-lens is the only one whose
d
-dimensional volume is maximal.

Moreover, notice that Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
3.9
yield the following statement in a straightforward way. Let
r>0
. Among
r
-ball bodies (resp.,
r
-disk domains) of given inradius in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
(resp.,
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
), the
r
-lens is the only one whose surface area (resp., perimeter) is maximal. Very recently Drach and Tatarko
[
43
]
extended this result to higher dimensions.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
r>0
. Among
r
-ball bodies of given inradius in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
, the
r
-lens is the only one whose surface area is maximal.

Theorems
3.9
and
3.11
support the following conjecture of the first named author
[
22
]
:

Conjecture 3.12
.

Let
0<k\leq d-2
and
r>0
. Among
r
-ball bodies of given inradius in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
, the
r
-lens is the only one whose
k
-th intrinsic volume is maximal.

We close this section with the following reverse circumradius inequality on the
d
-dimensional volume of
r
-ball bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, which was proved in
[
22
]
. We use the notation
S_{r,\lambda,d}
for the
r
-spindle whose circumradius is
\lambda
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
r>r_{0}>0
,
N>1,d>1
, and let
P:=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
with

. Then

(21)

V_{d}(S_{r,r_{0},d})\leq V_{d}\left({\rm conv}_{r}P\right).

Remark 3.14
.

Theorem
3.13
is equivalent to the statement that among
r
-ball bodies of given volume in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, the
r
-spindle has the largest circumradius.

Theorem

supports the following conjecture of the first named author
[

]
:

Conjecture 3.15
.

Let

,

, and let

. Then

(22)

V_{k}(S_{r,r_{0},d})\leq V_{k}\left({\rm conv}_{r}P\right).

4.
Spindle convex sets

4.1.
Approximation of spindle convex sets

Approximating convex sets by convex polytopes has been a longstanding problem of convex geometry. In these problems we consider a family
{\mathcal{F}}
of convex polytopes and a convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, and try to find the elements of
{\mathcal{F}}
with ‘minimal distance’ from
\mathbf{K}
. For example,
{\mathcal{F}}
may contain all convex polytopes with a given number of vertices or facets, where we may restrict our investigation to polytopes contained in
\mathbf{K}
or to polytopes containing
\mathbf{K}
, and we can measure ‘distance’ by various geometric quantities like the Hausdorff distance of
\mathbf{K}
and the polytope, or the volume of their symmetric difference. In some cases, instead of the ‘best’ approximation we intend to find the distance of a ‘typical’ approximation, and measure the average distance of
\mathbf{K}
from a randomly chosen element of
{\mathcal{F}}

using some fixed probability distribution on
\mathbf{K}
. The latter problem is called the problem of
random approximation
of a convex body. The systematic investigation for best approximation was started in the paper
[
82
]
of McClure and Vitale, whereas for random approximation in the papers
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
of Rényi and Sulanke. For more information on this topic, the interested reader is referred to the recent survey
[
91
]
.

In this section we consider a spindle convex variant of this problem (see the definition of spindle convexity right before Proposition
3.1
). In this setting, the ‘counterpart’ of a convex polytope can be either the spindle convex hull of finitely many points, called a
ball-polytope

[
14
]
*Definition 13.1, or the intersection of finitely many unit balls, called a
ball-polyhedron
. These two concepts coincide in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, and are called
circle-polygons
or
disk-polygons
. Our aim is to investigate how a spindle convex body can be approximated by ball-polytopes or ball-polyhedra.

With this aim, we introduce three metrics commonly used to measure the error of the approximation.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}
be convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Then the
volume
and
surface volume deviations
of
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}
are defined as

\delta_{V}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})={\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{L}\right)-{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{K}\cap\mathbf{L}\right),\quad\delta_{S}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})=\surf(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{L})-\surf(\mathbf{K}\cap\mathbf{L}),

respectively, where
\surf(\cdot)
denotes surface volume. In addition,
\delta_{H}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})
denotes the Hausdorff distance of
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}
.

In the case of plane convex bodies, which we also call
convex disks
, volume and surface volume deviation is usually called
area
and
perimeter deviation
, respectively.

The first result in this area, due to Fodor and Vígh
[
60
]
gives asymptotic formulas for the deviations of the best approximating inscribed and circumscribed disk-
n
-gons. To formulate this results, let
\mathbf{K}
be a spindle convex body with
C^{2}
-class boundary, where for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
,
\kappa(\mathbf{x})
denotes the curvature of
\mathbf{K}
at
\mathbf{x}
. Choose disk-polygons
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{V}
,
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{S},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{H}
, with at most
n
sides and inscribed in
\mathbf{K}
, and closest to it with respect to area and perimeter deviation, and with respect to Hausdorff distance, respectively. We choose best approximating disk polygons
\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{V},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{S},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{H}
similarly in the family of disk-polygons with at most
n
sides, circumscribed about
\mathbf{K}
. Then the following asymptotic formulas hold, where
d\mathbf{x}
denotes integration with respect to Hausdorff measure.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any spindle convex body
\mathbf{K}
with twice continuously differentiable boundary, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{V}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{V})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{S}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{S})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa^{2}(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{H}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{H})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/2}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{V}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{V})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{S}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{S})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(2\kappa^{2}(\mathbf{x})-3\kappa(\mathbf{x})+1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\delta_{H}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{H})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/2}\,d\mathbf{x}\right)^{2}.

In a very recent paper, Nagy and Vígh
[
87
]
generalized this result for so-called
\mathbf{L}
-convex sets, introduced by Mayer
[
81
]
and systematically investigated, independently, by Lángi et al.
[
76
]
and Polovinkin
[
89
]
.
To state their result, first we introduce this concept. Let
\mathbf{L}
be a convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. A set
\mathbf{K}
is said to be
\mathbf{L}
-ball-convex
, if it is equal to the intersection of all translates of
\mathbf{L}
that contain
\mathbf{K}
.
If
X\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is contained in a translate of
\mathbf{L}
, the
\mathbf{L}
-convex hull of
X
, denoted by
{\rm conv}_{\mathbf{L}}(X)
, is defined as the intersection of all translates of
\mathbf{L}
that contain
X
. In particular, if

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
are contained in a translate of
\mathbf{L}
, then their
\mathbf{L}
-convex hull is called the
\mathbf{L}
-spindle of

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
, denoted as

[\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}]_{\mathbf{L}}
.
A set
\mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is called
\mathbf{L}
-spindle-convex
, if it is contained in a translate of
\mathbf{L}
, and for any

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathbf{K}
, we have
[\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}]_{\mathbf{L}}\subseteq\mathbf{K}
. Clearly, every
\mathbf{L}
-ball-convex set is
\mathbf{L}
-spindle-convex. In the planar case the converse also holds, but for any
d>2
there are examples of
\mathbf{L}
-spindle convex sets that are not
\mathbf{L}
-ball-convex for some suitable convex body
\mathbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
(see e.g.
[
76
]
). On the other hand, there are also bodies
\mathbf{L}
different from ellipsoids for which
\mathbf{L}
-spindle and
\mathbf{L}
-ball convex sets coincide, as the example of a cube shows.
In the following we restrict ourselves to the planar case, and call an
\mathbf{L}
-ball-convex (or equivalently, an
\mathbf{L}
-spindle-convex) set shortly
\mathbf{L}
-convex.

In
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, the intersection of at most
n
translates of
\mathbf{L}
is called an
\mathbf{L}
-
n
-gon. This property is equivalent to the one that the set is the
\mathbf{L}
-convex hull of at most
n
points in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, contained in a translate of
\mathbf{L}

[
8
]
. Now, let
\mathbf{K}
be an
\mathbf{L}
-convex disk in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. Choose
\mathbf{L}
-
n
-gons
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{V}
,
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{S},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{H}
, contained in
\mathbf{K}
, and closest to it with respect to area and perimeter deviation, and with respect to Hausdorff distance, respectively. We choose best approximating
\mathbf{L}
-
n
-gons
\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{V},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{S},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{H}
similarly in the family of
\mathbf{L}
-
n
-gons, containing
\mathbf{K}
. To state the corresponding result in
[
87
]
, we assume that both
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{K}
have
C^{2}_{+}
-class boundary, that is,
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{L})
and
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
are twice continuously differentiable, with strictly positive curvature everywhere. We denote by
r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)
(resp. by
r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)
) the radius of curvature of
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{L})
(resp.
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
) at the unique point with outer unit normal vector
(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. We also note that the assumption that
\mathbf{K}
is
\mathbf{L}
-convex is equivalent to the property that
r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)\leq r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)
for every value of
\theta
. Then the following asymptotic formulas hold, which generalize the result in Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For convex disk
\mathbf{L}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
with
C^{2}_{+}
-class boundary, and for any
\mathbf{L}
-convex disk
\mathbf{K}
with
C^{2}_{+}
-class boundary:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{V}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{V})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)}\left(r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)-r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)\right)\right)^{1/3}\,d\theta\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{A}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{A})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}^{2}(\theta)}\left(r_{\mathbf{L}}^{2}(\theta)-r_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\theta)\right)\right)^{1/3}\,d\theta\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{H}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{n}^{H})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)}\left(r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)-r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)\right)\right)^{1/2}\,d\theta\right)^{2}.

Furthermore, if, in addition,
\mathbf{L}
is centrally symmetric, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{V}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{V})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)}\left(r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)-r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)\right)\right)^{1/3}\,d\theta\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{A}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{A})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}^{3}(\theta)-3r_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(\theta)r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)+2r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)r_{\mathbf{L}}^{2}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}^{2}(\theta)}\right)^{1/3}\,d\theta\right)^{3},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2}\cdot\delta_{H}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K}_{(n)}^{H})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)}{r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)}\left(r_{\mathbf{L}}(\theta)-r_{\mathbf{K}}(\theta)\right)\right)^{1/2}\,d\theta\right)^{2}.

4.2.
Random disk-polygons

Consider the following probability model: Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex disk in the plane
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. Choose points
\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}
independently from
\mathbf{K}
, using the uniform distribution. Set
\mathbf{K}_{n}={\rm conv}\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\}
. We call
\mathbf{K}_{n}
a
random convex
n
-gon
. Then it is a natural question to ask about the expected value of some geometric quantities of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
, for instance the number of its vertices, or its area or perimeter deviation from
\mathbf{K}
.
These questions were investigated in the classical papers
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
of Rényi and Sulanke, under some smoothness assumption on
\mathbf{K}
. We note that, unknown by Rényi and Sulanke at that time, there is a straighforward connection between the number of vertices of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
and its area deviation from
\mathbf{K}
, known as
Efron’s formula

[
44
]
.

The above problem has a natural analogue in case of spindle convex sets: Let
\mathbf{K}
be a spindle convex disk, and choose points
\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}
independently from
\mathbf{K}
, using the uniform distribution. Define
\mathbf{K}_{n}
as the spindle convex hull of
\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}
. Then we can ask about the expected value of the number of vertices of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
, and its area or perimeter deviation from
\mathbf{K}
. These questions were investigated by Fodor, Kevei and Vígh in
[
51
]
. We state their results as follows; where
{\mathbb{E}}(\cdot)
denotes expected value,
f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n})
denotes the number of vertices of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
, and for every
\mathbf{x}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
,
\kappa(\mathbf{x})
denotes the curvature of
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
at
\mathbf{x}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a spindle convex disk with
C^{2}
-class boundary and with
\kappa(\mathbf{x})>1
for every
\mathbf{x}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
. Then

(23)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{-1/3}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}(f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[3]{\frac{2}{3{\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)}}\Gamma\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x},

(24)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2/3}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}({\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\setminus\mathbf{K}_{n}\right))\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[3]{\frac{2{\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)^{2}}{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\,d\mathbf{x}

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{K}
has
C^{5}
-class boundary, with
\kappa(\mathbf{x})>1
for every
\mathbf{x}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
, then

(25)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n^{2/3}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}(\perim(\mathbf{K})-\perim(\mathbf{K}_{n}))\right)=\\
\frac{\sqrt[3]{144{\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)^{2}}}{36}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\int_{{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})}\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})-1\right)^{1/3}\left(3\kappa(\mathbf{x})+1\right)\,d\mathbf{x}.

In the above formulas, the integration is with respect to arclength i.e.
1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

It is worth noting that like in the original problem for linearly convex polygons, (
23
) is readily implied by (
24
) by an Efron-type formula, proved in
[
51
]
, stating that

(26)

{\mathbb{E}}(f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n}))=\frac{n{\mathbb{E}}({\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\setminus\mathbf{K}_{n-1}\right))}{{\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)}.

Note the necessary condition that the curvature of
\mathbf{K}
is strictly greater than
1
, that is the curvature of the unit circle. If we omit this condition, the order of magnitude of the above quantities may change. This is illustrated in the next theorem from
[
51
]
for the special case that
\mathbf{K}
is the Euclidean unit disk.

Theorem 4.5
.

If
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{B}^{2}[\mathbf{o},1]
, then

(27)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}(f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n\cdot{\mathbb{E}}({\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}\setminus\mathbf{K}_{n}\right))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{3}}{2},

(29)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(n\cdot{\mathbb{E}}(\perim(\mathbf{K})-\perim(\mathbf{K}_{n}))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{3}}{2}.

We remark that the interesting phenomenon in (
27
), namely that the expected value of the number of sides of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is a constant independent of
n
, does not appear in the original problem about the number of sides of linearly convex polygons. Later, a similar phenomenon was observed for the number of facets of certain random polytopes in spherical space (see
[
7
]
).

Similar to the previous result, we can investigate the asymptotic values of the variances
\var(\cdot)
of the same quantities. For the case of the number of vertices and for area deviation, this was examined in
[
61
]
by Fodor and Vígh. We present their results as follows.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a spindle convex disk with
C^{2}
-class boundary and with
\kappa(\mathbf{x})>1
for every
\mathbf{x}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
. Then

(30)

\displaystyle\var(f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{1/3}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\var({\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}_{n}\right))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-5/3}\right),

where the implied constants depend only on
\mathbf{K}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

If
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{B}^{2}[\mathbf{o},1]
, then

(32)

\displaystyle\var(f_{0}(\mathbf{K}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(1\right)

(33)

\displaystyle\var({\rm V}_{2}\left(\mathbf{K}_{n}\right))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2}\right).

It is worth noting that Theorems
4.4
-
4.7
, combined with Chebyshev’s inequality and the Borel-Cantelli lemma, imply the following statement
[
61
]
.

Theorem 4.8
.

If
\mathbf{K}
is a spindle convex disk with
C^{2}

4.4.
Wendel’s inequality

Wendel’s inequality
[
105
]
states that if
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are random points chosen from
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
independently according to an
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric probability distribution, then the probability that their convex hull does not contain the origin is

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{o}\notin{\rm conv}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\})=\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}.

Wagner and Welzl
[
104
]
extended this result by showing that if we drop the condition that the distribution is
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric, then the
=
sign in this formula should be replaced by the
\leq
sign.

Fodor, Pinzón and Vígh
[
56
]
investigated a spindle convex variant of this problem in the following way.
Let
\mathbf{K}
be a spindle convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Chose points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
randomly and independently from
\mathbf{K}
according to the uniform distribution.
Then we set

P(d,\mathbf{K},n)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{o}\notin{\rm conv}_{s}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}),

where
{\rm conv}_{s}(\cdot)
denotes spindle convex hull.

Theorem 4.20
.

We have

P(d,r\mathbf{B}^{d},2)=\frac{d(d+1)\omega_{d+1}}{r^{2d}\omega_{d}}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{q}^{\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})}r_{1}^{d-1}r_{2}^{d-1}\sin^{d-2}\varphi\,d\varphi\,dr_{2}\,dr_{1},

where
\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})=\arcsin\frac{r_{1}}{2}+\arcsin\frac{r_{2}}{2}
.

They also computed the value

, and investigated the case when two points are chosen according to the Gaussian distribution.

5.
Combinatorial structure of ball polyhedra

Ball-polyhedra
, that is, sets obtained as an intersection of finitely many balls of equal (say, unit) radii in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
have been extensively studied, see
[
14
,
75
]
,
[
78
]
*Section 8 and the references therein. In this section, we consider ball-polyhedra in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
, that have a natural face structure analogous to that of three-dimensional convex polyhedra. Throughout this section
P=\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\mathbf{B}^{3}\left[{\bf p}_{i},1\right]
denotes a ball-polyhedron with nonempty interior,
N\geq 3
such that the centers
{\bf p}_{1},\dots,{\bf p}_{N}
are all non-redundant, that is,
P\neq\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{j\}}\mathbf{B}^{3}\left[{\bf p}_{i},1\right]
for every
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Clearly,
P
has
N

faces:

P\cap{\rm bd}\left(\mathbf{B}^{3}\left[{\bf p}_{i},1\right]\right)
for each
i
. It is not difficult to see that the
i
-th face is a spherically convex set on the sphere
{\rm bd}\left(\mathbf{B}^{3}\left[{\bf p}_{i},1\right]\right)
. Edges are slightly more difficult to define, as for every distinct
i
and
j
, the
i
-th and
j
-th face may intersect in the empty set, in a point, or, in a finite union of points and closed circular arcs, cf.
[
16
]
. We will call
e
an
edge
of
P
, if
e
is a non-degenerate circular arc. The
vertices
of
P
are easy to define: points of
{\rm bd}(P)
belonging to at least three faces. It is easy to see that the boundary of
P
is the disjoint union of the spherical relative interior of its faces, the circular relative interior of its edges and the set of vertices. Following
[
14
]
, we will say that
P
is a
standard ball-polyhedron
, if the intersection of any two faces is empty, or one edge, or one vertex. Equivalently, if the faces, edges and vertices form an algebraic lattice with respect to containment.

A fundamental and highly non-trivial result in the combinatorial theory of polytopes is
Steinitz’s theorem
, according to which a graph
G
is the edge graph of a three-dimensional polytope if and only if,
G
is simple (no parallel edges and loops), planar and 3-connected. Its analogue for ball-polyhedra in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
does not hold as shown by simple examples in
[
16
]
, however, it does for standard ball-polyhedra by the main result of
[
4
]
.

5.1.
Strongly self-dual convex polytopes and ball-polyhedra

A special class of convex polytopes in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
was introduced by Lovász
[
77
]
as follows. We say that
P
is a
strongly self-dual polytope
, if all vertices of
P
lie on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
, for some
0<r<1
, all facets of
P
touch the sphere
r{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
, and there is a bijection
\sigma
between vertices and facets of
P
such that if
{\bf v}
is a vertex, then the facet
\sigma({\bf v})
is orthogonal to the vector
{\bf v}
. For a vertex
{\bf v}
of
P
, and a vertex
{\bf u}
of
\sigma(v)
, we call the line segment
[{\bf u},{\bf v}]
a
principal diagonal
of
P
. Clearly, every principal diagonal is of length
\alpha=\sqrt{2+2r}
, which we call the
parameter
of
P
. Lovász poses the question of determining the set of possible values of
\alpha
, and gives the following partial answer.

Theorem 5.1
.

For every
d\geq 2
and
\alpha_{1}<2
, there is a strongly self-dual polytope in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
with parameter at least
\alpha_{1}
.

The motivation to introduce these polytopes was the following. Clearly, the diameter of a strongly self-dual polytope is equal to its parameter
\alpha
. Thus, the diameter graph of the set of vertices of a strongly self-dual polytope
P
is identical to the graph obtained from the principal diagonals of
P
as edges of the graph. Proving that this graph has chromatic number
d+1
, and combining it with combinatorial results, the main result of
[
77
]
is that for every
0<\alpha<2
, there is finite set
V
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
with the following property: the graph with vertex set
V
whose edges are the pairs at Euclidean distance
\alpha
, has chromatic number at least
d+1
.

It is well known that a convex set
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is a set of constant width (that is, any two parallel supporting hyperplanes are of the same distance) if and only if,

\mathbf{K}=\bigcap_{x\in\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[x,r\right]
, where
r
is the diameter of
\mathbf{K}
. If
X
is the vertex set of a regular triangle of side length 1 on the plane, then
\mathbf{K}=\bigcap_{x\in X}\mathbf{B}^{2}\left[x,1\right]
, the Reuleaux triangle, is a set of constant width. However, if
X
is the vertex set of a regular tetrahedron of side length 1 in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
, then
\bigcap_{x\in X}\mathbf{B}^{3}\left[x,1\right]
is not a set of constant width, but it may be turned into a set of constant width 1 by “rounding off the edges” (to obtain Meissner bodies). Sallee
[
96
]
studied this method of constructing sets of constant width, and introduced the term
Reuleaux frame
(called a
Reuleaux polyhedron
in
[
85
]
) referring to ball-polyhedra (intersections of finitely many unit balls), where each center (of the unit balls) is a vertex, and vice versa. Montejano and Roldán-Pensado
[
84
]
, and Martini, Montejano and Oliveros
[
78
]
studied further how a Reuleaux polyhedron may be turned into a set of constant width.

Montejano, Pauli, Raggi and Roldán-Pensado
[
85
]
considered the following Steinitz-type question for Reuleaux polyhedra.
Call a planar, 3-connected graph
G

strongly self-dual
(cf.
[
101
]
), if there is a self-duality isomorphism
\tau
between
G
and its dual
G^{\ast}
satisfying two conditions.

•

For every pair of vertices
u,v
, we have that
u\in\tau(v)

\Leftrightarrow

v\in\tau(u)
, and

•

for every vertex
u
, we have that
u\notin\tau(u)
.

Perhaps currently, the most interesting open problem on the combinatorial structure of three-dimensional ball-polyhedra is the following conjecture from
[
85
]
.

Conjecture 5.2
.

For every planar, 3-connected, strongly self-dual graph
G
, there is a Reuleaux polyhedron in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
with edge-graph isomorphic to
G
.

The main result of
[
85
]
is the following weaker statement.

Theorem 5.3
.

For every planar, 3-connected, strongly self-dual graph
G
, there is a (not necessarily injective) map
\eta:V\longrightarrow{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
from the vertex set
V
such that the diameter of
\eta(V)
is 1, and for every
v\in V
and
u\in\tau(v)
we have
\|\eta(u)-\eta(v)\|=1
.

We note that the existence of an injective
\eta
is equivalent to Conjecture
5.2
.

5.2.
Diameter graphs

Borsuk’s classical problem asks whether any bounded set in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
may be partitioned into
d+1
subsets, each of smaller diameter than the set. Borsuk confirmed it on the plane, and, to great surprise, it was later proven to be false in high dimensions by Khan and Kalai
[
68
]
. The answer is still positive for
d=3
as shown independently by Grünbaum
[
66
]
, Heppes
[
67
]
and Straszewicz
[
102
]
by means of ball polytopes.
They considered Vázsonyi’s conjecture as well, and proved that in any set of
n
points in
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
, the diameter of the set is attained at most
2n-2
times. For a new proof, see Swanepoel’s
[
103
]
. The connection between diameter graphs of finite point sets in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
an ball-polyhedra was thoroughly explored in
[
75
]
, including further facts on strongly self-dual polyhedra.

Continuing the discussion of
diameter graphs
, that is, graphs whose vertex set is a finite set of points in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, and a pair form an edge, if they are a diameter of the set, we turn to the following conjecture by Z. Schur, stated in
[
100
]
:
In any dimension
d
, and for any
n
, the diameter graph of any set of
n
points in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
contains at most
n
cliques of size
d
.
Note that a size
d
clique in this graph is the vertex set of a regular
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex. It was confirmed by Hopf and Pannwitz for
d=2
in
[
41
]
, for
d=3
by Schur, Perles, Martini and Kupitz in
[
100
]
and for
d=4
by Kupavskii
[
74
]
. Building on a work of Morić and Pach
[
86
]
, finally, Kupavskii and Polyanskii
[
73
]
confirmed it in full generality. The proof relies on a fine analysis of intersections of balls.

6.1.
Intrinsic volumes of random ball-polyhedra

Extending the work of Rényi and Sulanke
[

from the plane to higher dimensions, Böröczky and Schneider
[

considered the mean width of random polytopes, that is, convex bodies obtained as the intersection of random half-spaces. Paouris and Pivovarov
[
88

studied the intrinsic volumes of random ball-polyhedra. They showed the following isoperimetric inequality.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
N,d\geq 1
and
R>0
. Let
f
be a continuous probability density function on
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
that is

sampled according to

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm V}_{k}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[X_{i},R\right]\right)>s\right]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm V}_{k}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[Z_{i},R\right]\right)>s\right].

As an immediate corollary, we have the following bound on the moments of the intrinsic volumes of random ball-polyhedra.

Corollary 6.2
.

With the assumptions of Theorem
6.1

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm V}_{k}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[X_{i},R\right]\right)^{p}\right]^{1/p}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm V}_{k}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[Z_{i},R\right]\right)^{p}\right]^{1/p}

holds for any

.

This result may be interpreted as a stochastic version of the

, according to which

(48)

\left(\frac{{\rm V}_{k}\left(K\right)}{{\rm V}_{k}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]\right)}\right)^{1/k}\leq\frac{{\rm V}_{1}\left(K\right)}{{\rm V}_{1}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]\right)}.

for any convex body

and

, cf.
[
99
]
*p.603. To see how Corollary

yields (

, the

of

and the

Inequality (

We will use the

The proof of Theorem

{\bf p}_{i}
(resp.,
{\bf q}_{i}
) with probability
1/N

. Then
X+W
has density

\frac{1}{N\cdot\omega_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]}(x-{\bf p}_{i}),

\frac{1}{N\cdot\omega_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]}(x-{\bf q}_{i}).

In this setting, (

\int_{{\mathbb{E}}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{N\cdot\omega_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]}(x-{\bf q}_{i})\right)^{N}\;{\mathrm{d}}x\geq

\int_{{\mathbb{E}}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{N\cdot\omega_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,1\right]}(x-{\bf p}_{i})\right)^{N}\;{\mathrm{d}}x.

If we expand the powers, and use the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture for the volume of the intersection, then a term-by-term comparison yields the inequality above.

For a nonnegative measurable
f:{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)

which vanishes at infinity, we define its
symmetrically decreasing rearrangement
as the radially symmetric function
f^{\ast}:{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
defined (almost everywhere) by the property

\{x\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\;:\;f^{\ast}(x)>t\}=\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,r_{f}(x)\right],

where
r_{f}(x)
is the radius for which
{\rm V}_{d}\left(\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,r_{f}(x)\right]\right)={\rm V}_{d}\left(\{x\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\;:\;f(x)>t\}\right)
. In geometric terms, assuming that
f
is a probability density function, we consider the sub-graph (a set in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d+1}
) of the probability distribution function corresponding to
f
, and take the Steiner symmetrization (see p.
6.1
for the definition) of this set about the one dimensional linear subspace spanned by the last basic vector
e_{d+1}
of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d+1}
. The set thus obtained will be the probability distribution function corresponding to the probability density function
f^{\ast}
.

For two probability densities
f
and
g
on
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, we say that
f
is
majorized
by

\int_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,r\right]}f^{\ast}\leq\int_{\mathbf{B}^{d}\left[o,r\right]}g^{\ast}

h_{\alpha}(f)\geq h_{\alpha}(g)
for
