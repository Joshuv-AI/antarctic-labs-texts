The application of the wound protector device “lap-protector” in costal cartilage harvest for auricular reconstruction to improve outcomes
Authors: Fan Guo, Jianwen Qu, Pengfei Sun, Changchen Wang, Bo Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study presents experience in using a wound protector device named lap-protector during costal cartilage harvest in auricular reconstruction in order to improve outcomes, particularly final scar quality and length. The present study retrospectively comprised fifty-five patients who underwent costal cartilage harvesting for auricular reconstruction were admitted between June to August 2022. The author divided the patients into 2 groups according to whether the lap-protector was used or not: group 1 underwent costal cartilage with the minimal invasive conventional technique by using the lap-protector, while group 2 underwent the same procedure without. Patients were followed-up for a 6-months period. The time of surgery, the amount of blood loss during surgery, postoperative pain, donor-site scar quality and length were recorded and measured for both groups. Twenty-five patients with lap-protector were compared to thirty without. There were no significant differences between the two groups in the demographic of patient characteristics (P > 0.05). The score of pain dropped steadily over the 5 days in both groups, and patients in group 1 reported a lower level of pain in the following days compared with group 2 (P < 0.05), except the fifth day. In those without, the length of the scar on average was 5.43 ± 0.44 cm, which was longer than the average 3.61 ± 0.29 cm in group 1 (P < 0.05). Analysis of VSS results showed a better formation of the scar in the group1 (P < 0.05). The differences in postoperative pain, scar length and quality between the two groups were statistically significant(P < 0.05). There were no differences between the two groups in terms of the operation time and the amount of bleeding during the operation (P > 0.05). The application of lap-protector in costal cartilage harvest can optimize the scar formation and reduce postoperative pain without prolonging the operative time, which is a convenient and effective technique for achieving satisfactory results.

Introduction

Microtia is a congenital condition that refers to a spectrum of congenital morphological malformation of the auricular that ranges from a small but otherwise well-formed ear to a complete lack of external ear1. As with other craniofacial developmental malformation, microtia patients may have the attendant psychosocial concerns. The significant facial difference may lead to excessive attention, ridicule, and mockery of their surroundings, causing varied degrees of psychological impact and problems2, especially for the children facing the challenge of development of physical and mental health. Auricular reconstruction can help these children feel more confident to integrate into the surrounding environment. Cartilage grafts are well-known as being reliable in surgery for auricular reconstruction3. In the classical total auricular reconstruction techniques, two or three intact costal cartilage grafts should be harvested, and the postoperative scar of the donor-site represents an important issue. The excessive length and poor quality of scar not only increase the risk of postoperative pain, infection, wound dehiscence, and scar hyperplasia, but also aggravate the psychological burden of the children to some extent. Limited incisions are becoming an increasingly important factor to increase patients’ satisfaction. Previous studies reporting the small-incision technique significantly reduces postoperative thoracic deformities and results in smaller, less conspicuous scars, trying to define the small-incision could reduce donor-site morbidity, offering improved patient outcomes4,5. However, a notable limitation of existing research lies in the lack of supportive auxiliary technical equipment for this technique.

Lap protectors are mostly used in minimally invasive thoracic and abdominal surgery. They were initially designed to reduce tumor spread and incision contamination caused by infectious lesions6,7. Studies have found that wounds heal more smoothly after application8,9. In plastic surgery, after adaptive modification, lap protectors are widely used in breast-related endoscopic surgery, liposuction and fat grafting10, etc., and have achieved remarkable effects in incision protection. However, there have been no reports on their application in costal cartilage harvesting. The motivation of the present study is to establish a minimally invasive surgical method that incorporates the use of a lap protector—one that is effective for costal cartilage harvesting while ensuring the formation of minimally unaesthetic surgical scars.

Materials and methods

Patients with unilateral microtia from grade II to III11, aged above 6 years (with height above 120 cm and chest circumference at the xiphoid level over 55 cm), who had the right 6th, 7th, and 8th cartilages harvested by the designated surgery team for auricular reconstruction were included in the study. Patients with the following criteria were excluded from research subjects: medical history that could interfere with the outcome of the donor (e.g. costal cartilage calcification, previous thorax trauma, and/or prior thorax surgical intervention), partial reconstruction (less than three cartilages), other reconstruction method with temporoparietal fascial flap(skin grafting from the chest donor), and/or incomplete follow-up (< 6 months).

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, patients who had undergone routine follow-up at least six months postoperatively included in the study between January to March 2023. In previous surgical practices, the lap protector has been a conventional accessory for patients undergoing surgery. Surgeons possess substantial familiarity with the operational procedures associated with its application. Preoperatively, patients retain the autonomy to make decisions regarding the use of the lap protector based on their individual preferences. The patients were divided into two groups based on whether the lap-protector was used or not, according to the medical records. For this study, a cohort of 55 patients were selected who underwent costal cartilage harvesting for auricular reconstruction, 25 patients underwent cartilage harvest through minimal-incision (group 1) accompanied lap-protector, and 30 patients underwent the surgery without protector (group 2). Patients’ data including medical records regarding age, height, weight, and details about the rib cartilage harvested procedure were collected.

Ethical considerations

The details of the study were explained to the participants as well as their families and their written consent were obtained during the follow-up period. For patients under 18 years of age, informed consent was obtained from their legal guardians (or parents). This study obtained ethical approval (Serial number auditing: 2024[208]) from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Clinical Research (Plastic Surgery Hospital of CAMS & PUMC) and was conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Operative techniques

Auricular reconstruction was performed on patients by a single plastic surgeon and team, as a three-stage procedure with the use of an autologous rib cartilage framework under general anesthesia. The first stage is tissue expander implantation to create a skin pocket, the second stage is auricular reconstruction using rib cartilage, followed by trimming of the reconstructed ear at the third stage one year later.

On the stage two, the patient was under general anesthesia. The position and shape of the 6th, 7th, and 8th costal cartilage on the right side were confirmed by palpation and marked. The donor-site was infiltrated with a solution of lidocaine 0.5% and epinephrine 1:200000. Then a 3.5 cm oblique skin incision was made paralleling the 7th costal margin on the right. A No.20 blade was used to incise the subcutaneous and connective tissue to reach the rectus muscle plane. In group 2, the incision was extended by a hook retractor and retracted as gently as possible. In group 1, besides using the conventional retractor, a height-adjustable lap-protector (Fig. 1(A)) was attached which consists of two flexible rings made of super elasticity alloys covered with polyurethane polyamide, and a thin silicone rubber membrane that is attached to the outer rim of the two rings. The surgeon grabbed the bottom ring and squeezed it to an oval shape. The transformed bottom ring was pushed slowly through the incision so that it slides along the surface just beneath the chest wall. Finally, the bottom ring is snapped into the chest wall cavity, in such a way that the top ring placed around the outside and bottom ring placed inside form a “sandwich” to protect the full-thickness wound (Fig. 1(B)). The rectus muscle was dissected along its intermediate line, and then the costal cartilages exposed. The overlying perichondrium was incised longitudinally along the upper margin line and lower margin line of the costal cartilage axis. Using a cartilage stripper to harvest the whole rib beneath the perichondrium, with the costochondral junction and the inner perichondrium preserved. In this way, the right 6th, 7th, and 8th intact costal cartilages were harvested sequentially (Fig. 1(C)). After hemostasis by bipolar coagulation, a saline test was used to exclude a pleural leak. Following harvest of the cartilage harvest, ropivacaine 0.3% and lidocaine 0.5% were administered for the intercostal nerve block. Upon completion of the harvest procedures, the bottom ring was taken out of the cavity by squeezing part of the bottom ring by forefinger and pulled out slowly through the incision. Finally, the wound was then closed in layers including the rectus muscle, subcutaneous tissue, and skin with 3 − 0, 3 − 0 and 5 − 0 absorbable sutures, respectively. The framework we fabricated using the rib cartilages according to the X-ray film template fashioned using the contralateral as a model prepared preoperative (Fig. 1(D)).

The “single expanded flap” technique was performed in all patients. The incision was made along the posterior of the skin on the edge of the expander, then the 100 ml kidney-shaped expander was removed from the expanded pocket. An expanded skin flap with an anterior pedicle was developed. The expander capsule and thickened skin flap along the edge of the expander were removed carefully. A “V”-shaped lobule incision was made on the remanent ear, the lobule flap rotated posteriorly and inferiorly according to the mark made by the position of the contralateral earlobe. After achieved adequate hemostasis, the framework was totally inserted into the expanded flap and placed in an appropriate position. The top of the lobule flap was divided into two pieces to wrap the bottom of the framework, which sutured with the expanded flap. After skin closure, the anatomical details of the framework were immediately evident with suction (Fig. 1(E)). Antibiotics were given at induction and for the first day postoperative. The drain was removed at the 5th day postoperative. An elastic chest strap was fitted at the donor site postoperatively.

Follow-up

Patients were followed up for a 6-month period. The operation time, the amount of blood loss and the visual analogue pain score ( scored using a standardized visual analogue pain score chart [VAS]12 on a 10-point scale at intervals of 1 day from the first day to the fifth day during their inpatient stay) were collected retrospectively from the medical records. Patients’ scars were assessed clinically in outpatient by one plastic surgeon 6 months postoperative, including the quality and length. The Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS)13 was used to evaluate the entity of the postoperative scarring phenomena, which encapsulates multiple factors, including pigmentation, height, pliability, and vascularity to give a numerical reflection of scar quality. This scale total ranges from 0(representing normal skin) to a maximum score of 15(representing worst scar imaginable). Any unfavorable event within 6 months from surgery was recorded as a perioperative complication. The assessment of the quality of the donor-site is performed by only one clinician to minimize observer variability.

Statistical analysis

Version 27.0 of the SPSS statistical software package (IBM, New York) was used to perform all statistical analyses. Continuous variables were represented by mean±standard deviation(SD), and classified variables were represented by number of cases and percentage. The chi-square test was used to compare the count data to check whether the two group were balanced, and P value greater than 0.05 indicates that the groups were comparable. A paired T-test was applied to compare the differences between the two groups, and the value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic of Patient characteristics

A total of 55 unilateral microtia patients were recruited in the study. Among them, 25 patients with lap-protector in group 1 and 30 patients without in group 2. In group 1 the cohort was composed of 9 females and 16 males, the average age was 8.32(SD ± 1.28) years, the average BMI was 19.90(SD ± 1.22) kg/m2. Among them were 4 patients suffering from microtia grade II, 21 patients from microtia grade III, and 6 cases were left side affected, 19 were right side. In the group 2, the cohort was composed of 10 females and 20 males, the average age was 8.02(SD ± 1.12), the average BMI was 20.12 (SD ± 1.13) kg/m2. Among them were 3 patients suffering from microtia grade II, 27 patients from microtia grade III and 9 cases were left side affected and 21 were right side. There was no significance between the two groups regarding the general clinical information (Table 1) (all P > 0.05), and both groups were comparable in sex, age, BMI, affected sides, and grades.

Comparison of surgery

The operation procedure performed successfully in all patients. Attachment and removal of the lap-protector device were easily carried out in group 1, and no adverse events were observed such as broken, fracture, retained foreign bodies, and tissue incarcerated in both groups. The average time of surgery in group 1 was 152.20(SD ± 13.55) minutes, while in group 2 was 148.33(SD ± 16.94) minutes. The amount of the blood loss in group 1 was 12.13(SD ± 1.37)ml, while in group 2 was 15.87(SD ± 1.28)ml. There was no difference between the two groups in terms of the operation time and the amount of bleeding during the operation (all P > 0.05), which is reported in Table 1.

No pneumothorax was observed post-operative, and no signs of wound dehiscence or infection were observed during the follow-up in two groups. With the reference to post-operative hematoma, only one patient in group 2 developed a subcutaneous hematoma, representing the decrease in the degree of hemoglobin, which was cured spontaneously after being treated conservatively.

Pain and scar evaluation

At 1,2,3,4, and 5 days after surgery, the VAS scores of the group 1 were 2.16(SD ± 0.62)、1.32༈SD ± 0.48༉、0.96༈SD ± 0.20༉、0.52༈SD ± 0.51༉、0.24༈SD ± 0.44༉of 10, respectively, and those in the group 2 were3.60༈SD ± 0.89༉、2.43༈SD ± 0.82༉、1.80༈SD ± 0.61༉、1.23༈SD ± 0.43༉、0.37༈SD ± 0.49༉of 10, respectively. Compared with group 2, the lap-protector group had a lower level of donor-site pain༈P < 0.05༉in the following days except the fifth day (P > 0.05), and the scores of pain dropped steadily over the 5 days in two groups, which is displayed in Fig. 2.

All patients’ scars get healing by first intention. The length of the scar on average was 3.61(SD ± 0.29)cm in group1 shorter than the average 5.43༈SD ± 0.44༉cm compared to group 2 after 6 months post-operation. Analysis of VSS results showed a better quality of the scar in the group with wound protector 1.16༈SD ± 0.55༉, which is compared to group without 2.50༈SD ± 0.90༉. The difference in postoperative pain, scar length and quality between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

All patients were satisfied with the final appearance of the donor-site scar, which had good shape, as well as appropriate color, texture, and length. The majority of patients noticed a difference in scar color, thickness and stiffness, which diminished over time. None of the patients had keloid formation. Clinical examples of good and less-than ideal donor sites are shown in Fig. 3(A) and Fig. 3(B).

To assess the adequacy of the sample size, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1. For the comparison of scar length between groups, the standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) was 4.80. With a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and sample sizes of 25 and 30, the achieved statistical power was 100%, which far exceeded the conventional threshold of 80%, indicating that the sample size was sufficient.

Discussion

The use of autogenous rib cartilage continues to be the mainstream approach for auricular reconstruction14. With the improvements in the technique of auricular reconstruction, the reconstructed auricular can meet high aesthetic requirements in morphology, asymmetry, and coordination characteristics. While the recovery of the donor-sites arouses insufficient awareness of surgeons after costal cartilage harvest. Achieving adequate surgical exposure is fundamental to harvest adequate rib cartilage, which has been accomplished by extending the incision length. The excessive length may increase the risk of complications as well as impairment of aesthetic outcomes. Ching et al.15 proposed an innovative technique that harvesting costal cartilage through transumbilical single-incision laparoscopic for rhinoplasty surgery. The superiority of the technique is leaving no scars on the skin of the chest wall, which is beneficial to patients with higher aesthetic requirements. However, the operative time of the technique lasts between 2 and 2.5 h, which is much longer than the traditional procedure about 30 min. Apart from, operation through endoscopic in rib cartilage harvest in auricular reconstruction facing the challenges like surgical difficulties, relatively long operation time, and additional cost16, due to the large demand of cartilage for grafting. The technique of costal cartilage harvest through laparoscopic still has lots to be refined before widely applied in ear construction clinical practice.

For the rib harvest site cosmetic outcome, efforts must be made to keep the scar as short as possible without compromising the harvest procedure itself. The minimally invasive costal cartilage harvesting incision is used in clinical practice17. The incision is placed slightly obliquely to allow maximum access and to allow skin tension lines to be followed. Retraction of the window created by the skin incision can allow exposure a large piece of cartilage for harvest. Achieving adequate surgical exposure for good surgical practice in minimal-invasive incision has been accomplished with the aid of self-retaining retractors performing strong traction force on the wound edges. To maintain a functional subcutaneous cavity by applying outwardly directed force on the roof to overcome the weight and elastic recoil of the superficial tissue layers, so as to widen the operation space. The force produced by the retractor directed in a certain direction is limited in surgical exposure, leading to greater and uneven force. Skin congestion and necrosis on the wound margin are more likely to occur after excessive outward force and repeated friction on the regional margin, resulting in delayed wound healing, widening, or hypertrophic scarring. In the results of our study, although the initial incision was 3.5 cm, the postoperative wound length increased to 5.43 (SD ± 0.44) cm in the group without lap-protector. A rational for wound length change could be the presence of skin edge trimming for better healing. The wound hyperplasia can also lead to visible lengthening at both ends of the wound. Additionally, to expose the surgical field, it may be necessary to dissect the subcutaneous tissue around the incision, which can cause additional potential damage. During the healing process, scarring may also form in these areas of potential damage. Overall, these multiple factors comprehensively result in the wound being longer than the original incision. Since this is a retrospective study, there is no relevant documentation in the medical records regarding whether the wound was extended during the operation, but this possibility cannot be completely ruled out. A lap-protector device was invented which is an instrument used to keep an incision open and protect the wound margin from infection during endoscopic surgery or open abdominal procedures18. It provides a relatively wide, circular opening in incision by the tension of the silicon rubber. It is easy to manipulate with minimal tissue damage because force is distributed equally around the incision margin due to its high elasticity and the cylindrical structure. It can provide a physical impervious barrier to liquid or solid materiel, protecting wound edges from trauma, soiling and bacterial contamination. The insulating material of the protector can protect the incision from diathermy damage caused accidentally. In addition, the compression provided by wound protectors results in less diathermy use by improved haemostasia. Compared to the application in abdominal surgery and endoscopic surgery, it is more suitable in operation through minimal incision.

The most common complaint after costal cartilage harvest in auricular reconstruction is donor-site pain19. Pain control is an important issue for patients following cartilage harvest, and this is especially for children. Postoperative pain may restrict the chest expansion when breathing and affect early mobility, inducing atelectasis, pneumonia, and possibly venous thrombosis. According to the literatures, all the patients reported sensible pain at the donor site, which reported chest wall pain peaked at 7 days after surgery and diminished slowly over 3 months20. Our study shows post operative pain was the most severe on day 1 in group with lap-protector, at 2.16 out of 10 VAS scale, and decreased steadily over time. On postoperative day 5, most patients reported no significant pain which interfered with daily activities. Compared to the conventional technique of costal cartilage harvest, our study shows lower level of pain with shorter pain duration which may be due to the use of minimally invasive technique. In addition, the use of lap-protector in minimally invasive technique resulted in a reduction of pain in the wound. In conjunction with a local analgesic (including intercostal nerve block and topical anesthesia around surgical area), the technique could reduce post-operative donor pain utmost, decreasing complications and producing faster healing4.

Traditionally, the surgical procedure is represented by an extended incision of 5.4–9.0 cm, due to the necessity of harvesting the intact 2–3 costal cartilage segment, as in auricular reconstruction21. For this reason, residual scarring is one of the major patients’ cosmetic concerns. Although most patients find it a price worth paying to reconstruct an ear. The aesthetic outcome of the wound is a key point to improve patients’ satisfaction. The mean incision length from this study was one-third that of conventional costal cartilage harvest procedure, considering the smaller incisions and minimal invasive maneuvers. The findings suggest that lap-protector could minimize the size and optimize the quality of scar without compromising the operation time in harvesting costal cartilage through minimally incision, enhancing patients’ satisfaction. This is probably explained by the protector converting wounds to an ellipse and retractor converting wounds to a rhomboid, which is verified in the previously published article mathematically22.

According to our clinical experience, we concluded that the subcutaneous fat layer of the morbidly obese patient group could provide a significant barrier to accessing muscle layers of the chest wall and compromise the space of the surgical exposure, which increase the difficulties in dissection and hemostasis, and prolong the operation time. With the use of the lap-protector, the depth of the operative site is reduced, especially for the thicker chest wall23.A possible explanation for this would be due to the thicker chest wall having an increased subcutaneous fat layer and being more responsive to compressive force applied from the lap-protectors. Lap-protector application could shorten the operation time and protect the wound from fat liquefaction due to local tissue ischemia as well as excessive traction force in obesity patients, empirically. In terms of key demographic and clinical characteristics, there were no significant differences between the two groups that could influence the outcomes including the BMI. Therefore, this conclusion cannot be drawn from our study, and further research will be needed to verify it in the future.

The height-adjustable lap-protector used in our center may change its silicone rubber membrane length due to the varying thickness of the chest wall. Attachment and removal of the lap-protector device through minimal incision were easily carried, providing superior surgical exposure for a given incision length compared to traditional techniques. In our center, the procedure of costal cartilage harvest is performed by a surgical team, while the procedure of expander removal is performed by another surgical team simultaneously. It reduces the risk of the surgical area coming into contact with other tissues, protecting surgical wounds from contamination, thus preventing infection. After removing the protector, the edge of the incision was smooth without any signs of damage, which is more conducive for suturing. Wound protectors have the ability in reducing the incision size and subsequent scar formation compared to the conventional technique. The resulting shorter scar is greatly appreciated both by patients and surgeons, as it is demonstrated by the results of the VAS.

Although the application of the lap-protector incurs additional costs, considering that the patients undergoing auricular reconstruction surgery are predominantly young, achieving a smaller and less conspicuous incision is deemed worthy of further exploration. Moreover, suboptimal wound healing may lead to extra medical expenditures related to scar revision. Notably, all participating patients’ parents reported satisfaction with the surgical outcomes postoperatively.

Despite promising results, this study had limitations, including small sample size and the relatively short follow-up. Although the assessment of the quality of the donor-site is performed by only one clinician to minimize observer variability, the same cannot be said for the pain scores. The patients’ reports of pain are subjective and prone to variability according to the threshold at which they are likely to flag problems. Another possible bias consists in the fact that the surgeon knew he was using the device in this prospective study. There may also be a degree of selection bias in the study.

Conclusion

This was the first study to investigate the lap-protector in costal cartilage harvest in microtia patients following auricular reconstruction, embodying the principle of minimal invasiveness in plastic surgery. The enrolled patients had relatively high compliance, as we required them to wear a chest strap regularly for 6 months after surgery. Therefore, there is a bias regarding whether the enrolled patients can represent all microtia patients. This study has shown that the use of lap-protector has proven useful in reducing the length of the scar and improving aesthetic outcomes in harvesting costal cartilage through minimally incision in auricular reconstruction. This device also provides a greater view of the surgical field in a limited incision, facilitating dissection and hemostasis without prolonging the operative time, which has potential clinical value. Further studies on the effectiveness of this device in large population is warranted. Meanwhile, it is also worth noting the implications of the device in patient population subgroups such as the morbidly obese, and a further investigation on considering the use of wound protectors when performing operating in this patient population group would be suggested.
