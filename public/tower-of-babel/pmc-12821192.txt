Determinants of healthcare access among women aged 15–49 years in tanzania: a population-based cross-sectional survey
Authors: Pankras Luoga, Alphoncina Kagaigai, Amani Anaeli, Malale Tungu, Jovinary Adam, Augustus Osborne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, access to healthcare among women of reproductive age is among top most health agenda. In line with the endeavor, the government of Tanzania strive to ensure healthcare is accessible to every community member particularly women of reproductive age (15–49 years). However, there are limited scientific evidence on the proportion of women aged 15–49 who can access and the determinants of healthcare services in Tanzania. This study intended to fill the gap.MethodsIn this study, we analysed secondary information of a weighted sample 15,254 women aged 15–49 years collected using cross-sectional design during the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey(TDHS). The binary dependent variable was healthcare access status and independent variables were women’s social demographical characteristics. Descriptive analysis and multivariable modified Poisson regression analysis were performed. The p-value < 0.05 at 95% was set to determine the significant determinant.ResultsThe proportion of women with access to healthcare in Tanzania, stand at 50.2%. After controlling for other covariates, the modified Poisson regression showed that women with secondary and higher education level (Adjusted Prevalence Ratio (APR);1.21,95%CI:1.12,1.30), women who are covered by health insurance (APR;1.18,95%CI:1.11,1.26), belonging to richest households (APR;1.62,95%CI:1.45,1.80), residing in southern zone (APR;1.32,95%CI:1.22,1.42) and women living in union (APR;1.10,95%C:1.05,1.16); had higher prevalence ratio of healthcare access compared to their counter parts. “Conversely, negative determinants of healthcare access included being aged 35–49 years (APR; 0.92; 95% CI: 0.88, 0.97), living in households with 5–6 members, and residing in rural areas (APR; 0.78; 95% CI: 0.73, 0.83). These groups had a lower prevalence of healthcare access compared to their counterparts.ConclusionOnly half of Tanzanian women of reproductive age had adequate healthcare access. Its positive determinants included having secondary and above education level, being covered by health insurance, belonging to richest households, residing in southern and coast zones and living in union. Negative determinants included residing in rural, coming from poor households and having no formal education. As a motive toward universal health coverage in Tanzania, the government should design targeted health interventions focusing on poor women in rural areas with low levels of education to improve the healthcare access for all.

Background

Access to healthcare is a fundamental component of achieving optimal health outcomes and a key indicator of progress toward universal health coverage worldwide. Despite notable advances in healthcare delivery over recent decades, substantial disparities in access persist, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1–4]. Globally, access to healthcare has been a major challenge. The burden is disproportionally affecting the global, with developing world experiencing more negative effects. The developing world reported to have lower proportion of its population with access to healthcare due to various reasons including economic issues, low health insurance coverage, distance to the health facility, means of transportation to the health facility and decision making dynamics [1, 2, 4, 5].

Nowhere are these challenges more pronounced than in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), access to healthcare is reported to be a major public concern [2, 6]. Previous studies reported the determinants of healthcare access in the region to include distance, health insurance status, wealth index [5, 7]. These barriers contribute to persistent inequities in healthcare utilization, with women particularly those of reproductive age often experiencing the greatest disadvantages.

In Tanzania, the government has shown strong commitment to improving healthcare access through the implementation of comprehensive policies, such as the Fifth Health Sector Strategic Plan (2021–2026), which prioritizes UHC and targets vulnerable populations, including women of reproductive age [3]. Women in this demographic are especially important to consider, as they are the primary users of health services due to their reproductive roles and broader responsibilities in family and community health. However, despite these policy efforts, there remains a paucity of nationally representative data on the proportion of women aged 15–49 years who can access healthcare and the specific determinants influencing their access. To address this critical knowledge gap, the present study aims to [1] estimate the national proportion of women of reproductive age in Tanzania with access to healthcare and [2] identify the key sociodemographic and contextual determinants associated with healthcare access in this population. By providing robust, up-to-date evidence, this study seeks to inform targeted interventions and policy strategies to advance equitable healthcare access for women in Tanzania and similar settings.

Theoretical framework for understanding the determinants of healthcare access in Tanzania

Understanding the determinants of the healthcare access among women in Tanzania is complex and requires a comprehensive approach that take into consideration multiple and interconnected factors. Therefore, this study used the DFPR Healthcare Access Framework, which emphasizes four major barriers to seeking healthcare: Distance to facility (D), Financial constraints (F), Permission to seek care (P), and Reluctance to go alone (R). The DFPR dimensions reflect geographic, economic, sociocultural, and psychosocial determinants of service access, utilization and aligns to the global published frameworks namely the Three Delays and Penchansky & Thomas frameworks, to assess barriers to health service access and utilization [2, 8]. Based on three delay framework by Thaddeus; the first delay occurs in decision making including getting permission and being reluctance to go alone. Second delay occurs in reaching health facility due to distance issues, remote and road infrastructures issues mostly found in rural areas and related to transport cost [2]. Moreover, based on Penchansky, barriers to healthcare access occur in availability reflected in distance to health facilities, affordability in terms of financial constraints and acceptability reflected in getting permission and being reluctance to go alone [8]. Though the relationship is nuance as some of these component may be described by independent variables like health insurance and residing in rural areas that may influence healthcare access [7, 9].

Methods

Study design and study population

In this study, we analysed secondary information of a weighted sample of 15,254 women aged 15–49 years collected using cross-sectional design drawn from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS). The data were collected from both residents and visitors who had spent the night in the household prior to the day of the survey. The survey conducted by National Bureau Statistics (NBS) funded by (United States Agency for International Development) USAID under technical support of Inner City Fund (ICF). The survey uses two stage stratified sampling technique to ensure national representativeness. In the first stage, the country was divided into geographic regions, which were further stratified into urban and rural areas. Within each stratum, enumeration areas (EAs) from the national census frame were selected using probability proportional to size (PPS) based on 2012 census [10], then followed by selection of the clusters from each strata and listing the households in the selected clusters. In the second stage, involved selection of households to be interviewed during the survey. The survey collected demographic and health indicators, including socio-demographic characteristics of women, healthcare access, and many others. The focus of this study was on the indicator of healthcare access among women of reproductive age of 15–49 years. In addition, in this study, the unit of analysis was a woman. Further methodological procedures used in the survey are detailed in the final report of the 2022 TDHS [11].

Variables included in the study

Dependent variable

The outcome variable, healthcare access, was constructed as a composite indicator based on four components: [1] distance to health facility [2], financial constraints [3], permission to seek care, and [4] reluctance to go alone. Each component was coded as a binary variable (0 = not a problem, 1 = a big problem), and a woman reporting no problems across all four components, was classified as having access. Any woman who responded to have a big problem in any of the four components, was classified as having a problem in accessing healthcare services. Using the developed composite variable of healthcare access, we coded 0 = having no access and 1 = having access. Each response on specific component was given equal weight and this approach follows established DHS methodology [12].

Independent variables

Age (15–24, 25–34, 35–49 years), type of place of residence(v025) (urban/rural), Education(v106) (no formal education, primary, secondary and higher), Marital status (in union/not in union), Wealth status(v190a) (Poorest, Poorer, Middle, Richer, Richest), Geographical zones (combine the regions into five based on previous studies [13, 14]; Lake zones, Northern zone, Central zone, Southern and Coast zone), current working status (no/yes), Exposure to mass media (combined radio, TV and newspapers) (no access at all, less than once a week and at least once a week), covered by Health insurance (v481) (no/yes), Sex of household head (male/female), Age of husband/partner (15–24, 25–34, 35–49 and 50 + years), and Education of husband/partner (no formal education, primary, secondary and higher),. The variable were selected based on literature on the similar topic [7, 9, 15, 16].

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted to present the distribution of participants in terms of frequency and percentages. Inferential analyses were conducted using chi-square test to determine the association between independent and dependent variables. However, before entering the independent variables in the final multivariable model, we tested for multi-collinearity and using a pairwise correlation with a cut-off point of 0.5 or higher to indicate collinearity. Using the output obtained, four independent variables (marital status, age of the woman, age of husband/partner and education level of husband/partner) showed multi-collinearity. We retained the woman’s age and marital status based on existing literature, their relevance to the current study, and their documented importance in influencing healthcare access among women in Tanzania and the broader African context [17]. In addition, we performed a multivariable modified Poisson regression model to determine the magnitude of the association between independent and dependent variables. The p-value < 0.05 at 95% was used to determine the significant determinant. The outputs were measured using the Crude Prevalence Ratio(CPR) and the Adjusted Prevalence Ratio (APR). All data were weighted using individual women weight (v005/1000000) to account for complex survey design and non-response rate. In addition, this provided a national representative estimate by correcting for overestimation and underestimation that occurred during data collection. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 18.

Discussion

The study aimed at determining the proportion and determinants of healthcare access among women aged 15–49 in Tanzania. Our analysis showed that 50.2%% of the Tanzanian women had access to healthcare. The observed proportion.

In summary, the significant determinants of healthcare access included having secondary and above education level, health insurance coverage, belonging to richest households, residing in southern and coast zones and living in union. Conversely, negative determinants of healthcare access included being aged 35–49 years, having a household size of 5–6 members and residing in rural areas.

Having secondary and above education level. Potential reasons may be due to information that raises awareness on importance of healthcare that is obtained in course of acquiring education [18]. This finding is consistent to the findings reported elsewhere [5, 7]. Educated women are more likely to be employed or occupy advantageous socio-economic positions, enabling them to pay user fees or insurance premiums, and thereby improving their ability to access health services. Notably, educated women tend to be more informed about the importance of seeking care, particularly for reproductive health services an awareness that may promote positive health seeking behaviors [19]. Educated individuals are also more likely to access accurate health information, which helps them avoid misconceptions and leads to greater utilization of health services.

Being covered by Health insurance had higher prevalence ratio of healthcare access. This may be due to fact that having insurance may obscure other hindrances including money that needs to be paid in turn of accessing healthcare services. This finding is similar to the findings reported in other scholarly works [20]. Women covered by health insurance in many countries across the Global South including Tanzania have an advantage in accessing health services compared to those who are not insured [13, 20–23]. Both in theory and in practice, being insured increases service utilisation [13] by removing one of the most critical barriers of affordability by removing the need to pay out of pocket [24]. This underscores the need to ensure that the majority of women and their households are enrolled in one of the available prepayment mechanisms, in order to facilitate smooth and equitable access to comprehensive health services.

Our analysis showed that women belonging to richest households had higher prevalence ratio of healthcare access compared to those belonging to poorest households. This could potential mean that richest households can support the women in terms of having insurance, money for transportation as well as for healthcare services. Similar findings are reported elsewhere where by coming from rich households reduced poor access to healthcare and increased access to healthcare among respondents [7, 20]. The willingness and actual utilization of health services are higher when a household has the ability to pay for those services. In practice, wealthier households often provide women with greater freedom to make their own decisions, particularly regarding their reproductive health. This increased autonomy subsequently leads to more frequent visits to health facilities, especially for reproductive health services.

Furthermore, our analysis showed that women residing in southern zone had higher prevalence ratio of access to healthcare than women residing in Lake zone. These geographical zones have been used by other studies [13], however, the relationship reported in this finding is not clearly established. We recommend more studies on this to establish clear relationship between residing in Southern zone and healthcare access. There could be several historical and contemporary explanations for this result. Historically, women residing in the Southern zones are predominantly from matrilineal societies, where they exercise significant freedom, including getting cooperation from their spouses and control over their reproductive health reflected in consistently higher utilization of family planning services [25, 26]. Women’s freedom, getting cooperation from their male partners and having control over their reproductive health issues may be associated with increased healthcare access. In contrast, women from the Lake Zone are subjected to patrilineal norms, where women have limited autonomy over their reproductive health, a situation that may hinder their access to health facilities. These findings reflect the need for women’s empowerment, health utilization, and deconstructing the cultural barriers which are harmful to women health across the country to enhance utilisation of health services among women. Empowered women can break the cultural barriers and subsequently improve power dynamics which in turn will have provident decisions and control over their reproductive health together with entire household [19].

Moreover, women living in union had higher prevalence ratio of healthcare access than women who are not in union. Previous studies on women and health service utilization have reported that married women are more likely to utilize healthcare services compared to unmarried women [13, 27]. This may be due to the fact that married women are more likely to require reproductive health services, given their higher chances of becoming pregnant. It is also worth noting that married women often have wider social networks, which can provide financial and emotional support, thereby improving their access to healthcare services during their reproductive years.

Women coming from a household with more than 5 members had reduced healthcare access compared to their counterparts coming from a household with four or less members. This could be due to women coming larger households may face greater financial or decision-making constraints that reduce their capacity to insure all members, which in turn limits access to formal care [28]. In addition, in Tanzania, even when individuals are covered by health insurance whether through the improved Community Health Fund (iCHF) or the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) both schemes cover medical costs for no more than six household members [29, 30]. This suggests that a woman living in a household with more than six members may face additional barriers to healthcare access, as some members potentially including herself may not be covered by the household’s health insurance. Similar findings are reported in other studies [13, 28].

Our findings indicated that on the significant determinants of the healthcare access among women in Tanzania included having secondary and above education level, health insurance coverage, belonging to richest households, residing in southern and coast zones and living in union. Those factors associated with reduced healthcare access included being aged 35–49 years, having a household size of 5–6 members and residing in rural areas. The factors show to be complex and interconnected relationship in nature that align with the DFPR framework as well as the global published frameworks namely the Three Delays and Penchansky & Thomas frameworks [2, 8]. The reported factors such as residing in rural areas to be associated with reduced healthcare access reflects accessibility issues that hinder women in accessing healthcare services, on the other side being educated and belonging to the richest economic households reflect awareness and financial issues respectively as facilitators of healthcare access among the study population [2, 8, 31].

Policy implications

To address the persistent disparities in healthcare access among women of reproductive age in Tanzania, policymakers should prioritize the expansion of universal health coverage by increasing health insurance enrollment, particularly among poor and rural populations. Efforts should also focus on improving access to quality education for girls and women, as higher educational attainment is closely linked to better healthcare access. Additionally, targeted investments are needed to strengthen healthcare infrastructure and transportation in underserved rural and low-access regions, while health promotion campaigns utilizing mass media and community outreach can empower women with information and encourage healthcare-seeking behavior. By implementing these integrated strategies, Tanzania can make meaningful progress toward achieving equitable and universal healthcare access for all women.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample drawn from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey, ensuring that the findings are generalizable to women of reproductive age across the country. The use of a validated and internationally recognized data collection tools (the DHS questionnaires) enhances the reliability and comparability of the results. Furthermore, the study employed robust statistical methods, including modified Poisson regression, to accurately estimate prevalence ratios and control for potential confounders. The rigorous analytical approach strengthens the validity of the identified associations between sociodemographic factors and healthcare access. The primary limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified determinants and healthcare access. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported measures may introduce recall or social desirability bias. We also acknowledge the limitation of using the composite variable of healthcare access developed using four components of distance, financial, permission to go to access healthcare and not wanting to go alone. Some of these components may overlap with independent variables such as financial component of healthcare access and the independent variables of health insurance and wealth quantile. Some potentially important factors, such as cultural beliefs or quality of care, were not captured in the dataset and could not be assessed. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the factors influencing healthcare access among Tanzanian women.

Conclusion

This study found that only half of Tanzanian women of reproductive age reported adequate access to healthcare. Key determinants of improved access included attaining secondary or higher education, having health insurance coverage, belonging to the richest wealth quintile, residing in the southern and coastal zones, and being married or in union. Conversely, rural residence, lower educational attainment, and lower household wealth were associated with reduced access. These findings underscore persistent inequalities in healthcare access and highlight the need for targeted interventions to address these disparities. The focused interventions to is recommended. Such tailored interventions on education improvement, increase health insurance, rural development strategies and wealth creation among communities. For instance, the use of TASAF programmes targeting poor individuals to boost their livelihood and provide education on importance of accessing healthcare services including attending regular checkups. These measures are important and can significantly increase the proportion of women who access healthcare services in Tanzania. Further research using a qualitative approach to explore underlying reasons hindering healthcare access among women in Tanzania is recommended.
