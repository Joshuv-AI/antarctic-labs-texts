Vascular hydraulic conductivity imaging in cancers in vivo based on spatiotemporal ultrasound elastography
Authors: Sharmin Majumder, Md Hadiur Rahman Khan, Ying Xuan Chua, Francesca Taraballi, Raffaella Righetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular hydraulic conductivity (Lp) plays important roles in cancer metastasis, progression, and treatments. To date, there are a few noninvasive methods to image Lp in cancers, and these methods rely on several assumptions. Common assumptions include that vascular conductivity is dominant over interstitial conductivity inside the tumor and that interstitial effects in the background are dominant over interstitial effects inside the tumor, limiting their applicability to cancers with a narrow range of Lp. In this article, we propose a new method to image a wide range of Lp in cancers using ultrasound spatiotemporal elastography data, without the limitations of existing methods. The method is based on the knowledge of Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, and volumetric strain. This method shows superior performance with respect to the previous methods in terms of percent-relative-error in simulation studies. In vivo experimental results in an orthotopic mouse model of breast cancer show that Lp estimated by ultrasound imaging using the proposed method is highly correlated with histological CD31 data. The proposed imaging methods can thus provide clinically significant information noninvasively and cost-effectively.

Transport across tumor vessel walls occurs via convection and diffusion, with most imaging methods estimating vascular permeability due to diffusion. However, few noninvasive techniques exist for imaging vascular hydraulic conductivity (Lp) in cancers due to convection, and those are plagued by several assumptions, significantly limiting their range of application. This article introduces a new noninvasive ultrasound spatiotemporal elasticity imaging method to accurately image Lp in tumors and normal tissues without these limitations. In vivo results on an orthotopic mouse model of breast cancer demonstrate a strong correlation between the ultrasound-estimated Lp and vessel density computed from CD31 staining, a histopathological marker of tumor vascularization. This method thus could provide a noninvasive and cost-effective tool for clinically obtaining histological-correlated cancer information.

Introduction

Pathological conditions disrupt the structural integrity and permeability of tissues, affecting both mechanical function and fluid transport (1, 2). At length scales comparable to the tumor radius (millimeters), microscale heterogeneities—such as those at the intercapillary distance (micrometers)—can be averaged, and a tissue may be modeled as a continuum composed of interstitial matrix, cells and microvascular and lymphatic networks (3). Cancers often lack the well-defined, functional lymphatic vessels present in normal tissues. Cancer's fluid transport mechanisms can be divided into three main subcompartments: vascular, cellular, and interstitial (4, 5). These transport processes may involve convective mechanisms or a combination of convection and diffusion (3). Convection is an active transport process that occurs due to the fluid pressure gradient, following Darcy's law for interstitial flow through a porous medium and Starling's law for volumetric flow rate out of the vasculature, whereas diffusion occurs due to concentration gradients of macromolecules. The vascular parameter related to convection process is the vascular hydraulic permeability or conductivity (measured in units of [m/Pa.s]). The parameter related to diffusion is the vascular permeability coefficient (measured in units of [m/s]) (3). Understanding both types of vascular transports can be clinically significant (3, 6). Diffusion plays a key role in the exchange of small molecules and is driven by concentration gradients across the vascular endothelium. In contrast, the transport of larger molecules, such as plasma proteins, is primarily governed by convection (filtration) (7). Most of current assessment methods focus on macromolecular transport and neglect vascular hydraulic permeability (Lp) when measuring the vascular permeability coefficient (P) (8–10). However, theoretical models (3, 6, 11, 12) and some experimental studies (13–16) examining convection in tumor vessels emphasize the critical role of hydraulic conductivity (Lp). Knowledge of Lp can improve the estimation accuracy of P. Moreover, knowledge of both Lp and P could impact the choice of cancer treatments (16) For instance, by using a mathematical model, Jain et al. (17) showed that antiangiogenic therapy of vascular normalization can decrease Lp, eventually decreasing interstitial fluid pressure. Hydraulic conductivity also affects the values of convection and consolidation time of drug molecules (11), making it a potentially important marker for prognosis and staging of a cancer (18).

In this study, we focus on new methods to noninvasively image Lp in tumors using ultrasound poroelastography (USPE) (14, 15). USPE is an elasticity imaging technique aiming at estimating the poroelastic response of a tissue upon sustained compression (3, 6, 11, 19–23). In a USPE experiment, a tissue is compressed for a prolonged time, during which radiofrequency (RF) data are collected. These RF data are used to compute the time-dependent axial and lateral strain elastograms (21, 24). We note that, in Refs. (14, 15), hydraulic conductivity in tumor vascular walls is referred to as “vascular permeability (VP).” Therefore, to be consistent with previous publications, we will use the symbol VP to refer to hydraulic conductivity for the remainder of this article. A major limitation of current USPE-based VP estimation methods is that these methods are based on axial strain time constant (TC) estimation, which requires high temporal resolution, is computationally expensive and prone to inaccuracies because of the use of simplified analytical models of poroelastic axial strain. Furthermore, these methods assume that VP is dominant over interstitial permeability (IP) in the tumor, or that IP in the tumor is much higher than IP in the normal tissue (14, 15).

In this article, we propose a novel USPE-based method to image VP in tumors. Our proposed method differs from the previously proposed USPE methods as it computes VP by knowledge of the volumetric strain and fluid pressure in the tissue. Therefore, the method does not require the estimation of the strain TC. Another advantage of the proposed method is that it can accurately compute VP without the assumption of dominance of VP over IP. Thus, the proposed method can be used to compute VP both in the cancer and in the surrounding tissue. This method has the potential to expand the range of tissue materials where USPE-based VP methods can be applied to and may be clinically helpful to noninvasively image VP in cancers and monitor VP changes due to treatments.

Materials and methods

A block diagram showing the proposed approach is given in Fig. 1. Tumor and the surrounding tissues are assumed as biphasic (solid and fluid) material with solid phase fully saturated by the interstitial fluid (25–27). The proposed estimation method uses spatiotemporal volumetric strain, bulk modulus, and fluid pressure induced by the applied stress on the tumor. Each step is detailed in the following few subsections.

Workflow diagram to estimate vascular hydraulic conductivity in tumors in vivo using the proposed approach based on USPE imaging.

Estimation of volumetric strain

Axial and lateral displacements are computed from ultrasound RF data using Dynamic Programming and Horn-Shunk method (24). Axial and lateral strains are then calculated by using Kalman filter-based least square estimation (28). The volumetric strain (ϵ) inside the tumor and the background are computed from the axial strain (ϵy) and lateral strain (ϵx). Volumetric strain of a material is the trace of strain tensor matrix, and that is defined as ϵ=−ϵx+ϵy+ϵz, where ϵz is the elevational strain. As we used a linear array ultrasound transducer for data acquisition, we computed the volumetric strain under the assumption of axisymmetric conditions as ϵ=−ϵx+2ϵy. However, our theoretical method is inherently 3D and does not require axisymmetric assumption, if axial, lateral, and elevational data are available.

Estimation of bulk modulus (K)

The drained bulk modulus (K) of the tumor and normal tissue can be computed as K=E3(1−ν), where E is the Young's modulus (YM) and ν is the Poisson's ratio (PR). To determine the YM and PR, we use the theory developed in (29, 30). Tumor geometry is approximated by its best fit ellipse using Eigen decomposition (14, 31).

Estimation of fluid pressure

In a poroelastic material, fluid redistributes in response to an applied compression until steady state is reached after a certain time, causing spatiotemporal volumetric strain and FP (32). Therefore, ϵ and p can be considered as functions of both space (denoted here as spherical coordinate, R) and time (t) for the duration of the experiment. Our analysis assumes that the solid phase and the fluid phase individually are incompressible while the tissue matrix is compressible.

Evaluation metric

Finite element (FE) and ultrasound (US) simulations were used to validate the proposed theory. Performance of the reconstructed VP images in FE and US simulations was evaluated using percent-relative-error (PRE), defined as, PRE=(ρt(r,c)−ρe(r,c)ρt(r,c))×100, where ρe is the estimated parameter and ρt is the true parameter. R and C represent the row and column in the VP image, respectively.

The performance of our proposed method was also compared to two previously published methods (14, 15). Based on the theory in (14), VP (Lp) is computed using the strain TC (τ). Therefore, we refer to this method as Lp_τ. According to the theory in (15), Lp is estimated using the strain TC and α parameter. Therefore, we refer to this method as Lp_τ_ α. In the proposed approach, Lp is computed by the knowledge of the volumetric strain (ϵ) and fluid pressure (p), and therefore, we refer to our method as Lp_ϵ_ p. To analyze the in vivo results, statistical two-tailed Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed on the estimated results with 95% confidence interval.

Simulations

FE simulations

The commercial FE simulation software ABAQUS (Dassault Systems Simulia Corp., Providence, RI, USA) was used to simulate cylindrical poroelastic samples under boundary conditions mimicking the ultrasound poroelastography experiments (35, 36). The poroelastic samples were modeled as linearly elastic, isotropic, and permeable solid phases fully saturated with an incompressible fluid. An axisymmetric model was used in this study. The model consisted of a cylindrical sample of 4 cm height and 4 cm diameter. For the fluid phase, free flow boundary conditions (pressure = 0) were prescribed at the edges of the domain. For the solid phase, stress free boundary conditions were prescribed at the edges of the domain. An inclusion of radius 0.75 cm with material properties distinct from the peripheral region was created at the center of the domain. We simulated an unconfined creep compression test between two impervious surfaces. The samples were compressed from the top and the displacement at the base of the domain was constrained along the vertical direction. For each sample, a single 2D solution plane was analyzed in ABAQUS using CAX4RP mesh elements, with the number of elements ranging from 24,530 to 24,900. The CAX4RP element features 4-node bilinear displacement and pore pressure, reduced integration, and hourglass control. An instantaneous stress of 2 kPa was applied for the creep experiment simulation. The boundary conditions for the poroelastography simulations are: (i) displacement is zero at the bottom plane of the sample; (ii) fluid pressure is zero along the right edge of the sample; (iii) a constant uniaxial stress is applied from the top boundary of the sample; (iv) the inclusion is assumed to be perfectly bonded to the surrounding tissue.

To validate the performance of the proposed method for a wide range of VP, we simulated 14 samples (A–N) with different mechanical, vascular, and interstitial transport properties. We specifically focused on different vascular fluid flow to interstitial fluid flow resistance ratios and different inclusion IP to background IP ratios. Mechanical and fluid transport parameters used in FE simulation are summarized in Table 1. The choice of the mechanical parameters of the inclusion and the background (E and ν) was set based on the previous works (37).

Ultrasound simulations

Pre- and postcompression ultrasound RF data were simulated for four samples (samples A, D, H, L from Table 2) using the transducer characteristics and the mechanical displacement data obtained from finite element modeling (FEM) employing Field II ultrasound simulation software (38, 39). The simulated ultrasound transducer resembled our experimental 38 mm linear array transducer having 128 elements with frequency bandwidth between 5–14 MHz, a 6.6 MHz center frequency, and 40 MHz sampling frequency. Beamforming was performed with 32 active elements and a single focal point. The elements of the simulated probe had a width of 0.23 mm and a height of 7 mm with a gap between elements of 0.08 mm. Using simulated precompressed and postcompression RF data at different time points, axial and lateral strains were estimated using the method outlined in (24). Due to the computational complexity of Field II simulator, only ten temporal frames for each sample were selected to perform US simulation.

Experiments

In vivo experiments

In vivo experiments were performed in two groups of a total of 26 mice implanted with triple negative breast cancer (TNBC). All mice were injected by MDA-MB-231 cell line obtained from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). In vivo experiments were approved by the Houston Methodist Research Institute, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC-approved protocol # AUP-0614-0033). The cancers were created by injecting cancer cells orthotopically in the mammary fat pad of immunocompromised female NOD/SCID gamma mice.

In one set of experiments (experiment 1: treatment group), 10 mice were categorized into treated (n = 5) and control (n = 5). For treatment, each mouse received 20 mg/kg of Docetaxel once per week for three consecutive weeks, beginning when the tumor volume reached 150–200 mm³ (40–42). In another set of experiments (experiment 2: size group), 16 mice were categorized into small (n = 7) with tumor volume < 200 mm3, medium (n = 5) with tumor volume > 200 mm3 and <400 mm3, and large (n = 4) with tumor volume > 400 mm3 to view the alterations of VP with size of the tumor.

During the ultrasound imaging session, each mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane and maintained on a thermostat-regulated heating pad, remaining sedated throughout the entire procedure and monitored by trained personnel at the Houston Methodist Research Institute. Animals were maintained on oxygen and isofluorane (1–3%) through inhalation using a VetEquip anesthesia system (Model is V-10 Mobile Laboratory Anesthesia System). An ultrasound Doppler sensor was also used to monitor the animal's cardiac rate. The total duration of each experiment was ∼5 min, which resulted in up to 5–7 RF data acquisitions at different locations/planes in the tumors. During each USPE experiment, 600 ultrasound RF frames were collected in one minute. From the RF data, B-mode images were also generated.

The experimental set-up used in this study is shown on Fig. 1. In vivo elastography was carried out using a 38-mm linear array transducer (Sonix RP, Ultrasonix, Richmond, BC, Canada) with a center frequency of 6.6 MHz and 50% fractional bandwidth. The imaging field of view was set to 40 mm (axial) by 38 mm (lateral). Axial spatial resolution is ∼200 microns and lateral/elevational spatial resolution is approximately 1 mm at the focus. To account for surface geometry and enhance focus within superficial tumors, an aqueous ultrasound gel pad (Aquaflex, Parker Laboratories, NJ, USA) was placed between the compressor plate and the tissue.

A force sensor (Tekscan FlexiForce, Tekscan, Inc., South Boston, MA, USA) was positioned between the top surface of the gel pad and the compressor plate to measure the applied force during compression. The sensor, model #A201, has a force range of 0–4.4 N and operates on a scale of 0–255. The area of the sensing surface is calculated as 7.1331 × 10−5m2 (Ar=πr2). The applied stress in pascals (Pa) is calculated using the formula: σ0=Fr×4.4255×Ar, where Fr is the force reading obtained from the sensor during the experiments. It is noted that σ0 is the axial component of the applied stress and the other two components (lateral and elevation) are zero. A Microsoft Windows-based interface software is available to monitor and record the applied force during the experiment. Ultrasound radiofrequency (RF) data acquisition was synchronized to the application of compression. Axial and lateral strains were computed by the method discussed in Estimation of volumetric strain section.

Histology CD31 analysis was performed to independently corroborate the in vivo results. For the analysis of the in vivo data, we correlated the estimated VP (using our proposed method) from in vivo breast tumors with the vessel density in tumors, obtained using histology. CD31 is a well-defined marker of angiogenesis, for example in early breast cancer (43), and well established for the monitoring of vessel density in malignant tissue.

Tumor histology

After the in vivo USE experiments, all mice were euthanized with high volume of isoflurane intake and histology CD31 analysis was performed on two groups of experiments—treatment-based, and size-based at the Houston Methodist Research Institute to independently corroborate the in vivo results. Each group was analyzed separately. Two to four cross-sectional planes were collected from each tumor. We employed a rat anti-mouse CD31 monoclonal antibody (clone SZ31; dianova, Cat. #DIA-310) for the size-based cohort and a rabbit anti-mouse CD31 monoclonal antibody (abcam, catalog number ab182981) for the treatment-based cohort to visualize endothelial cells in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) murine tumor sections. Briefly, 5 µm FFPE sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. After blocking endogenous peroxidase with 3% hydrogen peroxide and nonspecific binding with 5% normal goat serum (size-based cohort) and 2.5% normal horse serum (treatment-based cohort) for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD31 (diluted 1:20 in PBS containing 1% BSA for size-based group and 1:1,000 in antibody diluent, Dako, catalog number S3022 for treatment-based group). The slides were then incubated with ImmPRESS® HRP Horse Anti-Rabbit IgG (Vector Laboratories, catalog number MP-7401, ready to use) for 30 min at room temperature. The staining was detected by detection kit (ABC-DAB system; Vector Laboratories) following the manufacturer's protocol. Negative controls, omitting the primary antibody, confirmed the absence of background staining.

Three 5 µm sections for size-based group and four 5 µm sections for treatment-based group were obtained at standardized depths. Because the current study did not reconstruct entire tumor volumes, we adopted fractional CD31⁺ area [i.e. percentage of CD31⁺ pixels relative to total pixels within each regions of interest (ROI) (44–46)] rather than a volumetric vessel density (vessels/mm³). This metric better reflects microvascular content in single 5 µm sections. All CD31-stained slides were scanned at 20× magnification on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope equipped with a DS-Qi2 digital camera for the size-based cohort and 40× magnification using the Keyence BZ-X810 for the treatment-based cohort. High-resolution TIFF images were exported for analysis. Quantification was performed using Nikon NIS-Elements AR software (v. 5.20) and a custom Matlab script for size-based and treatment-based groups, respectively. Within each ROI, the brown staining indicative of CD31 was isolated via color deconvolution, and a single intensity threshold was applied uniformly across all images to define CD31-positive pixels. This threshold remained constant for the entire data set to ensure comparability. The software then computed the fractional CD31⁺ area (defined as the percentage of CD31-positive pixels relative to the total pixels in each ROI). Fractional CD31⁺ area values from the ROIs in each section were averaged, then averaged again across all sections per tumor to yield a single fractional area (%) for each tumor. These values were used in all comparisons and statistical analyses.

Two sample images (control and treated) of a histological CD31-stained blood vessel in breast tumor are shown in Fig. 3 with the vessel markers on it. The signal is usually brown (DAB staining) on a purple/blue background (which comes from the hematoxylin counterstain) at different ROI. ROIs for histology CD31 staining were taken from the tumor center as well as the tumor's periphery. USPE scanning was performed close to the tumor center. Therefore, imaging planes for USPE and cross-sectional planes for histology should have a close correspondence.

Discussion

Vascular hydraulic permeability is an important parameter that could improve diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of cancers (16, 47). A noninvasive and accurate method for assessing this parameter and monitoring its changes could hold substantial clinical value. In this study, we propose a novel/// noninvasive technique based on USPE, which utilizes spatiotemporal measurements of volumetric strain and fluid pressure to reconstruct VP. The proposed method does not require strain TC computation and is applicable to a broad range of tumors. With respect to previous USPE-based VP methods, this method allows assessment of a broader range of VP values while maintaining high accuracy and reliability. Two types of vessel permeabilities in cancers such as vascular hydraulic permeability (Lp), and vascular permeability coefficient (P) derived from convection and diffusion, respectively, have been proven clinically significant. Computation of vascular permeability coefficient from macromolecular transports requires accurate knowledge of Lp. Therefore, our proposed method to estimate Lp can also be used to improve accuracy of P estimation.

While the model developed here provides considerable insights into VP mechanisms, it has assumptions and limitations that should be considered. Tumor is assumed to have homogeneous α and k^ ratios. In practice, tumors can be highly heterogeneous with a vascular and necrotic regions. Our current simulation model assumes uniformity of VP inside the tumor. This is a common assumption in FE simulations related to cancer biomechanics (3, 4, 29, 37). However, our proposed method estimates VP on a pixel-by-pixel basis using local values of the strains and, therefore, should be able to capture the heterogeneity inside the tumor as well. Another limitation of the proposed method is that, to develop the model, we neglected the lymphatic drainage term from the continuity equation (Eq. 2) of incompressible flow. While this assumption may be valid in tumors due to the absence of well-defined functioning lymphatic vessels (4), it may not be true for normal tissue. Therefore, the method may need further revisions and an extensive performance analysis if the application focuses on normal tissues. In general, the boundary conditions used in our simulation models are representative of the in vivo experimental setup of our SPE experiments. Specifically, the bottom plane can be considered fixed due to the animal being anesthetized, while a constant pressure is applied from the top. It is important to note that, if future experiments are conducted using a different setup or under altered boundary conditions, the proposed method must be adapted accordingly to ensure accurate estimation of VP. To reconstruct Lp, we modeled a tumor as a biphasic poroelastic material where solid phase is fully saturated with interstitial fluid (IF). We assume that both the solid phase (collagen and proteoglycans) and fluid phase are intrinsically incompressible whereas the overall tissue material is compressible. These assumptions are common in theoretical models of tumors’ transport mechanisms (26, 27). The incompressibility assumption for IF is typically valid because IF is primarily composed of water. Our model neglects transient solid response that is not directly related to fluid flow, and it assumes that the inertial effects are negligible. Although these assumptions have been widely used in literature for modeling solid tumors (26, 27), further investigations of these assumptions could improve accuracy of VP estimation. Furthermore, to solve the governing equation of fluid pressure in the tumor, we considered spherical symmetry, following the method developed in Refs. (27, 48). In theory, however, the equation could be solved for different tumors’ shapes. As our USPE method works on a pixel-by-pixel basis, VP inside the tumor can be computed for tumors of any shape. In our simulation model, we assumed that the tumor is perfectly bonded to the surrounding normal tissue (49). This assumption is common in most studies in the field of cancer biomechanics and supported by experimental evidence (50).

The proposed method for VP reconstruction is inherently 3D. It uses the three normal components of the strain tensor—axial, lateral, and elevational—to compute the volumetric strain and reconstruct the YM and PR. However, due to the experimental challenges associated with estimating elevational strain in ultrasound elastography, in our analysis, we assumed that the elevational strain component is equivalent to the lateral strain component. In the future, given the availability of volumetric data, for example using 2D arrays, it should be possible to estimate the 3D shape of the tumor and apply the proposed method to estimate VP. Having information on both lateral and elevational strains would likely improve the performance of the reconstruction method.

The quality of the RD data and the accuracy of the strain estimates play a critical role in the performance of the proposed method. Using high frequency and high bandwidth ultrasound scanners would improve spatial resolution and sensitivity, and, consequently, the performance of the proposed method. In this study, we employed a recently developed strain estimation technique (24), which has been demonstrated to provide high-quality axial and lateral strain images. In the presence of noise introduced by the ultrasound simulations, the reconstruction error increased but remained below 20%, indicating the robustness of the proposed method under more realistic imaging conditions. However, for the case of irregularly shaped tumors, the accuracy of the method could be improved by the development of more advanced reconstruction algorithms. In this study, we computed vascular hydraulic permeability (VP) of the simulated samples assuming that the surface area to volume ratio (SV) of the tumor capillary walls is 20,000 m−1 (14). As reported in the literature, SV in tumors may vary from 7,000 m−1 to 25,000 m−1 (33, 51). According to our proposed estimation model, VP is inversely proportional to SV. Therefore, variations in SV will affect the estimated value of VP. However, for the in vivo tumors, SV was computed as SV=10fVtg (33) where Vt=(43)πl12l2 represents the volume of tumor with f = 54.68, g = −0.2021, and l1 and l2 as the major and minor axes lengths of the tumor in units of mm. The resulting SV is expressed in cm−1.

In the future, USPE may be adapted for use in humans by employing protocols analogous to those used in the animal experiments and standard elastography techniques. For tumors that are relatively superficial, such as breast tumors, the protocol described above could be readily adapted for human use. Similar to the experimental protocol used in the in vivo mouse studies described in In vivo experiments section, RF data should be acquired for a certain duration (typically <1 min) using a commercial US system. A compressor plate attached to the transducer should be used to ensure uniaxial compression. The primary challenge in translating the proposed method to clinical settings would be ensuring steady data acquisition for the duration of the imaging process (∼1 min) to maintain a consistent imaging plane and reduce motion artifacts. In recent works (52), we demonstrated that a 2-TC acquisition period (∼20–25 s) is sufficient to estimate cancer parameters with reasonable accuracy. Moreover, emerging AI-based techniques have been investigated and, in some cases, implemented to enhance the clinical applicability of ultrasound strain elastography (53, 54).

In this research, the feasibility of the proposed method in vivo has been shown on a limited number of cancers in a small animal model. In this study, a chemotherapy treatment (Docetaxel) protocol was applied to monitor the changes of VP during treatment. Chemotherapy is a well-characterized, clinically validated treatment that remains the mainstay of systemic therapy for TNBC and has demonstrated efficacy in both early-stage and metastatic TNBC in human patients. Moreover, docetaxel has been shown to alter tumor vascularization (40), thereby impacting vascular permeability. In the future, additional cutting-edge therapeutic strategies could be evaluated and benchmarked against standard chemotherapy. Based on our in vivo results shown in Fig. 8, VP increased with time in the control group (untreated) while it decreased significantly (P < 0.05) in the treated tumors. These results are consistent with the results obtained from CD31 vessel density staining and observations previously reported in literature (51, 55). However, additional experiments are required to further corroborate the use of the proposed methods in different experimental scenarios.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a noninvasive imaging technique for assessing vascular permeability (VP) within tumors using ultrasound poroelastography. Through FEM and ultrasound simulations, we demonstrated that the proposed method can accurately and robustly estimate VP inside cancers. Given the critical role of VP in cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning, this technique holds the potential for significant clinical impact, offering a promising tool for enhanced tumor characterization and personalized treatment strategies.
