Successful formation of sperm cells from transplanted primordial germ cells in sterile interspecific avian recipients
Authors: Mariann Molnár, Bence Lázár, Nikoletta Sztán, Barbara Végi, Árpád Drobnyák, Kitti Buda, Nándor Nagy, Emőke Szőcs, Nóra Pecsenye-Fejszák, Krisztina Liptói, Elen Gócza, Michael J McGrew, Eszter Várkonyi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimordial germ cells (PGCs) are stem cells, from which only gametes develop. In birds, the female sex is heterogametic, thus female gene conservation necessitates preservation of PGCs. PGC transplantation can generate germline chimeras in a host organism and develop into gametes. However, competition between host and transplanted PGCs hinder efficiency of germline chimera generation. We hypothezised that in sterile hybrid recipients with no germ cells of its own, transplanted donor PGCs may exclusively form gametes. Advantages of sterile hybrids as host for PGCs is compliant with many national regulations on genetically modified organisms and technically simpler procedure than the use of busulphan. Therefore, we investigated whether sterile interspecific hybrids may be suitable as recipients for supporting donor PGCs by injecting green fluorescent protein-labelled chicken PGCs into 3-day-old Guinea fowl and domestic fowl hybrid embryos and monitoring PGC development. The injected PGCs colonized almost 100% of the recipient gonads and produced mature spermatozoa after 44 weeks. However, gamete production in these hybrids was initiated much slower than in domestic fowls. This delay may be caused by suboptimal hormonal regulation of gametogenesis in the hybrids. Our results suggest that sterile interspecific hybrids may be suitable hosts for PGCs for efficient gamete production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74442-0.Keywords: Sterile interspecific recipient, Guinea fowl, Domestic fowl, Sperm cells, PGCs, Gonadal developmentSubject terms: Biotechnology, Developmental biology, Stem cells

Background

Within stem cells, the primordial germ cells (PGCs) are of unique importance. They are the only cell types to preserve and store the complete genetic material of avian species since the female sex of birds is heterogametic, and eggs cannot be cryopreserved because of their size and special constitution. PGCs, however, can be maintained in cell culture for a long time1 and can be cryopreserved very efficiently2. Furthermore, any genetic modification of the PGCs is passed on to the next generation in one step, elevating this cell type into the forefront of current avian genome editing research.

Primordial germ cells are relatively easy to obtain in birds, as they circulate in the bloodstream and are accessible during the embryonic developmental phase HH14-HH17 (Hamburger – Hamilton stages)3. They can be isolated from the heart or dorsal aorta of the embryo by a thin glass microcapillary4. In domestic fowl, a cell culture medium has been optimised in which the PGCs proliferate and do not lose their specific germ cell characteristics1,5,6. These PGCs, when re-injected into a recipient embryo, migrate to and colonise the embryonic gonads creating germline chimeras7. With the crossing of these chimeric animals it is possible to produce purely donor PGC-derived offspring.

However, the difficulty in the whole process is that the host organism/recipient also has its own germ cells, creating competition with the injected PGCs, and thus only a proportion of the offspring will originate from the donor individual. There are several directions of research to eliminate the recipient’s own PGCs. Firstly, ɣ-irradiation8–11, then a chemical treatment; busulphan was used to prevent the development of endogenous germ cells. Busulphan (1,4-butanediol dimethanesulphonate) is an alkylating agent that has been successfully used in mammals in the past to permanently damage the PGCs of the embryonic gonads and then degrade after ten hours of application12–15. The successful application of this method resulted from a technical development to target busulphan in an emulsion into the microenvironment of the embryonic gonad16. As a result of this method, donor-derived stem cells successfully colonised the gonads of recipient embryos with an extraordinary efficiency (99.5%)17. Despite high efficiency in case of successful treatment, application of this method occasionally causes embryonic abnormalities and death. Furthermore, the protocol requires skill and practice to perform, therefore the method is not widely used.

Recently, genome engineering has also been used to address this issue and inducible specific ablation of the germ cells was achieved18,19. In summary, these approaches use a transgene construct, which can be induced with a chemical agent in a cell type-specific manner. Upon induction, apoptosis of the germ cells is activated, thus, making the recipients sterile and eliminating PGC competition. Although an approach via genome modification can be efficient20, broader use of genetically modified animals remains a challenge in numerous countries, therefore development of alternative strategies remains of interest.

As the production and maintenance of genetically engineered animals is, even for experimental purposes, highly restricted in the European Union, our research team approached the problem from a different perspective. Presumptive sterile guinea fowl x domestic fowl interspecific hybrids were hypothesized to be a suitable host for domestic fowl PGCs. Our first results with this strategy were reported in 201921 where we showed that female Guinea fowl inseminated with sperm from domestic fowl roosters produce sterile hybrids. We demonstrated that these hybrid animals are suitable hosts by proving the gonadal colonization of recipient gonads by injecting PGCs in 18.5-day-old hybrid embryos. However, no formation of gametes was observed in that study.

In this study, interspecific hybrids were again produced in order to investigate the possible maturation of the injected PGCs into mature sperm during development of the recipient. Therefore, the injected hybrid individuals were grown to sexual maturity, euthanized and samples of the gonads were taken for analysis of gamete formation.

Results

Effect of PGCs injection on embryo survival and hatching

Of the 325 hybrid embryos injected, 104 died after the injection (D3) (Table 1). This was presumably due to the injection procedure itself. A further 156 embryos died between days four and ten of incubation (D4-10), 35 between days 11 and 21 of incubation (D11-21) and 13 between days 22 and 25 of incubation (D22-25). The earlier embryo lethalities (D4-10) were mostly caused by infections. The mortality later in embryonic development (D11-25) was mainly due to various developmental abnormalities (crossbill, open abdominal septum, cranial developmental abnormalities). Twelve chickens died immediately before hatching (perinatal death) and five hatched (Fig. 1). Two hybrids reached the sexual maturity (Fig. 2).

Anatomical and histological observations of testes of hybrids

Our preliminary studies showed that the gonads of untreated hybrids showed phenotypic divergence from both Guinea fowl and domestic fowl testes. In this study, we sought to determine whether the implantation of PGCs alters the morphology and/or histology of the gonads.

The two testes of the control hybrids were different in size and length. The left testis was longer than the right one, the latter appearing to have a broader anterior end. The two testes appeared smooth, yellow-creamy and slightly whiten at their anterior and posterior ends, and elongated in their shapes (Fig. 3). Compared to the control, the difference in size between the two testes in the injected hybrids still persisted, but their shapes were much more rounded, with a reduced length-to-width ratio. Comparing the speed of testicular development of hybrids with that of domestic fowl22, indicated that the maturation of hybrids is much slower, because the domestic hens reach sexual maturity around 22–23 weeks of age.

Figure 4 shows that in contrast to the control, which does not contain GFP-labelled (green fluorescent protein-labelled) cells, the gonads of the two injected hybrids show the GFP fluorescent signal almost in its entirety. Since GFP is expressed only in the cytoplasm of the cell, it only indicates the early developmental forms (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids) that occur during spermiogenesis, not the mature spermatozoa.

In one of the individuals, we also found spermatid cells in different stages of maturation as well as mature spermatozoa indicated by morphology (Figs. 5B, 6 and 7). The mature sperm cells were lacking GFP as expected from the expression dynamics driven by the CMV promoter. However, as no mature sperm was ever observed in hybrids (Guinea fowl x domestic fowl) without injection of PGCs (Fig. 5A), we conclude that these mature sperm cells were derived from the GFP-positive PGCs. In the second individual, mature sperm cells were absent but spermatids were visible on histological cross-sections (Fig. 5C). Discussion

The use of avian PGCs in various applications including gene editing, regenerative medicine and gene conservation is hindered by the technological challenge of efficient production of gametes from manipulated or non-treated PGCs. The major obstacle against progress is the availability of a suitable host, which is capable of producing 100% of the gametes of the donor organism and ideally without genome editing or modification as the practical application of these lines is still difficult due to EU legislation.

In this study we demonstrated successful production of mature sperm upon injection of PGCs into sterile avian hybrids. Our approach was motivated by experiments previously carried out in fish. Xu et al. (2019)23 reported the first successful implantation of donor spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) into adult sterile hybrid fish recipients (female blue drum Nibea mitsukurii x male white croaker Pennahia argentata), from which mature sperm were obtained after 25 weeks. Fluorescent and histological observations showed the colonization, proliferation, and differentiation of transplanted spermatogonial cells in the gonads of hybrid croakers. 10% of the transplanted recipients produced donor-derived GFP-positive spermatozoa.

Marinovic et al. (2022)24 also attempted to use host sterility for germline stem cell (GSC) transplantation in fish. They transplanted GSCs from other salmonid species into triploid rainbow trout larvae. Two months after transplantation, dissection of recipients revealed that both SSCs and oogonial (OSCs) stem cells of both species were able to colonize recipient gonads. But after three years of rearing, neither male nor female recipients showed signs of gametogenesis progression.

In birds, SSCs from a dead xenogeneic donor were transplanted into chicken recipients sterilized with busulfan. In this case, successful incorporation was documented until 42 days after transplantation, but mature sperm were not obtained from the recipients15. Van de Lavoir et al. (2012)25 reported that chicken PGCs injected into a Guinea fowl recipient partially colonized the gonads, underwent spermatogenesis in the xenogeneic host and developed into functional spermatozoa.

Our research team reported in 201921 that the Guinea fowl x domestic hen hybrid could potentially be a suitable sterile recipient. At that time, we reported the presence of incorporated PGCs in 7.5, 14.5 and 18.5-day-old embryonic gonads. The experiments were repeated and the hatched hybrids were grown to maturity. The results showed that donor-derived germ cells colonized almost 100% of recipient’s gonads. The injected hybrids also showed phenotypically a slight difference in stalk size and colour from controls suggesting that the microinjected PGCs resulted in morphology variation in the hosts.

Compared to previous studies, we have made significant progress, as the results presented here demonstrate that the injected GFP-PGCs can be detected not only in the gonads of developing embryos but also take the place of the recipient’s defective primordial germ cells during development, resulting in the formation of mature sperm. During avian spermiogenesis, practically the entire cytoplasm is lost with the formation of the flagellum26, so the mature spermatozoa shown in Figs. 6 and 7 no longer show the green fluorescence, as GFP can only be expressed in the cytoplasm. However, since GFP is expressed in spermatogonia, spermatocytes and spermatids that develop during spermiogenesis, we conclude that these mature spermatozoa originate from GFP-positive PGCs.

The reason for the high number of perinatal deaths was that the hybrid chickens had extreme difficulty in breaking through the hard Guinea fowl eggshell. Since the hatching parameters of domestic fowl and Guinea fowl differ significantly, the suitable parameters for hybrid embryos need to be tested. The method requires further improvement; the hatchability of the hybrids needs to be enhanced by increasing humidity and maintaining higher hygiene standards to reduce the occurrence of infections.

It has been found that gamete production in these hybrids is initiated much more slowly than in domestic fowls. This could be due to a deficiency in hormonal regulation resulting from the hybrid status, for example the absence or underactivation of Leydig’s interstitial endocrine cells in the testes27. The fact that Guinea fowls are relatively early in the domestication process and therefore only start to produce gametes when the right environmental conditions are present (minimum 18 °C and 14 h of light) may also affect the reproductive performance of the hybrids.

Our study has shown that artificially produced sterile interspecific hybrids can be the recipient of PGCs from other species and can be used to generate mature sperm cells during development. In the future, further research is needed to explore other hybrid combinations and study their characteristics and potential use for sterile recipients. The results presented here represent proof of principle evidence for the hybrid strategy in sperm production and may also offer experimental approach for cellular and molecular analysis of gametogenesis differences and species-specific differences in interaction between PGCs and the soma during gametogenesis in hybrids.

Conclusions

The aim of our work was to investigate the possibility that sterile interspecific hybrids might be suitable as host organisms to receive donor PGCs from other avian species. This was accomplished by injecting GFP-labelled PGCs into 3-day-old hybrid embryos. The results showed that the injected PGCs colonized almost 100% of the recipient gonads and were able to produce mature spermatozoa after 44 weeks. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of successful PGCs transplantation using a germ cell-less interspecific avian hybrid as a recipient. Further improvements are still needed to improve the efficiency of the procedure.

Methods

Ethics statement

Animals were kept and maintained according to general animal welfare prescriptions of the Hungarian Animal Protection Law (1998; XXVIII). Permission to undertake experimental animal research at the National Centre for Biodiversity and Gene Conservation was granted by the National Food Chain Safety Office, Animal Health and Animal Welfare Directorate, Budapest (Permission no. PE/EA/2485-6/2016). All experimental methods described herein were approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board of the Institute for Farm Animal Gene Conservation (No. 7/2011), in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

ARRIVE statement

We followed the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Experimental design

The Hungarian Landrace Guinea fowl and Yellow Hungarian chicken breed used in this study originated from the gene bank stock of the National Centre for Biodiversity and Gene Conservation, Institute for Farm Animal Gene Conservation. Thirty Guinea fowl females were artificially inseminated with mixed sperm of 10 Hungarian yellow cockerels. The eggs were collected twice a day. The bulk of the eggs obtained in this way were used for the injection experiments and the remainder as controls. Injection experiments were carried out three times a week. About 30 fertile eggs were injected per experiment. In total, 325 eggs were incubated as injected group and 60 eggs as controls.

Artificial insemination

Semen from cockerels was collected by abdominal massage according to Burrows and Quinn (1935)28. The fresh, pooled (10 samples) and diluted semen was inseminated in a dose of 100 ± 20 million spermatozoa/Guinea fowl female in all cases. For the dilution and calculation of sperm concentration LKS diluent was used29.

Incubation of the eggs and investigation of embryo survival

Incubation was carried out with a MIDI F500S hatchery machine (PL Machine Ltd., Tárnok, Hungary) with two 45° rotations per hour. The incubation temperature was 37.8 °C, relative humidity kept at 70%.

Egg fertility was determined by candling at 8th and 14th day after egg set. Apparent clear eggs and eggs with abnormal embryos were opened and examined. Any embryonic malformations found were recorded.

Injection of GFP-expressing PGC lines into 3-day-old hybrid embryos

In order to test the functional integrity of the hybrid gonads, GFP-expressing PGCs were injected into 3-day-old hybrid embryos. The GFP-expressing PGC lines were isolated from transgenic White Leghorn chicken embryos (the GFP-expressing White Leghorn line was established by McGrew et al.30). PGCs were cultured in vitro using a specific media with 0.2% chicken serum and method described by Whyte et al.. (2015)1. A male GFP-expressing cell line (4ZP) was selected for injection31. Hybrid eggs were incubated to stage HH15-16. 5000–8000 PGCs, in 1 µl of DMEM were injected into the heart of each hybrid embryo through a hole (5–6 mm in diameter) on the eggshell. After injection, 50 µl of sterile 1x D-PBS was added and then 2 layers of parafilm were used to close the hole. The injected eggs were incubated at 37.8 C° with 70% relative humidity.

Maintenance of the hatched viable hybrid offspring

The hatched hybrids were grown in a special chick rearing box until 4 weeks of age (0.5 m2/10 individuals). The box is equipped with automatic heating and lighting. The temperature was gradually reduced weekly from 30 °C to the final 22–24 °C. 4 week old individuals were placed on deep litter. They were fed ad libitum with granulated starter mash (Szinbád Ltd., Gödöllő, Hungary) and water supply. The raised hybrid and control individuals were euthanized at 44 weeks of age and their gonads were examined.

Histological analysis of the hybrid gonads

Two injected hybrid individuals and two control hybrid individual were euthanized for histological analysis. Animals were euthanized by applying a single intravenous injection of Phenobarbital in 100 mg/kg dose. The gonads were dissected, imaged, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 2 days (Excelsior AS Tissue Processor, No.: A82300001, Thermofisher). The samples were further incubated overnight in 10, 20 and 30% sucrose. For the embedding, 7.5% gelatine was used and then the blocks were frozen in -40 °C isopentane for cryosectioning. Sample blocks were stored at -80 °C until further usage. Cross-sections were made with a cryostat machine (Microm D-6900, Microm GmbH. Heidelberg, Germany) and the 10 μm sections were collected on Poly-L-Lysine coated slides. Slides were stored at -20 °C until further usage. Before staining, slides were rehydrated in PBS solution in 37 °C for 10 min. The samples were stained by haematoxylin-eosin technique, for which an autostainer was used (Leica Autostainer XL ST5010). As a first step, the slides were treated in xylene, which was followed by immersing them into 100%, 100% and 70% ethanol solutions. After a quick wash in distilled water, the sections were dipped into haematoxylin, then following another wash, into eosin. At last, slides were treated in 95%, then three times 100% ethanol solutions and until covering, they were stored in xylene. For covering the slides, glass coverslips were placed onto the samples with fine forceps and fixed with 1–2 drops of mounting medium.

Fluorescent immunostaining of the hybrid gonads

Despite the fact that the injected PGCs carried a GFP-expression cassette directed by CMV, an enhancer/promoter30, we observed only faint GFP expression upon examining the sections. Therefore, we used an anti-GFP primary antibody (Rockland Immunohistochemicals, cat. no: 600-401-215, diluted 1:150 in 1% PBS-BSA) for 1 h at room temperature followed by Alexa-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG 488 fluorescent secondary antibody (ThermoFisher, A21206) to detect all of the injected PGCs. Cell nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Vector Labs, Burlingame, California) and sections were covered by aqueous Poly/Mount (Polyscience Inc. Warrington, PA, 18606).

Image processing, including tiling and merging of pseudocoloured immunofluorescent images, was carried out with use of CellSens imaging software and by ImageJ (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/).

Statistical analysis

Pairwise Z-tests were performed and p-values were calculated on proportions for different early embryonic mortality and hatchability between the “Injected” and “Control” groups. The statistical analysis utilized packages ‘statsmodels’ (version: 0.14.1)32, ‘pandas’ (2.2.1)33, ‘numpy’ (1.26)34, and ‘matplotlib’ (3.8.3)35 within Python 3. The code was executed in Visual Studio Code (version: 1.86.2, Microsoft Corp.). Levels of significance were applied as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.
