Assessing progress of road safety legislation globally: criteria, methodology and evolution 2015–2023
Authors: Evelyn Murphy, Fangfang Luo, Joelle Auert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWHO has set minimum, evidence-based criteria to evaluate the quality of behavioural risk factors laws and reports country progress in global status reports on road safety (GSRRS). We describe criteria evolution and country progress. We analyse laws on speed, drink driving, helmets, seatbelts and child restraint. Global status reports 2009 and 2013 are based on countries’ responses. In Report 2015, the collection of legislation and assessment by WHO based on criteria began. Criteria were refined based on the evolving scientific evidence and practice. Report 2018 is the first year in which criteria are comparable with GSRRS 2023. Criteria are consistently applied using standardised interpretation criteria. Based on 2021–2030 Decade of Action for Road Safety framework, Report 2023 includes additional legislation topics but no criteria set. Assessment criteria: speed: national law with urban speed limit≤50 km/h and localities can modify limits. Drinking driving: national law based on blood alcohol concentration or breath alcohol concentration equivalent of ≤0.05 g/dL for general population and ≤0.02 g/dL for novice/young drivers. Helmets: national mandatory motorcycle helmet law covering all adult riders, for all engine types on all roads; helmet is properly strapped and reference to national or international helmet standard. Seatbelts: national mandatory seatbelt wearing exists applicable to all front- and back-seated vehicle occupants. Child restraint systems: national compulsory CRS use in place based on age/weight/height and referring to a standard. Results show a slow pace of legislation improvements. More research is needed on the quality of laws and implementation in low- and middle-income countries.Keywords: Legislation, Policy analysis, Behavior

Introduction

When properly implemented and enforced, road traffic laws prevent crashes from happening and mitigate the adverse consequences of risky behaviours in a crash. Establishing and enforcing laws to address risky behaviours have been shown to reduce road traffic injuries and fatalities.1 WHO has established minimum, evidence-based criteria to evaluate the quality of behavioural risk factors (BRFs) laws. It monitors and reports the status of country BRF legislation in global status reports on road safety (GSRRS) as country progress indicators. The reports were published in 2009, 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2023.2,6 While there was a literature establishing the effect of BRF interventions on behaviour change leading to reduced crashes, injuries and deaths,1 no literature existed on a defined set of criteria for the evaluation of BRF laws. This article presents that how criteria were set, their evolution and the status of country BRF laws in 2015–2023 when the criteria are applied.

Methods

In 2009 and 2013 GSRRSs, the reported status of legislation was based on information supplied by participating countries through a questionnaire. No independent legislation analysis was conducted by WHO. Starting with GSRRS 2015, WHO began collecting country legislation adopted by 31 December of the year preceding publication of the report and analysing them against minimum criteria. This major new undertaking led to the collection of extensive legislative data from several countries: GSRRS 2015, 180 countries; GSRRS 2018, 175 countries and GSRRS 2023, 172 countries. The legislation criteria were developed based on two conditions, the first being a primary condition and the second where available.

In 2023, a consultative advisory board was established to evaluate the structure of the four GSRRSs and outcomes and discuss the focus for GSRRS 2023. Following the advice of the advisory board, the objectives for GSRRS 2023 were broadened to better reflect the Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 framework,7 resulting in the addition of new legislation topics for which criteria are yet to be developed.

An interpretation guide (online supplemental annex 1), an iterative document developed to facilitate harmonised interpretation by reviewers, includes the evidence basis for the criteria.

The criteria are periodically reviewed and revised based on the evidence and lessons learnt through the implementation of country projects, such as the Bloomberg Initiative for Global Road Safety, during which WHO provided legislation support. One example is the evolution of the criteria on speed legislation. In 2008 and 2013, the criteria against which countries reported legislation via the questionnaire included (a) existence of a national law on speed limit; (b) the limit set for urban areas was not more than 50 km/h and (c) local authorities could reduce speed limits in their jurisdictions. Starting in 2015, when independent analysis by WHO was instituted, criterion (c) was changed to whether local authorities could modify limits within their jurisdiction. Below is an explanation.

Speed limit of 50 km/h: speed is a major cause of road traffic death and serious injuries, and even small reductions in speed affect fatal crashes and serious injuries. Evidence shows that a 1% increase in mean speed results in a 4% increase in risk of fatal crash and a 3% increase in risk of serious crash.8 Also, a 5% reduction in average speed can reduce fatalities by 30%.9 The risk of a pedestrian dying when hit by the front of a car increases rapidly by 4.5 times from 50 to 65 km/h.10 Given the fatal crash and serious injury risk associated with speed in the event of a crash and the exponential rise in pedestrian injury if hit at 50 km/h, the criterion used for quality legislation assessment and reporting purposes is the existence of a national law, setting an urban speed limit of not more than 50 km/h.

Ability of local authorities to reduce speed limits: in areas where there is a greater likelihood of conflict between cars and vulnerable road users, lower speeds of 30 km/h zones are more appropriate. Pedestrians have a 90% chance of surviving car crashes at speeds of 30 km/h or lower, but less than a 50% chance of surviving impacts at 45 km/h.11 Given the complexity of defining which roads should have a 30 km/h limit in national law, countries were asked in the questionnaire to report on the ability of local authorities to reduce speeds in both 2008 and 2013, without independent assessment by WHO.

Ability of local authorities to modify speed limits: in developing criteria, the urban speed limit of 50 km/h and ability of local authorities to reduce speed limits were carried over. But in 2015, the criterion related to local authorities was refined to recognise the importance of speed and the need for local authorities to be able to ‘define the function of each road and review existing speed limits and ensure that they are consistent, credible and, therefore, more easily enforceable’.12 More emphasis was placed on speed management and the ability of local authorities to set speed limits (lower or higher) considering their context and road function in their jurisdictions. So, in 2015, criterion on speed legislation evolved from the ability of local authorities to reduce speed (reported in 2008 and 2013 without independent assessment of legislation) to include whether local authorities had the ability to modify the limit, reported in 2015 and 2018 with independent assessment of the legislation quality (table 2).

Discussion

Starting with GSRRS 2015 legislation, data sources included legislation text communicated by National Data Coordinators designated by the ministries of health and responsible for data collection in each participating country; search of online legislative databases and country-level government websites for legislative documents related to BRFs and submission of legislation by lawyers involved in capacity building programmes under the Bloomberg Philanthropies Global Road Safety Initiative in specific countries.

The term legislation was broadly defined to include laws, decrees and standards issued by national law-making bodies (eg, national parliaments) in the countries as well as subsidiary rules and regulations, such as administrative rules that further clarify the law and make implementation possible. WHO maintains a database of all legislations collected for all GSRRSs by country.

Legislation was reviewed by WHO headquarters-based lawyers with periodic support from consultant lawyers working in their mother tongue. Analysts extracted and analysed relevant information using the same criteria to assess all countries’ legislation. Where interpretation could vary, a senior legal adviser at HQ reviewed interpretation decisions and a decision was reached following deliberations. An iterative interpretation guide was maintained to ensure consistency (online supplemental file 1).

When evidence on the existence of national legislation on a BRF and the effect in reducing RT crashes, serious injuries or deaths are not available or inconclusive, and the criteria were not used for assessing the quality of law but reported in the GSRRSs. These include, for example, national law restricting the use of hand-held or hands-free mobile phone use and national law prohibiting drug driving (table 2). While there is evidence that talking (either handheld or handsfree) impairs driving performance, the links between mobile phone use and traffic crashes in many countries are not known. Usage is not routinely collected as a part of police crash reports and enforcement is challenging; there is also limited information on effective interventions to reduce mobile phone use.13 14 Hence, information on legislation on mobile phone use—whether a country law prohibits hand-held or hands-free mobile phone use—is only provided for informational purposes. Likewise, use of illegal and some prescription drugs causes driving impairment; however, establishing causality between different drugs, testing and the limit at which certain drugs cause impairment is difficult.15 Thus, laws on drugs and driving are provided for information purposes. Also, finding no evidence in the literature on minimum age of ridership for children on motorcycles and based on our observations from country projects that children are transported on motorcycles, the information is reported in GSRRS 2018 but not included in the assessment of quality (table 2). The unconfirmed reported data highlight research gaps.

For countries where laws are set at subnational level (Australia, Canada, Mexico and USA), the relevant subnational laws were collected and assessed using the same criteria and the determination of whether the law met minimum criteria at national level was based on an 80% rule, meaning the laws from 80% of the total number of subnational jurisdictions must meet the criteria. The results of the legislation analyses are presented in GSRRS individual country profiles, statistical annexes and legislative maps.

The collection of BRF legislation and independent assessment using a set of minimum criteria, combined with NDC responses on legislation questions in the questionnaire, resulted in at times conflicting results. To resolve any conflicts, WHO’s legal analysis was shared with NDCs along with information, such as citations, commentary on interpretation and rationale in several rounds as a part of a ‘validation process’. During this process, NDCs had the opportunity to provide additional legislation documents for consideration. This process is undertaken for multiple rounds. If there is no supporting documentation provided, then the WHO legal interpretation was deemed final. The validation sheet (online supplemental file 2) with WHO’s final determination was shared with the NDC.

The seven topics introduced in GSRRS 2023 show an N/A in the previous reports and we only report on the existence of a national law as criteria have not yet been set. Regardless of the criteria used and the evolution, the resounding message in all global status reports was the slow pace at which countries improved BRF legislation. Second, the need for continued refinement of criteria is necessary to align with the evolving science, including country implementation experience, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which is not well documented. The results also highlight the need for more research on the links between legislation on other topics within a Safe Systems context and injury and fatality reductions.

Results

Set criteria for the assessment of legislation quality for BRF in 2023 are as follows.

Speed: national law in place; speed limits in urban areas do not exceed 50 km/h and localities have the authority to modify limits. Drinking driving: national law in place based on blood alcohol concentration (BAC) or breath alcohol concentration (BrAC) equivalent; BAC (or equivalent BrAC) does not exceed 0.05 g/dL for the general population and 0.02 g/dL for novice/young drivers. Helmets: national mandatory motorcycle helmet law in place covering all adult riders, on all engine types on all roads; helmet is properly strapped and law refers to national or international helmet standard. Seatbelts: national mandatory seatbelt wearing in place applicable to all front- and back-seated vehicle occupants. Child restraint systems: national compulsory CRS use in place based on age/height; restrictions to front seating and reference to a standard.

For newly added topics, no quality assessment was undertaken; and the existence of the national law was reported. Assessment was based on the documented evidence of the law through submission of legislative documents related to the topic.

Application of the criteria has resulted in the following. In GSRRS 2015, WHO reported that only 17 out of 180 participating countries had amended their laws on at least one or more BRFs to include minimal criteria from the previous report. In GSRRS 2018, it was 22 out of 175 participating countries compared with the GSRRS 2015. In GSRRS 2023, 29 out of 172 participating countries had amended their laws on one or more risk factors compared with GSRRS 2018.

Conclusion

The criteria were consistently applied by evaluators across all member states using detailed interpretation criteria. Results in applying these criteria show a slow pace of BRF legislation improvements despite decade-long advocacy for countries to improve on these. Between each GSRRS, few countries show improvements in one or more BRF laws identified by the Lancet—speed, drink driving, helmet and seatbelt, or on child restraint use. From 2015 to 2018, 22 countries showed improvements in one or more BRFs, and from 2018 to 2023 GSRRS, only 29 countries.

The criteria are intended as a minimal benchmark against which to measure progress on BRF legislation in countries and to help them readily identify opportunities for improvement. The criteria, along with the interpretation guidance, permit a harmonised approach to interpretation. The criteria do not include any in-depth enforceability elements, country contextual factors or case law built from judicial interpretations. Furthermore, it is important to note that the legislation assessment and criteria for the global status reports only cover BRFs; they do not include other areas of law, which are equally relevant (eg, infrastructure laws, financing mechanisms, such as funding pools set up by law, laws that facilitate access to emergency care and laws that transpose UN international regulations, such as vehicle safety and standards into national legislative frameworks to allow their implementation) to all part of the Safe Systems and Global Plan framework. The assessment process and results also highlight topics where additional research is needed on the links between BRF legislation and reductions in serious injuries and deaths, especially in LMICs. Newly introduced topics point to research areas for criteria to be set.

Stronger advocacy on a Safe System approach with legislation as a tool for implementation is needed. More research is needed on the barriers to legislation improvement, on the quality of laws and their effectiveness in reducing serious injuries and deaths on roads, especially in LMICs. A comprehensive package of legislation is needed for countries to consider. Finally, the role of regional governance bodies to harmonise laws can be strengthened.
