Intraoperative mechanical characterization of vertebral bone quality during percutaneous kyphoplasty
Authors: Yudong Zhao, Sheng Lu, Pan Liu, Xufeng Bai, Zuoqi Zhang, Xiaobin Zhu, Shaobo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis (OP) compromises bone strength and markedly increases the risk of vertebral compression fractures (VCFs), which are commonly treated with percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP). Although bone mineral density (BMD) is widely used to diagnose OP and inform surgical planning, it primarily reflects bone mass rather than mechanical competence. Here, we propose an intraoperative method that derives a bulk-modulus-like mechanical index, κ, from the balloon pressure–volume response during PKP. This method enables direct mechanical assessment of vertebral bone quality without additional surgical trauma or procedural steps. To evaluate its feasibility and robustness, a three-dimensional lumbar vertebral model was reconstructed from computed tomography data, and finite element simulations were performed to characterize the relationship between κ and the elastic modulus of cancellous bone. The effects of balloon geometry, puncture trajectory, material anisotropy and heterogeneity, elastoplasticity, and cortical bone failure were also investigated. The results showed that κ decreased linearly with reductions in cancellous-bone elastic modulus, enabling clear differentiation among the simulated bone-health states. Variations in balloon geometry and puncture trajectory altered κ by less than 5.5%, indicating that the measurement is robust to clinically relevant procedural variations. Moreover, the linear relationship between κ and cancellous-bone modulus was preserved across increasingly realistic constitutive models. These findings establish a mechanically grounded framework for intraoperative assessment of vertebral bone quality and support the potential use of κ to inform PKP procedures, postoperative management, and the acquisition of patient-specific in vivo bone mechanical-property data.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Bone quality, Mechanical assessment, Bulk-modulus-like index, In vivo, Percutaneous kyphoplasty

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP), a metabolic disease characterized by reduced strength and increased fracture risk of bone, is clinically diagnosed and quantified by bone mineral density (BMD).1,2 With the continued aging of the global population, the prevalence of OP is expected to increase substantially, posing a major public health challenge worldwide.3 Among osteoporotic fractures, the human spine is particularly vulnerable. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 700,000 osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) occur annually in the United States alone.4 These VCFs often result in chronic pain, physical disability, and a pronounced decline in quality of life among elderly patients 5, 6, 7. Typically measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), BMD reflects the average mineral content of bone tissue. However, BMD is fundamentally a density-based index and does not directly represent bone mechanical competence. Critical determinants of bone strength—such as microarchitecture, remodeling dynamics, and intrinsic tissue material properties—are not captured by BMD measurements 8, 9, 10. Like other imaging-based clinical assessments, BMD benefits from being non-invasive, yet it lacks a direct mechanical interpretation. Numerous studies have reported a modest or even weak correlation between increased BMD and reduced fracture risk,11,12 underscoring its limitations for characterizing osteoporotic bone fragility. These limitations underscore the need for complementary approaches that can more directly characterize the mechanical quality and fragility of osteoporotic bone.

Innovative techniques that can overcome the limitations of BMD are therefore highly desirable in clinical practice. Ex vivo mechanical testing can directly quantify bone mechanical properties using standardized protocols; however, it requires harvested specimens, elaborate specimen preparation, and specialized loading equipment.13,14 Consequently, it is destructive and unsuitable for routine clinical application. Various imaging-based and non-invasive techniques, including quantitative computed tomography (QCT), high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and quantitative ultrasound (QUS), have been developed to characterize bone density, volume fraction, microarchitecture, and other indicators of bone quality15, 16, 17, 18. HR-pQCT enables high-resolution assessment of trabecular architecture and cortical porosity and can be combined with finite element analysis (FEA) to estimate bone strength. Nevertheless, its application is largely restricted to peripheral skeletal sites, such as the radius and tibia, and it cannot assess axial skeletal sites such as the lumbar spine because of its small scanning aperture, limited field of view, and low-dose high-resolution acquisition configuration.15,19 CT-based finite element analysis (CT-FEA) is among the most accurate approaches for predicting vertebral strength. It uses patient-specific CT-derived geometry and density-dependent material properties to simulate the mechanical response of vertebral bone and has demonstrated strong correlations with ex vivo vertebral compression tests.13,17,20 However, CT-FEA involves multiple computational steps, including image segmentation, three-dimensional reconstruction, mesh generation, material property assignment, and numerical solution, resulting in substantial computational cost and limiting its suitability for rapid or real-time clinical assessment.17,20 Moreover, its predictive accuracy depends strongly on the adopted bone constitutive models, density–property relationships, and material parameters. MRI can assess bone quality through parameters such as marrow fat fraction and ultrashort echo-time imaging without ionizing radiation, but its broader clinical application is constrained by relatively low spatial resolution, high cost, and the absence of standardized criteria for mechanical assessment.16 Quantitative ultrasound provides a portable, inexpensive, and radiation-free alternative for bone assessment, but its established clinical application remains largely limited to calcaneal screening and fracture-risk stratification.21 Emerging ultrasound-based techniques, including axial-transmission methods and radiofrequency echographic multispectrometry (REMS), can provide indirect structural or fragility-related indices, whereas conventional ultrasound elastography remains unsuitable for routine measurement of mineralized-bone stiffness. In particular, direct assessment of deep vertebral mechanical properties is hindered by soft-tissue attenuation, strong reflection and scattering at bone interfaces, and the complex heterogeneous and anisotropic nature of bone.22,23 Overall, existing techniques either provide indirect surrogates of bone quality or estimate mechanical competence through model-dependent calculations. None can directly measure the true mechanical properties of vertebral bone in vivo and in real time.14,24 This unmet clinical need motivates the development of a mechanically based intraoperative assessment method using the pressure–volume response inherently recorded during Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP).

PKP is a standard minimally invasive treatment for osteoporotic VCFs.25 During the procedure (Fig. 1a), a percutaneous puncture is first performed to establish a working channel into the vertebral body, and then an inflatable balloon catheter is inserted through the channel and expanded within the cancellous bone to create a cavity and partially restore vertebral height. After balloon deflation and removal, bone cement is injected into the cavity to improve vertebral stability. Balloon expansion is controlled using a hydraulic inflation system comprising a fluid-injection syringe and inflatable balloon catheter (Fig. 1b). In this study, we leverage the in vivo surgical setting of PKP to propose a novel, patient-specific, and mechanically grounded approach for assessing vertebral bone quality. During PKP, the internal balloon pressure and injected fluid volume can be recorded to obtain a pressure-volume curves as illustrated in Fig. 1c, where P0 and V0 are respectively the initial pressure and volume when the balloon first comes into contact with the cancellous bone, and their subsequent increments are denoted as dP and dV, respectively. By analyzing the pressure–volume response (Fig. 1c), we establish a mechanical index κ, analogous to the bulk modulus, to quantify the effective mechanical stiffness of cancellous bone:

The PKP-based method proposed here is an approach directly assessing vertebral mechanical property in vivo, without introducing additional surgical invasion, tissue damage, imaging examination, or operative steps to the standard PKP procedure. The derived index κ characterizing the effective stiffness of the local vertebral bone, is a direct and patient-specific mechanical measurement of the mechanical quality of bone and well complements BMD to assess the bone mechanical quality and fracture risk. More importantly, the proposed method opens a new avenue for the direct acquisition of in vivo mechanical properties of human bone. Such patient-specific mechanical data are critically important for advancing digital orthopedics and improving the fidelity and predictive capability of finite element models. As a preliminary study, the present work aims to establish the proposed PKP-based assessment method, clarify the relationship between the derived mechanical index κ and vertebral bone stiffness, and examine the robustness of the measurement under clinically relevant variations in balloon geometry and puncture trajectory. CT-based finite element simulations of a vertebral body were performed using bone material models of progressively increasing fidelity, incorporating anisotropy, heterogeneity, plasticity, and cortical bone damage. The results demonstrate that the absolute value of κ remains highly consistent across different balloon geometries and puncture trajectories. Moreover, κ shows a positive linear relationship with the modulus of bone material, supporting its use as a mechanics-based indicator of bone quality.

Materials and methods

Establishment of the computational model

The workflow for finite element model construction, including the segmentation of the cortical and cancellous bone regions, is illustrated in Fig. 2. The study was approved by Medical Ethics Committee, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (2022058K). Lumbar computed tomography (CT) data were obtained from a 34-year-old male subject with no recorded vertebral fractures or orthopedic pathology. The images had an in-plane resolution of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm and a slice thickness of 1 mm. A three-dimensional (3D) model of the L4 vertebral segment was reconstructed from 305 axial CT slices using Mimics Research 19.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Non-osseous tissues, including cartilage and surrounding soft tissues, were removed by adjusting grayscale thresholds, and cortical and cancellous bone were segmented according to grayscale intensity and geometry. Because the characteristic dimensions of PKP balloons are on the millimeter scale and therefore substantially exceed the microscale dimensions of cancellous trabeculae 26, 27, 28, cancellous bone was modeled as an effective continuum material in our FEM simulations. This homogenized representation is commonly used in PKP simulations to improve computational stability and efficiency through avoiding excessive geometric complexity and contact interactions at the trabecular scale. The reconstructed geometry was smoothed in Geomagic Wrap 2021 to reduce surface irregularities and associated numerical artifacts. The cortical and cancellous bone models were then imported into HyperMesh 2017 (Altair Engineering, Troy, MI, USA) and discretized with four-node tetrahedral elements (C3D4).29 A mesh-converged model containing more than 400,000 elements was employed in all simulations. The cortical and cancellous bone domains shared nodes at their interface, enforcing perfect bonding and displacement continuity. The cancellous-bone region subjected to balloon expansion was predefined for subsequent application of loading boundary.

Workflow of finite element model construction from CT data and schematic illustration of cortical and cancellous bone segmentation.

Material properties and parameter setting

All FEM simulations were conducted in Abaqus 2016 (Dassault SIMULIA, France). Unless otherwise specified, both cortical and cancellous bone were initially modeled as homogeneous and isotropic, with representative material properties of healthy bone reported in the literature30 and summarized in Table 1. Subsequent analyses modified the constitutive models and parameters to investigate the influence of osteoporosis severity, material anisotropy, heterogeneity, plasticity, and bone failure. Balloon expansion within the cancellous bone was simulated using the surface-based fluid cavity capability in Abaqus, which enabled the cavity pressure and fluid volume to be recorded throughout inflation. Two initial cavity geometries were evaluated: an ellipsoid with principal axes of 20 mm, 4 mm, and 4 mm (initial volume V0 = 167.55 mm3) and a cylinder with a height of 20 mm and a diameter of 4 mm (initial volume V0 = 251.32 mm3). During PKP surgery, patients are positioned prone, and the spine is generally considered unloaded, and therefore no external load was applied in the FEM model. An explicit solver was employed for all the simulations. Compared with the implicit algorithm, the explicit approach produced comparable results (differences <5%) while reducing CPU time to approximately one-eighth. The maximum cavity pressure was set to 1.5 MPa, consistent with the upper range reported during clinical PKP.31,32

OP degrades the mechanical properties of cancellous bone through bone loss, microstructural deterioration, and reduction in tissue properties.12,33,34 In the baseline model, cancellous bone was modeled as a homogeneous, linear elastic material. OP severity was defined as the percentage reduction in effective elastic modulus relative to healthy bone. Three states were considered: normal, moderate OP, and severe OP. The elastic moduli of bone in moderate and severe OP were reduced by 50% and 80%, respectively, relative to the normal condition. Note that the simplistic reduction does not reproduce the full coupled changes in bone mass, architecture, tissue properties, and cortical integrity that occur in clinical OP. The final inflated balloon shape depends on both balloon properties and the surrounding bone. Since the mechanical properties of cancellous bone vary significantly across OP stages, and hence it is challenging to predict its shape. To test whether this balloon shape variation influences the proposed measurement, cylindrical and ellipsoidal cavities were simulated.

Healthy bone is mechanically anisotropy, with longitudinal superiority, i.e., mechanical superiority in the major physiological loading direction.33,34 However, this functionally favorable anisotropy diminishes with increasing OP severity, and so the bone material may be approximated as anisotropic or isotropic depending on health status. We therefore examined whether material anisotropy affect the applicability of the method and index κ. Specifically, transversely isotropic constitutive models were considered for both cancellous and cortical bone.

Cancellous bone is intrinsically heterogeneous, and therefore it is necessary to test whether material heterogeneity affect the applicability of the proposed protocol or the index κ. In FEM modeling of cancellous bone, heterogeneous material properties can be assigned based on CT data.36 To facilitate the material assignment, the cancellous bone was divided into 10 property levels according to regional Hounsfield unit (HU) values. During PKP, puncture first establishes a working channel for the subsequent procedures.40 Different puncture routes may be selected in clinical practice, depending on patient body size, vertebral morphology, fracture severity, and surgeons’ preferences. The commonly used transpedicular approach (Fig. 6a) provides clear anatomic landmarks but may limit access to the contralateral side. A lateral pedicle-margin approach (Fig. 6b) can improve access but may increase the risk of cement leakage.41 Since the two routes position the balloon differently, we examined whether puncture route affect the proposed measurement protocol and the derived index κ.

In vitro compression tests of human vertebral bodies indicate that compression fractures typically initiate as microfractures in cancellous bone and may eventually progress to cortical failure.42,43 We therefore compared simulations with and without cortical bone failure. Failed cortical bone was represented by reducing its elastic modulus to 10 MPa, approximately three orders of magnitude below the intact value.

For normal cancellous bone, reported effective yield strength, ultimate strength, and failure plastic strain are approximately 1.95 MPa, 2.65 MPa, and 8.2%, respectively. Since clinical intra-balloon pressure during PKP can reach 1.8 MPa,44 plastic deformation of cancellous bone may occur in some OP patients during PKP and could influence the proposed measurement. For the normal-bone model, the yield strength, ultimate strength, and failure plastic strain were set to 2 MPa, 2.7 MPa, and 10%, respectively, according to the experimental data.44 The corresponding properties were reduced by 50% for moderate OP and by 80% for severe OP. The injected fluid volume was first increased beyond the elastic limit and then decreased to simulate a complete loading-unloading cycle.

Results and discussion

Establishment of the κ-modulus relationship

Fig. 3a shows a linear relationship between the pressure increment ΔP and the fluid volume increment ΔV for both cylindrical and ellipsoidal balloon shapes. Furthermore, the derived mechanical index κ linearly decreases with increasing OP severity, and the curves for the two balloon shapes nearly overlapped, as shown in Fig. 3b; the relative difference in κ was less than 2% for each OP degree. These findings support the mechanical feasibility of the PKP-based method to assess bone quality and health. The linear relationship between ΔP and ΔV simplifies measurement and calculation of κ, while the negative linear relationship between κ and OP degree supports its use as an indicator of bone quality. The minimal effect of ballon shape further suggests that the measurement is robust to balloon geometry, which is advantageous for clinical implementation.

(a) Curve of pressure increment (ΔP) versus volume increment (ΔV) during balloon expansion; (b) Variation of the derived mechanical index κ with respect to the degree of OP and the relative difference of κ between two possible balloon shapes. Here bone materials are assumed to be isotropic, and two possible balloon shapes, cylindrical and ellipsoidal, are considered.

Persistence of the κ-modulus relationship under bone anisotropy

Fig. 4a and b shows representative ΔP-ΔV curves and the variation κ with OP severity, respectively. The trends were consistent with those obtained using the isotropic material model: ΔP remained linearly related to ΔV, and κ retained a negative linear relationship with OP severity. At the same ΔV or OP level, respectively, the anisotropic model produced higher ΔP and κ values than the isotropic model. This difference reflects the higher longitudinal modulus (Ezz) assigned to anisotropic bone and its adaptation to physiological loading. Balloon geometry again had little effect on κ, with relative differences below 3% at every OP level. Mechanically, this insensitivity is expected because κ approximates the bulk modulus under hydrostatic loading and is therefore related to the combined elastic response in all three material directions.

(a) Example curve of pressure increment (ΔP) versus fluid volume increment ΔV; (b) Variation of the derived mechanical index κ with the degree of OP and the relative difference of κ for two balloon shapes, considering the anisotropy of bone materials and comparing cylindrical and ellipsoidal balloon shapes.

Persistence of the κ-modulus relationship under bone heterogeneity

Fig. 5a and b shows representative ΔP-ΔV curves and the relationship between κ and OP severity, respectively. The heterogeneous models preserved the two principal trends observed in the homogeneous models: ΔP increased linearly with ΔV, and κ decreased linearly with increasing OP severity. Thus, cancellous-bone heterogeneity did not alter the fundamental response of the proposed PKP-based technique or compromise its feasibility for characterizing bone health. This result is mechanically plausible because κ represents an effective, volume-averaged property and is therefore relatively insensitive to local variations in material properties.

Persistence of the κ-modulus relationship under elastoplastic bone behavior

The complete ΔP-ΔV curves and von Mises stress contours after unloading are shown in Fig. 6. Because material plasticity produces a nonlinear ΔP-ΔV response, κ can be calculated from either (i) the initial linear portion of the loading curve or (ii) the linear portion of the unloading curve. In normal bone, cancellous tissue remained elastic and ΔP varied linearly with ΔV. In mild OP, the response entered the plastic regime, but the unloading curve remained nearly parallel to the elastic loading segment, so the two methods produced similar κ values. In severe OP, substantial plastic deformation occurred, and κ calculated from unloading was slightly lower than the value obtained from initial elastic loading. Thus, loading-based κ minimizes the influence of cancellous-bone plasticity, whereas unloading-based κ may capture the reduced yield strength of severely osteoporotic bone. The lower unloading value arises because irreversible plastic deformation in cancellous bone prevents full recovery of elastic deformation in the surrounding cortical bone. Consistent with this explanation, residual von Mises stresses were negligible in normal bone but increased in magnitude and spatial extent with OP severity (Fig. 6c–e).

Robustness of κ to variations in puncture trajectory

Since Sections 3.1–3.3 have demonstrated that balloon shape has negligible influence on κ, only the cylindrical cavity was considered in this analysis. FEM simulations were performed with three types of bone material models: isotropic, anisotropic, and heterogeneous. As shown in Fig. 7c and d, the relative difference in κ between the two puncture routes was small (<0.4% for isotropic models, <1.4% for anisotropic models, <5.5% for heterogeneous models), confirming robustness of the measurement to clinically relevant variation in puncture trajectory. In the isotropic models, the small discrepancy arose primarily from differences in the constraints imposed by surrounding cortical bone. Material anisotropy introduced an additional directional effect and slightly increased the discrepancy. In the heterogeneous models, changing the puncture path altered the local distribution of material properties around the pressurized cavity, producing a somewhat larger difference in κ. As OP progressed, regional elastic moduli became more similar, material heterogeneity diminished, and the response approached that of a homogeneous material; accordingly, the difference in κ decreased. Overall, the effect of puncture route remains within an acceptable range.

Influence of cortical bone failure on κ

Fig. 8a compares the ΔP-ΔV curves across OP severities with and without cortical bone failure, and Fig. 8b presents the corresponding κ values and relative differences. Cortical failure weakened structural confinement and facilitated balloon expansion within cancellous bone. Consequently, ΔP increased more slowly with ΔV, producing lower κ values. Cortical failure changed κ by approximately 10% in all three bone-health states; nevertheless, the states remained clearly distinguishable by κ. Clinically, this effect of cortical bone failure could be addressed in either of two ways: (i) cortical integrity could be assessed preoperatively on CT and used to correct κ as an estimate of cancellous-bone properties, or (ii) κ could be interpreted directly as an integrated indicator of vertebral health that reflects deterioration in both cancellous and cortical bone.

Limitations and future prospects

The present study is a preliminary computational investigation, and several limitations should be addressed before clinical translation. First, the proposed method has so far been validated only through FEM simulations. Experimental studies, followed by prospective clinical validation, are required to establish the reproducibility of κ, its relationship with conventional indicators such as BMD, and clinically meaningful reference ranges for different levels of bone quality.

Second, although anisotropy, heterogeneity, elastoplasticity, and cortical bone damage were considered to improve model fidelity, the current models still simplify the complex structure and mechanical behavior of vertebral bone. Future studies should incorporate more patient-specific material distributions, advanced constitutive and damage models, and additional procedure-related factors such as balloon size, membrane compliance, inflation rate, and vertebral fracture morphology. The mechanical contribution of the balloon and inflation system should also be experimentally calibrated.

Despite these limitations, the proposed method has strong translational potential because the required pressure–volume data are inherently generated during standard PKP and can be acquired without additional surgical invasion or imaging. Following clinical validation, κ may provide a mechanics-based complement to BMD and support individualized intraoperative and postoperative decision-making.

More importantly, systematic application of the method may enable direct acquisition of patient-specific in vivo mechanical properties of human bone. Such data could facilitate the establishment of mechanical-property databases, advance digital and intelligent orthopedics, and improve the fidelity and predictive capability of patient-specific FEM models.

Conclusions

By leveraging the equipment and workflow already used during PKP, this study proposes a clinically feasible method for the intraoperative mechanical characterization of vertebral bone without introducing additional tissue damage or operative steps. A bulk-modulus-like mechanical index, κ, is derived from the pressure–volume response during balloon inflation to quantify the effective mechanical stiffness of the local vertebral bone and to provide information complementary to conventional BMD-based assessment of OP.

The mechanical basis and robustness of the proposed index were systematically investigated through CT-based finite element simulations. Using progressively refined bone models, the approximately linear relationship between κ and vertebral bone stiffness was consistently preserved when anisotropy, heterogeneity, and elastoplasticity were incorporated. In parallel, the absolute value of κ showed only limited sensitivity to variations in balloon geometry and puncture trajectory, indicating good consistency under clinically relevant procedural variations. Cortical bone damage produced a larger influence on κ, but the modeled bone-health states remained distinguishable.

These findings support the potential of κ as a mechanics-based indicator that complements BMD by providing direct information on vertebral mechanical competence. Following experimental and clinical validation, the proposed method may assist intraoperative decision-making, improve patient-specific assessment of OP severity, and support postoperative treatment planning. More importantly, it opens a promising avenue for the direct acquisition of in vivo mechanical properties of human bone. Such patient-specific mechanical data may contribute to the development of digital orthopedics, improve the fidelity and predictive capability of FEM models, and ultimately support more individualized musculoskeletal assessment and treatment.
