Assessing the quality of general anesthesia-related short videos on TikTok: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yongbo Duan, Jun Kang, Chengjian Wang, Wenjun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTikTok has become a significant source of health information for the public, yet misinformation on the platform may adversely affect health decision-making. To date, no study has systematically evaluated the quality of general anesthesia-related short videos on TikTok.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the quality and reliability of general anesthesia-related videos on TikTok using validated instruments and to examine correlations among video source, duration, user engagement, and content quality.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of TikTok videos retrieved using the keyword “全身麻醉” (general anesthesia). Video quality was evaluated using the Global Quality Scale (GQS), modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), JAMA benchmark criteria, and a custom composite score assessing risk disclosure, procedural completeness, and accuracy. Uploaders were categorized by professional background. Statistical analyses included Holm-Bonferroni corrected non-parametric tests, Spearman correlations, and multivariable ordinal logistic regression adjusting for video duration.ResultsOf 150 retrieved videos, 127 met the inclusion criteria. Overall quality was low-to-moderate: median GQS 2.0 (IQR: 1.0–3.0), JAMA 2.0 (1.0–2.0), mDISCERN 2.0 (2.0–2.0), and custom score 4.0 (3.0–4.0), with notably poor risk disclosure (median 0.0). Videos from anesthesia professionals scored highest across all metrics. Engagement metrics did not correlate with video quality. Video duration correlated positively only with GQS (r = 0.339, P < 0.001). Multivariable analysis confirmed that uploader background independently predicted quality; patients and the general public had significantly lower odds of producing high-quality content compared with anesthesia professionals (P < 0.001).ConclusionsGeneral anesthesia-related videos on TikTok exhibit substantial quality deficiencies, particularly in risk communication. Video popularity does not reflect scientific reliability. Given that professional background independently predicts content quality, healthcare professionals should actively create evidence-based content, and platforms must algorithmically prioritize authoritative sources to mitigate misinformation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03920-x.

Background

General anesthesia is a cornerstone of modern surgical practice, yet its safety profile and risk of complications remain a primary concern for the public [1]. Despite this interest, patients often lack access to authoritative, comprehensive information and frequently rely on informal discussions or fragmented online sources for their understanding of anesthesia. With the growing popularity of short-form video platforms, an increasing number of patients and their families are turning to platforms such as TikTok for medical information [2–4]. However, the quality of content on these platforms is highly variable and may be compromised by oversimplification, commercial bias, or insufficient scientific rigor [5]. Existing research on health-related short videos has largely focused on chronic disease management (e.g., hypertension) [6], leaving a significant gap in the systematic evaluation of content pertaining to general anesthesia. Given the complex physiological mechanisms involved in anesthesia and the critical importance of risk communication, inaccurate information may contribute to preoperative anxiety and impair informed decision-making [7, 8]. Therefore, there is a clear practical and clinical need to evaluate the quality and content of short videos related to general anesthesia.

This study aims to quantitatively assess the quality and reliability of general anesthesia-related videos on TikTok using validated evaluation tools such as the Global Quality Scale (GQS), modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), and JAMA benchmark criteria. Furthermore, it seeks to examine the relationships among video source, duration, and user engagement metrics (e.g., likes and comments). The findings are expected to inform strategies for improving the dissemination of evidence-based anesthesia information and to provide actionable recommendations for enhancing the review and curation of medical content on short-form video platforms.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Data were collected from TikTok using a priori pilot searches to identify the most frequently used search terms among the general public. Multiple relevant keywords, including “全麻” (colloquial general anesthesia), “手术麻醉” (surgical anesthesia), and “麻醉风险” (anesthesia risk), were tested. The term “全身麻醉” (general anesthesia) was selected as the core search term because it yielded the largest volume of relevant results and best reflected the public’s actual search behavior. The top 150 videos were selected based on TikTok’s default comprehensive ranking algorithm, which integrates content relevance, user engagement, and public search habits. This sampling strategy reflects the content most frequently exposed to the general public, which aligns with our study aim of evaluating the quality of anesthesia-related science popularization content actually accessed by the public. Data collection was conducted on August 18, 2025, to minimize temporal bias. To mitigate potential distortions from search history and personalized recommendations, all searches were performed in a new browser session without user authentication. No restrictions were applied regarding the publication date of the videos.

A stringent screening process was subsequently applied to exclude videos that: (a) were irrelevant to the topic of general anesthesia; (b) were duplicates; (c) constituted commercial advertisements; (d) were in languages other than Chinese; or (e) had been uploaded less than one week prior to data collection. After screening, the following eight parameters were systematically recorded for each included video: title, user engagement metrics (number of likes, comments, collections, and shares), time elapsed since upload, video duration, and source of upload. Video sources were classified into four distinct categories: healthcare professionals (subclassified into anesthesiologists and non-anesthesiologist medical professionals), patients, and the general public.

Quality assessment

Two independent researchers (Y. Duan and J. Kang) performed a blinded evaluation using three validated instruments: the Global Quality Scale (GQS), the JAMA benchmark criteria, and the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tool. The GQS employs a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 to 5), with higher scores indicating superior overall quality (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials) [9]. The mDISCERN tool evaluates five domains—attribution, timeliness, content validity, and disclosure compliance—yielding a total score between 0 and 5 (Table S2) [10]. The JAMA benchmark criteria assess four key dimensions—authorship, timeliness, content validity, and disclosure of conflicts of interest—generating a composite score from 0 to 4 (Table S3) [11]. Given that established instruments (GQS, JAMA, mDISCERN) were not designed to specifically evaluate the adequacy of anesthesia-related risk communication or procedural description, a supplementary composite Video Score was developed for this study. The three dimensions—risk disclosure, procedural completeness, and informational accuracy—were derived from the core ethical and legal components of informed consent for anesthesia, as articulated in international professional guidelines and Chinese national standards for anesthesia practice [12]. To operationalize these domains for short-form video content, the research team refined the scoring criteria through repeated group discussion and consensus to improve clarity and applicability. Each dimension was scored on a three-point ordinal scale (0 = not mentioned; 1 = partially mentioned; 2 = comprehensively described). Prior to formal data extraction, the scoring framework underwent pilot testing by two independent raters using a separate set of 15 TikTok videos unrelated to the study sample; inter-rater agreement was excellent (Cohen’s κ = 0.79), and operational definitions were further optimized through consensus discussion. In the full study sample, the composite Video Score demonstrated strong convergent validity with the GQS (r = 0.531, P < 0.001), JAMA (r = 0.936, P < 0.001), and mDISCERN (r = 0.795, P < 0.001), supporting its consistency with established quality metrics while capturing anesthesia-specific content domains. To ensure methodological rigor, a double-blinded assessment was implemented where evaluators were unaware of each other’s ratings. Inter-assessor agreement, calculated using Cohen’s kappa (κ) for the entire dataset, yielded a value of 0.82, indicating almost perfect agreement. Any scoring discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a senior expert (W. Yan) until consensus was achieved.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.3, R Core Team). Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), normally distributed variables as mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with Dunn’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons and Holm-Bonferroni sequential correction for multiple testing. Effect sizes for between-group comparisons were reported as Kruskal-Wallis eta squared (η²), where η² ≥ 0.06 indicated a moderate effect and η² ≥ 0.14 indicated a large effect. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

Multivariable ordinal logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the independent association between uploader type (independent variable) and video quality scores (dependent variables, including GQS, JAMA, mDISCERN, and total video score), with video duration set as the adjustment variable. Cumulative odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for the regression models. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Video screening process and baseline characteristics

A total of 150 short videos related to general anesthesia were initially retrieved from TikTok through a systematic search. Following pre-defined exclusion criteria and independent screening and verification by two researchers, 23 videos were excluded—6 due to irrelevance to the topic and 17 as duplicates. Ultimately, 127 videos were included for quality assessment and data analysis. The screening process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of video selection

Baseline characteristics of the included videos are summarized in Table 1. Video duration exhibited a right-skewed distribution, with a median length of 58.00 s (IQR: 34.00–114.50), indicating that the majority of videos were between 1 and 2 min long. User engagement metrics demonstrated moderate audience interaction: median likes were 1694.00 (IQR: 331.50–6613.00), median collections were 207.00 (IQR: 42.50–691.00), median comments were 349.00 (IQR: 80.00–1526.00), and median shares were 311.00 (IQR: 77.50–1369.00).

Regarding quality assessment, median scores were as follows: Global Quality Scale (GQS), 2.00 (IQR: 1.00–3.00); JAMA benchmark, 2.00 (IQR: 1.00–2.00); modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), 2.00 (IQR: 2.00–2.00); and video score, 4.00 (IQR: 3.00–4.00). These results indicate an overall low to moderate quality of video content. Further analysis revealed notably poor performance in risk disclosure, with a median score of 0.00 (IQR: 0.00–1.00), suggesting that the majority of videos failed to adequately address anesthesia-related risks. Procedural completeness also scored low, with a median of 1.00 (IQR: 0.00–1.00), reflecting insufficient description of anesthesia procedures. Informational accuracy received a median score of 2.00 (IQR: 1.00–2.00), which was relatively higher than other dimensions but still requires substantial improvement.

Distribution of video sources

Based on professional background and identity, video uploaders were classified into four main categories (Fig. 2). Non-anesthesia healthcare professionals constituted the largest source of videos, contributing 64 videos (50.4% of the total). Anesthesia professionals uploaded 18 videos (14.2%). Combined, healthcare professionals in the broader sense produced a total of 82 videos, accounting for 64.6% of the sample and representing the primary creators of content on this topic.

Fig. 2Distribution of video uploaders

Distribution of video uploaders

Additionally, patients uploaded 20 videos (15.7%), which primarily featured personal experiences and subjective accounts. The general public contributed 25 videos (19.7%), typically containing personal opinions, non-professional discussions, or re-edited/reposted content. This distribution indicates that although healthcare professionals serve as the main source of content, a substantial proportion of videos originate from non-professional contributors.

Analysis of video parameters

Video duration differed significantly across uploader groups (P < 0.001; Table 2). Videos uploaded by patients were significantly longer, with a median duration of 114.50 s (IQR: 82.25, 169.00), likely reflecting the need to elaborate on personal experiences. Videos from both anesthesia professionals and non-anesthesia healthcare professionals were moderate and similar in length, with median durations of 58.50 and 50.50 s, respectively, indicating a tendency toward concise presentation among medical creators. Videos from the general public had the shortest median duration (41.00 s) but exhibited the widest distribution (IQR: 32.00, 140.00), suggesting substantial variability in production style within this group.

User engagement metrics followed a different pattern from content quality. No statistically significant differences were observed among the four groups in terms of likes, collections, or comments (P > 0.05). Notably, videos from the general public received the highest median number of likes (4204.00), though with considerable variability (IQR: 683.00, 20740.00), indicating inconsistent popularity within this category. Shares were the only engagement metric that differed significantly (P = 0.009). Videos from the general public achieved the highest median share count (629.00), significantly exceeding that of the patient group (85.50). Both anesthesia professionals and non-anesthesia healthcare professionals received intermediate and comparable median shares (397.00 vs. 359.50, respectively).

Analysis of video quality

Quality assessment revealed significant disparities in all quality metrics among uploader groups (Table 2; Figs. 3 and 4). Videos produced by anesthesia professionals consistently received the highest scores across all quality instruments, with median scores of 3.00 (IQR: 2.00, 3.00) on the Global Quality Scale (GQS), 2.00 for both the JAMA benchmark and mDISCERN tool, and 4.00 for the total video score (all P < 0.001). Videos from non-anesthesia healthcare professionals ranked second in quality, with median GQS, JAMA, and mDISCERN scores of 2.00 and a median video score of 4.00, though score distributions showed greater variability (wider IQRs) compared with those from anesthesia professionals.

In contrast, videos uploaded by patients demonstrated notably lower quality, with median GQS and JAMA scores of 1.00, a median mDISCERN score of 1.00 (IQR: 1.00, 2.00), and a median video score of 2.00. Videos from the general public were also of low quality, with median GQS and JAMA scores of 1.00, a median mDISCERN score of 2.00 (IQR: 1.00, 2.00), and a median video score of 3.00.

Detailed analysis of the video score components showed uniformly poor performance in risk disclosure across all groups, with median scores of 0.00. However, some videos from non-anesthesia healthcare professionals included risk information (upper IQR limit: 1.00), resulting in a statistically significant intergroup difference (P = 0.043). In procedural completeness, anesthesia professionals showed the most consistent performance (median: 1.00; IQR: 1.00, 1.00). Non-anesthesia professionals and patients exhibited mixed results (median: 1.00; IQR: 0.00, 1.00), while the general public largely omitted procedural descriptions (median: 0.00; IQR: 0.00, 0.00), with highly significant group differences (P < 0.001). For informational accuracy, anesthesia professionals again demonstrated high consistency (median: 2.00; IQR: 2.00, 2.00), followed by non-anesthesia professionals (median: 2.00; IQR: 1.00, 2.00). Videos from patients and the general public scored poorly (median: 1.00 for both), with extremely significant differences among groups (P < 0.001).

To determine the independent association between uploader type and video quality, multivariable ordinal logistic regression models were constructed with adjustment for video duration (Table 3). With anesthesia professionals set as the reference group, uploader type remained independently associated with all video quality scores after adjustment. Patients and the general public exhibited significantly lower cumulative odds of achieving higher total video quality scores, GQS scores, and mDISCERN scores compared with anesthesia professionals (all P < 0.001). Non-anesthesia healthcare professionals demonstrated significantly lower GQS scores (OR = 0.301, 95%CI: 0.104–0.869, P = 0.026) but did not differ significantly from anesthesia professionals in total video quality or mDISCERN scores (both P > 0.05). For JAMA scores, complete data separation was observed across groups, with all videos from anesthesia professionals achieving the maximum score while all other groups demonstrated the minimum score, limiting further statistical comparison.

Regarding quality indicators, video duration demonstrated a significant positive correlation with GQS scores (r = 0.339, P < 0.001), whereas no significant correlation was observed between video duration and JAMA scores, mDISCERN scores, or total video score (P > 0.05). This indicates that while longer videos may contain more comprehensive content—as reflected in higher GQS ratings—they do not necessarily ensure greater authority or reliability of the information presented.

Notably, none of the user engagement metrics (likes, comments, collections, or shares) showed significant correlations with any of the video quality scores (GQS, JAMA, mDISCERN, or total video score; P > 0.05). This suggests that the popularity of a video is not indicative of its content quality. In contrast, the quality assessment tools demonstrated high internal consistency, with significant positive correlations between GQS and JAMA (r = 0.487, P < 0.001), GQS and mDISCERN (r = 0.432, P < 0.001), GQS and total video score (r = 0.531, P < 0.001), JAMA and mDISCERN (r = 0.566, P < 0.001), JAMA and total video score (r = 0.936, P < 0.001), and mDISCERN and total video score (r = 0.795, P < 0.001), indicating good convergent validity among these instruments in evaluating video quality.

Discussion

This study represents the first systematic cross-sectional evaluation of the quality and reliability of short videos related to general anesthesia on TikTok. Four primary findings emerged: First, the overall quality of video content was low to moderate, with a critical deficiency in the disclosure of anesthesia-related risks. Second, video quality was strongly associated with the professional background of uploaders, with anesthesiologists producing significantly higher-quality content than non-professional sources. Third, user engagement metrics (likes, comments, collections, and shares) showed no significant correlation with information quality. Fourth, video duration showed a significant positive correlation with GQS scores, but no significant correlations with JAMA or mDISCERN scores, which assess the authority and reliability of content.

Interpretation of key findings

The generally poor quality of general anesthesia-related videos observed in this study aligns with previous research on short videos covering other medical topics [13]. A particularly critical shortcoming was the near-absence of risk disclosure (median score 0.00 across all groups), indicating that viewers are likely underestimating the potential risks and complications of general anesthesia. This information gap may impair informed decision-making and even increase preoperative anxiety [14, 15]. Although videos uploaded by healthcare professionals—particularly anesthesiologists—demonstrated better accuracy and procedural completeness, they still fell short in fulfilling risk communication responsibilities. This suggests a systematic deficiency in translating professional knowledge into public-facing health communication.

A particularly concerning finding in our study was the absence of any significant correlation between video popularity (measured by likes, comments, collections, and shares) and objective markers of information quality across all validated assessment scales. Specifically, videos from the general public achieved the highest median likes and shares, yet had among the lowest quality scores, while videos produced by anesthesia professionals— which consistently ranked highest across all quality metrics—only achieved intermediate levels of user engagement. This finding directly challenges the pervasive assumption among the general public that popularity equates to credibility for online health information, and aligns with results from rigorous prior studies examining medical content in short-form videos [16, 17]. This dissociation indicates that TikTok’s core recommendation algorithm likely prioritizes user engagement, entertainment value, and emotional shareability over scientific accuracy and methodological rigor. Such an algorithmic mechanism inevitably creates a perverse incentive for content creation: sensationalized, emotionally charged, or oversimplified narratives gain disproportionate visibility, while evidence-based, clinically accurate content that lacks high-engagement stylistic elements struggles to reach target audiences [18]. For general anesthesia-related content in particular, this dynamic amplifies the spread of unsubstantiated claims, incomplete risk information, and misleading narratives about perioperative care.

The relationship between video duration and content quality exhibited a distinct, dimension-dependent pattern in this study. Longer videos were predominantly first-person perioperative narratives from patients, whose detailed, experience-driven descriptive content correlated positively with Global Quality Scale (GQS) scores, a metric reflecting overall content comprehensiveness and audience acceptability [19]. However, this duration-associated improvement in descriptive completeness did not extend to metrics assessing the scientific rigor and authoritative credibility of the content (i.e., JAMA benchmark criteria, mDISCERN scale, and our custom composite video score). This dissociation highlights a critical principle: video duration alone does not guarantee high-quality, reliable anesthesia-related health information. Instead, adherence to standardized scientific norms, transparent disclosure of author credentials, and grounding in evidence-based clinical guidance are the fundamental pillars of trustworthy medical science popularization [20, 21].

Clinical and public health recommendations based on study findings

Derived from the core empirical results of this study—including the suboptimal overall quality of anesthesia popularization content, inadequate risk disclosure, superior scientific rigor of content created by anesthesia professionals, and the dissociation between video popularity and information quality—targeted and practicable clinical and public health recommendations are proposed. Anesthesia professionals may establish a standardized short-video content framework covering the introduction of general anesthesia procedures, standardized perioperative risk notification, perioperative rehabilitation guidance, and clarification of public misconceptions, with risk disclosure as an essential component in accordance with the ethical norms of clinical informed consent. Standardized popularization videos can also be integrated into the perioperative patient education pathway as an auxiliary tool for preoperative counseling and postoperative guidance, to alleviate preoperative anxiety and improve treatment compliance. For short-video platforms, the qualification verification mechanism for creators of anesthesia-related medical content may be improved, with authoritative certification for practicing anesthesiologists; traffic restrictions and risk prompts should be applied to content lacking standardized risk disclosure or evidence-based support, and recommendation algorithms may be optimized to prioritize content from qualified professionals with robust evidence. The general public should enhance their ability to critically appraise medical information, prioritize content from professionally certified creators, focus on risk disclosure completeness and evidence-based validity rather than user engagement, and recognize that online popularization content serves only as a reference and cannot substitute for professional clinical advice.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

First, potential retrieval and selection bias may exist. Although a priori pilot searches confirmed the representativeness of our core search term, the use of a single keyword may have omitted relevant content. Our sample was restricted to the top 150 videos from TikTok’s opaque proprietary ranking algorithm, which reflects high-exposure content accessed by the public rather than the full scope of available anesthesia-related videos, potentially introducing algorithm-driven bias. Second, the custom composite video score has inherent methodological limitations. While the framework was developed with reference to validated tools, underwent pilot testing, and demonstrated good convergent validity with established scales, it has not completed external validation. Equal weighting of dimensions may affect its discriminative ability, and residual risk of subjective rating bias cannot be fully eliminated. Third, the generalizability of the findings is restricted. This study was limited to Chinese-language content on the Chinese version of TikTok, and cannot be directly generalized to other short video platforms, language contexts, or healthcare settings. Finally, this cross-sectional study can only identify associations between variables, rather than establish causal relationships.

Conclusion

This study reveals widespread quality deficiencies in general anesthesia-related TikTok videos, particularly the near-absence of risk disclosure, despite content from anesthesia professionals demonstrating superior scientific rigor. The observed dissociation between popularity and information quality underscores the unreliability of engagement metrics as proxies for accuracy. Concerted efforts from healthcare professionals, platform administrators, and viewers are urgently needed to elevate the standard of publicly accessible anesthesia education.
