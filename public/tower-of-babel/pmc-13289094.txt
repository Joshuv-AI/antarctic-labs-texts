Predictors of gingival margin migration after fixed orthodontic treatment: a linear mixed model analysis of labial and lingual surfaces
Authors: Suheyla Kaya, Dilara Kahraman, Aybuke Ensarioğlu, Banu Mert, Arzu Ari Demirkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the influence of clinical periodontal, radiological, and demographic factors on gingival margin migration after fixed orthodontic treatment using linear mixed model analysis, with separate assessments of labial and lingual surfaces.MethodsThis retrospective single-cohort study analyzed pre and post treatment changes in 88 patients who underwent orthodontic treatment without a non treated control group. Patients were periodontally healthy based on the absence of bone loss on panoramic radiographs and three dimensional intraoral scans. The mean age was 19.25 ± 7.46 years, and measurements were performed on mandibular anterior teeth. Clinical crown length and keratinized tissue width were measured using three dimensional intraoral scans, and cephalometric variables were obtained from lateral radiographs. Gingival margin migration was defined as the change in clinical crown length from before to after treatment. Predictor variables included dental crowding, alveolar ridge width, incisor mandibular plane angle, gonion–menton–sella–nasion angle, symphysis morphology, age, sex, treatment duration, and keratinized tissue width. Linear mixed models were used to assess gingival margin migration across all surfaces and separately for labial and lingual surfaces, using baseline and treatment related changes as predictors.ResultsOf 530 screened patients, 88 were included (72% female). In baseline models, keratinized tissue width, dental crowding, and sex were significant predictors of gingival margin migration. Lingual migration was additionally associated with incisor mandibular plane angle, symphysis morphology, and age. On the labial surface, incisors showed more coronal gingival margin position than canines relative to baseline. In models based on treatment related changes, keratinized tissue width, dental crowding, and age were consistent predictors across all surfaces. Labial margins showed more coronal position than lingual margins, while lingual migration was negatively associated with changes in incisor mandibular plane angle. Changes in keratinized tissue width had the strongest effect.ConclusionsBaseline keratinized tissue width and its increase during treatment were the strongest predictors of gingival margin stability. Dental crowding, incisor inclination, and symphysis morphology showed surface and tooth specific effects, whereas alveolar crest width had no significant impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08756-y.Keywords: Gingival Migration, Orthodontic Treatment, Risk Assessment, Digital Imaging

Background

Gingival recession, defined as the apical migration of the marginal gingiva, exposes the root surface to the oral environment and may lead to aesthetic concerns, increased plaque accumulation, dentine hypersensitivity, and a higher risk of root caries [1]. Several studies have suggested that individuals with thinner periodontal phenotypes are more susceptible to gingival recession, particularly in the mandibular incisor region, which is commonly affected during orthodontic treatment [2, 3]. In addition, a thin, minimal, or absent band of keratinized tissue on the buccal surfaces has frequently been considered a risk factor for recession, especially under orthodontic forces [4, 5].

One of the major challenges in understanding the relationship between orthodontic treatment and gingival recession lies in the multifactorial etiology of the condition. Although orthodontic tooth movement is often considered a contributing factor, oral hygiene habits, brushing technique, smoking, and pre-existing periodontal conditions may also influence the development of recession [3, 5]. Establishing a direct causal relationship is further complicated by the delayed manifestation of gingival recession, which may appear years after treatment completion [6, 7]. These challenges are further compounded by methodological limitations in the existing literature regarding soft tissue evaluation. Most previous studies evaluated gingival recession only from the buccal aspect [6, 8–10], whereas relatively few included lingual measurements [11, 12]. In addition, many studies assessed recession qualitatively by reporting only its presence or absence [13, 14] rather than quantifying it in millimeters [15, 16]. Although some studies incorporated digital technologies, many still relied on scanned plaster models or intraoral photographs, which may be affected by inconsistent image quality and limited standardization [12, 16–18]. Collectively, these methodological limitations may reduce the sensitivity and accuracy of detecting subtle soft tissue changes.

In contrast, this study employed a fully digital workflow using three-dimensional intraoral scans obtained both before and after orthodontic treatment. This approach enabled precise and reproducible millimeter level measurements of gingival migration on both labial and lingual surfaces. Keratinized tissue width (KTW) can be reliably assessed using digital measurements based on visible color differentiation. A previous study has demonstrated that digital measurements are highly accurate and reproducible compared to conventional clinical probing, with minimal differences from histologic measurements [19], supporting their use in clinical and research settings. This comprehensive digital methodology may provide a more accurate evaluation of soft tissue dynamics during orthodontic treatment.

Although gingival margin (GM) level is typically evaluated relative to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ), this retrospective study assessed GM changes using clinical crown length (CCL) [20, 21]. This approach was chosen because patients with baseline gingival recession or gingival overgrowth were excluded, the GM was located coronal to the CEJ, and three dimensional intraoral scans do not allow reliable assessment of the coronal GM position relative to the CEJ. Therefore, changes in CCL represented apical or coronal migration of the GM relative to its baseline position. Unlike previous studies that categorized GM changes simply as apical or coronal, the present study used the original millimeter scale CCL data and analyzed gingival margin migration (GMM) as a continuous variable. Considering the complex and multifactorial nature of GMM, this study further evaluated the potential contributions of dental, periodontal, skeletal, and demographic factors to soft tissue alterations following orthodontic treatment.

Method

Study design

This retrospective single-cohort study analyzed pre and post treatment changes in GM levels following fixed orthodontic treatment. The study was conducted at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul Okan University. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Istanbul University Cerrahpaşa (Approval No: 02.05.2023–171). Individuals who underwent fixed orthodontic treatment between September 2018 and February 2023 were included. The null hypothesis was soft tissue, dentoalveolar, and individual variables were not associated with changes in gingival margin level following fixed orthodontic treatment. The collection and analysis of soft tissue data from three dimensional intraoral scans were performed between August 2023 and September 2024, while radiographic analyses based on lateral cephalometric radiographs were conducted between November 2024 and February 2025. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

A total of 530 patients treated at Istanbul Okan University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Orthodontics, were screened. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 88 patients were included in the final cohort. Inclusion criteria required patients to have undergone fixed orthodontic treatment in both arches and to have both pre and post treatment lateral cephalometric radiographs and intraoral scans obtained with an intraoral scanner before treatment and after debonding. Patients aged 16–40 years with no radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss at baseline were included to minimize the influence of growth, age, and inflammation related periodontal changes. Individuals younger than 16 years were excluded due to ongoing gingival and periodontal development, while those older than 40 years were excluded to avoid age related gingival changes. Age was also included as a predictor in the statistical analysis.

Exclusion criteria included a history of maxillofacial or dental trauma or surgery in the anterior mandibular region, use of medications affecting periodontal tissues, smoking, uncontrolled systemic diseases, radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss, prior orthodontic or adjunctive treatment, retained impacted teeth, congenital or developmental anomalies, baseline gingival recession or papillary loss, incisal wear, restorations on lower anterior teeth, and previous appliance use before fixed orthodontic treatment. Patients with visible plaque accumulation on 3D scans were excluded to reduce the potential influence of gingival inflammation. As direct plaque or gingival indices were not available due to the retrospective design, this criterion was used as an indirect control for inflammatory confounding.

Variables

Measurements were taken from both the labial and lingual surfaces of mandibular anterior teeth (central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines). Outcome variable included CCL change (T0-T1). Negative ΔCCL values indicate apical migration of the GM. Predictor variables encompassed demographic data such as age (year) and gender, treatment duration (month), cephalometric parameters including Incisor Mandibular Plane Angle (IMPA), Gonion-Menton-Sella-Nasion angle (Go–Me–SN), symphysis width and height, crowding, and alveolar crest width at the apical, middle, and coronal regions, as well as tooth number (mandibular canines and incisors, teeth 43–33) and tooth surface (labial and lingual).

Data sources and measurements

Final scans were routinely obtained on the same day as appliance removal and retainer placement in clinical orthodontic practice at the clinic. All periodontal measurements were performed digitally using the Ortho Analyzer software (3Shape) with an accuracy of 0.01 mm, based on three-dimensional intraoral scans obtained before (T0) and after (T1) treatment.

The widest mesiodistal dimension of each tooth was determined, and a perpendicular reference line from this point intersecting the GM was used for measuring GMM. CCL was defined as the distance from the incisal edge to the GM. KTW was measured as the perpendicular distance from the GM to the mucogingival junction, identified based on visible color differentiation between keratinized gingiva and alveolar mucosa (Fig. 1). Both measurements were taken consistently before and after treatment to allow for reliable comparison.

Screenshot demonstrating the measurement of keratinized tissue width and clinical crown length. KTW was measured as the linear distance from the gingival margin to the mucogingival junction, while CCL was measured as the distance from the incisal edge to the gingival margin on three-dimensional intraoral scans using Ortho Analyzer software

IMPA was measured as the angle between the long axis of the mandibular incisors and the mandibular plane. Go–Me–SN was measured as the angle between the mandibular plane Go–Me and the SN plane. Symphysis width was measured as the linear distance between the anterior and posterior cortical bone at the widest region of the mandibular symphysis. Symphysis height was assessed as the linear distance between the most superior and inferior points of the mandibular symphysis. Alveolar width was measured as the distance between the roots of the mandibular incisors and the alveolar cortical bone at the apical, middle, and crestal levels. All measurements were performed on lateral cephalometric radiographs. Dental crowding was calculated on digital models by subtracting the total mesiodistal tooth width of the anterior six teeth from the arch length between these teeth.

Bias and blinding

To reduce measurement bias, periodontal parameters (CCL and KTW) were assessed by SK and other predictor data were collected by DK, maintaining blinding throughout data collection and analysis. Potential confounders such as smoking, systemic diseases, and baseline alveolar bone loss were excluded via strict criteria.

Method error

Intra-examiner reliability of the measurements was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). The results demonstrated excellent consistency and reproducibility across all variables, with Cronbach’s Alpha values ranging from 0.981 to 1.000 and ICC values between 0.963 and 1.000 (p < 0.001 for all).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values, were calculated for all variables at pre treatment and post treatment, as well as for the corresponding change values. Change values were calculated as the difference between post treatment and pre treatment measurements.

Linear mixed effects models were used to evaluate the association between fixed orthodontic treatment and GM changes, while accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data, in which multiple measurement sites were nested within patients. Separate models were constructed using baseline values and change values to evaluate treatment related effects while accounting for inter individual variability at baseline. Models based solely on post treatment values were not used, as they do not account for baseline differences between individuals.

All models included both fixed and random effects. Fixed-effect predictors were selected a priori based on literature and clinical relevance and included patient-level variables such as IMPA [6, 12, 22], Go–Me–SN [11, 23, 24], KTW [25, 26], treatment duration, age, gender, symphysis width and height [27, 28], and alveolar ridge width [16] measured at apical, middle, and coronal levels and tooth-level variables (tooth number (43–33) and surface (labial or lingual). Pre-treatment and post-treatment values were not simultaneously included as fixed effects because of their strong correlation and the resulting risk of multicollinearity.

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values for both baseline and change models. For baseline values, all VIFs were below 2.1 and tolerance values exceeded 0.49. Similarly, for the change values, VIFs were below 1.4 and tolerance values exceeded 0.72.

Patient ID was included as a random intercept to account for within subject clustering of repeated measurements across multiple sites within each individual. A variance components (VC) covariance structure was specified for the random effects. Random slopes were not included due to the limited number of clusters.

Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with Satterthwaite approximation for degrees of freedom. Type III sums of squares were used for fixed-effect inference. Convergence was assessed using predefined criteria.

Initial full models including all selected predictors were constructed and are presented in the supplementary material. Subsequently, reduced models were obtained by sequentially removing non-significant predictors while retaining clinically relevant variables. Model selection was guided by changes in − 2 Restricted Log Likelihood, Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), corrected AIC (AICC), Bozdogan’s CAIC, and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with preference given to more parsimonious models without loss of interpretability.

Separate models were fitted for all surfaces combined and for labial and lingual surfaces analyzed independently. In region-specific analyses, the tooth surface variable was excluded to avoid redundancy.

Model assumptions, including residual normality, homoscedasticity, and linearity, were evaluated through visual inspection of residual plots and normal Q–Q plots. ICC’s for patient-level random intercepts ranged from 0.28 to 0.43, indicating moderate to substantial clustering of measurements within patients and supporting the use of mixed-effects modeling.

All analyses were performed using the Linear Mixed Models procedure in IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Fixed-effect estimates were reported as regression coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), t-values, and p-values for labial, lingual, and combined surface models. The study included 88 patients (clusters) and 1,056 measurement sites, satisfying recommended sample size requirements for mixed-effects modeling, including adequate cluster size and at least 10 observations per fixed-effect predictor, in accordance with published guidelines [29, 30].

Results

Participants

A significant number of cases had to be excluded despite initially meeting the general inclusion and exclusion criteria. Most exclusions were due to technical limitations and inconsistencies in scan quality, such as missing or incomplete time points, inadequate visualization of soft tissue landmarks, or the use of plaster model scans instead of direct intraoral scans. Additional exclusion criteria included poor oral hygiene, defined as obvious plaque accumulation detectable on 3D scans, the presence of unerupted or missing teeth, prior or ongoing adjunctive soft tissue treatments, and missing cephalometric data.

Of the 530 patients initially screened, 442 were excluded based on these criteria. After the application of all exclusion criteria, 88 patients remained eligible for final analysis. The final sample consisted of 72% female and 28% male patients. A detailed breakdown of the reasons for exclusion is presented in the flow diagram (Supplementary Figure).

Linear mixed-effects model for gingival margin change

Predictive model (pre treatment variables)

Linear mixed effects model analysis was conducted to examine the effects of baseline variables on CCL change across labial, lingual, and all surfaces. Across all models, gender, KTW, and crowding were significant predictors of GMM. Labial surfaces demonstrated tooth-specific differences: teeth 31, 32, and 41 showed significant positive effects, whereas tooth 33 exhibited a negative. In lingual surfaces, symphysis morphology also contributed, with symphysis height positively and width negatively associated with CCL change; age showed a positive effect as well. IMPA appeared to have a borderline significant effect on lingual surfaces. In the all-surface model, fixed-effect analyses including tooth surfaces were significant, and the overall trends were similar to those observed in the lingual model (Table 2).

Treatment effect model (change variables)

Linear mixed effects model analysis was conducted to investigate the impact of treatment-related changes in dental and anatomical parameters on GMM across labial, lingual, and all surfaces. In the treatment effect models, common fixed effects across all surfaces included age, KTW, and crowding. In lingual surfaces, changes in IMPA also contributed to GMM, whereas in the all-surface model, in addition to these common predictors, gender and tooth surface had a significant effect. For labial surfaces, tooth number, in addition to the common predictors, showed a significant impact. Across all three models, the largest estimated effect was observed for changes in KTW (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify clinical and radiological factors associated with GMM following orthodontic treatment, using pre- and post-treatment measurements rather than a cross-sectional approach. Linear mixed effects models were developed to assess the GMM. Digital measurement offers greater accuracy in assessing keratinized mucosa dimensions compared to conventional periodontal probing, which is prone to errors related to probe placement, angulation, and rounding. Intraoral scanners can differentiate tissue types based on color and surface contours, enabling more precise and consistent measurements. Consequently, digital scanning provides higher intra and interrater reliability, making it a superior and more reliable alternative to traditional clinical methods [19, 31]. Digital data acquisition has now become a routine workflow in orthodontic clinics, replacing conventional plaster models. Therefore, it provides a valuable source of information for retrospective studies evaluating soft tissues. However, it should be noted that certain periodontal parameters such as bleeding on probing and probing pocket depth can only be assessed clinically and cannot yet be evaluated through digital platforms. These limitations must be acknowledged when considering the scope of soft tissue assessment using digital methods. Nevertheless, in this study, only the changes in KTW and CCL changes were evaluated, parameters that can be reliably assessed through intraoral scanning.

Most studies have evaluated the GM level solely from the vestibular aspect [6, 8–10], with relatively few investigating the lingual region [11, 12]. Furthermore, only a limited number of studies have quantified gingival recession in millimeters [15, 16] rather than simply reporting its presence or absence [13, 14]. While traditional plaster models remain the predominant method [6–8, 12, 27], some research has incorporated digital technologies; however, the majority of these rely on scanned plaster models [16, 17] or clinical photographs [12, 17, 18] instead of fully digital workflows. The lack of standardization in intraoral photography and variability in laboratory fabrication of study models present challenges to the sensitivity and accuracy of these assessment methods [9, 10]. In contrast, the present study evaluated GMM using three-dimensional scans obtained before and after orthodontic treatment. KTW was also digitally measured by detecting color changes, replicating intraoral assessment. To ensure methodological consistency, all measurements both for CCL change for GMM and KTW were performed along a perpendicular line intersecting the widest mesiodistal point of the tooth. This standardized approach, combined with the inclusion of both labial and lingual evaluations and precise digital measurement techniques, differentiates our study from previous investigations.

The findings regarding KTW highlight an important clinical distinction between apical gingival migration and true gingival recession. A greater baseline KTW was associated with a more negative change in CCL (T0–T1), reflecting increased apical migration during orthodontic treatment. However, this migration was not indicative of gingival recession. On the contrary, an increase in KTW change (T1-T0) during treatment exerted a positive effect on CCL change, effectively counterbalancing apical displacement. Clinically, these results suggest that when an adequate zone of keratinized tissue is present, the gingiva is capable of undergoing apical repositioning as an adaptive response to orthodontic tooth movement without evidence of uncompensated gingival recession. Thus, baseline KTW may not be a predictor of tissue loss. While greater baseline KTW was associated with increased apical gingival migration, this effect may be interpreted as a reflection of enhanced soft tissue mobility rather than susceptibility to recession. In contrast, increases in keratinized tissue width during treatment may have a compensatory effect on gingival margin migration, and could potentially contribute to greater stability of the gingival margin over time. Taken together, these findings may suggest a two-phase pattern of soft tissue behavior, in which baseline keratinized tissue width could allow for controlled gingival repositioning, while increases in keratinized tissue width over time may contribute to stabilization of the gingival margin and help reduce the risk of uncompensated recession. Notably, the effect estimates may suggest a stronger influence of keratinized tissue width on clinical crown length change at lingual surfaces compared with labial surfaces, potentially indicating a surface-specific trend. These observations are consistent with previous findings by Closs et al. [25], who reported that coronal migration of the GM after orthodontic treatment was more likely in sites with smaller baseline KTW, whereas sites with larger initial KTW either maintained their gingival position or developed recession.

Crowding showed a significant effect in both baseline and change models in this study. In this cohort of orthodontically treated individuals, pre treatment crowding appears to be one of the factors contributing to GMM. While orthodontic treatment may influence gingival migration patterns, the results suggest that underlying local predisposing factors are likely major determinants. This interpretation is supported by previous findings [13] indicating that orthodontically untreated individuals with crowding greater than 3 mm per arch exhibit significantly more gingival recession sites compared with those with mild or no crowding. These observations imply that untreated crowding could represent a major risk factor for gingival recession. Accordingly, evaluating orthodontic treatment effects within models that simultaneously account for local predisposing factors appears more appropriate than attributing changes solely to the treatment. When tooth movement is performed in a controlled manner, orthodontic treatment may contribute to a more favorable periodontal environment. However, during the correction of severe crowding, excessive displacement may still be associated with apical migration of the GM, as reflected by the significant effects of crowding on GMM. Consistent with these findings, evidence from another study [32] that no differences in gingival recession outcomes were observed between extraction and non extraction treatments, and no treatment-related variables including extraction decisions were identified as predictors of gingival recession. These results support the notion that GMM in orthodontic patients is more strongly associated with pre-existing local factors, rather than with orthodontic treatment itself or the choice of treatment modality.

In the analyses stratified by tooth number and tooth surface, tooth 43 was used as the reference in both the model based on baseline values and the model evaluating treatment related changes. In both models, teeth 31 and 32 exhibited a significantly more coronal displacement of the GM compared with tooth 43. In the baseline model, tooth 41 also showed a significant difference relative to tooth 43; however, this difference lost statistical significance in the change model, indicating a borderline effect. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between teeth 33 and 43 in either model. These findings suggest that canines tend to exhibit a more apical migration pattern of the GM compared with incisors, whereas mandibular incisors demonstrate a tendency toward more coronal GMM. When considering tooth surface, the labial GM was consistently 0.17–0.19 mm more coronal migration than the lingual surface. Similarly, in another study [33] more pronounced apical migration observed in canines aligns with our findings.

In this retrospective study, alveolar bone parameters were assessed using available cephalometric analyses, which limited the evaluation to ridge width at a single reference point (31–41, FDI) rather than allowing tooth specific measurements. Baseline values showed that the apical ridge width averaged over 4 mm, whereas middle and coronal widths were generally under 1.5 mm. This difference may explain the relatively lower resorption observed in the apical region. Similar to findings in extraction socket healing, where buccal bone thinner than 2 mm tends to resorb, the thinner alveolar bone in the middle and coronal regions appears more vulnerable to remodeling, while the thicker apical bone likely remains more resistant due to its stronger anatomical support [34]. A limitation of the present study is the lack of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) data, which would allow a more detailed, tooth-specific assessment of alveolar bone changes. However, previous CBCT based study [35] evaluating non extraction orthodontic treatment of mandibular crowding have reported bone dehiscence occurring primarily on the labial surface of the incisors. The severity of dental crowding was associated with the risk of developing labial bone loss, highlighting the role of crowding itself in alveolar bone remodeling.

A 2010 systematic review found that more proclined lower incisors are generally linked to greater or more frequent gingival recession, with one study showing a significant difference [3]. Conversely, another study found that higher initial incisor inclination was associated with a smaller increase in clinical crown height, suggesting potentially less gingival recession in such cases [27]. In our study, baseline data model revealed a positive association between initial IMPA and GMM; however, this relationship reached statistical significance only on the lingual surfaces. In contrast, in the model in which change variables were analyzed as fixed effects, the change in IMPA remained a significant parameter on the lingual surfaces but exhibited a negative association. In contrast to earlier reviews, a 2018 systematic review found no strong evidence that incisor proclination causes gingival recession [36]. Similarly, a 2023 review reported inconclusive results [4]. While some studies showed a link between proclination and gingival recession [22, 37, 38] or increased clinical crown height [6, 27, 32], overall evidence remains insufficient to confirm a clear cause-and-effect relationship.

The 2023 systematic review found that greater symphysis height is linked to lower risk of gingival recession [4]. In this study, baseline symphysis height did not emerge as a significant predictor in the labial model alone; however, it demonstrated a positive association with GMM in both the lingual and all surfaces models, indicating that greater baseline symphysis height may contribute to reduced apical migration of the GM. However, results for symphysis width were mixed: some studies found a significant association [32, 38, 39], while others did not [27, 28]. In this study, baseline symphysis width showed a negative association with GMM, which was significant only in the lingual model.

Some studies have formed experimental and control groups by comparing individuals who have undergone orthodontic treatment with those who have not [40, 41] However, including a control group may not always be necessary [20], as gingival migration seems to be influenced more by underlying confounding factors than by orthodontic treatment itself. Orthodontic tooth movement may simply act as a trigger that exposes or worsens these risk factors. A control group would only be meaningful if it accounts for these confounders to isolate the specific effect of tooth movement. When both groups share similar risk profiles, gingival migration can still occur regardless of orthodontic treatment. For example, a tooth positioned more labially within the alveolar bone may predispose the gingival tissues to apical migration, even in the absence of orthodontic tooth movement. Similarly, progressive decreases in KTW and thin crestal bone in the coronal region can all independently increase recession risk. In orthodontic cases, recession may result from changes in tooth position relative to alveolar bone, not the treatment itself. These points highlight the importance of identifying and quantifying individual risk factors rather than attributing recession solely to orthodontic forces. Future prospective studies should therefore develop robust multifactorial models that assess the contribution of each factor, ideally at the tooth and surface specific level, using linear mixed effects model analysis.

Alveolar ridge width and other hard tissue parameters were assessed using two dimensional cephalometric analysis, whereas soft tissue parameters were evaluated using high resolution three dimensional, tooth and surface specific data. Alveolar bone related measurements were derived from the mandibular central incisors and were not obtained on a tooth or surface specific basis; therefore, alveolar ridge width was treated as a generalized parameter rather than a site-specific variable. In the absence of CBCT based, tooth and surface specific measurements, the same linear and angular cephalometric values were assumed to represent multiple teeth. This, together with the reliance on two dimensional cephalometric imaging rather than three dimensional CBCT derived data, limited the assessment of site specific hard tissue characteristics and may have reduced the sensitivity of the linear mixed effects models to detect associations between hard tissue parameters and clinical crown length changes, potentially explaining the lack of statistical significance observed for alveolar ridge width in the regression analyses. In addition, the retrospective design of the study imposed further constraints, as clinically relevant parameters such as probing depth, gingival inflammation, and plaque accumulation could not be evaluated. Finally, the relatively small sample size represents another limitation, which may have further reduced statistical power.

Despite these limitations, a key strength of the study is the use of linear mixed effects modeling, which allowed the analysis of continuous data without categorization related information loss while accounting for multiple potential confounders. To address anatomical variability, separate models were constructed for all surfaces combined as well as for labial and lingual surfaces individually. To the best of our knowledge, this surface specific analytical approach has not been previously reported in the orthodontic literature and provides a more detailed characterization of GMM following orthodontic treatment.

Conclusion

GMM was consistently more coronal on labial surfaces than on lingual surfaces, with a statistically significant difference between the two. Baseline KTW and its treatment-related increase emerged as the most influential determinants of GM behavior, supporting controlled gingival repositioning and contributing to long-term stability by preventing uncompensated recession. Dental crowding at baseline and its correction during treatment were also associated with GM changes, while incisor inclination showed a surface-specific effect. Incisors demonstrated greater coronal GMM than canines, and increased symphysis height was related to enhanced coronal migration on lingual surfaces. In contrast, alveolar crest width did not appear to significantly affect GM changes.
