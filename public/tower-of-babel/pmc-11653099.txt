A Facial Motion Retargeting Pipeline for Appearance Agnostic 3D Characters
Authors: ChangAn Zhu, Chris Joslin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT3D facial motion retargeting has the advantage of capturing and recreating the nuances of human facial motions and speeding up the time‐consuming 3D facial animation process. However, the facial motion retargeting pipeline is limited in reflecting the facial motion's semantic information (i.e., meaning and intensity), especially when applied to nonhuman characters. The retargeting quality heavily relies on the target face rig, which requires time‐consuming preparation such as 3D scanning of human faces and modeling of blendshapes. In this paper, we propose a facial motion retargeting pipeline aiming to provide fast and semantically accurate retargeting results for diverse characters. The new framework comprises a target face parameterization module based on face anatomy and a compatible source motion interpretation module. From the quantitative and qualitative evaluations, we found that the proposed retargeting pipeline can naturally recreate the expressions performed by a motion capture subject in equivalent meanings and intensities, such semantic accuracy extends to the faces of nonhuman characters without labor‐demanding preparations.

Introduction

3D facial motion retargeting has been widely used in production for higher fidelity character facial animation and less manual work. Although it has advantages in capturing detailed facial dynamics and allowing effective directorial oversight, it shows limited semantic accuracy and expressiveness on the target 3D faces when dealing with diverse facial structures (e.g., the face of the orc Azog from The Hobbit). Therefore, the industry still heavily relies on keyframe animation to finetune the result [1]. Moreover, the target face often requires intricate rigging or parameterization to realize high‐quality animation.

A 3D facial motion retargeting pipeline usually involves capturing an actor's facial motion as the source motion, interpreting this source motion into semantically meaningful parameters, and sending these parameters to the parameterization of a target 3D face. The motion capture (mocap) and interpretation processes are encapsulated as one module in many facial mocap applications (e.g., FaceWare [2], LiveLink [3], ARKit [4]), where the applications take the mocap data, interpret, and output it as a set of standardized blendshape weights. In research, the interpretation methods translate facial motion into more diverse forms, such as blendshape weights [5], that correspond to the action units (AU) of the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) [6], expression‐related coefficients of a statistics model [7, 8], or facial anatomical components' activation values [9, 10, 11]. The target face parameterization commonly used in the industry is the blendshape model, whose elemental units are a set of topology‐consistent face meshes presenting different poses. This parameterization works for human, human‐like, and nonhuman characters but requires intensive preparation of the facial poses. Recent research has addressed the issue with deep‐learning and 3D Morphable Models (3DMM) [12, 13, 14, 15], which automatically generate 3D faces of expressions for human characters. Some research has pushed the fidelity of facial animation through anatomy‐based parameterization [16, 17], where physics simulations are used for interactive and more realistic facial animation. Yet few of these parameterizations and the downstream retargeting pipelines are compatible beyond human characters unless the target face has blendshapes.

In this research, our objective is to construct a 3D facial motion retargeting pipeline that accurately retargets an actor's facial motion to appearance agnostic faces in a semantically accurate and visually plausible way. The method should significantly reduce the time spent on target face parameterization and allow intuitive artistic control. We propose retargeting facial motion at the anatomical level to guarantee cross‐character compatibility. Our retargeting pipeline contains two main modules. As shown in Figure 1, the source motion interpretation module (Figure 1a) processes marker‐based mocap data to FACS AU labels and measures their intensities by assessing the marker geometries per frame. The target face parameterization module (Figure 1b) takes a 3D face mesh and automatically creates a rig that contains a set of simple polygon meshes representing the abstractions of facial muscles. Each muscle abstraction has its own local deformation space, covering the muscle shapes of its active contraction and passive movements and is directly drivable by the interpreted source motion.

Related Works

Research on facial motion retargeting has provided several interpretations of the retargeting problem. Some earlier representative methods [18, 19, 20] fall in the category of cross‐mapping, which creates a mapping from the source motion space to the space of the target faces' poses based on a sparse semantic correspondence among the two spaces. Recent retargeting frameworks emphasize modeling a universal parameterization for the various target faces and have the source motion interpreted into parameters that can drive the parameterizations. Retargeting methods following this process have been considered under the category of “parallel parameterization” [21] and our retargeting method is rooted in this category.

Source Parameterization

The source motion can originate from various sources, available retargeting methods have utilized facial motion from the combinations of videos [2, 22, 23], facial landmarks [24], depth [20], or 3D marker‐based mocap [11, 19, 25]. The process of source motion interpretation, also known as “facial motion parameterization,” [26] translates these diverse forms of facial motion into parameters that represent its semantic information (i.e., meaning and intensity), such as expressions [27], visemes [28], and facial actions [6]. It is useful for both parameterizing a facial performance and interpreting the animation from a 3D face. Two systems that inspired our motion interpretation module are the Facial Action Coding System [6] and the MPEG‐4 Facial Animation Parameters [29, 30].

The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) [6] codes individual or multiple facial muscles' actions and defines them as action units (AU). Since FACS stresses describing the observable facial muscle movements rather than the emotion, it eliminates the potential coding deviation from culture, identity, gender, or age variations. Benefiting from its anatomy‐based nature, it can be extended to code the facial motions of different species [31, 32], as long as they share corresponding facial muscles with humans.

The MPEG‐4 facial animation parameters (FAPs) system [30] refers to facial features to define the FAPs. The system starts by acquiring the facial animation parameter units (FAPUs) based on the fractions of some facial features' distances, then computing the intensity of an FAP (e.g., raising the left corner of the mouth) using these units [33]. Since the facial action's intensity units are defined per face, the system applies to different faces and consistently reflects the intensities of the facial actions.

Although the two systems were suggested more than two decades ago, their core concepts are inspiring research and production even nowadays. Our motion interpretation module refers to FACS action units to determine which facial muscle should be activated and refers to FAPs for computing the intensities of the muscle activation.

Retargeting Methods and Target Parameterization

In research, retargeting methods within the category of parallel parameterization usually emphasize their face parameterization capability, therefore, we discuss the various retargeting methods based on their target face parameterization methods.

Blendshapes Transfer

As mentioned in Section 1, blendshape parameterization allows a linear morphing among a set of topology‐consistent poses of the face (i.e., blendshapes) to enable facial animation [5], it has been widely used in production for keyframe‐based facial animation for its high compatibility to the various categories of characters, intuitive control, and plausible animation result.

In retargeting‐related research, some methods directly transfer the blendshape meshes or the registered expression scans of the source face to the target face using expression cloning [34] or deformation transfer [35]; some methods slice a target face mesh into patches and transfer the source face's deformation locally, then smooth out the connecting edges of the patches through 3D expressions reconstructed from videos [22] or character blendshapes [36]. Methods have also been proposed to create personalized blendshapes through video input, then retarget the video performance to the target face's blendshape parameterization as blendshape weights [37, 38]. Yet blendshape parameterization and its related retargeting methods are not free of drawbacks. The blendshapes usually have overlapped influence at certain facial regions [5], which might introduce unnatural facial poses when multiple blendshapes are activated simultaneously, therefore involving many manual adjustments [1, 5] when used in retargeting.

3DMM Coefficients Transfer

The 3D Morphable Model or 3DMM is a statistical representation of a 3D face data set and should not be confused with a blendshape parameterization. 3DMM extracts the statistical main variations of a face data set and allows the coefficients corresponding to these main variations to control the morphing [39]. As discussed in the previous section, blendshape parameterization usually has overlapped influences on a facial region, while the main variations of a 3DMM do not impose overlapping influences on the target face, namely, they are statistically independent. Therefore, a 3DMM‐generated facial pose is less likely to look unnatural.

The early version of 3DMM utilized a linear statistic method—principal components analysis (PCA), to extract the main variations, which led to perceptually uninterpretable elemental units—the eigenfaces [40, 41]. In contrast to the blendshape parameterization, whose elemental units are easily understandable face meshes of poses, eigenfaces do not contain semantic meanings and hence do not allow artistic control. Therefore, many recent works employed deep‐learning to replace PCA, realizing nonlinear 3DMM [12, 42, 43, 44] for 3D face parameterization, where the focus is to decouple the interpretable variations such as the expressions and identities of a 3D face data set. Consequently, the retargeting is simplified into transferring the expression‐related coefficients of the source identity to the target identity within the same 3DMM. Relevant works have been done on improving the retargeting accuracy through emotion recognition [45], enhancing the mouth pose retargeting with speech‐focused model [46], using different neural network architectures to better decouple the variations in expressions, identities, poses [13, 47, 48, 49], and even the expression style [14] of a 3D face data set. There are also retargeting methods that employ nonlinear 3DMM in face reenactment networks for high‐quality 3D face reenactment [50, 51, 52, 53]. Although retargeting methods using 3DMM parameterization are less likely to generate anatomically incorrect facial poses, the statistical nature of 3DMM leads to its dependency on well‐regularized training data sets for high‐quality face parameterization and animation, its compatible range of characters is also limited to real‐life retrievable faces or their instances.

Face Anatomy Transfer

The essence of anatomy‐based face parameterization and retargeting is to create the replicas of a target face's anatomical components and update or transfer the statuses of these replicas to deform a face or retarget the facial motion.

Commercial 3D software has developed several geometric deformation‐based solutions to mimic the contraction of facial muscles, examples including Rigify for Blender [54], which uses joints to simulate facial muscles' influences on a target face's deformation, and MetaHuman Creator [3], which fuses facial muscle‐inspired joints influence and whole‐face blendshapes to automatically rig and animate faces. In research, several retargeting methods based on face anatomy have been developed for simulating the realistic physical behaviors of faces [10, 14, 16, 55, 56]. Some researchers found that utilizing face anatomy to parameterize and animate a 3D face allows a wide range of compatible target characters such as monsters, goblins, and even the Avatars [11, 57].

Our retargeting method shares similarities with methods in blendshapes transfer and face anatomy transfer. Two works closest to our idea are from Cong et al. [57] and Chandran et al. [36]. Our target face parameterization and retargeting strategies are different from the two in that the muscle blendshapes from Cong et al. [57] need manually specified muscle positions and the muscles and faces are volume‐based. However, our muscle patches are automatically extracted from a single face mesh input and are surface‐based, therefore are easier to edit than the volume muscles. Chandran et al. [36] defined a set of patches in a checkerboard pattern and transferred the deformation of each patch from face to face. The method requires at least 20 whole‐face blendshapes from the target for a plausible result. In contrast, we designed a set of muscle‐analogous patches for retargeting and our method needs zero whole‐face blendshapes.

A Facial Muscle Based Retargeting

We propose a retargeting method rooted in the category of parallel parameterization. For the source motion interpretation, we translated the marker‐based mocap data into FACS AU labels and intensity values. For the target face parameterization, we designed a muscle‐based face model that activates the abstracted facial muscles to present motion. After animating a parameterized face, the parameters describing the muscle activations can be directly transferred to animate other equivalently parameterized faces.

Facial Muscle Behaviors

We provide a brief description of the facial muscle behaviors as a foundation to elaborate our retargeting pipeline. In the facial anatomy of most mammals, the main active component is the facial muscle, where the muscles for expressions contract and move the fat pads and skin attached to them and the muscles for mastication move the jaw bone for actions like mouth‐opening.

Facial muscles can be categorized into linear and sphincter muscles [58], where the linear muscles stretch and pull and the sphincter muscles open, close, squeeze, and roll. The facial muscles usually emerge from the skull and insert into the other muscles or the skin, therefore we can consider the emergence and insertion points of a facial muscle as its start and end. Muscle action is complex because it influences other muscles, the jaw bone, the fat pads, and the skin. When an expression is presented (e.g., a big smile), several relevant muscles actively contract [27] and pull the other inactive muscles and anatomical components, leading to their passive actions. If we only recreate the actions of the active muscles and ignore the passive actions from other anatomical components, the facial animation would appear unnatural. Therefore, both our source motion interpretation and target face parameterization modules consider the facial muscles' behaviors.

Source Motion Interpretation

Mocap Markers Layout With Face Anatomy and Empirical Considerations

Since marker‐based facial mocap provides high precision and can record subtle facial movements, we collected marker‐based mocap data and used them as the source motion. We empirically approximated these marker positions by asking our mocap subjects to perform different FACS AUs, which activate only one or a few muscles at a time, therefore assist positioning the muscles and the relevant markers. As shown in Figure 2, we placed markers at the start and end points of the linear muscles to capture their contraction behaviors like pulling or stretching along one direction; and markers around the sphincter muscles to form a circular shape to capture their behaviors of opening, closing, squeezing, and rolling. We also added extra markers to provide more information relating to the passive muscle actions and skin sliding. As shown in Figure 2, we selected 51 mocap markers for motion interpretation. The forehead has fewer markers as the muscle structure around the area is relatively simple. The sphincter muscles around the eyes and mouth move in a more complex pattern [58], therefore having more markers.

The mocap markers used in our motion interpretation module.

Action Unit Intensities and Passive Muscle Actions

To transfer the interpreted source motion to the muscle‐based target face parameterization, the interpreted source motion should contain information that describes muscle actions. FACS [6] codes the facial muscle behaviors into action units (AUs), which can be used to translate the source motion into parameters relating to muscle activation, therefore allowing us to establish a semantic connection between the mocap data and the face parameterization. However, FACS only describes the meaning of an AU (e.g., the description of AU1 is inner brow raising), its related muscle activation (e.g., the activated muscle for AU1 is Frontalis), and their sparse intensity levels (from letter A to E, meaning weakest to strongest) but does not specify how this intensity is measured and what are the passive muscle actions. Therefore, we refer to FAPs [30] to utilize the geometric information from facial features to measure the numerically consistent intensity of facial actions, though the facial features in our case are the mocap markers and we re‐designed the placement of these features as discussed in the previous paragraph. We also provide a clear definition of the passive muscle actions involved in an AU.

As shown in Table 1, we divide the markers into different marker groups to classify the AUs and measure their intensities. For example, the marker group used for AU1 (markers 0, 1, 2) indicates that we use the Euclidean distance between the markers 0 and 1 (denoted as 0‐1 in Table 1), and the distance between markers 0 and 2 (0‐2) to determine the activation and intensity of AU1 for the left and right Frontalis. We term these Euclidean distances and other geometric information provided by the mocap markers as “Marker Geometric Parameters” (MGPs). The MGPs include marker positions, the Euclidean distances between markers, the fraction value of some of these distances (e.g., the eye aspect ratio), and the normal vectors of planes formed by multiple markers (e.g., the plane formed by the markers on the chin and two sides of the jaw). Then, we use an automated procedure to run through the Range of Motion (RoM) sequence captured for a mocap actor to acquire the threshold values (i.e., the min, max, and neutral values) of the MGPs, effectively the RoM profile of a mocap actor. The threshold values saved in the RoM profile are then compared against the MGPs computed in each frame, determining which AU and its associating passive muscles should be triggered. When a muscle patch is activated, its influenced muscles' relevant passive actions will be simultaneously triggered with the same intensity, allowing a smooth mesh transition from the activated muscle patch to its adjacent patches.

Target Face Parameterization

Our target face parameterization emphasizes the deformation brought by the active and passive movements of facial muscles, therefore, we term it “the muscle rig” in the following sections. We construct the muscle rig with simple polygon meshes following the shapes of facial muscles and consider the original 3D face mesh as the skin. We bind the skin mesh to the muscle rig using proximity warp [60], which takes the deformation of the muscle patches and applies their weighted influence on the skin mesh.

The Muscle Rig

To create the muscle rig, we start by registering a 3D face mesh to a template mesh, from which all the output face meshes share the same topology (i.e., the face meshes will have the same vertex order). The mesh registration process can be done with co‐registration [61], FLAME [62], or applications like MetaHuman Creator [3] for human faces. Then, we extract the muscle patches from the face mesh based on a predefined patch template. These template patches are manually extracted from the template mesh, therefore having corresponded facets on the template mesh. In this case, we can extract the same set of muscle patches from any registered face mesh. There are 28 template muscle patches, where a few facial muscles that have overlapped influences to a facial region or usually contract together are merged. Specifically, the Levator Labii Superioris Alaeque Nasi (LLSAN) and Levator Labii Superioris (LLS) are in one patch, the Corrugator Supercilii and Depressor Supercilii are in one patch, and the actions from Levator Anguli Oris and Depressor Anguli Oris are merged in the local deformation space of Orbicularis Oris. Note that in the motion interpretation process (Section 3.2), we interpret the individual muscle's motion for more precise motion analysis, while in the muscle rig, we merge a few muscles into one muscle patch to avoid redundancy when defining their deformation spaces. For instance, the LLS contraction only leads to the action of “upper lip raiser,” whereas the LLSAN contraction leads to “nose wrinkler” and slight “upper lip raiser.” Therefore, the two muscle actions need to be interpreted independently. However, in the muscle rig, we merge the two muscles into one patch as they have overlapped influences on the upper lip, we then deform the same patch at its two different regions to reflect the two muscles' independent contractions, this avoids repetitively creating deformation spaces for facial muscles that work similarly.

As is shown in Figure 3, the muscle patches are a set of polygons analogous to the facial muscle shapes. Since the registered face meshes share the same topology, the patches extracted from them are also consistent in topology, which enables transferring the muscles' deformation spaces across different faces.

The Local Deformation Spaces of the Muscle Patches

To define the local deformation spaces of the muscle patches, we created template blendshapes for each muscle patch. These muscle blendshapes include the muscle patch deformations caused by active and passive muscle actions, the rigid transformation of the jaw bone, and the fats' bulging effect. We acquire the deformation of the template muscle patches' passive actions from the whole‐face blendshapes of the template face. For instance, when the outer side of the muscle Frontalis contracts, the corresponding whole‐face blendshape is “outer eyebrows up”. If we extract the adjacent muscle patches at this status, their shapes describe their passive actions. The muscle patches are zero‐thickness with noncorrelated deformation spaces, hence are easy to edit and do not impose overlapping influence on a facial region compared with the whole‐face blendshape models.

This idea is similar to the Local Blendshape Model as suggested in the work of Wu et al. [22] and Chandra et al. [36]. Yet contrary to their methods where the patches are in an evenly distributed checkerboard pattern, our muscle patches follow the shapes and layout of facial muscles (Figure 3), the deformation space of a muscle patch does not describe a subset of a whole‐face blendshape or an expression scan as in their work but the muscle's independent statuses when it contracts or pulled by other muscles. In addition, the Local Blendshape Model requires video‐reconstructed expression meshes or face‐specific blendshapes as constraints to ensure anatomically appropriate facial poses, while our method requires zero whole‐face blendshapes, we only need to create these muscle blendshapes once and they are transferable to any registered face mesh.

With shared topology across the muscle patches in different faces, the blendshapes of the template muscle patches can be transferred to the muscle patches of another face through a local delta transfer. This process involves first acquiring the offset between the deformed status and the neutral status of a template muscle patch (Equation 1), then adding this offset to the neutral status of the corresponding muscle patch on a new face (Equation 2). In Equation (2), mp represents a new face's muscle patch that has N muscle blendshapes, b0 is the muscle patch's neutral status and wi represents the weight of its ith blendshape.

This delta transfer is local to each patch's position, allowing separate control of the global properties of a patch. For instance, its deformation scale relating to the jaw's rotation. Characters with different face sizes might have different jaw rotational angles. If we simply transfer the deformation of a facial action like “mouth‐open” from the template face to a new face in a whole‐face manner, the transfer from a bigger face to a smaller face can result in overstretch on the smaller face (Figure 4b). Similarly, when the character has big eyes like Alita, with whole‐face delta transfer, the eyes‐closed blendshape of a realistic proportion source face cannot close the eyes of Alita (Figure 4e). However, with the local delta transfer for each muscle patch, we can adjust global parameters subject to different identities to guarantee the robustness of the muscle rig when encountering proportionally diverse faces during the retargeting. Therefore, we adjusted Equations (2) to (3): (3) mp=b0+α∑i=0N−1wiΔbit where α is a scalar to control the scale of this transfer, we set α>1 when the target muscle patch is proportionally bigger than the template and α<1 when it is smaller. The value of α can be assigned by artists or automatically optimized by minimizing the retargeting error as discussed in Section 4.2. As shown in Figure 4, the local deformation space that we defined for the muscle patches allows us to prevent overstretch in a small face (Figure 4c) like Alita's but close her larger eyes properly (Figure 4f).

Retargeting

Retargeting becomes a simple parameter transfer task after interpreting the source motion and parameterizing the target faces. In our framework, the muscle rig contains the abstraction of the facial muscles and their active and passive statuses, where the active shapes correspond to the AUs and the passive shapes correspond to the AU‐related passive muscle actions as we defined in Table 1. We normalize the intensities of these shapes to the range of 0 to 1. Then, we can directly apply these muscle patches' blendshape activation intensities from the interpreted motion data to the muscle patches, or transfer them from one parameterized face to another. Figure 5 demonstrates the data‐to‐face and face‐to‐face retargeting using our pipeline, where the parameters interpreted from an actor's mocap data (top row) can animate the mocap actor's face (middle row), and the same parameters can be transferred from the animated mocap actor's face to a character's face (bottom row) with equivalent semantic information. The marker‐based mocap process averaged 58.97 min/subject as the Vicon Cara system needs to process the data offline. Alternatively, real‐time facial‐landmark‐based mocap might be used to reduce the time. The muscle rig averaged 28.33 min for parameterizing each face (see more details in Section 4.4). Evaluation

To evaluate the retargeting performance of our method, we compare our retargeting result with the result from a commercial face animation application MetaHuman Animator [3] in terms of the semantic retargeting error (quantitative metric) and visual plausibility (qualitative metric).

Face Meshes and Mocap Data

To prepare the data for evaluation, we collected the 3D static scan of three actors and the 3D faces of three movie characters. We collected the marker‐based mocap data from five actors using Vicon Cara [63], from which we acquired the mocap data of the actors in terms of their facial range of motion sequences, seven pan‐culture recognizable expressions (happy, sad, surprise, anger, disgust, fear, and contempt) [27], and their freestyle performances. After processing, the mocap data is essentially 3D marker movements that can be imported into most 3D software. We also recorded the 2D videos of the mocap actors throughout all mocap sequences for a clearer visual reference.

Quantitative Evaluation

For the quantitative evaluation, we found that acquiring the ground truth 3D mesh for evaluating the retargeting accuracy either requires a 3D face data set from dynamic scans (i.e., 3D scan of the entire performance or many expressions of a mocap actor) [48] or needs semantically equivalent blendshapes across many characters [8]. This restricts us from testing our method using the face meshes outside available face data sets or using character faces without blendshapes. Therefore, we designed an evaluation that is flexible with character categories and is independent of dynamic face scans or blendshapes.

Our quantitative evaluation took inspiration from Soel et al. [64], which uses the Radial Basis Function (RBF) interpolation to proportionally match the markers on the pose of a target face to the mocap markers. In their work, the process was for establishing the semantic correspondence between the mocap data and the target face blendshapes, while we found it useful to quantify the semantic information's retargeting error. As demonstrated in Figure 6, we start by attaching a set of markers on a target face and then pose this face at ten random frames to semantically match its poses with the poses in the corresponding mocap frames. Note that the markers on the posed target face share the same layout as those used during the mocap process (not all mocap markers are replicated, we only used those involved in the interpretation process), we term these markers as “reference markers” (Figure 6a) and the mocap markers as “ground truth (GT) markers” (Figure 6b). Duplicating the same target face with markers attached, we animate the target face duplication with our retargeting pipeline. This animated target face duplication will move the markers on it, we term these markers “experiment markers” (Figure 6c). Based on the equation of RBF interpolation (Equation 4), we use the GT markers' positions at the ten random frames as the known data points xi and the offsets between the GT markers and their corresponding reference markers as the values at the known data points: (4) s(x)=∑i=0M−1λiϕ(||x−xi||)+q(x) where λi represents the coefficients of the RBF centered at xi, ϕ is a Thin‐Plate Spline (TPS) RBF for its capability to interpolate spatial data [65], q(x) is a linear polynomial for stabilizing the interpolation [64]. Then, by providing the offsets between the GT markers and their corresponding reference markers as the values at the known data point yi, we can solve: (5) s(xi)=yi to acquire the RBF coefficient λi and the linear polynomial q, therefore acquiring the RBF interpolator. Now, the acquired RBF interpolator defines the warp between the GT markers and the reference markers, essentially the semantic correspondence between the mocap data frames and the poses of the target face. If we apply this warp to the experiment markers, the experiment markers deform to share the same proportion with the GT markers but still preserve the semantic information of the retargeting animated target face (Figure 6d, the 3D face of the mocap actor is overlaid for reference), which can be compared to GT markers (Figure 6b) to acquire the semantic information's retargeting error. With this design, we can compare the actors' mocap data with their own animated 3D faces and also compare their mocap with the fantasy or fictional characters' faces animated based on their performance. Parameterization Time and Artistic Control

Since our research objective includes significantly shortening the time spent on parameterizing the 3D faces and allowing artistic control, we report the time spent on parameterizing the 3D faces and their editability. With the muscle rig, our time spent on parameterizing each 3D face averaged 28.33 min with a standard deviation of 1.97 min. The MetaHuman pipeline averaged 27.50 min with a standard deviation of 8.59 min for each face's parameterization, while it cannot provide a robust parameterization for the human‐like (Alita) and two other nonhuman (Gollum and Azog) characters. In terms of artistic control, MetaHuman parameterized faces are hard to adjust since the face meshes are automatically skinned to the rig with many constraints applied, while our muscle rig allows editing the local blendshapes of each muscle patch to tune their deformation intensities, the skin mesh can also be adjusted without damaging the connection between the muscle patches and the skin mesh.

Conclusion and Discussion

In this research, we proposed a retargeting pipeline that transfers facial motion's semantic information at the anatomy level. The proposed retargeting pipeline automatically interprets marker‐based mocap data into facial muscle actions related parameters and parameterizes 3D faces using facial muscle abstractions. The parameterization module defines the local deformation spaces for the facial muscle abstractions, which requires zero whole‐face blendshapes. It is quick to set up, easy to edit, and transferable to various characters' faces. The motion interpretation module translates marker‐based mocap data into FACS action units and their corresponding intensities, driving the parameterized faces to accurately present facial motions as those from the mocap actor. Within the interpretation module, we contributed a comprehensive definition of the facial muscles' passive actions when muscles relating to FACS action units activate. Our quantitative and qualitative evaluations have supported that the proposed pipeline can retarget facial motion in a semantically accurate and visually plausible manner.

Our retargeting pipeline is not free of limitations. The pipeline is not yet capable of interpreting actions like “cheek puff” and “lip sucks” due to the limited information provided by marker‐based mocap, which might be aided with learning‐based methods for more detailed and precise motion interpretation. The muscle rig contains patch‐specific scalars to fit the parameterization to various facial structures, in the current stage, we specify these scalars manually, but we would like to explore an automatic optimization for the scalars' values to eliminate potential errors brought by manual assignment. The muscle rig relies on normalizing the target face to share the same topology with the template mesh, therefore, highly stylized or animal‐like faces might not be as easy to normalize and be parameterized by the muscle rig. The RBF‐based marker warping we used in the evaluation has allowed us to quantify the retargeting error for fictional and fantasy characters, we believe this warping is potentially useful as an optimization cue to refine the retargeting result in future research.
