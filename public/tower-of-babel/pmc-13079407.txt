Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency in a 42‐Day‐Old Infant: A Case Report
Authors: Rasoul Goli, Nasrin Karimi, Ako Hassanzadeh, Somayeh Rezaei, Mansour Arad, Noushin Shahmirza, Reza Atharifar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeukocyte Adhesion Deficiency (LAD) is a rare primary immunodeficiency characterized by impaired leukocyte adhesion and migration, resulting in recurrent bacterial infections, poor wound healing, and delayed umbilical cord separation. We report a 42‐day‐old female infant who presented with recurrent skin infections and delayed cord detachment in the absence of fever and purulent discharge. Flow cytometry showed markedly reduced CD18 expression (1.8%), and genetic analysis confirmed a homozygous ITGB2 mutation (c.844C>T; p.Arg282Trp), consistent with LAD Type I. Neutrophil oxidative burst was normal, excluding chronic granulomatous disease. Early diagnosis allowed timely initiation of prophylactic antibiotics and close follow‐up to prevent complications. This case emphasizes the diagnostic importance of recognizing infection without pus and delayed umbilical separation as hallmark features of LAD.Keywords: case report, immunodeficiency, leukocyte adhesion deficiency, pediatric, umbilical cord infection

Introduction

Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency (LAD) is a rare immunodeficiency characterized by impaired leukocyte adhesion due to mutations in the CD18 gene, inherited in an autosomal recessive manner [1]. LAD is classified into three main types based on the underlying molecular defect. LAD Type I is caused by mutations in the ITGB2 gene, which encodes the β2‐integrin (CD18) subunit, resulting in markedly reduced or absent CD18 expression on leukocytes [2]. LAD Type II arises from defects in the fucosylation pathway, affecting the synthesis of the sialyl‐Lewis^x ligand (CD15s), which is essential for selectin‐mediated leukocyte rolling [3]. LAD Type III is characterized by mutations that impair integrin activation despite normal expression levels due to defects in the FERMT3 (kindlin‐3) gene [4]. Among these types, LAD Type I is the most common and severe, typically presenting in early infancy with recurrent bacterial infections, delayed umbilical cord separation, and poor wound healing [2]. Understanding this classification is critical for accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate therapeutic strategies [1].

Epidemiological data indicate that LAD occurs in approximately 1 in 1 million live births, with higher prevalence in certain populations [5]. Understanding the natural history of LAD is vital, as it can inform management strategies and improve patient outcomes [6].

Clinically, patients with LAD often present in early childhood with recurrent, severe bacterial infections, delayed wound healing, and a notable absence of pus formation [2]. In addition to recurrent infections, they may exhibit complications such as gingivitis and skin ulcers [6]. The lack of effective leukocyte trafficking leads to a compromised immune system, posing significant challenges in managing infections and overall health [7].

Genetic disorders, particularly those inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, often present early in life with characteristic clinical features, and accurate molecular diagnosis is essential for guiding management, prognosis, and family counseling [8, 9, 10].

The diagnosis of LAD can be complex, often requiring clinical assessment, laboratory tests, and genetic analysis [1]. Flow cytometry can assess integrin expression on leukocytes, while definitive diagnosis is achieved through genetic testing [6].

Management of LAD focuses on preventive care and treatment of infections [7]. Prophylactic antibiotics, immunoglobulin replacement therapy, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be considered, particularly in severe cases [11]. The choice of treatment depends on the severity of the condition and the patient's response to initial therapies [12].

This case report aims to provide a comprehensive examination of a patient diagnosed with LAD, detailing the clinical presentations, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic approaches employed. By sharing this case, we intend to raise awareness among healthcare professionals about the importance of recognizing primary immunodeficiencies early and implementing appropriate management strategies. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines for case reports [13].

Case History/Examination

We report the case of a 42‐day‐old female infant who presented to a pediatric emergency department with erythema around the umbilical cord (Figure 1, the arrow indicates the area of umbilical erythema) and subsequent skin lesions, including erythematous spots on the jaw and inner thigh (Figure 2). The infant experienced mild, non‐projectile vomiting 2–3 times per day, without feeding intolerance or dehydration.

The infant was born at term to healthy, non‐consanguineous parents from the same rural Kurdish community, where marriages within extended families are relatively common. Pregnancy and delivery were uneventful, and no neonatal complications were reported.

A notable clinical feature was delayed umbilical cord separation, which occurred at 22 days of age and required sterile cutting in the hospital due to local infection concerns. The family history revealed that a maternal cousin had experienced delayed umbilical cord separation and recurrent infections in infancy, raising suspicion of a possible inherited immunological disorder.

On physical examination, localized erythema and signs of infection were observed at the umbilical site. Despite the presence of infection, no significant pus formation was noted, which is a characteristic clinical feature of LAD. The infant was afebrile and hemodynamically stable, with no tachycardia or signs of respiratory distress.

Laboratory evaluation revealed marked leukocytosis with neutrophilia on complete blood count. Given the clinical suspicion of a primary immunodeficiency, further immunological investigations were performed. Flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood leukocytes demonstrated severely reduced CD18 expression, with only 1.8% of neutrophils and 2.3% of monocytes expressing CD18, compared with normal levels exceeding 95% in healthy individuals.

Subsequent genetic analysis confirmed a homozygous pathogenic mutation in the ITGB2 gene (c.844C>T; p.Arg282Trp). Based on the clinical presentation, markedly reduced CD18 expression, and confirmatory genetic findings, a definitive diagnosis of Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type I (LAD I) was established.

Microbiological culture obtained from the skin lesions grew Staphylococcus aureus , and the infant was treated with clindamycin for 10 days. During hospitalization, a localized umbilical abscess developed, requiring surgical management, including incision and drainage and removal of necrotic tissue. Following comprehensive evaluations and consultations with a pediatric immunologist, a diagnosis of LAD syndrome was established. The parents were educated about the condition and its implications for the infant's immune system.

Treatment of Active Infection: Upon admission, the infant presented with a localized umbilical abscess and Staphylococcus aureus infection confirmed by culture. Because the abscess was clinically well localized and clearly identifiable on physical examination, imaging studies such as ultrasound were not considered necessary prior to surgical intervention. The patient was treated with clindamycin (10 mg/kg every 8 h) for 10 days, targeting the identified pathogen. Surgical management included incision and drainage of the abscess, followed by debridement of necrotic tissue to promote proper healing and reduce the risk of further infection.

The decision for HSCT in LAD I is primarily based on the degree of CD11/CD18 expression on neutrophils, which reflects the severity of the adhesion defect.

While patients with markedly reduced (< 2%) CD11/CD18 expression are clear candidates for HSCT, the timing of transplantation should take into account the patient's clinical status, including control of active infections and overall medical stability.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The infant has remained clinically stable with no recurrence of infections over a follow‐up period of 4 months after initiation of prophylactic antibiotic therapy. During this period, the patient demonstrated appropriate growth, and no new infectious complications were observed.

This case highlights the importance of recognizing key early clinical signs of LAD, particularly delayed umbilical cord separation and infection without pus formation, which should raise suspicion of an underlying leukocyte adhesion defect. Early recognition and confirmation through flow cytometry and genetic testing allow timely initiation of preventive measures, appropriate infection management, and consideration of HSCT as a potential curative therapy.

Increased awareness of these characteristic features among clinicians can facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve outcomes for infants affected by this rare but potentially life‐threatening primary immunodeficiency.

Discussion

In this case, the infant was diagnosed with LAD Type I, confirmed by markedly reduced CD18 expression on flow cytometry and a homozygous ITGB2 (c.844C>T; p.Arg282Trp) mutation. She presented with bacterial skin infections and delayed umbilical cord separation, without fever or systemic signs, which are characteristic of LAD due to impaired leukocyte adhesion and migration.

The absence of fever in our patient can be explained by the underlying defect in β2‐integrin (CD18), which impairs firm adhesion of neutrophils to the vascular endothelium and their subsequent transmigration into infected tissues. As a result, inadequate leukocyte accumulation at sites of infection leads to diminished local cytokine release and a blunted systemic inflammatory response. This pathophysiological mechanism may attenuate febrile signaling despite the presence of active bacterial infection [14].

Comparison with previous reports, including Gorjipour et al. [15], Nagendran et al. [16], and larger cohort studies by Kambli et al. [17] and Novoa et al. [18], highlights the considerable heterogeneity in the clinical presentation and severity of Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency type I. Reported cases range from neonates presenting with early‐onset, life‐threatening sepsis and severe omphalitis to patients with milder phenotypes characterized by recurrent localized skin, mucosal, or periodontal infections. Variability has also been observed in systemic manifestations such as fever and inflammatory response, which are often absent despite active infection. Importantly, cohort studies have demonstrated a correlation between disease severity and the degree of residual CD18 expression, with patients exhibiting < 2% expression experiencing earlier onset, more severe infections, and poorer outcomes. In contrast, patients with partial CD18 expression may have delayed diagnosis and a relatively attenuated clinical course. Our patient falls within the severe end of this clinical spectrum based on markedly reduced CD18 expression and early presentation, yet notably lacked fever and systemic inflammatory signs, underscoring the diagnostic challenge and the importance of clinical suspicion in such cases.

Early diagnosis in our patient allowed prompt management with prophylactic antibiotics, surgical intervention when needed, and close monitoring, consistent with recommendations in the literature. Such early interventions can prevent severe complications and improve outcomes.

Genetic counseling is an essential part of care, particularly in communities with potential consanguinity [19]. Counseling informs families about inheritance patterns, recurrence risks, sibling testing, and family planning options [20]. In this case, a counseling session has been planned to ensure the family understands the implications of the diagnosis and available preventive strategies.

This case emphasizes the importance of clinician awareness of LAD's characteristic features and the need for integrated clinical, laboratory, and genetic evaluation to optimize patient management and family support.
