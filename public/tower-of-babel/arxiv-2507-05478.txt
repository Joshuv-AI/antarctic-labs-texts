Dynamic Regret Reduces to Kernelized Static Regret
Andrew Jacobsen, Alessandro Rudi, Francesco Orabona, Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi

Abstract
We study dynamic regret in online convex optimization, where the objective is to achieve low cumulative loss relative to an arbitrary benchmark sequence. By observing that competing with an arbitrary sequence of comparators u 1 , … , u T u_{1},\ldots,u_{T} in 𝒲 ⊆ ℝ d \mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} is equivalent to competing with a fixed comparator function u : [ 1 , T ] → 𝒲 u:[1,T]\to\mathcal{W} , we frame dynamic regret minimization as a static regret problem in a function space . By carefully constructing a suitable function space in the form of a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), our reduction enables us to recover the optimal R T ​ ( u 1 , … , u T ) = 𝒪 ⁡ ( ∑ t ‖ u t − u t − 1 ‖ ​ T ) R_{T}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T})=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\sum_{t}\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|T}) dynamic regret guarantee in the setting of linear losses, and yields new scale-free and directionally-adaptive dynamic regret guarantees. Moreover, unlike prior dynamic-to-static reductions—which are valid only for linear losses—our reduction holds for any sequence of losses, allowing us to recover 𝒪 ⁡ ( ‖ u ‖ 2 + d eff ​ ( λ ) ​ ln ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}\big(\|u\|^{2}+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\ln T\big) bounds in exp-concave and improper linear regression settings, where d eff ​ ( λ ) d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda) is a measure of complexity of the RKHS. Despite working in an infinite-dimensional space, the resulting reduction leads to algorithms that are computable in practice, due to the reproducing property of RKHSs.

Introduction

This paper introduces new techniques for
Online Convex Optimization
(OCO),
a framework for designing and analyzing algorithms which
learn on-the-fly from a stream of data
(
Gordon 1999
;
Zinkevich 2003
;
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
;
Orabona 2019
;
Cesa-Bianchi and Orabona 2021
)
.
Formally, consider
T
rounds of interaction between the learner and the
environment. In each round, the learner chooses
w_{t}\in\mathcal{W}
from
a convex set
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the environment reveals
a convex loss function
\ell_{t}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the learner
incurs a loss of
\ell_{t}(w_{t})
.
The classic objective in this setting is to minimize the
learner’s
regret
relative to any fixed benchmark
u\in\mathcal{W}
:

\Regret(u):=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(w_{t})-\ell_{t}(u))~.

In this paper, we study the more general problem of minimizing the learner’s
regret relative to any
sequence

of benchmarks
u_{1},\dots,u_{T}\in\mathcal{W}

(
Herbster and Warmuth 1998
;
Herbster and Warmuth 2001
;
Zinkevich 2003
)
:

\DRegret(u_{1},\dots,u_{T}):=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(w_{t})-\ell_{t}(u_{t}))~.

This objective is typically referred to as
dynamic

regret, to distinguish it from the special case where
the comparator sequence is fixed
u_{1}=\dots=u_{T}
(referred to as

static
regret).
Intuitively, dynamic regret captures a notion of
non-stationarity
in learning problems. Problem instances where
u_{1}=\cdots=u_{T}
model classic problem settings, wherein there is a fixed “solution” whose performance we want to emulate, while a time-varying comparator sequence models problem settings where the learner needs to continuously adapt to a changing environment
in which the best solution is time-varying and must be tracked over time.
The complexity of a given comparator sequence is typically characterized by its

path-length
:

\displaystyle P_{T}=\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\right\|~.

Clearly, if the path-length is large there is no hope to obtain low dynamic regret. The goal is thus to
obtain performance guarantees that gracefully
adapt
to the level of non-stationarity.
For instance,
in the setting of
G
-Lipschitz losses
the minimax optimal dynamic regret guarantee
is of the order of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{(1+P_{T})T})
,
which scales naturally with the complexity of the benchmark sequence and recovers the
optimal
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
static regret guarantee
when the comparator is fixed.

Contributions.

In this work we introduce a new framework for reducing
dynamic regret minimization to static regret minimization.
Our key insight is that competing with a
sequence

u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}
in
\mathcal{W}
can be equivalently
framed as competing with some fixed
function

u(\cdot)

such that
u(t)=u_{t}
for all
t
. In this view,
we effectively transform dynamic regret minimization
over a domain
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a
static
regret minimization
problem over a domain of
functions
, depicted graphically in
Figure

1
.

The choice of the function space is crucial, as it controls the trade-offs of the resulting algorithm. To complete the construction, we carefully design a rich family of function spaces which embed the comparator sequence in a way that (1) optimizes the inherent trade-offs of the function class to achieve optimal dynamic regret guarantees and (2) ensures that the resulting algorithm is computable in practice, despite being stated as an infinite-dimensional optimization problem.
Indeed, the family we design is an instance of a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), a well-studied class of
functions endowed with the familiar structure of a Hilbert space.
The reduction to learning in an RKHS is particularly natural
in the context of online learning—the vast majority of modern online learning
theory is developed for
static regret minimization in Hilbert spaces
, so
our reduction enables the use of the familiar
online learning toolkit while also allowing us to draw upon
deep connections
between dynamic regret minimization, kernel methods, and signal processing theory.

In the linear losses setting, our construction
enables us to achieve the optimal dynamic regret guarantees of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
up to poly-logarithmic terms. Notably, the resulting algorithm is naturally
horizon independent
, and is easily extended to a
scale-free
version.
These are the first algorithms that obtain the optimal
\sqrt{P_{T}}
dependence without prior knowledge of the horizon
T
natively, without resorting to the doubling trick.
Our reduction also enables us to derive new

directionally-adaptive
guarantees,
which
scale as

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\big(\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},u_{t}\right\rangle^{2}\big)}\Big)
, where
\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
are measures of the complexity of the comparator function and complexity of the function class
\mathcal{H}
respectively.

Interestingly, because our reduction only involves viewing the comparator sequence through a different lens, it holds for
any
sequence of loss functions,
contrasting prior works which
are valid only for linear losses
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
)
.
We show that this allows us to account for loss

curvature
and obtain

\mathcal{O}(\lambda\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\log T)
dynamic regret
in the context of exp-concave losses and improper linear regression settings.

Related Works.

Our work is most directly related to a recent thread of research in the linear loss setting initiated by
Zhang et al. 2024
. Their strategy approaches
dynamic regret from a signal processing perspective, wherein the
comparator sequence is stacked into a high-dimensional “signal”
\widetilde{u}=\mathrm{vec}\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{dT}
, and
1
-dimensional static regret algorithms are employed to learn the coefficients of a basis of features which decompose that signal, leading to

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
dynamic regret via a carefully chosen
dictionary of features.

Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
generalize this perspective by
designing static regret algorithms that are applied directly in this high-dimensional space, and derive the
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
bound
by choosing a suitable dual-norm pair in this space, such that

\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}})
.
Our work further extends this perspective by interpreting the comparator sequence as samples of
a
function
in an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, and designing algorithms
which obtain suitable static regret guarantees in function space.
The reduction of
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
can in fact be understood as a special case of our framework by choosing the discrete RKHS
\mathcal{H}
associated with the Dirac kernel.

More broadly, the concept of dynamic regret was originally introduced by
Herbster and Warmuth 1998
;
Herbster and Warmuth 2001
. Later,
Zinkevich 2003
showed that OGD
naturally obtains
\mathcal{O}(P_{T}\sqrt{T})
dynamic regret and

Yang et al. 2016

showed that
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
can be achieved
when prior knowledge of
P_{T}
is available. The first to achieve the
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
rate
without
prior knowledge of
P_{T}
was

Zhang et al. 2018
, who also
proved a matching lower bound. Since then, there have been several refinements to the result, replacing the
T
factor with data-dependent quantities such as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|g_{t}\right\|^{2}
or
\sum_{t}\sup_{x}\lvert{\ell_{t}(x)-\ell_{t-1}(x)}\rvert

(
Cutkosky 2020b
;
Campolongo and Orabona 2021
;
Hall and Willett 2016
)
, or relaxing boundedness
assumptions on the domain or losses
(
Jacobsen and Cutkosky 2022
;
Luo et al. 2022
;
Jacobsen and Cutkosky 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
Going beyond linear losses, various improvements in adaptivity can be obtained
when the losses are smooth or exp-concave, such as replacing the
T
factor with

\sum_{t}\ell_{t}(u_{t})
or
\sum_{t}\sup_{w}\left\|\nabla\ell_{t}(w)-\nabla\ell_{t-1}(w)\right\|^{2}

(
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
.
In the squared loss setting
\ell_{t}(w)=\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-w)^{2}
, minimax optimal rates
of
R_{T}(\mathring{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathring{y}_{T})=\mathcal{O}(C_{T}^{2/3}T^{1/3})
have been obtained where
C_{T}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\lvert{\mathring{y}_{t}-\mathring{y}_{t-1}}\rvert
is the path-length of the benchmark
predictions

(
Koolen et al. 2015
;
Sadhanala et al. 2016
;
Baby and Wang 2019
;
Baby and Wang 2021
;
Baby and Wang 2023
)
.

Notations.

Hilbert spaces are denoted by upper case calligraphic letters.
Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we denote the associated
inner product by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
We denote
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
the space of linear operators from

\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{W}
.
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
is itself a Hilbert space when equipped with the
Hilbert-Schmidt inner product,
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(A^{*}B\right)
,
where
A^{*}\in L(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{H})
is the adjoint of
A
.
The subdifferential set of a function
f
at
x

is denoted by
\partial f(x)
. We will occasionally abuse notation and
write
\nabla f(x)
to mean an arbitrary element of
\partial f(x)
. We will
denote by
[T]
the set
\{1,2,\dots,T\}
. The Fourier transform of a
function
Q
is denoted
F[Q](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q(\omega)e^{-2\pi ix\omega}d\omega

and, when clear from context, we will generally abbreviate
F[Q](x)=:\widehat{Q}(x)
.

Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}
be a Hilbert space of functions
a on compact set
\mathcal{X}
.
The space
\mathcal{H}
is a RKHS
(
Aronszajn 1950
)

if there exists a positive definite function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
k(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{H}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, that has the
reproducing property
, i.e., we have
f(x)=\left\langle f,k(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
The function
k
is called the
kernel function
associated
with
\mathcal{H}
and the function
\phi(x)=k(\cdot,x)
is called the

feature map
. It is known that the kernel function uniquely
characterizes the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
.
A kernel is
universal

if it can approximate any real-valued continuous function on
\mathcal{X}
to
arbitrary accuracy. Many of the standard kernel functions are universal, including the
Gaussian RBF kernel, the Matérn kernel, and inverse multiquadratic kernel.

All kernels considered in this work are universal kernels.

For a detailed introduction to kernel methods, see, e.g.,
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
;
Wendland 2004
;
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
;
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
.

We will often be interested in functions taking
values in
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case the usual RKHS machinery
extends in a straight-forward way via a coordinate-wise
extension. Indeed, we can represent any
w:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

as a tuple
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})
such that

w_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
for each
i
.

An Equivalence Between Static and Dynamic Regret

In this section, we present the main tool that we will use to
develop dynamic regret guarantees for online learning.
The key idea is to interpret the comparator sequence

u_{1},\dots,u_{T}\in\mathcal{W}
as the evaluations of
a function
u(\cdot)
at the discrete time-points

t\in[T]
, allowing us to re-frame dynamic regret minimization
as a
static regret minimization in function space
.

Note that most existing work in online learning revolves around learning in Hilbert spaces, not general function spaces, so if we hope to leverage these existing tools we should
embed the comparator sequence in a
Hilbert space of functions
. In particular,
our approach will be to embed the comparator sequence in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of functions representable by a
reproducing kernel
k(s,t)
and feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
.
Note that this is always possible by selecting a universal kernel on
\mathcal{X}
. Our reduction is conceptually shown in
Algorithm

1
and the following
theorem shows that the dynamic regret w.r.t. comparator sequence
u_{1},\dots,u_{T}

in
\mathcal{W}
is equivalent to
static
regret w.r.t.
a function
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}

on the auxiliary loss sequence

\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\colon W\mapsto\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))
.

Theorem 1

(Dynamic Regret via Kernelized Static Regret)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a compact set,
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{H}
be an RKHS with associated
feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
, and for any
W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
let
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))
.
Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{T}
be an arbitrary sequence in
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
and suppose that
on each round we play
w_{t}=W_{t}\phi(t)\in\mathcal{W}
. Then,
for any comparator sequence
u_{1},\dots,u_{T}
in
\mathcal{W}
and
U\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}

satisfying
u_{t}=U\phi(t)
for all
t
,

\displaystyle\DRegret(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(w_{t})-\ell_{t}(u_{t}))=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W_{t})-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(U))=:\widetilde{\Reg}_{T}(U)\ .

Note that the reduction holds for
any

operator
U\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
which interpolates
the comparator sequence,
u_{t}=U\phi(t)\ \forall t
.
Hence, we can always let
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
be the minimum norm function in

\mathcal{H}
which interpolates these points, and take
U
to be its representation in
{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
.
In fact, since
k
is a
universal kernel, we can
assume that
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
approximates

any
continuous function on
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[1,T]
to arbitrary accuracy, so the assumption that
u(\cdot)

lives in an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
does not actually restrict the functions that we can compare against in any significant way.
We will see a concrete example of an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
which reconstructs arbitrary comparator sequences
in later sections (e.g.,
Theorem

4
).

Our reduction bears a strong resemblance to the reduction
recently proposed by
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
, which
works by embedding the comparator sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{dT}

by simply “stacking” the comparator sequence into
one long vector,
\widetilde{u}=\mathrm{vec}\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{dT}
.
In fact, we show in
Appendix

B
that our framework

precisely
recovers the reduction in
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024

by choosing the discrete RKHS
\mathcal{H}
associated with the Dirac kernel.
However,
notice in particular that this means that their reduction
is inherently tied to
finite
-dimensional features,
whereas ours enables
infinite
-dimensional features.
As we will see in

Section

4
, this distinction is key to obtaining
the optimal path-length dependencies in a horizon-independent manner.
Moreover, note that the regret equality in
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
holds

only
in the context of linear losses,
\ell_{t}(w)\mapsto\left\langle g_{t},w\right\rangle
,
whereas in our framework the regret equality holds for any sequence of losses

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{T}
.
We will see that this
distinction is important in
Section

5
—for example, our reduction allows us to
preserve the curvature of the losses needed to obtain
\mathcal{O}(\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\ln T)

bounds when the original losses
\ell_{t}
are strongly convex
or exp-concave, where
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
is a measure of complexity of the RKHS.

4
Linear Losses

We first consider
the setting of online linear optimization. In this setting, on round
t
the learner receives
linear loss
\ell_{t}(w)=\left\langle g_{t},w\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
, so we construct the auxiliary loss
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}\colon L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)=\left\langle g_{t},W\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle g_{t}\otimes\phi(t),W\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\left\langle G_{t},W\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}},

where
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})

is the rank one operator such that
(g_{t}\otimes\phi(t))(h)=\left\langle\phi(t),h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}g_{t}
for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
As such, it is important that the base algorithm
facilitates an application
of the kernel trick to avoid explicitly evaluating
the feature map
\phi(t)
, which may be infinite-dimensional in general.
To help make things concrete and provide intuitions,
the following example shows that
most algorithms based on Follow the Regularized Leader (FTRL)
are amenable to the kernel trick.

Example 1
.

(Kernel Trick for Kernelized FTRL)
Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{T}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{W}

and
let
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
for all
t
.
Let
\theta_{t}=-\sum_{s=1}^{{t-1}}G_{s}
,

V_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{{t-1}}\left\|G_{t}\right\|^{2}_{{\mathrm{HS}}}
, let
\Psi_{t}(\cdot;V_{t})
be a convex function such that its Fenchel conjugate
\Psi_{t}^{*}
is differentiable,
and
consider the following FTRL update:

\displaystyle W_{t}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{W}\left\langle\theta_{t},W\right\rangle+\Psi_{t}(\left\|W\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}};V_{t})=\nabla_{\theta}\Psi_{t}^{*}\left(\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}};V_{t}\right)=\frac{\theta_{t}}{\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}}(\Psi_{t}^{*})^{\prime}(\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}};V_{t})~.

Then,
V_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left\|g_{s}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}k(t,t)
(
Lemma

9
),

\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}^{2}=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}k(s,s^{\prime})\left\langle g_{s},g_{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
(
Lemma

8
), and
on round
t
,
Algorithm

1
plays

\displaystyle w_{t}=W_{t}\phi(t)=-\frac{(\Psi_{t}^{*})^{\prime}(\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}};V_{t})}{\left\|\theta_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}k(s,t)g_{s}~.

The example shows that most common instances of FTRL
can be kernelized without explicit computation of the feature map.
The example also demonstrates an important consideration when applying static
regret decompositions of this nature: the update described above would require

\mathcal{O}(t)
time and memory to implement in general,
while existing algorithms for dynamic regret can often be implemented using

\mathcal{O}(\ln T)
computation and memory
(
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Jacobsen and Cutkosky 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
.
Luckily, there is already a deep and well-developed literature on efficient approximations for kernel methods
that can be leveraged to translate the algorithms developed from the kernelized
OCO point-of-view into more practically implementable algorithms
(
Sun et al. 2015
;
Liu et al. 2021
, see, e.g.,)
. Since these extensions are already well-understood and since implementating these details would not yield any new insights in the current paper, we
will not consider them further here, focusing instead on
the theoretical development.

Now that we have seen how to translate an algorithm’s updates
to the kernelized setting, we turn now to
how to translate its static regret guarantees
into dynamic regret guarantees.
The following result shows that an algorithm’s
kernelized static regret
guarantee of translates in a straight-forward way to a
dynamic regret guarantee in the original problem.
The proof is immediate by applying
Theorem

1

and computing

\left\|G_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}=\left\|g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}=\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{{\mathcal{W}}}\sqrt{k(t,t)}

by
Lemma

9
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an online learning algorithm defined on Hilbert space

\mathcal{V}
.
Suppose that for any sequence of convex loss functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{T}
on
\mathcal{V}
,

4.1
Controlling the trade-offs induced by the function space

Proposition

1
demonstrates a clear
trade-off between the RKHS norm
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and the associated kernel

k(t,t)
induced by the choice of function space: smaller the RKHS norms correspond to larger function spaces, hence higher values of
k(t,t)
.
In order to obtain the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}T})
dynamic regret, we need to design a
kernel such that
\left\|U\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{P_{T}})
and
k(t,t)
is controlled for all
t
.
Throughout this section, we will assume for simplicity that
d=1
but note that
the extension to
d>1
is straightforward via the coordinate-wise extension in
Section

2
.

Recall that a
translation invariant
kernel over
\mathbb{R}
is characterized
by the Fourier transform of its
spectral density

Q

(
Wendland 2004
)
, where
Q
is a real non-negative integrable
function. In particular, a translation invariant kernel and its associated norm are

k(t,t^{\prime})=\widehat{Q}(t-t^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q(\omega)e^{-2\pi i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}d\omega,\quad\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lvert{\widehat{f}(\omega)}\rvert^{2}}{Q(\omega)}d\omega,

(3)

where we use the short-hand notation
\widehat{g}=F[g]
to denote the Fourier
transorm of a function
g(\cdot)
.
The following example demonstrates why the desired trade-offs between
\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
k(t,t)

are non-trivial using standard translation-invariant kernels.

Example 2

(Existing kernels lead to sub-optimal trade-offs
(
Wendland 2004
)
)
.

At first glance, the spline kernel seems like a natural candidate; indeed, it has
\|u\|^{2}_{\cal H}=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(\sum_{t}\left\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\right\|^{2})
(
Theorem

7
).
However, the spline kernel also has
k(t,t)=t
, leading to a suboptimal rate. On the contrary, for the classical translation invariant kernels such as the Gaussian or the Matern kernels, we have
k(t,t)=O(1)
but
\|u\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\|\nabla^{n}u\|^{2}
for
c_{n}
positive and summable. In this second case, note that
k(t,t)
has the good rate but
\|u\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
and
\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=c^{2}T
already for constant comparators on
[0,T]
,
u(t)=c1_{[0,T]}(t)
,
c>0
, precluding the optimal rate.

Given the above, we next turn our attention to designing a new kernel that will achieve the desired trade-offs.
Since we need to find a delicate balance in the trade-off of
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and
k(t,t)
to achieve optimal rates,
in the first part of the section we first derive a result that identifies general sufficient conditions to bound the RKHS norm of a translation invariant kernel in terms of the
continuous path-length

\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}=\int\lvert{\nabla u(t)}\rvert dt
(
Theorem

3
). Then, in
Proposition

2
, we design an explicit kernel satisfying such conditions, leading to a trade-off of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}T})
. Finally,
in
Theorem

4
we show that under mild conditions (which are satisfied by the kernel in the
Proposition

2
), it is
always possible to find an

u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
such that
u(t)=u_{t}
for all
t
and

\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}=O(\sum_{t}\left\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}})
, so achieving dynamic regret scaling with
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{O}(\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}})
recovers the
usual path-length. The proof of the following theorem can be found in
Section

D.1

Theorem 3
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an integrable strictly positive even function on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and such that
R(x):=2\pi/(x(1+(x/2\pi)^{2m})Q(x))
, is also integrable for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 1
. Let
k
be defined in terms of
Q
as in
Eq.

3
. Then
k
is a translation invariant universal kernel with
k(t,t)\leq\|Q\|_{L^{1}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The RKHS
\mathcal{H}
associated to
k
contains the space of finitely supported functions with bounded derivatives up to order
2m
, and moreover, for any
T>0
and any
2m
-times differentiable function
f
that is supported on
[0,T+1]
,

\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}~~\leq~~c(T)\;\|\nabla f\|_{L^{1}}\;\|f-\nabla^{2m}f\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
c(T):=\|F[R]\|_{L^{1}([-T-1,T+1])}
. If
R
is monotonically decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, then,

c(T)~\leq~\inf_{\alpha>0}~2\pi(T+1)^{2}\int_{0}^{\alpha}R(x)xdx+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\frac{R(x)}{x}dx~,\qquad\forall T>0

With this in hand, the following
proposition provides an example of spectral density
Q
which will leads to
the desired dependency
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}})
, up to poly-logarithmic terms.
Proof can be found in
Section

D.4
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be defined as

Q(\omega)=\frac{1/4\,\log\log\pi}{|\omega|~\,(1+|\omega|^{2}/4\pi^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}\,\,\log(\pi+|\omega|^{-\frac{1}{2}})\,\,\log^{2}\log(\pi+|\omega|^{-\frac{1}{2}})}~.

Then we can apply
Theorem

3
with
m=1
: the function
k
defined in terms of
Q
as in
Eq.

3
is a translation invariant kernel with

k(t,t)\leq 8\pi^{2},~\forall t\in\mathbb{R}
; the associated RKHS norm satisfies

\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}~~\leq~~c^{2}~\|\nabla f\|_{L^{1}}\,\|f-\nabla^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}}~(\ln(1+T)\ln\ln(1+T))^{2},

for any
f
that is
2
-times differentiable and supported in
[0,T+1]
, where
T>2
and
c\leq(2\pi e)^{2}
.

Notably the kernel characterized by
Proposition

2

is

horizon independent
, requiring no upper bound on
T
to control
\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
and
k(t,t)
.
This is a non-trivial property to guarantee using existing methods without resorting to the doubling trick, which is well-known to perform poorly in practice.

A subtlety that we have glossed over thus far is
that the
continuous path-length
,
\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}=\int\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}dt
, does not
necessarily compare favorably to the
classic
discrete path-length

P_{T}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|u_{t}-u_{{t-1}}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}
since the function may vary wildly between the interpolated points.
The next theorem shows that we can always find a function such that
\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}=\mathcal{O}(P_{T})
. Proof can be found in
Section

D.2
.

Theorem 4
.

Let

v_{1},\dots,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\mathcal{H}
be the RKHS associated to kernel
k

contain finitely supported functions with bounded derivatives up to order
2m
, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 1
. Then there exists a function
u\in\mathcal{H}
supported on
[0,T+1]
, such that
u(t)=v_{t}
for all
t\in[T]
and

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|v_{1}\|_{\mathcal{W}}+C\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T}\|v_{t}-v_{t-1}\|_{\mathcal{W}},

\displaystyle\qquad\left\|u-\nabla^{2m}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C^{\prime}\max_{t}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}.

with
C,C^{\prime}
depending only on
m
and given in explicitly in the proof.

The theorem demonstrates that the continuous path-length can be bound by the usual discrete path-length under mild assumptions on the RKHS that are satisfied by the translation invariant kernel with spectral density
Q
chosen according to
Theorem

3
. Based on this observation, we immediately see that the RKHS characterized by the kernel in
Proposition

2

satisfies the condition of the theorem,
and has RKHS norm satisfying

\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|u-\nabla^{2}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}})=\mathcal{O}(M^{2}+M\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}})
where

M=\max_{t}\left\|u_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}
.

Optimal Path-length Dependencies.

Applying our reduction

1

with the translation invariant kernel characterized
by
Proposition

, followed by

to bound
\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{1}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{M^{2}+MP_{T}})

immediately yields the following dynamic regret guarantee for OLO.

Theorem 5
.

Let
G>0
and apply the algorithm characterized in
Proposition

with the kernel
with spectral density described by

.
Then for any

5
Curved Losses

An advantage of our reduction over the dynamic-to-static
reduction of
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
is that, by preserving the curvature of the losses, our reduction allows us to apply
(quasi)second-order methods like Online Newton Step (ONS)
(
Hazan et al. 2007
)
. The following proposition shows that
exp-concave losses retain the crucial property
required to apply ONS under our reduction (proof in
Appendix

E
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\ell_{t}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\beta
-exp-concave function,
let
\mathcal{H}
be an RKHS with feature map
\phi(t)\in\mathcal{H}
,
and define
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))
for
W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
.
Then for any
X,Y\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(X)-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(Y)\leq\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(X),X-Y\big\rangle_{{\mathrm{HS}}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\ell}(X),X-Y\big\rangle^{2}_{{\mathrm{HS}}}~.

Note that this is precisely the curvature assumption that
is required to run Kernelized ONS (KONS)
(
Calandriello et al. 2017b
;
Calandriello et al. 2017a
)
.
Hence, applying our reduction
Theorem

1

with KONS to the loss sequence
\widetilde{\ell}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\ell}_{T}

leads immediately to the following dynamic regret guarantee.

Theorem 6
.

For
any sequence
u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}\in\mathcal{W}
and for

U\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
satisfying
u_{t}=U\phi(t)
for all
t
,

Algorithm

1
applied with KONS guarantees

\displaystyle R_{T}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Big(\lambda\left\|U\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}^{2}+d_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\beta G^{2}k_{\max}}\right)\frac{\ln\left(2\beta G^{2}k_{\max}T\right)}{\beta}\Big),

where
G\geq\left\|\nabla\ell_{t}(w)\right\|
for all
w\in W
,
k_{\max}=\max_{t}k(t,t)
,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{T}(K_{T}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right)
, and

K_{T}=(\left\langle\nabla\ell_{i}(w_{i}),\nabla\ell_{j}(w_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}k(i,j))_{i,j=1}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.

An analogous result also holds in the setting of
online regression. In that setting,
at the start of round
t
the learner
first observes a
context

x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
,
then predicts a
\widehat{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
incurs a loss
\ell_{t}(\widehat{y})=\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-\widehat{y})^{2}
.
In this setting, our reduction
recovers
kernelized online regression
,
by letting
\widehat{y}_{t}=\left\langle f,\phi(x_{t})\right\rangle

where
f\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R})
and
\phi(x_{t})\in\mathcal{H}
is the feature map
associated with
\mathcal{H}
. Hence, applying the Kernelized Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth (VAW)
forecaster
(
Azoury and Warmuth 2001
;
Vovk 2001
)
guarantees regret of the same form as
above. The result follows from
Jézéquel et al. 2019
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}
and for all
t
let
\ell_{t}(\widehat{y})=\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-\widehat{y})^{2}
.
Then for any sequence
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{T},y_{T})
in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{W}
and
any benchmark sequence
\mathring{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathring{y}_{T}
in
\mathbb{R}
and
u\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\mathring{y}_{t}=\left\langle u,\phi(x_{t})\right\rangle
for all
t
,
the Kernelized VAW Forecaster guarantees

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t}(\widehat{y}_{t})-\ell_{t}(\mathring{y}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)y_{\max}^{2}\log\Big(e+\frac{eTk_{\max}^{2}}{\lambda}\Big),

where
k_{\max}=\max_{t}k(t,t)
and
y_{\max}^{2}=\max_{t}y_{t}^{2}
.

Notably, it is known that the dependence on
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
for kernel ridge regression is optimal
(
Lattimore 2023
)
, demonstrating that these trade-offs are unimprovable in the context of dynamic regret as well. Hence, the result above reduces dynamic regret minimization in this setting to the problem of designing a suitable kernel which favorably trades off
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
and
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)
.
One interesting example in this setting is
the linear spline kernel.

Example 3
.

The linear spline kernel
k(s,t)=\min(s,t)

has well-known RKHS norm of
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int|\nabla u(t)|^{2}dt
. Moreover, in
Section

E.1
we show that we can bound
\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\sum_{t}\lvert{\mathring{y}_{t}-\mathring{y}_{t-1}}\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}}):=C_{T}^{\prime}
and
that the effective dimension is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=\mathcal{O}(T/\sqrt{\lambda})
(
Theorems

7
and
8
respectively).
Optimally tuning
\lambda
leads to

\DRegret(\mathring{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathring{y}_{T})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{2/3}(C_{T}^{\prime})^{2/3})

which matches the minimax optimal rate
for forecasting
in the class of discrete Sobolev sequences of bounded variation
(see,
e.g.
,
Sadhanala et al. 2016
;
Baby and Wang 2019
). Note that
\lambda
can be optimally tuned without prior knowledge of any data-dependent quantities using a mixture-of-experts argument along with a simple clipping argument
(
Mayo et al. 2022
;
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
)
.

We note that in the special case of the 1-dimensional squared loss
\ell_{t}(y)=\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-y)^{2}
, it is possible to achieve
R_{T}(\mathring{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathring{y}_{T})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{1/3}C_{T}^{2/3})
where

C_{T}=\sum_{t}\lvert{\mathring{y}_{t}-\mathring{y}_{t-1}}\rvert
is the (un-squared) path-length of the benchmark
predictions
, and this bound is
minimax optimal among the class of discrete TV-bounded sequences, which is more general than the Sobolev class in the example above
(
Baby and Wang 2019
)
. Designing a kernel with a suitable effective dimension to achieve this trade-off has proven non-trivial and is left as a direction for future work.

6
Directional Adaptivity

An exciting benefit of reducing to static regret
is that we can leverage more “exotic” static regret guarantees
to uncover new and interesting trade-offs in dynamic regret,
essentially for free.
For example, in recent years
there has been an interest in algorithms which adapt
to the
directional
covariance between the
comparator and the losses
(
van Erven and Koolen 2016
;
Cutkosky and Orabona 2018
;
Cutkosky 2020a
;
Mhammedi and Koolen 2020
;
Cutkosky and Mhammedi 2024
)
, to guarantee

\displaystyle R_{T}(u)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\sqrt{d\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},u\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}}\Big).

These bounds recover the usual

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|g_{t}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W},*}}\big)
bounds in the worst
case, but could be significantly smaller if the comparator tends to be
orthogonal to the losses.
Passing from dynamic regret to static regret via
Theorem

1
,
the following proposition shows that guarantees of this form translate into dynamic regret guarantees
which naturally decouple the
comparator variability
\left\|U\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}
from the
a
per-round
directional variance penalty
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},u_{t}\right\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}
. The full statement and proof of this result can be found in
Appendix

F
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{T}
be an arbitrary sequence of
G
-Lipschitz convex
loss functions over
\mathcal{W}
.
There exists an algorithm such that for any sequence of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}
in
\mathcal{W}
and
U\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
satisfying
u_{t}=U\phi(t)
for all
t
, the dynamic regret
R_{T}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T})
is bounded by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(L_{k}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)+\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\left[(\lambda+L^{2}_{k})\left\|U\right\|^{2}_{{\mathrm{HS}}}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},u_{t}\right\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}\right]\ln\Big(e+\frac{e\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\Big)}\Big),

where
g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(w_{t})
,

L_{k}=G\sqrt{\max_{t}k(t,t)}
,
K_{T}=(\left\langle g_{t},g_{s}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}k(t,s))_{t,s\in[T]}
, and

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{T}(\lambda I+K_{T})^{-1}\right)
.

7
Discussion

In this paper we developed a general reduction from
dynamic regret to static regret based on embedding the
comparator sequence as a function in an RKHS.
We showed that
the optimal

Appendix A
Additional Functional Analysis Background

In this section we briefly recall some additional definitions
and background from functional analysis, which will be
useful for understanding the proofs of our results but were not
relevant for the main text.

Additional Notations.

The

dual space
of a normed space
\mathcal{H}
is the space of bounded linear functionals

\mathcal{H}^{*}=L(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R})
, and the associated
norm is the
dual norm

\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H},*}=\sup_{\left\|h\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}g(h)

for any
g\in\mathcal{H}^{*}
.
An operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{W}
is

Hilbert-Schmidt
if for any orthonormal basis
\left\{h_{i}\right\}_{i}

of
\mathcal{H}
we have
\left\|T\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}:=\sum_{i}\left\|Th_{i}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}<\infty
.
The space
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
is itself a Hilbert space
with inner product
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i}\left\langle Ah_{i},Bh_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
.

A Brief Review of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces.

A linear functional
\varphi\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R})
is
bounded

if there exists a constant
M
such that
\lvert{\varphi(x)}\rvert\leq M\left\|x\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
for all

h\in\mathcal{H}
.
A
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) is
a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

of functions
h:X\to\mathbb{R}
for which
the
evaluation functional

\delta_{x}:h\mapsto h(x)
is bounded for all

x\in X
.
For any such space, Riesz representation theorem tells us that for any
x\in X

there is a unique
k_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\delta_{x}(h)=\left\langle h,k_{x}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
The function
k(x,x^{\prime})=k_{x}(x^{\prime})
is called the

reproducing kernel
associated with
\mathcal{H}
.
The reproducing kernel is often expressed in terms of
the
feature map

\phi(x)=k_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
as

k(x,x^{\prime})=\left\langle\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

We will often be interested in functions taking
values in
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case the preceding discussion
can be extended in a straightforward way
by considering a coordinate-wise extension.
In particular, observe that in this setting an operator
W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
can be
represented as a tuple
(W_{1},\ldots,W_{d})
such that

W_{i}\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{H}^{*}
for each
i\in[d]
.
Riesz representation theorem then tells us that
there is a
w_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
W_{i}(h)=\left\langle w_{i},h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, so using the reproducing property we have
W_{i}(\phi(t))=\left\langle w_{i},\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=w_{i}(t)
.
Hence, each
W\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
is identified by
a tuple
(W_{1},\ldots,W_{d})\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
and we can write

\displaystyle w(t)=\big(\left\langle w_{1},\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\ldots,\left\langle w_{d},\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)=\big(W_{1}(\phi(t)),\ldots,W_{d}(\phi(t))\big)=W\phi(t)\in\mathcal{W}.

Note the that space
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
is itself a Hilbert space when
equipped with the Hilbert-Schmidt norm,
which in the coordinate-wise extension above
can be expressed as

\left\|W\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}^{2}=\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|w_{i}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
.
This can be seen by definition of
the Hilbert-Schmidt norm: let
\left\{h_{i}\right\}_{i}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
,
then

\displaystyle\left\|W\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\|Wh_{i}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}=\sum_{i}\left\|\left(W_{1}(h_{i}),\ldots,W_{d}(h_{i})\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\|\left(\left\langle w_{1},h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\ldots,\left\langle w_{d},h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\langle w_{j},h_{i}\right\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i}\left\langle w_{j},h_{i}\right\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\|w_{j}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\left\|w\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2},

where the last line uses Parseval’s identity.

\mathcal{W}
be a Hilbert space and let
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle

denote the associated inner product, and
let
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1},\ldots,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{T+1}
be an arbitrary
sequence of non-decreasing norms on
\mathcal{W}
, and let

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{0}:=\sqrt{\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle}\leq\left\|\cdot\right\|_{t}
for all
t
.
Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{T}
be convex functions over
\mathcal{W}

satisfying
\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{t,*}\leq G
for all
t
and
g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(w_{t})
.
Let
\epsilon,\lambda>0
,

V_{t}=4G^{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{{t-1}}\left\|g_{s}\right\|^{2}_{s,*}
,

\alpha_{t}=\frac{\epsilon G}{\sqrt{V_{t}}\log^{2}(V_{t}/G^{2})}
,
and set

\psi_{t}(w)=3\int_{0}^{\left\|w\right\|_{t}}\min_{\eta\leq\frac{1}{G}}\left[\frac{\ln\left(x/\alpha_{t}+1\right)}{\eta}+\eta V_{t}\right]dx
, and
on each round update
w_{{t+1}}=\arg\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\left\langle\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s},w\right\rangle+\psi_{{t+1}}(w)
.
Then
for all
u\in\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle R_{T}(u)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{O}}\left(G\epsilon+\left\|u\right\|\left[\sqrt{V_{T}\ln\left(\frac{\left\|u\right\|\sqrt{V_{T}}}{\epsilon G}+1\right)}\vee G\ln\left(\frac{\left\|u\right\|\sqrt{V_{T}}}{\epsilon G}+1\right)\right]\right)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
hides constant and
\log(\log)
factors (but not
\log
factors).

Proof.

Begin by applying the standard FTRL regret template (see,
e.g.
,

Orabona 2019
):

\displaystyle R_{T}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}-u\right\rangle\leq\psi_{T+1}(u)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}F_{t}(w_{t})-F_{{t+1}}(w_{{t+1}})+\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}\right\rangle,

where
F_{t}(w)=\left\langle\sum_{s=1}^{{t-1}}g_{s},w\right\rangle+\psi_{t}(w)
.
Observe that the summation can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}F_{t}(w_{t})-F_{{t+1}}(w_{{t+1}})+\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\rangle+F_{t}(w_{t})-F_{t}(w_{{t+1}})+(\psi_{t}-\psi_{{t+1}})(w_{{t+1}})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(a)}{=}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla F_{t}(w_{t}),w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\rangle-D_{F_{t}}(w_{{t+1}}|w_{t})+(\psi_{t}-\psi_{{t+1}})(w_{{t+1}})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(b)}{\leq}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\rangle-D_{F_{t}}(w_{{t+1}}|w_{t})+(\psi_{t}-\psi_{{t+1}})(w_{{t+1}})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(c)}{=}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{t,*}\left\|w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\|_{t}-D_{\psi_{t}}(w_{{t+1}}|w_{t})-(\psi_{{t+1}}-\psi_{t})(w_{{t+1}}),

where
(a)
uses the definition of Bregman divergence to write

f(a)-f(b)=\left\langle\nabla f(a),a-b\right\rangle-D_{f}(b|a)
,
(b)
uses the fact that
w_{t}=\arg\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}}F_{t}(w)
, hence

\left\langle\nabla F_{t}(w_{t}),w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\rangle\leq 0
by the first-order optimality
condition, and
(c)
uses the fact that Bregman divergences are invariant to
linear terms, so from the definition of
F_{t}
we have
D_{F_{t}}(\cdot|\cdot)=D_{\psi_{t}}(\cdot|\cdot)
. Moreover, since

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1},\ldots,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{T}
is a non-decreasing sequence of norms, we can bound the terms

\displaystyle(\psi_{{t+1}}-\psi_{t})(w)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{{t+1}}(\left\|w\right\|_{{t+1}})-\Psi_{t}(\left\|w\right\|_{t})\geq\underbrace{\Psi_{{t+1}}(\left\|w\right\|_{t})-\Psi_{t}(\left\|w\right\|_{t})}_{=:\Delta_{t}^{\Psi}(\left\|w\right\|_{t})},

so overall
the regret is bounded by

\displaystyle R_{T}(u)

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{T+1}(u)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{t,*}\left\|w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\|_{t}-D_{\psi_{t}}(w_{{t+1}}|w_{t})-\Delta_{t}^{\Psi}(\left\|w_{{t+1}}\right\|_{t})}_{=:\delta_{t}}.

From here, the rest of the proof follows using the same arguments as

Jacobsen and Cutkosky 2022
, but using our

Lemma

5
to bound

D_{\psi_{t}}(w_{{t+1}}|w_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|w_{t}-w_{{t+1}}\right\|^{2}\Psi_{t}(\left\|\widetilde{w}\right\|_{t})

instead of their Lemma 7.
∎

F.1.1
A Stability Lemma for Weighted Norms

In this section generalize the stability lemma of
Jacobsen and Cutkosky 2022

to weighted norms
\left\|x\right\|_{M}=\sqrt{\left\langle x,Mx\right\rangle}
. This is
the main technical detail that is not covered by the proof
of their static regret algorithm.
Throughout this section we
assume the domain
\mathcal{W}
is a Hilbert space with associated inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. The following helper lemma follows via
a straight-forward but somewhat tedious computation.

Lemma 4
.

Let
g(x):\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
let
f(x)=\sqrt{g(x)}
. Then for
x\in\mathcal{W}
s.t.

g(x)>0
we have

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\frac{\nabla g(x)}{2f(x)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^{2}f(x)=\frac{\nabla^{2}g(x)}{2f(x)}-\frac{\nabla g(x)\otimes\nabla g(x)}{4f(x)^{3}},

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product.

Using this, we have the following Hessian bounds for elliptically-symmetric
functions:

Lemma 5
.

Let
M\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
be a positive definite linear operator and assume
M

is self-adjoint w.r.t.
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. Let

\left\|x\right\|_{M}=\sqrt{\left\langle x,Mx\right\rangle}
be the weighted norm induced by
M
and
let
\psi(w)=\Psi(\left\|w\right\|_{M})
for some convex function
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
bounded away from
0
and any
u\in\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\nabla^{2}\psi(w)u\right\rangle\geq\min\left\{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M}),\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\right\}\left\|u\right\|_{M}^{2}

Moreover, if
\Psi^{\prime}(\cdot)
is concave and non-negative, then for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
bounded
away from
0
and
u\in\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\nabla^{2}\psi(w)u\right\rangle\geq\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{M}~.

Proof.

The proof follows a similar argument to

(
Orabona and Pál 2021
, Lemma 23)
.
Let us first compute the gradients of
f(x)=\left\|x\right\|_{M}=\sqrt{\left\langle x,Mx\right\rangle}
.
Let
g(x)=\left\langle x,Mx\right\rangle
and observe that if
M
is self-adjoint w.r.t.

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
, we have
\nabla g(x)=2Mx
and

\nabla^{2}g(x)=2M
.
Hence, applying
Lemma

4
we have

\displaystyle\nabla f(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{2Mw}{2\left\|w\right\|_{M}}=\frac{Mw}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{2M}{2\left\|w\right\|_{M}}-\frac{4Mw\otimes Mw}{4\left\|w\right\|^{3}_{M}}=\frac{M}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}-\frac{Mw\otimes Mw}{\left\|w\right\|^{3}_{M}}.

Using this, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(w)=\nabla\Psi(\left\|w\right\|_{M})=\nabla\left\|w\right\|_{M}\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})=\frac{Mw}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M}),

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi(w)

\displaystyle=\nabla\left(\nabla\left\|w\right\|_{M}\Psi_{t}^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\left\|w\right\|_{M}\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})+\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\left(\nabla\left\|w\right\|_{M}\otimes\nabla\left\|w\right\|_{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{t}^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\left(\frac{M}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}-\frac{(Mw\otimes Mw)}{\left\|w\right\|^{3}_{M}}\right)+\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\frac{(Mw\otimes Mw)}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}^{2}}-\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}^{3}}\right)}_{=:\beta}(Mw\otimes Mw)+\underbrace{\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}}_{=:\gamma}M

\displaystyle=\beta(Mw\otimes Mw)+\gamma M.

Hence, for any
u\in\mathcal{W}

we have

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\nabla^{2}\psi(w)u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle u,(\beta Mw\otimes Mw+\gamma M)u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta\left\langle u,Mw\right\rangle^{2}+\gamma\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{M}

Now decompose
u=w+v
for some
v
such that

\left\langle Mw,v\right\rangle=0
; such a
v
always exists for positive definite
M
. Then

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\nabla^{2}\psi(w)u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta\left\langle v+w,Mw\right\rangle^{2}+\gamma\left\|v+w\right\|^{2}_{M}=\beta\left\|w\right\|^{4}_{M}+\gamma\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{M}+\gamma\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{M}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{M}}-\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|^{3}_{M}}\right)\left\|w\right\|^{4}_{M}+\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\left(\left\|w\right\|_{M}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{M}+\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{M}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M}),\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\right\}\left(\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{M}+\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M}),\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\right\}\left\|u\right\|^{2}_{M},

where the last step uses the fact that
w
and
v
are orthogonal w.r.t.
M
.

For the second statement of the lemma, we need
only show that
\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})/\left\|w\right\|_{M}\geq\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})
.
This is indeed the case by concavity and non-negativity of
\Psi^{\prime}(\cdot)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\geq\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(0)+\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})(\left\|w\right\|_{M}-0)}{\left\|w\right\|_{M}}\geq\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\left\|w\right\|_{M})~.

∎

Appendix G
Supporting Lemmas

The following lemma is a straight-forward generalization of the usual
log-determinant lemma (see,
e.g.
,
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
, Lemma
11.11)
), taking a bit of extra care to
handle determinants of potentially infinite-dimensional matrices.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and for all
t
let
v_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
. Suppose

v_{t}\otimes v_{t}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
defines a bounded linear operator for all
t

and let
A_{t}=I+\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}\otimes v_{s}
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{t},A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\right\rangle=1-\frac{\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{{t-1}}\right)}{\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{t}\right)}.

Proof.

Observe that for any
t
, we have
A_{t}=A_{{t-1}}+v_{t}\otimes v_{t}
,
so re-arranging terms, factoring, and taking determinants of both sides we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{t}\right)\mathrm{Det}\left(I-A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\otimes v_{t}\right)=\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{{t-1}}\right).

(9)

Note that each of these determinants are well-defined in terms of the Fredholm
determinant: each of the three terms above is a trace-class
perturbation of the identity operator.
Moreover, observe that
A_{t}^{-1}(v_{t}\otimes v_{t})
is a rank-one
operator having single eigenvalue equal to

\lambda=\left\langle v_{t},A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\right\rangle
. Indeed,
for any
w\in\mathcal{H}
we have

A_{t}^{-1}(v_{t}\otimes v_{t})(w)=\left\langle v_{t},w\right\rangle A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}
,
hence, for
w=A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}
we have

\displaystyle A_{t}^{-1}(v_{t}\otimes v_{t})(w)=A_{t}^{-1}(v_{t}\otimes v_{t})(A_{t}^{-1}v_{t})=\left\langle v_{t},A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\right\rangle A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}=\lambda w~.

Therefore, from the standard rank-one perturbation identity for the determinant
we have
\mathrm{Det}\left(I-A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\otimes v_{t}\right)=1-\left\langle v_{t},A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\right\rangle
,
so re-arranging
Equation

9
yields

\left\langle v_{t},A_{t}^{-1}v_{t}\right\rangle=1-\frac{\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{{t-1}}\right)}{\mathrm{Det}\left(A_{t}\right)}~.\qed

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space. For all
t
let
G_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
be a bounded linear
operator and define

S_{t}=\lambda I+\sum_{s=1}^{t}G_{s}\otimes G_{s}
for
\lambda>0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle G_{t},S_{t}^{-1}G_{t}\right\rangle\leq d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\ln\left(e+\frac{e\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right),

where
K_{T}=(\left\langle g_{t},g_{s}\right\rangle k(t,s))_{t,s\in[T]}
and

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{T}(\lambda I+K_{T})^{-1}\right)
.

Proof.

First apply
Lemma

6
with
v_{t}=G_{t}/\sqrt{\lambda}
followed by the elementary inequality

1-x\leq\ln\left(1/x\right)
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle G_{t},S_{t}^{-1}G_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\langle G_{t},\left(S_{t}/\lambda\right)^{-1}G_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}1-\frac{\mathrm{Det}\left(S_{{t-1}}/\lambda\right)}{\mathrm{Det}\left(S_{t}/\lambda\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{Det}\left(S_{t}/\lambda\right)}{{\mathrm{Det}\left(S_{{t-1}}/\lambda\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\mathrm{Det}\left(I+\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}\otimes G_{t}}{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{t}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right),

where the last line uses the well-known fact that the gram matrix

K_{T}=(\left\langle g_{t},g_{s}\right\rangle k(t,s))_{t,s\in[T]}
and the
empirical covariance operator
\sum_{t=1}^{T}G_{t}\otimes G_{t}
have the same
eigenvalues. Moreover, following
Jézéquel et al. 2019
we can use the inequality

\ln\left(1+x\right)\leq\frac{x}{1+x}(1+\ln\left(1+x\right))

to expose a dependence on the effective dimension

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{T}(\lambda I+K_{T})^{-1}\right)
as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle G_{t},S_{t}^{-1}G_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lambda_{t}(K_{T})}{\lambda+\lambda_{t}(K_{T})}\left[1+\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{t}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left[1+\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right)\right]\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lambda_{t}(K_{T})}{\lambda+\lambda_{t}(K_{t})}

\displaystyle=\left[1+\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right)\right]\mathrm{Tr}\left(K_{T}(\lambda I+K_{T})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\left[1+\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\ln\left(e+\frac{e\lambda_{\max}(K_{T})}{\lambda}\right)~.\qed

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\mathcal{H}
be an RKHS with associated feature map
\phi
. For all
t\in[T]
, let
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
denote
the rank-one operator mapping
G_{t}(h)=\left\langle\phi(t),h\right\rangle g_{t}\in\mathcal{W}
. Then for any
t
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}G_{s}\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}^{t}k(s,s^{\prime})\left\langle g_{s},g_{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}~.

Proof.

Let
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Observe that
for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}G_{s}\right)(h)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\langle\phi(s),h\right\rangle g_{s}
. Hence, by definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}G_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}G_{t}h_{i}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{i}\left\langle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\langle\phi(s),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}g_{s},\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{t}\left\langle\phi(s^{\prime}),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}g_{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}^{t}\left\langle\phi(s),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle\phi(s^{\prime}),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle g_{s},g_{s}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\left\langle g_{s},g_{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}\sum_{i}\left\langle\phi(s),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle\phi(s^{\prime}),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\left\langle g_{s},g_{s^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}k(s,s^{\prime}),

where the last line observes that for orthonormal basis
h_{i}
we have
\sum_{i}\left\langle\phi(s),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\left\langle\phi(s^{\prime}),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle\phi(s),\phi(s^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=k(s,s^{\prime})
.
∎

The following theorem shows how to compute the norm of

G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)
, which is the auxiliary loss for OLO under our framework.
Here we state the result in terms of
g_{t}\in\mathcal{W}^{*}
for generality,
but note that in the main text we implicitly invoke Riesz

g_{t}\in\mathcal{W}
,
G_{t}\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})

\left\|G_{t}\right\|=\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}\sqrt{k(t,t)}

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a RKHS with associated feature map
\phi(t)

and let
\mathcal{W}
be a Hilbert space.
Let
\ell_{t}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function
and for any
W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
let
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))
.
Then for any
W\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
,
g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))
, and
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)
,

\displaystyle\left\|G_{t}\right\|_{{\mathrm{HS}}}^{2}=\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W},*}^{2}k(t,t),

where
k(s,t)=\left\langle\phi(s),\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is the kernel associated with

\mathcal{H}
and

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{W},*}
is the dual norm of
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proof.

We have via
Lemma

10
that

\displaystyle G_{t}:=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)\subseteq L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})^{*},

where
g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{*}
.
By Riesz representation theorem,
we can identify a
\widehat{g}_{t}\in\mathcal{W}
such that
for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
,
g_{t}(w)=\left\langle\widehat{g}_{t},w\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
,
and likewise
we can identify

G_{t}\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})^{*}
with a rank-one operator

\widehat{G}_{t}\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
with action

\widehat{G}_{t}(h)=\left\langle\phi(t),h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\widehat{g}_{t}
.
Hence, we have by definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
that for any orthonormal basis
\left\{h_{i}\right\}_{i}
of
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\left\|G_{t}\right\|^{2}_{{\mathrm{HS}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\|G_{t}h_{i}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\langle\phi(t),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left\|\widehat{g}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W},*}\left\|\phi(t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

where the last line again uses Riesz representation theorem to write
\left\|\widehat{g}_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}}=\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W},*}

and then uses
\sum_{i}\left\langle\phi(t),h_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\left\|\phi(t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

by Parseval’s identity. Moreover,
since
\phi(t)
are the features of an RKHS with

\left\|\phi(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\left\langle\phi(t),\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=k(t,t)~.\qed

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a RKHS with feature map
\phi(t)\in\mathcal{H}
,
and let
\mathcal{W}
be a Hilbert space.
Let
\ell_{t}:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}

\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))

W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}

\displaystyle G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})^{*},

W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}

w_{t}=W\phi(t)\in\mathcal{W}

g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(w_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{*}
.
Define
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})^{*}
the
functional on
L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})
with action

\displaystyle G_{t}(W)=\left\langle g_{t},W\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}},\quad\forall W\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}.

Now observe that for
g_{t}\in\partial\ell_{t}(w_{t})
, for any
w\in\mathcal{W}

we have

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(w)\geq\ell_{t}(w_{t})+\left\langle g_{t},w-w_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))+\left\langle g_{t},w-W\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}},

hence for any
V\in{L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})}
we can
take
w=V\phi(t)
to get

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(V\phi(t))\geq\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))+\left\langle g_{t},V\phi(t)-W\phi(t)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\ell_{t}(W\phi(t))+\left\langle g_{t},(V-W)\phi(t)\right\rangle

that is,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(V)\geq\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)+G_{t}(V-W).

so
G_{t}=g_{t}\otimes\phi(t)\in\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(W)\subseteq L(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{W})^{*}
.
∎

For completeness, the following lemma provides the inverse of
a matrix with entries
K_{ij}=\min(i,j)
. An analogous result
can be seen in the proof of
Jacobsen and Orabona 2024
, where a
variant of the matrix
K

appears as an intermediate calculation.

Lemma 11
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
be a matrix with entries
K_{i,j}=\min(i,j)
. Then

K^{-1}
is a tri-diagonal matrix of the form

\displaystyle K^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
-1&2&-1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&-1&2&-1&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&-1&2&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&\\
0&0&0&0&\dots&2&-1\\
0&0&0&0&\dots&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

It can easily be checked that

K
has Cholesky decomposition

K=U^{\top}U
where
U
is the upper-triangular
matrix of
1^{\prime}s
. Hence,

K^{-1}=U^{-1}(U^{\top})^{-1}
. Moreover, the inverse of
U
is
the first-order finite-differences operator
with entries

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j\\
-1&\text{if }j=i+1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Indeed,

(U\Sigma)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{T}U_{ik}\Sigma_{kj}=-U_{i,j-1}+U_{ij}=1
for
i=j
and
zero otherwise.
Computing

K^{-1}=\Sigma\Sigma^{\top}
yields the tri-diagonal matrix of the stated form.
∎
