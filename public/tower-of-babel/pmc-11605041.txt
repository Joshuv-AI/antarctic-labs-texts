Epidemiologic Trends and Socioeconomic Disparities of Ankylosing Spondylitis in South Korea: A Nationwide Population-Based Study, 2010–2021
Authors: Seoung Wan Nam, Jihye Lim, Dae Jin Park, Jun Young Lee, Jae Hyun Jung, Dae Ryong Kang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the epidemiological trends and socioeconomic disparities associated with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) in South Korea over a decade (2010–2021) using National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims data.Materials and MethodsEmploying data from the NHIS database, this study identified 31753 incident AS patients in 2010–2021. We calculated the annual age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates and analyzed crude incidence rates and diagnostic patterns across age groups. Additionally, we compared the mean annual proportions of medical aid recipients between AS patients and the general population, utilizing p for trend analysis to assess the differences between the two groups across increasing age groups.ResultsThe study period saw a steady increase in age-standardized prevalence (from 34.6 to 91.0 per 100000) and incidence rates (from 4.41 to 8.33 per 100000 person-years), with the most substantial rise of incidence found in younger demographics. Diagnostic trends revealed a shift from internal medicine (IM) to other specialties including orthopedics with increasing patient age groups (p for trend=0.008), indicating old-age diagnosis in non-IM departments. A 1.4-fold higher proportion of medical aid recipients in AS patients (p<0.001) and the widening gap of dependency on medical aid in older age groups (p for trend=0.012) compared to the general population highlight socioeconomic disparities.ConclusionThis comprehensive analysis reveals the growing epidemiological burden of AS, especially in younger populations, and the socioeconomic disparities regarding the disease in South Korea. It underscores the need for early diagnosis and effective treatment strategies, paving the way for health interventions and policies aimed at improving patient outcomes and addressing socioeconomic disparities.Keywords: Ankylosing spondylitis, epidemiology, socioeconomic disparities, South Korea

INTRODUCTION

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory arthritis primarily affecting the axial skeleton, leading to significant morbidity and presenting numerous healthcare challenges.1,2 Characterized by its onset at a young age, AS progresses over time, impacting patients throughout their lifetimes and contributing to an escalating cumulative societal burden annually.2,3 Globally, the epidemiology of AS has been a subject of considerable interest due to its impact on quality of life and healthcare systems. Previous literature has suggested a recent rise in the incidence and prevalence of AS, emphasizing the need for comprehensive studies to understand the evolving trends and associated factors.4,5 Our decade-long retrospective cohort study, utilizing data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) in South Korea, presents a crucial opportunity to explore the dynamic trends in AS epidemiology and socioeconomic disparities associated with the disease.

Park, et al.4 reported an annual increase in both the prevalence and incidence of AS from 2010 to 2015 using data from the Korean Health Insurance Review Agency. Moreover, Lee, et al.2 presented increased disability and healthcare expenditures associated with AS using NHIS sample cohort comprising 1111 patients with incident AS from 2003 to 2013. While the impact of AS on healthcare systems and patient quality of life has been extensively documented globally, comprehensive national-level data, particularly from East Asia, are still limited.3,6 Our study encompasses a comprehensive analysis of the entire AS patient cohort in the NHIS database reflecting more recent changes. We not only provide a detailed analysis of the epidemiologic trends but also delve into the clinical characteristics of Korean AS patients, healthcare utilization patterns, and notably, the socioeconomic disparities related to the disease-this includes an in-depth examination of medical aid recipients. Our research underscores the importance of early diagnosis and targeted intervention, providing a benchmark for future studies and policy-making.

Our study aims to comprehensively analyze the evolving landscape of AS epidemiology and associated socioeconomic disparities over the past decade (2010–2021), providing valuable insights for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers alike.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

This study utilized data from the Korean NHIS claims database, known as the National Health Information Database (NHID). The NHID covers approximately 97% of South Korea’s population due to the government’s mandatory National Health Insurance (NHI) system. This system allows patients to pay around 30% of their healthcare costs, with the remaining 3% of the population receiving full coverage through the Medical Aid Program. Healthcare institutions submit claims for the remaining 70% of medical costs, prospectively integrating medical information for nearly all patients into the NHIS claim database. The database includes demographics, socioeconomic data, medical treatments, procedures, disease diagnoses [International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10)], and rare intractable disease (RID) registration information.7,8 AS was included in the RID registration program by the NHI in 2009. RID patients receive enhanced coverage, contributing approximately 10% of their total medical costs. Diagnoses follow uniform criteria distributed by the NHI, with rigorous program application reviews to ensure compliance.4

Study subjects

Patients with claims data for AS between January 1, 2007 and December 31, 2021 were extracted from NHID. AS was defined as ICD-10 code M45.x with RID code V140. In the RID program, patients were diagnosed as AS if they met the modified New York criteria.9 These clinical criteria included: 1) low back pain and stiffness for at least 3 months that was improved by exercise and not relieved by rest; 2) limitation of lumbar spine motion in sagittal and frontal planes; and 3) limitation of chest expansion relative to normal values for age and sex. The radiological criterion was grade ≥2 bilateral sacroiliitis or grade 3 to 4 unilateral sacroiliitis. Definite diagnosis of AS was made if the patient fulfilled radiological criterion along with at least one clinical criterion.9

A total of 68584 patients with diagnostic codes for AS were identified. Among them, we included 60215 patients who were identified with AS at least two times in their outpatient claims at intervals of 7 days or more. We used a wash-out period (2007–2009) to calculate the incidence rate for newly diagnosed AS cases since 2010. Patients who received the diagnosis of other rheumatic diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, adult-onset Still disease, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, systemic sclerosis) or potential AS-mimicking diseases [diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), osteitis condensans, Paget’s disease] were excluded. ICD-10 codes for these excluded diseases are provided in Supplementary Table 1(only online). Additionally, patients diagnosed with AS prior to the age of 16 were excluded. Following this process, 28462 patients were excluded, leaving a total of 31753 patients identified as having AS (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as mean±standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency (percentages) for categorical variables. Differences in characteristics between the two groups were compared using the independent t-test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. The annual prevalence rate was calculated by dividing the number of patients with AS by the total population for a given year. The annual incidence rate was calculated by dividing the number of patients with newly diagnosed AS for a given year by the total population for a given year. Age-standardized rates were calculated to adjust for the differences in the age structure of populations between study years, using the direct standardization method, based on the 2010 Korean standard population. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated assuming a Poisson distribution. Prevalence and incidence rates were presented per 100000 people and per 100000 person-years, respectively. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction was employed to compare the mean age at diagnosis across various medical specialties. P for trend analysis was conducted to examine the trend of change in the proportion of diagnoses made by non-internal medicine departments across increasing age groups. Mean annual proportions of medical aid recipients by age group from 2010 to 2021 were compared between newly diagnosed AS patients and the total population of South Korea, with p for trend analysis used to assess the trends in the differences of proportions between the two groups across increasing age groups. Statistical significance was confirmed at p<0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wonju Severance Christian Hospital (IRB No. CR 322304). Owing to the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of patients with AS in 2010–2021

Clinical characteristics of patients with incident diagnosis of AS from 2010 to 2021 are presented in Table 1. The mean age at diagnosis was 39.7±15.2, which was higher in females (43.1±15.9 vs. 38.5±14.8 in males, p<0.001). The male to female ratio was approximately 2.87:1. Female patients presented with more prevalent comorbidities such as hypertension and dyslipidemia (p<0.001). Uveitis was the most common extra-articular manifestation (EAM) of AS, occurring in 31.1% of patients, with a higher prevalence in females (33.1% vs. 30.5% in males, p<0.001). EAMs, including uveitis, psoriasis, and ulcerative colitis, were more common in female patients (p<0.05).

Medications ever used with the diagnostic codes of AS were analyzed in this cohort. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, sulfasalazine, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibitors were more prevalently used in male patients (p<0.001 for each). Conversely, oral glucocorticoid use was more prevalent in female patients (40.6% vs. 38.1% in male patients, p<0.001). Interleukin (IL)-17 inhibitors, secukinumab and ixekizumab, were approved as second-line biologic therapies after TNF-α inhibitors for reimbursement indication of AS in August 2017 and October 2020, respectively, in South Korea. These medications were used by 1.1% of the AS patient cohort. Additionally, hip joint arthroplasty was more frequently performed in male patients (1.3% vs. 1.0% in female patients, p=0.022).

Diagnostic context and socioeconomic factors in patients with AS in 2010–2021

The residential area at AS diagnosis was categorized into urban and rural, comprising 47.4% and 52.6% of AS patients, respectively. Urban areas included Seoul and six major South Korean metropolitan cities. In South Korea, household income levels are inferred from social security types and health insurance premium levels. Among incident AS patients, 4.3% were medical aid recipients from the lowest income bracket. Diagnoses of AS predominantly occurred in the internal medicine department (76.1%), particularly by rheumatologists (69.7% of all AS diagnoses), with orthopedic surgeons accounting for 16.5% of diagnoses (Supplementary Table 2, only online). Due to the limitations of the claims data, it was not possible to distinguish whether diagnoses made in internal medicine at primary care facilities were specifically by rheumatologists or other subspecialties.

Annual prevalence and incidence rates of AS in South Korea

The age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates of AS from 2010 to 2021 are shown in Fig. 2. The age-standardized prevalence rate exhibited a consistent upward trend, escalating from 34.60 (95% CI: 34.03–35.17) per 100000 individuals in 2010 to 91.01 (95% CI: 90.08–91.94) per 100000 individuals in 2021. Likewise, the age-standardized incidence rate demonstrated year-over-year growth, rising from 4.41 (95% CI: 4.20–4.61) per 100000 person-years in 2010 to 8.33 (95% CI: 8.04–8.62) per 100000 person-years in 2021. In 2018, there was a slight decrease in incidence compared to the preceding years; however, the overall increasing trend remained consistent throughout the study period. This consistent trend of rising prevalence and incidence rates was observed in both genders (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, only online).

Annual incidence trends in AS by age group

In the period from 2010 to 2021, there was a general upward trend in the crude incidence rate of AS, particularly among younger age groups, while the rates in those aged 70 and over showed variability (Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 5 and 6, only online). Notably, the most substantial rise was seen in the younger population, with the incidence rate in individuals aged 20–29 years increasing from 6.38 to 13.72 per 100000 person-years. Similarly, the incidence rate for the 30–39 years age group increased from 6.45 to 10.63 per 100000 person-years. While most age groups demonstrated an upward trend, the rate of increase displayed a more variable pattern in those aged over 70, with a notable surge in diagnoses among elderly female patients between 2015 and 2016. Individuals in their 20s consistently exhibited the highest incidence rates across most years, followed by those in their 30s.

Annual crude incidence rate of ankylosing spondylitis by age group from 2010 to 2021. (A) Total patients. (B) Male patients. (C) Female patients.

Diagnosing specialties across age groups

The analysis of AS diagnosis data from 2010 to 2021 revealed age-related trends in the specialties of diagnosing physicians (Fig. 4). The proportion of diagnoses in the internal medicine department decreased with patient age, with a corresponding increase in diagnoses by other specialties, such as orthopedic surgery, notably after the age of 60 (p for trend=0.008). The average age at diagnosis varied significantly across different medical specialties: 37.9±14.1 years for internal medicine, 43.1±16.4 years for rehabilitation medicine, 43.7±16.8 years for orthopedic surgery, 48.7±16.6 years for neurosurgery, and 52.6±19.4 years for other departments (p<0.001) (Supplementary Table 2, only online). Notably, patients diagnosed in the internal medicine department were younger compared to those diagnosed in all other departments, as determined by post hoc analysis of ANOVA.

Distribution of diagnosing physician specialty by age group for AS diagnoses from 2010 to 2021. Values are presented as frequency (%). AS, ankylosing spondylitis.

Socioeconomic disparities among AS patients: focus on medical aid recipients

A stepwise increase in the proportion of medical aid recipients was observed in newly diagnosed AS patients starting in the 30–39 age group, becoming more pronounced in those over 60. Medical aid recipients were significantly older at the time of AS diagnosis, with an average age of 53.8±18.8 years, in contrast to 39.1±14.8 years for patients with health insurance (p<0.001). The older age groups demonstrated a higher percentage of medical aid recipients compared to the total population, with this difference intensifying in the older age brackets (p for trend=0.012) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, only online). Additionally, the mean annual proportion of medical aid recipients from 2010 to 2021 for prevalent AS patients and the total population of South Korea were 4.05% and 2.93%, respectively, showing statistically higher prevalence in patients with AS (p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

This decade-long retrospective cohort study illuminated the evolving trends in the epidemiology of AS in South Korea utilizing comprehensive data from the NHIS. Our findings revealed a consistent increase in the age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates of AS over 10 years, underscoring the growing clinical and societal burden of the disease. Notably, we observed the highest increment in incidence rates among younger age groups, indicating a shift in the disease’s demographic impact over the study period. Furthermore, our analysis revealed significant socioeconomic disparities associated with this disease, as evidenced by the higher proportion of medical aid recipients among AS patients, and an escalating dependency on medical aid with advancing age, compared to the general population. These findings provide indirect evidence of the detrimental effect of AS on patients’ socioeconomic productivity. This study stands as one of the most comprehensive analyses of AS in the context of South Korea. Through this research, we emphasize the critical importance of proactive social intervention for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of AS to alleviate its socioeconomic burden effectively. Additionally, addressing these socioeconomic disparities necessitates targeted societal and policy considerations to ensure equitable healthcare access and support for AS patients.

Park, et al.4 previously reported the annual prevalence and incidence of AS in South Korea from 2010 to 2015. Although their results showed an annual increasing trend in both the prevalence and incidence of AS, similar to our findings, there were numerical differences to some degree for several reasons. First, our study defined patients with AS as those who visited outpatient clinic two or more times for diagnostic codes of AS separated in time by ≥7 days, instead of a single visit, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy as endorsed by prior research.10,11 Second, we excluded patients with juvenile-onset AS by excluding patients with the first diagnosis of AS before the age of 16 years.12 Additionally, we refined our patient pool by excluding those with other rheumatic diseases or AS-mimicking diseases, such as DISH (Fig. 1). This approach allowed for a more precise patient cohort. Compared to previous studies,4,13 our study offers a more detailed and extended analysis of AS epidemiological trends, especially in terms of diagnostic patterns and their related socioeconomic disparities.

According to the results of a meta-analysis, the global prevalence of AS ranged from 0.02% (95% CI: 0.00–0.21) in Sub-Saharan Africa to 0.35% (95% CI: 0.24–0.48) in Northern Arctic communities, with East Asia showing a prevalence of 0.16% (95% CI: 0.10–0.25).14 Our study indicated that the prevalence of AS in South Korea falls within the lower range compared to other countries in Asia, with an age-standardized prevalence rate increasing from 34.6 to 91.01 per 100000 over the past decade. Our study results also demonstrated an increasing trend in the incidence of AS, particularly pronounced in younger age groups (Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, only online). Park, et al.’s4 analysis from 2010 to 2015 indicated that the incidence of AS in women peaked in the age range of 70–89, based on an analysis from 2015. However, our decade-long analysis revealed that this peak was transient, occurring only during the years 2015–2016 (Fig. 3). The reasons for this pattern remain unclear, despite considering factors such as changes in health policies. Notably, there was a significant increase in AS diagnoses among elderly women in South Korea, particularly in primary care settings and orthopedic departments, during 2015 and 2016 (data not shown). Individuals in their 20s consistently exhibited the highest incidence rates across most years, followed by those in their 30s. This suggests that the rising incidence among younger age groups is a key driver of the overall trends in AS prevalence and incidence. This trend does not necessarily indicate a shift in the disease pattern but rather reflects a positive move towards early diagnosis, contrasting with a higher likelihood of delayed diagnosis in individuals over 50. According to a recent study, symptoms of axial spondyloarthropathy typically emerge before the age of 45, with a median onset age of 26 worldwide, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and policies aimed at improving awareness of the disease.15 Adopting more sensitive diagnostic modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), may also be helpful in diagnosing AS, especially in the early stage of the disease.16 Our observation indicates that after the initiation of insurance coverage for MRI for AS patients in South Korea in 2010, there has been an increasing trend in pre-diagnostic sacroiliac joint MRI within the previous three months of diagnosis over 10 years (p for trend<0.001), which may have contributed to the increase in AS detection (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online).

Our new analysis of AS diagnosis data from 2010 to 2021 revealed intriguing patterns in the specialties of diagnosing physicians (Fig. 4). Notably, there was a discernible shift in the diagnosis patterns with increasing age, reflected in a decreasing proportion of diagnoses in the internal medicine department and a corresponding rise in diagnoses by other specialties, such as orthopedic surgery, particularly after the age of 60. This trend may signify the manifestation of degenerative musculoskeletal diseases leading patients to seek care from orthopedics or rehabilitation medicine as they age.17 Additionally, the variability in the age of diagnosis across different medical specialties highlights the diverse clinical pathways AS patients encounter within the healthcare system. Notably, patients diagnosed within the internal medicine department tended to be younger than those diagnosed in other specialties, suggesting potential variations in diagnostic approaches and referral practices among medical disciplines. Previous studies have pointed to delayed referral to rheumatology as a cause for the delay in the diagnosis of AS.5,18 Encouraging early referral to rheumatology may further optimize diagnostic and management strategies for AS patients.16,17,18

Lee, et al.2 previously highlighted the increasing societal burden of AS in South Korea, noting higher disability rates and healthcare expenditures compared to controls, with increased prevalence of EAMs and comorbidities. The frequency of EAMs, in the order of uveitis, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease, in Korean AS patients has shown a consistent pattern, aligning with global findings (Table 1).2,19,20 AS significantly impacts work disability or productivity loss, with lower employment rates and higher instances of work disability compared to the general population. Indirect costs due to AS’s productivity loss accounted for 73% of its total costs in the US, highlighting its economic impact.3 Our results showed 1.4 times higher proportion of medical aid recipients among AS patients compared to the total population (p<0.001), suggesting a link between AS and low socioeconomic status. The analysis of medical aid recipient trends by age group underscores disparities in healthcare access and financial resources among AS patients, particularly in economically disadvantaged groups (Fig. 5). Furthermore, this could suggest a progressive increase in the likelihood of AS patients belonging to the low-income bracket over time, possibly due to factors such as disability, encompassing both social and physical ramifications, resulting from AS itself.21 Given the increasing incidence of AS, especially in younger populations, emphasizing early diagnosis and management is vital to curb its progression and reduce socioeconomic impacts. Additionally, specific social and policy measures are crucial to enhance care access and support for economically disadvantaged groups.22,23,24,25 Initiatives could include the development of education programs by professional societies for both healthcare providers and patients to heighten AS awareness, policies that encourage early referrals to rheumatology, and financial assistance for diagnostic tests like MRI or ultrasound.16,17,18

The present study has several limitations. First, the AS diagnostic code has not been thoroughly validated in the Korean NHID.1,11 Despite this, due to the strict regulations related to diagnosis and registration within the RID registration program, the accuracy of AS diagnoses is considered high in South Korea.4 Furthermore, we enhanced diagnostic accuracy by including patients who visited the outpatient clinic two or more times with AS diagnostic codes.10,11 To address this limitation, future research should focus on the validation of these diagnostic codes, with the aim of enhancing the accuracy of retrospective studies using NHID. Second, due to data limitations, we were unable to verify the presence of work disability related to AS that could have affected patients’ socioeconomic productivity. Third, we were unable to determine when each patient began experiencing musculoskeletal symptoms prior to diagnosis, preventing assessment of the extent of delayed diagnosis. However, previous studies have convincingly shown that AS hardly occurs in individuals over the age of 45.1,15,26 Therefore, patients diagnosed with AS after the age of 50 are highly likely to represent cases of delayed diagnosis.

In conclusion, our comprehensive decade-long retrospective cohort study using NHIS data provides crucial insights into the epidemiology and socioeconomic impact of AS in South Korea. These findings underscore the imperative for early diagnosis and the implementation of effective treatments to alleviate the disease burden. Our research paves the way for proactive health interventions and policies tailored to address the challenges posed by AS, aiming to improve patient outcomes and reduce socioeconomic disparities associated with the disease.
