Evaluation of reference genes for accurate qRT-PCR normalization in bitter gourd across different tissues and stress conditions
Authors: Shihan Zeng, Tian Xie, Xiaoqi Shu, Zhuning Zhou, Daiyue Yang, Hongrui Ma, Xuzhen Li, Feifan Chen, Libo Tian, Sang Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) is a widely used technique for gene expression analysis, and its accuracy depends critically on stable reference genes for normalization. Bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L.), an annual cucurbit valued for both nutritional and medicinal properties, has recently attracted increasing attention in molecular research. However, systematic evaluation of suitable reference genes in this species remains lacking.ResultsIn this study, eight candidate reference transcripts—McFBX, McARMT, McEXO70, McALDH12A1, McCPR5, McDRB2, McBLOC1S1, and McNPF4.6—were identified through integrated analysis of three public bitter gourd transcriptome datasets, based on high expression abundance and low variability. Their expression stability was evaluated across tissues, genotypes, and treatment conditions of bitter gourd using three commonly applied algorithms: geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper, and RefFinder was subsequently used to perform a comprehensive stability ranking of these reference transcripts. For tissue-specific qRT-PCR, all eight genes showed acceptable stability, with McFBX being the most stable, followed by McNPF4.6. Under stress and hormone treatments, McARMT and McNPF4.6 were identified as the most appropriate controls, while McEXO70 was best suited for complex experimental designs involving multiple tissues, genotypes, and treatments.ConclusionBy broadening the evaluation framework, this study systematically validated the universality and reliability of reference genes under diverse scenarios. These findings provide a robust set of internal controls for accurate qRT-PCR analyses in bitter gourd and will improve the reproducibility of future molecular studies in this species.Keywords: Momordica charantia, Gene expression, Reference gene, QRT-PCR

Background

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), which amplifies fluorescence-labeled PCR products using cDNA synthesized from RNA templates, is widely used for quantifying gene expression because of its high accuracy, simplicity, sensitivity, and specificity [1, 2]. It is particularly effective for comparing gene expression across samples exposed to different conditions, such as varying tissue types, developmental stages, or treatment points, thereby enabling the dissection of transcriptional mechanisms underlying gene function. However, the accuracy of qRT-PCR can be influenced by factors such as RNA integrity, the amount of RNA input for reverse transcription, and the efficiency of the reverse transcription process [3, 4]. To minimize technical variation and improve reliability, normalization against a stably and constitutively expressed internal control gene is essential. These internal control genes, commonly termed housekeeping or reference genes, are critical for reliable interpretation of qRT-PCR data [5, 6]. Previous studies have shown that no single reference gene exhibits consistently stable expression across all tissue types and experimental conditions [7, 8]. Therefore, identifying and validating suitable reference genes for specific tissues and experimental conditions is critical to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of qRT-PCR analyses [9].

Bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L.), an annual herbaceous cucurbit, serves as both a dietary and medicinal crop. Phytochemical and pharmacological studies have shown that bitter gourd is rich in bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides, peptides, saponins, flavonoids, and phenolic substances, which are associated with antioxidant, antitumor, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities [10–12]. Because of its medicinal value and increasing economic importance, bitter gourd has gained attention in genetic studies aimed at improving key horticultural traits [13, 14]. In other cucurbits such as cucumber and watermelon, the biosynthesis of bitterness-associated compounds is transcriptionally regulated by basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors [15, 16], highlighting the key role of transcriptional regulation in metabolite accumulation. Thus, understanding transcriptional regulation in bitter gourd is essential not only for elucidating its biological traits but also for enabling targeted metabolic engineering aimed at enhancing bioactive compound production. However, despite increasing research interest, systematic studies on the selection and validation of reference genes for qRT-PCR in bitter gourd remain scarce compared with those in model species and major crops. Traditional reference genes such as Actin, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and 18S rRNA have often been used based on sequence homology [17–20]. However, the expression stability of these genes under different conditions and across diverse tissue types in bitter gourd has not been comprehensively evaluated. Moreover, genes validated as stable in other species may not be reliable in bitter gourd because of species-specific regulatory differences [21].

The advent of high-throughput sequencing had produced abundant transcriptomic datasets under diverse experimental conditions, providing essential resources for identifying stable reference genes genome-wide. As these technologies matured, transcript quantification became more accurate and reliable. Systematic analyses of multiple RNA-seq datasets subsequently identified candidate genes with consistent expression, which were validated using geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper through qRT-PCR assays. This framework facilitated the selection of suitable reference genes for different experimental designs, thereby improving the accuracy of gene expression studies in bitter gourd.

In this study, we downloaded three publicly available RNA-seq datasets from NCBI and mapped the reads to the bitter gourd reference genome assembly OHB3-1_v2.0 [22]. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), and eight candidate reference genes (Moc01g23280, Moc01g27500, Moc09g36860, Moc03g31160, Moc01g28000, Moc02g03500, Moc02g07710, and Moc01g32680) were preliminarily identified. These candidates were evaluated for stability using Ct values obtained through qRT-PCR across a range of experimental conditions, including different tissues from different genotypes, as well as stress and hormone treatments (SHT). The geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper algorithms were used to assess their stability rankings [23–25]. To further verify reliability, the expression profiles of two well-characterized genes, namely McERECTA and McAPX (Ascorbate peroxidase), were examined using the candidate reference genes. Our results provide a validated set of reference genes for accurate normalization in bitter gourd gene expression studies, thereby contributing to molecular biology research, physiological studies, and genetic improvement of this important crop.

Methods

Public transcriptomic data acquisition and analysis

To identify preliminary candidate reference genes, we utilized three publicly available RNA-seq datasets from independent projects: PRJNA598964, which investigated Fusarium oxysporum infection [26]; PRJNA613314, which examined ethephon treatment [27], and PRJNA687997, which covered multiple tissue types [28]. All datasets were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Raw sequencing reads were first assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.11.9). The cleaned reads were then aligned to the bitter gourd reference genome assembly OHB3-1_v2.0 using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), a fast and sensitive spliced aligner [22, 29],with parameters: --p 8 --dta --max-intronlen 15000.

Transcript assembly and express quantification were performed using StringTie (v2.1.4) to generate individual Gene Transfer Format (GTF) files [30], with the options -p 10 -G -e -o, which estimate transcript abundance and generate FPKM values normalized by transcript length and library size. FPKM values were calculated according to the standard formula : FPKM = [number of fragments mapping to a gene]/([length of gene in kb] × [total number of mapped fragments in millions]). Detail information for all RNA-Seq datasets, including accession numbers, sample size, biological replicates, and sequencing depth, is provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Reference candidate selection and primer design

Candidate transcripts were selected based on high and stable expression across conditions. Genes with zero FPKM in any sample were excluded. For each gene, the standard deviation (σ) and mean (μ) of FPKM values were calculated, and the coefficient of variation (CV = σ/μ) was determined. Genes were ranked by CV, and the ten with the lowest CV values were retained as preliminary candidates. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction and |Log2FC| ≥ 1 were removed. Coding sequences (CDS) of retained candidates were retrieved from CuGenDBv2 (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/v2/) and used for primer design. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S2.

Plant materials and sample conditions

Two advanced inbred lines of bitter gourd, HNU025 and HNU004, were used in this study. HNU025 was originally collected from South China, while HNU004 represents a wild accession from Sri Lanka [31]. Samples were collected under diverse experimental conditions, including different tissues, hormone treatments, and biotic and abiotic stresses.

For tissue sampling, both HNU025 and HNU004 plants were grown under natural field conditions at Hainan University following standard agronomic practices. Collected tissues included leaves, stems, tendrils, shoot apical meristems (SAM), male flowers, and fruits at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 days post-anthesis (dpa). The shoot apical meristem samples were carefully dissected from the primary growing tip of 3–4-week-old seedlings using sterile forceps under a stereomicroscope. Each SAM sample contained approximately 1–2 mm of the dome-shaped meristematic region together with the surrounding leaf primordia, which were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after excision. Three biological replicates were collected for each genotype. For stress and hormone treatments (SHT), only HNU025 plants were used. Seedlings were grown in a controlled growth chamber, and treatments were applied at the two-true-leaf stage (approximately two-week old) when leaves were fully expanded. HNU025 was selected for phenotypic traits in our laboratory, and its response to these specific treatments had been well documented. This provided a consistent and reliable background for validating key candidate reference genes under stress conditions. Hormone treatments consisted of foliar spraying with 100 µM methyl jasmonate (JA), 100 µM salicylic acid (SA), or 100 µM abscisic acid (ABA). Abiotic stress was applied through cold treatment at 4 °C, 12-hour optical cycle, 70% relative humidity. Biotic stress was imposed by inoculating leaves with a spore suspension of Podosphaera xanthii (1 × 106 spores/mL in 0.05% Tween-20), prepared from naturally infected leaves, under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod, 70% relative humidity, and 22℃ [32]. For biotic treatments, leaf samples were collected at 0, 12, and 48 hours post-treatment (hpt) and at 4 and 7 days post-treatment (dpt). Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C until RNA extraction.

To further validate the stability of candidate reference genes identified from transcriptome analysis, two contrasting bitter gourd genotypes, HNU025 and HNU004 [31], were selected for qRT-PCR evaluation under diverse experimental conditions. Samples were collected from multiple tissues (leaf, stem, tendril, shoot apical meristem, male flower, and fruits at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 days post-anthesis) and were also subjected to biotic, abiotic, and hormone treatments. Biotic stress was induced by inoculation with Podosphaera xanthii, abiotic stress was applied through cold treatment (4°C), and hormonal treatments included foliar application of methyl jasmonate (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and abscisic acid (ABA). To account for the influence of different biological and environmental factors, the dataset was organized into six experimental subsets according to genotype, tissue type, and treatment condition: (1) tissue-specific comparisons in HNU025, (2) tissue-specific comparisons in HNU004, (3) tissue-specific comparisons across both genotypes, (4) stress and hormone treatments in HNU025, (5) a combined dataset integrating subsets (1) and (4) in HNU025, and (6) a comprehensive dataset including all conditions. All collected samples were subsequently used for total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis, as described in the following section.

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and qRT-PCR

Frozen samples were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (FOREGENE, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit (NOVA, China). The resulting cDNA was diluted ten-fold with nuclease-free water and used as a template for qRT-PCR assays. qRT-PCR was performed in a 10 µL reaction mixture containing 0.2 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of diluted cDNA, 5 µL of Taq SYBR Green qPCR Premix (NOVA, China), and 2.6 µL of nuclease-free water, using the JENAPCR qTOWER3 G touch instrument. Amplification was carried out using the following program: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Approximately 100 mg of fresh tissue was collected per sample for RNA extraction. Three technical replicates and three biological replicates were set for each sample. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to confirm the specificity of amplification products.

Expression stability analysis of candidate reference genes

To comprehensively evaluate expression stability across the six experimental subsets, three widely used algorithms- geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper were employed for data analysis. Ct values from all samples were analyzed using geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper to comprehensively evaluate the stability of candidate reference genes. According to the user manuals of and NormFinder, raw Ct values were transformed into relative quantities using the formula 2-ΔCt (ΔCt = Individual Ct – minimum Ct) prior to analysis. geNorm calculated the average expression stability value (M), while NormFinder provided stability values (SV); in both cases, higher values indicated lower stability. In contrast, BestKeeper directly analyzed raw Ct values without conversion to relative quantities, BestKeeper assessed stability based on the coefficient of variation (CV) and standard deviation (SD), with lower values considered more stable. In geNorm, the average ranking of each candidate was obtained by summing the rankings of that gene across subsets and dividing by the number of samples. The two genes with the lowest average rankings were identified as the most stable reference genes in each subset. To further validate the reliability of the selected reference genes, their performance was tested by normalizing the expression of McERECTA and McAPX. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [33].

Results

Selection of candidate reference genes based on RNA-seq data

To identify stably expressed non-differentially expressed genes (non-DEGs) across multiple transcriptome datasets, we first analyzed each dataset independently and defined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as those with a log2 fold change |Log2FC| ≥ 1. All DEGs were subsequently excluded from their respective datasets. The remaining non-DEGs that were consistently retained across all three datasets were combined, resulting in 27,178 transcripts (Fig. 1A; Supplemental Table S3).

To further refine this set, genes with an FPKM value of zero in any sample were removed, thereby excluding those not expressed under particular conditions or in specific tissues. This filtering reduced the dataset to 979 genes (Supplemental Table S4). For each of these genes, the CV of FPKM values was calculated to evaluate expression stability. Based on CV rankings, the ten genes with the lowest CV values were selected as preliminary reference transcripts candidates, as they exhibited both relatively high expression and consistent transcript abundance across diverse conditions.

These preliminary candidates included Moc01g23280, Moc01g27500, Moc09g36860, Moc06g01450, Moc03g31160, Moc01g28000, Moc02g03500, Moc02g07710, Moc01g18660, and Moc01g32680 (Fig. 1B; transcripts annotations in Table 1). Notably, these genes represent non-traditional reference transcripts, in contrast to commonly reported housekeeping genes in previous studies.

Expression profiling for the candidate reference transcripts

To assess the expression stability of the ten candidate reference transcripts, qRT-PCR was performed using bitter gourd samples collected from multiple tissues, including leaf, stem, tendril, SAM, and male flower, as well as fruits at five developmental stages (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 dpa). The Ct values obtained across all samples were compiled for further analysis. Primer specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis, which consistently produced a single sharp peak, indicating amplification of a single product (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Most candidate genes displayed relatively stable expression, with Ct values ranging between 22 and 28 (Fig. 1C). McALDH12A1 showed the highest expression level, reflected by the lowest mean Ct value (23.575 ± 1.928), followed by McARMT (24.543 ± 2.157) and McNPF4.6 (24.684 ± 1.988). Although McEXO70 was not the most highly expressed, it exhibited the greatest stability, with the lowest SD (1.838), suggesting consistent expression across all tested conditions (Supplemental Table S2). By contrast, McSIS3L (Ct range: 26.326–39.307) and McAPR5 (Ct range: 21.613–38.485) exhibited substantial variability, indicating poor stability across tissues and developmental stages. Consequently, these two genes were excluded from subsequent analyses, and the remaining eight candidates were retained for further validation.

Comparative analysis of reference gene stability using geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper

To evaluate the stability of the eight candidate reference genes identified from RNA-seq analysis, expression data obtained from qRT-PCR were analyzed using three commonly used algorithms: geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper. These approaches provided complementary assessments of gene expression stability across different experimental subsets. The comparative results are summarized below.

The geNorm algorithm estimates gene stability based on the M value, with values below 1.5 generally considered acceptable. In each subset, the two most stable reference genes were those with the lowest M values (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table S5). An average-ranking approach was further applied to evaluate overall performance across all subsets (Table 2). In the three tissue-specific subsets (1–3), McFBX consistently ranked among the top two, paired with McCPR5 in HNU025, McARMT in HNU004, and McNPF4.6 in the combined genotype comparison, underscoring its robustness across tissues and genotypes. In the SHT subsets, McARMT and McEXO70 exhibited the lowest M values, and this trend persisted when tissue and treatment samples were combined, indicating that these two genes were particularly stable under stress and hormone conditions. In the most complex dataset (subset 6), which integrated tissue, treatment, and genotype variation, McEXO70 and McFBX emerged as the most stable. These findings suggest that McFBX is highly reliable for tissue-specific analyses, whereas McEXO70 is more suitable for complex experimental designs.

Nonetheless, discrepancies were evident across subsets. For example, McCPR5, which ranked highly in tissue-specific analyses (subsets 1–3), performed poorly under treatment-related conditions (subsets 4–6). This inconsistency highlights the importance of evaluating reference genes separately for each experimental design.

NormFinder

NormFinder produced stability rankings that partly overlapped with those from geNorm but also revealed distinct patterns (Table 3). In tissue-specific comparisons, McNPF4.6 and McARMT were the most stable in HNU025, whereas McEXO70 and McCPR5 ranked highest in HNU004. In the combined genotype subset, McARMT and McNPF4.6 were the top performers. The range of SV values was wider in HNU025 (0.080–0.175) and in the combined dataset (0.014–0.043) than in HNU004, suggesting a genotype effect on reference gene stability. Notably, although McNPF4.6 and McARMT ranked slightly lower in HNU004, both exhibited low SV values (0.043 and 0.021, respectively), supporting their reliability for tissue-specific studies. Under SHT conditions, McARMT and McBLOC1S1 were the most stable (SV = 0.018 and 0.023, respectively), and this trend remained consistent in subset 5 (tissues plus treatments) and subset 6 (all conditions). Taken together, these results indicate that McARMT represents the most universally stable reference gene across experimental conditions according to NormFinder.

BestKeeper

BestKeeper evaluates reference gene stability based on the SD and CV of Ct values, with an SD below 1.0 generally considered acceptable. In our analysis, only McEXO70 in subset 2 met this criterion. Most other subsets, particularly those with larger sample sizes or greater complexity, exhibited higher SD values, while subsets 1, 2, and 4—containing fewer samples—showed SD values close to the threshold of 1.0. Stability rankings revealed that McEXO70 was the most stable in subsets 2, 5, and 6, whereas McBLOC1S1 ranked highest in subsets 1 and 3 (Table 4). Interestingly, both genes performed poorly in subset 4 (SHT), where McNPF4.6 emerged as the most stable. However, McNPF4.6 ranked low in the remaining subsets, suggesting that its apparent stability under SHT may reflect limited responsiveness to stress-induced transcriptional variation. This makes it potentially valuable for stress-specific studies. By contrast, its performance declined markedly when additional variables such as tissue type and genotype were included, as observed in subsets 5 and 6.

ReFinder

To resolve the inconsistencies among the rankings generated by geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper, we applied RefFinder [34], a publicly available integrative tool that provides a consensus ranking of candidate reference genes. RefFinder computes the geometric mean of the individual ranking weights from each algorithm to generate an overall stability score (Table 5). In the tissue-specific analyses of the two genotypes HNU025 and HNU004 (subsets 1–3), McFBX ranked first and second, respectively. When both genotypes were analyzed jointly in subset 3, McNPF4.6 was identified as the most stable reference gene. Under stress conditions (subsets 4 and 5), McARMT consistently showed the highest stability. When all experimental conditions were combined, McEXO70 emerged as the most stable reference gene overall. These results were in strong agreement with our manual assessment, confirming that under different experimental designs, these candidate genes exhibit reliable stability and can be used as optimal reference genes for their respective conditions.

Validation of the reference genes using targets with known expression patterns

To validate the performance of the selected reference genes, we examined two target genes with well-documented spatial or stress-responsive expression profiles in plants. The first gene, ERECTA, encodes a leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase involved in aboveground morphogenesis and is typically enriched in the SAM [35, 36]. qRT-PCR analysis showed that McERECTA expression was highest in the SAM when normalized with all eight candidate reference genes, consistent with its established pattern in other species (Fig. 3A-H). Among the candidates, McFBX produced the strongest fold-change between SAM and leaves, supporting the stability rankings from geNorm, though less consistent with NormFinder and BestKeeper (Fig. 3A). McNPF4.6, ranked highest by NormFinder, also generated a SAM-enriched profile, but with a comparatively lower fold-change (~4-fold higher in SAM than in leaves) (Fig. 3H). These results confirm the suitability of McFBX and McNPF4.6 for detecting spatial expression differences, underscoring their reliability in tissue-specific qRT-PCR analyses of bitter gourd.

The second gene, APX, encodes an ascorbate peroxidase, a well-established marker of pathogen infection and hormone responses [37, 38]. To assess reference gene performance under pathogen challenge, McAPX expression was measured after inoculation using four normalization strategies: single reference genes (the stable McARMT, McNPF4.6, and the unstable McDRB2) and a combination of McARMT and McNPF4.6 (Fig. 4). At 0 hpt, all strategies yielded similar McAPX levels. Normalization with McARMT, McNPF4.6, or their combination showed upregulation at 12 and 48 hpt, downregulation at 4 dpt, and upregulation again at 7 dpt, consistent with previous reports [38]. In contrast, normalization with McDRB2 generated an atypical profile, with initial downregulation at 12 hpt followed by patterns similar to those observed with the stable references. These results demonstrate that McARMT, McNPF4.6, and their combination are suitable for studies under pathogen stress, whereas McDRB2 is unsuitable for normalization.

Discussion

Quantitative real-time PCR is one of the most widely used techniques for gene expression analysis in molecular biology. The accuracy of this method depends critically on reliable reference genes to normalize expression data and minimize experimental variation. However, gene expression often varies substantially across developmental stages, tissues, and environmental conditions, which can compromise the stability of conventional reference genes. In addition, post-transcriptional regulation, alternative splicing, and non-coding RNAs may influence expression patterns, further complicating the selection of suitable reference genes. Despite these challenges, most studies on bitter gourd have focused primarily on stress conditions and relied heavily on classical housekeeping genes. Extensive research has shown that reference gene stability is relative rather than absolute, and no single gene maintains stable expression across all biological contexts. Blind reliance on traditional housekeeping genes without prior validation risks generating inaccurate results. Therefore, the choice of reference genes must be tailored to the biological materials and treatment conditions of each study, if possible.

In this study, we addressed these limitations in bitter gourd by implementing a transcriptome-based screening strategy followed by validation across diverse tissues, genotypes, and environmental conditions of this species. To further evaluate expression stability, we integrated multiple statistical algorithms, including geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper [23–25], which provide complementary analytical perspectives. The combined use of these approaches enhanced the robustness of our conclusions and improved the accuracy of reference gene selection.

Our results demonstrated that reference gene performance varied depending on the experimental context. McFBX was identified as the most stable gene across tissues and genotypes. McARMT and McNPF4.6 were better suited for conditions involving SHT. Notably, accurate normalization should exclude unstable genes, as their inclusion may compromise data reliability. However, it is often impractical to identify a universally ideal reference gene under narrowly defined conditions, given the variation in research materials and experimental setups among studies. To balance accuracy and practicality, McEXO70 emerged as a reasonable compromise for complex designs involving multiple tissues, treatments, and genotypes.

We also evaluated several reference transcripts that have been commonly reported in previous studies. Actin, which is widely used in qRT-PCR analyses, was assessed using three annotated homologs in the bitter gourd genome (Moc01g21230, Moc02g08720, and Moc02g21040). All three homologs showed significant differential expression across samples in public transcriptome datasets (Supplemental Table S6), suggesting that they are unsuitable for general normalization in bitter gourd. To further illustrate their variability, we examined the expression profiles of these homologs among different sample group in public transcriptome datasets (not from our own experiments). As summarized in Supplemental Table S6, all three Actin homologs (Moc01g21230, Moc02g08720, and Moc02g21040) exhibited considerable expression variation across the three public transcriptome datasets. The most pronounced changes were observed in PRJNA687997, where expression levels in male flowers were nearly 200-fold higher than in leaves (Log2FC ≈ 7–8). Moderate differences were also detected in PRJNA598964, while PRJNA613314 showed little to no significant variation. These results collectively demonstrate that Actin homologs are unstable under different experimental conditions, further confirming their unsuitability as reference transcripts in bitter gourd. It is worth noting that McTIP41, which was previously reported as a stable reference gene [21], showed consistently stable expression across transcriptome datasets, although its FPKM values were close to zero in some samples. McTIP41 is not typically considered a universal reference gene, which further underscores the potential of transcriptome-based mining strategies to uncover non-classical reference genes with stable expression across diverse conditions. Collectively, these results highlight that traditionally used housekeeping genes may not always provide reliable normalization and emphasize the advantage of a transcriptome-driven approach that integrates both expression variability and the coefficient of variation to identify more robust candidates. Based on our results, we propose that McFBX and McNPF4.6 are suitable for tissue-specific expression assays, McARMT and McNPF4.6 are appropriate for studies involving pathogen infection and hormone treatments in leaves, and McEXO70 represents a practical choice for complex experimental designs that encompass multiple tissues, treatments, and genotypes.

The functions of the top-performing reference genes may help explain their observed stability. McFBX encodes an F-box protein that forms a stable complex with S-phase kinase-associated protein 1 (SKP1) via its highly conserved F-box domain, a motif universally presents in eukaryotes [39]. For instance, FBXO47 regulates TRF1/2 interactions and suppresses ubiquitin-dependent degradation of TRF2; loss of FBXO47 leads to telomere synapsis failure and telomere protection defects, highlighting its essential role in telomere maintenance [40]. Similarly, FBXO32 stabilizes cyclin D through non-degradative ubiquitination [41]. Such regulatory mechanisms are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis across tissues, which may partly account for the observed stability of McFBX expression. McNPF4.6, a member of the nitrate transporter 1/peptide transporter family (NPF), mediates low-affinity nitrate transport (LATS). The expression of NPF genes is generally stable, showing only modest changes even under sharp fluctuations in external nitrate concentrations (0.1 to 10 mM) [42]. McARMT encodes a TPR/ARM repeat protein, a structural motif widely conserved across eukaryotes as well as most prokaryotic and archaeal genomes [43]. Although the precise mechanism underlying its stable expression in bitter gourd is unclear, the evolutionary conservation of TPR/ARM domains suggests inherent structural robustness. McEXO70, a core subunit of the exocyst octameric complex (Sec3/Sec5/Sec6/Sec8/Sec10/Sec15/EXO70/EXO84), plays a critical role in vesicle tethering during exocytosis [44, 45]. In this study, McEXO70 functioned as a compromise reference gene across diverse tissues, treatments, and genotypes. While it may not be the most stable gene under specific conditions, it provides a practical option for complex experimental designs involving multiple variables.

The efficiency of the screened reference genes across different experimental designs was further validated. As a first example, the ERECTA gene, typically highly expressed in the SAM, was examined. In our study, McERECTA consistently showed the highest expression in the SAM when normalized with all eight candidate reference genes, consistent with its reported expression pattern in other species [35, 36, 46]. Among these candidates, McARMT performed best, producing the largest expression difference between the SAM and other tissues. As a second example, the APX gene, a well-characterized marker of pathogen infection and hormone responses, was analyzed. When normalized with the most stable reference genes, McAPX expression patterns were consistent with previous studies [37, 38]. In contrast, normalization with the least stable genes produced inconsistent and biologically implausible profiles, underscoring the importance of selecting appropriate reference genes. These findings with APX further confirmed that the use of unreliable reference genes can obscure true expression patterns.

Finally, our study highlights the methodological importance of integrating multiple algorithms to evaluate reference gene stability. It should be noted, however, that the reference genes proposed in this study were validated for stress and hormone treatment only in the HNU025 genotype. Therefore, their stability across other genetic backgrounds, such as HNU004, remains to be further investigated in future studies. Although each method has its own strengths and limitations, their combined application provides a more robust framework for reference gene selection. We identified a set of reliable reference genes for bitter gourd; however, their stability should still be validated under specific experimental conditions, particularly those involving novel treatments or developmental stages. It should be noted that these reference genes were identified and validated specifically for Momordica charantia, and their universal applicability across different plant species should be interpreted with caution. In recent years, strategies for reference gene selection in Cucurbitaceae crops have advanced considerably. In model species such as cucumber and melon, candidate reference genes are often identified based on homologs of commonly used housekeeping genes from Arabidopsis or other model plants (e.g., Actin, EF1α, UBQ), followed by limited stability validation [21, 47]. Although practical, this homology-based approach may introduce normalization errors, as sequence conservation does not necessarily ensure stable expression. Our study on bitter gourd presents a more data-driven and unbiased strategy. By integrating multiple RNA-seq datasets covering diverse tissues and stress conditions, we systematically screened genes with intrinsically low expression variability, independent of prior annotation. This framework emphasizes intrinsic stability over functional conservation and can be readily adapted to other Cucurbitaceae crops to improve the accuracy of gene expression normalization. The validated reference genes are expected to facilitate more accurate gene expression studies in bitter gourd, and the strategy presented here may also serve as a general framework for identifying and validating reference genes in other plant species.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically evaluated the selection and validation of stable reference transcripts for gene expression analysis in bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L.). By integrating multiple transcriptome datasets, we identified eight candidate genes—McFBX, McARMT, McEXO70, McALDH12A1, McCPR5, McDRB2, McBLOC1S1, and McNPF4.6—and assessed their expression stability across diverse tissues, genotypes, and experimental conditions using the geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper algorithms. The results demonstrated that all candidates were suitable for tissue-based expression comparisons, with McFBX showing the highest stability, followed by McNPF4.6. Under stress and hormone treatment conditions, McARMT and McNPF4.6 were the most reliable references, whereas McEXO70 was particularly suitable for complex experimental designs involving multiple tissues, genotypes, and treatments. Together, this work provides a set of highly reliable reference genes that can significantly improve the reproducibility of molecular studies in bitter gourd and establishes a foundation for standardized qRT-PCR analyses in these species.
