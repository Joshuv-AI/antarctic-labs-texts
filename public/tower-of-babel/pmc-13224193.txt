A unique case of C2 osteochondroma mimicking cervical dystonia
Authors: Siddartha Reddy Musali, Suma Kandukuri, Manoj Chandra, Sruthi Kola, Eshwar Chandra Nandury, Aswini Prabakaran, Rukmini K. Mridula, Rupam Borgohain, Mudumba Vijayasaradhi, Rajesh Alugolu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Osteochondromas are common benign tumors of the bone that are rarely encountered in the spine. Here, a 30-year-old female presented with the gradual 5-month and then subacute 2-week onset of progressive abnormal cervical posturing mimicking dystonia that, on magnetic resonance (MR), proved to be attributed to a C2/3 facet osteochondroma.Case Description: Over 2 weeks, a 30-year-old female developed progressively severe neck pain and abnormal posturing of the neck mimicking cervical dystonia. When the MR documented a C2/3 facet joint osteochondroma, the patient was managed with a decompressive laminectomy. The lesion histopathologically proved to be an osteochondroma.Conclusion: Osteochondromas of the cervical spine may present with severe pain and neck deformity that symptomatically may mimic cervical dystonia.

INTRODUCTION

Osteochondromas are benign tumors of the bone. They may be solitary or multiple in origin when associated with hereditary multiple osteochondromatosis.[4] They typically arrive in the appendicular skeleton, with only 1–4% originating in the spine;[1] most spinal lesions are located in the cervical region.[4] Patients may exhibit radiculopathy or myelopathy, depending on the extent/location of the lesion. Although most may be managed with medications (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) and/or radiofrequency ablation, others may require surgical decompression/excision.

CASE REPORT

A 30-year-old female patient presented with the gradual 5-month and then subacute 2-week onset of torticollis and acute neck pain. On examination, she had atrophy of the suboccipital muscles and her neck postured toward the left (i.e., laterocollis to the right side).

Diagnostic studies for osteochondroma included the following: X-ray, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and fluorine-18 positron emission tomography CT (F18PET-CT).

The patient underwent plain X-rays, MRI, CT, and 18FPETCT. Radiograph of the cervical spine showed mild sclerotic changes in the right C2-C3 facet joint [Figure 1]. The initial cervical MR imaging showed abnormal edema in the right paraspinal muscles at the C2/C3 level. The repeat MRI revealed an ovoid lesion measuring 11 mm × 7 mm on the right at the C2-C3 level, adjacent to the neural foramen, that was intermediate in signal on the T1W images but hypointense on the T2W images. It originated from the right C3 transverse process and was accompanied by right-sided soft-tissue thickening along the right C2-C3 paravertebral space involving the longus colli muscle. It did not enhance with contrast. Further, it abutted the vertebral artery [Figure 2]. The cervical CT showed a well-defined sclerotic lesion with a round, smooth margin arising from the right C3 superior articular facet, measuring 8 mm × 7 mm with an exophytic component that extended into the right neural foramen [Figure 3]. The F18 PET-CT scan showed a small, well-defined osteoid lesion anteriorly at the C2-C3 facet level on the right, measuring 6 mm × 7 mm. Increased tracer uptake suggested either a benign osteoid osteoma or osteochondroma [Figure 4] seen discretely at the anterior aspect of the right C2-C3 facet joint measuring 6*7 mm with increased tracer uptake suggestive of either a benign osteoid osteoma/or osteochondroma. Our provisional diagnosis was osteoid osteoma; in the absence of a variegated appearance, osteochondroma was not initially considered.

Surgery

Surgery was chosen, based on the lesion’s focal impingement on the thecal sac, right-sided C2-C3 nerve roots over radiofrequency ablation. Following a C2/C3 laminectomy, the right C2/3 facet joint was drilled, and the lesion was debulked. This allowed for the “shelling out the rim of the tumor” and was followed by bone graft placement into the remnant of the facet joint to facilitate fusion [Figure 5]. Postoperatively, the patient’s neurological deficit (i.e., neck pain and torticollis) fully resolved.

Arrow showing the debulking/shelling out of the rim of the tumor.

Histopathology

Histo-pathological examination showed a tumor composed of a thin cartilaginous cap, focally lined by a thin fibrous capsule, continuous with mature bone; there were accompanying foci of enchondral ossification at the junction of the cartilaginous cap and the underlying bone. Morphological features were suggestive of osteochondroma [Figure 6].

Histo-pathological examination: (X4) Arrow showing a cartilaginous capsule overlying mature bony trabeculae and underlying bone marrow, confirming a diagnosis of osteochondroma.

DISCUSSION

Osteochondromas account for 8.5% of all the bony tumours, and about 36% of benign osseous lesions.[3] They only rarely (i.e., 1-4%) affect the spinal column, where they typically arise in the cervical region; most occur at C2 [Table 1],[2,4] followed by the C3 and C6 levels.[6,10,11] These lesions that arise due to trauma1 or a congenital perichondral deficiency are usually cartilaginous-capped benign bony neoplasms arising from the outer surface of the bone [Table 2].[5] They may be solitary or multiple when associated with hereditary multiple exostosis.[3]Most arise in males in their 20’s or 30s.[7] Patients may present with radiculopathy (29.5%), myelopathy (27%), numbness, weakness, pain, or a focal mass.[8,12]

On MRs, these tumours, usually iso-intense with a low signal rim of cortical bone, readily demonstrating the extent of the spinal cord/nerve root compression.[5] CTs classically demonstrate the accompanying bony detail characterized as an exophytic bone mass with internal calcification and sclerotic changes in the adjacent bone. Here, the patient’s CT study showed a well-defined, sclerotic, partially exophytic bone with an eccentric lucent rim. Patients with symptomatic lesions should be treated surgically.[11] Operative intervention should include complete excision of the cartilaginous cap to prevent recurrence.[9,11] Incomplete excisions are associated with 2-5% rates of recurrences within 5 years.

CONCLUSION

Osteochondromas of the cervical spine may present radiographically adjacent to unilateral facet joints and extend into the adjacent neural foramina. They may contribute to focal radiculopathy and/or myelopathy depending on their size and level.
