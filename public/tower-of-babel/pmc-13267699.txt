Carrier-free targeted co-assembly of glycyrrhizin and puerarin overcomes ocular barriers for corneal alkali burn therapy via macrophage reprogramming
Authors: Hanhan Peng, Haoyu Li, Jianing Lin, Licheng Li, Muyan Li, Hai Huang, Junyong Wu, Baihua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corneal alkali burns (CAB) represent a devastating form of ocular trauma characterized by the rapid penetration of alkaline agents, triggering a destructive cascade of inflammatory infiltration, neovascularization, and fibrotic remodeling that compromises ocular transparency. Current pharmacological interventions are largely palliative and severely hampered by rapid precorneal clearance and poor epithelial penetrability. To overcome these formidable dynamic and static ocular barriers, we engineered a carrier-free, targeted nanoplatform via the spontaneous co-assembly of natural bioactives glycyrrhizin (GA) and puerarin (PUE), surface-functionalized with cyclic arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (GP@cRGD NPs). The GP@cRGD NPs exhibited robust physicochemical stability and rapid release kinetics precisely tailored for acute trauma intervention. Crucially, cRGD modification significantly enhanced precorneal retention and trans-epithelial penetration via integrin-targeted interactions. In vivo evaluations demonstrated that topical administration of GP@cRGD NPs markedly accelerated epithelial regeneration, suppressed pathological neovascularization, and successfully restored corneal optical clarity in a murine CAB model. Mechanistically, in vivo transcriptomic profiling and immunofluorescence corroborated that this nanomedicine synergistically reprogrammed the local immune microenvironment by directly inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and extinguishing downstream pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling cascades. Ultimately, this study demonstrates that our targeted, multi-mechanistic nano-formulation holds substantial promise for clinical translation, emerging as a powerful therapeutic intervention against severe chemical burns of the eye.

Introduction

Corneal alkali burns (CAB) represent a severe ocular emergency caused by chemical exposure, characterized by rapid epithelial penetration and subsequent stromal degradation [[1], [2], [3]]. Because the cornea is inherently avascular, the sustained alkaline stress disrupts the local microenvironment, triggering acute epithelial loss, persistent inflammation, and delayed fibrotic and angiogenic remodeling [[4], [5], [6]]. This cascade of pathological mechanisms destroys the delicate homeostasis of the ocular surface, culminating in permanent corneal opacification, the development of secondary glaucoma, and potentially catastrophic corneal perforation. The clinical management of CAB is highly challenging due to the temporal complexity of its pathology. Effective intervention demands the simultaneous suppression of acute inflammatory storms (mainly due to the imbalance of the immune microenvironment caused by macrophage instability) and the continuous modulation of delayed fibrotic and angiogenic remodeling [7,8]. Currently, topical eye drops remain the primary therapeutic approach [[9], [10], [11]]. However, their clinical efficacy is severely restricted by the eye's innate static and dynamic barriers, such as high tear turnover and tight epithelial junctions, which cause rapid drug clearance [12]. Increasing the dosing frequency often induces ocular toxicity [13]. More importantly, conventional single-agent therapies fail to simultaneously block the parallel inflammatory, fibrotic, and angiogenic pathways driving CAB progression. Therefore, developing a delivery system capable of prolonged ocular retention and multi-targeted therapeutic action is essential for effective CAB management [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]].

Natural bioactive compounds present a highly promising, multi-target strategy for managing the complex pathophysiology of CAB. Glycyrrhizin (GA), a triterpenoid saponin, exerts strong anti-inflammatory effects by modulating macrophage polarization and restoring immune homeostasis [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Concurrently, puerarin (PUE), a natural isoflavone, provides complementary anti-fibrotic and anti-angiogenic activities while accelerating epithelial barrier restoration [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Combining GA and PUE holds immense translational potential for CAB treatment. However, the inherent poor aqueous solubility of PUE and the rapid precorneal clearance of both agents restrict their clinical application. While traditional nanocarriers can improve drug stability, they are frequently limited by low drug-loading capacities and potential carrier-induced toxicity, complicating their clinical translation. To resolve these pharmacological limitations, carrier-free, self-assembled nanomedicines provide a highly practical and translatable delivery strategy [28,29]. By assembling active drugs directly into nanoscale structures via non-covalent interactions, this approach improves aqueous solubility and bioavailability without relying on inert excipients. To further enhance targeted tissue retention and deep stromal penetration, it is crucial to exploit specific pathological markers. During CAB pathogenesis, αvβ3 integrins are prominently upregulated on damaged corneal epithelial cells, activated macrophages, and proliferating neovascular endothelial cells, making them an ideal target for precisely directing therapeutic agents to the diseased niches.Driven by this rationale, we engineered a carrier-free, cyclic arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (cRGD)-functionalized co-assembled nanomedicine (GP@cRGD NPs) integrating GA and PUE. Through intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking, GA and PUE spontaneously form stable nanoscale aggregates, successfully overcoming the solubility constraint of PUE. The surface functionalization with cRGD specifically homes the nanoparticles to the αvβ3-overexpressing pathological sites. We hypothesized that this active targeting nanoplatform would resist ocular surface clearance mechanisms and deliver a localized, synergistic blockade against the CAB triad: inflammation, fibrosis, and neovascularization. Our comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrate that GP@cRGD NPs achieve superior precorneal adhesion, actively prevent the pathological phenotypic shift of M1 macrophages, and effectively abrogate stromal fibrosis and neovascularization (Fig. 1). This study provides a robust experimental basis for a novel, carrier-free nanotherapeutic strategy tailored for the comprehensive management of severe chemical ocular injuries.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the cyclic RGD peptide-functionalized GA/PUE co-loaded nanoparticles (GP@cRGD) for targeted corneal alkali burn therapy. Materials and methods

Materials

GA was acquired from Nanjing Dilger Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). PUE and Vitamin E polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) were acquired from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Alpha-tocopherol succinate (α-Tos) was purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Urea, sodium chloride (NaCl) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was purchased from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Dalian, China). BCA Protein Assay Kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of GP NPs and GP@cRGD NPs

Briefly, 5 mg of GA and 5 mg of PUE were dissolved in 200 μL of DMSO, followed by the addition of stabilizers including 0.5 mg of TPGS and 2 mg of α-Tos. TPGS, an FDA-approved pharmaceutical excipient, was used to reduce interfacial tension and enhance nanoparticle stability via steric stabilization due to its amphiphilic structure [30,31]. α-Tos was included to further stabilize the nanoparticles through hydrophobic interactions. Subsequently, the organic phase was sonicated for 2 min at room temperature while shielded from light exposure. 4.8 mL of ultrapure water was used as the aqueous phase, while the organic phase was slowly added dropwise into the aqueous phase at a rate of one drop every 5 s under continuous stirring at 600 rpm and room temperature. The system was further stirred at 600 rpm and room temperature for an additional 15 min to allow structural stabilization of the GA-PUE NPs (GP). For the preparation of GP@cRGD NPs, 20 μL of DSPE-PEG2000-cRGD (50 mg/mL) was immediately added dropwise to the GP system at a rate of one drop every 5 s after completion of the organic phase addition to achieve surface modification. All NPs were purified by dialysis against ultrapure water for 1 h at room temperature under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm using dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut off 5000 Da. For the preparation of coumarin 6 (C6)-labeled GP and (C6)-labeled GP@cRGD, C6 was dissolved in DMSO with drugs prior to following procedures.

Characterization of GP NPs and GP@cRGD NPs

The hydrodynamic size distribution (DLS), polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of GP and GP@cRGD were characterized by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer ZS90, Malvern, UK). Morphology of GP and GP@cRGD were observed by transmission electron microscopy (Tecnai G2 Spirit Twin, FEI, Holland).

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption profiles of GA, PUE, GA-PUE solution (G-P), GP and GP@cRGD were obtained with a multifunctional microplate reader (Varioskan™ LUX, Thermo Scientific, USA). On this basis, the UV-vis spectral features and hydrodynamic size distribution of GP and GP@cRGD were further evaluated in different disruptive agents (NaCl, urea and SDS) to clarify the intermolecular forces driving NPs self-assembly.

The concentration of GA and PUE was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (e2695, Waters Corporation, USA) to determine the encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE), and to evaluate their in vitro release kinetics (Figs. S1–2). The column was an Agilent 5 TC-C18(2), C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Briefly, GA was analyzed using the mobile phase of acetonitrile and 0.05% (v/v) phosphoric acid aqueous solution (50:50, v/v), with a detection wavelength of 273 nm and a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. For PUE, the mobile phase comprised A (0.1% citric acid) and B (methanol) at a volume ratio of 75:25, with the detection wavelength set at 250 nm.

Prior to EE and LE analysis, the unencapsulated GA and PUE were removed from the nanoparticle dispersion using the dialysis bag method. Briefly, the nanoparticle dispersion was placed into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cut off 5000 Da and dialyzed against ultrapure water for 1 h at room temperature under gentle magnetic stirring (300 rpm), allowing unencapsulated drugs to diffuse out. After dialysis, 100 μL of the purified nanoparticle dispersion was mixed with 900 μL of methanol and sonicated for 30 min to disrupt the self-assembled nanostructures prior to HPLC analysis for EE determination. For LE determination, the purified nanoparticles were first lyophilized, and the obtained dry powders were weighed. The freeze-dried nanoparticles were then redissolved in methanol and ultrasonicated for 30 min to completely disrupt the self-assembled nanostructures and ensure full release of encapsulated drugs. The resulting solution was subjected for the determination of LE by HPLC analysis. The EE and LE were calculated based on the equations provided below:(1)EE (%) = WLoaded / WTotal × 100(2)LE (%) = WLoaded / WNPs × 100In vitro drug release profiles were determined using the dialysis bag method. Phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was employed as the release medium. The temperature of the system was maintained at 37 ± 0.5°C under continuous magnetic stirring at 600 rpm throughout the experiment. At designated sampling time points, 100 μL samples were collected and replenished with an equal volume of fresh release medium. The collected samples were quantified by HPLC.

The interaction sites of NPs were investigated using a Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Nicolet iN10, USA). Briefly, the dialyzed NPs solution was lyophilized to obtain NPs powders. GA, PUE, physical mixture of GA and PUE, and GP powders were mixed with pre-dried potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of 1:200, thoroughly ground in an agate mortar, and compressed into thin films. The blank KBr film was used as the background. Then the samples were detected with a scanning wavelength range of 4000-400 cm−1 for 32 times, at a resolution of 0.5 cm−1.

Quantification of cRGD by the BCA assay [32]. Briefly, 20 μL of each standard solution and the unknown sample were mixed with 200 μL of BCA working reagent and added to a 96-well plate. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 4 h, and the absorbance was measured at 562 nm. A standard curve was constructed using concentrations ranging from 0 to 150 μg/mL. Ultrafiltration was performed on dialyzed GP and GP@cRGD solutions to remove free cRGD. The retained nanoparticles were re-dispersed in ultrapure water for BCA analysis.

Molecular dynamic simulation of GP NPs

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.6, while structural visualization was conducted using VMD 1.9.3 [33]. Structural optimization and single-point energy calculations of GA and PUE were carried out using ORCA 6.0.1, followed by RESP2 atomic charge derivation using Multiwfn 3.8 [34,35]. All molecular parameters were generated using the GAFF2 force field as implemented in Sobtop 1.0 (dev4). An 8 nm × 8 nm × 8 nm cubic water box was subsequently constructed, and GA and PUE molecules were randomly inserted at a molar ratio of 1:2 (30 GA and 60 PUE molecules). This molar ratio was derived from the experimentally optimized 1:1 mass ratio of GA and PUE, based on their molecular weights (822.942 g mol−1 and 416.382 g mol−1, respectively), corresponding approximately to a molar ratio of 1:2. Next, the system was subjected to 5000 steps of energy minimization, followed by sequential equilibration for 100 ps in the NVT ensemble and 100 ps in the NPT ensemble. Subsequently, 100 ns production MD simulation was subsequently conducted. The root mean square deviation (RMSD), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), radius of gyration (Rg) and hydrogen bond numbers (H-bonds) were analyzed, and intermolecular interactions were visualized through the mIGM module in Multiwfn 3.8 [36].

Cell culture

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs, BeNa Culture Collection, Beijing, China) and human keratocytes (HKs, ScienCell, CA, USA) were cultured as described previously [37,38]. Briefly, Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (Hyclone, Shanghai, China) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) was utilized to culture HCECs and HKs. RAW 264.7 cells (QiDa Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were maintained with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated in a standard humidified environment containing 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Scratch wound healing assay

To evaluate the protective efficacy of various therapeutic interventions on epithelial migratory capacity following alkali-induced injury, an in vitro scratch wound assay was performed. Briefly, HCECs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to full confluence. To simulate in vitro alkali burn injury, the cellular monolayers were exposed to 6 mM NaOH for 1 min, followed by extensive washing with PBS. Following the mechanical generation of a standardized linear wound with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, cells were cultured in FBS-free medium containing the respective treatments to isolate the assessment of migratory capacity from potential proliferative effects. Representative images of the wound gaps were captured at 0 and 24 h post-wounding, and the wound closure was quantitatively analyzed utilizing Fiji software.

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

To assess the phenotypic transition of macrophages, the expression profiles of CD86 and CD206 were evaluated via immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, following the aspiration of the standard culture medium, the cells were subjected to serum starvation in FBS-free medium for 24 h. Subsequently, classical M1 macrophage polarization was induced by co-stimulating the cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, MCE, Shanghai, China) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL, MCE) for 24 h. Following the induction phase, the polarized cells were co-incubated with various therapeutic formulations (GA, PUE, G-P, GP, and GP@cRGD) for an additional 24 h. Untreated naive cells and unmedicated M1-polarized cells were maintained in parallel to serve as the baseline and disease model controls, respectively. Post-treatment, the residual media was discarded, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature. After extensive washing with PBS, non-specific antigenic binding sites were blocked by incubating the cells with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C in the dark with specific fluorophore-conjugated antibodies targeting CD86 (1:200, Biolegend, CA, USA) and CD206 (1:200, Biolegend). Following thorough PBS washing, the cellular nuclei were counterstained, and the samples were mounted. The semi-quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity was executed using Fiji software.

Biocompatibility analysis

To qualitatively assess cytotoxicity, cells were subjected to Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) dual staining. Briefly, after removing the culture media and washing the cell monolayers with PBS, the samples were stained utilizing a Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) strictly following the manufacturer's guidelines. The fluorescent signals distinguishing live from dead cells were subsequently visualized and captured via a fluorescence microscope (Axio Imager M2, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany). For quantitative viability analysis, a routine Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was conducted [39]. Cells cultured in 96-well plates were exposed to predetermined nanoparticle concentrations for 24 h. Afterward, the CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was introduced to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation in the dark. The optical density at 450 nm was then measured with a microplate reader, with relative cell viability calculated as a percentage of the untreated control.

Animals and establishment of the CAB model in mice

All animal procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 20250310). Specific-pathogen-free male C57BL/6J mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, were obtained from SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Changsha, China) and maintained under standard laboratory conditions. Animals were housed in the Animal Center of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, under a controlled 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with free access to standard laboratory chow and water. At the designated time points, in vivo examinations were performed under anesthesia, after which the mice were euthanized by overdose anesthesia in accordance with institutional animal ethics guidelines.

Prior to experimental manipulation, each mouse underwent slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination to confirm ocular surface integrity. General anesthesia was administered via an intraperitoneal injection of 0.3% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 0.2 mL per 100 g of body weight. Local anesthesia was subsequently provided using one drop of obuvacaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Osaka, Japan). To establish the alkali burn model, a circular piece of filter paper (2 mm in diameter) saturated with 1 N NaOH was placed centrally on the right cornea for exactly 30 s. Immediately after removing the filter paper, the eye was flushed with 10 mL of sterile saline to neutralize and clear any remaining alkali. The contralateral left eye was left completely untouched to function as a healthy internal reference. For drug intervention, topical ocular administration was performed once daily according to the assigned treatment groups, with 10 μL of the corresponding formulation instilled into each eye at each administration.

Ocular observations

To monitor disease progression, slit-lamp biomicroscopic examinations were conducted on post-injury days 0, 3, 7, and 14. Before each assessment, the animals were sedated via an intraperitoneal injection of 0.1% sodium pentobarbital. Ocular photographs were systematically captured to grade corneal opacification based on predetermined clinical criteria (Table S1) [40].

Sodium fluorescein staining

The evaluation of corneal epithelial healing was conducted on post-burn days 0, 3, 7, and 14. Specifically, a 1% sodium fluorescein solution was administered topically to highlight the damaged ocular regions. These fluorescent patterns were subsequently captured via slit-lamp biomicroscopy, allowing for the precise quantification of the unhealed epithelial area utilizing ImageJ software.

Intraocular pressure measurements

Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured at the central cornea using a TonoVet rebound tonometer (Icare, Finland). For each eye, IOP was recorded before NPs administration and at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days after treatment.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

On the 28th day following the induction of the injury, the animals were placed under anesthesia to facilitate the collection of the eyes, alongside major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. These harvested tissues were immediately preserved in 4% PFA, subsequently embedded in paraffin blocks, and cut into standard sections. Prior to histological evaluation, the tissue slices underwent deparaffinization using sequential xylene and ethanol solution. The samples were then immersed in hematoxylin for 10 min and counterstained with eosin for 5 min. Finally, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped using a neutral mounting medium before being examined and photographed with an Axio Imager M2 light microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

To perform immunofluorescent evaluations, the paraffin-embedded ocular specimens (5 μm) were initially subjected to deparaffinization and subsequently rehydrated using descending concentrations of ethanol. Following epitope unmasking via a commercial antigen retrieval buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), the tissue slices were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100. Non-specific binding was subsequently blocked by applying a mixture of 10% normal goat serum and 5% BSA for 1 h at ambient temperature. The samples were then exposed to the designated primary antibodies (listed in Table S2) overnight at 4°C. The next day, the sections were treated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for an additional 1 h at room temperature. Finally, DAPI was utilized for nuclear counterstaining before the slides were coverslipped and examined under an Axio Imager M2 fluorescence microscope. The thickness and staining procedures of all sections were strictly kept consistent.

Whole-mount immunofluorescence staining of cornea

To evaluate corneal neovascularization, whole-mount immunofluorescence was executed based on established protocols with slight modifications [41]. Initially, intact enucleated eyes underwent a 2-h fixation in 4% PFA at 4°C. Following a comprehensive PBS wash, the corneas were excised and immersed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C for secondary fixation. Post-fixation, the isolated tissues were rinsed thrice in PBS and treated with a permeabilizing block buffer (5% goat serum and 0.5% Triton X-100) for 2 h at ambient temperature to minimize background signals. The specimens were subsequently exposed to anti-CD31 primary antibodies (Table S2) at 4°C overnight. The following day, the corneas were incubated with corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. After three final PBS washes, four radial incisions were made on each cornea to allow for flattening. The flat-mounted tissues were finally sealed using an antifade reagent and examined under an Axio Imager M2 fluorescence microscope.

Blood biochemistry test

For blood specimen collection, heparin was utilized as the anticoagulant. To isolate the plasma fraction, the acquired samples underwent centrifugation at 4°C for 15 min at 3000 rpm. The isolated plasma supernatant was subsequently subjected to immediate evaluation utilizing an automated biochemical analyzer (Boke Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd, Shandong, China).

RNA sequencing

Transcriptomic profiling via RNA-seq was executed by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) in accordance with their standard operating procedures. At 14 days post-modeling, the animals were euthanized to harvest the globes. The corneas were surgically isolated at the limbus, swiftly washed, and processed for total RNA isolation. To construct the libraries, mRNA was isolated utilizing mRNA Capture Beads, thermally fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Following purification with Hieff NGS® DNA Selection Beads, the cDNA fragments underwent PCR amplification. Sequencing of the prepared libraries was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus system. Subsequent bioinformatic processing—encompassing read mapping against the reference genome, transcript quantification, identification of differentially expressed genes, and comprehensive functional enrichment profiling—was carried out via the OmicsMaster cloud platform (https://report.omicsmaster.com).

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.0). Data derived from normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons involving only two independent experimental groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-test was applied. When assessing variance across three or more cohorts, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) paired with Tukey's post hoc test was utilized. A P-value of less than 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance. Furthermore, to guarantee data robustness and reproducibility, every experiment was repeated with a minimum of three independent biological replicates.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of the NPs

It is well-acknowledged that the drug feeding ratio is critical for the synthesis of self-assembled NPs [42]. In this study, different drug feeding mass ratios of GA and PUE were evaluated based on hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) [43]. The relatively small particle size contributes to the effective accumulation and penetration, while also enabling efficient surface modification for functionalization strategies [44]. Among the tested conditions, the 1:1 mass ratio resulted in nanoparticles with the most favorable hydrodynamic size and PDI (Table S3). DLS analysis showed that GP exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 132.17 ± 2.27 nm (n = 3) at a GA:PUE mass ratio of 1:1, which increased to 171.41 ± 1.65 nm (n = 3) after cRGD modification (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed that both GP and GP@cRGD were homogeneously distributed and presented a spherical morphology with a relatively uniform particle size at a GA:PUE mass ratio of 1:1 (Fig. 2C–D). The average particle sizes of GP and GP@cRGD determined by TEM were 90.87 ± 13.06 nm and 99.87 ± 10.33 nm, respectively (Fig. 2C–D). Compared with DLS measurements, the TEM-derived particle sizes of GP and GP@CRGD nanoparticles were slightly smaller. This discrepancy can be primarily attributed to the inherent differences between the two characterization techniques. TEM measures nanoparticles in a dehydrated state under vacuum conditions, whereas DLS measures particles dispersed in an aqueous environment. In aqueous media, the formation of hydration layers and the PEG corona can significantly increase the apparent hydrodynamic diameter, resulting in larger size values measured by DLS [[45], [46], [47]]. In addition, the zeta potential of GP@cRGD (−34.10 ± 2.13 mV) decreased compared with that of GP (−27.60 ± 1.31 mV) (Fig. 2B). Accordingly, GP with a GA-PUE feeding mass ratio of 1:1 was prepared for subsequent studies. The hydrodynamic diameter, PDI and zeta potential of GP and GP@cRGD remained stable over 35 days, indicating favorable stability (Fig. 2E–F). The UV–vis absorption spectra were subsequently analyzed (Fig. 2G). GP NPs exhibited a significant blue-shift compared with the mixed solution of GA and PUE (G-P), demonstrating that GP formation was not a simple physical mixture but involved intermolecular interactions. To explore the self-assembly interactions involved in NPs, different disruptive agent, including NaCl, SDS and urea, were added. Sodium chloride (NaCl), urea, and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) are widely used to interfere with electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, respectively. Meanwhile, the UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded and particle size was monitored at predetermined time points. Significant changes in the UV-vis spectra of GP and GP@cRGD were observed after incubation in SDS (Fig. 2H–I). In contrast, no significant changes in UV-vis spectra were observed in urea and NaCl. The particle size analysis revealed that both GP and GP@cRGD exhibited noticeable size variations upon SDS intervention indicating that hydrophobic interaction played a dominant role in maintaining NPs stability (Fig. 2J). Additionally, urea intervention had slight impact on size changes, suggesting that hydrogen bonding contributes to the self-assembly process. Conversely, NaCl intervention resulted in negligible changes in particle size, indicating that electrostatic interactions were not the predominant force in NPs self-assembly. Collectively, these findings indicated that hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding contributed critically to the self-assembly of the NPs.

FT-IR spectroscopy was performed to investigate the intermolecular interactions of self-assembled nanoparticles (Fig. 2K). The FT-IR spectrum of the physical mixture retained the characteristic absorption peaks of both GA and PUE without obvious spectral changes, exhibiting a simple superposition of the characteristic bands of the two components. This result suggests limited intermolecular interactions between GA and PUE under physical mixing conditions. Compared with the physical mixture, the O–H stretching vibration band of GP exhibited a pronounced red shift from 3396.56 cm−1 to 3326.75 cm−1, accompanied by enhanced intensity and peak broadening, suggesting strengthened intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions within the assembled system. In addition, the characteristic C=O stretching vibration band of the carboxyl group in GA exhibited a slight blue shift from 1714.48 cm−1 to 1719.57 cm−1, while the conjugated carbonyl (C=O) vibration band of PUE exhibited a red shift from 1636.67 cm−1 to 1630.58 cm−1 after self-assembly. These spectral variations suggest that the vibrational environments of the carbonyl groups were altered during the self-assembly process. Together with the evident red shift and broadening of the O–H stretching vibration band, these results suggested the presence of intermolecular interactions, potentially involving hydrogen bonding, during nanoparticle formation.

To enable the localization and analysis of NPs in subsequent cellular experiments, coumarin 6 (C6)-labeled NPs, expressed as C6-GP and C6-GP@cRGD, were prepared. The C6-labeled formulations exhibited similar particle size distributions and zeta potentials to those of their unlabeled NPs (Fig. S3). Both formulations demonstrated good stability in vitro, maintaining stability for up to 12 h in culture medium containing 10% FBS (Fig. 2L–M). Furthermore, the release kinetics of GA and PUE from GP and GP@cRGD were evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 ± 0.5°C (Fig. 2N–O). The cumulative release of free GA and PUE reached 86.73 ± 2.13% and 90.43 ± 2.89% within 24 h, respectively. Both GP and GP@cRGD exhibited an initial rapid release phase. For GP, the cumulative 24 h release of GA and PUE was 75.24 ± 2.91% and 75.88 ± 1.59%, respectively, compared with 73.09 ± 2.67% and 73.96 ± 1.67% for GP@cRGD. These results indicate that cRGD surface modification has a negligible effect on the intrinsic release behavior of the nanoparticles. The rapid release behavior of the self-assembled nanoparticles may be attributed to the diffusion of weakly bound or surface-localized drug molecules under sink conditions at the early stage, which generates a steep concentration gradient between the dialysis bag and the external medium, thereby facilitating rapid drug transport. Although sustained release is often prioritized in conventional ocular drug delivery, the rapid drug release profile observed in GP@cRGD is highly compatible with the hyper-acute pathophysiology of CAB. Following alkaline injury, the ocular surface experiences rapid agent penetration and an immediate, severe inflammatory storm. An initial fast release is particularly beneficial for topical ocular administration because effective drug concentrations can be achieved within a limited period of time, which may be difficult to accomplish with conventional sustained-release systems during the early stage of acute ocular injury [31]. This is crucial for early intervention against the acute inflammatory cascade before progressive fibrotic remodeling and neovascularization are irreversibly established.

Next, considering the complex ocular surface microenvironment, the stability of GP@cRGD was evaluated in artificial tear fluids over a wide pH range (pH 5.5-8.5). GP@cRGD exhibited good stability under physiologically relevant conditions (pH 6.5 and 7.4), as evidenced by negligible changes in particle size and PDI over 24h (Fig. S4). In contrast, noticeable increases in particle size were observed after 24 h under extreme acidic or alkaline conditions (pH 5.5 and 8.5). This phenomenon may be associated with the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions within the self-assembled structure, leading to nanoparticle dissociation. These findings support the stability of the formulation under physiologically relevant ocular conditions, which is critical for maintaining nanoparticle integrity after topical administration [48]. The EE and LE of GP@cRGD were determined by HPLC, and results demonstrated that both GA and PUE were effectively encapsulated into this NP. The EE and LE of GA were 84.63% ± 2.61% and 35.04% ± 3.77% (n = 3), respectively, while those of PUE were 64.63% ± 4.72% and 36.43% ± 3.94% (n = 3), respectively (Fig. S5). The favorable EE and LE highlight the potential of this nanoparticle system as an effective therapeutic delivery platform. In addition, the EE and LE of the formulation remained stable over 35 days (Fig. S5). The HPLC method used for the determination of EE and LE exhibited good linearity over the tested concentration range, as well as satisfactory precision and recovery (Table S4–7).

To quantify the surface-bound cRGD, a standard curve of cRGD was first established using the BCA protein assay (Fig. S6). The surface modification efficiency of cRGD was 78.56 ± 4.16% (n = 3), indicating successful surface modification by DSPE-PEG2000-cRGD. The stability of cRGD modification under physiological conditions was further evaluated. The retention rate of cRGD remained 87.14 ± 5.16% (n = 3), indicating that most of the surface-modified cRGD remained stably associated with the nanoparticles under physiological tear conditions. This stability may be attributed to the strong hydrophobic anchoring of the DSPE segment within the nanoparticle core and the steric stabilization provided by the PEG chain, which together reduce ligand dissociation in aqueous physiological environments [49].

Mechanistically, the superior performance of GP@cRGD can be attributed to cRGD-mediated integrin targeting [50]. Integrins, particularly αvβ3 and related subtypes, are expressed on corneal epithelial and stromal cells and are often upregulated under inflammatory or injury conditions such as CAB. The cRGD ligand may facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby enhancing cellular internalization and promoting transcellular transport across the epithelial barrier [50]. Beyond improving uptake kinetics, integrin engagement may also increase nanoparticle-cell surface adhesion, reducing precorneal elimination and prolonging residence time on the ocular surface. This dual mechanism—enhanced retention combined with active internalization—likely underlies the sustained fluorescence signals observed in vivo. From a drug delivery perspective, achieving full-thickness corneal penetration through topical administration remains a significant challenge due to the multilayered corneal architecture and tightly regulated paracellular pathways. The ability of GP@cRGD to reach the deep stromal compartment suggests that the formulation effectively exploits transcellular trafficking pathways rather than relying solely on passive diffusion. Such deep penetration is particularly relevant for CAB, where pathological processes involve not only epithelial dysfunction but also stromal inflammation and remodeling.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GP@cRGD rapidly overcome ocular surface barriers, achieve sustained epithelial retention, and effectively penetrate the entire corneal thickness following topical administration. Although macroscopic precorneal retention was not explicitly quantified, GP@cRGD exhibited robust transcorneal penetration and sustained intra-tissue accumulation. Importantly, cRGD functionalization substantially enhances both uptake efficiency and tissue permeability compared with non-targeted GP, thereby establishing a robust pharmacokinetic and tissue-distribution foundation for rapid and comprehensive therapeutic intervention in CAB.

Combined with the high cellular uptake efficiency and strong penetration capability of GP@cRGD, this disruption may facilitate their contact with intraocular tissues and raise concerns regarding potential off-target effects on other organs. Therefore, we comprehensively evaluated the biosafety of GP@cRGD by assessing ocular parameters, organ histology, and serum biochemical indices in mice, together with direct in vitro cytotoxicity assays.

Consistently, in vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that GP@cRGD and their individual components (i.e., GA, PUE, G-P, and GP) did not induce detectable cytotoxic effects in HCECs or HKs across the tested concentration range, including at the highest concentration of 20 μM (Fig. 6H–I and S9). This model reliably recapitulates the characteristic pathological features of CAB, including corneal inflammation, fibrosis, and neovascularization. In both clinical practice and experimental research, slit-lamp examination provides a convenient and intuitive assessment of CAB severity. Accordingly, slit-lamp biomicroscopy was first employed to evaluate the therapeutic effects of GA, PUE, G-P, GP, and GP@cRGD on alkali-burned corneas. As shown in Fig. 7B, immediately after model induction, the central cornea exhibited marked edema and opacity. Baseline clinical scores were comparable among all groups, confirming the consistency and stability of the CAB model. This acute structural damage acts as a potent catalyst for a rapid and overwhelming inflammatory cascade [52,53]. If not promptly suppressed, this early inflammatory storm inevitably drives irreversible downstream complications, including progressive fibrotic scarring and aberrant neovascularization [54]. At 3 days post-injury, no statistically significant differences in clinical scores were observed between the PBS group and the groups treated with free components or aqueous formulations (Fig. 7B and C). While the GP@cRGD group exhibited a visible therapeutic trend toward recovery, the quantitative variations between this group and the other experimental arms remained statistically non-significant during this initial evaluation phase. With disease progression, therapeutic differences gradually emerged. Beginning at 7 days post-injury, GA and G-P treatment groups showed noticeable improvement, whereas the PUE group remained comparable to the PBS group. By day 14, all treatment groups demonstrated significant clinical improvement compared with PBS-treated controls. Notably, from day 7 onward, the GP@cRGD group exhibited significantly superior therapeutic efficacy compared with all other groups. By day 14, corneal opacity in the GP@cRGD group was largely resolved, with only a small residual area of central haze, while the remaining cornea appeared nearly transparent and close to normal. In contrast, corneal opacity in the PBS group progressively worsened over time, and pronounced corneal neovascularization was evident by day 14, accompanied by significantly higher clinical scores than those observed in the treatment groups.

Corneal epithelial injury was further evaluated using fluorescein sodium staining. Aligning with the macroscopic biomicroscopic assessments, the chemical injury produced a sharply defined, round zone of epithelial ablation at the corneal center, clearly highlighted by intense sodium fluorescein uptake (Fig. 7D). At days 3 and 7 post-injury, GP@cRGD treatment significantly reduced fluorescein staining, which appeared predominantly as scattered punctate signals with limited areas of coalescence. In contrast, extensive confluent fluorescein staining persisted in the PBS group as well as in groups treated with free drugs or aqueous formulations. By day 14, fluorescein staining scores were significantly reduced compared with baseline in all groups, including the PBS group (Fig. 7E). This reduction may not necessarily reflect effective epithelial regeneration, but rather aberrant epithelial hyperplasia and stromal fibrosis in untreated corneas, which can artifactually decrease fluorescein uptake. At this time point, free drug and aqueous formulation groups exhibited comparable therapeutic outcomes. Although some eyes in the aqueous formulation group showed relatively lower fluorescein staining, no statistically significant differences were observed between these groups. Overall, both were superior to PBS but inferior to GP@cRGD treatment. Fig. 7F provides a more intuitive visualization of corneal epithelial healing dynamics across all groups, clearly demonstrating that GP@cRGD achieved the most pronounced and sustained therapeutic benefit in this study. Collectively, these results indicate that all components of GP@cRGD contribute to the clinical improvement of CAB; however, among the different formulations tested, GP@cRGD exhibited the most robust and consistent therapeutic efficacy.

In the context of CAB, the cessation of direct alkaline exposure does not immediately halt tissue degradation; the residual saponification of lipid membranes perpetuates progressive structural damage over an extended period. While this ongoing deterioration is not strictly a disease recurrence, the subsequent reparative phase is frequently compromised by deficient cell-matrix anchorage. This poor structural adhesion predisposes the newly regenerated epithelial layer to spontaneous detachment, thereby triggering secondary pathological insults. To evaluate the propensity for this secondary hit and the long-term therapeutic efficacy of our formulation, we longitudinally monitored the clinical outcomes of the untreated CAB control group versus the GP@cRGD-treated group. Drug administration was discontinued after 14 days of treatment, followed by continued observation for long-term evaluation. By days 14 and 21, although the untreated CAB group displayed sluggish, compensatory epithelial closure, the corneas were afflicted by severe stromal edema, persistent opacity, and the aggressive ingrowth of neovascularization (Fig. S10). Conversely, the GP@cRGD-treated corneas achieved remarkable optical transparency (clear visualization of the intricate iris architecture and pupil) alongside robust epithelial integrity. This pronounced therapeutic superiority of GP@cRGD was sustained through day 28, with the corneas maintaining pristine transparency, a stable epithelial barrier, and an absence of macroscopic neovascularization. Intriguingly, at day 28, the untreated CAB group exhibited recurrent, massive fluorescein staining in the central optical zone, inextricably linked to severe neovascularization and the overt formation of central corneal ulcers, as confirmed by slit-lamp biomicroscopy. Such ulcerative deterioration serves as an ominous precursor to imminent corneal perforation. Collectively, these longitudinal findings underscore that GP@cRGD intervention effectively sustains long-term corneal microenvironmental homeostasis, guarantees firm epithelial anchorage, and comprehensively suppresses pathogenic neovascularization. Without such targeted intervention, the inherently dysfunctional nature of the aberrantly regenerated epithelium in CAB ultimately dictates a catastrophic trajectory toward recurrent ulceration and perforation.

Corneal epithelial cell migration is a fundamental prerequisite for re-epithelialization and optimal wound healing, distinct from the fibrotic scar formation typically mediated by corneal stromal cells. The in vitro results demonstrated that NaOH exposure drastically impaired the inherent migratory capacity of HCECs compared to the untreated control. Conversely, the administration of the therapeutic formulations significantly restored and accelerated the migration of the alkali-injured cells. Notably, the GP@cRGD treatment group exhibited the most robust wound closure kinetics, achieving near-complete re-epithelialization of the scratch gap within 24 h (Fig. S11).

While our study primarily utilized PBS as the negative control to evaluate therapeutic efficacy, it is crucial to position the GP@cRGD nanomedicine within the context of current clinical paradigms. Currently, the clinical management of CAB relies heavily on the combined use of topical corticosteroids and off-label anti-VEGF agents. However, these conventional therapies face severe translational bottlenecks. Prolonged application of topical corticosteroids severely inhibits collagen synthesis and fibroblast activity, predisposing the fragile alkali-burned cornea to delayed epithelialization, sterile ulceration, and catastrophic corneal melting [5,58]. Furthermore, steroid-induced ocular hypertension limits their therapeutic window. In contrast, the GA component in our co-assembled system achieves potent suppression of the inflammatory cascades without the collagen-depleting toxicity associated with steroids. Concurrently, the efficacy of topical anti-VEGF monotherapy is frequently compromised by its short ocular half-life, necessitating frequent administrations. More importantly, non-selective and prolonged VEGF blockade can disrupt normal physiological wound healing and neurotrophic support, leading to profound epithelial toxicity and stromal thinning [58]. Therefore, the GP@cRGD co-assembly strategy not only circumvents the severe side effects of traditional steroid and anti-VEGF therapies but also offers a highly efficient, dual-action (anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic) alternative for the comprehensive management of chemical ocular injuries.

Taken together, the present study highlights the exceptional and prolonged restorative capacity of the GP@cRGD nanoplatform in reconstructing corneal integrity and halting abnormal angiogenesis following alkali burns; nonetheless, the exact molecular pathways driving these benefits require future elucidation.

Molecular mechanisms of GP@cRGD NPs on CAB

The above findings demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of GP@cRGD in CAB; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms required further clarification. GA and PUE, the principal components of GP@cRGD, are both reported to possess anti-inflammatory properties. We therefore hypothesized that GP@cRGD may exert its therapeutic effects primarily through modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment.

To explore this possibility, we first performed bulk RNA sequencing on corneal tissues from control and CAB mice. Transcriptomic profiling revealed clear segregation between the two groups, with strong intra-group consistency, indicating robust model establishment and data reliability (Fig. 9A–B). Differential expression analysis revealed a substantial enrichment of macrophage-associated transcripts in CAB corneas, underscoring the central role of macrophage activation in the pathogenesis of chemical ocular injury. Given that macrophage infiltration and pro-inflammatory M1 polarization are pivotal drivers of corneal inflammation, fibrosis, and neovascularization, we systematically categorized the key differentially expressed genes based on their functional ontology. Specifically, we interrogated the transcriptional profiles of classical macrophage markers (Adgre1, Ly6c1, Ly6c2, Mrc1, and Cd68), an M1 polarization indicator (Il1b), chemokine-driven migration mediators (Ccl2, Ccl3, Ccl4, Ccl5, Ccl7, Ccl8, Ccl12, and Cxcl3), extracellular matrix remodeling effectors (Mmp9, Mmp12, Mmp13, and Timp1), and fibro-angiogenic regulators (Serpine1). As depicted in Fig. 9C, the expression levels of these gene clusters were drastically upregulated following CAB injury compared to the naïve control, whereas GP@cRGD treatment robustly reversed these pathological elevations. To further elucidate these macrophage-centric biological cascades, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). In the CAB group, the enriched gene sets were profoundly associated with macrophage migration, cytokine production, chemotaxis, activation, and differentiation, highly indicative of a massive influx and overactivation of innate immune cells. Strikingly, GP@cRGD intervention markedly abrogated these pathological enrichments (Fig. 9D). This transcriptomic evidence conclusively demonstrates that the therapeutic efficacy of GP@cRGD is governed by its potent capacity to reprogram macrophage dynamics and orchestrate the ocular immune microenvironment. Collectively, these data indicate that CAB induces a pronounced pro-inflammatory macrophage response, likely contributing to stromal remodeling and pathological neovascularization. To determine whether GP@cRGD modulates these transcriptional alterations, corneas from GP@cRGD-treated CAB mice were subjected to RNA sequencing and compared with untreated CAB controls. GP@cRGD-treated samples displayed high intra-group consistency and were transcriptionally distinct from CAB samples (Fig. 9E–F). Importantly, macrophage-related genes previously upregulated in CAB were markedly downregulated following GP@cRGD treatment (Fig. 9G). Consistently, GSEA demonstrated significant suppression of macrophage-associated biological pathways, including inflammatory activation and migration programs (Fig. 9H). Immunofluorescence staining further validated these sequencing results. Compared with control corneas, CAB tissues exhibited extensive macrophage infiltration accompanied by a predominance of M1 polarization, as indicated by F4/80 and CD86 staining (Fig. 9I). Aligning perfectly with the severe leukocyte influx visualized through H&E histology, these molecular findings offer a clear pathological rationale for the subsequent development of corneal opacification, fibrotic scarring, and abnormal angiogenesis. Treatment with GA or G-P partially reduced macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization, whereas PUE alone exerted a relatively modest effect. Given the comparable inhibitory effects observed in the GA and G-P groups, and the relatively modest response in the PUE group, it is plausible that the macrophage-modulating activity of G-P is primarily derived from GA. Notably, GA, G-P, and GP@cRGD significantly decreased macrophage accumulation compared with the CAB group (Fig. S14), with GP@cRGD demonstrating the most pronounced effect, significantly restoring macrophage levels. This immunomodulatory profile was further corroborated by in vitro polarization experiments using the RAW 264.7 cell line (Figs. S15 and S16). These findings underscore macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization as pivotal pathological drivers in CAB, processes that GP@cRGD NPs robustly attenuate. Therapeutically, the co-assembled GP core functions as a multi-target modality engineered to fundamentally remodel the pathological immune microenvironment. CAB typically trigger a severe inflammatory cascade, hallmarked by robust macrophage infiltration, which subsequently drives progressive fibrosis and pathological angiogenesis. Within this formulation, GA and PUE directly restrict excessive macrophage recruitment and actively abrogate pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypic polarization. This upstream microenvironmental conditioning successfully extinguishes downstream inflammasome activation, suppresses cytokine cascades, and ultimately mitigates the ensuing fibrotic remodeling and neovascularization [59,60].

To gain deeper mechanistic insights into the dual anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties of the GP@cRGD nanoplatform, we quantified the expression levels of critical pathological mediators, specifically IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, NLRP3, and α-SMA (Fig. 10A). CAB induction markedly elevated the expression of these molecules in corneal tissues, consistent with activation of inflammasome signaling and stromal fibrotic remodeling. Intervention with GA, PUE, G-P, or GP@cRGD markedly downregulated the production of both pro-inflammatory mediators and critical fibrogenesis-associated proteins. Among the monotherapies, GA and PUE exhibited comparable anti-inflammatory effects, with GA showing slightly stronger inhibition of IL-1β expression (Fig. 10B). The G-P formulation generally produced greater suppression than either component alone, suggesting potential additive or cooperative effects. Notably, GP@cRGD consistently demonstrated the most robust inhibitory activity across all assessed markers (Fig. 10B). Collectively, our data validate a therapeutic paradigm wherein the GP@cRGD ameliorates CAB primarily by attenuating macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization, thereby suppressing downstream inflammatory cascades, inflammasome activation, and fibrosis-related signaling (Fig. 10C). It is noteworthy that while studies have documented the anti-inflammatory potential of PUE, the free PUE treatment group in our in vivo CAB model exhibited limited therapeutic efficacy in reversing macrophage polarization and inhibiting neovascularization. This discrepancy fundamentally stems from the inherent physicochemical limitations of free PUE, including its notoriously poor aqueous solubility, limited transcorneal permeability, and rapid clearance by tear turnover, which collectively prevent the drug from reaching an effective therapeutic window within the dense corneal stroma. In this context, the pharmaceutical necessity and clinical superiority of our GA-PUE co-assembly strategy become particularly evident. The amphiphilic nature of GA not only resolves the solubility bottleneck of PUE via supramolecular co-assembly but also acts synergistically to suppress the inflammatory microenvironment. Furthermore, the resulting nanostructure, functionalized with cRGD, effectively breaches the ocular surface barriers, and prolongs precorneal retention. Therefore, this co-assembled targeted delivery system successfully translates the profound pharmacological potential of PUE into tangible in vivo clinical benefits.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a carrier-free, cRGD-functionalized nanotherapeutic platform (GP@cRGD) engineered via the spontaneous co-assembly of GA and PUE. By leveraging intermolecular interactions, this formulation effectively resolves the intrinsic solubility limitations of PUE and achieves high drug loading without relying on inert excipients. Upon topical administration, the nanoparticles demonstrated superior precorneal adhesion and targeted cellular uptake. Functionally, GP@cRGD delivered a synergistic blockade against the CAB pathogenic triad by modulating macrophage plasticity, suppressing stromal fibrosis, and inhibiting pathological neovascularization.

Despite these therapeutic benefits, certain limitations of the current study warrant acknowledgment. First, while transcriptomic profiling confirmed target receptor upregulation in the CAB microenvironment, the absence of a competitive inhibition assay using free cRGD leaves a gap in direct in vitro functional evidence for integrin-mediated endocytosis. Second, the in vivo efficacy evaluation lacked a head-to-head comparison with current clinical standard-of-care agents, such as topical corticosteroids or anti-VEGF eye drops, which partially limits a comprehensive assessment of the formulation's relative clinical superiority. Future investigations will prioritize these in-depth mechanistic validations and active-control trials to facilitate clinical translation.

Beyond the immediate application in CAB, cRGD-functionalized co-assembly strategies offer a practical approach to addressing the translational limitations of conventional small-molecule inhibitors, including poor aqueous solubility, restricted transmembrane permeability, and rapid physiological clearance. By improving targeted tissue penetration and sustained drug accumulation, this carrier-free platform establishes a solid foundation for treating severe ocular surface diseases and may extend to a broader spectrum of systemic inflammatory disorders and solid tumors [61,62].
