WNT6 promotes choriocarcinoma progression by driving proliferation, invasion, and metastasis
Authors: Meilian Peng, Tianfu Lin, Caijin Wu, Chengyu Lv, Xiaodan Mao, Qibin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveChoriocarcinoma has a high propensity for distant metastasis, imposing a substantial psychological burden on patients. Unveiling the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor invasion and metastasis holds great clinical significance.MethodsImmunohistochemistry was employed to detect WNT6 expression in normal early pregnancy villi, regressive hydatidiform moles, malignant hydatidiform moles and gestational trophoblastic neoplasia. Quantitation of mRNA expression by real-time PCR and Western blotting were utilized to quantify WNT6 expression in human chorionic trophoblastic cells and choriocarcinoma cells. Lentiviruses were used to construct stable cell models with either overexpressed or knocked-down WNT6, aiming to explore the impact of WNT6 expression on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ResultsThe expression of WNT6 was significantly higher in malignant tissues and cells compared to normal counterparts. In the malignant hydatidiform mole group, WNT6 expression was correlated with tumor metastasis and the FIGO anatomical stage. Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis indicated that the disease progression time of the high-WNT6-expression subgroup in the malignant hydatidiform mole group was notably shorter than that of the low-expression subgroup. CCK-8 assay, wound healing assay, and Transwell assays demonstrated that overexpression of WNT6 could enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HTR-8 cells. Conversely, interfering with WNT6 expression led to opposite outcomes.ConclusionHigh WNT6 expression is associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis. WNT6 is involved in the development of gestational trophoblastic diseases and can serve as a reference biomarker for disease progression and prognosis assessment.

Introduction

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a tumour formed by abnormally proliferating placental epithelioid trophoblastic cells. GTN includes four different pathological entities: invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, epithelioid trophoblastic tumour (ETT), placental-site trophoblastic tumour (PSTT) and mixed trophoblastic tumour [1]. Choriocarcinoma is a rare and highly aggressive malignant trophoblastic tumor that mainly spreads through the blood and is prone to early and distant metastasis. The most common sites of metastasis include the lungs, vagina, pelvis, liver, and brain. Choriocarcinoma originates from the abnormal proliferation, invasion and metastasis of gestational trophoblasts involved in placental formation. Half of the patients are secondary to hydatidiform mole, but also secondary to labor, abortion, ectopic pregnancy, etc. Its incidence in North America and Europe is 5 cases per 100,000 individuals. In Soueast Asia, however, the incidence is approximately 23 cases per 100,000 individuals [2]. , respectively. The diagnosis and progression of CC are based on the level of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which is released by syncytiotrophoblast cells [3, 4]. hCG serves as a sensitive and specific tumour marker for disease diagnosis and follow-up monitoring after treatment, and the clinical application of hCG has changed the clinical prognosis of GTN patients. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, in terms of hCG levels, hCG levels plateau for 4 consecutive values over 3 weeks (± 10%) or hCG levels rise ≥ 10% for 3 values over 2 weeks, the patient can be diagnosed with GTN after molar pregnancy. Most patients with nonmetastatic GTN can be cured through monotherapy (methotrexate or actinomycin-D), and a few metastatic patients can be cured through multidrug chemotherapy [5–7]. Currently, more and more high-risk patients are using immunotherapy [8, 9]. However, there have been reports indicating that false positive detection of hCG may lead to misdiagnosis of the disease, resulting in patients receiving unnecessary invasive treatment [10–14]. Therefore, developing new and more specific diagnostic biomarkers is crucial for the the accurate diagnosis and effective treatment of GTN.

The Wingless/Integrated (WNT) signalling family assumes a pivotal role in various physiological processes by binding to receptors on the cell membrane. WNT6 is a secreted glycoprotein that acts as a ligand and activator of the WNT/β-catenin pathway. It is crucial in early embryonic development and facilitates the normal formation of multiple organs during late development stages. Aberrant expression of WNT6 in adult cells can give rise to tumorigenesis, metastasis, and chemoresistance (for instance, in bladder cancer and gastric cancer) [15].

In this study, we investigated the expression of WNT6 in gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) tissue samples to evaluate its correlation with various basic clinical features and the prognosis of GTD patients. Additionally, in vitro experiments with cells expressing different levels of WNT6 were also conducted to assess the impact of WNT6 on trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion, as well as the occurrence and development of GTD.

Materials and methods

Tumour tissue specimens

Eighty-two formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks obtained from February 2015 to February 2020 at the Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital, including 34 cases of hydatidiform moles with normal hCG levels followed up for 2 years were considered regressive hydatidiform moles (rHMs), 42 cases of hydatidiform moles with hCG levels reaching the GTN diagnostic criteria after 2 years of follow-up were considered malignant hydatidiform moles (mHMs), and 6 choriocarcinoma samples were obtained. In addition, we collected 10 samples of fresh and intact early pregnancy villous tissues (< 12 gestational weeks). No GTN patients underwent preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. The tissue samples were stored at -80 °C before use. All samples were reviewed by gynaecological pathologists with expertise in GTD, and clinical determinations were made by therapists according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2000 standards. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines and cell culture.

Human choriocarcinoma cells (JEG-3 and JAR) and human chorionic trophoblast cells (HTR-8) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Human choriocarcinoma cells (JEG-3, JAR) were grown in MEM, and human chorionic trophoblast cells (HTR-8) were grown in RPMI 1640 culture media supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, #F8318 sigma) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (#15140122, Gibco). The cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C and passaged every 2 or 3 days. The human choriocarcinoma cell lines were identified through short tandem repeat (STR) analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

A WNT6 mouse anti-human polyclonal antibody (dilution 1:1000; # 24201-1-AP, Proteintech) was used. The staining results were independently evaluated by two professional pathologists using a double-blind method. The average number of randomly selected positive cells in 5 fields under an optical microscope (400×) was calculated. The staining intensity was evaluated as follows: cells with no staining in the cytoplasm, negative, 0 points (-); cells with light yellow cytoplasm, weakly positive, 1 point (+); cells with brown cytoplasm, moderately positive, 2 points (++); and cells with brown cytoplasm, strongly positive, 3 points (+++). The scoring criteria for the staining area were as follows: 0 points for 0% positive cells, 1 point for 1–25%, 2 points for 26–50%, 3 points for 51–75%, and 4 points for 76–100%. Finally, the IHC score was calculated as the sum of the staining intensity and staining area scores. The IHC scoring data were imported into SPSS Software and ROC curve was further used to determine the optimal cut-off value. If the score was lower than the cut-off value, the sample was considered to have low expression; otherwise, it was considered to have high expression.

Quantitation of mRNA expression by real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (#DP424, TIANGEN). The mRNA was transcribed into cDNA using an Access RT-PCR System (#A2801, Promega). qPCR assays were performed using LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix (#ls2062, Promega). The following primers, which were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, were used: WNT6, forwards 5′-CTTCCACTGGTGCTGCGTA-3′ and reverse 5′- GCTGCGCGAAGTCAGTCTA-3′ (PCR product, 171 bp); GAPDH, forwards 5′-GCA CCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC-3′ and reverse 5′-TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGG A-3′ (138 bp). The WNT6 mRNA levels were compared to those of GAPDH (endogenous control), and relative expression values were calculated by applying the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt).

Western blotting analysis

Whole-cell protein lysates were generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol (#ABK0035C, ABKBio). Thirty micrograms of whole-cell protein lysate was loaded into each lane of an 8% polyacrylamide gel. The proteins were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Using iris scissors excised regions encompassing 30–60 kDa (Target WNT6) based on pre-stained protein ladder. Notched the top left corner of each membrane segment for consistent orientation during imaging. The membranes were incubated with a monoclonal mouse antibody specific for WNT6 (dilution 1:1000; # 24201-1-AP, Sanying Wuhan) in blocking buffer, followed by incubation with an alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000; #ta-005, Tagene). The immunoreactive bands were visualized using the CDP-Star RTU luminescence system (Tropix).

Lentivirus production and cell transduction

A lentiviral construct expressing WNT6 shRNA (WNT6-shRNA-LV, 110572-1) and a negative control construct (WMT6-shRNA-NC) were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). The target sequences of the WNT6 shRNAs were as follows: 5′-ccCTACCAGCATCTGCAGGAA-3′; negative control (NC), 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′. JEG3 cells were infected with lentivirus particles and selected in medium containing puromycin. HTR-8 and JEG3 cells in the logarithmic phase of growth were seeded into 96-well plates. After 12 h of culture, the supernatants were discarded, and the diluted virus suspension in medium was added to the wells. After overnight culture, the transfection mixture was replaced with normal complete growth medium to avoid cell toxicity. After 48 h, the transfection efficiency was monitored using fluorescence microscopy, and single cells were seeded into a 96-well plate with culture medium supplemented with puromycin (0.5 µg/ml). The culture medium was replaced every two days to remove dead cells. The transfection efficiency was observed by fluorescence microscopy and confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting analyses.

Wound healing assay

Briefly, the cells were plated and cultured overnight to approximately 80–90% confluence in a 6-well plate. A wound was created by scratching a straight line in the confluent cell layer with a pipette tip (200 µL). The scratch areas in the wells were washed with PBS until the cells in those areas were thoroughly removed, and the scratch area was imaged at 0 and 24 h. The horizontal migration rate was calculated using the following formula: (width 0 h − width 24 h)/width 0 h×100%.

Proliferation assays

A counter was used to count the cells, and the cell suspension was diluted to 1 × 106/ml based on the count results. The diluted cell suspension was inoculated into a 96-well plate at 100 µL per well and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit 8 (#ABK0011C, ABKBio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 10 µL CCK-8 was added and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and measured the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader (Thermo, USA). All the data were obtained from three independent experiments.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

A total of 6 × 104 cells were suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium and placed into the upper chamber of 8.0 μm pore Transwell plates (#3422, Corning-Costar) precoated (for invasion assays) or not precoated (for migration assays) with Matrigel (#356234, Biosciences). Then, 500 µL of basic culture medium supplemented with 20% serum was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h (for the migration assay) or 48 h (for the invasion assay) of incubation, nonmigrated cells were removed from the upper chamber with cotton swabs, and the cells attached to the lower surface of the chamber were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet; then, the number of cells in 5 random fields was counted under a microscope at 200× magnification. All of the experiments were carried out in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The data are displayed as the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. The differences in immunohistochemical scores among the groups were analysed using an independent-samples t-test The differences in basic clinical characteristics between the regressive mole group and the malignant mole group were subjected to chi-square (χ2) tests and Mann‒Whitney U tests. The K‒M survival curve and log-rank test were used for survival data analyses. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses in this study were conducted using SPSS 19.0 (IBM, USA).

Results

High WNT6 expression is associated with poor prognosis in patients with hydatidiform mole syndrome

To investigate the clinical relevance of WNT6 expression in GTD patients, we collected tissues from normal early pregnancy villi, regressive moles, malignant moles, and choriocarcinomas and used IHC to detect the WNT6 protein levels in each sample. As shown in Fig. 1.a-d, WNT6 expression was upregulated in choriocarcinoma tissues. A comparison of the WNT6 IHC scores in the normal early pregnancy villus group, regressive mole group, malignant mole group, and choriocarcinoma group is shown in Fig. 1.e. The expression of WNT6 in normal early pregnancy villus tissue was significantly lower than that in GTD samples (P < 0.001), and the expression level of WNT6 in the malignant mole group was higher than that in the regressive mole group (P < 0.001). The expression in the choriocarcinoma group was significantly higher than that in the malignant mole group (P = 0.008). Further data analysis revealed statistically significant differences in age, number of gestations, and metastasis rate between patients with regressive and malignant hydatidiform moles (Table 1). We found that the expression of WNT6 was not associated with related prognostic factors in patients with regressive hydatidiform mole (Table 2). This might be attributable to the relatively lower expression level and the small sample size of WNT6 in non- malignant hydatidiform mole samples. We explored the relationship between the WNT6 expression level and malignant progression in patients with malignant moles using complete sample data. Notably, the expression of WNT6 in the tumour metastasis group was significantly greater than that in the nonmetastasis group (P = 0.016), and the expression of WNT6 in the FIGO anatomical stage ≥ stage III group was significantly greater than that in the ≤ stage II group (P = 0.001) (Table 3). The results of univariate Cox regression analysis showed that age, expression level of WNT6(Low vs. High), Interval between previous pregnancie and pregnancy frequency were prognostic factors for GTN (Table 4). Further multivariate regression analysis revealed that WNT6 was not an independent prognostic factor (p = 0.095, HR = 1.805,95% CI: 0.902–3.612(Table 4). This result may be related to the small sample size. K‒M survival analysis was performed on 42 patients with malignant moles stratified according to the expression of WNT6. As depicted in Fig. 1. f, the time to disease progression (median progression time = 8 weeks) in the high WNT6 expression group was significantly shorter than that in the low WNT6 expression group (median progression time = 26 weeks) (P = 0.012, HR = 2.115, 95% CI: 1.145–3.907). Taken together, these results indicate that the level of the WNT6 protein is elevated in GTN tissue and that a higher WNT6 expression level is associated with a greater risk of tumour progression and metastasis.

Downregulation of WNT6 inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of choriocarcinoma cells

The WNT6 protein is predominantly located in the cytoplasm and is likely to play an important role in the development of choriocarcinoma. Previous reports have shown that the WNT6 protein is elevated in tumour cells and promotes tumour cell migration and invasion through diverse mechanisms [16–18]. Thus, in this study, we analysed the expression of WNT6 in the human chorionic trophoblast cell line HTR-8 and two choriocarcinoma cell lines, JEG3 and JAR. Compared with that in HTR-8 cells, the expression of WNT6 in the choriocarcinoma cell line was significantly increased, and the expression of the WNT6 protein in the choriocarcinoma cell line JEG-3 was significantly higher than that in JAR cells (Fig. 2a). Therefore, we selected HTR-8 and JEG-3 cells for subsequent studies. We established stable WNT6-knockdown (JEG3-shWNT6) JEG3 cells and stable WNT6-overexpressing (HTR-8-OEWNT6) HTR-8 cells to determine the role of WNT6 in proliferation, migration and invasion (Fig. 2b-c). The expression of WNT6 was significantly decreased in JEG3-shWNT6-3 cells; therefore, these cells were chosen for further functional and mechanistic research. CCK-8 assays indicated that the overexpression of WNT6 in HTR-8 cells significantly enhanced their proliferative activity, and this effect became more pronounced over time (Fig. 3a). Conversely, lentiviral knockdown of WNT6 expression in JEG-3 cells inhibited their proliferative activity, and the degree of inhibition increased with time (Fig. 3a). These results indicate that WNT6 can promote the proliferation and viability of trophoblasts. Wound healing assays showed that cells with higher WNT6 expression exhibited significantly faster wound closure than did their respective controls (Fig. 3b). In addition, Transwell assays showed that downregulation of WNT6 expression significantly diminished the migration and invasion ability of JEG3 cells, whereas upregulation of WNT6 expression significantly enhanced these abilities in HTR-8 cells (Fig. 3c). Collectively, these findings indicate that inhibiting WNT6 expression inhibits the metastasis and invasion of choriocarcinoma cells.

Discussion

Choriocarcinoma has a high tendency to invade the uterus and surrounding organs and frequently metastasizes to distant organs, especially the lungs [19]. Although the overall survival rate of patients with low-risk GTN is approximately 100%, it remains challenging to achieve complete remission for patients with high-risk metastatic choriocarcinoma. This situation results in high mortality rates, a decreased quality of life for patients, and a substantial economic burden [19]. In our preliminary research, we discovered that WNT6 might also be associated with the invasion and metastasis of choriocarcinoma [20].

WNT6, a member of the β-catenin-dependent WNT family, bind to and activates β-catenin, thereby stimulating various downstream target genes (such as MMP7 and CCND1) and promoting tumour development [21, 22]. Numerous studies have shown that WNT6 is highly expressed in various cancers, including meningeal gliomas, colon cancers, gastric cancers, and ovarian cancers. Moreover, higher WNT6 expression is associated with poorer clinical prognosis. Research has demonstrated that WNT6 is involved in the occurrence and development of ovarian cancer by activating the typical WNT/Notch1 signalling pathway [18]. There are also studies indicating that WNT6 expression is associated with increased cell viability, proliferation, invasion, migration, and resistance to chemotherapy drugs and regulates the stem cell population in meningeal gliomas and is associated with poor clinical prognosis. Evidence suggests that the p53-hsa-miR-34a axis sensitizes Glioblastoma multiforme cells to chemotherapy with temozolomide by decreasing WNT6 expression and β-catenin-dependent Wnt signaling [16, 17].

In this study, we aimed to explore the role of WNT6 in the development and migration of choriocarcinoma, which is currently unclear. Our results indicated that the expression level of WNT6 is significantly higher in gestational trophoblastic disease villi compared to normal early pregnancy villi. Moreover, its expression in malignant moles is significantly greater than that in regressive moles. Our results are consistent with the findings for WNT6 in other types of tumours. WNT6 expression is higher in tumour tissues and cells than in normal tissues and tends to further increase as the disease progresses [15–17]. Therefore, we speculate that high WNT6 expression is associated with tumour progression and poor prognosis. In this study, we also found that the expression of WNT6 was significantly correlated with tumour metastasis and FIGO anatomical stage in patients with malignant mole. In addition, the time to disease progression to GTN in the high-WNT6-expressing group was significantly shorter than that in the low-WNT6-expressing group. The results were statistically significant and the 95% CI did not contain 1, so we were 95% confident that the true hazard ratio was between 1.145 and 3.907. Thus, the higher the expression level of WNT6 is, the more likely the tumour is to progress or even metastasize. The results of univariate and COX regression analyses confirmed that high WNT6 expression was associated with disease progression, but the multivariate results were not suggestive, considering the association with the small sample size of this study. Therefore, we believe that high WNT6 expression can serve as a reference indicator for the progression and prognosis of hydatidiform mole, which will be beneficial for the early diagnosis of GTN after hydatidiform mole. We further validated the expression and role of WNT6 in choriocarcinoma through in vitro experiments, and the results were consistent with those obtained from clinicopathological samples. The expression of WNT6 in JAR and JEG-3 cells was significantly higher than that in HTR-8 cells, and the expression level in JEG-3 cells was higher than that in JAR cells. To further investigate the role of WNT6 in the biological behaviour of choriocarcinoma, we constructed stable WNT6 overexpression and knockdown models in HTR-8 and JEG-3 cells, respectively. Cell characteristics including proliferation, invasion, and migration are indicators of the malignant biological functions of tumor cells. Although WNT6 is a relatively under-researched member of the WNT family, its biological functions in gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) remain largely unknown. By detecting the proliferation, migration and invasion of cells with different WNT6 expression levels through CCK-8, wound healing and Transwell assays, our results showed that the knockdown of WNT6 inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of trophoblastic cells, while the overexpression of WNT6 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of choriocarcinoma cells. The above results are consistent with previous findings on the correlation between WNT6 and increased tumour proliferation, migration, and invasion in other tumours [15–17]. Although this study revealed the high expression of WNT6 in gestational trophoblastic disease and its correlation with its biological behaviour, further exploration of the molecular mechanism of the role of WNT6 has not been conducted. The WNT/β-catenin signalling pathway is associated with the progression of various cancers, such as gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and endometrial cancer. WNT6 has been shown to activate the WNT, SFK, and STAT pathways. It is unclear whether WNT6 acts as a key molecule in choriocarcinoma invasion by signalling through the WNT/β-catenin pathway. Based on previous research results, we speculate that WNT6 may have carcinogenic effects and may also regulate transcription; these possibilities need further exploration.
