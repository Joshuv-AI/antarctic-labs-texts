Advanced computer-aided diagnosis for age-related macular degeneration: integrating segmentation and feature extraction for precise diagnosis
Authors: Hossam Magdy Balaha, Mohamed T. Azam, Ali Mahmoud, Mohammed Ghazal, Ayman El-Baz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related Macular Degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of vision loss globally; necessitating early detection and precise diagnosis for effective intervention. This study presents a computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) system for AMD detection and diagnosis over two main stages; that are SegNet-MobileNet for segmentation and feature extraction for classification. The SegNet-MobileNet architecture merges the SegNet’s precise segmentation with the MobileNet’s efficacy; achieving high accuracy in delineating AMD lesions from retinal images. Also, the features extracted from regions of interest (ROIs) capture diverse aspects of retinal structure and texture mandatory for the AMD diagnosis. A dataset of 864 retinal images (accompanied by detailed demographic and clinical information) was collected and analyzed. Through the proper evaluation of ML algorithms, we reported that ensemble methods (such as CatBoost, Extra Trees, and XGBoost) demonstrate superior performance in distinguishing AMD cases, with accuracies exceeding 97%. Model explainability is utilized through the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) technique; providing insights into feature importance and model decision-making. This study demonstrates the potential of advanced methodologies in contributing to AMD diagnosis and laying the groundwork for future research directions in the ophthalmic imaging and diagnostic field. While promising, these results indicate the need for larger, multi-center validation to ensure generalizability.

Introduction

Eye disorders concerns in older individuals have risen recently; particularly for specific illnesses such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) which is considered a leading reason of vision problems for people over 45 years1. The AMD affects the macula which is considered an important area for clear color vision located on the retina’s outer pole; leading to symptoms like blurred vision, dark spots, and distortions2. Traditional diagnosis methods/techniques (such as slit-lamp examinations by eye specialists) face challenges due to skill variations and record-keeping limitations3. Hence, early detection and diagnosis is mandatory for AMD and other eye disorders to prevent complications.

To facilitate early detection and accurate diagnosis, fundus imaging has emerged as a mandatory tool. It is crucial to categorize fundus images into groups such as geographic atrophy (GA), intermediate AMD, normal, and wet AMD to better understand the progression of AMD and assess the effectiveness of treatments4. The GA represents an advanced dry stage with progressive loss of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells and distinct patches of atrophy, resulting in central vision loss5. Intermediate AMD lies between early and advanced stages that are marked by drusen pigment changes. The “normal” category includes images without AMD-related changes. Wet AMD (severe form) involves abnormal growth of blood vessels beneath the retina, leading to retinal degeneration and rapid central vision loss if untreated6.

Given the complexity and variability of fundus images, manual differentiation by human experts can be time-consuming and prone to different inconsistencies. To address that, machine learning (ML) techniques hold the promise in accurately discriminating between the fundus images into different AMD stages7. Various ML algorithms have been explored for AMD fundus image classification by different researcher such as Hussain et al.8. Also, Burlina et al.9 studied the accuracy of deep learning (DL) methods in identifying AMD from fundus images. They reported high accuracies ranging from 88.4% to 91.6%; along with kappa scores close to or exceeding 0.8 which are comparable to the human experts performance . In addition, Kankanahalli et al.10 developed an automated analysis system for retinal fundus images to detect and categorize AMD severity according to the Age-Related Eye Disease Study (AREDS) protocol. They concluded high sensitivities and specificities ranging from 96.0% to 98.6% across different classification scenarios. Additionally, Chea et al.11 explored a set of DL algorithms for diagnosing multiple eye diseases and they reported maximum and mean accuracies of 91.16% and 85.79% respectively.

That said, those studies demonstrated significant advances in utilizing ML/DL for AMD detection; however, there remains a need for an efficient, interpretable, and integrated systems that can capture the merits of the state-of-the-art segmentation, feature extraction, and classification techniques. To address this gap, the current study proposes a Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) approach for detecting and diagnosing AMD by integrating SegNet-MobileNet Segmentation and ML classification using hand-crafted features as its main stages. SegNet-MobileNet Segmentation combines SegNet’s precision in retinal lesion segmentation with MobileNet’s computational efficiency. In breif, hand-crafted features extraction captures essential retinal structure and texture aspects while feature selection refines the feature space by eliminating redundancy. Features-based classification and optimization evaluate a range of ML algorithms, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) technique ensures model explainability by visualizing feature importance. The hypothesis behind the current study revolves around the effectiveness of the suggested CAD approach in detecting and diagnosing AMD. It posits that by the integration of the different stages, the CAD system can precisely recognize the AMD lesions in retinal images; leading to improved diagnostic accuracy and early intervention.

The contributions of the current study can be summarized as follows: (1) it introduces a CAD system tailored specifically for the AMD detection; utilizing a combination of state-of-the-art techniques and methodologies; (2) the study proposes a modified architecture (which is the SegNet-MobileNet) that enhances the AMD detection segmentation performance by combining the SegNet with the MobileNet; (3) it conducts feature extraction, selection, and classification, evaluating ML algorithms for AMD diagnosis optimization; and finally (4) the study ensures model explainability via SHAP analysis.

The remainder of the study is organized as follows: Section “Materials and methods” provides a detailed description of the materials and methods used; including (a) the dataset description, (b) pre-processing steps, (c) the proposed SegNet-MobileNet segmentation approach, (d) feature extraction and selection techniques, (e) classification algorithms, (f) hyperparameter optimization using tree-structured parzen estimator (TPE), and (g) model explainability via SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Section “Experiments and discussion” reports the experimental setup, results, and discussion and it highlights the performance of the utilized ML classifiers and the interpretability of the model through SHAP visualizations. Section “Study limitations” presents the limitations of the current study. Finally, Section “Conclusions and future directions” summarizes the study key findings, contributions, and implications; while suggesting potential directions for future research.

Materials and methods

The current study proposes a novel CAD system (Fig. 1) for AMD detection and diagnosis. The framework comprises four stages: (1) SegNet-MobileNet segmentation combining SegNet’s decoder with MobileNet’s encoder, (2) feature extraction from preprocessed ROIs including GLCM and other texture descriptors, (3) feature selection to refine the feature space by identifying collinear features, and (4) classification with TPE-based optimization. Eleven ML algorithms were evaluated (CatBoost, Extra Trees, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, Decision Trees, AdaBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors, SVM, Logistic Regression, and MLP). Hyperparameter tuning employed the Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator (TPE), and model explainability was ensured through SHAP analysis.Figure 1The suggested computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) framework in the current study for AMD diagnosis and interpretation.

Materials

In this study, ethical guidelines were strictly adhered to, obtaining informed consent from all participants before data collection. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient selection was comprehensive, encompassing various stages and types of AMD, with a dataset of 864 retinal images, each accompanied by detailed demographic and clinical information. Approval for study protocols was obtained from relevant institutions, including the University of Louisville and Mansoura University. Retinal images were gathered using fundus color imaging; ensuring high-quality, high-resolution images of both eyes of each patient. The dataset was split into training, validation, and testing sets at the patient level to ensure independence and prevent data leakage. SegNet-MobileNet segmentation

Semantic segmentation plays a vital role in medical image analysis, especially in detecting and diagnosing AMD12. Semantic segmentation techniques enable automatic identification of AMD lesions from retinal images, aiding in early diagnosis and disease progression monitoring13. However, achieving high segmentation accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency poses a significant challenge in this field. The paper proposes a modified architecture, SegNet-MobileNet, which combines SegNet’s decoder architecture with MobileNet’s lightweight encoder to enhance segmentation performance in AMD detection14.

Data pre-processing for classification

The data pre-processing stage aims to enhance the quality and consistency of the classification. It involves several steps, including computing the average CDF for different classes, applying the CLAHE algorithm for contrast enhancement and noise reduction, standardizing intensity distribution by interpolating individual image CDFs with the mean CDF, extracting relevant ROIs using masks, and refining ROIs through contour analysis to define object boundaries and compute object properties.

Average CDF Calculation for Each Class: This step calculates the average CDF for each class in the dataset. The mathematical expression is: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{CDF_{C}}(x) = \frac{1}{N_C} \times \sum _{i=0}^{N_C}{{CDF_{C_{i}}(x)}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{CDF_{C}}(x)$$\end{document} represents the average CDF for class C at pixel intensity x, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_C$$\end{document} is the total number of images in class C, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{CDF_{C_{i}}(x)}}$$\end{document} denotes the CDF of the i-th image in class C.

Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) applies histogram equalization locally to small regions of an image, addressing noise amplification concerns. Features extraction and selection

In the diagnosis of AMD, the process of extracting the relevant features from regions of interest (ROIs) is considered a mandatory stage to ensure precise assessment and classification. As presented and detailed in Table 1, multiple features categories are methodically extracted to characterize the textural, statistical, and structural markers attached to the AMD comprehensively.

These features include First Order Statistics (FOS) for intensity distribution analysis22, Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) for texture characterization23, Statistical Feature Matrix (SFM) for capturing spatial dependencies24, Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM) for quantifying texture uniformity25, Local Binary Patterns (LBP) for edge and pattern detection26, Shape Parameters for geometric property evaluation27, Law’s Texture Energy Measures (LTE) for identifying fine textural variations28, Threshold Adjacency Statistics (TAS) for quantifying spatial relationships between thresholded pixel regions29, and Hu Moments for rotation-invariant shape descriptors30. This brings the total feature count to 238 descriptors across all nine categories (Table 2).

Feature Extraction Scope: Features are extracted from two complementary sources: (1) the full retinal fundus image, capturing global texture and structural patterns including the macular region where AMD pathology primarily manifests; and (2) the segmented optic disc region, providing localized vascular and textural biomarkers. These feature sets are concatenated into a unified 238-dimensional vector, ensuring comprehensive representation of both macular pathology and vascular health indicators. This dual-source approach addresses potential anatomical biases while utilizing complementary diagnostic information from multiple retinal regions.

Each feature category provides unique insights into specific pathological changes in retinal morphology: FOS and GLCM capture drusen texture irregularity and retinal pigment epithelium disruption; SFM and GLRLM quantify retinal layer periodicity and structural homogeneity; LBP and LTE detect microtextural alterations associated with early AMD; Shape Parameters and Hu Moments characterize geometric changes in lesion boundaries; and TAS quantifies spatial distribution patterns of hyper- and hypo-reflective regions indicative of geographic atrophy progression. This multi-faceted approach ensures that both macroscopic structural changes and subtle microtextural alterations are effectively captured, improving the diagnostic precision of the ML models.

Feature Normalization: Prior to feature selection, all extracted features underwent normalization to ensure numerical stability and prevent scale-dominated bias. The TPE optimization framework evaluated multiple scaling techniques (Standard, MinMax, Robust, Quantile Transformer, etc.) as tunable pipeline components, with the optimal scaler selected per feature category based on cross-validation performance (Table 4).

Feature Selection: Moreover, feature selection is a critical stage in refining the diagnostic process for AMD, with the aim of enhancing the accuracy and interpretability of ML models. To address redundancy and multicollinearity, a comprehensive suite of feature selection techniques was explored and optimized via the TPE framework, as detailed in Table 3. The optimization process evaluated 250 trials per feature category, tuning both the selection technique and feature retention ratio (10–100%). The optimal pipeline configurations for each feature category (Table 4) utilized specific techniques including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), Chi-Squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}), Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and Mutual Information (MI), alongside initial collinearity checks.

Features-based classification

The process of feature-based classification and optimization is mathematically formulated as follows: Let X represent the refined feature set, Y denote the target variable (AMD diagnosis), and f(X) signify the classification function. The objective is to identify the optimal function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^{*}(X)$$\end{document} that maximizes diagnostic performance. This is formulated as an optimization problem: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^{*} = \underset{f}{\text {arg max}} \, P(f(X))$$\end{document} where P represents a performance metric (e.g., accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve). A suite of machine learning algorithms, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{A_1, A_2, \dots , A_n\}$$\end{document}, is employed to learn the mapping from the feature space to the target variable. Each algorithm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} is associated with a comprehensive configuration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _i$$\end{document}, encompassing both model-specific hyperparameters and pipeline components (e.g., scaling, feature selection). Thus, the optimization problem is expressed as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta ^{*} = \underset{i, \theta _i}{\text {arg max}} \, P(A_i(X; \theta _i))$$\end{document}.

The performance of each configuration is assessed using a stratified train-test split (80/20) to ensure robustness and generalizability while maintaining class distribution. The configuration yielding the highest performance score is selected as the optimal model alongside its associated pipeline parameters for AMD diagnosis. To further enhance performance, hyperparameter optimization is conducted using the TPE algorithm. TPE was selected for its efficiency in exploring high-dimensional parameter spaces and its ability to identify near-optimal configurations within the defined search space. The detailed search space and optimization parameters are provided in Sect. 2.6.

Moreover, the TPE optimization framework simultaneously tunes pre-processing components. Outlier detection techniques (including IQR, Z-Score, Isolation Forest, LOF, Elliptic Envelope, One-Class SVM, DBSCAN, and Mahalanobis distance) were evaluated to identify and optionally remove anomalous samples from the feature space. Class balancing techniques were considered but fixed to “None” for this experimental configuration, as preliminary analysis indicated minimal class imbalance in the dataset. The search space for these components is detailed in Table 3.

Class imbalance handling methods were evaluated during pipeline optimization but were fixed to “None” for the final experimental configuration. Preliminary analysis indicated a balanced class distribution (AMD vs. non-AMD) in the dataset, and initial trials showed no performance gain from resampling techniques such as SMOTE or random oversampling.

Tree-structured parzen estimator (TPE) optimization

The TPE is a state-of-the-art optimization algorithm commonly used in Bayesian optimization methodologies32. It provides a systematic mechanism for efficiently exploring high-dimensional parameter spaces, making it invaluable for hyperparameter tuning in ML models33. It operates by constructing probabilistic models of the objective function f(x), which in this study represents the classification performance metric, to guide the search for optimal hyperparameters. Compared to grid search or random search, TPE utilizes prior evaluations to iteratively refine its search strategy, ensuring faster convergence toward optimal solutions.

These are modeled using kernel density estimation (KDE) with Parzen windows. Model explainability via SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

Model explainability using SHAP is a powerful function for interpreting ML models34,35. It utilizes concepts from the cooperative game theory to fairly distribute the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction36. The SHAP value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _i$$\end{document} for a specific feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} quantifies its marginal contribution to the model’s output for a specific instance. This ensures that the model’s predictions are both interpretable and transparent; which is particularly critical in medical applications such as diagnosing AMD.

The SHAP value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _i$$\end{document} is calculated using Eq. Beyond statistical significance, effect sizes computed via Cohen’s d ranged from 0.82 to 1.47 across metric comparisons, indicating large practical significance that extends beyond mere statistical detection. Study discussion

Integrating multiple feature categories into a unified dataset offers several critical advantages for machine learning-based AMD diagnosis. The combination of texture, shape, and statistical descriptors enables the model to capture complementary information from different domains, thereby enhancing its ability to detect complex pathological patterns that may be missed when using individual feature types in isolation.

One significant merit of feature integration is the ability to exploit the strengths of different feature types while mitigating their individual limitations. Texture-based features (such as GLCM and GLRLM) effectively capture spatial relationships and patterns in retinal images, but when used alone, they may overlook structural information provided by shape parameters or intensity distributions from First Order Statistics. By aggregating all nine feature categories (238 total features), the framework accesses a richer data representation, enabling more informed predictions. This is evidenced in Table 4, where ensemble methods trained on the combined feature set achieved accuracies exceeding 97% with mean performance above 95%, outperforming individual feature categories.

Another merit of combining features is improved generalization across diverse imaging conditions. Medical datasets often exhibit variability due to factors such as image quality, patient demographics, and disease progression stages. By incorporating a wide range of features, the framework adapts better to this variability, reducing overfitting risks. For instance, TAS and LBP features provide unique perspectives on texture and structure that help the model generalize across different fundus camera types and imaging protocols. This robustness is particularly important for real-world clinical deployment where consistency and reliability are paramount.

The statistical analysis in Table 5 further validates the benefits of feature integration. When evaluated individually, certain feature types (such as TAS and GLRLM) demonstrated strong performance with ensemble classifiers, achieving mean accuracy scores above 93%. However, simpler models (like Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting) struggled to maintain consistent performance across individual feature sets. By aggregating the entire feature space, the model harnesses collective predictive power, leading to more balanced outcomes with narrow standard deviations (accuracy: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97.57\% \pm 0.20\%$$\end{document}, MCC: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$93.55\% \pm 0.54\%$$\end{document}). Paired t-test results confirm that ensemble methods trained on combined features significantly outperformed those trained on individual feature sets (p-values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.01$$\end{document}, Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d = 0.82$$\end{document}-1.47).

From an interpretability perspective, feature integration facilitates deeper understanding of relative feature importance across disease stages. SHAP visualizations (Figs. 8, 9, 10, and 11) reveal how different feature types contribute to predictions. The beeswarm plot (Fig. 8) shows that TAS descriptors (TAS43, TAS15) and GLCM correlation metrics exhibit the highest mean absolute SHAP values, indicating their dominant role in AMD detection. The comparative bar plot (Fig. 9) demonstrates stage-specific feature weighting: texture descriptors dominate in early/intermediate stages while statistical measures (FOS entropy, skewness) gain importance in advanced GA and wet AMD cases. This pattern aligns with known pathological progression from microtextural alterations to macroscopic structural degeneration, validating the model’s clinical plausibility.

The error analysis waterfall plot (Fig. 11) identifies specific failure modes, revealing that misclassified cases often involve elevated FOS entropy and coefficient of variation values that mimic pathological patterns. This insight guides future improvements through targeted data augmentation or enhanced artifact detection pre-processing.

Medical relevance of the current study

The current study holds significant medical applicability as it introduces a CAD system specifically designed for AMD detection and diagnosis. AMD is a primary cause of vision loss among elderly populations, and early detection is critical for timely intervention and treatment. By utilizing integrated ML/DL architectures (SegNet-MobileNet segmentation combined with ensemble classification), this study proposes a robust framework that differentiates AMD-related patterns in retinal fundus images with high accuracy.

One key contribution is the assimilation of feature extraction, selection, and classification algorithms optimized for AMD diagnosis. Hand-crafted features (texture, shape, and statistical summaries) capture critical aspects of retinal structures, while TPE-based feature selection refines the feature space by eliminating redundancy. This multi-faceted approach ensures the model utilizes the most informative features, improving its ability to distinguish between AMD and non-AMD cases. The SHAP explainability component enhances model interpretability, allowing clinicians to validate predictions against well-established medical knowledge. For example, clinically significant markers (retinal thickness proxies, drusen area indicators) emerged as highly influential in the model’s decision-making process, aligning with ophthalmological expertise.

The high performance of the SegNet-MobileNet model (Dice: 94.70%, IoU: 90.46%) and ensemble classifiers (accuracy: 97.57%) suggests strong potential for integration into clinical workflows. Automated systems utilizing these models could assist ophthalmologists by: (1) reducing manual analysis burden, enabling focus on patient care; (2) providing consistent, reproducible assessments unaffected by inter-observer variability; and (3) offering visual explanations (SHAP plots) that support diagnostic confidence. However, clinical deployment requires further validation through multi-center studies with larger, more diverse populations to ensure generalizability across different imaging devices and patient demographics.

This study paves the way for extending binary classification systems to multi-class scenarios (distinguishing GA, intermediate AMD, and wet AMD). Such granularity would facilitate earlier and more precise interventions, conclusively improving patient outcomes. By addressing traditional diagnostic method limitations, this work contributes to the development of reliable, automated tools for AMD detection. The proposed approach has the potential to support ophthalmic imaging and diagnostics, contributing to healthcare accessibility for aging populations worldwide. Future efforts should focus on refining these models, addressing identified limitations, and validating performance in real-world clinical settings through prospective trials.

Ethical considerations and privacy

In this study, ethical guidelines were strictly adhered to in order to guarantee the protection of participants’ rights and privacy. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, and study protocols were approved by relevant institutional review boards, including those at the University of Louisville and Mansoura University. Retinal images were anonymized to remove any personally identifiable information; thereby safeguarding patient confidentiality. The anonymization process involved stripping metadata such as patient names, identification numbers, and other sensitive details from the image files. This ensures compliance with data protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)37 and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)38.

The study emphasizes transparency in data use. All participants were informed about the research purpose, methods employed, and potential applications of the findings. This transparency fosters trust between researchers and participants, which is critical in medical studies involving sensitive health data. Access to the original dataset was restricted to authorized personnel involved in the study and IRB oversight. Data storage and processing were conducted on secure servers with encryption protocols to prevent unauthorized access. These considerations ensure that the study adheres to the highest ethical standards.

Study limitations

The current study reports promising results in detecting and diagnosing AMD; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset consists of 864 retinal images, which may restrict the framework’s generalizability to broader populations. While patient-level splitting prevented data leakage, multi-center validation with larger, more diverse cohorts is necessary to ensure robustness across different imaging devices, acquisition protocols, and demographic groups. Second, the dependence on hand-crafted extracted features may introduce biases depending on the feature extraction process and ROI segmentation accuracy. Although SHAP analysis validated that influential features align with clinical knowledge (e.g., texture irregularity, statistical moments), automated feature learning through deep architectures could potentially capture additional discriminative patterns. Future work should explore hybrid approaches combining hand-crafted and deep-learned features.

Third, while the SegNet-MobileNet architecture achieved high segmentation performance (Dice: 94.70%), the computational requirements for training deep learning models might pose challenges in resource-constrained clinical settings. Optimization for edge deployment or cloud-based inference may be necessary for practical integration. Fourth, the error analysis (Fig. 11) revealed specific failure modes where elevated FOS entropy and coefficient of variation values led to misclassification of normal cases as wet AMD. This suggests that imaging artifacts or borderline textural features can confound the model, indicating a need for enhanced artifact detection pre-processing or additional training on ambiguous cases. Finally, we can explore hybrid interpretability approaches combining feature-level SHAP analysis with pixel-level attention methods (e.g., Grad-CAM) as demonstrated in recent studies39, enabling both biomarker-level and spatial validation of model decisions.

Conclusions and future directions

This study presents a comprehensive CAD framework for AMD detection, integrating SegNet-MobileNet segmentation with extensive hand-crafted feature extraction and ensemble machine learning classification. By optimizing a pipeline of 238 features across nine categories using the Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator, the proposed system achieved robust diagnostic performance with an accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97.57\% \pm 0.20\%$$\end{document} and AUC values exceeding 0.995 across multiple independent trials. Beyond predictive accuracy, the integration of SHAP explainability validated the clinical plausibility of the model, demonstrating that influential features such as texture irregularity and structural periodicity align with known pathological markers of AMD progression. These findings underscore the efficacy of combining precise segmentation with interpretable machine learning to support early AMD detection while maintaining transparency for clinical validation.

Despite these promising results, future research will focus on addressing current limitations to enhance generalizability and clinical utility. Priority will be given to multi-center validation using larger, more diverse datasets to ensure robustness across different imaging devices and demographic groups. Subsequent work will also explore hybrid feature architectures that combine hand-crafted descriptors with deep-learned representations. Ultimately, prospective clinical trials and optimization for resource-constrained environments will be pursued to facilitate the integration of this interpretable CAD system into real-world ophthalmic workflows; aiming to improve accessibility and early intervention outcomes for aging populations.
