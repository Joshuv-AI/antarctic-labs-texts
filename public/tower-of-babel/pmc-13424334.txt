Research and experiments on the performance optimization of hydraulic impact hammers
Authors: Hu Chen, Boqiang Shi, Hui Guo, Zirui Liu, Bingbing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to enhance the impact performance and stability of hydraulic impact hammers, this paper first conducts a Design of Experiments (DOE) study on hydraulic impact hammers, identifying ten key parameters that influence their impact performance and stability. Consequently, multi-objective optimization is employed to enhance the impact performance and stability of hydraulic impact hammers. In comparison with the baseline configuration, the optimized configuration attains a 4.6% increase in impact power and an 11.3% reduction in working pressure. Furthermore, a performance analysis surrogate model for the hydraulic impact hammer was developed to enhance the computational efficiency of multi-objective optimization. Research indicates that the errors in impact power and working pressure between the optimized configuration of the surrogate model trained using Elliptic Basis Functions (EBF) and that of the traditional model are minimal, at 0.50% and 0.30%, respectively. In comparison with the baseline configuration, the EBF-trained surrogate model optimization configuration increases impact power by 4.1% and reduces working pressure by 11.0%. Finally, experimental validation confirms the accuracy of both the traditional model and surrogate model optimization configurations. In summary, this study provides a reliable theoretical foundation and experimental support for the multi-objective optimization of hydraulic impact hammers, offering a clear paradigm for their multi-objective optimization design.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The hydraulic impact hammer is composed of a piston, reversing valve, an accumulator, cylinders, and a drill rod. It is a highly versatile tool that is employed in a variety of industrial settings, including mining and infrastructure construction. The piston reciprocates under the control of the reversing valve, thereby converting the fluid’s pressure energy into the piston’s impact energy. This, in turn, drives the piston to strike the drill rod and subsequently break the rock. Impact performance and stability are critical metrics for assessing the efficacy of hydraulic impact hammers. These two characteristics exhibit contrasting characteristics. Hydraulic impact hammers are characterized by intricate designs with pronounced nonlinear characteristics. This characteristic complicates the development of performance optimization solutions and renders the validation process arduous. Consequently, research focused on the optimization of their performance is of critical importance.

A number of scholars have developed mathematical models of hydraulic impact systems for the purpose of studying their performance. Oh et al.1 established a mathematical model of a hydraulic impact system as a tool for analyzing impact performance. Ye et al.2 developed a mathematical model of a hydraulic impact hammer and investigated methods of increasing both impact frequency and impact power. Yang et al.3 constructed a nonlinear mathematical model of a hydraulic impact hammer and identified the optimal values for the critical structural parameters of the piston and the reversing valve. Li et al.4 constructed a simulation model of a hydraulic impact hammer and examined the impact of system oil pressure and nitrogen chamber pre-charge pressure on performance. Hu et al.5 developed a mathematical model of a hydraulic impact system based on quasi-steady theory and finite difference theory. Guo et al.6 developed a nonlinear mathematical model of a hydraulic impact system and investigated the influences of inlet oil flow, pre-charge pressure for an accumulator with high pressure, and system setting pressure on impact system performance. Franco et al.7 developed a simplified calculation model of a hydraulic impact hammer to support product design. Redelin et al.8 constructed a mathematical model for the hydraulic impact hammer and studied the effect of accumulator initial filling pressure on performance. Galdin et al.9 developed a mathematical model of a hydropneumatic impact device and studied the relationship between impact energy and pre-charge pressure of the accumulator. Giuffrida et al.10 developed a nonlinear mathematical model of the hydraulic impact system and completed experimental validation, thereby establishing the theoretical foundation for simulation optimization. Yang et al.11 investigated the effects of system flow rate, accumulator pressure, and relief valve set pressure on impact energy and efficiency, providing a reference for multi-parameter coupled optimization.

Furthermore, a number of researchers have directed their efforts toward the optimization of hydraulic impact systems. Shin et al.12 enhanced both impact frequency and energy through parameter optimization. Xu et al.13 investigated key parameters for increasing impact energy in hydraulic systems and optimized piston diameter. Kim et al.14 employed a stepwise quadratic response surface method to optimize the design parameter set and proposed design modifications to enhance the impact performance of hydraulic impact systems. Noh et al.15 addressed the trade-off between impact energy and frequency by proposing a multi-objective optimization strategy based on sensitivity analysis. The construction of a weighted objective function was the key to achieving simultaneous improvement in both parameters. Rechkin16 applied CFD modeling to the design and research of pneumatic and hydraulic impact devices, analyzing complex flow field characteristics during impactor operation through numerical simulation to provide theoretical foundations for structural optimization. Baek et al.17 introduced Taguchi methods for orthogonal experimental design of hydraulic impact systems, determining optimal combinations of design variables by analyzing signal-to-noise ratios. This approach effectively resolves matching issues in multi-parameter optimization.

In recent years, there has been a progressive application of several advanced algorithms to the optimization calculations of hydraulic impact systems. Hojjati et al.18 proposed a hybrid evolutionary algorithm based on genetic expression programming and particle swarm optimization to analyze data, providing an accurate performance prediction model for hydraulic impact systems. Cao et al.19 proposed a multi-objective optimization method for air hammer structure design based on the Microhabitat Quantum Genetic Algorithm (MNQGA), enabling simultaneous or separate optimization to maximize impact energy and minimize air consumption. Frid et al.20 conducted a multi-domain joint optimization study for hydraulic impact systems, comparing composite shape methods, particle swarm optimization, and differential evolution. The findings of the study demonstrated that differential evolution exhibited optimal convergence efficiency in the resolution of high-dimensional nonlinear optimization problems. Furthermore, research on optimizing complex problems through surrogate modeling is becoming increasingly prevalent, enhancing computational efficiency in hydraulic impact system performance optimization. Yang et al.21 developed a flow area parameter model based on CFD data to replace time-consuming numerical simulations. When employed in conjunction with the NSGA-II algorithm, this approach led to the optimization of spool valve structures across multiple objectives, thereby markedly enhancing computational efficiency.

In summary, previous studies have developed mathematical models for hydraulic impact hammers and examined the parameters that influence their performance. Nevertheless, several issues persist. Firstly, extant research has not examined the contribution of structural and working parameters to performance responses. The prevailing focus has been on the impact of single-parameter changes, with a paucity of multi-parameter synergistic analyses. Furthermore, studies on multi-objective optimization for hydraulic impact hammer performance have been relatively scarce, and there has been no investigation into enhancing the computational efficiency of multi-objective optimization solutions. Presently, there is a paucity of systematic multi-parameter collaborative quantitative analysis frameworks and efficient optimization strategies for typical high-dimensional nonlinear systems, such as hydraulic impact hammers. Consequently, the efficacy of global optimization within strongly coupled design spaces remains limited, and the identification of engineering-feasible solutions is challenging to precise.

The objective of this study is to enhance the performance of hydraulic impact hammers. This study integrates optimized Latin hypercube sampling (OLHS), the EBF surrogate model, and entropy-weight-based TOPSIS decision-making into an integrated “sampling–modeling–optimization–decision” framework to address the optimization problem of strong multi-parameter coupling and conflicting objectives in hydraulic impact hammers. Through a meticulous examination of the contributions of 18 distinct parameters to impact performance and stability, this research enhances both domains through the implementation of multi-objective optimization. Furthermore, the efficiency of addressing multi-objective optimization problems is enhanced by the establishment of a surrogate model, and the precision of the optimized configuration is validated through experimental verification. This study verifies the superiority of the EBF surrogate model in the multi-objective optimization of hydraulic impact hammers and provides a reusable technical paradigm for the intelligent design of similar complex hydraulic systems. In summary, this work provides a reliable theoretical foundation and experimental support for optimizing the performance of hydraulic impact hammers. Numerical model

The schematic cross-section of a hydraulic impact hammer is presented in Fig. 2. The movement of the piston is facilitated by the combined forces of hydraulic oil and nitrogen gas, while the motion of the reversing valve is driven by hydraulic oil. The front chamber is invariably connected to the inlet pipe and maintains elevated pressure. The back chamber is sometimes connected to the inlet pipe for high pressure and sometimes connected to the outlet pipe for low pressure. The pressure within the nitrogen chamber is subject to variation in accordance with the piston’s position. The accumulator is invariably connected to the inlet pipe and functions as a high-pressure accumulator.

Schematic cross-section of a hydraulic impact hammer.

When the front chamber is subjected to high pressure and the back chamber is subjected to low pressure, the resultant force on the piston directs the piston upward, causing the return stroke, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). Upon attaining a specific position during the return stroke, an increase in pressure within the signal chamber occurs, thereby inducing a positional change in the reversing valve. This establishes a connection between the back chamber and high pressure, thereby directing the resultant force in a downward direction and initiating the impact stroke, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b). Upon attaining a particular position during the stroke, the piston triggers a decrease in pressure within the signal chamber, thereby initiating the reset of the reversing valve. Consequently, the back chamber is subjected to low pressure, prompting the piston to execute the return stroke once more, thus perpetuating the cycle.

Research on design of experiments

Working pressure () and impact power () are the most critical indicators reflecting the stability and impact performance of a hydraulic impact hammer. Working pressure refers to the maximum pressure in the front chamber during the piston return stroke. Lower working pressure indicates higher stability. Greater impact power signifies better impact performance. To analyze the influence of typical structural parameters and operating parameters on working pressure and impact power, a Design of Experiments (DOE) study is conducted based on an established numerical calculation model for hydraulic impact hammers. Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) ensures uniform distribution in the one-dimensional projection of each parameter dimension through a stratified random sampling strategy. However, conventional LHS may suffer from sample clustering. Optimized Latin hypercube sampling (OLHS) improves the space-filling performance based on the Maximin criterion, and the optimization process is implemented using the simulated annealing algorithm. To more accurately capture the overall characteristics of the hydraulic impact hammer, an OLHS method is employed, with the number of experiments set to 20 times the number of study factors. Taking a specific model of hydraulic impact hammer as an example, the range of values for the study factors is determined based on engineering practice, as presented in Table 1.

Multi-objective optimization of surrogate models

Establishing surrogate models

The complexity of the numerical calculation model for hydraulic impact hammers, in conjunction with both the significant computational expense and the inefficient solution efficiency of multi-objective optimization, necessitates the establishment of a surrogate model of hydraulic impact hammer performance analysis of multi-objective optimization. The hydraulic impact hammer performance analysis surrogate model is capable of extracting the mapping relationship between input and output parameters from data. This facilitates the acquisition of simulation results with a high degree of accuracy at a low computational cost. This approach offers an efficient and feasible solution for multi-objective optimization calculations of hydraulic impact hammers.

First, a global exploration of the 10 design variables for multi-objective optimization is conducted through DOE research, employing the OLHS method with a sample size 100 times the number of design variables. It is noteworthy that surrogate models typically exhibit maximum prediction errors at the boundaries, and the incorporation of corner points can significantly mitigate boundary errors. Consequently, the global exploration must incorporate the corner points of the design variables, which totals 2n points, where n denotes the number of design variables. The full-factor sampling method is employed to sample 1,024 corner points across the 10 study factors. The results from full-factor sampling and OLHS are combined, yielding a total of 2,024 sampling points. A surrogate model is established based on these sampling points, and then multi-objective optimization is performed using the NSGA-II algorithm.

There are numerous methods for establishing surrogate models, with response surface modeling (RSM), radial basis functions (RBF), elliptic basis functions (EBF), and Kriging being common approaches. It is important to note that RSM is only suitable for simple quadratic nonlinear models. RBF demonstrates particular aptitude in the management of high-dimensional nonlinear models and the interpolation of sparse data. EBF exhibits remarkable proficiency in accommodating intricate surfaces. Kriging is well-suited for deterministic analysis and has proven to be highly effective in capturing local nonlinearities.

In light of the aforementioned global exploration sampling points, performance analysis surrogate models for the hydraulic impact hammer are trained using RSM, RBF, EBF, and Kriging, respectively. Pareto frontiers of traditional and surrogate models.

The proximity between the Pareto frontier of the surrogate model and that of the traditional model is evaluated by means of Generation Distance, Inverse Generation Distance, and Hypervolume Ratio. Generation Distance is defined as the extent to which points on the surrogate model’s Pareto frontier are displaced from those of the traditional model’s Pareto frontier. This is calculated as the mean Euclidean distance between each point on the surrogate model’s Pareto frontier and the nearest point on the traditional model’s Pareto frontier. Inverse generation distance is a metric that quantifies the distance between points on the traditional model’s Pareto frontier and the closest points on the surrogate model’s Pareto frontier. This is calculated as the mean Euclidean distance between each point on the traditional model’s Pareto frontier and the nearest point on the surrogate model’s Pareto frontier. Hypervolume ratio is defined as the ratio of the area enclosed by the Pareto frontier of the surrogate model and the reference point, to the area enclosed by the Pareto frontier of the traditional model and the reference point. A hypervolume ratio greater than 100% is indicative of an optimistic prediction, while a ratio less than 100% is indicative of a negative prediction. Experimental validation

In order to ascertain the actual impact performance optimization of hydraulic impact hammers and to verify the accuracy of both the optimized configuration of the traditional model and the optimized configuration of the surrogate model, this study conducted working pressure experiments and impact power experiments on hydraulic impact hammers. The experimental system primarily consisted of an excavator, a hydraulic impact hammer, pressure sensors, a high-speed camera, and a data acquisition instrument, as presented in Fig. 7. The experiment utilized hydraulic oil No. 46, with an oil temperature set at 313.15 K and a system overflow pressure set at 340 bar. The pressure sensors utilized in this study are the HT-PD PR140 model manufactured by HYDROTECHNIK. The high-speed camera utilized in this study is the VEO 410 model manufactured by PHANTOM. A pressure sensor with a range of 600 bar was installed on the high-pressure port of the reversing valve. The data acquisition instrument was set to a sampling frequency of 10,000 Hz to measure the working pressure. The piston and cylinder surfaces were marked with tracking points. The high-speed camera lens was adjusted to face the piston horizontally. The camera’s sampling frequency was set to 10,000 fps to measure piston displacement, enabling calculation of impact power. A series of experiments were conducted to assess the baseline configuration, the optimized configuration of the traditional model, and the optimized configuration of the surrogate model.

Conclusion

In this study, a DOE research was conducted on hydraulic impact hammers, with the objective of analyzing the effects of 18 structural and operating parameters on working pressure and impact power. The study indicates that the piston middle diameter demonstrates a substantial negative correlation with working pressure, with an observed value of 35.34%. The nitrogen chamber pre-charge pressure exhibits the most substantial positive correlation with working pressure, with an observed percentage of 26.61%. The lower inner diameter of the reversing valve demonstrates a substantial positive correlation with impact power, with a correlation coefficient of 14.26%. The upper inner diameter of the reversing valve exhibited a substantial negative correlation with impact power, with a percentage of 12.38%. The present study identified ten key parameters influencing the impact performance and stability of hydraulic impact hammers: the piston middle diameter, the nitrogen chamber precharge pressure, the piston back diameter, the accumulator precharge pressure, the piston stroke, the low-pressure oil groove width, the lower inner diameter of the reversing valve, the upper inner diameter of the reversing valve, the piston middle-lower section length, and the reversing valve stroke.

This paper enhances the impact performance and stability of a hydraulic impact hammer through multi-objective optimization. Ten key parameters are selected as design variables, with constraints imposed on the maximum values of working pressure, back chamber peak pressure, and outlet pipe peak pressure. Multi-objective optimization is conducted with the goals of maximizing impact power and minimizing working pressure, yielding the Pareto frontier for and . Furthermore, the optimal configuration is determined using TOPSIS optimized with the entropy weighting approach. Compared to the baseline configuration, the optimized configuration achieves a 4.6% increase in impact power and an 11.3% reduction in working pressure, thereby enhancing the impact performance and stability of the hydraulic impact hammer.

Furthermore, this paper establishes surrogate models for analyzing the performance of hydraulic impact hammers, enhancing the computational efficiency of multi-objective optimization. Multi-objective optimization is achieved through the utilization of surrogate models that are trained using four distinct methods. RSM, RBF, EBF, and Kriging. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that the EBF-trained surrogate model for hydraulic impact hammer performance analysis exhibits a high degree of accuracy in the multi-objective optimization of impact power and working pressure. This model also exhibits a notable increase in computational speed compared to traditional models. This approach offers an efficient and viable solution for multi-objective optimization calculations of hydraulic impact hammers. The errors in impact power and working pressure between the optimized configuration of the EBF-trained surrogate model and that of the traditional model are at most minimal, at 0.50% and 0.30%, respectively. The EBF-trained surrogate model optimization configuration, in comparison with the baseline configuration, increases impact power by 4.1% and reduces working pressure by 11.0%. Consequently, the impact performance and stability of the hydraulic impact hammer are enhanced.

This paper verifies the accuracy of the optimized configurations for both the traditional model and the surrogate model through working pressure and impact power experiments on a hydraulic impact hammer. The experimental findings indicate that, in comparison with the baseline configuration, the working pressure of the optimized configuration of the traditional model decreased by 10.78%, while the impact power increased by 4.21%. Similarly, the work pressure of the optimized configuration for the EBF-trained surrogate model decreased by 10.64%, while the impact power increased by 4.37%. The simulation and experimental errors for both working pressure and impact power in the baseline configuration, the optimized configuration of the traditional model, and the optimized configuration of the surrogate model are all below 5%. In summary, this study provides a reliable theoretical foundation and experimental support for multi-objective optimization research on hydraulic impact hammers, offering an accurate and efficient computational tool for their multi-objective optimization design.
