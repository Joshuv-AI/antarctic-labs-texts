Virtual Reality Interventions for Pediatric Pain and Anxiety Management During Emergency Department Procedures: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Tanesha A. Dimanopoulos, Maleea D. Holbert, Brandon Meikle, Robert S. Ware, Diane Crellin, Henry Xiang, Simon Craig, Karin Plummer, Ashleigh Barr, Bronwyn R. Griffin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesVirtual reality (VR) is a promising nonpharmacological intervention for managing procedural pain and anxiety in children. Its effectiveness in emergency department (ED) settings, where painful procedures occur under time pressure, remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated VR’s impact on reducing pain and anxiety during pediatric ED procedures.MethodsA systematic search of Embase, PubMed, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library databases from inception to July 14, 2025, identified eligible randomized and nonrandomized studies. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions. Pain and anxiety outcomes were pooled using a random-effects model, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework.ResultsSixteen studies including 1929 participants (905 VR, 766 control, 258 active/other interventions) were included, with most focusing on needle-related procedures. VR reduced self-reported peak procedural pain compared to routine care (mean difference (MD) = −0.76, 95% CI [−1.25, −0.27]) and active controls (MD = −0.51, 95% CI [−0.85, −0.17]). VR also reduced self-reported anxiety/fear relative to routine care (MD = −0.78, 95% CI [−1.48, −0.07]) and active controls (MD = −0.59, 95% CI [−1.04, −0.13]). Caregiver-reported pain (MD = −1.22, 95% CI [−2.08, −0.37]) and anxiety/fear (MD = −1.52, 95% CI [−2.7, −0.34]) were similarly improved. Risk of bias was moderate to high.ConclusionVR shows moderate reductions in pediatric procedural pain and anxiety in EDs, though study heterogeneity and methodological limitations reduce confidence in these findings. High-quality trials are needed to confirm effectiveness.

1 Introduction

Background

Children and adolescents frequently undergo painful diagnostic and therapeutic procedures in the emergency department (ED). Needle-related procedures such as venipuncture and cannulation are among the most common and distressing experiences1 and are associated with high levels of pain, fear, and anxiety (broadly termed procedural pain).2, 3, 4, 5 Emergency care environments present distinct challenges, including time-sensitive procedures, high levels of stress, competing demands, and staff shortages.6,7 These challenges can contribute to suboptimal management of procedural pain and anxiety.8 Beyond their immediate impact, poorly managed procedural pain and anxiety can prolong ED length of stay, contribute to delays in diagnosis and treatment, increase staff workload and resource utilization, and negatively shape children’s health care experiences and engagement across the life course.2,9, 10, 11, 12

Pharmacological interventions remain a cornerstone of procedural pain and distress management in EDs, with evidence-based guidelines supporting the routine use of analgesia and topical or local anesthesia for painful procedures.13, 14, 15 In emergency settings, their use may be influenced by factors such as potential side effects, the need for postprocedure monitoring, and delays while medications take effect.16,17 Nonpharmacological strategies (eg, distraction with toys, music, immersive technologies) are key components of a multimodal analgesia approach and are intended to be used alongside pharmacological interventions.15 However, implementation can be limited by practical challenges, including staff training requirements, variability in practice, and access to equipment.11,18,19

Importance

Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising digital health technology for reducing pediatric pain and anxiety across various medical settings.20,21 Evidence from neuroimaging and clinical studies suggests that immersive VR modulates central pain-processing networks and engages top-down inhibitory mechanisms, indicating analgesic effects that extend beyond simple attentional distraction.22,23 This approach has proven effective in procedural contexts like burn care,24 cannulation, and venipuncture25 by creating a psychological distraction and shifting children’s focus away from pain distress.22 Despite growing interest, the adoption of VR in pediatric emergency care remains inconsistent and underexplored, with limited systematic reviews addressing the potential benefits and challenges in these complex settings.26 Although a recent systematic review has been conducted on this topic,27 clinical significance and parent proxy reporting were not assessed, nor were active controls considered.

Goals of This Investigation

The primary aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the effectiveness of VR, compared with routine care and active control interventions, in reducing pain and anxiety during pediatric ED procedures, with a focus on child- and parent-reported outcomes. Our secondary aims were to compare VR to other distraction methods and identify implementation strategies to support the adoption, integration, and long-term sustainability of VR in ED environments. By incorporating studies published up to July 2025 and addressing key methodological gaps, this review offers a more current understanding of VR’s role in pediatric emergency care and shares the growing evidence related to its feasibility and effectiveness in routine clinical practice.27

Methods

Study Design and Registration

This systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)28 guidelines (PRISMA Checklist can be found in Supplementary File 1). The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews at the National Institute for Health Research and the Center for Reviews and Dissemination at the University of York (CRD42024581411).

Search Strategy

The Cochrane Review Group search strategy29 was followed to identify published studies. Embase, PubMed, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library databases were searched from their inception to July 14, 2025, by 2 reviewers (TAD and BRG). The search strategy incorporated Medical Subject Headings and keywords related to (1) virtual reality, (2) pediatric, child, infant or adolescent, (3) pain, anxiety, discomfort, fear or distress, and (4) ED. Boolean operators were used for combining the search terms. The full search strategy is shown in Supplementary File 2. A health librarian specializing in systematic reviews assisted in refining the search strategy. Reference lists of included studies and previously published systematic reviews were searched to identify additional studies.

Selection of Studies

We sought to assess and synthesize all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or nonrandomized studies of interventions (NRSIs) that met the selection criteria in accordance with guidance from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.29 Nonrandomized studies of interventions included prospective cohort studies, case-control studies, controlled before-and-after studies, and interrupted time-series designs. No date or language restrictions were applied to ensure comprehensive coverage. Data Extraction and Synthesis

Retrieved studies were uploaded into EndNote 20 (EndNote, Clarivate Analytics) and then into Covidence (Covidence® systematic review software, Australia) for screening. Two assessors (TAD and MDH) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of selected studies according to inclusion criteria (Table 1).30 Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Data extraction was conducted by 3 authors (TAD, MDH, and BM) using a standardized template. Each article was independently reviewed by 2 authors. In cases of disagreement, a fourth, senior author (BRG) conducted an independent extraction and provided guidance on reaching consensus. Where necessary, study authors were contacted to clarify findings and/or seek unpublished data.

Data Analysis

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

Two assessors independently evaluated the risk of bias for each outcome in each study (TAD and BM). The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool was used to assess bias in RCTs,31 whereas the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used to assess risk of bias in nonrandomized studies.32 Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The Risk-of-Bias VISualization (robvis) tool was used to create risk-of-bias plots.33

Data analysis and synthesis

For meta-analysis, a random-effects model was used to calculate the global effect size, and forest plots were generated to visually represent the results. Methods used to transform data are described in Supplementary File 3.

In addition to quantitative synthesis, a narrative synthesis was conducted for all extracted data. Specifically, to contextualize how studies implemented VR, we described the strategies employed, including implementation strategies designed to enhance the adoption, integration, and sustainability of VR use in ED.

Summary of findings

The certainty of evidence at the outcome level was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.34 Each outcome began with a high-quality rating, as most of the included studies were RCTs. The certainty was then downgraded based on predefined criteria (Supplementary File 4). Final ratings were determined through independent assessments by 2 authors (TAD and BM), with any discrepancies resolved by discussion and consensus.

Results

Selection of Studies

The database search identified 588 records. Following duplicate removal, title and abstract, and full-text screening, 16 studies met the inclusion criteria. All eligible studies were published in English. No additional studies were identified through reference list screening. Across the 16 studies, the total sample size was 1929 participants (range 46 to 458), with 905 in VR groups, 766 in control groups, and 258 in other intervention groups. Most studies focused on the use of VR during needle-related procedures (n = 13), with 3 studies examining the use of VR during suturing of lacerations.37, 38, 39 Age of participants ranged from 6 to 17 years, with all but 3 studies reporting an approximately even gender distribution.37,40,41

Characteristics of Interventions

Several studies used standalone or all-in-one VR headsets,35,36,40,50 often featuring interactive games or 360° nature scenes. VR content ranged from experiences such as roller coasters37,46,50 and ocean adventures41,43 to more interactive games requiring children to direct movements or throw virtual objects.41,45 Six studies used mobile phone VR setups, where smartphones were paired with VR headsets or accessories.41, 42, 43, 44,47,50 One study used a Windows XP PC connected to an eMagin Z800 HMD with motion tracking, enabling interaction via a mouse.45

Characteristics of Controls

Control conditions varied, but often involved no specific distractions or routine care, such as verbal comfort from caregivers or child life specialists, toys, or asking the child questions. Some studies introduced more active control conditions, such as tablets or DVDs showing natural landscapes35 or playing animal sounds.35

Risk of Bias

For the pain outcomes of RCTs, assessed using the Cochrane ROB2 tool, 1 study was rated as low risk, 10 studies had some concerns, and 2 studies were rated as high risk (Supplementary File 5). For anxiety outcomes, 3 studies were rated as low risk, 6 studies had some concerns, and 2 studies were rated as high risk (Supplementary File 5). Although allocation concealment was generally adequate, participant and researcher blinding was often unfeasible due to the visible nature of the VR interventions.

Risk of bias in nonrandomized studies, assessed using the ROBINS-I tool at the outcome level for both pain and anxiety, was moderate to high risk. Thus, a single traffic light plot is presented (Supplementary File 5). The detailed domain-level and overall ROB2 and ROBINS-I assessments for each study are presented in Supplementary File 5.

Pain and Anxiety Outcomes

Pain and anxiety ratings (Supplementary File 6) were primarily obtained through self-report (n = 16). Parent or caregiver reports were used in 14 studies,35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42,44,45,49,50 while nurse or clinician assessments were reported in 7 studies.35,36,38,40, 41, 42,44,50

Systemic and topical analgesia were generally permitted as part of routine clinical care. Four studies (n = 4) explicitly excluded children who had received analgesic or sedative medications prior to the procedure.39,42,47,50 Two studies deliberately omitted analgesic or sedative use.44,48 Eight studies allowed analgesia or topical interventions to be administered,37,38,40,43,45,46,49 while analgesia use was not explicitly reported in 2 studies.35,36

Effectiveness of VR Distraction

Most studies reported that VR reduced procedural pain and anxiety, though some findings were mixed or nonsignificant. Significant reductions in pain were observed in 6 studies.36,42, 43, 44, 45,47 Three studies found no differences in pain or anxiety between VR and control groups.41,46,49

Comparison With Other Distraction Techniques

When compared with other distraction methods (eg, music, kaleidoscopes, or Buzzy®), VR generally performed as well or better. Interactive VR appeared more effective than passive distraction approaches, particularly in older children, and was associated with greater reductions in anxiety and shorter procedure times.35 Some studies found no significant differences in pain or anxiety across interventions, though fear was sometimes lower in VR or other distraction groups compared to routine care.47,50

Intervention Effect

Quantitative synthesis

Due to inconsistencies in outcome data reporting across the 14 included studies, only 11 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Two studies did not report a measurement of pain after the procedure, and 1 study grouped pain scores as categories.36 All attempts to contact the corresponding authors of these 3 studies for raw data were unsuccessful.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the influence of outliers on the findings by excluding extreme studies (Supplementary File 8). Removing these studies generally reduced heterogeneity and attenuated effect sizes, indicating that some of the variability in the original analyses was driven by a few influential trials. For self-reported pain and anxiety outcomes, the removal of outliers not only substantially lowered heterogeneity but also led to less certainty regarding the robustness of the findings.

A weak recommendation was made in favor of VR over control (routine care) and other (ie, active control) interventions for both pain and anxiety/fear, based on low certainty among the reviewed studies. Limitations

This meta-analysis included a small number of studies, limiting assessment of publication bias and reducing statistical power to detect heterogeneity. Substantial heterogeneity was present, particularly in comparisons with routine care, likely reflecting variation in analgesia use and inconsistent standard of care comparators across studies.

Broad inclusion criteria aimed to reflect the diversity of real-world pediatric ED settings and improve generalizability; however, more focused criteria in future studies may better clarify which patients and procedures benefit most from VR.

Blinding of participants and clinicians was generally not possible, introducing potential bias inherent to VR interventions. Excluding studies that reported postintervention data only may have omitted relevant evidence.

Variable effect sizes and low overall certainty of the evidence highlight the need for larger, high-quality trials. Future research should also examine feasibility, costs, and which VR characteristics (eg, duration, interactivity, content) produce clinically meaningful benefits.

Discussion

VR intervention, compared to both active control and routine care, resulted in a reduction in self-reported peak procedural pain and anxiety. Sensitivity analyses revealed that a small number of studies exerted a substantial influence on pooled effect sizes and heterogeneity. In particular, Akarsu et al42 and Goktas and Avci47 were identified as clear outliers. Both included multiple comparison groups with active controls (eg, tablet cartoons, kaleidoscope, music). When these outliers were excluded, effect sizes diminished although trends still favored the VR intervention. Notably, Akarsu et al measured pain during the procedure, which might amplify effect sizes; however, another study that also used real-time pain assessment was not identified as an outlier, suggesting that timing alone did not explain Akarsu’s extreme effect. When accounting for the influence of these studies, the overall effect of VR appeared more modest than indicated by unadjusted results, but VR remained potentially meaningful in reducing pediatric procedural pain and anxiety.

Differences in use of analgesia between studies might also contribute to heterogeneity and varied effect sizes. Despite evidence-based pediatric guidelines,13, 14, 15 there was inconsistent use of analgesia and topical or local anesthesia for painful procedures. This variability might have amplified procedural distress in control groups or altered the apparent effectiveness of VR, highlighting a deviation from recommended best practice. Further analysis, including meta-analyses and/or sensitivity analyses on analgesia use, might provide additional evidence. However, the heterogeneity of the study designs or lack of reporting in these studies prevented these analyses from being conducted. Furthermore, the length and invasiveness of procedures (ie, venipuncture versus laceration repair) might have impacted the effect of VR on pain and anxiety, beyond the influence of analgesia.

Drawing on analogous pain research and broader anxiety literature in both pediatric and adult populations, a ≥1 point change on an 11-point pain and anxiety numerical rating scale (NRS) is considered a reasonable indicator of clinically meaningful improvement.51, 52, 53, 54 Although numerous studies have attempted to establish minimum clinically important differences for pediatric pain scales, findings remain inconsistent across populations, study designs, and measurement tools.55 Applying this threshold to the self-reported outcomes, the pooled MDs for pain or anxiety observed in this review indicated a moderate benefit in favor of VR that, although statistically significant, did not consistently reach the clinical significance threshold.

An important aspect of this review was the inclusion of parent- or caregiver-reported pain and anxiety/fear scores, which demonstrated a substantially larger pooled effect (MD = −1.22, 95% CI [−2.08, −0.37] and MD = −1.52, 95% CI [−2.7, −0.34], respectively). These reductions exceeded the defined threshold for clinical significance, suggesting that VR may produce meaningful improvements in observable distress, even where child-reported measures indicate more moderate effects. However, parent reports might not always accurately reflect the child’s experience, and there is often inconsistency between child and caregiver-reported pain and distress.56 Children may also have difficulty quantifying their distress during procedures, particularly in high-anxiety environments such as EDs, whereas parents or caregivers often rely on observable behavioral cues, such as crying, avoidance, muscle tension, or agitation.57 Parent-reported findings provided an additional observational perspective and indicated that the intervention might have more pronounced effects on child behaviors linked to pain and fear, even if these were not fully captured via self-report scales. Consequently, although the self-reported findings alone might not justify implementation of VR, the addition of caregiver-reported outcomes provided stronger justification for VR as an adjunct to routine care.

Although VR outperformed the routine care control, which often involved minimal or no distraction, its advantage over the active controls such as toys, music, or video was less pronounced. These established distraction techniques are already known to reduce pain and anxiety,15 raising the question of whether the observed benefits of VR reflect the immersive qualities unique to VR or simply a more engaging form of distraction. Active control methods could also divert attention away from pain and anxiety,58, 59, 60, 61 indicating that the mechanism underlying VR’s effectiveness might not be unique. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of routine care across studies also complicated direct comparisons. Further research comparing VR directly against diverse distraction techniques is required to determine whether VR offers additional benefits beyond traditional distraction approaches.

Implications for Practice

The findings of this study have several implications for clinical practice. Immersive VR shows promise as an adjunct to pharmacological interventions for managing procedural pain and anxiety in pediatric ED settings, particularly for needle-based procedures. Caregiver reported, clinically meaningful improvements strengthen the case for VR as a useful tool. However, variability in duration, hardware, software, and content across studies likely contributed to inconsistent findings. Although standardized VR programs support research consistency, they may limit personalization, which is important as engagement and acceptability vary across age groups and clinical contexts. Tailoring VR interventions to individual needs may therefore enhance procedural cooperation and effectiveness, but implementation requires attention to practical considerations, such as staff training, device costs, cleaning protocols, and ensuring developmentally appropriate content, as demonstrated by Storey et al.62

Future Research

Future trials should incorporate preregistered protocols, baseline and follow-up data collection, clearly defined and standardized outcome measures, and the use of active control conditions matched for engagement. The wide variation in VR interventions and procedure types (eg, cannulation and laceration repair) across studies introduces considerable heterogeneity, making it difficult to determine the most effective VR modalities. Furthermore, the nature of VR interventions makes blinding difficult, which may further complicate investigations. Although VR can be customized, further exploration of which features (eg, immersion, narrative content, interactivity) are most effective will provide insight into how VR can be optimized for pediatric patients.

Practical challenges, such as device costs, cleaning protocols, setup time, and staff training, must be addressed for VR to be successfully integrated into busy pediatric EDs. Future studies should assess these factors across different health care settings and patient populations, especially in comparison to other distraction techniques. Developing low-cost, user-friendly VR solutions and evaluating equity of access and cultural appropriateness will be essential for responsible implementation.63

In summary, this systematic review demonstrated that VR could reduce procedural pain and anxiety in pediatric ED settings, with a moderate effect on self-reported pain and anxiety. Parent-reported outcomes, although more strongly positive, should be interpreted cautiously alongside child self-report. Although current findings were limited by study heterogeneity and overall evidence quality, VR’s adaptability and immersive potential make it a promising adjunct for improving children’s experiences during distressing ED procedures.
