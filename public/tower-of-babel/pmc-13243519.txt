Temporal expression of CXCR2 ligands in a rat model of LPS—induced pulpitis
Authors: Marion Florimond, Sandra Minic, Coralie Torrens, Lucas T Duong, Catherine Chaussain, Emmanuelle Renard, Tchilalo Boukpessi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophils play a central yet dual role in pulpitis, the inflammatory response to dental caries. Because CXCR2 ligands regulate neutrophil recruitment and function, this study focused on the temporal expression of two major CXCR2 ligands (CXCL1 and CXCL2) during the progression of LPS induced pulpitis. A controlled rat model of progressive pulpitis (n = 102) was developed using LPS from Escherichia coli or PBS applied to mechanically exposed pulp, followed by sealing of the pulp–dentin wound to allow inflammation to progress over defined time points. Extracted teeth underwent histological, immunohistochemical (myeloperoxidase, CXCL1, CXCL2) and genomic analyses (IL-6, MMP-9, CXCL1).The model reproduced the temporal dynamics of pulp–dentin inflammation, with a progressive increase in IL-6 expression and tissue alterations. IL-6 and MMP-9 transcription suggested a pivotal shift in the inflammatory process around 48 h. Neutrophil recruitment and activation, reflected by myeloperoxidase expression, closely paralleled tissue damage. Interestingly, CXCL1 correlated with neutrophil activity and lesion severity, whereas CXCL2 showed delayed and localized expression, indicating a potential role in fine-tuning the response. As this study focused specifically on ligand dynamics, it characterizes the temporal pattern of CXCL1 and CXCL2 expression and provides new insight into chemokine‑driven inflammatory processes during pulpitis in rat.Keywords: Pulp biology, Neutrophil biology, Cytokine(s), Inflammation, ChemokinesSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Immunology, Medical research, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Given the significant impact of carious disease on public health and patients’ quality of life1, improving the management of this condition remains a critical priority. Indeed, with over 2 billion people affected globally and well-established links between systemic health and dental infections2, there is substantial evidence that enhanced practices could greatly benefit patients. In this perspective, a deeper understanding of pulp-dentin biology is crucial to unlocking new diagnostic and therapeutic dimensions3.

Carious disease arises from a dysbiosis within the oral ecosystem, leading to carious lesions caused by the demineralization of dental tissues due to metabolic byproducts of cariogenic bacteria in the dental biofilm4. Beneath the peripheral enamel, the pulp-dentin complex forms the tooth’s vital core, possessing both immune and reparative capabilities. As the caries process progresses, bacteria penetrate the enamel and subsequently invade the underlying dentin, a tubular, mineralized connective tissue characterized by its permeability (Fig. 1A). Odontoblasts are post-mitotic cells of mesenchymal origin, presenting cellular extensions enclosed within the dentinal tubules and cell bodies arranged in a palisade along the pulp periphery. Their strategic location establishes them as the first line of cellular defense within the pulp-dentin complex, enabling the early detection of tissue debris and bacterial products (such as lipopolysaccharides LPS) through their pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). Activated odontoblasts secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, which trigger the recruitment of resident immune cells and circulating leukocytes. Inflammation within the pulp-dentin complex, known as pulpitis, occurs in the unique context of a confined space, restricting the potential of tissue expansion. This cascade of events can result in one of two outcomes: either healing through the formation of mineralized tissue known as tertiary dentin (Fig. 1B, C), or pulpal necrosis, which is inevitably followed by bacterial colonization and may result in local or systemic infectious complications (Fig. 1D).

Schematic representation of the pathogenesis of pulpitis induced by a carious lesion. (A) Cariogenic bacteria within the dysbiotic dental biofilm damage the hard tissues of the tooth, including peripheral enamel and underlying dentin. Due to the tubular and permeable architecture of dentin, bacterial products and tissue debris diffuse toward the pulp. These warning signals activate receptors on odontoblasts, which then release pro-inflammatory cytokines. This initiates a cascade of cellular and molecular events within the pulp, leading to pulpitis. (B) The most favorable outcome of pulpitis is the resolution of inflammation and healing through the formation of mineralized tissue known as tertiary dentin. There are two types of tertiary dentin1: reactionary dentin, secreted by activated odontoblasts, and2 reparative dentin, produced by dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) when the odontoblastic layer is compromised. The resolution of inflammation and the deposition of tertiary dentin are determined by the molecular microenvironment, which depends on the nature of the infectious stimulus and both general and local host factors. (C) Radiographically, the pulp appears radiolucent, while the more mineralized dentin appears radiopaque. The formation of tertiary dentin is observed as a reduction in pulp space (highlighted in red), which becomes occupied by newly formed dentin tissue. In contrast, the carious lesion within the dentin appears radiolucent (highlighted in white) due to tissue demineralization. (D) If the inflammation fails to resolve, the pulp tissue undergoes necrosis, and the pulp space becomes colonized by bacteria. These bacteria can spread to surrounding tissues, causing local infections in the bone (abscesses) and subsequently infiltrating soft tissues (cellulitis). In severe cases, this can lead to systemic complications such as bacteremia or septicemia.

However, among all the actors involved during pulpitis, the double-edged role of neutrophils, while being reported, remains insufficiently understood and needs to be clarified5. Indeed, their early and crucial function in antibacterial immunity is intricately associated with collateral tissue damage6. Despite this, the regulation of their recruitment and activity has received relatively limited attention in the context of pulpitis. Improving our understanding of pulp neutrophil physiology could therefore lead to a significant breakthrough in unraveling pulp-dentin biology.

The CXC-chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) has been identified as essential for neutrophil homeostasis, playing key roles in their release from the bone marrow, maturation, migration and activity7–10. Due to its role in mediating neutrophil chemotaxis (Fig. 2), the CXCR2/ligands axis is being investigated in many inflammatory and infectious pathologies as a potential therapeutic target11,12. However, despite evidence that CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL8, the main CXCR2 ligands, are overexpressed during pulpitis in several models13–15, their specific role in pulpitis remains uninvestigated.

Schematic illustration of the role of the CXCR2 / ligands axis in neutrophil recruitment. (A, B) Bacterial infection releases PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) and DAMPs (damage associated molecular patterns), triggering the production of inflammatory mediators that activate immune cells and endothelial cells (ECs). (C) Activated ECs then display an increased surface expression of adhesion molecules such as selectins and integrins, which bind to surface receptors on circulating leukocytes. (D) It has been described that activated ECs can present CXCL1 and CXCL2, that are involved in adhesion of CXCR2 + neutrophils to the luminal wall. Neutrophils then secrete CXCL2, which allows transendothelial migration (TEM) by interacting with the atypical chemokine receptor 1 (ACKR1). (E) CXCL2 has also been described as being produced by activated pericytes to mediate subendothelial crawling. (F, G, H) Once in interstitial tissue, neutrophils are directed by a haptotactic and chemotactic gradient to the site of inflammation, where their various cytotoxic mechanisms make them a potent antibacterial effector. Indeed, it has been described that the presence of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a component of Gram-negative bacteria, triggers production of CXCL1 and CXCL2 by TLR4-expressing mast cells and macrophages, guiding neutrophils migration. (I, J) It has been suggested that within tissues, neutrophils can secrete CXCL2 to recruit other neutrophils, with autocrine regulation with a negative feedback loop.

Neutrophil recruitment in inflamed pulp tissue is largely orchestrated by chemokines of the CXC family, including the CXCR2 ligands CXCL1 and CXCL2. While recent transcriptomic studies have identified CXCR2‑expressing neutrophil subsets as key drivers of pulp inflammation, the temporal regulation of their ligands during pulpitis remains poorly characterized, particularly in vivo. The present study therefore aimed to provide a descriptive analysis of CXCL1 and CXCL2 expression in a controlled rat model of LPS‑induced pulpitis. Our objective was not to assess CXCR2 expression directly, but to characterize ligand dynamics that may contribute to CXCR2‑dependent inflammatory pathways.

Results

The LPS-induced pulpitis model triggers the inflammatory process associated with tissue damage

In the LPS-induced pulpitis in vivo model described in this work (Fig. 3A), inflammation was characterized at both the molecular and tissue level. The gene expression of two molecular markers were analyzed: interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Fig. 3B) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) (Fig. 3C).

Molecular and tissular characterization of the LPS-induced inflammation. (A) Graphical representation of the experimental protocol for the inflammation model. All 102 animals were included in the analysis; no animals were excluded. The sample size was decided using the resource equation method (PMID 24250214). Rats were randomly assigned to one of the groups. A numbering strategy was used to minimize potential confusion of animals, while permitting a level of blinding of operators during the experiment. (B) Analysis of IL-6 gene expression levels over time. Values below graphs indicate multiple comparison of p-values between different groups. (C) Analysis of MMP-9 gene expression levels over time. Values below graphs indicate multiple comparison of p-values between different groups. (D–I) Evolution of tissue damage during inflammation. Hematoxylin and eosin staining of histological sections of rat maxillary first molars.

IL-6 expression

In the PBS group, IL-6 expression was significantly increased [p < 0.05] from 30 min to 96 h, compared with the healthy state (T0). In the LPS group, IL-6 expression was significantly increased [p < 0.05] from 24 h to 72 h. In addition, there was an increasing trend after 24 h (p = 0.0778) and a decreasing trend after 48 h (p = 0.0778), suggesting paroxysmal IL-6 gene expression between 24 and 48 h. It should be noted that at 120 h, no group showed any significant difference in IL-6 gene expression compared with the healthy state.

MMP-9 expression

In the LPS group, there was a tendency for increased MMP-9 expression starting at 48 h (p = 0.065) and continued until 72 h (p = 0.056), suggesting that paroxysmal gene expression of MMP-9 was reached between 48 and 72 h. It should be noted that the distribution of values was more heterogeneous across times in the PBS group.

These findings indicate that the described model can promptly initiate an inflammatory response, which appears to peak around 48 h in the LPS group, as evidenced by trends in the transcriptional activity of IL-6 and MMP-9. Notably, the higher variability observed in the PBS group suggests distinct mechanisms in the regulation of inflammation when driven by LPS-dependent signaling pathways.

Histological assessment

Tissue changes linked to inflammation were assessed through histological examination, where areas of cellular degeneration were easily identified by alterations in tissue architecture (Fig. 3D). The area of cellular degeneration appears to increase with the duration of inflammation induction, both in the LPS group and the PBS group (Fig. 3E–J), suggesting the induction of gradual inflammation.

Overall, these data suggest that the described model effectively induces inflammation, with its kinetics observable at both molecular and tissue levels.

Neutrophil activity is associated with tissue degradation during pulpitis

Neutrophil activity and recruitment were assessed by detecting myeloperoxidase (MPO) via immunohistochemistry. MPO, an enzyme predominantly found in the primary azurophilic granules of neutrophil, has been described as a marker of neutrophils recruitment and activity16,17. It is important to note that MPO was absent in healthy state (data not shown). MPO was detected as early as 30 min in both radicular and coronal pulp tissue and was present throughout the observation period (Fig. 4A–P). In regions where pulp tissue architecture was preserved (Fig. 4A–L), MPO appeared in a localized and condensed form, suggesting intracellular localization. Interestingly, from 24 h onwards, a zone of cellular degeneration was observed, in which MPO appeared in large and diffuse quantities (Fig. 4M–P), indicating a release of MPO into the extracellular environment. However, a halo surrounding the area of cell degeneration, where nuclei were still identifiable despite the presence of extracellular MPO (Fig. 4Q), suggested that MPO release precedes cell degeneration.

Analysis of MPO expression during pulpitis by immunohistochemistry. (A–Q) Focus on different coronal or root areas at different timepoints (scale bar: 50 μm - D: dentin - V: vessel - CD: cell degeneration).

These results indicate that neutrophil recruitment is distributed throughout the pulp tissue over the course of the inflammatory process. Additionally, these findings shed light on the spatio-temporal connections between neutrophil activity and tissue damage during pulpitis.

CXCL1 expression is early, generalized and continuous

As CXCL1 has been described as playing a major role in neutrophil recruitment by interacting with CXCR2, its protein and gene expression were assessed during pulpitis (Fig. 5).

Analysis of CXCL1 protein and gene expression during pulpitis. (A–P) Analysis of CXCL1 expression during pulpitis by immunohistochemistry with focus on different coronal or root areas at different timepoints (scale bar: 50 μm). (Q) Analysis of CXCL1 gene expression levels over time. Values below graphs indicate multiple comparison of p-values between different groups.

Protein expression

In this model, CXCL1 protein expression was found early, widespread throughout the tissue, and continuous during the inflammatory process (Fig. 5A-P). Interestingly, CXCL1 was essentially expressed in areas of cellular degeneration, where MPO was found also abundantly present (Fig. 5M-P). However, we must note the relative low number of CXCL1-positive cells at the corresponding time points. CXCL1protein expression therefore exhibits marked spatial and temporal similarities with MPO protein expression. These findings suggest, for the first time, in the context of pulpitis, a potentially association between CXCL1 release and neutrophil recruitment.

Gene quantification

In the LPS group, significant differences [p = 0.018] in CXCL1 gene expression were observed from 24 h and up to 72 h, compared with expression levels at 120 h (Fig. 5Q). These results suggest that paroxysmal gene expression of CXCL1 occurred between 24 h and 72 h. In the PBS group, the distribution of CXCL1 gene expression values appeared more heterogeneous than in the LPS group. These findings support the previous hypothesis of a turning point in transcriptional activity around 48 h, as well as a difference in the regulation of inflammation mediated by LPS-dependent signalling pathways compared to PBS.

CXCL1 expression increased in both PBS and LPS conditions, consistent with its role as an early and non‑specific inflammatory chemokine following pulp exposure. Although the LPS group showed a more homogeneous and sustained expression profile, these findings indicate that CXCL1 primarily reflects tissue injury rather than a specific LPS‑dependent response.

CXCL2 expression occurs in a delayed, inconsistent and localized pattern

CXCL2, another chemokine implicated in neutrophil recruitment, was also assessed for protein expression during pulpitis (Fig. 6).

Arrows indicate slight expressions (scale bar: 50 μm - V: vessels). Protein expression

Interestingly, CXCL2 exhibited a different expression profile compared to CXCL1. Unlike CXCL1, CXCL2 was not detected at 30 min (Fig. 6 A–D) and was often absent at 24 h (Fig. 6F, H, N), except in rare samples where a very slight expression was observed in specific locations, such as perivascular areas or sub-odontoblastic layer (Fig. 6E, G, M). In the latest stages, i.e. after 72 h, CXCL2 expression appeared to be more frequent and more extensive (Fig. 6I, O, P). In some samples, a strong expression was found in perivascular areas (Figure 6Q, R), which could indicate a potential role in neutrophil diapedesis.

CXCL2 protein expression was detected but remained spatially restricted and heterogeneous, preventing reliable gene‑level quantification. In our model, CXCL1 and CXCL2 displayed clearly distinct expression profiles, suggesting that they may fulfill different functions during pulp inflammation. The delayed, inconsistent and localized pattern of CXCL2 expression supports the hypothesis that this chemokine contributes to a more fine‑tuned regulation of neutrophil recruitment during pulpitis.

Discussion

The experimental animal model used in this study provides a relevant and controlled framework for investigating the spatio‑temporal dynamics of pulp inflammation. The LPS‑induced protocol successfully triggered an inflammatory response that reproduces key features of carious pulpitis, although it does not encompass the full complexity of the carious process. As expected, IL‑6 expression increased compared with the healthy state, consistent with previous animal and human studies14,15,18,19. At the tissue level, architectural alterations characteristic of pulpitis were observed20, supporting the validity of this model for studying inflammatory processes within the pulp–dentin complex.

Although MMP‑9 expression did not reach statistical significance, its temporal pattern paralleled that of IL‑6, with a clear upward trend between 48 h and 72 h in the LPS group. This suggests a potential inflammatory turning point around 48 h. The absence of statistical significance may reflect the limited sample size and the use of non‑parametric analyses. Interestingly, the greater heterogeneity observed in the PBS group for both MMP‑9 and CXCL1 expression suggests distinct regulatory mechanisms depending on whether inflammation is driven by LPS‑dependent or non‑LPS‑dependent pathways.

Neutrophil activity, assessed through MPO expression, showed a strong spatio‑temporal concordance with tissue degeneration. MPO appeared intracellular in preserved regions and extracellular in areas of degeneration, with an intermediate halo where tissue architecture remained identifiable. This sequence suggests that MPO release precedes and may contribute to tissue damage. These observations align with the broader literature showing that neutrophils, while essential for antibacterial defense, can induce collateral damage through the release of proteases, histones, cytokines, and reactive oxygen species21–24.

Recent transcriptomic studies have identified a distinct CXCR2⁺ neutrophil subset in human pulpitis, characterized by a mature pro‑inflammatory phenotype and enhanced NETs formation. Inhibition of CXCR2 in vivo was shown to reduce neutrophil infiltration and promote reparative mineralization, supporting a functional role for CXCR2‑dependent pathways in pulp inflammation. These mechanistic findings highlight the importance of CXCR2 signaling in regulating neutrophil behavior. In this context, our study provides complementary in vivo data by characterizing the temporal and spatial expression of two major CXCR2 ligands, CXCL1 and CXCL2, during LPS‑induced pulpitis25.

To further explore the mechanisms underlying neutrophil behavior, we examined CXCR2‑ligand expression, known to regulate neutrophil maturation, chemotaxis, and function10,26. CXCL1 exhibited early, widespread, and sustained expression, with strong labeling in areas of degeneration where MPO was abundant. Despite the relatively low number of CXCL1‑positive cells, this may be sufficient to guide neutrophil migration, and the spatial and temporal overlap between CXCL1 and MPO supports a major role for CXCL1 in neutrophil recruitment during pulpitis.

In contrast, CXCL2 displayed a delayed, localized, and inconsistent expression pattern, particularly in perivascular or sub‑odontoblastic regions. This profile suggests a role in fine‑tuning neutrophil recruitment rather than initiating it, consistent with previous reports indicating that CXCL1 and CXCL2 may be produced by different cell types and fulfill distinct functions26. The inability to quantify CXCL2 gene expression likely reflects its restricted and variable protein expression. As our study focused specifically on ligand dynamics, CXCR2 expression and signaling were not directly evaluated. The distinct temporal profiles of CXCL1 and CXCL2 observed in our model therefore suggest that these chemokines may fulfill different roles during pulp inflammation, with CXCL1 acting as an early and robust injury‑associated signal, while the delayed and localized expression of CXCL2 may contribute to more fine‑tuned regulation of neutrophil recruitment. These descriptive findings provide a framework for interpreting CXCR2‑dependent mechanisms reported in recent transcriptomic and functional studies.

The intense perivascular CXCL2 labeling observed at later stages raises the possibility of a role in neutrophil transendothelial migration, as reported in other inflammatory contexts where CXCL2 is produced by neutrophils or perivascular macrophages7,26,27. This observation also prompts questions regarding potential involvement in reverse transendothelial migration, a phenomenon implicated in the resolution or propagation of inflammation23,28. Moreover, the differential expression patterns of CXCL1 and CXCL2 highlight the need to further investigate the contribution of macrophages, which have been described as key producers of both chemokines and important regulators of neutrophil recruitment7,29.

Overall, this study provides descriptive evidence that CXCR2 ligands exhibit distinct temporal and spatial profiles in a model of LPS‑induced pulpitis. While descriptive in nature, these findings are consistent with research in other inflammatory diseases showing that dysregulation of CXCR2 contributes to chronic inflammation12 and that pharmacological or genetic modulation of this axis can reduce tissue damage and improve outcomes in animal models and in humans16,30–33. However, in the context of pulpitis, several challenges remain, including the complexity of CXCR2 signaling across multiple cell types, its functional overlap with CXCR134, and species‑specific differences such as the absence of CXCL8 in rodents3.

Given these limitations, further studies will be needed to clarify the translational relevance of our findings. Mechanistic studies will continue to rely on rodent models or minipigs, which allow precise control of inflammatory induction and detailed spatio‑temporal analyses. However, bridging these findings to human pathology will require observational and biomarker‑based studies in human pulpitis, including immunohistochemical or transcriptomic analyses of extracted human pulp tissue, correlations between CXCR2/ligand expression and clinical phenotypes, and the exploration of CXCR2‑related biomarkers in minimally invasive samples. These approaches represent realistic and clinically meaningful steps toward translation.

Conclusion

This study presents a controlled murine model based on LPS/PBS exposure, providing insight into the temporal dynamics of pulp inflammation. The distinct spatial and temporal expression patterns of CXCL1 and CXCL2, together with their association with neutrophil activity and tissue alterations, suggest complementary roles for these chemokines in modulating the inflammatory response. These descriptive findings lay the groundwork for future mechanistic and translational studies aimed at clarifying how CXCR2‑dependent pathways may contribute to pulpitis in humans.

Method

Animals

A total of 102 eight-week-old SPF male Sprague Dawley rats (Janvier Labs, Saint-Berthevin, France) were used for this study. The animal experiment was designed and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments and was approved by the Animal Care Committee of Université de Paris (protocol No. C92- 049-01/ APAFiS no.19–042).

Induction of pulpitis

Following general anesthesia via intraperitoneal injection of 10% ketamine (Imalgene 500, Merial, France) and 2% xylazine (Rompun, Bayer, Lyon, France), the pulp of the maxillary right and left first molars was mechanically exposed. With the aid of an operative microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), cavities were prepared on the occlusal surfaces using a 0.6 mm-diameter round carbide bur (E020524100610, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) on a micromotor running at 13,000 rpm (VivaAce, NSK, Tochigi, Japan). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Escherichia coli O111:B4 (L2630, Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany) was applied to the exposed pulp tissue at a concentration of 10 mg/mL or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 min using 0.5 mm-diameter microbrushes (Microbrush international, Grafton, WI, USA). The cavities were then filled (Cavit, 3 M ESPE, Saint Paul, Minnesota, USA) and the inflammatory process was allowed to progress for various durations (30 min, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96–120 h). Twelve molars were kept intact as healthy samples. Sixty-five rats were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation for mRNA quantification while thirty-seven rats were euthanized by intracardiac perfusion for histological analysis. The surgical procedure was controlled radiographically by ex vivo micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis (Quantum FX µCT scanner, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

mRNA quantification

After euthanasia and sampling, 127 of the 130 first molars were processed for RNA extraction and reverse transcription. Total RNAs were isolated with RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). cDNA synthesis was obtained with 1 µg of total RNA which was used as a template for reverse transcription following to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Assays were designed by Fluidigm company (Biomark™ HD, Fluidigm company, San Francisco, USA) using the Preamp Master Mix kit and the Pooled Taqman assay mix (Fluidigm, cat. no. PN 100–5744) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The thermal cycling conditions were set at 95 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of 95 °C for 15s, and 60 °C for 2 min.

Sample preparation for histology

After euthanasia and sampling, 74 samples were decalcified in a 4.13% EDTA (pH 7.4) solution maintained at 45 °C a Milestone KOS Microwave Tissue Processor (Sorisole, Bergamo, Italy) for approximately 4 weeks. Tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and 4 μm thick sections were cut along the mesiodistal axis using a microtome (Microm HM 325, Microm Microtech, Brignais, France).

Histological analysis

Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to assess tissue changes. For histological analysis, the samples were examined using a microscope (Leitz DMRB, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemical analysis, sections were incubated with primary antibodies anti-CXCL1, anti-CXCL2 (PA5-46960 and 701126, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA; respective dilutions: 15µg/mL and 1/20) or anti-myeloperoxidase (AB_208670, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; dilution: 1/200). Sections then were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies (Vectastain ABC Kit, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, Californie, USA; dilution: 1/200) and slides were developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate (SigmafastTM 3’3-diaminobenzidine, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Negative controls were performed by omitting the primary antibody.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted in collaboration with AnaStats (AnaStats Scop ARL, Rilly-sur-Vienne, France) using R software (4.2.0).

A filtering process was first performed using several methods, including multivariates approaches (a PCA done with the FactoMineR 2.4 package - nonspecific filtering - and a PLS-DA done with the mixOmics 6.12.1 package - specific filtering) and a univariate approach (non-parametric rank-based ANOVAs done with the nparLD 2.2 package - specific filtering). The 20 analytes selected from filtering were then inferentially analyzed one by one using a univariate method. Non-parametric tests were performed with rank-based tests to avoid the issues associated with analyzing data containing very extreme values: a global test (with the nparLD 2.2 package) and multiple comparisons between groups (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) with the FDR (false discovery rate) adjustment method to correct inflation error.
