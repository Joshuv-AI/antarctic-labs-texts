StarSignDNA: mutation-to-signature attribution for accurate representation of mutational processes in low-mutation contexts
Authors: Christian Domilongo Bope, Sumana Kalyanasundaram, Knut D Rand, Sigve Nakken, Ole Christian Lingjærde, Eivind Hovig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAll cell lineages accumulate mutations over time, increasing the probability that some lineages eventually become malignant. Many of the processes responsible for generating mutations leave a characteristic footprint in the genome that allows their presence to be detected. However, the mutational pattern in a tumour is usually the combined result of multiple mutational processes being at work simultaneously, and the problem of disentangling the different footprints and their relative impact then becomes a deconvolution problem. Several algorithms have been developed for this purpose, most of them involving a factorisation of the mutation count matrix into two non-negative matrices, representing, respectively, the underlying mutational signatures and the relative exposure to these signatures. Here, we introduce the StarSignDNA algorithm for mutational signature analysis, which offers efficient re-fitting and de novo mutational signature extraction. StarSignDNA performs mutation-to-signature attribution, assigning each mutation to its most probable causative mutational signature(s) based on the mutation’s trinucleotide context and the signature profiles. StarSignDNA demonstrates robust and clinically relevant performance in low-mutation contexts, with particular strengths in re-fitting analysis and balanced performance in de novo signature discovery. The package offers a command-line-based interface and data visualisation routines. The package is available at https://github.com/uio-bmi/StarSignDNA and can be installed via PyPI.

Introduction

A diverse set of mutational processes shapes cells that may develop into cancers. These processes encompass endogenous cellular mechanisms, such as errors in the DNA replication and repair machinery, and exogenous sources of DNA damage, such as tobacco and UV exposure [1]. The imprints of mutational processes that have occurred during tumourigenesis are reflected in distinct spectra of acquired DNA aberrations in tumour samples, and these aberrations are now effectively captured with high-throughput sequencing technologies. A characteristic pattern of mutations caused by a mutational process is now commonly referred to as a mutational signature [2]. Accurate identification of mutational signatures in tumour samples is critical for understanding the mechanisms driving tumour development. It can also add novel value when it comes to patient stratification toward personalised treatment regimes [3]. While distinct patterns caused by somatic copy number aberrations and types of insertion/deletion mutations have been found [4], here we focus on the signatures exerted by single base substitutions (SBS), the most common type of aberration in cancer genomes.

Several algorithms and computational tools have been developed to detect and estimate mutational signatures in cancer genomes. These methods can broadly be classified into two main categories: discovery of novel signatures in a collection of sequenced tumour samples (also referred to as de novo signature identification), and fitting known reference signatures to an existing mutational profile (signature reconstruction or re-fitting). Non-negative Matrix Factorisation (NMF), or its Bayesian version (BNMF) [5–7] [8–14], is a matrix decomposition approach that breaks down a complex matrix into smaller and more manageable subproblems. NMF plays a critical role in mutational signature analysis, serving as a cornerstone for de novo mutational signature extraction and refitting algorithms. In de novo analysis, NMF enables the identification of previously unknown mutational signatures by decomposing mutation count matrices into mutation patterns and their corresponding contributions. For mutational signature re-fitting, NMF allows for the decomposition of observed mutational profiles into known signatures, aiding in accurately assigning mutation processes to specific samples. This makes NMF an indispensable tool for uncovering the underlying mutational processes and refining existing knowledge about mutational signatures in cancer genomics.

In addition to NMF-based approaches, probabilistic methods such as Emu, use Expectation Maximisation. In Emu, the underlying probabilistic model assumes that input samples are independent and that the number of mutational signatures is inferred using the Bayesian Information Criterion [15]. Another method, pmsignature significantly streamlines mutation signature models by drastically reducing the number of parameters while simultaneously accounting for a greater number of contextual factors [16].

The collective effort to identify signatures is collated in the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC, https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk). COSMIC also links each signature to the proposed underlying processes.

However, despite advances in the field, challenges remain in accurately characterising mutational signatures. A key challenge is to robustly recover signature compositions when mutation data is limited. For re-fitting, low mutation count (less than 50 mutations per sample) can lead to unreliable discovery of some active signatures, increased prediction variance, and higher fitting error [17–19]. To address this challenge, a common practice in signature discovery is regularisation. Regularisation promotes sparsity in the model and essentially encourages fewer non-zero coefficients. This can help to prevent overfitting, but it can also introduce a bias into the predictions, which can be negligible when using a better optimisation approach. Several methods achieve regularisation in different ways; for example, by introducing constraints on the model, by using a Bayesian variant of Non-Negative Matrix Factorisation (NMF) with a penalty on model complexity, by employing an L1-based regularisation for sparsity on the signatures, or by leveraging the L1 norm penalty to promote sparsity [12, 20, 21]. These techniques aim to achieve better predictions by reducing the estimated variance.

A key challenge for most de novo signature extraction models is accurately predicting the optimal number of signatures (K) that reflect true underlying biological processes, namely, signatures that closely match known references with high cosine similarity. The current de novo signature discovery methods face two primary challenges: (i) Determining the optimal number of signatures (K) to extract, as no definitive method exists to select the most appropriate K; and (ii) Balancing the number of signatures (K) with their distinctiveness. Ideally, the discovered signatures should exhibit a high degree of uniqueness, typically measured by a cosine similarity threshold of around 0.8, while maintaining a reasonable number of signatures to capture the underlying mutational processes.

It is important to remember that simply fitting the data well (minimising residual error) does not necessarily reflect actual biological processes [11, 22]. The common practices to determine the number of K signatures are the following: (1) K should be selected in such a way that a higher K will imply a lower residual error [11]; (2) Calling signatures hierarchically on subsets of samples, adding more signatures to fit every sample. The selection of K in the first approach is arbitrary, relying either on prior knowledge of the tumour being studied or on intuition, and the second leads to overfitting, since there is a need to use more signatures for better fitting, without any constraint. SignatureAnalyzer uses automatic relevance determination, which starts with a high number of signatures and attempts to eliminate signatures of low relevance [5, 23, 24]. SUITOR is an unsupervised cross-validation method that selects the optimal number of signatures to achieve the minimal prediction error in the validation set and extends the probabilistic model to allow missing data in the training set [25]. SparseSignatures employs cross-validation to jointly determine the optimal number of signatures and the shrinkage parameter [21].

This paper introduces StarSignDNA, an NMF model that offers both de novo and re-fitting mutation signature analysis, with particular strengths for scenarios in which mutation data is limited. The algorithm uses regularisation for its dual benefits [20, 26, 27]. Variable selection is essential in mutational signature analysis to identify the most relevant signatures among many possible ones, enhancing interpretability. Shrinkage helps prevent overfitting by reducing the influence of weak signatures.

Distinct mutagenic processes are assumed to leave different footprints in the genome in the form of different mutational signatures. StarSignDNA algorithms

StarSignDNA consists of four main algorithms designed for different analytical tasks: (1) single-sample re-fitting for exposure estimation, (2) de novo signature discovery, (3) multi-sample re-fitting, and (4) cross-validation for parameter selection. The core algorithms employ L1-regularised optimisation with adaptive learning rates and convergence criteria. Detailed pseudocode for all algorithms is provided in Supplementary Materials.

Methodological comparison of StarSignDNA with existing mutational signature analysis Tools

The comparison tables (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) highlight StarSignDNA’s significant methodological advantages over existing mutational signature analysis tools. In signature refitting (Supplementary Table S1), StarSignDNA uniquely offers bootstrap-based confidence interval estimation and mutation context awareness. Its robust overfitting control—integrating filtering, confidence intervals, weighted exposures, and orthogonality constraints exceeds the partial strategies used by other methods. For de novo signature extraction (Supplementary Table S2), StarSignDNA replaces the Frobenius norm with cross-validated Probability Mass Function (PMF) loss minimisation to determine the optimal number of signatures, thereby prioritising biologically meaningful residual error over mathematical convenience [11, 22]. To further improve the separation between extracted signatures, StarSignDNA incorporates a soft orthogonality penalty into the NMF update rules. This penalty term discourages high correlation between signature vectors while preserving the non-negativity constraints essential for biological interpretation. These innovations, as detailed in the supplementary tables, collectively enable StarSignDNA to achieve superior signature discovery accuracy and enhanced biological interpretability, representing a significant advancement in the methodology of mutational signature analysis.

Results

Re-fitting Mutational signatures

A benchmarking comparison was conducted to evaluate the performance of StarSignDNA alongside four other re-fitting mutational signatures tools: MutationalPatterns [29], deconstructSigs [11], sigLASSO [20], and SigProfilerSingleSample (SigProfilerSS, https://github.com/AlexandrovLab/). The datasets comprising 40,002 simulated cancer genomes serve as the ground truth for benchmarking.

The signature categorisation proposed by Pancotti et al. [30], distinguishing between high-flatness and low-flatness signatures, was applied. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates this classification, with high-flatness signatures (red) clustering distinctly from low-flatness signatures (blue), while the remaining signatures are grouped as Other (black).

The first category comprised 20,000 synthetic samples characterised by high flatness, featuring five prominent signatures: SBS3, SBS5, SBS40c, SBS92, and SBS94 (Table 1, Fig. 1). These signatures are of high clinical significance, being among the most frequently observed mutational signatures across diverse cancer types, yet they can be challenging to detect accurately when the number of mutations is limited. Therefore, accurately identifying these abundant and biologically relevant signatures is crucial for evaluating the performance of signature discovery tools.

De novo mutational signatures

StarSignDNA validation on simulation datasets

StarSignDNA de novo model was benchmarked versus SigProfilerExtractor (SigProfiler) [33], an NMF-based method, SparseSignatures [12], a cross-validation method to select both the number of signatures and the shrinkage parameter simultaneously, SUITOR [34], a probabilistic model, and SigneR [16], a Bayesian approach. The benchmarking was performed using the fourth category of simulated datasets. We generated 50 replicate datasets, of 146 patients each, with four ground truth mutational COSMIC signatures, being SBS1, SBS5, BSB18, and SBS40 (Supplementary document).

In the de novo signature extraction method, identifying the optimal number of signatures (K) is critical. The value of K reflects the number of underlying biological processes contributing to the mutations. K was set from 2 to 18, allowing each algorithm to select the optimal K value. It is important to note that while the true K for the simulation dataset is known to be four, additional processes might be present since the simulation dataset was derived from real prostate data. The analysis focused on two key aspects: (i) the number of signatures (K) extracted by each method and (ii) the number of mutational processes recovered by each method. The latter was estimated by evaluating the higher signatures, defined as those de novo signatures exhibiting a cosine similarity of at least 0.8 to a reference signature in COSMIC. We selected 0.8 as a conservative and biologically meaningful cutoff, as values above this level indicate that the dominant mutational patterns are preserved and the extracted and reference signatures are likely to reflect the same underlying mutational process. This threshold is consistent with previous studies that have applied cosine similarity cutoffs in the range of to establish robust signature matches [2, 35, 36]. To ensure rigour, we harmonised the application of this threshold across all datasets analysed in this study.

Considering the number of extracted signatures (K), all methods identified at least four (K = 4), corresponding to the overweighted signatures in the data. SUITOR and SigneR consistently detected only the overweighted signatures across all 50 replicates. SparseSignatures exhibited variability: K = 6 in two replicates, K = 4 in 21 replicates, and K = 3 in 27 replicates. SigProfiler showed a similar behaviour with K = 4 in 43 replicates, K = 3 in three, and K = 2 in four replicates. StarSignDNA identified more than four signatures: K = 6 in 23 replicates, K = 5 in 23 replicates, K = 4 in two replicates, and K = 3 in two replicates. It’s important to note that finding more signatures reduces residual error and improves data fitting. The primary goal of a de novo algorithm goes beyond simply fitting the data. Ideally, it should also identify processes reflecting distinct underlying biological mechanisms. Regarding the residual error, SigneR, SigProfiler, and StarSignDNA achieved the lowest values shown in (Fig. 5B). However, (Fig. 5A) reveals that SigneR and SigProfiler fail to detect active processes beyond the overweighted ones. In contrast, StarSignDNA and SigneR better reconstruct the original input signatures (Fig. 5C). SparseSignatures, on the other hand, utilise regularisation on the signatures themselves, which explains its higher sparsity. StarSignDNA also exhibits a higher sparsity level than other methods (Fig. 5D).

To evaluate the quality of mutational signatures inferred by de novo algorithms, we calculated the quality similarity score , which quantifies the pairwise similarity between signatures identified by different tools. A higher one-to-one indicates greater alignment or agreement between tools, with values approaching 1.0, reflecting near-identical signature solutions. This indicates that the de novo signatures inferred by StarSignDNA were more consistent with known COSMIC profiles.

Discussion

StarSignDNA is an algorithm designed for mutational signature analysis, supporting de novo discovery and re-fitting. The re-fitting engine is designed to handle low mutation count samples, and the de novo engine shows a higher signature prediction ratio. In this study, we benchmarked StarSignDNA against four re-fitting tools and five de novo algorithms using both synthetic samples (40,002) and real samples (12,201). Since the non-orthogonality of the COSMIC signatures by construction impacts the stability of the exposure [37], incorporating L1 regularisation contributes to improving the signature prediction by reducing the variance of the exposures.

The studies by Maura et al. [34] highlighted that high flatness signatures, characterised by similar frequencies across all 96 mutational classes, can introduce background noise and co-linearities, thereby complicating the process of signature extraction and making it less reliable.

To assess performance, we benchmark five re-fitting algorithms using datasets categorised into these two signature types, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of their capabilities.

StarSignDNA demonstrated the best performance, with the highest score across mutation counts, ranging from 100 to 500 per sample. StarSignDNA was followed by SigProfilerSS at 100 mutations per sample and deconstructSigs at 500 mutations per sample (Fig. 1B). As Riva et al. [19] emphasised, a minimum of 50 mutations per sample is sufficient to achieve reliable predictions. Based on this, we evaluated the performance of StarSignDNA under varying mutation counts per sample Fig. 2. Specifically, StarSignDNA was tested with 50 mutations per sample (50% of the original data, Fig. 2A), 70 mutations per sample (70%, Fig. 2B), and 80 mutations per sample (80%, Fig. 2C). Even under these reduced conditions, StarSignDNA consistently maintained a higher score, demonstrating its robustness and reliability in low mutation count scenarios compared to the reference dataset (SimA(100)), where the number of mutations per sample is 100 (Fig. 2D). Future work could extend these perturbation analyses by incorporating synthetic sequencing error models and variable sequencing depths, thereby further evaluating the robustness of StarSignDNA to technical variability.

The same pattern was observed for the low flatness signature (Fig. 3A), where StarSignDNA outperformed the other re-fitting algorithms considering 100 mutations per sample. For 500 mutations per sample, StarSignDNA, MutationalPatterns, deconstructSigs, and sigLASSO performed equally well, while SigProfilerSS underperformed Fig. 3B. The performance of StarSignDNA was validated using the TCGA dataset, comprising 8,893 tumours across 33 cancer types, where StarSignDNA reached the highest score in 18 cancer types across 33 types.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4B, the number of reference signatures used in the re-fitting methods affects their performance and increases the risk of overfitting ([34], [35], [38]). This challenge can be mitigated when cohort samples are considered, and it becomes more difficult when analysing single samples. To address this, StarSignDNA incorporates two approaches: (i) confidence interval predictions using samples and (ii) allowing users to select specific signatures to include in the analysis, mitigating overfitting and reducing computational runtime. The methodological advantages of the StarSignDNA re-fitting engine are presented in a structured comparison of its features in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, alongside commonly used tools for signature re-fitting. StarSignDNA distinguishes itself by integrating several novel components, including: (i) Bootstrap-based confidence interval estimation for signature exposures, (ii) Context-aware mutation weighting to refine exposures after the initial estimation, and (iii) Adaptive filtering of reference signatures to reduce overfitting in sparse mutation contexts.

These features collectively enhance accuracy, interpretability, and robustness, particularly for single-sample inputs with low mutation burden, where existing tools such as sigLASSO, deconstructSigs, MutationalPatterns, and SigProfilerSS either lack proper regularisation strategies or do not estimate uncertainty in the predictions.

In de novo mutational signature analysis, StarSignDNA focuses on addressing the challenge of predicting the relationship between the optimal number of extracted signatures (K) and those with higher cosine similarity (high confidence signatures) [36]. The results presented in Table 4 demonstrate the sensitivity of StarSignDNA, particularly for high flatness signatures, such as SBS3. StarSignDNA detected SBS3 signatures in 36 replicates, followed by SigProfiler in three replicates, while other methods failed to identify it.

The results of primary and metastatic cancer samples (Table 5 and Table 6) further demonstrate that StarSignDNA incrementally outperforms other algorithms when evaluated using the prediction ratio, median correlation per patient, and signature sparsity metrics. In particular, StarSignDNA achieved the highest quality score in all five algorithms tested. The methodological advantages of StarSignDNA’s extraction are presented in a structured comparison in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, along with other widely used five-refitting and five de novo tools. StarSignDNA employs several innovations, including cross-validated model selection based on a probability mass function (PMF) loss function, and soft orthogonality penalties to minimise redundancy among extracted signatures. Compared to existing de novo tools like SigneR, SUITOR, SparseSignatures, and SigProfilerExtractor, these components allow StarSignDNA to more reliably extract distinct and biologically meaningful signatures, even under conditions of limited mutation counts.

In summary, we demonstrate that StarSignDNA is a reliable tool for de novo and re-fitting mutational signature analysis. StarSignDNA’s re-fitting engine shows clear superiority in low-mutation contexts, while the de novo engine provides balanced performance with enhanced biological interpretability. The tool offers unique features, such as prediction confidence and customisable reference signatures for re-fitting analysis, while providing prediction ratios for de novo analysis. These enhancements improve current methods and provide valuable information for genomics and oncology research.
