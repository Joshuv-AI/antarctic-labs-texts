Socioeconomic inequality and its drivers in unmet needs for family planning in Kenya
Authors: Samwel N Wakibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily planning has both health and social benefits for women. A lack of access can lead to unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortion, high maternal and infant mortality, and loss of economic opportunities. The global unmet need for family planning was 15.6% in 2019, while the unmet need for modern family planning among women aged 15–49 years in low- and middle-income countries was unacceptably high at 24%. In Kenya, it was 20% in 2022 despite SDG target 3.7.1 guaranteeing universal coverage.MethodsMost studies on unmet needs focus on the prevalence and factors associated with the uptake of family planning, but assessments of socioeconomic inequality within low-utilization groups are rarely given much attention. This is despite socioeconomic inequality being a central theme in achieving the SDGs. This study assessed socioeconomic inequality in the unmet need for family planning measured by the household wealth index and used putative determinants to explain the inequality.The study used data from the KDHS (2022). Associations between individual factors and unmet needs for family planning were determined using logistic regression models, whereas socioeconomic inequality was assessed using concentration curves and concentration indices.ResultsThe prevalence rate of unmet needs was 14%; 7.6% for spacing and 6.4% for limiting. And among the poorest women was 21.6% compared to 10.1% among the richest. Being poor (OR: 1.788, 95% CI: 1.324–2.414), young (OR: 1.569, 95% CI: 1.212–2.030), having more than one living child (OR: 1.393, 95% CI: 1.157–1.676), not employed (OR: 1.561, 95% CI: 1.375–1.772), having no education (OR: 1.456, 95% CI: 1.022–2.074) and lacking media exposure (OR: 1.249, 95% CI: 1.027–1.519) increased the odds of unmet needs. The socioeconomic inequality in unmet needs was pro-poor (CIX < 0) and was substantially high (CIX<−0.4) among women with more than three children (CIX=−0.41), with no education (CIX=−0.46) or partners have no education (CIX=−0.68) or lack media exposure (CIX=−0.49).ConclusionThe study recommends youth friendly - highly subsidized family planning services and commodities, and family planning information and education for women of low socioeconomic status.Keywords: Socioeconomic inequality, Unmet need, Family planning, Concentration index

Introduction

Global fertility has more than halved in 70 years, from TFR values of 5 in 1950 to 2.2 in 2021, while in sub-Saharan Africa, there were four children per female in 2021 [1]. In Kenya, fertility has declined from 8.1 in 1978 to 3.4 in 2022 [2]. The worldwide improvements are attributed to family planning (FP).

Family planning offers both health and social benefits for women. Unmet need for FP is defined as the percentage of women of reproductive age, either married or in marital union, who want to stop or delay childbearing but are not using any method of contraception. This, together with inefficacy of a contraceptive method being used may lead to unintended pregnancies, most of which ultimately result in induced abortion [3, 4]. In general, unintended pregnancies can have adverse impacts on the lives of women, children and families, including reduced economic opportunities; poor health outcomes and complications; and even death from unsafe abortions, especially where abortion is restricted/illegal [5]. Unsafe abortion is one of the leading causes of maternal death globally [6].

Sustainable development goal target 3.7.1 promises universal access to family planning information and education and sexual and reproductive health-care services. However, despite the promise, the desired family size and timing have not been met; gaps linger in women’s reproductive intentions. The concept of unmet need for FP services and products points to the gap between women’s reproductive intentions and their contraceptive behaviour.

According to [4], about 1.1 billion women of reproductive age (15–49) have a need for family planning globally, but 270 million of them are not accessing for various reasons, including availability, affordability or distance; personal, cultural, or religious objections; fear of side effects, health concerns, lack of knowledge, and ambiguous attitudes toward pregnancy.

The global prevalence of unmet need for family planning is 15.6% in comparison to about 25% in sub-Saharan Africa [7, 8]. has also reported an overall prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa as 23.7%, of which unmet need for spacing and limiting was 15.8% and 7.9% respectively. And as a result of unmet needs, about half of all pregnancies − 121 Million pregnancies that occurred among women of reproductive age every year between 2015 and 2019 were unintended [3].

According to [9], Kenya in 2021 committed to reducing the unmet need for FP among all women from 14% to 10% by 2030 and ensuring the sustained availability of FP commodities to the last mile. To realize the full benefits of contraception, optimal utilization is needed, and the methods should be the most effective [10] i.e. modern methods - they are more effective than traditional methods.

The global contraceptive prevalence rate for women in marital unions was estimated to be 65% for any method and 58.7% for modern methods in 2022 [11]. It was lowest in sub-Saharan Africa at 55.5%, and in Kenya, it was 57% among women in marital unions [2].

Researchers have identified factors associated with contraceptive use as demographic: age, marital status, religion, place of residence, and socioeconomic status (SES); women’s empowerment: employment, role in decision making [12]; fertility history: parity, having an unintended pregnancy; fertility intentions: ideal number of children, want to avoid or delay birth; and exposure to information - knowledge, perceptions of safety and efficacy of FP and use [13].

[14] reported that in Turkey, women who were older, more educated, wealthier, and had more than one child were less likely to have an unmet need for family planning. The employment status of women and their spouses, and type of place of residence were significantly associated with unmet needs.

According to [7], unmet need for contraception is associated with SES. The issue of socioeconomic inequality is a common theme in the 2030 agenda on sustainable development goals and is echoed in most SDGs. It is recognized as central to the achievement of the SDGs, and nations are called upon to narrow its inequalities, especially with respect to health [15].

Socioeconomic status is a composite measure of a person’s or family’s economic and social standing - it refers to the absolute or relative levels of economic resources, power, and prestige closely associated with the wealth of an individual, community, or country [16]. It is assessed using multiple factors, including income, education, employment status, among other factors [17], and is usually categorized as low, medium, and high.

The impact of socioeconomic status on health has been extensively studied, and shown that low socioeconomic status is related to lower values of various health and quality-of-health measures. According to [18], health, including sexual and reproductive health (SRH), follows a social gradient; that is, those in lower socioeconomic positions experience worse health and illness than those in higher socioeconomic statuses do.

However, the effect of socioeconomic status (SES) on putative risk factors of unmet need for family planning in Kenya is not well understood; the available literature on unmet need for contraception in Kenya overlooks aspects of socioeconomic inequality. This knowledge is important in formulating appropriate programmes or policies to empower the vulnerable groups. Helping groups in disadvantaged SES to achieve the good health that others in more advantaged SES attained would help to prevent the widening of health inequalities and meet SDG target 3.7.1 on universal coverage.

The objective of this study is therefore to determine the significance of socioeconomic inequality in accessing FP services. It assesses the effect of SES inequality on putative determinants in determining unmet needs for FP.

Methodology

Study site and population

The study analysed women in marital unions aged 15–49 years who are not pregnant or postpartum amenorrheic and wish to avoid pregnancy in the next two years, but are not using FP. The data used is from the Kenya Demographic and Health Survey, which was collected between February and July 2022 (henceforth referred to as 2022KDHS). It was specifically extracted from the woman file and weighted.

Variables and measurements

The main goal of this analysis is to evaluate unmet needs for family planning and to determine the significance of socioeconomic inequality in accessing FP methods. The main outcome variable is unmet need, which is the sum of the unmet need for spacing and limiting. It is dichotomized as (yes/no).

Multinomial analysis was also undertaken to determine if determinants varied for spacing and limiting independently. Assessing for spacing and limiting separately is important as each describes a different desire - unmet need for limiting reflects desired family size and spacing is for timing. Recoding for multinomial analysis is: spacing, limiting, no unmet need.

Socioeconomic status is the main explanatory variable for the study. The socioeconomic status of women was measured by the household wealth index, which was categorized into quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest).

The other explanatory variables were selected on the basis of their availability in the KDHS dataset and their significant associations with the unmet need for contraception in past studies. These variables included respondent’s current age, number of living children, type of place of residence, employment status of respondent, maternal highest level of education, partner’s education level, and exposure to mass media (print/television/radio) for at least once a week.

To determine the significance of socioeconomic inequality in the unmet need for FP methods, the study employed the concentration curves and index approach.

A concentration curve is a graphical representation of a cumulative share of health sector variable against cumulative shares of the population, ranked by an indicator of socioeconomic status for example wealth. While a concentration index (CIX) is a measure of inequality that quantifies the extent to which an outcome variable (like unmet need for FP) is disproportionately concentrated among a segment of the population (often based on socioeconomic status).

A concentration index (CIX) is computed as twice the covariance between unmet need and an individual’s socioeconomic rank divided by the mean of the health variable. Theoretically, the CIX lies between − 1 and + 1.

The sign of the concentration index indicates the direction of the relationship between the health variable and position in the socioeconomic status (living standards) distribution, while the magnitude reflects both the strength of the relationship and the degree of variability in the health variable [19]. It is negative when it’s more concentrated on the poorest (pro-poor) and positive when it is concentrated more on the richest (pro-rich). A pro-rich (positive) CIX < 0.15 is regarded as low, 0.15 < CIX < 0.4 is moderate, 0.4–0.6 is high and CIX > 0.6 is very high; while a pro-poor (negative) CIX >- 0.15 is regarded as low, −0.15 > CIX>−0.4 is moderate, −0.4 > CIX>−0.6 is high and CIX<−0.6 is very high.

In this study, a concentration curve (CC) plots the cumulative share of the unmet need (y-axis) against the cumulative share of households, ranked from poorest to richest (x-axis). If everyone has an unmet need for FP services irrespective of their SES rank, the CC will consistently lie on the equality (45-degree) line. However, if concentration of unmet need is higher among the poor people (pro-poor), the CC will lie above the line of equality, and if lower (pro-rich i.e. higher concentration of unmet need among rich people than the poor), the CC will lie below the line of equality. The gap between the CC and the equality line depicts the extent of inequality [19].

Socioeconomic status was measured by the household wealth index and categorized into quantiles. Two groups, poorest and richest, were selected as the limits, and socioeconomic inequality was compared between these two groups [20]. Each category of the predictor variable was fitted in the socioeconomic scheme of richest and poorest.

Before analysis, the data was weighted using the Women’s individual sample weight (DHS variable V005) and predictor variables assessed for multicollinearity. The variable with highest variable inflation factor (VIF) score was wealth quintile at 2.83. As a rule of thumb a VIF < 3 is not a cause for concern and therefore no adjustment were made. SPSS and MsExcel software were used for data analysis.

For the total (spacing or limiting) unmet need for family planning (Model 1), the adjusted odds of unmet needs among women in the lowest quintile were 1.8 times higher than those for women in the highest wealth quintile (AOR = 1.788; 95% CI 1.324–2.414). In Model 2, women in the lower wealth quintiles were more likely to report unmet need for spacing than those in the richest quintile. However, except for women in the poorest quintile, women in the lower quintile were less likely to report an unmet need for limiting than those in the richest quintile.

According to Model 1 above, women aged 15–24 years had the highest odds of unmet needs for family planning compared to women aged 40–49 years were (AOR: 1.569; 95% CI: 1.212, 2.030). Women of younger ages were also more likely to report unmet need for spacing than 40–49-year-olds were. However, they were less likely to report an unmet need for limiting than 40–49-year-olds were.

Overall, women with two or more children who are alive are more likely to report unmet needs for FP than those with one child. Women with six or more children alive, had the highest odds (AOR: 2.414; 95% CI: 1.849, 3.152) of experiencing unmet need compared to those with one child. Similar results were observed for limiting. However, women with fewer children who are alive were more likely to report unmet need for spacing than those with more than six or more children.

The other determinant of unmet need was employment status of respondent; women who were not currently employed were more likely to experience unmet need (AOR: 1.561; 95% CI: 1.375–1.772), and is highest for spacing (AOR: 2.231; 95% CI: 1.872–2.658) than those who were currently employed. With relation to type of place of residence, although not statistically significant, women in urban areas were more likely to report unmet need for spacing, whereas their counterparts in rural areas were more likely to report unmet need for limiting.

In this study, although overall associations were not statistically significant across all education levels, women with no formal education had significantly higher odds of unmet need (AOR: 1.456; 95% CI: 1.022–2.074) than women with higher level of education, and the odds were even higher for unmet need for limiting. Partners level of education was found to determine unmet need for FP. Women whose partners had a lower level of education had significantly lower odds (AOR: 0.581; 95% CI: 0.407–0.829) of experiencing unmet need for family planning than those whose partners had higher education levels.

As shown in Table 4 above, exposure to mass media is a determinant of unmet need for family planning. The odds of experiencing unmet need was higher for women without exposure to mass media (AOR: 1.249; 95% CI: 1.027–1.519) compared to those with exposure.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study is in the use of a large sample size that is nationally representative to assess socioeconomic inequality and its drivers in unmet needs for contraception. However, it is a cross-sectional study whereby the outcomes and predictors reflected the situation at one point in time which prevents establishing causality relations between the dependent and independent variables, but rather a mere association.

Discussion

This study found a 14% overall prevalence of unmet need for family planning in Kenya, with a slightly higher unmet need for spacing (7.6%) compared to limiting (6.4%). This finding is consistent with the findings of previous studies in Kenya [2].

Many studies have reported a positive association between socioeconomic position and health condition [21]. This study revealed significant socioeconomic inequality in the unmet need for FP in Kenya, which is disproportionately concentrated among poorest women than the richest for both spacing and limiting. Age, number of living children, employment status, level of education of partner, and media exposure were found to explain the socioeconomic inequality in unmet need; the gap between the poorest and the richest in Kenya.

The finding that the unmet need for FP among young women is pro-poor agrees with the findings of [7] and [7, 22]. reported that young women in sub-Saharan Africa in the richest wealth quintile had lower odds of unmet need for contraception than those in the poorest wealth quintile, whereas [22] reported that young women with low uptake of FP mainly come from vulnerable families and communities. To increase uptake among the poor women, contraceptives should be made affordable or given to them free of charge.

The reasons advanced in literature for low uptake of FP among young people include lack of desire to avoid early childbearing because it is culturally/socially acceptable, lack of desire to use contraception for fear of side effects, a lack of confidence and ability to seek/negotiate contraception use for lack of self-assurance or independence, and poor access [22]. This suggests a need for targeted educational interventions addressing knowledge gaps and sociocultural barriers related to family planning.

The number of living children has been identified as a determinant of unmet needs in many studies, including this one. A high number of living children was also found to contribute to the observed socioeconomic inequality in the unmet need for FP. This is not surprising, as empirical findings indicate a negative correlation between the level of education and poverty on the one hand and (desired) family size [23]; women with a low level of education or living in poverty have been found to desire and have more children. Poverty may also negatively impact access and affordability [22, 23]. Having a large number of children also denies a woman an opportunity to gainful employment and hence resources to procure FP commodities. An educational family planning program that emphasizes social and economic benefits of FP is therefore recommended.

The pro-poor inequality in rural areas may be explained by limited supplies due to distance, limited awareness due to educational barriers, as the level of education is lower in rural areas, rural poverty, whereby income levels are low, and high fertility preferences influenced by cultural norms [23]. Increasing supplies and bring them closer to people in rural areas would improve the situation.

Conclusion and recommendations

There is socioeconomic inequality in unmet needs for FP in Kenya. The major contributors to pro-poor socioeconomic inequality are the number of children alive, the type of place of residence, level of education of both women and partner and media exposure. Unmet need for FP is highest among young women, living in poor households in rural areas, with no education, unemployed and with no media exposure. To improve uptake among these women and promote equity, the study recommends highly subsidized contraceptive provision in rural clinics, coupled with youth-friendly services and culturally sensitive communication strategies, to reduce unmet need, particularly among young and poor women.
