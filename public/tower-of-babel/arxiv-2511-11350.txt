Risk averse deterministic Kalman filters for uncertain dynamical systems
Karl Kunisch ⋆,† and Jesper Schröder †

Abstract
. Taking a deterministic viewpoint this work investigates extensions of the Kalman-Bucy filter for state reconstruction to systems containing parametric uncertainty in the state operator. The emphasis lies on risk averse designs reducing the probability of large reconstruction errors. In a theoretical analysis error bounds in terms of the variance of the uncertainties are derived. The article concludes with a numerical implementation of two examples allowing for a comparison of risk neutral and risk averse estimators.



Keywords: state estimation, filtering, parameter dependent systems, Kalman filter

AMS subject classification: 34F05, 49N10, 91G70, 93B53, 93C15

1.
Introduction

This work proposes estimators for state reconstruction of linear perturbed dynamical systems of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{\sigma}x(t)+Bv(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\eta,

with unknown, deterministic disturbances
v\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The system matrix
A_{\sigma}
depends on a parametric uncertainties indicated by
\sigma\in\Sigma
. The objective is an online approximation of the system’s state
x
based on incomplete measurements given as

y(t)=Cx(t)+\mu(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

that are affected by the output disturbance
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{r})
.

Utilizing flawed measurements to estimate the state of systems suffering from perturbances is of high interest in the applied sciences at least since Wiener’s seminal work
[
27
]
.
Kalman’s and Bucy’s work in
[
15
]
is tailored to the linear setting, for known parameter
\sigma
, and modeling the disturbances as Gaussian white noise, see also
[
16
]
for the time-discrete analogue. The so-called Kalman-Bucy filter is easy to implement, numerically efficient, and widely applied.

Since the parametric uncertainty introduces nonlinearities and multiplicative noise, it is not directly applicable to the class of systems considered here. In this work a risk averse extension is proposed based on Mortensen’s strategy of recasting the Kalman filter as the energy minimal state
[
21
]
. Specifically, we study probability measures with respect to the parameter of the energy. The measures of interest are the entropic risk measure and the essential supremum for which the minimizers are characterized based on first order optimality conditions. The proposed measures utilize the energies for each system corresponding to a parameter
\sigma\in\Sigma
separately, followed by a variational criterion which determines a universal state-estimator for the ensemble.
An important part of our work consists in establishing estimates on the difference between the proposed state estimator and that estimator which would be optimal if we knew the exact parameter
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
For both measures we analyze their temporal regularity. The entropic risk measure depends on a risk aversion parameter
\theta>0
. We verify that for
\theta\to 0
the resulting estimator converges to the risk neutral estimator and for
\theta\to\infty
we obtain convergence to the minimizer of the essential supremum.
In the numerical part of our work the proposed state estimators are compared to results emerging from the risk neutral approach associated with the expectation which we investigated in earlier work
[
17
]
.

Let us further comment on the two measures which we investigate.
The entropic risk measure was introduced in the context of financial mathematics to optimize portfolios with a flexible level of risk aversion
[
11
]
. More recently it was adapted for optimal control under uncertainty
[
12
,
13
]
leading to risk averse control strategies reducing the likelihood of large costs. This functional also arises in many other applied sciences including machine learning and neural networks
[
4
]
, or statistical mechanics,
[
23
]
, for example.
It is well known that for infinitely large risk aversion the entropic risk measure corresponds to the essential supremum which in the framework of this work turns into a maximum. The associated estimator is hence given by the solution of a min-max problem which on a technical level is closely related
to the minimum ball problem arising in operations research. In the 2-dimensional case this problem has a long history, see for instance
[
14
]
and the references provided there. The
\mathbb{R}^{n}
case was investigated for instance in
[
5
,
7
]
. These papers also contain many references on the algorithmic developments for this class of problems. In this paper, to analyze the min-max formulation as well as the entropic risk formulation we follow a different analytical route by treating these problems by convex analysis techniques. In this way we can use results from
[
20
]
to obtain existence and uniqueness of solutions and from
[
22
]
to derive optimality conditions, in an elegant manner.
The latter are of vital importance for the error analysis which we provide.

2.
The Kalman filter

We begin with a brief presentation of two different derivations of the Kalman-Bucy filter for time-invariant systems. While our proposed estimators rely only on the deterministic formulation a recap of the stochastic formulation enriches the interpretation of our approach.
We include these well known concepts for the purpose of a self-contained work.

2.1.
The stochastic formulation

We illustrate the stochastic filter based on the original work
[
15
]
and refer to
[
31
,
29
]
for a more rigorous treatment in terms of SDEs.
Consider the disturbed system with system state
X_{t}
and measured output
Y_{t}
modeled by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}X_{t}

\displaystyle=AX_{t}+BV_{t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=CX_{t}+W_{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

with system matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
, noise input matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
, and output matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{r,n}
.
The dynamics and the measurement are disturbed by the multivariate Gaussian random variables
V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, respectively. The so-called white noise terms have zero mean and covariances given by

\displaystyle\text{cov}[V_{t},V_{s}]

\displaystyle=R\,\delta(t-s),

\displaystyle\text{cov}[W_{t},W_{s}]

\displaystyle=Q\,\delta(t-s),

where
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m,m}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{r,r}
are given symmetric, positive definite matrices.
The distribution of the state at time
t
is Gaussian and denoted by
X_{t}
, where the initial distribution
X_{0}
is characterized via its mean
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
, symmetric, positive definite. The two noise terms and the initial distribution are assumed to be mutually independent and finally
Y_{t}
denotes the distribution of the output at time
t
.

According to
[
15
]
the goal is to optimally estimate the realized state
x(t)
of the system given realized, measured outputs
y(s)
,
0\leq s\leq t
.
In their work Kalman and Bucy show that the optimal estimate
\widehat{x}(t)
is given via

(2.2)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=A\widehat{x}(t)+\Pi(t)C^{\top}Q^{-1}(y(t)-C\widehat{x}(t))\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\widehat{x}(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

where
\Pi
is the unique solution to the differential Riccati equation

(2.3)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}(t)

\displaystyle=A\Pi(t)+\Pi(t)A^{\top}-\Pi(t)C^{\top}Q^{-1}C\Pi(t)+BRB^{\top}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\Pi(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma.

In the stochastic interpretation, given a measured output the state of the system at time
t
is estimated via the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{x}(t),\Pi(t))
where the mean and covariance are characterized via (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), respectively.

2.2.
The deterministic formulation

In this subsection we consider a deterministic model of a disturbed system brought forward by Mortensen in
[
21
]
, see also
[
28
]
. It reads

(2.4)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+Bv(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\eta,

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=Cx(t)+\mu(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle t\in(0,T),

where the system matrices
A
,
B
, and
C
are as in (
2.1
). The disturbances in dynamics, initial value, and output are modeled by
v\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{r})
and are assumed to be deterministic and unknown.
To increase readability for the remainder of this work we denote
\mathcal{L}_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{p}=L^{p}(t_{1},t_{2};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{H}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=H^{1}(t_{1},t_{2};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
0<p\leq\infty
and

The strategy for estimating
x(t)
, at
t\in(0,T]
consists in identifying the energy minimal disturbances
\eta^{*}[t]
,
v^{*}[t]
, and
\mu^{*}[t]
that fit the measured output
y(s)
,
0\leq s\leq t
, i.e., in solving

(2.5)

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in\mathcal{L}_{0,t}^{2},\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{0,t}^{2}}\penalty

\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)\|_{R^{-1}}^{2}+\|\mu(s)\|_{Q^{-1}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\penalty\ \eqref{eq: detModel}\penalty\ \text{on}\penalty\ (0,t).

This minimizer is used to solve (
2.4
) for the associated trajectory
x^{*}[t]
and the estimator is defined as
\widehat{x}(t)=x^{*}[t](t)
. Here and in the following for a positive definite matrix
M
and vector
x
of fitting size we denote
\|x\|_{M}^{2}=x^{\top}Mx
.
The matrices
\Gamma
,
R
, and
Q
correspond to the noise covariances from the stochastic formulation. Using their inverses as weighting matrices is a strategy also used in generalized least squares
[
1
]
.
In
[
28
]
Willems demonstrates that this estimator is given precisely by the Kalman filter equations known from the stochastic formulation. Indeed
\widehat{x}
solves

(2.6)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=A\widehat{x}(t)+\Pi(t)C^{\top}Q^{-1}\left(y(t)-C\widehat{x}(t)\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\widehat{x}(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

where
\Pi
is given as the unique solution of

(2.7)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}(t)

\displaystyle=A\Pi(t)+\Pi(t)A^{\top}-\Pi(t)C^{\top}Q^{-1}C\Pi(t)+BRB^{\top}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\Pi(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma.

Our analysis follows the slightly different formulation of
[
21
]
.
By adding a final state
\xi
and inserting the identities for
x(0)
and
y
Mortensen recasts (
2.5
) into a problem of optimal control. It reads

(2.8)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{t},v\in\mathcal{L}_{0,t}^{2}}\penalty\ J(x,v;t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\|x(0)-x_{0}\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)\|_{R^{-1}}^{2}+\|y-Cx(s)\|_{Q^{-1}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \dot{x}(s)

\displaystyle=Ax(s)+Bv(s)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ s\in(0,t),

(2.10)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ x(t)

\displaystyle=\xi

with associated value function

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{t},v\in\mathcal{L}_{0,t}^{2}}J(x,v;t,\xi)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{subject to}\penalty\ \eqref{eq: state}-\eqref{eq: FV}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T],

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=\|\xi-x_{0}\|_{\Gamma^{-1}}^{2}.

It represents the minimal amount of energy required to ensure that at time
t
the system is in the state
\xi
. In
[
17
, Lem. 2.5]
it is shown that in the present case of linear dynamics the value function
\mathcal{V}
is quadratic in space and given as

(2.12)

\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)=(\xi-\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}\Pi^{-1}(t)(\xi-\widehat{x}(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\|y(s)-C\widehat{x}(s)\|_{Q^{-1}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
\widehat{x}
and
\Pi
are given via the Kalman filter equations (
2.6
) and (
2.7
). Hence the estimator
\widehat{x}
constructed via (
2.5
) can equivalently be characterized as

\widehat{x}(t)\coloneqq\argmin_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi).

We point out that the control formulation at hand is merely a tool to reconstruct energy minimal disturbances that most likely caused the measured output.

Even though the following derivations and arguments are based on the deterministic perspective, we still refer to the solution
\Pi
of (
2.7
) as the (error) covariance associated with the Kalman filter allowing for an interpretation of our results. We denote the inverse of the covariance as
P\coloneqq\Pi^{-1}
and following
[
8
, Sec. 5.4]
we refer to it as the precision matrix.
By multiplying (
2.7
) with
P(t)
from the left and from the right we find that the precision matrix is characterized via the differential Riccati equation

(2.13)

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)

\displaystyle=-A^{\top}P(t)-P(t)A-P(t)BRB^{\top}P(t)+C^{\top}Q^{-1}C,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle P(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1}.

3.
Kalman filtering for uncertain systems

The Kalman filter described in the previous section assumes exact knowledge of both the system dynamics represented by
A
and the nature of the occurring noise represented by the covariance matrices
\Gamma
,
R
, and
Q
. In practical applications, however, this information may not be available in exact form and the matrices may depend on parameters,
[
19
,
26
]
.

3.1.
Modeling uncertain parameters

Following the description in
[
17
]
the uncertainties of the system matrix
A
are modeled as follows.
Let
s_{A}\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a matrix valued function

a\colon\mathbb{R}^{s_{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,n}.

Further let

N_{A}\in\mathbb{N}

and

\Sigma_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s_{A}}

be of cardinality

|\Sigma_{A}|=N_{A}
. Consider the discrete probability space
(\Sigma_{A},\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{A}),P_{A})

, where

\mathcal{P_{A}}(\Sigma_{A})

denotes the power set of
\Sigma_{A}
and for
S\subset\Sigma_{A}
define the probability function

P_{A}(S)=\frac{1}{N_{A}}|S|
.
Then the linear dynamics of the system are characterized by the matrix valued random variable

\sigma\in\Sigma_{A}\mapsto a_{\sigma}=a(\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n},

and
a_{\sigma}
denotes the system matrix that realizes with the parameter
\sigma\in\Sigma_{A}
.
The uncertain weighting matrices
\gamma_{\sigma}
,
r_{\sigma}
, and
q_{\sigma}
are defined as random variables on
\Sigma_{\Gamma}
,
\Sigma_{R}
, and
\Sigma_{Q}
with respective cardinalities and dimensions
N_{\Gamma}
,
s_{\Gamma}
,
N_{R}
,
s_{R}
, and
N_{Q}
,
s_{Q}
. The associated probabilities are defined as that for
A
. We denote
N=N_{A}\,N_{\Gamma}\,N_{R}\,N_{Q}
and
s=s_{A}\,s_{\Gamma}\,s_{R}\,s_{Q}
. Then

\Sigma=\Sigma_{A}\times\Sigma_{\Gamma}\times\Sigma_{R}\times\Sigma_{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}

has cardinality
N
. Further we define the product space
(\Sigma,\mathcal{F},P)
, via

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{A})\otimes\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{\Gamma})\otimes\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{R})\otimes\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{Q})=\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),

\displaystyle P(S_{A}\otimes S_{\Gamma}\otimes S_{R}\otimes S_{Q})

\displaystyle=P_{A}(S_{A})\,P_{\Gamma}(S_{\Gamma})\,P_{R}(S_{R})\,P_{Q}(S_{Q})=\frac{1}{|S_{A}\otimes S_{\Gamma}\otimes S_{R}\otimes S_{Q}|}.

Finally the uncertain system and weighting matrices are defined as random variables on
(\Sigma,\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),P)
, where for
S\subset\Sigma
we set
P(S)=\tfrac{1}{N}|S|
. Denote
\sigma=(\sigma_{A},\sigma_{\Gamma},\sigma_{R},\sigma_{Q})\in\Sigma
and define

\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma\mapsto A_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=a_{\sigma_{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n},\quad\sigma\in\Sigma\mapsto\Gamma_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\sigma_{\Gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n},

\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma\mapsto R_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=r_{\sigma_{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m,m},\quad\sigma\in\Sigma\mapsto Q_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=q_{\sigma_{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r,r}.

Note that by construction they are mutually independent.
For convenience we denote the
N
elements of
\Sigma
by
\sigma_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,N
and dependencies on
\sigma_{k}
may be indicated by indexing
k
, e.g.,
A_{\sigma_{k}}=A_{k}
. Also, for a given parameter
\sigma
the associated four-tuple consisting of system matrix and covariance matrices is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}=(A_{{\sigma}},\Gamma_{{\sigma}},R_{{\sigma}},Q_{{\sigma}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n,n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m,m}\times\mathbb{R}^{r,r}
. To quantify the distance between such tuples for
p\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the norm

\|\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}\|_{p}^{p}=\|A_{\sigma}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n,n}}^{p}+\|\Gamma_{\sigma}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n,n}}^{p}+\|R_{\sigma}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m,m}}^{p}+\|Q_{\sigma}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{r,r}}^{p}
.

For any
\sigma_{k}\in\Sigma
the corresponding Kalman filter is denoted by
\widehat{x}_{k}
and the associated covariance and precision matrices are denoted by
\Pi_{k}
and
P_{k}=\Pi_{k}^{-1}
, respectively. They are characterized via

(3.1)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{k}\widehat{x}_{k}(t)+\Pi_{k}(t)C^{\top}Q_{k}^{-1}\left(y(t)-C\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{k}\Pi_{k}(t)+\Pi_{k}(t)A_{k}^{\top}-\Pi_{k}(t)C^{\top}Q_{k}^{-1}C\Pi_{k}(t)+BR_{k}B^{\top}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{k}.

We note that the solution
\Pi_{k}
is infinitely often continuously differentiable. Since the same holds for the mapping
M\mapsto M^{-1}
defined on the set of symmetric positive definite matrices, we obtain that for all
k
we have

(3.3)

P_{k}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n,n}).

To make our arguments more concise, we denote for
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
t\in[0,T]

r_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\|y(s)-C\widehat{x}_{k}(s)\|_{Q_{k}^{-1}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Analogous to (
2.12
) we obtain

(3.4)

\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)=\|x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t).

Next the estimators which will be investigated are described.

3.2.
Minimizing the expected energy

We begin by presenting a design that was proposed and studied in an earlier work, cf.
[
17
]
.
For
t\in[0,T]
consider the minimization problem

(3.5)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(t,{x})\right]=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\penalty\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x).

This minimization problem defines the first estimator. In
[
17
, Prop. 3.4]
we showed existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
3.5
) and throughout this work we denote the estimator defined as the pointwise solution of (
3.5
) as
\widehat{x}_{0}(t)
.
We further cite the following characterization.

Proposition 3.1
.

The state estimator
\widehat{x}_{0}
is well-defined and given in terms of the Kalman filter trajectories and precision matrices associated with the individual Kalman filters. For
t\in[0,T]
it holds

(3.6)

\widehat{x}_{0}(t)=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(t)\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)\,\widehat{x}_{k}(t),

where
\mathcal{P}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)
.

This representation immediately implies a result on the regularity of the estimator.

Corollary 3.2
.

The estimator
\widehat{x}_{0}
is square integrable and admits a square integrable weak derivative, i.e.,
\widehat{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{T}
. Its weak derivative is given by

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{0}(t)=-\mathcal{P}^{-1}(t)\,\dot{\mathcal{P}}(t)\,\widehat{x}_{0}(t)+\mathcal{P}^{-1}(t)\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)\dot{\widehat{x}}(t)+\dot{P}_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t).

Proof.

The regularity of
P_{k}
presented in (
3.3
) implies
\mathcal{P}^{-1}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n,n})
. For the solutions of (
3.1
) we have
\widehat{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{T}
. The claim for
\widehat{x}_{0}
follows from the representation given in (
3.6
). The formula for the weak derivative is obtained by an application of the chain rule and (
3.6
).
∎

We proceed by introducing more risk averse designs.

3.3.
Minimizing the worst case energy

For the construction of the second estimator consider the problem of minimizing the worst case energy

(3.7)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\penalty\ \esssup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(t,x)=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\penalty\ \max_{k=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x).

We begin by ensuring existence of a unique solution at any time.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
the problem (
3.7
) admits a unique solution.

Proof.

The value functions
\mathcal{V}_{k}
defined in (
3.4
) are strictly convex and coercive in
x
. Since the maximum in (
3.7
) is taken over finitely many indices
k
, the expression
\max_{k}\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,\cdot)
inherits the strict convexity and coercivity properties. As a consequence, the existence of a unique minimizer then follows.
∎

This result enables the definition of the second estimator.

Definition 3.4
.

The state estimator minimizing the worst case energy at time
t\in[0,T]
is denoted as
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
and given by the unique solution of the minimization problem (
3.7
).

In the following we characterize the minimizer via a representation in terms of the family members
\widehat{x}_{k}
. This is done for every time point individually. Hence, from here on we fix a time
t\in[0,T]
.
To facilitate the discussion using tools from convex analysis we introduce the following notation. We define

f[t]\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ f[t](x)=\max_{k=1,\dots,N}\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x).

Now solving (
3.7
) for the fixed time
t
is equivalent to solving

(3.8)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f[t](x).

To formulate the first order optimality condition we introduce the set of active indices.

Definition 3.5
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
we define

I[t](x)=\{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}\colon\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)=f[t](x)\},

i.e.,
I[t](x)
is the set of all indices for which the maximum is attained.

With these definitions at hand we are able to characterize the minimizer
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
be arbitrary.
There exists a subset
S(t)\subset I[t](\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t))
with cardinality
|S(t)|=p\leq n+1
and coefficients
\alpha(t)\in[0,1]^{p}
such that it holds

\displaystyle(i)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S(t)}\alpha_{k}(t)=1,

\displaystyle(ii)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\left\{P_{k}(t)(\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\right\}_{k\in S(t)}\penalty\ \text{are affine independent},

\displaystyle(iii)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)=\left(\sum_{k\in S(t)}\alpha_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k\in S}\alpha_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t).

In other words the estimator can be characterized in terms of a subset of
n+1
or fewer family members in which the maximum is attained.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
t\in[0,T]
. Then
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
is the unique solution of (
3.8
). As discussed in the proof of
Lemma

3.3

f[t]
is strictly convex, hence
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
satisfies the optimality condition
[
9
, Def. 5.1. and (5.5)]
given by

0\in\partial f[t](\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)),

where
\partial f[t](\bar{x})
denotes the subdifferential of
f[t]
at
\bar{x}
. The subdifferential of a maximum is well known, cf.
[
24
, Prop. 4.5.2 and Rem. 4.5.3]
. Denoting the convex hull of a set
X
by
\mathrm{conv}(X)
we indeed have

\displaystyle\partial f[t](\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\{P_{k}(t)(\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\colon k\in I[t](\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t))\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{conv}\{P_{k}(t)(\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\colon k\in S(t)\},

where the second equality is justified by Caratheodory’s theorem with a set
S(t)\subset I[t](\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t))
such that
(ii)
holds. The affine independence in particular implies
|S(t)|\leq n+1
. The optimality condition now ensures existence of
\alpha(t)\in[0,1]^{p}
such that
(i)
holds and

0=\sum_{k\in S(t)}\alpha_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)(\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)).

Rearranging terms yields
(iii)
and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

This representation does not yield any time regularity of
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
. Since there is no straight forward way of characterizing the time dependence of the coefficients
\alpha_{k}
, at this point we can not even deduce measurability of the estimator. A result regarding this is obtained via a convergence argument in
Corollary

4.2
below.

The two presented estimators
\widehat{x}_{0}
and
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
are the two extremes on the spectrum of risk aversion. While the minimizer of the expected value
\widehat{x}_{0}
does not take risk into account at all, the worst-case minimizer
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
is by construction the most risk averse. In the following we introduce a concept filling the gap in between these two extremes.

3.4.
Minimizing the entropic risk of the energy

In this subsection we introduce another estimator as the minimizer of the so-called
entropic risk measure
. To the best of the authors’ knowledge it was introduced in the context of financial mathematics, cf.
[
11
]
. We commence with a brief presentation of the concept and its properties for the setting of this work. For a more general definition and detailed derivation we refer to
[
11
]
.
This functional is also important in the context of machine learning, cf. for example
[
4
]
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
X\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
be a random variable on the probability space
(\Sigma,\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),P)
defined in
Section

3.1
. The entropic risk measure of
X
with risk aversion
\theta\in(0,\infty)
is defined as

\rho_{\theta}(X)=\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\theta X}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta X(\sigma_{k})}\right).

We proceed by presenting some well known properties of the entropic risk measure. While we do not claim any novelty, for the sake of completeness we give a proof tailored to the setting of this work.

Lemma 3.9
.

For all random variables
X
on the probability space
(\Sigma,\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),P)
it holds

\rho_{\theta}(X)\to\esssup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}X(\sigma)=\max_{k=1,\dots,N}X(\sigma_{k})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
X
be such a fixed random variable and let
k^{*}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
be such that
X(\sigma_{k^{*}})=\max_{k=1,\dots,N}X(\sigma_{k})=\esssup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}X(\sigma)
.
Since the exponential is positive, for all
\theta\in(0,\infty)
we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta X(\sigma_{k})}\geq\frac{1}{N}e^{\theta X(\sigma_{k^{*}})}.

Applying the logarithm on both sides and dividing by
\theta
yields

\rho_{\theta}(X)\geq X(\sigma_{k^{*}})+\frac{\ln(\tfrac{1}{N})}{\theta}.

We hence find that for all
\theta\in(0,\infty)
it holds

X(\sigma_{k^{*}})+\frac{\ln(\tfrac{1}{N})}{\theta}\leq\rho_{\theta}(X)\leq X(\sigma_{k^{*}}),

where the second estimate is justified by the monotonicity of the logarithm and the exponential. The assertion follows by considering the limit for
\theta\to\infty
.
∎

We now turn to the construction of the risk averse estimator.
For its definition we consider the following minimization problem. For a fixed
t\in[0,T]
consider

(3.9)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(t,x)\right)=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}\right).

We proceed to formally define the third estimator as the minimizer of the entropic risk applied to the energy.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
y\in\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{2}
and
t\in[0,T]
. The state estimator minimizing the entropic risk of the energy with risk aversion parameter
\theta\in(0,\infty)
is denoted by
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
and defined as the solution of (
3.9
), assuming it exists and is unique.

The following lemma shows that the estimator is well defined and characterizes it via an implicit equation.

Lemma 3.11
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
,
y\in\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{2}
, and
\theta\in(0,\infty)
there exists exactly one solution to the minimization problem (
3.9
). The solution, denoted by
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
, satisfies

(3.10)

\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)

and is the only element of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying this relation.

Proof.

Let
t\in[0,T]
be fixed. For the purpose of this proof we define
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
via

F(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}\right).

In the following we show that
F
is strictly convex. To that end note that it is smooth and the chain rule yields its gradient as

(3.11)

\nabla F(x)=2\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)\penalty\ P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t).

To characterize the Hessian we denote the outer product of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with itself via
[x]_{\otimes}^{2}=x\otimes x=xx^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
and define

(3.12)

0\leq c_{k}[\theta,x](t)=\frac{e^{\theta(\|x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|x-\widehat{x}_{j}(t)\|_{P_{j}(t)}^{2}+r_{j}(t))}}\leq 1.

Note that for all
x
and
t
it holds
\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)=1
. The chain rule then yields the Hessian as

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(x)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)P_{k}(t)+4\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)\left[P_{k}(t)(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\right]_{\otimes}^{2}

\displaystyle-4\theta\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\right]_{\otimes}^{2}.

Now for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
we find

\displaystyle z^{\top}\nabla^{2}F(x)z

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)\penalty\ z^{\top}P_{k}(t)z+4\theta\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)\|z^{\top}P_{k}(t)(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle-4\theta\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)z^{\top}P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|^{2}.

Due to the positive definiteness of
P_{k}
the first summand is greater than zero. Since the
c_{k}
are non negative and sum up to one, the convexity of the squared norm ensures that the sum of the last two terms is greater or equal than zero. Hence, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F(x)
is positive definite implying strict convexity of
F
.
One can quickly verify that
F
also is coercive ensuring that it admits exactly one minimizer. Further, any element
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
minimizes
F
if and only if
\nabla F(\bar{x})=0
. Setting the gradient given in (
3.11
) to zero and performing standard calculus yields the claimed equation for
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
.
∎

Note that an alternative to the characterization via (
3.10
) is given by

(3.13)

\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)](t)P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)](t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)

with the coefficients given as in (
3.12
) satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}=1
and
c_{k}\in[0,1]
.

We conclude the section by stating a result on the time regularity of
\widehat{x}_{\theta}
depending on the regularity of the output
y
. The proof is presented in
Section

A.2
.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
\theta\in(0,\infty)
.

(i)

Assuming
y\in\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{2p}
with an integer
1\leq p<\infty
it holds
\widehat{x}_{\theta}\in W^{1,p}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(ii)

For
y\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{r})
it holds
\widehat{x}_{\theta}\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In both settings the (weak) derivative is given via the formula

(3.14)

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\theta}(t)=-M(t,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t))^{-1}V(t,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)),

where

(3.15)

\displaystyle M(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)}\left(P_{k}(t)+2\theta P_{k}(t)(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\otimes(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))P_{k}(t)\right),

and

(3.16)

\displaystyle V(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)}\,\left(P_{k}(t)\dot{\widehat{x}}_{k}(t)-\dot{P}_{k}(t)(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t))\right)

\displaystyle+\theta\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)}\left(\|y(t)-C\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{Q_{k}^{-1}}^{2}-2\langle x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t),P_{k}(t)\dot{\widehat{x}}_{k}(t)\rangle\right)P_{k}(t)(x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)).

4.
Continuity of the state estimator in the risk aversion parameter

In this section we analyze the behavior of the limit cases of the risk aversion parameter. We find that indeed,
\widehat{x}_{\theta}
fills the gap between
\widehat{x}_{0}
and
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
in the sense that the estimators are continuous with respect to the risk aversion, in particular for the extreme cases.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
be fixed. It holds

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)

\displaystyle\to\widehat{x}_{0}(t),\penalty\

\displaystyle\theta\to 0,

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)

\displaystyle\to\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\theta\to\infty,

where
\widehat{x}_{0}
and
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
are the estimators introduced in
Section

3.2
and
Definition

3.4
, respectively.
Moreover
\theta\mapsto\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
is continuous in
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

To show the first limit we note that due to
Lemma

A.1
and
Lemma

A.3
in the appendix we have that
\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}
is bounded with respect to
\theta
. Since additionally
r_{k}[y](t)
is independent of
\theta
, we find

\theta\left(\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}[y](t)\right)\to 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to 0.

Since all involved operations are continuous, passing to the limit in (
3.10
) reveals

\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)\to\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\penalty\ \sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)\penalty\ \widehat{x}_{k}(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to 0

and the first claim follows with
Proposition

3.1
.

To show the second convergence we utilize the characterization of
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
given in (
3.13
) which reads as

(4.1)

\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)](t)P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)](t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t),

where the coefficients
0\leq c_{k}[\theta,x](t)\leq 1
are defined as in (
3.12
).
Now let
\theta_{l}
be an arbitrary sequence such that
\theta_{l}\to\infty
for
l\to\infty
. Since the
c_{k}[\theta_{l},\widehat{x}_{\theta_{l}}(t)](t)
are from a compact interval, any subsequence admits yet another subsequence
\theta_{g}
such that for any
k
we have that
c_{k}[\theta_{g},\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)](t)
converges for
g\to\infty
. We denote the corresponding limits by
\beta_{k}(t)
. Since by construction for every
g\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,T]
the elements
\{c_{k}[\theta_{g},\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)](t)\}_{k=1}^{N}
are non negative and sum up to one, the same holds for
\{\beta_{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{N}
. With (
4.1
) it follows

\lim_{g\to\infty}\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\eqqcolon\bar{x}.

Note that at this point
\bar{x}
may depend on the choice of the subsequence
\theta_{g}
.
To remedy this, we now show that in fact
\bar{x}=\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
. To that end denote

F[\theta](x)=\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\theta(\|x-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t))}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ F_{\infty}(x)=\max_{k}\mathcal{V}_{k}(t,x)

and consider

(4.2)

\left\|F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t))-F_{\infty}(\bar{x})\right\|\leq\left\|F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t))-F[\theta_{g}](\bar{x})\right\|+\left\|F[\theta_{g}](\bar{x})-F_{\infty}(\bar{x})\vphantom{F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t))}\right\|.

Utilizing
Lemma

3.9
we find that the second summand on the right hand side converges to zero. To show convergence of the first summand on the right hand side we first estimate the gradient
\|\nabla F[\theta](x)\|
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\theta\in(0,\infty)
. We have

\|\nabla F[\theta](x)\|=2\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}[\theta,x](t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\right\|,

where the coefficients
c_{k}[\theta,x]
are defined as above and hence bounded by one. Due to the continuity of
P_{k}
and
\widehat{x}_{k}
there exists a constant
L>0
independent of
t
,
\theta
,
x
, and
k
such that

\|\nabla F[\theta](x)\|\leq L.

We now apply Taylor’s theorem
[
30
, Thm. 4.C]
and obtain

\displaystyle\left\|F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t))-F[\theta_{g}](\bar{x})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla F[\theta_{g}](\tau\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)+(1-\tau)\bar{x})\|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \|\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq L\|\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)-\bar{x}\|,

where the convergence of
\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)
implies convergence to zero of the right hand side.
We have therefore shown that the right hand side of (
4.2
) converges to zero for
g\to\infty
, implying

\lim_{g\to\infty}F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t))=F_{\infty}(\bar{x}).

As by construction
\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}
minimizes
F[\theta_{g}]
we have

F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}})\leq F[\theta_{g}](\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)).

Applying again
Lemma

3.9
we pass to the limit on both sides and obtain

F_{\infty}(\bar{x})\leq F_{\infty}(\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)).

Since
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
is the unique minimizer of
F_{\infty}
, it follows
\bar{x}=\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
.
We have now shown that any subsequence of
\widehat{x}_{\theta_{l}}(t)
admits a subsequence
\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)
that converges to
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
and the second convergence claim is shown.

It remains to show the continuity for
\theta\in(0,\infty)
.
This follows from the fact that for each
t\in[0,T]
this mapping is uniformly bounded with respect to
\theta
, see
Lemma

A.3
. This allows to extract converging subsequences for families
\theta\to\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
with
\theta\to\bar{\theta}
for some
\bar{\theta}\in(0,\infty)
. Subsequently one can pass to the limit in (
3.10
) to assert that this limit is necessarily
\widehat{x}_{\bar{\theta}}(t)
.
∎

The arguments made in the proof further yield a representation of
\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)
with coefficients that are measurable in time. We obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.2
.

The worst case minimizer
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
is measurable and essentially bounded, and hence
\widehat{x}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Above it was shown that it holds

\widehat{x}_{\infty}(t)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t),

where
\beta_{k}(t)
are the pointwise limits of
c_{k}[\theta_{g},\widehat{x}_{\theta_{g}}(t)](t)
as defined in (
3.12
). Since the
c_{k}[\theta_{g},\widehat{x}_{\theta,}(t)]
are measurable, so are the
\beta_{k}(t)
. As discussed in the proof of
Proposition

4.1
we have
0\leq\beta_{k}(t)\leq 1
. Moreover
P_{k}
and
\widehat{x}_{k}
are continuous in
t\in[0,T]
. Consequently
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)\widehat{x}_{k}(t)
and
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)
are measurable.
According to
Lemma

A.2

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)
is invertible with an inverse bounded uniformly in
t
. The continuity of
M\mapsto M^{-1}
on positive definite matrices implies measurability of
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}(t)P_{k}(t)
.
These facts imply the measurability and uniform boundedness of
\hat{x}_{\infty}.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Using the affine independence established in
Theorem

3.6
one could show a relation between the here discussed representation via
\beta_{k}
and the one via
\alpha_{k}
as presented in
Theorem

3.6
. Since this does not offer any further insight towards the properties of
\widehat{x}_{\infty}
, we omit the required technical arguments.

Due to the
\theta
-uniform bound on
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
the pointwise convergence implies convergence in appropriate function spaces.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
y\in\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{2}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq p<\infty
. It holds

\displaystyle\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}-\widehat{x}_{0}\|_{W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\to 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to 0,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}-\widehat{x}_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{p}}

\displaystyle\to 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to\infty.

Proof.

We first consider the limit for
\theta\to\infty
. The pointwise convergence established in
Proposition

4.1
together with the
\theta
-uniform bound from
Lemma

A.3
allow for an application of the dominated convergence theorem yielding the asserted convergence in
\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{p}

.

The exact same argument yields convergence of
\widehat{x}_{\theta}
to
\widehat{x}_{0}
in
\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{1}
for
\theta\to 0
. It remains to show

convergence of the derivatives. According to
Theorem

3.12
for all
\theta\in(0,\infty)
the weak derivative of
\widehat{x}_{\theta}
exists, lies in
\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{1}
and for almost all
t\in[0,T]
is given by the formula (
3.14
). Passing to the limit
\theta\to 0
in said formula and comparing the outcome with the formula given in
Corollary

3.2
we find that we have pointwise convergence almost everywhere, i.e., for almost all

it holds

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\theta}(t)\to\dot{\widehat{x}}_{0}(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to 0.

It remains to establish an integrable upper bound that dominates
\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\theta}(t)
almost everywhere. Denoting the uniform bound for
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)
established in
Lemma

A.3
by
C=\hat{C}(\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\|y\|_{\mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{2}})^{2}
and utilizing (
3.14
) we find

\|\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\theta}(t)\|\leq\|M(\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t),t)^{-1}\|_{2}\|V(\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t),t)\|,

with
M
and
V
as in (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), respectively. We begin by estimating the first factor. In the following, for two symmetric matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
we write
M_{1}\geq M_{2}
whenever
M_{1}-M_{2}
is positive semi-definite. From the definition of
M
we deduce that

M(\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t),t)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(t)\geq P_{1}(t).

It follows

(4.3)

\|M(\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t),t)^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\|P_{1}(t)\|_{2}\leq\max_{s\in[0,T]}\|P_{1}(s)\|_{2}=c_{1}

with
c_{1}
independent of
t
and
\theta
. Turning to

the only critical term is

(4.4)

\theta\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\|\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|_{P_{k}(t)}^{2}+r_{k}(t)}\|y(t)-C\widehat{x}_{k}(t)\|^{2}P_{k}(t)(\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)-\widehat{x}_{k}(t)).

The remaining ones are either continuous in

or they can be uniformly estimated using
Lemma

A.3
. Note in particular that
\theta
appearing as factor does not pose any issue, as we are considering the limit for
\theta\to 0
. Turning to (

)
we use the known bounds for

,
\widehat{x}_{k}
and
\widehat{x}_{\theta}(t)

this term can be bounded by an

function.

We have now shown that

converges pointwise almost everywhere and is pointwise almost everywhere bounded by an integrable function.
Consequently the theorem of dominated convergence yields

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\theta}\to\dot{\widehat{x}}_{0}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \theta\to 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ \mathcal{L}_{0,T}^{1}.

Together with the
L^{1}
convergence of
\widehat{x}_{\theta}
to
\widehat{x}_{0}

\widehat{x}_{\theta}

∎

We consider disturbed uncertain linear systems of the form

7.
Conclusion

This work presents a novel approach for risk averse state estimation under uncertainty for linear, time-invariant, finite-dimensional systems. Investigating analogous concepts for time-dependent, nonlinear systems as well as for systems governed by PDEs could be the focus of future research.

This section contains auxiliary results required for the analysis of this work. The first lemma is concerned with the eigenvalues of the precision matrices and their impact on the weighted norms.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
t\in[0,T]
, and
\lambda_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
be an eigenvalue of
P_{k}(t)
, where
P_{k}
are the precision matrices as introduced in
Section

3.1
.
There exist real numbers
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}},\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}>0
independent of
t
and
k
such that

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\leq\lambda_{k}(t)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}.

In particular for every
