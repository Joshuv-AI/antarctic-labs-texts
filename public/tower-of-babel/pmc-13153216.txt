Cerebro Wave Bee transformer to leverage EEG based hand movement classification using optimized bio-inspired learning rates
Authors: N Priyadharshini Jayadurga, M Chandralekha, Hema Subramaniam

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectroencephalogram (EEG) based classification of hand movements have an eloquent significance in the diverse fields like biomedical engineering, neuroscience, and assistive technologies. EEG can be used to convert brain waves into useful commands supporting multiple tasks. The article introduces a novel EEG-based hand-movement classification using Honey Bee Optimization (HBO) as an offline hyperparameter optimization that finds the best learning rate to be used in the Transformer model before training. Conventional learning rate techniques are unable to adapt to the fluctuations in the dynamics of training in transformer models, hindering the potential of deep learning models. The suggested architecture involves the application of HBO to optimize the learning rate prior to training, which allows the model to stabilize better and makes it a part of the obtained accuracy of 95.60%. This facilitates a more stable convergence and the classification of EEG hand-movements. The Bio-inspired optimization techniques ensures that machine learning models can be optimized to increase their performance and flexibility. The research can be applied to current medical signal analysis and new breakthroughs in neurotechnology whereby accurate hand movement identification is essential.Keywords: Electroencephalogram, Honey Bee Optimization, Transformer model, Bio-inspired optimization models, NeurotechnologySubject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a medical image technology which records the activity of the brain without the invasive procedures. It involves placing electrodes on the scalp to record the tiny electric charges generated by the neuronal activity in the brain. EEG has been known to have a great temporal resolution, but poor spatial resolution compared to other types of imaging techniques like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or Computed Tomography Scan (CT scans). The conventional methods of eye blink detection in EEG research are based on signal processing and standard machine learning methods to derive patterns1–3. It has greatly enhanced Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) capabilities. BCIs use EEG to read neural signals to facilitate direct brain-to-external device interaction. Hand movement classification with the use of EEG data is one of the most important applications of BCI. This has a profound impact on various fields such as stroke rehabilitation, the working of the prosthetics and the improvement of the human-computer interfaces. Despite the significant potential of EEG-based systems, there exists a set of inherent challenges which lacks their general use and effectiveness. The complexity of neurological signals is one of the major challenges, indicating a considerable amount of individual variability, and the sensitivity of EEG data to a wide range of noise and interference. These factors complicate the process of categorizing EEG signals in high accuracy and reliability. The recent academic works have also pointed at the considerable gaps in research that need to be filled with the aim of developing this field:

Interpretability and model complexity: Transformers are advanced deep learning models that display significant characteristics of effective sequential data analysis, including EEG. The complicated nature of transformer models is difficult to comprehend which is a possible issue in the field of clinical applications whose outcomes are highly valued regarding trust and ethics. Also, deep learning models are highly sophisticated integrating both spatial and temporal space, including hybrid-domain methods. This hinders their real-world implementation due to their outstanding computing needs4.

Literature review

Hand movement classification through EEG represents a vital component of BCI applications because noise sensitivity along with inter-subject variability and inefficiency in computation prevents its broad implementation. Transformer models demonstrate promising abilities to detect complex EEG patterns yet their performance efficacy decreases because of fixed learning rates along with significant demands on computing resources. The model’s adaptability gets improved using HBO which serves as bio-inspired dynamic hyperparameter tuning techniques. The existing EEG classification approaches and the integration of Transformer models with bio-inspired algorithms receive evaluation in this section before discussing the innovation of Cerebro-Wave Bee Transformer (CWBT) for addressing these challenges.

EEG based hand movement classification

Hand movements in brain-computer interface (BCI) applications have been extensively classified using EEG signals, supporting prosthetic control, neurorehabilitation, and assistive technologies. Traditional machine learning models including Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests, which depend on handcrafted feature extraction methods including Fourier Transform (FFT) and Wavelet Transform4, are unconventional classification approaches. Due to the substantial inter-subject variability in EEG signals, these techniques cannot be generalized across subjects.

Recent developments in deep learning have facilitated automatic feature extraction from EEG data, thereby boosting classification accuracy. Leveraging the ability of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to extract spatial and temporal features, have been applied for EEG signal classification4. CNNs suffer with long-range dependencies even with these advances and RNNs suffer from vanishing gradient problems, thereby restricting their performance in EEG based classification tasks. Transformer models for EEG classification

Initially developed for natural language processing (NLP), the Transformer model’s self-attention mechanism—which can capture both short-term and long-term dependencies without recurrent connections—has shown promise in EEG signal processing6. Transformer-based models efficiently handle high-dimensional EEG data and analyse global dependencies across EEG channels unlike CNNs and RNNs. Transformer-based designs for EEG classification have been investigated in several works. Transformer-based EEG classification model as stated6 enhance accuracy over CNN and LSTM models. It is still challenging as the computational complexity and hyperparameter sensitivity call for different approaches of optimization. With their self-attention systems, transformer designs have become rather effective for capturing temporal and spatial dependencies in EEG signals. Nevertheless, variations in optimization techniques, data handling, and model complexity cause existing studies to differ in their efficacy. This comparison emphasizes the need of improved models such as CWBT, which combines adaptive bio-inspired learning rate tuning to improve generalization, accuracy, and training economy.

Bio-inspired optimization for deep learning models

Training stability and performance of deep learning models depend critically on hyperparameter tuning. Conventional tuning techniques including random and grid searches are not adaptive during training and computationally costly7. Introduced to dynamically optimize hyperparameters are bio-inspired optimization algorithms including Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). To improve model adaptation, these methods replicate swarm intelligence and natural selection. Inspired by honey bee foraging activity, the Honey Bee Optimization (HBO) algorithm has shown success in dynamic hyperparameter tuning. HBO allows deep learning models to adjust learning rates depending on training progress, so balancing the exploration-exploitation trade-off. Higher classification accuracy in EEG-based applications has been obtained by past studies20 effectively combining HBO with CNNs and XGBoost models.

Especially in terms of convergence and generalizability, these approaches have shown better model performance. Persistent with benefits are the issues including computational cost and parameter sensitivity. By dynamically changing learning rates for strong and efficient classification, the combination of HBO with Transformer architecture in the CWBT model solves these problems.

Both datasets were converted to an integrated 14 × 4 spectral representation per trial to ensure architectural consistency. Although a Transformer encoder is incorporated, the suggested model does not acquire temporal dependencies over time. The EEG trial is represented by the 14 × 4 spectral matrix which encompasses 14 EEG channels treated as tokens and the powers of 4 frequency-bands form the token features. As a result, the self-attention mechanism approximates spatial-spectral inter-channel interactions, but not time dynamics of the EEG signal. The reason behind this design decision was the need to provide architectural regularity among datasets with heterogeneous sampling rates, trial lengths, and preprocessing pipelines for the fair evaluation of the datasets. This enables the model to systematically process each EEG channel as a sequence token.

This is the fundamental dataset used in the study. It was accessed from Kaggle26 on August 25, 2024. In this data, every trial is classified into one of three categories, namely, left-hand movement, right-hand movement, or no-movement (rest). Kaggle dataset has the following distribution of classes: Class 0 (no-movement) has 2421 samples, Class 1 (left movement) has 2346 samples, and Class 2 (right movement) has 2389 samples, which means that the dataset is well-balanced. In the case of the BCI Competition IV 2a, on pre-processing and segmentation, one has 576 trials in each of the three classes, namely rest baseline, left-hand image, and right-hand image, which produces an ideal structure of classes. These are precisely given numbers so that balance of classes and reproducibility can be assessed. These classes constitute the foundation of the multi-class classification task which is the focus of the present research. The dataset was recorded using the EMOTIV EPOC + EEG headset consisting of 14 channels, sampled at 128 Hz with 16-bit A/D conversion. The raw EEG was pre-processed by the dataset providers with Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and mean spectral power values in four main EEG frequencies ranging between 0.54 and 30 Hz in delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands. In each trial, this gave a fourteen-by-four matrix (each row was associated with an EEG electrode and the columns identified the average power in each frequency band). This matrix was viewed as a sequence of fourteen tokens, and each token was a four-dimensional feature of the spectral properties of a single channel to be used in the Transformer architecture. All feature values were standardised to z-score before training and the data was divided into 80% training set and 20% validation set. Also, ten-fold stratified cross-validation was conducted to have a stronger estimate of the model performance and prevent possible bias in data. The snapshot of the dataset in stated in Table 4.

To determine the generalizability of the CWBT model, the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset was used. This dataset is not like the Kaggle dataset as it has different sampling frequency, channel layout, and recording devices. To be consistent, the raw BCI IV 2a data was subjected to a full processing pipeline. The signals were band-pass filtered at 0.5–30 Hz to isolate motor related EEG. The motor imagery trials were divided into event markers and then resampled at 250 Hz to 128 Hz to match the sampling rate of the Kaggle dataset. Given that BCI IV 2a comprises of twenty-two electrodes, fourteen electrodes were chosen due to spatial proximity to EMOTIV EPOC+ montage. The spatially aligned channels were then converted to the frequency domain by FFT and the average power of delta, theta, alpha, and beta were extracted in the same way as the Kaggle preprocessing.

After such transformations, all trials of the BCI IV 2a data were converted to a fourteen-by-four spectral matrix, which can be input directly into the CWBT model. Each component of the cross-validation was normalized using z-score statistics that were calculated in every fold of the cross-validation process. The last input of the Transformer encoder was a sequence of fourteen tokens to both datasets, with each token corresponding to a single EEG channel. A 256-dimensional embedding vector was linearly projected on each of the four-dimensional tokens before passing through the multi-head self-attention layers. This representation allowed the model to provide spatial-spectral dependencies across the EEG channels even without the raw temporal information. To prevent all chances of data leakage and to be confident that the reported 95.60% accuracy is not due to the overlapping of training and validation sets, the data-splitting strategy was carefully regulated. Kaggle dataset lacks identifiers of the subjects; thus, trial-wise splitting was utilized and every trial was considered as an independent sample. To further confirm the generalizability, a 10-fold stratified cross-validation was made to assure the performance was not influenced by that 80:20 split. On the BCI IV 2a dataset where subject data is available, preprocessing and feature extraction were done in each fold and subject-independent cross-validation was used to separate training and validation using subject independent trials, making sure that these groups do not contain subject trials. This helps avoid the leakage of data and allows subject-level generalization to be fairly evaluated.

Even though the unified 14 × 4 representation is not dynamic and does not encompass raw temporal dynamics, a novel design choice is required to make both datasets compatible with each other and to be used in this study consequently. Kaggle and BCI IV 2a datasets are very different in terms of sampling rate, length of trials, and channel configuration, and therefore, direct sequence-based modelling of raw EEG time series is not possible. The transformation of the two datasets to a uniform spectral format retains the most informative motor related frequency bands and removes temporal differences unique to the equipment. Under such formulation, the Transformer does not learn time-series evolution but rather, each channel-band feature vector is a token, and self-attention can be learned to learn spatial-spectral channel-channel relationships. These cross-channel interactions are significant to the motor execution, and these are not effectively represented by other simpler versions of the model like MLPs or shallow CNNs. Section 4 has included baseline comparisons to confirm that these less complex models are less effective compared to the Transformer encoder, which proves the effectiveness of attention-based modelling even when aggregated spectral features are used.

Architecture of the transformer model

The architecture of the deep learning model plays a crucial role in determining the efficiency and effectiveness of classification tasks. This study acquaints a transformer model as stated in Algorithm 2, to exploit progressive self-attention mechanisms for handling complex patterns in data. The architecture as shown in Fig. 4 is devised to enhance the representational power and the generalization capabilities of the proposed model. The pivotal constituents of the transformer model include an embedding layer, a couple of batch normalization layers, dropout layer for regularization, a Transformer encoder with multiple self-attention heads, an output layer, and a final classification layer. Each of the elements is tailored to intensify the dynamics of learning and ameliorate the performance of classification tasks. The following subsections outline the architectural constituents and their contributions to the model’s functionality.

Architecture of transformer model.

Architecture of transformer-based model implemented in CWBT framework.

Embedding layer

The transformer model consists of an initial embedding layer. This layer is responsible for transmuting the input features into a continuous vector space of high dimensionality. This conversion is essential as it remodels the input features in the representational framework. A linear transformation is utilized in this layer to accelerate the dimensionality of input data to 256-dimensional space. This permits intricate operations within the transformer encoder.

Batch normalization layer

The transformer model consists of two vital batch normalization layers at decisive points to promote stability and enrich the model’s performance.

First batch normalization layer

The first batch normalization layer is placed after the embedding layer. It standardizes the output values to have zero mean and unit variance. This reconciliation supports numerical stability and swift training operations.

Second batch normalization layer

The second normalization layer is strategically implemented next to the output layer. This layer converts the linear transformation outputs to a standard form before encountering the activation functions. This layer vouches reliable system performance by addressing problems that arise from feature size variability.

Dropout layer

The dropout layer in the transformer model is strategically positioned between the first normalization layer and transformer encoder. It serves as a regularization tool to suppress overfitting. By randomly disabling a subset of features during training, this layer helps the network to develop more generalized representations of specific patterns. Using a dropout rate of 10% strikes a balance between effective learning and mitigates risks of overfitting. The dropout is turned off during inference to fully utilize all learned features, maximizing the model’s performance. By incorporating dropout, the model becomes more adaptable to real-world conditions, enhancing its robustness and generalization capabilities.

Transformer encoder

At the heart of the Transformer model is the Transformer encoder which relies on self-attention mechanisms to dynamically process input data. Each encoder layer incorporates multiple attention heads, which allows the model to analyse data from different perspectives simultaneously. The multi-head attention mechanism combines insights from each head to build a comprehensive representation of the input features. Position-wise feed-forward network within each encoder layer applies a linear transformation adding depth and non-linearity to the model. The makes the model to learn global relationships across channel-wise spectral tokens in self-attention fashion, without learning the temporal evolution of the EEG signal. This design not only improves processing speed but also enhances scalability, making the encoder well-suited for handling complex datasets. To further reinforce robustness, dropout is integrated into the encoder layer, to reduce overfitting by randomly omitting features during training.

Output layer

The output layer plays a crucial role in transforming the high-dimensional outputs from the Transformer’s encoder into meaningful class predictions. It applies a linear transformation that reduces the 256-dimensional encoded features to match the number of target classes, simplifying the classification process. After this transformation, batch normalization helps stabilize the output by ensuring consistent scales and variances, which improves training stability and speeds up model convergence. Finally, a softmax activation function converts the processed outputs into a probability distribution over the possible classes. This step is particularly important for multi-class classification, as it provides interpretable probabilities that indicate the model’s confidence in each prediction.

Classification layer

In the Transformer model, the classification layer serves as the key for converting the 256-dimensional encoder output into meaningful class predictions. This process begins with a linear transformation that adjusts the feature vectors to match the number of target classes, making complex data easier to classify. Next, batch normalization is applied which standardizes these outputs, enhancing consistency and optimizing model performance. Finally, the softmax activation function transforms the processed data into a probability distribution across the possible classes, providing clear, interpretable predictions grounded in statistical confidence. This structured approach is essential for accurate and reliable multi-class classification.

Learning rate optimization using Honey Bee Optimization technique

The selection of suitable learning rate is of utmost importance when training neural network models as it leverages convergence rate and performance of the model. Traditional methods of hyperparameter tuning such as grid search27 or random search28 is inefficient when many parameters are involved. To overcome this issue, a novel strategy utilizing bio-inspired algorithm i.e., Honey Bee Optimization (HBO) is proposed for adapting the learning rate of the transformer model.

This technique inspired by the foraging behaviour of honey bees as depicted in Fig. 5 efficiently models the exploitation and exploration phases intrinsic in natural foraging to optimize the hyperparameters. The algorithm is initialized with multiple agents, termed as “bees” where each bee represents a possible solution. These bees identify the best solution by searching the parameter space and tailoring their positions around the so far identified best solution29. The position adaptations are based on the performance of the bee on current location and a degree of randomness, which conceals the volatile nature of authentic foraging. The number of bees corresponds to the number of times the optimization process will be executed. Each bee symbolizes a sole trial of testing the learning rate. For each bee, the learning rate obtained is modified by multiplying the current best learning rate by a random factor between 0.5 and 1.5. This range of 0.5 and 1.5 is chosen based on trial-and-error strategy. The random factor is incorporated to present variability across the tested learning rates like the behaviour of bees in randomly exploring diverse areas.

Workflow of HBO for the selection of optimal learning rate in the CWBT model.

A diverse feature incorporated in this technique is the “temperature” parameter akin to the cooling schedule in simulated annealing30. This parameter is essential for controlling the amplitude affiliated to the bee’s exploratory moves. Higher values of temperature permit wider exploration and avoids primal local minima in the optimization process whereas lower values of temperature promote accurate adjustments as optimal solution is reached. In this application, the Honey Bee Optimization starts with a starting temperature of , which controls the learning-rate perturbation experienced in the initial exploration phase. A multiplicative decay factor of 0.95 is used to gradually reduce the temperature at every iteration allowing the search process to change between broad exploration to local refinements around subsets of learning-rate regions that are promising. Training every bee candidate rate is done with 5 epochs and the validation accuracy is noted, and the best-performing candidate is chosen to be used in the next iteration. This value of T0, temperature decay and duration of per-bee training is a guarantee that the HBO search process is reproducible and well-controllable.

The candidate learning rate of the bee agent is trained on the training set with a fixed number of five provisional epochs to test accuracy and then the validation. This is a limited-epoch assessment specifically selected to give an effective initial estimate to test the ability of learning-rates and ensure that the computational cost of the HBO search can be effectively addressed. Empirically, five epochs had been identified as enough to identify stable and unstable learning-rate candidates without adding too much variance (random initialization or noise) to the initial training dynamics. The approach guarantees reproducibility and efficiency on the offline hyperparameter optimization stage. The final model is then trained to achieve full convergence with the chosen optimal learning rate. Each bee imposes its new position and trains the model for a predefined number of epochs by provisionally adopting it as the learning rate and ascertains its performance on a validation set. If the bee has identified an efficient learning rate evidenced by greater validation accuracy compared to the previous one, this updated learning rate is utilized for further exploitations.

In addition to the “temperature” parameter, another parameter designated as “cooling rate” is employed to achieve a balance between extensive initial exploration and subsequent refinements31. The cooling rate is used to diminish the temperature after each iteration to gradually minimize the size of dynamic adjustments. The efficiency of Honey Bee Optimization (HBO) algorithm rests in its bipartite ability to discover the search space and regulate the solution as it converges. The versatility of this method depends on the dynamic and volatile landscape of neural network training, where the optimal learning rate is varied based on the concrete architecture and data.

By harnessing HBO technique, the transformer model leverages the bio-inspired algorithm to achieve an efficient hyperparameter tuning process. This strategy elucidates a sturdy application of nature-inspired algorithms, thereby contributing to a broader procedure for machine learning researchers and practitioners.

HBO’s computational consideration and real-time application on BCI devices

While the HBO method combines several validation evaluations across several candidate learning rates shown by bee agents, it is important to note that this optimization framework runs just during the offline training phase of the model development pipeline. Integrating HBO serves to dynamically change the learning rate in a bio-inspired way by using adaptive exploration-exploitation concepts to improve transformer model generalization capacity and convergence behavior.

Every bee iteration trains the model for a small number of epochs using a perturbed learning rate, then evaluates on the validation set to find the most optimal hyperparameter configuration. As shown by the CWBT model obtaining 95.60% accuracy in EEG-based hand movement classification, this process greatly increases model robustness and final accuracy even if it generates additional computational overhead during training.

Classification of EEG hand movements with Cerebro Wave Bee transformer model.

Crucially, this overhead does not reach the deployment level. The final model is fixed and used for inference once the ideal learning rate has been chosen using HBO. In real-time execution of BCI applications, the CWBT model runs as a standard Transformer-based classifier free from any HBO-related computational actions. This architectural separation guarantees that the CWBT model stays lightweight and practical for real-time BCI implementations, matching the low-latency criteria of assistive neurotechnology.

Though HBO improves the training efficiency and classification performance significantly, it does not compromise the real-time usability of the model. Future research may investigate ways to lower optimization cost by incorporating early stopping strategies per bee or surrogate-based evaluations particularly for limited hardware environments.

Model training approach

The CWBT model encompasses a structured training pipeline which enables an effective method to optimize and generalize the Transformer-based network for EEG-based hand movement detection. AdamW optimizer begins the learning process by serving as an optimizer variant dedicated to weight decay regularization. A learning rate determination method based on bee inspired adaptive learning rate algorithm is used to search through candidate rates until it finds the one achieving maximum validation accuracy. During training, the model works through 200 epochs which begin with forward propagation followed by backpropagation. The forward propagation method directs the input data from the embedding layer to Transformer encoder layers and ends at the fully connected output layer with Negative Log Likelihood Loss (NLLLoss) implemented during multi-class categorization. The model uses backpropagation with computed loss to produce gradients through gradient descent optimization.

The training process uses early stopping to maximize efficiency without overfitting. Early stopping is activated only after the completion of 50 epochs to prevent premature termination. After training, the model needs to be assessed to determine its generalization capabilities. The computed final accuracy represents a count of correct predictions against the actual ground truth labels. The training process incorporates adaptive learning rate adjustments and dropout regularization combined with early stopping and validation-based evaluation for efficient EEG-based hand movement classification.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup entails data preprocessing, model architecture, hyperparameter optimization, training strategy, and evaluation metric assessment. The EEG hand movement data obtained from multiple CSV documents is combined into one unified dataset. The first column in the dataset shows the class indicators and other columns corresponds to EEG data of various electrodes. The data features are uniformly scaled using StandardScaler for better model convergence. The dataset is divided into training and validation sets in the ratio of 80:20 for assessing the model performance.

A Transformer-based neural network model is employed to detect sequential dependencies within EEG signals. The model consists of an initial embedding layer to transform input features into 256-dimensional space and then batch normalization is applied to ensure stability. The core part of the model utilizes three-layer transformer encoder composed of eight attention heads for effective learning of the features’ cross connections. The final layer is run with log-SoftMax which is used to transform the raw logits into log-probabilities that can be used with the NLLLoss function to perform multi-class classification with a 10% dropout. This dropout feature is employed to prevent overfitting. And the fully connected output layer receives the transformed results for classification. The learning rate optimization process uses adaptive learning rate algorithm based on bee colony optimization to reach peak performance. The model employs an initial learning rate of 0.001 and selects its candidate learning rates through brief assessment periods of validation accuracy achievement.

Eventually, the cooling process parameter set as 0.95 in the initial phase of the CWBT algorithm reduces the amount of exploration to enhance the learning rate selection process. The training process uses the AdamW optimizer with the weight decay regularization of (1e-2) to enable better generalization ability. NLLLoss serves as the loss function for multi-class classification. The training loop contains execution of all steps 18 including forward propagation, backpropagation, and parameter updates. The combination of Dropout and batch normalization stabilizes learning while preventing overfitting. The early stopping mechanism stops training after 50 epochs while detecting 20 successive epoch validations without performance enhancement. The model is evaluated to assess its generalized performance. The experimental configuration combines Transformer-based deep learning with adaptive learning rate adjustments to obtain efficient EEG-based hand movement classification.

To assure the performance gains of CWBT model are meaningful and not data-set specific and optimizer specific, several other experiments were carried out. Three baseline versions of Transformer were firstly trained on the same dataset namely - Transformer with fixed learning rate, Transformer with a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler, and Transformer with standard Adam without HBO. Additionally, classical machine learning models such as a Support Vector Machine (RBF kernel), a Random Forest classifier, and a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) were also tested on these pre-processed features to understand whether the simpler models could achieve dominating results.

The Transformer architecture was also trained on an ablation study by training with and without HBO under identical settings and thus isolating the contribution of adaptive learning rate tuning. Moreover, HBO, fixed learning rate scheduling, ReduceLROnPlateau and ten candidate learning rates grid search convergence comparison were conducted. The accuracy of the validation was also tracked with the number of iterations so that the question of whether HBO is faster or more efficient to converge can be answered than the traditional strategies.

The entire experimental setup, therefore, is a Transformer-based deep learning with bio-inspired hyperparameter search, classical baselines, and ablation analysis to fully evaluate EEG-based hand movement classification. The experiment stated in this work were evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross validation technique. The performance metric was computed individually for each fold and the results reported in Sect. 5 was stated as the mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) across the folds.

Conclusion

This paper examined the research on Transformer-based EEG hand-movement classifier with an offline Honey Bee Optimization (HBO) strategy to identify learning-rate strategy. The findings indicate that HBO is a quantitatively significant improvement as it leads to training stability and enables the transformer to obtain increased training validation derived from both fixed-learning-rate baselines and grid-search baselines. The results indicate the effectiveness of bio-inspired optimization algorithms in hyperparameter optimization of deep neural networks used in EEG decoding. Although the model works very well on the pre-processed Kaggle data and demonstrates early generalization to BCI IV 2a, the current study is restricted by its use of a static spectral representation of 14 × 4 instead of raw EEG time series. The method relies on the characteristics generated by the EMOTIV preprocessing pipeline and it can be therefore a variation in performance with various devices, subjects and recording conditions. Additionally, stronger claims are conditional since cross-dataset generalization is not yet fully standardized because preprocessing workflows and subject-independent folds must be fully standardized. Comprehensively, the value of this paper is mainly in proving that HBO-based learning-rate optimization works successfully with Transformer models in EEG classification.

Although the suggested CWBT framework demonstrated good results, the current paper assesses the model in an offline environment with pre-processed spectral EEG representations only. The model neither takes raw EEG time-series data nor specifically considers the latency of real-time inference or online adaptation limitations needed by real-world BCI systems. Even though the validation of the BCI Competition IV-2a data set seems to be encouraging with cross-dataset generalization, standardized subject-independent testing across heterogeneous acquisition protocols remains an open issue. The future directions will be to introduce the CWBT framework to raw multichannel EEG data, introduce time dynamics, determine real time latency of BCI hardware devices, and develop standard cross-dataset and cross-subject evaluation standards to permit practical neurotechnology use. This framework will be applied to raw multichannel EEG in future work to include temporal dynamics and assess the subject-level robustness of the system to understand the real-world applicability of BCI.
