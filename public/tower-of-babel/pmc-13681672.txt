Cytomics-driven insights for investigating host–pathogen interactions
Authors: Andrea Cossarizza, Cristina Mussini, Diana Boraschi, Aldo Tagliabue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cytomics, the high-dimensional profiling of immune cells, provides a systems-level view of host responses to infection. Unlike traditional diagnostics focused on pathogen detection or early immune activation, cytomics captures rare and functionally specialized immune subsets linked to disease severity and clinical outcome. In this review, we explore how advances in spectral cytometry, mass cytometry, and multimodal single-cell platforms enable unprecedented resolution of immune complexity during infection by summarizing cytomics evolution, high-dimensional single-cell and multimodal technologies, as well as their roles in defining immune heterogeneity and host–pathogen interactions across infectious diseases. Several single-cell technologies rely on antibodies conjugated to fluorochromes or non-radioactive isotopes, generating high-dimensional datasets processed with advanced computational tools, enabling unprecedented precision in defining cell populations and supporting diagnostic advances. During infections, cytomic approaches map immune architecture, identify dysfunctional or protective cell states, and monitor therapeutic responses. In viral infections, it detects hyperinflammatory monocytes and exhausted T-cell subsets; in bacterial diseases, it reveals immunosuppressive monocytes and disrupted neutrophil networks; in parasitic infections, it clarifies mechanisms of persistence and clearance. Integrated with complementary omics technologies, cytomics informs personalized immunomodulatory strategies and rational vaccine design. This review provides a unified framework for applying cytomics to precision diagnostics, therapeutic decision-making, vaccine development, and population-level preparedness, positioning high-dimensional immune profiling as a cornerstone of modern infectious-disease management.

INTRODUCTION

Cytomics, defined as the high-dimensional, single-cell analysis of cellular phenotypes, signaling states, and intercellular interactions, has fundamentally transformed our understanding of human immunity and disease [1]. Traditionally, immunological and infectious disease research relied on bulk measurements of molecular biomarkers by immunoenzymatic (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA]) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays, which provide averaged signals from millions of cells. While these methods have proven invaluable for pathogen detection and immune profiling, they inherently mask cellular heterogeneity, transient states, and rare but biologically significant subpopulations that often drive disease progression or therapeutic response.

The concept of analyzing immune responses at the single-cell level emerged in the late 20th century with early flow cytometry studies, which allowed the detection of multiple markers simultaneously on individual lymphocytes [2]. Over the last decade, technological innovations—such as mass cytometry (cytometry by time-of-flight [CyTOF]), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), imaging mass cytometry (IMC), spectral flow cytometry, and spatial transcriptomics, among others—have exponentially increased the dimensionality of cellular profiling, enabling the simultaneous analysis of dozens to hundreds of markers per cell [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. This high-dimensional perspective provides a systems-level understanding of immunity, revealing how cells communicate, respond to pathogens, and coordinate tissue-level defense mechanisms.

In infectious diseases, the host immune response is as critical as the pathogen itself in determining clinical outcomes. For instance, in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), patients with similar viral loads may experience vastly different trajectories—ranging from asymptomatic infection to life-threatening cytokine storms—depending on the activation status of T cells, monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and B lymphocytes, as well as the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [[9], [10], [11]]. In bacterial sepsis, mortality is often dictated not by pathogen burden but by the host's dysregulated inflammatory response and subsequent immunosuppression [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Similarly, in chronic viral infections such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or hepatitis B, the differentiation and exhaustion status of T cells predict disease progression and response to therapy [[16], [17], [18]]. Cytomics enables researchers to identify these critical functional states, detect rare or complex cell populations that may drive pathology, and uncover signatures predictive of outcomes. For instance, one can trace the presence of antigen-specific cells in people who have been vaccinated or who have encountered viruses such as human papilloma virus (HPV), revealing the immune system’s memory at work.

One of the transformative aspects of cytomics is its ability to integrate phenotypic, functional, and spatial information. High-dimensional (either conventional or spectral) flow cytometry and CyTOF allow the quantification of cell surface and intracellular proteins, signaling phosphorylation events, and cytokine production at the single-cell level [19]. IMC and spatial transcriptomics add tissue context, revealing where immune cells are located, how they interact, and how these interactions impact pathogen containment or dissemination [20]. For example, in tuberculosis, the organization and composition of granulomas—a spatially structured immune microenvironment—determines whether Mycobacterium tuberculosis remains latent or progresses to active disease [21]. Similarly, spatial mapping of immune infiltrates in COVID-19 lungs has identified tissue-localized hyperinflammatory monocytes and exhausted T cells contributing to severe pathology [22].

Cytomics, whose evolution is outlined in Table 1, stands at a unique crossroads: it naturally weaves together with other omics disciplines—genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics—creating a multidimensional lens through which to explore the complexity of host–pathogen interactions [23,24]. Computational modeling, machine learning, and network analysis applied to these datasets can uncover regulatory circuits, predict cellular trajectories, and identify potential therapeutic targets. In vaccine research, cytomic profiling can define correlates of protection, identify suboptimal immune responses in at-risk populations, and guide the design of next-generation immunogens [[25], [26], [27]]. Beyond basic research, cytomics has significant clinical and translational relevance. Single-cell profiling can aid in diagnosis, distinguishing active vs latent infections, as in the case of tuberculosis [28] or bacterial vs viral etiologies [29]. It can guide prognosis, identify immune signatures associated with severe outcomes, and optimize therapeutic interventions, including immunomodulatory therapies and personalized vaccination strategies. Despite these advances, the field faces technical and conceptual challenges. Sample preparation, standardization of assays across laboratories, data integration from multi-omics platforms, and the interpretation of extremely high-dimensional data remain significant hurdles [19]. Nonetheless, ongoing developments in computational biology, artificial intelligence, and single-cell technologies are rapidly addressing these challenges, promising a future in which cytomics is integral to both infectious disease research and precision clinical care. In this review, we provide a comprehensive, discursive overview of cytomics (Figure 2), paying particular attention to infectious diseases. HIGH-DIMENSIONAL SINGLE-CELL TECHNOLOGIES

High-dimensional single-cell technologies represent the foundation of modern cytomics, enabling researchers to dissect cellular heterogeneity, functional states, and spatial organization with unprecedented precision. Traditional bulk immunological assays can mask rare but crucial populations and transient signaling events. In contrast, high-dimensional approaches allow a systems-level understanding of the immune response, particularly during infectious diseases, by capturing both cellular diversity and dynamic functional states [[30], [31], [32]].

Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry, either conventional or spectral, continues to serve as a foundational and widely adopted technique in single-cell analysis, owing to its speed, versatility, and ability to perform high-throughput, multiparametric assessments of immune cells in suspension. It remains an essential tool in both basic and clinical immunology, facilitating the simultaneous measurement of multiple markers on thousands of cells per second. Recent technological advancements have significantly enhanced its capabilities. In particular, the development of spectral flow cytometers has expanded the dimensionality of data that can be acquired, now permitting the concurrent analysis of up to 50 distinct parameters per cell [[33], [34], [35]]. This level of resolution allows for a comprehensive view of cellular phenotypes by integrating information from surface molecules, intracellular signaling proteins, and cytokine expression profiles.

Multiparametric depth is particularly valuable for identifying rare and functionally important immune cell subsets that may be present only at small frequencies but play disproportionately large roles in host defense or disease progression [36]. These include tissue-homing T cells that migrate to sites of infection or inflammation, antibody-producing plasmablasts, various subsets of dendritic cells involved in antigen presentation, and activated monocytes that contribute to both innate and adaptive immunity [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. The ability to distinguish and characterize these populations with precision is critical for effective immune monitoring, especially in the context of infection, vaccination, and immune-mediated disorders.

Beyond phenotypic profiling, flow cytometry also supports a wide range of functional assays that can assess immune cell behavior in response to stimuli [19]. Techniques such as ICS allow for the detection of cytokine production following antigen-specific or polyclonal stimulation, offering direct insights into T-cell and NK-cell functionality. Additionally, proliferation tracking using dyes like the fluorescent label carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE), as well as degranulation assays (e.g., CD107a staining), enables researchers to evaluate cytotoxic activity and proliferative responses. These assays are essential for dissecting immune competence vs dysfunction under different pathological conditions. For instance, in patients with sepsis or chronic viral infections, flow cytometric analysis has revealed profound impairments in T-cell functionality and disruptions in NK cell activity. In contrast, during sepsis or in acute viral infections, flow cytometry has enabled the detection of hyperactivated subsets of monocytes and T cells, changes in mitochondrial functionality or propensity to undergo apoptosis, which have been shown to correlate with more severe disease manifestations [[41], [42], [43], [44]].

Moreover, recent innovations in high-throughput flow cytometry have further extended its applicability in large-scale studies. The incorporation of barcoding and sample multiplexing strategies now allows simultaneous analysis of multiple samples in a single run, greatly enhancing efficiency and comparability [45]. This is particularly beneficial during infectious disease outbreaks or global pandemics, where the need for rapid and large-scale immune profiling is critical. By enabling analysis of hundreds or even thousands of samples across large patient cohorts, high-throughput flow cytometry supports timely population-level insights and helps inform public health responses and therapeutic interventions.

Mass Cytometry

Mass cytometry, also known as CyTOF, is a cutting-edge single-cell analysis technique that addresses one of the main limitations of traditional fluorescent flow cytometry: spectral overlap. Instead of using fluorescent tags, CyTOF employs antibodies labeled with rare metal isotopes, which are detected using mass spectrometry. Mass cytometry is ultimately constrained by the number of resolvable metal isotopes, enabling routine panels of 40–45 markers, expert-level designs reaching 50–55 parameters, and a theoretical ceiling of approximately 60 [46,47]. The result is a dramatic increase in the dimensionality of data that can be obtained from each cell, making CyTOF particularly useful in the field of systems immunology as underlined in Figure 3, which shows how to identify several different immune cell types in a few milliliters of peripheral blood, using data from a public database (https://zenodo.org/records/15495774). After washing, cells were incubated with a panel of metal-tagged surface antibodies in a protein-containing buffer to preserve epitope integrity. Samples were then fixed and permeabilized to enable intracellular staining of targets such as the transcription factor FOXP3. Following intracellular staining, cells were labeled with an iridium DNA intercalator to allow identification of intact single cells and exclusion of doublets [10]. CyTOF provides the ability to concurrently analyze a wide array of cellular features, including cell surface markers, intracellular signaling molecules, transcription factors, cytokines, and metabolic indicators. This multiplexing capability offers researchers a holistic and integrative view of immune cell states and functions within complex biological systems [48]. It allows for the dissection of cellular heterogeneity in unprecedented detail, uncovering functional relationships between immune cell subsets and how they respond under various physiological and pathological conditions.

In the context of infectious disease research, CyTOF has emerged as an indispensable tool for defining immune signatures associated with disease pathogenesis, severity, progression, and responsiveness to therapy. For example, studies involving COVID-19 patients have utilized CyTOF to uncover key immunological alterations, including the expansion of hyperactivated monocyte populations, the presence of exhausted T-cell phenotypes, and fluctuations in regulatory T-cell frequencies. These changes have been strongly correlated with poor clinical outcomes and have provided critical insights into the mechanisms driving severe disease [49,50]. Similarly, in the study of chronic viral infections such as HIV and hepatitis C, or in the study of tuberculosis, CyTOF has revealed subtle but biologically meaningful disruptions in the immune landscape [[51], [52], [53]]. These include changes in NK cell function, shifts in memory T-cell subsets, and dysregulation of immune regulatory pathways that were previously almost undetectable using conventional flow cytometry techniques.

Furthermore, the most recent advancements in CyTOF methodology have introduced workflows that incorporate multi-omic capabilities [54]. These approaches integrate measurements of signaling pathways, metabolic activity, and epigenetic modifications, all at the single-cell level. This enables researchers to move beyond surface phenotyping and delve into the functional and regulatory layers of immune responses. By capturing multiple dimensions of cellular behavior, such integrated CyTOF platforms are paving the way for a more comprehensive, systems-level understanding of how the host immune system responds to infection and inflammation.

Cytomics and Single-cell RNA Sequencing

The ability to separate cells with extremely high efficiency and precision now makes it possible to investigate, in detail, the most refined molecular and cellular mechanisms. Indeed, one of the most relevant applications of cytomic techniques is their integration with next-generation molecular methodologies. Cell sorting based on an extensive panel of phenotypic or functional markers now enables the highly selective isolation of target cell populations for downstream sequencing analyses. Unlike bulk RNA sequencing, which averages gene expression across entire populations, scRNA-seq, a technology that enables detailed transcriptomic profiling at the resolution of individual cells, allows researchers to capture and analyze the intrinsic heterogeneity that exists within cells with a similar phenotype, or in complex tissues [55,56]. This includes differences in gene expression across various cell types, cell states, and functional responses. In immunology and infectious disease research, this level of granularity is crucial for understanding how diverse immune cell populations behave under homeostatic conditions and how they respond dynamically to pathogenic threats [57].

Through this high-resolution lens, scRNA-seq has revealed transcriptional programs that are highly specific to different activation states, stages of differentiation, or pathogen exposure. Importantly, it has the capacity to uncover rare or previously undetected immune cell subsets that may be critical in orchestrating protective responses or, conversely, in driving immunopathology. These rare populations might be masked in bulk analyses due to their low abundance but can be functionally significant in disease progression or resolution. Therefore, scRNA-seq plays a vital role not only in mapping cellular diversity but also in identifying novel therapeutic targets.

An additional layer of insight is gained when scRNA-seq is paired with T cell receptor (TCR) or B cell receptor (BCR) sequencing [58,59]. This powerful combination makes it possible to directly link antigen specificity with the transcriptional and functional phenotype of individual lymphocytes. Such integration allows researchers to monitor clonal expansions, track immune repertoire dynamics, and observe how specific clones respond to infections or vaccines over time [60]. A clear application of this is seen in studies of COVID-19, where scRNA-seq coupled with TCR/BCR analysis identified notable expansions of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and antibody-secreting plasmablasts. These immune responses were found to correlate strongly with disease severity, immune protection, and recovery profiles, offering crucial insights into mechanisms of viral control and immunopathology [61,62].

To further deepen our understanding of immune function, more advanced multimodal technologies like the cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) have been developed [63]. CITE-seq enables the simultaneous quantification of surface protein expression and mRNA transcripts within the same single cell [64]. This dual-layered approach merges proteomic and transcriptomic data, providing a more refined characterization of immune cell identities and functional states. It allows for a more holistic interpretation of cellular phenotypes that cannot be captured by transcriptomics alone as protein levels often reflect post-transcriptional regulation. By bridging these two domains, CITE-seq offers a powerful tool for dissecting the complexity of immune responses, both in health and during disease progression.

Imaging Mass Cytometry and Spatial Transcriptomics

Understanding immune responses in tissues requires spatial resolution. IMC is a relatively new technology that sits at the intersection of imaging and high-dimensional proteomics, allowing scientists to visualize the distribution of dozens of proteins simultaneously within a single tissue section. Unlike conventional immunohistochemistry, which is constrained to a limited number of fluorescent markers, IMC parallels CyTOF by employing metal-tagged antibodies rather than fluorophores. This strategy eliminates spectral overlap, enabling highly multiplexed spatial profiling at subcellular resolution [65]. Combining high-dimensional protein measurement with tissue imaging enables the mapping of immune cells within their anatomical context [66,67]. What makes IMC particularly discursive in its impact is that it allows researchers to explore cellular neighborhoods, tissue architecture, and spatial relationships at an unprecedented scale.

Spatial transcriptomics is a rapidly advancing method that maps gene expression directly within intact tissue sections, preserving spatial context that bulk and single-cell RNA-seq lose [[68], [69], [70]]. Tissue slices are placed on slides with spatially barcoded probes that capture RNA, allowing each transcript to be traced back to its exact location after sequencing. This turns a histological section into a high-resolution gene expression map, revealing how tissue structure shapes cellular function. By maintaining a native architecture, spatial transcriptomics exposes cell–cell interactions, microenvironmental influences, and functional gradients that dissociative methods cannot detect. This is especially valuable in immunology and infectious disease, where the position and activation state of immune cells determine outcomes such as pathogen control, inflammation, immunoparalysis, or tissue damage [71]. The approach has clarified granuloma organization in tuberculosis, identifying macrophage and T-cell subsets linked to latent vs progressive disease [72], and has revealed localized inflammatory monocyte clusters and exhausted T cells in COVID-19 lungs [73,74]. Overall, spatial profiling provides unprecedented insight into how immune cells behave within their physical niches, transforming our understanding of protective and pathogenic immune responses.

Multimodal and Integrated Approaches

The integration of multiple high-dimensional technologies, including CyTOF, scRNA-seq, epigenetic profiling, and spatially resolved imaging methods, offers complementary and synergistic perspectives on immune function [75]. By combining these diverse modalities, researchers can capture both phenotypic and functional dimensions of immune cells, cross-validate findings across platforms, and enhance the detection of rare or transient cell populations that may be overlooked when using a single modality.

Such multimodal datasets also enable the reconstruction of dynamic immune trajectories over time or in response to therapy. For example, integrating surface marker expression from CyTOF with transcriptomic profiles from scRNA-seq allows simultaneous mapping of cell identity, signaling states, and gene expression programs [76,77]. Epigenetic and chromatin accessibility data can reveal regulatory networks driving immune plasticity, while spatial imaging contextualizes these cellular states within tissue architecture, highlighting interactions between immune and stromal compartments.

Applications of multimodal cytomics in infectious diseases have uncovered intricate patterns of immune dysregulation that were previously difficult to resolve [78]. These include metabolic reprogramming in monocytes during bacterial sepsis, functional exhaustion in CD8+ T cells during chronic viral infections, and the emergence of pathogenic intercellular circuits driving inflammation or immunopathology—patterns that single-modality approaches often fail to detect [[79], [80], [81]]. By integrating complementary datasets, researchers can gain a systems-level understanding of immune responses, uncover mechanistic insights into host–pathogen interactions, and identify potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Data Analysis in High-dimensional Cytomics

The extraordinary richness of high-dimensional cytomic datasets represents both a unique opportunity and a formidable analytical challenge. Unlike traditional flow cytometry, which could monitor a relatively limited number of parameters simultaneously, modern cytomic approaches capture dozens of cellular features at once, generating complex, multidimensional landscapes. Consequently, when employing a panel of 50 markers per cell, and assuming that each marker functions as a strictly binary variable—detecting the presence or absence of a given molecular feature—the resulting combinatorial space encompasses 250 (i.e., about 1.13 × 1015, or more than one quadrillion) of theoretically distinguishable cellular states. This exponential expansion reflects the fact that every marker contributes an independent informational bit, thereby enabling the discrimination of an extremely large number of potential phenotypic populations within the sample. To translate this enormous amount of data into biologically meaningful information, it became clear that computational pipelines would become indispensable [82]. These pipelines typically encompass preprocessing, quality control, dimensionality reduction, clustering, trajectory inference, network modeling, and predictive analytics [83].

However, computational analysis in cytomics faces substantial challenges, particularly as datasets grow in dimensionality and complexity and as multimodal profiling becomes increasingly common. Analytical pipelines remain highly variable across platforms and laboratories, with differences in preprocessing, normalization, marker transformation, and gating strategies introducing significant reproducibility issues and often leading to divergent biological interpretations [84]. Latent-space models such as UMAP, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), diffusion maps, and variational autoencoders further complicate downstream analysis [85,86]. Although invaluable for visualization and exploratory clustering, these methods can distort biological relationships and fluorescence compensation, amplify batch effects, and generate embeddings that are highly sensitive to parameter choices, initialization, and noise structure [87,88]. Such distortions are especially problematic when inferring trajectories, identifying rare populations, or comparing datasets across studies. Integrating cytomics with transcriptomic, epigenomic, or spatial modalities adds another layer of difficulty due to mismatched feature sets, divergent dynamic ranges, and fundamentally different noise profiles, all of which increase the risk of overcorrection during batch harmonization or anchor-based alignment. These limitations are compounded by the lack of standardized metadata, inconsistent benchmarking metrics, and limited ground truth for validating computational outputs, making it challenging to assess whether observed patterns reflect true biology or methodological artifacts. Collectively, these pitfalls underscore the urgent need for rigorous standardization, transparent reporting, and robust cross-platform harmonization strategies to ensure that multimodal cytomic analyses yield biologically reliable, interpretable, and reproducible insights.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering

Because high-dimensional datasets are difficult to visualize directly, dimensionality reduction techniques have been developed to project them into more interpretable two- or three-dimensional spaces. Among the most widely used are principal component analysis, UMAP, and t-SNE, which facilitate the visualization of immune cell diversity and the identification of distinct clusters [[89], [90], [91]]. To move beyond visualization, clustering algorithms such as flow self-organizing map (FlowSOM), PhenoGraph, and the Louvain method provide automated classification of cellular populations [84,92,93]. These approaches are especially powerful for detecting rare immune subsets, which, despite their low frequency, may be critical in dictating disease outcome, protection, or therapeutic response.

Trajectory and pseudotime analysis

In addition to static mapping, cytomics also enables the reconstruction of cellular dynamics. Trajectory inference methods, including Monocle, Slingshot, and Palantir, are designed to model how cells transition between states during processes such as differentiation, activation, or exhaustion [[94], [95], [96]]. By ordering cells along a computational axis called “pseudotime,” these tools allow researchers to reconstruct developmental or activation pathways [97]. For example, in infectious diseases and vaccination studies, trajectory analysis can reveal how naïve T cells acquire effector functions or how dysfunctional, exhausted populations emerge during chronic infection [98].

Network analysis and intercellular communication

A refined understanding of immune responses increasingly hinges on the ability to model cell–cell communication with high resolution. Advances in single-cell and spatially resolved technologies now allow the reconstruction of intricate intercellular networks by integrating ligand–receptor landscapes, cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways, and the transcriptional programs they elicit. When assembled into coherent interaction maps, these multilayered datasets reveal regulatory hubs, directional signaling flows, and emergent circuit properties that demarcate effective, self-limiting immune responses from those that become maladaptive or pathogenic. Such systems-level perspectives provide a mechanistic framework for interpreting interindividual variability—for example, why some hosts rapidly coordinate antiviral immunity, whereas others exhibit impaired communication modules that predispose to excessive inflammation, tissue injury, or progression to severe disease. By capturing the architecture and dynamics of immune crosstalk, these approaches offer powerful insights into disease trajectories and highlight potential nodes for therapeutic intervention.

Machine learning and predictive modeling

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a powerful ally for cytomics. Machine learning approaches, such as random forests (RF), support vector machines (SVM), and deep learning (DL) architectures, are now applied to predict clinical outcomes from cytometric data [[99], [100], [101]]. These approaches can uncover immune signatures that correlate with disease severity, vaccine responsiveness, or therapeutic success. Importantly, such predictive models extend the scope of cytomics beyond discovery, positioning it as a tool for personalized immunology and precision medicine in heterogeneous infectious disease populations.

In summary, advanced computational pipelines—from dimensionality reduction to predictive modeling—are reshaping how cytomic data are interpreted, moving from static descriptions to dynamic and predictive frameworks. Yet, the power of these approaches depends not only on algorithms but also on the continuous evolution of experimental technologies that generate the data. As cytomics integrates with other high-throughput single-cell and spatial platforms, new opportunities emerge to capture immune responses in their full biological and tissue context. These emerging trends are driving the field toward a more holistic and translational view of immunity, bridging computational analysis with innovative experimental design.

Emerging Trends

The field of cytomics is rapidly expanding beyond classical applications, embracing innovative experimental and analytical frontiers. Among the most promising is single-cell multi-omics, which integrates cytometric data with additional layers such as scRNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and proteomics, thereby capturing immune cell identity and regulation at unprecedented resolution. Equally transformative are live-cell mass cytometry platforms, which allow dynamic monitoring of cellular responses in real time, adding functional information to high-dimensional profiling. At the same time, spatially resolved technologies, including multiplexed ion beam imaging (MIBI) and IMC, preserve the architecture of tissues while mapping the molecular and cellular components within them [71,102]. By doing so, they reveal not only which immune cells are present but also how they are positioned and interact within microenvironments such as tumors, inflamed tissues, or infected organs. Finally, the combination of in situ sequencing further enriches this landscape by capturing gene expression directly in tissue sections, thereby integrating spatial and molecular information in a single assay [103].

Together, these innovations promise to shift cytomics from descriptive profiling toward a truly mechanistic understanding of immune responses [104,105]. They also hold enormous translational potential: mapping immune dynamics in context will support the development of more accurate diagnostics, rational vaccine design, and precision-guided immunotherapies for infectious diseases and beyond.

High-dimensional single-cell technologies, integrated with advanced computational frameworks, enable deep characterization of immune heterogeneity, dynamic functional states, and spatial tissue architecture. By combining transcriptomic, proteomic, epigenetic, and spatially resolved datasets, these approaches provide a multidimensional view of how immune cells behave and interact within complex tissue environments. The integration of multimodal data with predictive modeling yields mechanistic insights, supports the identification of biomarkers of disease severity, informs rational vaccine design, and guides the development of targeted immunotherapies. Together, these innovations establish a robust foundation for precision immunology in the context of infectious diseases, allowing more accurate stratification of patients and more effective therapeutic interventions.

IMMUNE CELL HETEROGENEITY AND PATHWAYS

The immune system is profoundly heterogeneous, plastic, and exquisitely context-dependent, with each cellular compartment exhibiting layers of variability that extend far beyond classical lineage definitions. T cells, B cells, and diverse innate populations differ not only in phenotype and effector function but also in metabolic wiring, signaling thresholds, chromatin accessibility, spatial positioning within tissues, and the temporal dynamics that govern their activation and differentiation. This multidimensional diversity is further shaped by age, microbiota, prior antigenic exposures, inflammatory milieu, and tissue-specific cues, creating a continuously shifting landscape of immune states. Understanding immune heterogeneity at this level is critical for improving outcomes in infectious diseases and guiding the future of precision immunology.

A comprehensive description of immune cell populations and an in-depth explanation of immune system function fall beyond the scope of this review. The immune system functions as a highly adaptive and interconnected network, composed of myriad cell types, signaling pathways, and tissue environments that together orchestrate defense against pathogens while maintaining host homeostasis. A central insight emerging from high-dimensional cytomics and single-cell analyses is the extraordinary heterogeneity of immune cells, even within populations traditionally considered uniform. Classical immunology has already described and characterized many immune cell types, including T and B lymphocytes, NK cells, dendritic cells, and a broad range of innate immune populations such as monocytes, neutrophils, and several others. In recent years, increasingly refined analyses have uncovered continuous spectra of transcriptional, epigenetic, and functional states that govern immune cell behavior. This intrinsic heterogeneity underlies the immune system’s adaptability, allowing for tailored responses to a wide range of pathogens. At the same time, it provides a mechanistic basis for the persistence of certain infections and the emergence of dysregulated inflammatory responses.

Viral infections highlight the importance of rare immune subpopulations in shaping protective or pathogenic responses. Indeed, in the most accessible human tissue for immunological studies, namely, peripheral blood, antigen-specific cells occur at extremely low frequencies and can therefore be regarded as bona fide rare cells. Some T cells rapidly expand into cytotoxic effectors capable of clearing infected cells, while others adopt regulatory or memory-like programs to temper inflammation and prevent tissue damage. Similarly, B cells display heterogeneity in somatic hypermutation and isotype switching, which affects both immediate neutralization and long-term humoral immunity. Innate immune cells—including monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and NK cells—exhibit functional plasticity shaped by local tissue signals, pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and systemic inflammatory mediators. High-dimensional cytomics—including highly polychromatic, multi-laser or spectral flow cytometry, mass cytometry, scRNA-seq, CITE-seq, and spatial transcriptomics—has been instrumental in dissecting the complex layers of immune heterogeneity. These technologies enable the identification of rare cellular populations, dynamic transcriptional states, and spatially resolved interactions that were previously inaccessible, thereby providing unprecedented resolution in the study of immune architecture and function.

Heterogeneity also reflects host-specific factors, such as genetic polymorphisms, prior infections, comorbidities, and age. As demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, these factors critically shape the immune landscape, influencing individual susceptibility to infection, the severity of disease manifestations, and the efficacy of vaccines and immunotherapeutic interventions. [10,11,[106], [107], [108]]. Consequently, understanding immune heterogeneity is not only a matter of cataloging cell types but also involves mapping functional trajectories and context-dependent behaviors across multiple scales—from molecular signaling to tissue-level organization.

T-cell Subsets and Functional States

T cells are central to adaptive immunity, mediating cytotoxicity, helper functions, and long-term immunological memory [109]. Traditional classifications of T cells into naïve, central memory (TCM), effector memory (TEM), and effector subsets provide a basic framework, but single-cell analyses reveal layers of previously unrecognized complexity [110]. Naïve T cells display variability in TCR repertoire, surface receptor expression, and basal transcriptional programs, which influence the speed and magnitude of their response to novel antigens.

Memory T cells exhibit functional heterogeneity shaped by trafficking patterns, cytokine production and responsiveness, and metabolic states [111]. TCM cells reside in lymphoid tissues and provide systemic immune surveillance, TEM cells circulate in the periphery to mount immediate effector responses, and tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) occupy barrier tissues such as the lung, gut, and skin, providing rapid localized immunity [112]. TRM cells display transcriptional and epigenetic plasticity that allows adaptation to the local tissue microenvironment, including interactions with stromal and epithelial cells. These cells also contribute to trained immunity, where previous exposures influence subsequent immune responses.

Chronic infections, such as HIV and hepatitis C, can induce T-cell exhaustion, characterized by the upregulation of inhibitory receptors including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), and transcriptional programs that reduce proliferation and cytokine production [17,113]. However, exhaustion is not homogeneous [114,115]. Progenitor-exhausted T cells maintain proliferative potential and responsiveness to checkpoint blockade, whereas terminally exhausted cells have limited functional capacity. In acute infections, highly cytotoxic effector T cells produce IFN-γ, TNF, and granzyme B, rapidly eliminating infected cells, but excessive activation may contribute to tissue pathology as in the case of HIV [116].

Metabolic heterogeneity further distinguishes T-cell subsets [117]. Effector T cells preferentially utilize glycolysis to sustain rapid proliferation and cytokine production, whereas memory T cells rely on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation to support longevity and secondary responses. Epigenetic modifications reinforce these metabolic states, creating distinct functional subsets with varying potential for plasticity, exhaustion, and adaptability to changing microenvironments. Subsets like T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) exhibit additional functional specialization and control immune activities [[118], [119], [120]]. Tfh cells provide essential help to B cells in germinal centers, influencing class switching, affinity maturation, and memory formation, whereas Tregs regulate excessive immune activation and prevent tissue damage.

B-cell Heterogeneity and Humoral Immunity

B cells orchestrate humoral immunity through antibody production, antigen presentation, and immune regulation. Single-cell studies reveal extensive heterogeneity across naïve, transitional, memory, plasmablast, and plasma cell populations [121,122]. During acute infection, plasmablasts rapidly expand to secrete high levels of antibodies, providing immediate defense, whereas memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells sustain long-term immunity [123].

Chronic infections, including HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C, are associated with the accumulation of atypical or exhausted memory B cells, which exhibit impaired proliferation, altered signaling, and diminished antibody production, contributing to viral persistence and suboptimal vaccine responses [[124], [125], [126]]. Multimodal approaches, integrating BCR sequencing, transcriptomics, epigenetic profiling, and surface phenotyping, are now used [127] and have identified rare but highly functional B-cell subsets capable of producing broadly neutralizing antibodies, essential for next-generation vaccine development and therapeutic interventions [128,129].

B-cell heterogeneity is shaped not only by intrinsic programming but also by extrinsic signals from cytokines, tissue microenvironments, and interactions with Tfh cells. Spatial single-cell analyses demonstrate that the anatomical localization of B cells within lymphoid organs or inflamed tissues profoundly affects their functional capacity, influencing germinal center reactions, class switching, and longevity of the humoral response.

Innate Immune Cell Heterogeneity

Innate immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, and NK cells, exhibit remarkable plasticity and a broad spectrum of functional diversity that is essential for orchestrating effective immune responses [130]. Monocyte and macrophage populations are highly heterogeneous, differing in key functional attributes such as cytokine production profiles, antigen presentation capabilities, migratory behavior, and patterns of tissue residency [131]. In the context of severe COVID-19 infection, a subset of hyperinflammatory monocytes becomes activated and secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, TNF, and IL-1β [132]. This cytokine storm contributes to systemic inflammation and exacerbates disease severity, and its inhibition has impressive clinical results [133]. Conversely, other monocyte subsets may adopt regulatory and anti-inflammatory programs aimed at limiting excessive tissue damage and promoting resolution of inflammation [131]. Meanwhile, tissue-resident macrophages dynamically adjust their transcriptional programs in response to local environmental signals, enabling a finely tuned balance between mounting effective pathogen defense and preserving tissue integrity and homeostasis [134].

Dendritic cells function as elite sentinels bridging innate and adaptive immunity by capturing antigens in peripheral tissues and migrating to lymph nodes to prime T cells [135,136]. This transition, fueled by complex transcriptomic remodeling, allows DCs to not only initiate the immune response but also dictate its polarization, shifting between inflammation and tolerance [137,138]. Consequently, their functional state is the primary determinant in both the clearance of chronic infections and the evasion of tumor surveillance [139]. This pivotal role is underpinned by significant functional heterogeneity, particularly regarding antigen presentation efficiency, cytokine secretion patterns, and T-cell priming capacity. Ultimately, these properties are finely tuned by tissue localization, the nature of the pathogen, and the inflammatory milieu, leading to highly diverse immunological outcomes [140].

NK cells represent a distinct lineage of innate lymphocytes specialized in the surveillance of cellular integrity, identified more than 50 years ago [141,142]. Their primary functional hallmark is the ability to orchestrate a rapid cytolytic response against aberrantly transformed or virally hijacked cells without the prerequisite of the recognition of an antigen presented by the major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). The operational “decision-making” process of NK cells is governed by an intricate rheostat of surface receptors: inhibitory signals, which recognize constitutive self-molecules (MHC-I), competitively balance activating signals triggered by stress-induced ligands [143,144]. This integration of opposing inputs allows NK cells to detect the “missing-self” signature, a common evasion tactic used by tumors and pathogens to bypass T-cell detection. Upon target engagement, NK cells execute a dual-pronged strategy. First, they trigger polarized degranulation, releasing a lethal cocktail of perforins and granzymes that induce osmotic instability and programmed cell death in the target [145]. Second, they function as immunological rheostats, secreting a potent array of pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably IFN-γ [146]. This secretome is critical for the functional maturation of DCs and the subsequent recruitment of the adaptive arm. Emerging paradigms now recognize NK cells not merely as short-lived effectors but as highly adaptable cells capable of epigenetic remodeling and “memory-like” persistence, offering a promising, “off-the-shelf” platform for innovative cell-based immunotherapies. Indeed, a pivotal study found that, using CyTOF and markers for 28 NK cell receptors, peripheral blood NK cells can display an incredible degree of diversity, with an estimated 6000 to 30,000 phenotypic populations within an individual and > 100,000 phenotypes considering 5 sets of monozygotic twins and 12 unrelated donors [147]. During chronic antiviral immune responses, such as those directed against HIV-1 or West Nile virus, the functional heterogeneity of the NK cell repertoire can significantly expand. This shift culminates in a state of terminal differentiation, prioritizing robust cytokine production over rapid cell proliferation and immediate cytolytic activity [148].

These approaches have greatly enhanced our understanding of how different innate immune cell subsets contribute to both protective host defense mechanisms and pathological immunopathology.

Signaling Networks and Functional Programs

Immune heterogeneity is orchestrated by a complex and finely tuned network of signaling pathways that integrate a multitude of extracellular and intracellular cues. These include receptor-mediated signals triggered by antigen recognition, cytokine milieus, cellular metabolic states, and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms that collectively shape immune cell fate and function [149]. Engagement of the TCR and BCR initiates the activation of several critical intracellular signaling cascades, including the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of mitogen-activated protein kinase B cells (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT), mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathways, among others [[150], [151], [152], [153]]. These pathways are central regulators of immune cell proliferation, differentiation into specialized effector subsets, and the execution of diverse effector functions such as gene rearrangements, antibody production, cytokine production, and cytotoxicity.

Innate immune sensing depends on a complementary set of pattern recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), RIG-I-like receptors, the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, and inflammasomes [[154], [155], [156]]. These receptors detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns and initiate rapid signaling events that lead to the production and release of inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons. Importantly, these innate pathways engage in extensive crosstalk with adaptive immune cells, coordinating a comprehensive and context-appropriate immune response to infection [157]. The signaling cascades triggered by both innate and adaptive receptors do not operate in isolation; rather, they form a highly interconnected network characterized by multiple feedback and feedforward loops that precisely regulate the magnitude, timing, and duration of immune activation. This complex regulation is essential to ensure effective pathogen clearance while limiting collateral tissue damage. When dysregulated, these signaling pathways can contribute to the development of chronic infections, immune exhaustion, and various forms of immunopathology. Nevertheless, these very pathways also provide promising targets for therapeutic interventions, including immune checkpoint inhibition, modulation of cytokine signaling, and metabolic reprogramming strategies aimed at restoring immune balance and enhancing host defense.

Cellular Crosstalk and Network Dynamics

Immune function is orchestrated through highly complex networks of cell–cell communication that, as previously described, rely on multiple mechanisms, including soluble mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, direct ligand–receptor interactions at the cell surface, and intimate physical contacts between immune cells. These multifaceted communication pathways enable immune cells to rapidly transmit signals, coordinate their responses, and dynamically adapt to changing environmental cues during infection, tissue repair, or inflammation. Recent advances in high-dimensional single-cell technologies and spatial cytomics have revolutionized our understanding of these interactions by allowing researchers to simultaneously analyze numerous cellular markers while preserving spatial context within tissues. Such approaches have uncovered specialized microanatomical hubs or niches where multiple immune cell types congregate and engage in dynamic, reciprocal interactions that influence cell fate decisions and functional outcomes.

For example, spatial profiling studies of lungs from patients infected with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) have revealed distinct regions where hyperactivated monocytes, exhausted T cells, and antibody-secreting plasmablasts colocalize, creating localized zones of intense inflammation and contributing to tissue injury and impaired lung function [[158], [159], [160], [161]]. These spatial clusters highlight how immune cell interactions within specific tissue microenvironments can drive pathogenic processes, such as the dysregulated immune responses observed in severe COVID-19. Beyond viral infections, such microenvironmental immune cell hubs are also relevant in cancer, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammatory conditions, where they can either promote disease progression or mediate protective immunity.

Moreover, as depicted in Figure 5, the crosstalk between different immune cell types—including DC and T cells, which coordinate antigen presentation and T-cell activation; NK cells and T cells, which synergize in cytotoxic responses; and Tfh cells and B cells, which facilitate antibody production—illustrates the complexity and specificity of immune regulation. These cellular interactions govern not only the execution of cytotoxic responses to eliminate infected or transformed cells but also the establishment of regulatory networks that maintain immune tolerance and prevent excessive tissue damage. This dynamic interplay underscores that immune heterogeneity arises not only from intrinsic cellular programs and differentiation states but also from extrinsic, context-dependent interactions within the tissue microenvironment. Rather than acting independently, immune cells rely on continuous crosstalk shaped by direct receptor–ligand interactions, soluble mediators, and cues from the surrounding tissue environment. Temporal Dynamics and Plasticity

Immune heterogeneity is not static but highly dynamic, continually evolving throughout the course of infection, vaccination, or other immune challenges. Immune cells undergo transitions across a spectrum of functional states, including activation, exhaustion, memory formation, and regulatory phenotypes. These transitions are shaped by a variety of factors, such as the level and duration of pathogen burden, the specific tissue microenvironment in which immune responses unfold, and the effects of therapeutic interventions like antivirals, immunomodulators, or vaccines. This dynamic plasticity enables the immune system to adapt rapidly to changing conditions but also poses challenges in distinguishing protective vs maladaptive responses. Cutting-edge analytical methods, including trajectory inference algorithms and multimodal single-cell technologies, now allow researchers to reconstruct these complex temporal programs of immune cell differentiation and state changes at unprecedented resolution. These tools enable the identification of lineage relationships, the mapping of plasticity between states, and the discovery of rare but functionally important subpopulations that might otherwise remain undetected.

By providing a detailed understanding of how immune cells adapt and evolve during an immune response to infections, such approaches yield critical mechanistic insights into the determinants of effective immunity vs immune dysfunction. They reveal key checkpoints and molecular drivers of immune exhaustion or sustained activation, as well as pathways underlying the generation and maintenance of immunological memory. This knowledge informs the rational design of personalized immunotherapies and vaccines by pinpointing which immune cell states or transitions should be promoted or inhibited to optimize clinical outcomes. Ultimately, these dynamic profiling strategies hold promise for advancing precision medicine by tailoring treatments based on an individual’s unique immune trajectory and response landscape.

Infectious diseases continue to pose one of the greatest challenges to global health, with pathogens employing sophisticated strategies to establish infection, evade immunity, and provoke tissue pathology. Cytomics provides a transformative framework for dissecting complex host–pathogen interactions. Traditional bulk immunological approaches often average responses over millions of cells, obscuring rare but functionally critical subpopulations. As already discussed, cytomics enables the identification of rare populations, the mapping of immune cell differentiation trajectories, and the quantification of functional states in both systemic and tissue-specific contexts. This level of resolution is particularly valuable in viral, bacterial, and parasitic infections, where immune responses are highly individualized, temporally dynamic, and spatially compartmentalized.

Cytomics also illuminates mechanisms of immune evasion and pathogen persistence. Viruses can induce T-cell exhaustion or suppress antigen presentation, bacterial pathogens can skew myeloid differentiation, and parasites can manipulate macrophage polarization or regulatory T-cell activity. High-dimensional single-cell analyses provide mechanistic insight into these processes and guide the development of therapeutic interventions, vaccines, and precision medicine strategies.

Cytomic Profiling of Viral Infections

Viral infections exemplify the power of cytomics in dissecting immune complexity. During acute infection, immune defense involves coordinated action of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, NK cells, B cells producing neutralizing antibodies, and antigen-presenting dendritic cells, with each population exhibiting remarkable heterogeneity. High-dimensional cytometry enables simultaneous tracking of effector, memory, exhausted, and regulatory populations, revealing their temporal dynamics and functional plasticity.

Single-cell mass cytometry studies on the response or on the vaccination for SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and HIV have revealed extensive heterogeneity among CD8+ T cells [[162], [163], [164], [165], [166], [167]]. For instance, in SARS-CoV-2, subsets of CD8+ T cells exhibit potent cytotoxicity, others display early exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and LAG-3, and a subset maintains memory potential critical for long-term immunity [168,169]. Similarly, B-cell cytomics identifies plasmablasts producing high-affinity neutralizing antibodies, memory B cells capable of rapid recall, and atypical or exhausted B-cell populations with impaired function [170,171].

During viral infection, NK cells display functional and phenotypic heterogeneity, including cytotoxic NK cells, cytokine-producing subsets, and tissue-resident NK cells in organs such as the lung, liver, and gut, which collectively orchestrate local antiviral responses [172]. Monocytes and macrophages exhibit functional diversity; for example, hyperinflammatory monocytes producing IL-6, TNF, and IL-1β correlate with severe COVID-19, while regulatory monocytes expressing IL-10 support tissue homeostasis and limit immunopathology [[173], [174], [175]].

Cytomics enables longitudinal tracking of antiviral immune dynamics, offering high-resolution insights into the temporal evolution of immune cell populations. Since the onset of the HIV epidemic, this infection has provided a major impetus for the development and refinement of advanced cytometric methodologies (Figure 7), which have subsequently been applied to a growing spectrum of infectious diseases. These approaches remain widely used today to monitor patients throughout the course of infection and treatment, enabling precise characterization of immune trajectories and therapeutic responses [[176], [177], [178], [179]]. Cytomic profiling has revealed the progressive expansion of exhausted T-cell subsets and dysregulated B-cell compartments in chronic infections such as HIV [[180], [181], [182], [183]], in stark contrast to acute infections like influenza, which are typically marked by a transient burst of effector responses followed by rapid immune resolution and restoration of homeostasis [184,185]. Cytomics in Bacterial Infections

Bacterial infections can be rapidly identified through cytomic approaches, which enable high-resolution profiling of host–pathogen interactions [186]. The immune response elicited by different bacterial species is highly variable and plays a critical role in determining the outcome of infection, ranging from effective clearance to chronic persistence or immunopathological damage. Pathogens such as M. tuberculosis, Salmonella enterica, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli, all of which can be readily detected and phenotypically or functionally characterized through cytomic approaches [[187], [188], [189], [190], [191]], elicit highly heterogeneous immune responses across both innate and adaptive compartments [[192], [193], [194], [195], [196], [197]].

High-dimensional cytomics identifies protective and pathological subsets. For instance, Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells producing IFN-γ are critical for intracellular bacterial clearance, whereas regulatory T cells or exhausted T-cell populations may allow bacterial persistence [[198], [199], [200], [201]]. Monocytes and macrophages show similar duality: pro-inflammatory subsets kill bacteria but may also induce tissue damage, while alternatively activated subsets limit pathology but permit bacterial survival [202].

Spatial cytomics reveals how tissue localization influences infection outcome. In tuberculosis or in leprosy, granulomas contain heterogeneous macrophages and T-cell populations, with their spatial arrangement determining bacterial containment vs dissemination [21,203,204]. In sepsis, dysregulated neutrophil and monocyte networks contribute to systemic inflammation or immunoparalysis [[205], [206], [207]].

Temporal dynamics are also critical. Early bacterial infections are dominated by NK cells, neutrophils, and inflammatory monocytes, while later stages involve adaptive T- and B-cell responses. Cytomics allows trajectory mapping, identifying transitions from effector to memory or regulatory states, which informs vaccine design and immunotherapeutic interventions.

Cytomics in Parasitic Infections

Parasitic infections, including malaria (Plasmodium spp.), leishmaniasis (Leishmania spp.), schistosomiasis, and toxoplasmosis, challenge the immune system with complex life cycles, tissue tropism, and immune evasion strategies. Cytomics has revealed diverse NK, γδ T, monocyte, dendritic cell, and B-cell subsets with unique functional programs, activation states, and tissue localization [[208], [209], [210], [211], [212], [213], [214], [215]]. Indeed, in malaria, cytomics identifies NK cell subsets with heightened cytotoxicity, γδ T cells producing IFN-γ, and monocytes coordinating inflammatory and regulatory programs; temporal mapping shows how these subsets evolve during parasite liver, blood, and gametocyte stages; in leishmaniasis, macrophage polarization determines parasite survival; cytomic profiling distinguishes M1 macrophages producing nitric oxide for parasite killing vs M2 macrophages that allow persistence.

Spatial analyses further reveal tissue-specific immune organization and the role of epigenetics in such immune responses. For example, in visceral leishmaniasis, immune cells cluster in the spleen and liver, forming microenvironments that influence parasite control and immunopathology; similar spatial heterogeneity occurs in schistosomiasis granulomas, where cytomics identifies immune clusters that mediate fibrosis vs those controlling egg-induced inflammation [[216], [217], [218], [219], [220]].

Data Integration and Computational Approaches

Cytomic datasets are inherently high-dimensional and multimodal, often encompassing millions of cells with dozens of functional, phenotypic, and signaling parameters. Advanced computational methods are essential for extracting meaningful biological insights from these complex datasets. Techniques such as t-SNE, UMAP, FlowSOM, PhenoGraph, and trajectory inference algorithms allow for the identification of rare cell subsets, functional transitions, and differentiation pathways, offering a detailed map of immune dynamics across multiple disease and treatment contexts. By revealing previously unrecognized subpopulations and their relationships, these computational pipelines provide a granular understanding of immune regulation and dysfunction.

Integration with complementary datasets—including transcriptomic, proteomic, epigenomic, and spatial imaging modalities—provides a truly multilayered perspective on immune responses. Epigenomic profiling uncovers the regulatory circuits that govern immune cell identity and plasticity, revealing how chromatin states shape functional potential. At the same time, advanced spatial imaging technologies, such as MIBI and IMC, add the crucial dimension of tissue architecture, mapping where cells reside and how they interact within their native microenvironments. Together, these approaches create a coherent framework that links molecular regulation to spatially organized immune behavior.

Several computational strategies facilitate this integration [221]. In particular, anchor-based approaches identify shared features or markers between datasets to create “anchors” that link similar cell populations across modalities. In practice, this allows CyTOF-defined populations to be projected onto scRNA-seq clusters, facilitating the integration of protein-level and transcriptomic information [222]. By establishing these correspondences, researchers can more accurately compare functional states, uncover rare or transitional populations, and validate findings across different measurement platforms.

Multimodal latent space models, such as MOFA+ or TotalVI, capture shared and unique sources of variation across datasets, enabling the identification of rare or transitional cell states that may be overlooked when analyzing CyTOF or scRNA-seq individually [223,224]. These models integrate multiple modalities—protein expression, gene expression, or chromatin accessibility—into a unified latent space, facilitating the discovery of coordinated immune programs and functional interactions within heterogeneous cell populations. By doing so, they allow researchers to reconstruct complex cellular hierarchies, identify key regulators of immune responses, and predict how cells transition between activation states in response to infection or therapy.

The weighted nearest neighbor (WNN) method, implemented in Seurat, integrates both RNA and protein expression data to define cell clusters with greater accuracy [225,226]. By weighting information from each modality according to its relevance for distinguishing cell types or states, WNN allows the identification of subtle differences in immune cell activation and differentiation. This multimodal approach, along with its improvements [227], is particularly valuable for dissecting complex immune responses as it can reveal rare cell populations, transitional states, and functional heterogeneity that might be obscured when analyzing single modalities alone.

CONCLUDING REMARKS AND PERSPECTIVES

Cytomics is transforming how we study host–pathogen interactions by combining machine learning, multi-omic integration, and high-resolution immune profiling. These approaches reveal dynamic cellular behaviors, predict disease trajectories, and guide personalized treatments. Real-time and spatial cytomic technologies map immune activity directly within tissues, linking mechanistic insights to clinical outcomes. As computational pipelines and large-scale immune monitoring mature, cytomics becomes a systems immunology platform that informs diagnostics, vaccine design, and targeted therapies. Ultimately, it delivers actionable, patient-specific insights that reshape infectious-disease management and public health strategy.

Improving the Understanding of Host–Pathogen Interactions

Machine learning and predictive modeling are increasingly applied to these integrated datasets to forecast infection outcomes, identify protective immune signatures, and guide therapeutic interventions. Predictive cytomic models can stratify patients with sepsis based on monocyte and T-cell network patterns, predict progression of viral infections, or assess vaccine responsiveness by analyzing dynamic changes in NK cells, γδ T cells, and memory B-cell populations. Advanced algorithms, including DL, RF, and SVM, can integrate multi-omic features to model complex immune behaviors and infer clinically actionable patterns, enabling precision immunology and personalized medicine.

Emerging technologies promise to further enhance our ability to study the immune system in both health and disease. Real-time cytomic monitoring allows continuous assessment of immune cell activation and signaling, while multiplex imaging cytometry provides spatially resolved maps of cellular interactions in tissue microenvironments. Integrated multi-omic platforms combine transcriptomic, proteomic, and epigenetic layers with cytomic and spatial data, generating a holistic view of infection progression and therapeutic response. These innovations facilitate dynamic, spatially resolved tracking of immune landscapes, enabling interventions to be tailored to specific tissue contexts, pathogen stages, and patient immune profiles.

Cytomics will continue to expand our understanding of host–pathogen interactions by providing frameworks to dissect cellular heterogeneity, immune plasticity, and coordinated network dynamics across viral, bacterial, and parasitic infections. By mapping rare cell types, functional transitions, and intercellular networks, cytomics informs vaccine design, guides therapeutic intervention, and enables personalized medicine strategies. Furthermore, the combination of computational modeling, machine learning, and integrated multi-omic analyses positions cytomics at the forefront of predictive immunology, capable of anticipating disease trajectories and informing precision treatment strategies. These approaches not only deepen mechanistic understanding but also support the translation of cytomic insights into clinical applications, including the identification of biomarkers for disease severity, therapeutic responsiveness, and immune resilience.

Clinical Role for Cytomics

Cytomics bridges the gap between immunology and clinical applications, providing an unparalleled resolution of immune cell phenotypes, functional states, and intercellular network dynamics. Single-cell-level analysis uncovers rare immune populations, identifies transitional states such as T-cell activation or exhaustion, and maps complex signaling pathways that orchestrate immune responses. This fine-grained understanding not only illuminates the basic biology of immune regulation but also clarifies how immune networks respond to diverse challenges, including viral infections like influenza and SARS-CoV-2, bacterial pathogens such as M. tuberculosis, and parasitic infections like malaria or leishmaniasis. By dissecting these responses at the cellular and molecular level, cytomics provides a framework to connect mechanistic immunology with clinical outcomes, enabling a more precise interpretation of disease progression.

Furthermore, cytomics has transformative potential in diagnostics and prognostication. High-dimensional immune profiling can reveal functional signatures predictive of disease severity, treatment response, or susceptibility to infection. For instance, during sepsis, specific monocyte and T-cell network patterns identified via mass cytometry can stratify patients at higher risk of poor outcomes, guiding early therapeutic decisions. Similarly, in the context of vaccination, cytomic profiling of NK cells, γδ T cells, and memory B cells can identify individuals likely to mount strong protective responses, informing personalized immunization strategies. Such approaches move beyond traditional biomarkers, offering a dynamic and multiparametric view of immunity that reflects both the cellular composition and the functional state of the immune system.

Targeted therapeutics and rational vaccine design are also enhanced by cytomic insights. By integrating data across multiple omics layers—proteomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and spatial imaging—researchers can identify key regulatory nodes and signaling hubs that serve as optimal intervention points. This allows therapies to be precisely directed at dysregulated immune pathways, for example, modulating hyperinflammatory responses in COVID-19 patients or enhancing antigen-specific B-cell activation in suboptimal vaccine responders [228]. In vaccine research, cytomics can reveal correlates of protection, enabling the development of next-generation vaccines that elicit robust and durable immunity across diverse populations.

Cytomics in the Future

At a population level, cytomics will contribute to public health optimization and epidemiological surveillance. By profiling immune landscapes across individuals and cohorts, it is possible to monitor vaccine efficacy, track immune recovery after infection, and detect emerging immune vulnerabilities in communities. For example, large-scale immune monitoring in influenza or COVID-19 vaccine programs can identify population subgroups with inadequate immune responses, informing booster strategies or alternative vaccination approaches. These insights are particularly valuable in vulnerable populations, including the elderly, immunocompromised individuals, and those with chronic diseases, where conventional approaches may not capture subtle immune deficits.

Finally, the integration of next-generation computational pipelines, high-dimensional machine learning frameworks, and interoperable multi-omic datasets—eventually supported by international consortia that enable rigorous cross-cohort harmonization and federated data-sharing architectures [229]—is positioning cytomics as a quantitative systems-immunology platform. This methodological ecosystem incorporates advanced feature-space engineering, batch-correction strategies, probabilistic modeling of cellular states, and trajectory inference algorithms, collectively enabling the resolution of immune perturbation signatures and host–pathogen interaction dynamics with unprecedented analytical precision. Cytomics enables the design of adaptive treatment regimens, informs individualized vaccine scheduling, and supports the rational development of immunotherapies calibrated to patient-specific immune architectures.

As technological platforms advance—integrating real-time cytomic monitoring, spatially resolved multimodal imaging, and in situ functional profiling—cytomics is poised to transform infectious disease management. Beyond providing a mechanistic resolution of immune processes, it delivers clinically actionable insights that guide therapeutic decision-making, enhance patient stratification, and ultimately shape public health strategies in an increasingly complex infectious disease landscape.
