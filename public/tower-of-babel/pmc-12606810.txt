Association between living arrangements and health outcomes among stroke survivors in China
Authors: Bifan Zhu, Duo Chen, Xiaohui Hou, Kun Fang, Yunwei Zhang, Chunlin Jin, Hansheng Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is the leading cause of death and a major cause of disability in China, posing significant challenges for post-stroke care and rehabilitation. Understanding how different living arrangements affect the health outcomes of stroke survivors is increasingly important. This study aims to compare different living arrangements-either living at home or in care institutions-and to evaluate their impact on stroke survivors’ health.MethodsBased on the cross-sectional survey data from the Long-Term Care Insurance of Shanghai, China from January to June 2023, we identify the key characteristics of stroke survivors living in different locations. Kernel-based propensity score matching is employed to enhance comparability between groups. Health outcomes, including self-reported health status, and post-stroke infections, are compared between the two living locations.ResultsAmong 54,575 stroke survivors, 47,347 (86.76%) reside at home, 7,228 (13.24%) live in care institutions. Stroke survivors residing in health institutions are generally older and exhibit worse disability status compared to those living at home. The median Barthel Index of the home group is 10 (6–13), and 5 (2–9) in the institution group (P < 0.05). Individuals living at home (odds ratio [95% CI]) report poorer health status (1.349[1.238–1.470]) and higher risks of pulmonary infections (1.319[1.150–1.513]), urinary tract infections (1.745[1.458–2.090]) and pressure sores (2.410[2.027–2.866]). The Barthel Index correlates with improved self-reported health status, and a reduced risk of pulmonary infections and pressure sores.ConclusionsThis is the first large-scale study in China to systematically assess the living arrangements of stroke survivors, revealing significant disparities in health outcomes based on living location. The findings underscore potential limitations in both home care and institutionalized care for stroke survivors and highlight the need for enhanced support in both settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06503-6.

Introduction

Stroke has emerged as a significant public health challenge in China, with approximately 3.4 million incident cases in 2020 [1]. Nearly two-thirds of stroke survivors will experience significant morbidity [2] and 12.5% of whom are left disabled [3]. Post-stroke infection is common morbidity in stroke patients, affecting approximately 9.14% ~ 30% of patients [4, 5]. Common post-stroke infections include pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and pressure sores, which are reported to be associated with worse clinical outcomes [6]. Hence effective stroke intervention requires close collaboration among care providers across the continuum of care, including preventive services, emergency care, acute treatment, rehabilitation, and long-term care.

To ensure timely and appropriate treatment, several countries-such as the United States [7], Canada [8], and Japan [9]-have established integrated stroke care systems that coordinate various services delivered across multiple settings, from prevention, through hospitalization, rehabilitation to discharging home. China began developing its stroke care system in 2012. The current system primarily emphasizes emergency response and acute care [10], while rehabilitation services and long-term care for stroke patients remain underdeveloped [11], leading to inadequate service provision. For instance, the proportion of stroke patients discharged to rehabilitation and nursing hospitals is 13.58% in Shanghai, China, lower than the corresponding rates in the US (44%) [12], Canada (35%) [13], and Australia (39%) [14].

China is currently building a "9073" eldercare model, which means 90% of the care for older people is provided at home, 7% of the older population rely on community care, and 3% of the older population are cared by institutions depending on their health needs. Understanding how different living arrangements affect the health outcomes of the stroke survivors is crucial for policy making and resource allocation. However, there is lack of study on the current living arrangements and health outcomes of stroke survivors using large scale survey data in China. The aim of this study is to identify the long-term living arrangements of stroke survivors, either living at home, or in care institutions, and its impact on the health outcomes of stroke survivors in China.

Methods

Study design and data source

The Unified Needs Assessment Form for Elderly Care Questionnaire (hereinafter referred to as the Questionnaire) is the official tool utilized by Shanghai Healthcare Security Bureau since 2016 for determining the level of elderly care need for individuals covered by the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI). The development and validation process of the Questionnaire is described in detail in another manuscript [15]. Shanghai LTCI covers all basic medical insurance enrollees aged 60 and above in Shanghai, reaching 6.06 million population in 2023. All eligible participants who need compensation from the LTCI need to be assessed using the Questionnaire and are provided with different services regarding their care needs.

Study population

The study collects the cross-sectional survey data from Shanghai Healthcare Security Bureau from January to June 2023. The inclusion criteria were: 1) older adults aged 60 and above; 2) basic medical insurance enrollees in Shanghai; 3) applying for LTCI compensation and voluntarily participating in the survey; 4) diagnosed with stroke. A total of 177,808 residents in Shanghai applied for LTCI compensation and were assessed using the Questionnaire. Among these, 56,535 individuals were diagnosed with stroke.

Two trained assessors, including a general practitioner who is familiar with the applicant’s health status, conducted the survey in the participant’s living area. They independently assessed the applicants' daily functioning and health condition. Stratified by living arrangements, the sample population is divided into two groups, those who live at home and those who live in a care institution (either in a nursing home or in a medical institution)-when the assessment is conducted. This study design adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Measurements and variables

The Questionnaire contains four main components: 1)Demographic characteristics and living situation of the applicants; 2) Functional status evaluated by the trained assessor; 3) Overall health status of the applicants; 4) Underlying diseases, including eighteen common chronic diseases affecting functional ability of the older population, the clinical diagnosis and progression of which are evaluated by the general practitioner.

The Questionnaire is used to collect the participants’ demographic characteristics, body function and clinical characteristics. Demographic characteristics include age, gender, education level, main sources of income, marital status, number of family members, cohabitation status, which are collected by the trained assessor based on self-report or by caregivers. Characteristics of body function include Barthel Index, and loss in body function, which are evaluated by the trained assessor. The Barthel Index is used to evaluate the patient’s ability to perform daily activities without assistance [16]. Participants are assigned into four groups based on their Barthel Index: normal, mild disability, moderate disability, and severe disability. Clinical characteristics include types of stroke, and major comorbidities, which are collected by the general practitioner.

The occurrence of pulmonary infections, urinary tract infections and pressure sores are defined as main outcomes of post-stroke infections. The evaluation of post-stroke infections is conducted by the general practitioner who is familiar with the participant’s medical conditions, based on whose medical records during the past six months. The major health outcome of the participant is self-reported health status. Participants were asked to rate their health status on a scale comprising five categories: very good, good, average, bad, and very bad. They were then categorized into two groups based on their responses: those reporting poor health status bad or very bad) and those not reporting poor health status (very good, good, or average).

Statistical analysis

The study uses descriptive statistical analysis to examine the demographics, clinical characteristics, and the occurrence of post-stroke infections of the stroke survivors living in different places. For categorical variables, we report the frequency and proportion, while for continuous variables, since the data does not conform to the normal distribution, the median and interquartile range (IQR) are used. The study utilizes the Pearson χ2 test and independent t-test to compare characteristics between the two groups.

Kernel-based propensity score matching is utilized to minimize bias between the two groups. A bandwidth of 0.05 is selected in the kernel-based matching process to compromise between bias and precision. The covariates used for matching include age, sex, education level, income source, marital status, number of family members, type of stroke, Barthel Index, body function, and comorbidities. After completing the matching process, a logistic regression analysis is performed on the matched sample to examine the relationship between living location and health outcomes. The p values less than 0.05 are considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses are conducted using STATA 17.0 MP.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 177,808 residents in Shanghai applied for compensation from the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) and were assessed using the Questionnaire between January and June 2023. Among these, 56,535 individuals were diagnosed with stroke. We excluded those with extremely severe disease status, those in the end stages of life, individuals diagnosed with advanced tumors, and those lacking self-reported health status information. Consequently, 54,575 stroke survivors were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study population inclusion flowchart

Study population inclusion flowchart

Among the study group, 47,347 people live at home, accounting for 86.76%, while 7,228 people live in care institutions, accounting for 13.24%. The mean age for institutionalized stroke survivors is 83.69 compared with 80.66 for those living in their own homes (p < 0.05). In terms of social support, the percentage of individuals who are unmarried, divorced, or widowed is higher among those residing in care institutions. The proportion of stroke survivors living alone is 15.14% in the home group and 9.92% in the institution group.

Clinical characteristics

In terms of clinical characteristics, the proportion of hemorrhagic stroke is higher among the home group (6.33%) than in the institution group (5.13%). The median Barthel Index in the home group is 10, with an interquartile range of 6 to 13, while in the institution group the number is 5 (2–9). The rate of moderate and severe disability is higher in the institution group (95.77% vs 74.28%, p < 0.05). The institution group also exhibits a higher rate of functional decline, including motor dysfunction, speech and cognitive impairments, and social dysfunction (p < 0.05). The overall occurrence of post-stroke infection among the home group and the institution group are 9.81% and 10.76%. The rate of pulmonary infection is higher among the institution group than in the home group (6.36% vs 5.46%, p < 0.05), whereas the incidence of urinary tract infections and pressure sores do not differ significantly between the two groups. Logistic regression analysis based on the matched sample revealed that stroke survivors living at home are associated with a significantly higher likelihood of reporting poor health status (OR = 1.349, 95% CI: 1.238–1.470, p < 0.01) compared to those living in health institutions. Additionally, the home group faces increased risks of developing pulmonary infections (OR = 1.319, 95% CI: 1.150–1.513, p < 0.01), urinary tract infections (OR = 1.745, 95% CI: 1.458–2.090, p < 0.01) and pressure sores (OR = 2.410, 95% CI: 2.027–2.866, p < 0.01).

Males have a higher likelihood of developing pulmonary infections while demonstrating a reduced likelihood of reporting poor health status and experiencing urinary tract infections. Participants who derive their income from sources other than pensions, primarily family support, are less likely to report poor health status and to experience both pulmonary and urinary tract infections.

The Barthel Index correlates with a reduced risk of poor health status (OR = 0.832, 95% CI: 0.812–0.851, p < 0.01), as well as a reduced risk of pulmonary infections (OR = 0.949, 95% CI: 0.916–0.982, p < 0.01) and pressure sores (OR = 0.771, 95% CI: 0.735–0.808, p < 0.01). Participants with speech impairment are at greater risk of developing pulmonary infections. While cognitive deficit is associated with poorer health status and an increased risk of developing pressure sores. Individuals with specific comorbidities are also more likely to report poorer health status. Notably, the presence of coronary heart disease was associated with an increased likelihood of reporting poorer health (OR = 1.231, 95% CI: 1.026–1.476, p < 0.05), as was the presence of lung disease Discussion

Characteristics of stroke survivors with different living arrangements

Our study indicates that the percentage of stroke survivors residing at home (86.76%) aligns with findings from prior studies [17]. Additionally, the proportion of stroke survivors living alone (14.45%) is consistent with a study conducted in Japan (14.9%) [18], although it is lower than that reported in other studies. For instance, Redfors P et al. identified that 28% of stroke survivors in Sweden live alone [19], while the number is 22.8% in Canada [20]. This difference may be partially attributed to the older age of our sample population, which is related to the inclusion criteria of the LTCI system in Shanghai, China, that only covers residents aged 60 years and above. The mean age of our sample population is relatively high (81.05 ± 8.44), compared with 57 years in the Swedish study, and 72.24 years in the Canadian study. Older adults are more dependent on family support or institutional care due to increased physical and cognitive limitations, which may reduce the likelihood of living alone after a stroke.

Stroke survivors living in care institutions tend to be older and exhibit higher risks of functional decline, consistent with findings from previous research. Prior studies have shown that patients with more severe diseases and greater functional disabilities are more likely to be transferred to hospitals and nursing homes, indicating extra need for intensive care [21, 22]. The median Barthel Index of those living in care institutions (either in nursing homes or in medical institutions) is 5/20 (interquartile range: 2–9), which is comparable to the research conducted in South London, with a median Barthel Index of 6/20, and the interquartile range is 2–10 [17]. However, for stroke survivors living in their own homes, the rate of moderate and severe disability reaches 74.28%, with a median Barthel Index of 10 (interquartile range: 6–13). While in South London, most stroke survivors residing at home live without disability, with a median Barthel Index of 20 (interquartile range: 17–20). The differences might be due to the older age of our sample population. Selection bias may also occur, as only older individuals whose disability status significantly affects their daily lives would apply for LTCI subsidies and thus be assessed using the Questionnaire. Despite this, the results still reveal that access to rehabilitation and long-term care for stroke survivors remains limited in China. Rehabilitation medicine in China began relatively late, and there is a shortage of rehabilitation professionals and facilities. It is estimated that among patients in need of rehabilitation, only 16.7% are able to receive appropriate care [23]. A large number of stroke patients are unable to be referred timely for rehabilitation services, which leads to suboptimal recovery of patient function. Given the limited accessibility to institutionalized care, many individuals with significant care needs reside in environments that may not adequately meet their requirements.

Factors associated with self-reported health status and post-stroke infections

In our study, the proportion of stroke survivors reporting normal or good health status is 29.99%, which is lower than the results reported in a study conducted in Sweden, where 40.2% to 46.3% of participants reported good health status at various intervals post-stroke [24]. Additionally, a population-based study involving 145 stroke survivors ten years after stroke found that 62% reported good self-rated health [25]. Since our study only includes patients aged 60 years and older, the advanced age of the participants may contribute to the observed decline in health status. The possibility of moral hazard should also be considered, as participants assessed via the Questionnaire may overstate their health problems in order to be classified at a higher level of care need, thereby qualifying for increased LTCI subsidies.

Although the prevalence of post-stroke infections was reported to be as high as 30%, a latest systematic review has shown a threefold decline in the number. The pooled prevalence of post-stroke infections was estimated to be 9.14% [5], which aligns with our findings, the overall prevalence of post-stroke infections in our study was 9.94%. Regarding risk factors for post-stroke infections, previous studies have shown that male sex and a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are predictive of post-stroke pneumonia [26]. Conversely, female sex has been identified as a risk factor for urinary tract infections [27, 28]. While greater functional impairments have been associated with the development of pressure sores [29]. Our study observed similar patterns in these associations. Consistent with previous studies, performance in activities of daily living is positively associated with better self-reported health status [24].

The living arrangements, i.e., whether the stroke survivors reside at home or in care institutions, can greatly influence the patients’ recovery and health outcomes. Our research indicates that individuals living at home report poorer health status, and face increased risks of pulmonary infections, urinary tract infections and pressure sores. Previous studies have reported similar findings, indicating higher risks of infections and poorer physical health in home settings due to limited medical care and equipment [30]. For instance, a systematic review found that the prevalence of pressure sores among stroke patients living at home was significantly higher compared to those hospitalized (17.25% vs 3.06%), suggesting inadequate post-discharge care and monitoring [31]. Stroke patients usually require ongoing rehabilitation service and nursing care after discharge. However, due to a lack of medical knowledge and caregiving skills among family members, they are often unable to detect complications in time or provide effective support. A cross-sectional survey conducted in Shanghai revealed discouraging status of the home-based health management provided by community health centers. The overall service frequency for elderly stroke patients with functional impairments ranged from once every six months to once annually. Notably, 18.75% of the community health centers do not provide home-based health management for stroke patients [32]. These results underscore the challenges faced by stroke survivors in home settings, particularly in receiving adequate care, which may lead to higher rates of post-stroke infections and negative health outcomes.

As a consequence of population aging and improved survival rates following stroke, the functional disability and the complexity of health problems among stroke survivors are expected to increase. Given the current “9073” pension policy, a substantial proportion of stroke survivors continue to reside at home into older age, family is considered to play an important role in function recovery and prevention of post-stroke complications in the long-term recovery stage. It is essential to implement strategies that enhance family support. Effective family support includes providing caregivers with the necessary resources, education and training (especially for informal caregivers at home) through telenursing [33]. Emotional support for family members is also essential in sustaining caregiving capacity and well-being [34]. In addition, implementing home-based nursing care delivered by specialized professionals can significantly improve patients’ ability to live independently and reduce the incidence of complications [35].

Study limitations

There are several limitations in this study. First, disease course and the time of stroke onset of the study population remain unclear. Second, selection bias may have influenced the sample, as individuals applying for LTCI subsidies are typically those whose disability status significantly affects daily functioning. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to older adults with less severe impairments who do not seek LTCI support. Finally, the study is conducted in Shanghai, an economically developed urban area in China with a significant aging population. Therefore, the generalizability of the study findings may be limited to similar settings. Future studies should focus on conducting time series analyses to track the health outcomes of stroke survivors longitudinally, providing insights into the long-term effects of different care environments. Expanding the sample population to include individuals who do not apply for LTCI would help mitigate selection bias and enhance the generalizability of findings. Additionally, leveraging linked administrative and clinical data across care settings-from acute treatment through rehabilitation and long-term care-would enable more accurate tracking of patient trajectories and a deeper understanding of how living arrangements influence health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant disparities in health outcomes among stroke survivors based on their living arrangements. Notably, those residing at home have a higher likelihood of reporting poor health status and a greater incidence of post-stroke infections compared to those who are institutionalized. These findings underscore systemic challenges in home-based care delivery, particularly within the framework of China’s “9073” eldercare model, which emphasizes aging in place for the majority of older adults.

Given the increasing prevalence of stroke-related disability and the limited reach of rehabilitation services, our results suggest that current infrastructure and caregiving resources for home-dwelling stroke survivors are insufficient to meet their complex needs. Policy efforts should focus on strengthening home and community-based services by investing in caregiver training, expanding access to professional nursing care, and enhancing coordination across care settings, ensuring that stroke survivors receive appropriate care in their preferred living environment.
