Daunorubicin‐45 Vs. Daunorubicin‐60 for Induction in Intermediate‐Age Patients of AML: Results From a Randomized Trial
Authors: Chunlin Zhou, Le Li, Yan Li, Kaiqi Liu, Yuntao Liu, Dong Lin, Benfa Gong, Guangji Zhang, Xiaoyuan Gong, Qiuyun Fang, Bingcheng Liu, Shaowei Qiu, Ying Wang, Hui Wei, Yingchang Mi, Jianxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDaunorubicin dose optimization remains crucial for AML treatment. While 90 mg/m2 benefits younger adults and 45 mg/m2 remains standard for older patients, the optimal dose for patients aged 55–65 years is unclear. We evaluated whether intermediate dose escalation to 60 mg/m2 would improve outcomes compared to that of standard 45 mg/m2 in this population. In this prospective, randomized, open‐label, single‐center trial, newly diagnosed AML patients aged 55–65 years were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive daunorubicin 45 mg/m2 (Group S) or 60 mg/m2 (Group M) on days 1–3, combined with cytarabine for the induction. At median follow‐up of 35.9 months, no significant difference in OS was observed between the two groups (HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.80–1.91; p = 0.333). Median OS was 48.7 months (95% CI 28.2–NR) in Group S vs. 33.0 months (95% CI 22.2–71.6) in Group M. Five‐year survival rates were comparable (45.2% vs. 39.8%). CR rates after first induction were equivalent (47.1% vs. 44.4%; RD +2.7%, 95% CI –17.4 to 12.0; p = 0.720). MRD‐negative CR rates showed no difference (35.6% vs. 32.2%; p = 0.632). RFS (HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.84–2.26; p = 0.201) and EFS (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.81–1.74; p = 0.389) were similar in both arms. Regarding safety, while overall infection rates were similar, Group M showed a significantly higher incidence of documented intestinal infections compared to Group S (22.5% vs. 8.0%; p = 0.011). Exploratory subgroup analyses revealed no consistent patterns favoring either strategy across clinical or molecular subgroups. Daunorubicin dose escalation from 45 to 60 mg/m2 provides no clinical benefit but significantly increases mucosal toxicity. Therefore, 45 mg/m2 should remain the preferred regimen for this population. Introduction

AML is a group of malignant clonal diseases originating from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. For over two decades, the combination of cytarabine (Ara‐C) and an anthracycline, such as DNR, typically known as the “7 + 3” regimen, has been the standard induction therapy for AML. Research has continuously focused on adjusting the dose of anthracyclines to achieve better therapeutic effects across different patient groups [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The initial CALGB study [8] demonstrated that for patients younger than 60 years, DNR at 45 mg/m2 significantly improved CR rates compared to that of 30 mg/m2 (72% vs. 59%, p < 0.03). However, for patients older than 60 years, a 30 mg/m2 DNR dose was found to be significantly more effective in achieving CR than that of 45 mg/m2 (47% vs. 31%, p < 0.05). Following this, with the establishment of the 45 mg/m2 dose as a standard in clinical practice and improvements in supportive care, multiple studies began to support increasing the DNR dose to 90 mg/m23,5,6. The ECOG E1900 trial [9] was the first to clearly demonstrate that in younger AML patients (under 60 years old), 90 mg/m2 DNR was superior to 45 mg/m2 in both CR rates (70.6% vs. 57.3%, p < 0.001) and OS (median OS 23.7 months vs. 15.7 months, p = 0.001). This benefit extended to favorable and intermediate cytogenetic risk groups, and with longer follow‐up and multivariate analysis, also to the unfavorable cytogenetic risk group, as well as to patients with FLT3‐ITD, NPM1, and DNMT3A mutations. For older patients (aged over 60 years), while 90 mg/m2 DNR did improve the CR rate (64% vs. 54%, p = 0.002), the OS benefit was not significant. However, a survival benefit (EFS increasing from 14% to 29%, OS from 23% to 38%) was specifically observed in the 60–65 years age subgroup [3]. This highlights the importance of optimizing DNR dosage regimens for specific patient populations. Currently, for younger adults with good performance status, 90 mg/m2 DNR can enhance remission rates and significantly improve survival. Conversely, for patients over 65 years old, a more conservative choice is often considered due to concerns about treatment tolerability, as general treatment outcomes tend to deteriorate with increasing age.

Patients aged 55–65 years represent a critical transitional age group. While they might tolerate intensive treatment better than those over 65 years old, they may still be more susceptible to high‐dose toxicity than the youngest cohorts. Given the increased 60‐day mortality observed in the UK NCRI AML17 trial [5] when comparing 90 mg/m2 DNR with 60 mg/m2 DNR (10% vs. 5%, p = 0.001), even in a cohort with a median age of 53 years, the 60 mg/m2 dose emerges as a potentially relevant option to balance efficacy and toxicity. The crucial question of whether this “intermediate” 60 mg/m2 dose is sufficient to provide better induction and survival in this specific “intermediate‐age” or known as the “younger‐older” age group, particularly in direct comparison to 45 mg/m2, still lacks definitive prospective treatment data.

Therefore, we conducted this phase IIb, prospective, randomized open‐label clinical study, planning to enroll approximately 200 intermediate‐age patients with newly‐diagnosed AML (excluding APL) aged 55 ~ 65 years who were diagnosed between April 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023, in the Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College. Patients were randomly assigned to two groups: The intermediate‐dose DNR group (Group M) and the standard DA induction therapy group (Group S). This study aimed to evaluate the CR rate, OS, and treatment‐related toxicities of different therapeutic regimens in these AML patients.

Methods

Participants

Newly diagnosed AML patients (excluding APL) aged 55–65 years were enrolled from our institution between April 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023. Diagnosis was initially confirmed by bone marrow morphology, immunophenotyping, and cytogenetics according to the 2008 Revision of the WHO classification [10]. Due to the long study duration, and to ensure consistency with the most current classification, all diagnoses and prognostic stratifications for the purpose of efficacy evaluation were subsequently re‐evaluated and confirmed using the International Consensus Classification [11] and the ELN recommendations [12]. The sample size was calculated based on the hypothesis that dose intensification would improve overall survival compared to standard therapy. Assuming a 5‐year overall survival of approximately 10%–15% in the control arm (45 mg/m2) based on historical benchmarks [2, 3] for this age group, and targeting an HR of 0.67 with 80% power and a two‐sided alpha of 0.05, approximately 196 events were required. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 200 patients. Although the final enrollment (N = 177) was slightly below this target due to feasibility constraints, the study maintained sufficient power to detect significant differences in safety endpoints. Patients were randomized 1:1 using computer‐generated sequences with variable block sizes. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee and conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (NCT02432872).

Treatment Regimen

The detailed implementation of the treatment protocol is illustrated in Figure 1. Patients were randomized to Group M (n = 90, daunorubicin 60 mg/m2/day) or Group S (n = 87, daunorubicin 45 mg/m2/day), both administered days 1–3 with cytarabine 100 mg/m2/day on days 1–7. To balance the treatment pathways after the first induction, regardless of whether CR was achieved, all patients received a second induction with the IAC/MAC regimen. Post‐remission therapy comprised two cycles of intermediate‐dose cytarabine followed by two cycles of DA consolidation. Allogeneic transplantation was recommended for adverse/intermediate‐risk patients and high‐risk favorable patients per physician assessment. Treatment continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, subject or investigator's refusal, or as defined by protocol.

CONSORT diagram. Abbreviations: NE, not evaluable; HSCT, hematological stem cell transplantation; ITT, intention‐to‐treat; CR, complete remission.

Endpoints and Evaluation

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as time from enrollment to death or last follow‐up. Secondary endpoints included RFS, EFS, CRc, and MRD negativity. CRc encompassed CR, CRh/Cri, and MLFS. Treatment response was assessed 3–4 weeks after chemotherapy completion according to ICC2022 criteria. MRD negativity was defined as < 0.1% target cells by multiparameter flow cytometry. Follow‐up evaluations occurred every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 5 years post‐treatment. AEs were recorded, reported, and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 (NCI‐CTCAE v5.0).

Supportive Care

Supportive care measures included prophylactic antibiotics and antifungal agents according to institutional guidelines. Recombinant human G‐CSF was not used prophylactically. Therapeutic G‐CSF was administered only when the ANC was < 0.5 × 109/L and was discontinued after the ANC exceeded 1.0 × 109/L for three consecutive days. All patients received irradiated blood products as indicated.

Complete treatment implementation could be found in the Doc S1.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses followed intention‐to‐treat principles using pre‐specified hierarchical testing (OS, RFS, EFS, CRc rate, MRD negativity) to control family‐wise error at α = 0.05. Continuous variables were compared using Welch's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical data were analyzed with χ2 or Fisher's exact tests. Binary endpoints were expressed as risk differences with 95% confidence intervals. Time‐to‐event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios were obtained from Cox proportional‐hazards models. All analyses used R 4.4.2 (packages listed in Table S2) with two‐sided p ≤ 0.05 indicating significance. Induction death was defined as death from any cause within 30 days of treatment initiation.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

At baseline, the two treatment cohorts were broadly comparable, as shown in Table 1. A total of 177 patients were randomly assigned to receive either DNR 45 mg/m2 (n = 87) or DNR 60 mg/m2 (n = 90). The median age was 58 years (range 56.0–60.5) in Group S and 59 years (range 57.0–62.0) in Group M ( p = 0.117). Male patients comprised 51.7% and 45.6% of Groups S and M, respectively ( p = 0.454). Most patients had an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1 (89.7% in Group S vs. 90.0% in Group M; p = 0.793). Physical examination findings were similar between the two groups, with hepatomegaly present in 13.4% vs. 14.1% ( p = 0.825), splenomegaly in 14.6% vs. 16.5% ( p = 0.597), and lymphadenopathy in 6.2% vs. 5.9% ( p = 1.000) of patients in Groups S and M, respectively. The median bone marrow blast percentage was 64% (range 44%–77%) in Group S and 62% (range 42%–78%) in Group M ( p = 0.990). Most patients (93.1% vs. 96.7%) had white blood cell counts < 100,000/μL at presentation ( p = 0.324). FAB classification distribution was similar between the two groups ( p = 0.886), with the M5 subtype being most common (51.7% vs. 52.2%).

Notably, there were significant differences in ELN‐2022 risk stratification between the two groups ( p = 0.020). Group S had a higher proportion of adverse‐risk patients compared to Group M (36.8% vs. 18.9%), while favorable‐risk patients were more frequent in Group M (46.7% vs. 41.4%). Intermediate‐risk patients comprised 21.8% of Group S and 34.4% of Group M. Cytogenetic abnormalities showed no significant differences between these two groups. However, MDS‐related mutations were significantly more prevalent in Group S than in Group M (33.3% vs. 15.6%; p = 0.008). This difference was driven primarily by the SRSF2 mutation, which occurred exclusively in Group S (5.7% vs. 0%; p = 0.027). Other epigenetic‐related mutations (43.7% vs. 41.1%; p = 0.762) and RAS‐related mutations (36.8% vs. 36.7%; p = 1.000) were balanced between the two groups.

Survival

With a median follow‐up of 35.9 months, no statistically significant difference in OS was observed between the two treatment groups. All time‐to‐event outcomes were shown in Figure 2. The median OS was 48.7 months (95% CI 28.2–NR) in Group S and 33.0 months (95% CI 22.2–71.6) in Group M. The hazard ratio for death in Group M compared to Group S was 1.24 (95% CI 0.80–1.91; p = 0.333), indicating no significant survival advantage for either dosing strategy. At 5 years, approximately 45% of patients in Group S and 40% of patients in Group M remained alive, with survival curves demonstrating overlapping confidence intervals throughout the follow‐up period.

When patients who underwent HSCT were censored at the time of transplant, the survival difference remained non‐significant (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.77–1.84; p = 0.433). This analysis accounted for the potential confounding effect of subsequent HSCT on survival outcomes.

Since the primary endpoint of OS did not reach statistical significance, secondary survival endpoints were analyzed with nominal p‐values. Relapse‐free survival showed no statistically significant difference between groups (HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.84–2.26; p = 0.201). Similarly, event‐free survival did not differ significantly between treatment arms (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.81–1.74; p = 0.389). These findings were consistent with the primary OS analysis, suggesting comparable long‐term efficacy between the two DNR dosing strategies in this intermediate‐age population.

Response Outcomes

In the intention‐to‐treat set (Group S, n = 87; Group M, n = 90), no clinically or statistically significant advantage of either dose was observed for any remission end‐point (Table 2). After the first induction cycle, CR was achieved in 47.1% of Group S patients vs. 44.4% of Group M patients, corresponding to a risk difference (RD) of +2.7 percentage points (95% CI −17.4 to 12.0; p = 0.720). MRD‐negative CR (CRMRD−, assessed by multiparameter flow cytometry) occurred in 35.6% and 32.2% of patients, respectively (RD +3.4 pp., 95% CI −17.4 to 10.5; p = 0.632).

After two induction cycles, the overall remission rate (CR + CRh + CRi + MLFS) was 74.7% in Group S and 72.2% in Group M (RD +2.5 pp., 95% CI −15.5 to 10.5; p = 0.708). Within this composite, conventional CR was obtained in 67.8% vs. 61.1% of patients (RD +6.7 pp., 95% CI −20.8 to 7.4; p = 0.352), whereas CRh, Cri, and MLFS were infrequent and balanced (4/87 vs. 6/90, 2/87 vs. 3/90, and 0 vs. 1/90, respectively). Second‐cycle MRD‐negative CR was documented in 57.5% of Group S and 51.1% of Group M recipients (RD +6.4 pp., 95% CI −21.0 to 8.3; p = 0.396). All RDs were calculated with Wilson score 95% confidence intervals; absolute between‐group CIs were confirmed with the Newcombe hybrid Wilson method. Two‐sided χ2 tests (no continuity correction) yielded p > 0.35 for every comparison, indicating that the higher DNR dose did not improve early response relative to the standard 45 mg regimen.

Exploratory subgroup analyses of OS showed no consistent pattern favoring either dosing strategy across predefined patient subgroups (Figure 3; PH test results are shown in Figure S1 and Table S1). Point estimates varied across subgroups, but confidence intervals were wide and crossed the line of no effect (HR = 1.0) for most comparisons, indicating no significant survival differences between Group S and Group M within these populations. Across major clinical and molecular subgroups, including gender, age categories (55–60 vs. 60–65 years), ECOG performance status, ELN‐2022 risk stratification, and various cytogenetic and molecular alterations, hazard ratios ranged from 0.78 to 2.60, with overlapping confidence intervals that generally included the null value. Notable molecular subgroups, such as NPM1‐mutated, FLT3‐ITD‐positive, and patients with epigenetic or RAS pathway mutations, showed no clear benefit from either DNR dosing strategy.

One outlying finding was observed in patients with WBC ≥ 100,000/μL, where a substantially elevated hazard ratio was detected (HR 11.51, 95% CI 1.15–114.82). However, this finding requires cautious interpretation due to several critical limitations: The extremely small sample size (n = 9; 6 in Group S vs. 3 in Group M), the exceptionally wide confidence interval reflecting high statistical uncertainty, and the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses in a study where the primary endpoint was negative. This observation may represent a chance finding and would require validation in larger patient cohorts before any clinical conclusions can be drawn.

Consistent with the OS findings, subgroup analyses of early response rates showed similar patterns across patient subgroups (Figure S2). These exploratory analyses collectively support the main study conclusion that DNR dose escalation from 45 to 60 mg/m2 does not provide clinical benefit in this intermediate‐age AML population.

Safety

The safety profile was evaluated in 176 patients who received at least one dose of study treatment (87 in Group S, 89 in Group M, Table 3). Both regimens demonstrated comparable safety profiles with no clinically meaningful differences in treatment‐related toxicities.

Hematologic Toxicities: As expected with intensive induction chemotherapy, severe myelosuppression occurred universally. Leukopenia was profound in both groups, with median nadir white blood cell counts of 0.48 × 109/L (IQR: 0.30–0.69) in Group S vs. 0.42 × 109/L (IQR: 0.28–0.59) in Group M (p = 0.224). The median duration of severe leukopenia (WBC < 1.0 × 109/L) was comparable at 10 vs. 11 days, respectively (p = 0.103). Neutropenia was similarly severe, with median nadir absolute neutrophil counts of 0.03 × 109/L in Group S and 0.02 × 109/L in Group M ( p = 0.152). Time to neutrophil recovery (ANC ≥ 500/μL) was equivalent between the two groups, with median durations of severe neutropenia of 12 vs. 13 days ( p = 0.130). Thrombocytopenia was equally pronounced, with median nadir platelet counts of 8.0 × 109/L vs. 6.0 × 109/L ( p = 0.322) and comparable recovery times.

Non‐hematologic Toxicities: Infectious complications were further analyzed. While the overall rates of documented infections were comparable between groups (80.9% vs. 79.3%), we identified a significantly higher incidence of documented intestinal infections in Group M compared to Group S (22.5% vs. 8.0%, p = 0.011). Other specific infections, including pneumonia, bacteremia, and invasive fungal disease, showed no significant differences (Table 3). Hepatotoxicity was infrequent and balanced between groups (5% vs. 6%, p = 1.000). However, all events were grade 1–2 and did not result in treatment modifications or clinical sequelae. Electrolyte disturbances were common but manageable, with hypokalemia occurring in 66% vs. 60% of patients (p = 0.239).

Treatment‐related Mortality and Hospitalization: Early mortality was low and not significantly different between groups, with induction death occurring in 1 patient (1.1%) in Group S and 3 patients (3.4%) in Group M ( p = 0.621). Median hospitalization duration was equivalent (23 vs. 22 days, p = 0.757), indicating no impact of dose intensity on treatment delivery or recovery time.

The safety profile demonstrates comparable toxicity profiles between dosing regimens. Given the absence of efficacy benefit from dose escalation and the similar safety profiles, these findings support the continued use of standard‐dose DNR (45 mg/m2) in this intermediate‐age population, as there is no compelling rationale for routine dose intensification to 60 mg/m2.

Discussion

In this randomized trial, we demonstrate that DNR dose escalation from 45 to 60 mg/m2 does not improve treatment outcomes in intermediate‐age AML patients (55–65 years) with good performance status. These results support continued use of standard‐dose DNR (45 mg/m2) in intermediate‐age AML patients, avoiding unnecessary dose escalation that provides no benefit while potentially increasing long‐term cardiotoxicity risk. For these “younger‐older” patients, treatment decisions should focus on optimizing supportive care, incorporating molecularly targeted agents when appropriate, and considering clinical trial participation rather than empiric dose intensification.

Several important limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First is the significant baseline imbalance that occurred between treatment arms, particularly in ELN‐2022 risk stratification, with more adverse‐risk patients allocated to the standard‐dose group. Although multivariable adjustment and inverse probability weighting analyses confirmed the robustness of primary findings, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded in this unblinded randomized trial. While the uniformity of supportive care at our institution minimized inter‐site variability, the single‐center nature inevitably limits the external validity and generalizability of our findings. Treatment practices may vary across institutions and influence outcomes, and validation through larger, multicenter cooperative group trials is warranted. Also, subgroup analyses were exploratory and limited by sample size, preventing definitive conclusions about dose effects in molecularly defined populations. This limitation was particularly evident in the FLT3‐ITD subgroup. Assessment of Cox proportional hazards assumptions using Schoenfeld residuals revealed significant violations of the proportional hazards assumption in FLT3‐ITD patients (p < 0.01), with residual plots showing marked slope changes at approximately 9 months of follow‐up (Figure S1 and Table S3). Attempts to address this violation through landmark analyses at various time points (9, 12, and 24 months) were unsuccessful due to insufficient statistical power. Finally, a limitation of our study design is the administration of a uniform second induction (IAC/MAC) to all patients regardless of their initial response. While this strategy serves as a therapeutic ‘safety net’ to minimize induction failure in this high‐risk intermediate‐age population, it inherently introduces a confounding factor. The possibility that this protocol‐mandated, uniform second course reduced potential differences between the arms in long‐term survival outcomes cannot be excluded. The immediate response metrics (CR rate and CRMRD‐ rate) after the first course showed no statistically significant differences between the two arms; nonetheless, it is important to note that the study was designed for survival outcomes rather than to establish statistical equivalence for these early response endpoints. Therefore, the lack of long‐term divergence should be interpreted carefully in the context of the study design.

Our study complements and refines the dose–response relationship established by earlier landmark studies. The ECOG‐1900 trial definitively showed that escalating DNR from 45 to 90 mg/m2 improves outcomes in patients under 60 years, with benefits diminishing in older cohorts. Subsequent investigations confirmed this age‐dependent effect, leading to widespread adoption of 90 mg/m2 in younger patients. Our data now fill a critical knowledge gap by demonstrating that intermediate dose escalation to 60 mg/m2 in the 55–65 year‐age group confers no therapeutic advantage. Furthermore, as a national referral center, our cohort represents a vigorous ‘stress test’ for dose intensification, characterized by a high prevalence of adverse‐risk features. The complete absence of a survival signal in this enriched, difficult‐to‐treat population suggests that if dose escalation fails here, it is unlikely to confer benefit in a broader, lower‐risk community setting. This reinforces the existence of a threshold effect in the DNR dose–response curve, where increments falling short of 90 mg/m2 are insufficient to overcome age‐related biologic resistance while still carrying potential cumulative toxicity risks.
