XTC-Net: an explainable hybrid model for automated atelectasis detection from chest radiographs
Authors: Reenu Rajpoot, Sweta Jain, Vijay Bhaskar Semwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtelectasis, characterized by partial or complete lung collapse, presents notable challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. Timely identification is essential to avoid further pulmonary complications and to facilitate early intervention. Leveraging artificial intelligence for the automated detection of atelectasis can significantly improve diagnostic efficiency, reduce clinical workload, and enhance patient care. This work presents an interpretable deep learning model that synergistically integrates Xception, Transformer, and Capsule Network components for the accurate detection of atelectasis from chest radiographs. The Xception module is employed to extract spatially rich features, while the Transformer component models long-range dependencies critical for understanding complex anatomical patterns. The Capsule Network further enhances the system’s sensitivity to subtle structural variations associated with atelectatic regions. The training and validation of the model were conducted using a publicly accessible chest X-ray dataset, achieving impressive performance metrics: 99.73% accuracy, 99.74% sensitivity, and an F1 score of 99.73%. Furthermore, to evaluate the model’s generalizability, external validation was performed using the NIH ChestX-ray dataset, which demonstrated consistent performance and highlighted the applicability of the proposed approach beyond the primary dataset. These results underscore the model’s capability for reliable and interpretable automated diagnosis, supporting its future integration into clinical workflows.Keywords: Atelectasis, Capsule network, Deep learning, Transformer, XAI, XceptionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Atelectasis, a condition where part or all of a lung collapses, is a serious challenge in clinical practice, and its early and accurate detection is very important for proper treatment1. The condition may occur due to blocked airways, pressure on the lungs, or after surgery. If not treated on time, it can reduce oxygen levels, weaken breathing, and increase the risk of infections2,3. These complications highlight why early and precise diagnosis is essential, since delayed detection can make treatment more difficult and worsen patient outcomes.

Chest X-rays are the most widely available and affordable method for examining lung conditions4,5. Their availability in both small clinics and large hospitals makes them a common choice for screening and follow-up in respiratory care, including atelectasis. However, identifying atelectasis from chest X-rays is not straightforward. Its appearance can vary widely and may overlap with other chest problems. Because of this, accurate diagnosis often depends on a radiologist’s expertise, which can lead to variation between observers and occasional errors, especially in busy clinical settings.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning have greatly improved medical image analysis. Automated image classification has been applied successfully to chest X-rays6, CT scans7, and MRI8, showing clear improvements in performance, accuracy, and consistency9–11. By reducing reliance on human judgment, deep learning has accelerated the development of computer-aided diagnosis systems that can support clinicians in real time12,13. In chest imaging, many studies have used deep learning to detect diseases such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, and pleural effusion, often with strong results14,15. Still, the detection of atelectasis has received less attention, even though it is clinically important and often difficult to diagnose correctly. A key challenge in applying deep learning to healthcare is whether models can work well on new and different datasets. Many systems perform strongly on the training data but do not generalize well to other patient groups or imaging conditions. This is especially important in medical imaging, where changes in equipment, patient populations, and disease frequency can affect performance. External validation on independent datasets is therefore essential to prove the reliability of such systems. At the same time, interpretability is also critical: models must not only predict accurately but also show which image features guide their decisions, so clinicians can trust and use them in practice.

In this study, we propose XTC-Net, a new three-stage fusion model designed for the automated detection of atelectasis from chest X-rays. This model combines three techniques: Xception, Transformer blocks, and Capsule Networks (CapsNets). Xception helps extract meaningful spatial features, the Transformer module captures long-range relationships important for understanding chest structures, and CapsNets improve the detection of small but important shape differences linked to atelectasis. By combining these components, the model achieves effective feature learning and interpretable predictions. It was trained and evaluated on widely recognized chest X-ray datasets, consistently outperforming several existing benchmark approaches16,17. Furthermore, results obtained through five-fold cross-validation and subsequent analysis underscore its strong potential for integration into automated clinical workflows.

We believe this hybrid model can capture both detailed and global image features needed for reliable classification of atelectasis. Our goal is to create an accurate and efficient system that works not only on the training dataset but also on external datasets, while providing interpretable outputs that support clinical decision-making. In doing so, we aim to move closer to practical and trustworthy computer-aided diagnostic tools for respiratory diseases. The important research contributions in our work are as follows:

As part of the preprocessing pipeline, we apply Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to enhance the local contrast in chest X-ray images. Although CLAHE is a commonly adopted technique, its targeted use in this study improves visual quality and facilitates more effective extraction of discriminative features for atelectasis detection.

Literature work

This paper seeks to improve detection strategies for atelectasis and contribute to the advancement of current diagnostic practices. In recent years, several investigations have explored the application of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for identifying atelectasis in chest X-ray images.

Ayalew et al.18 achieved a test accuracy of 98.79% for atelectasis detection using a combination of VGG19 and Inception models. Their approach leveraged transfer learning to extract deep features from chest X-rays, which were then fine-tuned for classification. The study demonstrated that integrating multiple CNN architectures can significantly improve performance for detecting challenging lung conditions such as atelectasis. Nam et al.19 introduced DLAD-10, a chest radiograph-based deep learning model known for its strong classification performance, achieving ROC AUC values ranging from 0.895 to 1.00. DLAD-10 notably enhanced diagnostic accuracy, expedited reporting for critical and urgent cases, and improved workflow efficiency in radiology.

Huang et al.20 investigated transfer learning’s efficacy for atelectasis detection on portable chest X-rays, leveraging a sizable dataset from MIMIC-CXR. Training on 60,000 images yielded an AUC of 88.57%, sensitivity of 75.10%, specificity of 88.30%, and accuracy of 81.70%. External validation conducted on 300 ICU chest X-ray images sourced from the ICU unit of a Jinan University-affiliated hospital showed strong performance, with an AUC of 98.39%, sensitivity of 70.70%, specificity of 100%, and accuracy of 86.90%, supporting the model’s generalizability and lesion localization accuracy. In a related effort, Shanthi et al.21 discussed a deep learning approach aimed at the prediction of thoracic conditions such as Atelectasis, Cardiomegaly, and Pleural Effusion. Their model achieved 82% accuracy on both training and testing sets, highlighting the effectiveness of CNNs in enhancing disease detection and accelerating diagnostic workflows. These studies collectively demonstrate the growing promise of AI-driven methods for improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in chest disease classification.

Rahib et al.22 assessed BPNNs and CpNNs on 380 images, with BPNN2 achieving 99.19% and 89.57% recognition rates for training and testing, respectively. CpNN2 demonstrated the highest overall recognition rates, while CNN outperformed with the lowest MSE of 0.0013 and superior recognition rates. Comparative analysis with earlier works highlighted CNN’s promising performance in chest X-ray disease classification. Bharati et al.23 proposed VDSNet, a composite deep learning model that incorporates VGG, spatial transformer networks (STN), and data augmentation techniques within a CNN-based structure to improve lung disease detection. Evaluated on the NIH chest X-ray dataset, VDSNet accomplished a validation accuracy of 73% across the entire dataset, surpassing several existing models. Even when tested on a smaller subset, the model retained strong performance and required less training time, indicating its suitability for efficient and accessible diagnostic support in clinical environments. While existing deep learning models have shown promise in detecting atelectasis from chest X-ray images, they often face key limitations such as reduced generalizability beyond their training datasets, a lack of interpretability, and difficulty in capturing the subtle and heterogeneous patterns of atelectasis. Many prior approaches are evaluated only on internal datasets, which raises concerns about their applicability in real-world clinical practice. To address these gaps, we propose XTC-Net, a hybrid model that integrates Xception for efficient spatial feature extraction, Transformer blocks for modeling long-range dependencies, and Capsule Networks for dynamic routing and enhanced sensitivity to fine structural variations. In addition, interpretability is ensured through Grad-CAM visualizations, which highlight clinically relevant regions that contribute to predictions, increasing trust in the model’s decisions. Finally, unlike most existing works, the model is evaluated not only through internal cross-validation but also on an independent external dataset, demonstrating its reliability and applicability across diverse clinical environments.

Proposed XTC-Net model

The proposed XTC-Net aims at distinguishing images with deep learning, particularly those pertaining to the medical imagery of atelectasis. The model integrates the Xception architecture with attention and capsule networks. At a high level, it leverages the Xception to extract imaging features, forming the foundation for image classification. The pre-trained network is used to derive significant visual features from chest X-rays, which facilitates more effective learning and therefore improves performance. The model also utilizes a transformer block to capture more advanced spatial dependencies among the extracted features. At the classification stage, a capsule-inspired block is incorporated, where convolutional feature maps are reshaped and projected into a higher-dimensional embedding space. This design captures spatial hierarchies and orientations in a more expressive manner than plain dense layers, while still enabling efficient training. Finally, the flattened capsule representations are passed through a sigmoid classifier to produce the prediction. Xception model

The Xception model refers to the fact introduced by Francois Chollet25, building on the Modified Inception Network model. It contains a succession of depthwise sequentially distributed convolutional and residual connections. This configuration enhances computation and cuts the memory capacity by paying attention to the spatial and channel dependencies in the feature representations separately. The Xception architecture features 36 trainable layers, structured into 14 distinct blocks interconnected via residual links. The architecture utilizes depthwise separable convolutions to independently learn spatial features and channel-specific information, resulting in more efficient feature extraction. To address challenges like vanishing gradients and restricted learning capacity, residual connections–based on the approach introduced by He et al.26–are employed. These connections use element-wise addition rather than concatenation, allowing information from earlier layers to flow more effectively through the network. The proposed model employs the pre-trained Xception network, shown in Fig. 4, as its foundational feature extractor. This backbone is followed by additional convolutional operations and a capsule-inspired transformer module for classification. The Xception layers remain frozen during training, with only the newly added layers being fine-tuned.

Implementation

This section explains the major stages in developing the proposed XTC-Net model, covering data handling, preprocessing procedures, training settings, evaluation criteria, and the experimental framework employed to analyze the model’s effectiveness.

Dataset

The dataset comprises 4112 chest X-ray images, including 2075 normal images and 2037 images with atelectasis, collected from publicly available repositories16,17. To maintain balanced classification, the dataset was curated to contain nearly equal numbers of normal and atelectasis cases. After preprocessing, the dataset was partitioned using a five-fold cross-validation strategy. In each fold, 20% of the samples were set aside as the test set, while the other 80% was split into 90% for training and 10% for validation (corresponding to 72% and 8% of the entire dataset, respectively). This configuration ensures consistent and balanced evaluation of the model.

Alongside the main dataset, the NIH ChestX-ray collection30,31 was utilized for external validation. This resource comprises 112,120 chest radiographs from 30,805 patients, each labeled with 14 thoracic disease categories. To fit our binary classification setting, a balanced subset of 400 Atelectasis cases and 400 “No Finding” (normal) images was selected. This subset served exclusively as an external test set to evaluate the model’s generalizability on data from a distribution different from that of the training set. Dataset distribution

Model performance on atelectasis detection was examined using five-fold cross-validation. The dataset was split into five balanced partitions, each preserving the distribution of the two classes. In every cycle, one partition acted as the test set, and the other four were aggregated. A random 10% of this aggregated data was designated as the validation set, while the remainder was used for training the model. This setup ensured that the test set remained fixed and independent in each fold, while validation samples varied depending on the split, supporting robust hyperparameter tuning and monitoring during training. Nevertheless, the use of rotation across folds allowed comprehensive utilization of the dataset while maintaining disjoint splits within each fold. Experimental setup

We employ Adam for optimization and adopt cross-entropy loss, which is ideal for differentiating among multiple classes, during training. To boost the diversity of training samples and strengthen the model’s resilience to overfitting, several augmentation techniques are employed, including random rotations, translations, zoom operations, and horizontal flips. The model’s predictive capability is measured using comprehensive evaluation metrics such as specificity, sensitivity, overall accuracy, and the geometric mean. Additionally, performance is further analyzed through class-level summaries provided by a confusion matrix and a detailed classification report.

Results and discussion

The proposed XTC-Net model was rigorously evaluated for its ability to classify atelectasis in chest radiographs. The model achieved an average accuracy of 99.73% (95% CI: 99.61–99.85), sensitivity of 99.74% (95% CI: 99.59–99.89), and specificity close to 100%, demonstrating robustness and stability across partitions. Precision and F1-score were also notably high, averaging 99.66% (95% CI: 99.46–99.86) and 99.73% (95% CI: 99.61–99.85), respectively. Ablation study of XTC-Net components

The ablation study in Table 8 highlights the role of each component and backbone in shaping the performance of XTC-Net. When the Capsule module or Transformer block is removed, performance drops compared to the complete model, indicating that both contribute complementary benefits to feature representation and decision-making. Similarly, replacing the Xception backbone with alternative CNN architectures (ResNet50, DenseNet-169, VGG16, Inception-V3) also leads to reduced accuracy and F1-scores, though DenseNet-169 provides relatively competitive results. Among all variants, the XTC-Net consistently outperforms others across all metrics (Accuracy = 99.73%, F1-score = 99.73%), demonstrating that the combination of Xception, Transformer, and Capsule modules yields the most robust and discriminative feature learning for Atelectasis classification.

Performance benchmarking against existing models

Deep learning has gained considerable attention for diagnosing and classifying atelectasis in chest radiographs. To evaluate its effectiveness, we implemented the XTC-Net model for this classification task. Table 9 presents a performance comparison with existing methods, demonstrating that XTC-Net achieves superior accuracy and exhibits strong resilience and reliability in identifying various types of atelectasis.

Although XTC-Net achieved an accuracy of 99.73% on the internal dataset, its performance decreased to 90.59% on the external dataset. This reduction can be attributed to domain shifts between datasets, including variations in image acquisition protocols, preprocessing steps, and scanner characteristics. Differences in patient populations and disease prevalence further impact the model’s generalization. These findings highlight the inherent challenge of maintaining consistent performance across diverse data sources in medical imaging applications.

Quantitative evaluation of model explanations

We quantitatively assessed the trustworthiness of the visual explanations provided by each component of XTC-Net using positive and negative perturbation tests33. During a positive perturbation evaluation, the image areas marked as highly significant by the interpretation methods are obscured first. If the model’s accuracy drops sharply, it confirms that these regions are essential for correct predictions. On the other hand, in a negative perturbation evaluation, masking begins with regions judged to be unimportant, which usually causes only a minor or negligible reduction in performance. The results demonstrate that adding the transformer and capsule modules progressively improves the faithfulness of the visual explanations, confirming that each component contributes not only to predictive performance but also to interpretable and clinically meaningful model reasoning.

Limitations

XTC-Net demonstrates promising results for automated atelectasis detection from chest radiographs. While the outcomes are encouraging, a few aspects can be further refined in future work. The dataset employed in this study, though comprehensive, may not capture the complete diversity of real-world clinical data, such as variations in patient positioning, imaging devices, and acquisition protocols, which could influence generalizability. Additionally, the absence of patient-level identifiers limited the application of grouped cross-validation. The difference observed between internal (99.73%) and external validation (90.59%) highlights the impact of domain variations, suggesting opportunities to enhance model robustness through domain adaptation techniques. Lastly, since XTC-Net integrates Transformer and Capsule Network components, optimizing its computational efficiency could further support its deployment in resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Conclusion and future work

In this study, we proposed XTC-Net, an explainable hybrid model integrating Transformer and Capsule Network architectures for automated atelectasis detection from chest radiographs. The model effectively combines the global feature extraction capability of Transformer blocks with the spatial representation strength of Capsule Networks, achieving a high image-level classification accuracy of 99.73% while maintaining interpretability through Grad-CAM-based visualization. Evaluation across internal and external datasets demonstrated strong and consistent performance, confirming the model’s potential for real-world diagnostic applications.

Future work will focus on extending validation across diverse clinical settings to improve the model’s generalization and robustness. We also plan to optimize XTC-Net for computational efficiency and real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments, enabling practical integration within clinical decision-support systems. In addition, incorporating expert feedback will help align model explanations with clinical reasoning, and multimodal information such as CT scans and patient metadata will be explored to enhance diagnostic reliability and decision support.
