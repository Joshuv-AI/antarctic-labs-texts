ZAD mediates chromatin binding and insulator activity of Drosophila Pita and can be replaced with the human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain
Authors: Yulia Vasileva, Olga Kyrchanova, Natalia Klimenko, Maria Necheukhina, Anna Fedotova, Oksana Maksimenko, Pavel Georgiev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe zinc finger-associated domain (ZAD), found in numerous Drosophila architectural proteins, such as Pita, enables homodimerization. Despite its prevalence in insects, only one human protein, ZFP276, possesses a ZAD-like domain. To date, the role of Pita has been studied in the formation of the boundaries of regulatory domains in the Bithorax complex, and the functional significance of its ZAD remains unclear.ResultsUsing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated pita replacement with an attP site, we generated flies expressing modified Pita variants. Null pita mutants die in the late stages of embryogenesis. Flies expressing Pita lacking ZAD, PitaΔZ, exhibit reduced viability. Genome-wide chromatin immunoprecipitation revealed that PitaΔZ retains binding to housekeeping gene promoters and insulators, cooperating with other architectural C2H2 proteins and CP190. However, ZAD is essential for Pita binding to specific chromatin regions and its insulator function. Strikingly, the ZAD-like domain from human ZFP276 can functionally substitute for the ZAD in Pita.ConclusionsZAD is critical for the insulator activity of Pita and its ability to efficiently bind to specific genomic regions. The human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain may function similarly to the ZAD of Pita, raising the question of why ZADs spread in insects but not in mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-025-00641-2.

Introduction

Proteins with multiple Cys2-His2 zinc-fingers (C2H2) arranged in arrays connected by five-amino acid linkers, constitute the largest family of transcription factors in animals [1–5]. Many proteins have more than five C2H2 domains organized in a cluster. A defining feature of these multidomain C2H2 proteins is their capacity for specific recognition of extended DNA sequences of 12–40 bp [6]. The best-known member of this group, CTCF, is a major mammalian architectural protein that is involved in the formation of chromatin loops between regulatory elements and topologically associated domains (TADs), promoter organization, and diverse nuclear processes [7–10]. Evidence suggests other, less-characterized mammalian C2H2 proteins fulfill similar architectural roles [11–14]. In Drosophila, a subset of C2H2 proteins, including the mammalian homologue CTCF, dCTCF, function as architectural factors by organizing promoters, insulators, and long-range chromatin contacts [15].

A distinctive evolutionary adaptation within many insects is the distribution of C2H2 proteins with an N-terminal zinc finger-associated domain (ZAD) [6, 16, 17]. The defining property of ZAD is its strong propensity for homodimerization, with heterodimerization being rare [18–20]. This tendency towards homodimerization underlies the remarkable expansion and diversification of ZAD-C2H2 proteins in lineages such as Drosophila, where they comprise nearly half of all C2H2 proteins [19]. In a transgenic model system, ZAD-mediated homodimerization facilitates specific long-range chromatin interactions capable of modulating enhancer-promoter communication [21, 22]. Although proteins with the ZAD-C2H2 architecture have become widespread among some insects, only one potential analogue is known in mammals, ZFP276, the N-terminal domain of which has common structural and homodimerization properties with Drosophila ZADs [20, 23]. The ZFP276 protein contains N-terminal ZAD-related domain and C-terminal cluster consisting of five C2H2 domains. ZFP276 controls expression of a variety of growth and metastasis-related genes and also facilitates the translocation of β-catenin from cytoplasm to nucleus [24].

Although most ZAD-C2H2 proteins remain poorly studied, characterized members have a profound impact on chromatin organization. Pita and Zw5 proteins are involved in organization of promoters and insulators [21, 25, 26]. M1BP and ZIPIC play key role in formation of housekeeping (hk) gene promoters [27–31]. Some other known ZAD-C2H2 proteins (Molting Defective, Ouija Board, and Séance) determine the activity of heterochromatic promoters that regulate the expression of ecdysone biosynthesis genes during larval development [32, 33]. Kipferl is involved in recruiting the Rhino complex to heterochromatin, which is required for the expression of piwi RNAs that suppress mobile elements in the gonads [34, 35].

The well-studied Drosophila ZAD-C2H2 protein Pita is an example of a factor with architectural functions, notably in establishing several boundaries between regulatory domains in the Bithorax complex (BX-C) [36, 37]. Pita contains an N-terminal ZAD domain that uniquely forms stable tetramers [20] and a C-terminal cluster of 10 C2H2 zinc fingers, six of which are responsible for DNA binding with a specific 18 bp motif [28]. The unstructured linker between the ZAD and the C2H2 cluster contains a 13-amino acid core (220–232 aa) and an adjacent auxiliary region that work together to mediate the interaction of Pita with the BTB domain of CP190 [26].

Here, we investigated whether ZAD is required for Pita activity and tested the ability of the human ZFP276 N-terminal ZAD-like domain to substitute for ZAD. Expression of a ZAD-deleted Pita mutant (PitaΔZ) only partially rescued Pita function, failing notably in insulator activity. However, a chimeric protein, PitaZ276, in which the Pita ZAD was replaced by the ZFP276 N-terminal ZAD-like domain, fully restored Pita function. These results demonstrate that the human ZFP276 N-terminal ZAD-like domain is functionally equivalent to a Drosophila ZAD.

Results

Generation of pita mutations and transgenic lines

The pita gene (CG3941, synonym spdk, cytological position 59E3) contains three exons (Fig. 1A), with the Dcp-1 gene located within its first large intron. There are no well-characterized null pita mutations. To achieve complete pita inactivation, we generated a 261 bp deletion (pita1attP, dmel|2R: 23554440.23554180, r6.53) within the first exon (Fig. 1A). The pita1attP carries the attP site for the φC31 recombinase-mediated pita replacement and 3×P3:DsRed reporter, flanked with loxP sites, for the detection of deletion events. Homozygous pita1attP mutants survived only to late stage of embryogenesis, with no larvae observed, in contrast with earlier studies on strong hypomorphic mutations in the pita(spdk) gene [38, 39].

To rescue pita function, we inserted the Pitawt transgene into the pita1attP platform. Pitawt expresses a C-terminally 3×FLAG-tagged (3×F) Pita protein under the control of the Ubi-p63E promoter (Fig. 1B). Pitawt fully complements the homozygous pita1attP mutation, restoring the normal fly phenotype, viability, and fertility. This confirms the absence of off-target effects from the Cas9-mediated deletion.

To investigate ZAD function and test the ability of the human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain for functional replacement of the ZAD of Pita, we generated two additional transgenes: PitaΔZ, lacking amino acids (aa) 1–109 (including the entire ZAD), and the chimeric PitaZ276, in which the ZAD of Pita is replaced with ZAD-like domain of ZFP276 (77–163 aa). Both variants were also tagged at the C-terminus with 3×F. (Fig. 1B). Flies expressing PitaZ276 exhibited a wild-type (wt) phenotype, viability, and fertility, similar to Pitawt at both 18 °C and 25 °C. In contrast, while flies expressing PitaΔZ displayed a wt phenotype and viability only at 25 °C. PitaΔZ adults retain viability and fertility when moved from 25 °C to 18 °C. However, the offspring have a high mortality rate at the embryonic stage and their development is slower by 2–3 days relative to Pitawt and PitaZ276 flies. Adult females have a near-normal phenotype, but their fertility is significantly reduced due to underdeveloped vaginal plates (Additional File 1: Fig. S1). We also studied how reduced expression of Pita variants would affect their functional activity in heterozygotes with the null pita1attP mutation. PitaZ276/pita1attP and Pitawt/pita1attP flies are viable and fertile, while PitaΔZ/pita1attP are lethal.

Western blot analysis revealed that Pitawt expression was at least threefold higher than endogenous Pita in wt (y1w1118) flies (Fig. 1C). PitaΔZ expression levels were comparable to endogenous Pita, while PitaZ276 expression was approximately twofold higher than PitaΔZ (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that all Pita variants are expressed at higher or comparable levels to endogenous Pita. The functional activity of PitaΔZ is reduced relative to Pitawt and PitaZ276.

Pita expression level does not affect chromatin binding efficiency

We assessed the genome-wide binding efficiency of Pita variants using chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) with antibodies against the unstructured C-terminal region of Pita [26] and the FLAG epitope. Chromatin was isolated from 2- to 3-day-old adults of wt and transgenic lines expressing Pitawt, PitaΔZ, and PitaZ276.

Given the threefold higher expression of Pitawt compared to endogenous Pita, we analyzed Pitawt binding profile relative to Pita in the wt line. Overall binding profiles were highly similar across most genomic regions and identical binding motifs were identified (Fig. 2A). Despite the expression difference, Pita protein bound with comparable efficiency to nearly all motif-containing regions and a large subset of motif-free regions (Wilcoxon signed-rank test p = 0.296, in combined set of N = 1929 sites, Fig. 2B). Only a small number of motif-free sites showed binding efficiency directly proportional to the Pita expression level. These findings demonstrate that the Pita expression level is not the primary determinant of the chromatin binding of Pita.

ZAD is essential for Pita binding to a specific group of chromatin sites

ChIP-seq results using both Pita-specific (N = 1189 sites for Pitawt) (Additional File 1: Fig. S2) and FLAG (N = 1785) (Fig. 3) antibodies yielded comparable peak numbers and distributions for Pitawt, PitaΔZ, and PitaZ276, allowing classification into four well-defined site groups. Below, the main analysis is carried out on the data obtained with antibodies to the FLAG epitope.

Group 1 (WT, N = 578 for FLAG antibodies) contains sites that show reduced binding for both PitaΔZ and PitaZ276 compared to Pitawt (Fig. 3A). These sites harbor a relatively degenerate motif (Fig. 3B, C). While the motif was not detected in many peaks (N = 350), reproducible binding confirmed by both antibodies validates Pita occupancy (Additional File 1: Fig. S2). Pitawt binding was generally stronger at motif-containing sites within this group (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 2·10− 7). The markedly lower binding of PitaZ276 implicates the Pita ZAD in binding to these genomic regions.

In Group 2 (WT_Z276, N = 516), sites show reduced binding only in PitaΔZ, not PitaZ276, compared with Pitawt (Fig. 3A). Most peaks contained a motif closer to the consensus than Group 1 (Fig. 3B). As in Group 1, motif-free sites bound Pita variants noticeably weaker (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 1·10− 9 for Pitawt and p = 8·10− 13 for PitaZ276) (Fig. 3B). While Pita predominantly binds promoters, less than half of Group 1 (35%) and 2 (44%) sites were promoter-associated and many resided within gene bodies (exons, introns, and untranslated regions) (Fig. 3D). Notably, motif-free sites were more frequent in promoters (39 vs. 27% for WT and 71 vs. 59% for WT_Z276).

The Pita sites in these groups (Fig. 4) do not co-localize with the architectural C2H2 proteins M1BP, ZIPIC, and dCTCF that recruit CP190 to promoters of housekeeping (hk) genes and GAF binding sites that are associated with developmentally regulated genes and their enhancers [27]. ATAC-seq data showed that the Pita peaks from these groups were found in relatively dense chromatin (Fig. 4).

In the remaining Groups 3 and 4, Pita binding sites have similar genome distribution and properties that are different from the Groups 1 and 2. Sites in group 3 (WT_Z276_ΔZ, N = 657) are bound equally efficiently by all three Pita variants (Fig. 3A). Most sites contained consensus motifs (Fig. 3B, C), motif scores in Groups 3 and 4 were stronger than in Groups 1 and 2 (Mann-Whitney U test, p < 1⋅10− 15), with only 27% lacking an identifiable motif. Group 4 (ΔZ-gain, N = 234) includes sites where PitaΔZ binds more efficiently than Pitawt or PitaZ276 (Fig. 3A). Almost all sites in this group contained a consensus motif (85%, Fig. 3B, C).

Peaks in Groups 3 and 4 predominantly colocalize with M1BP, ZIPIC, dCTCF, and CP190 proteins in hk gene promoters (Fig. 4). ATAC-seq data in adult flies [40] confirmed that these Pita peaks reside in open chromatin (Fig. 4), consistent with hk promoters localization. We found that GAF was enriched in Groups 3 and 4, similar to M1BP (Fig. 4).

Finally, we assessed the enrichment of Pita binding groups at topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries (Fig. 3E). Unlike mammals, Drosophila TAD boundaries are mainly formed by hk gene promoters [41]. Architectural proteins, such as dCTCF, Su(Hw), M1BP, Zw5, and Pita, contribute to some TAD boundaries, but CP190, which interacts with many architectural proteins and hk promoters, plays a more central role [21, 30, 42–46]. Only 14–38% of Pita sites co-localized with TAD boundaries. Reflecting their association with hk promoters, Pita sites from Groups 3 and 4 were found within TAD boundaries approximately twice as often as those from Groups 1 and 2 (Chi-squared test, p value < 1⋅10− 5 for Group 3 or Group 4 vs. Group 1 or Group 2, Fig. 3E).

Distribution of Pita ZAD function in BX-C

The architectural proteins Pita and dCTCF collaboratively organize most of the boundaries between regulatory domains in the Bithorax complex (BX-C) (Fig. 5A). BX-C consists of three Hox genes: the Ultrabithorax (Ubx), abdominal-A (abd-A), and Abdominal-B (Abd-B).

The Abd-B has several promoters and a broad promoter region. Two Pita peaks mark the boundaries of a small (~ 11.5 kb) TAD in this region (Fig. 5B). Distal peak, ~ 1 kb upstream of the Abd-B-RJ promoter, contains three co-oriented Pita motifs adjacent to a CTCF motif. All three Pita variants bound this site efficiently, and ZAD deletion did not impair PitaΔZ binding. At the proximal TAD boundary, no Pita motif was detected, but weak Pita binding occurred, which was lost in the PitaΔZ line.

Pita participates in organizing the Fab-7 boundary, separating the iab-6 and iab-7 domains that regulate Abd-B in the A6 and A7 segments, respectively. Fab-7 comprises four DNAse I hypersensitive sites (HSs) [47, 48], three of which contain four Pita motifs: HS* (one Pita motif), HS1 (one motif), HS2 (two motifs) (Fig. 5B), and HS3 (corresponds to Polycomb response element (PRE)). HS*, HS1, and HS2 together form an effective insulator [47, 49], while individual HSs require HS3 for insulator activity [36]. Pitawt and PitaZ276 bind to the Fab-7 efficiently, but PitaΔZ binding was significantly reduced. Thus, Pita sites of Fab-7 boundary belong to Group 2 (WT_Z276).

One of Group 4 (ΔZ-enhanced) Pita peak is located in the Mcp insulator, separating the regulatory regions of the abd-A and Abd-B genes. The 210-bp core region of Mcp contains adjacent Pita and dCTCF binding sites that are essential for cooperative insulator function [37]. In this case, PitaΔZ bound to Mcp much more strongly than Pitawt or PitaZ276 (Fig. 5B).

Pita binding motifs were found in Fub and Fub-2, boundaries of a large TAD that includes the Ubx gene with its regulatory regions. Fub-2, containing three Pita motifs (Fig. 5B), separates the Ubx 3’ region from a hk gene cluster. All Pita variants bound to Fub and Fub-2 with similar efficiency.

Functional compensation of Pita ZAD deletion by the human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain

The Fub boundary separates the regulatory regions of the Ubx and abd-A genes. The bxd/pbx domain regulates Ubx and is responsible for the specification of the A1 abdominal segment. The iab-2 controls abd-A in the A2 segment providing its distinctive morphological features (Fig. 6A, B). In this boundary, in addition to one Pita motif, binding sites for dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Mzfp1 have been described [37, 50]. Loss of Fub boundary leads to bxd/pbx and iab-2 domain fusion, resulting in ectopic activation of abd-A in the A1 segment, and transformation of the A1 segment into a copy of the A2 (F2attP, Fig. 6B) [37, 51]. We previously showed that a 411 bp proximal fragment of the Fub boundary, F2411, containing Su(Hw), Pita, and Mzfp1 motifs, retains insulator activity (Fig. 6A) [50]. Mutation of the Su(Hw) motif (F2411ΔS) did not disrupt binding of the other two architectural C2H2 proteins and the insulator function of F2411ΔS (Fig. 6B). We investigated whether Pita variants would retain the ability to maintain F2411ΔS insulator activity. Combining F2411ΔS homozygotes with different Pita variants revealed that Pitawt and PitaZ276 maintain F2411ΔS insulator activity, while PitaΔZ results in loss of insulation and strong transformation of A1 into A2 (Fig. 6B). Quantitative ChIP (qChIP) confirmed that Pitawt and PitaZ276 bind with F2411ΔS equally well, whereas PitaΔZ binding was significantly reduced (Fig. 6C). Thus, Pita binding with F2411ΔS depends from ZAD and the ZFP276 ZAD-like domain fully compensates for ZAD deletion of Pita.

ZAD is essential for Pita insulator activity

As mention above, the Fab-7 boundary blocks interactions between the iab-6 and iab-7 regulatory domains [47, 48] The iab-6 and iab-7 domains regulate the Abd-B gene in the A6 and A7 segments, respectively, providing their specific morphological features (Fig. 7A, B). Fab-7 deletion or disruption of its insulator activity resulted in fusion of the iab-6 and iab-7 regulatory domains and premature iab-7 activation in the A6 segment. Subsequent overexpression of Abd-B in the A6 segment resulted in A6 segment loss in adult males (Fig. 7B).

We previously demonstrated that five co-oriented Pita consensus motifs (Pita×5) form a strong insulator. When Pita×5 replaces the Fab-7, it isolates iab-6 from iab-7 regulatory domain and the Abd-B promoter, resulting in the complete transformation of the A6 segment into a copy of A5 [36, 52]. To test the role of Pita ZAD in insulator activity, we created a new Fab-7 platform, F72attP, by replacing a 2183 bp boundary fragment (3R: 16900022.16897839, r6.53) with an attP site (Fig. 7A). As expected, F72attP deletion also resulted in the fusion of the iab-6 and iab-7 and the disappearance of A6 (Fig. 7B).

To study the efficiency of Pita binding using quantitative chromatin immunoprecipitation, we fused Pita×5 with a unique 96 bp DNA fragment (RT-Pita×5) and designed highly specific qChIP primers. RT-Pita×5 was inserted into the F72attP platform, Pita×5, resulting in the restoration of the A6 segment in males (Fig. 7B), confirming that RT-Pita×5 has insulator activity.

Combining Pita×5 with different Pita lines revealed that the expression of Pitawt or PitaZ276 retains Pita×5 insulator functions (Fig. 7B). In contrast, PitaΔZ expression disrupted Pita×5 insulator activity, leading to almost complete loss of the A6 segment in males (Fig. 7B). qChIP showed that PitaΔZ bound with Pita×5 sites as efficiently as Pitawt and PitaZ276, ruling out weak binding as the cause of insulation loss. Although CP190 promotes Pita×5 insulator activity [26], its binding to the artificial insulator was weak and also unchanged across Pita variants (Fig. 7C). Therefore, ZAD is essential for the insulator activity of Pita, and the ZFP276 ZAD-like domain can fully substitute for it in this function.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that the architectural protein Pita binds to two functionally distinct classes of regulatory elements, distinguished by their chromatin context and dependence on the ZAD.

The first class (Groups 3 and 4) encompasses Pita binding sites within hk gene promoters and part of the insulators/boundaries of regulatory domains, such as in BX-C (e.g., sites within Mcp). These elements reside in regions of open chromatin throughout Drosophila development. Available data suggest that hk gene promoters contain motifs recognized by diverse architectural C2H2 proteins, including M1BP and ZIPIC [29–31, 50]. Our findings indicate that Pita binds efficiently to near-consensus motifs within these promoters even in the absence of its ZAD (PitaΔZ). It is likely that open chromatin and other C2H2 architectural proteins promote more efficient and stable binding of Pita to motifs on these elements. Furthermore, the ubiquitous co-localization of CP190 with most hk promoters [46, 53–55] likely plays a crucial role. CP190 interacts directly with Pita and many other architectural C2H2 proteins [30, 56, 57], potentially promoting cooperative assembly and stabilization of protein complexes on these regulatory elements.

The second class includes Pita binding sites within promoters and other genomic regions that lack constitutive open chromatin (Groups 1 and 2). In our study, we did not identify transcription factors that interact with Pita upon binding to this type of regulatory regions. The Pita binding sites of this group can be further divided into two subgroups based on the ZAD functionality.

The first subgroup (e.g., sites within Group 1) includes regulatory regions that require the specific ZAD of Pita for efficient binding. The unique tetramerization interface of the Pita ZAD [20], distinct from its dimerization interface, suggests a potential role in mediating specific protein-protein interactions. The inability of the heterologous human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain (in PitaZ276) to rescue binding at these sites supports the hypothesis that the Pita ZAD engages in specific interactions of Pita with currently unidentified partner proteins. These interactions could stabilize Pita binding to degenerate motifs or within constrained chromatin, reducing reliance on motif strength. The second subgroup (e.g., sites within Group 2 (Fab-7, F2411ΔS)) includes regulatory elements with functional substitution of the Pita ZAD with the human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain (PitaZ276). This finding suggests that the core ability of ZAD/ZAD-like domains to mediate homodimerization is the critical feature for the binding of Pita at these sites. Homodimerization likely stabilizes Pita binding, potentially compensating for suboptimal chromatin environments or motif features. This parallels the role of homodimerizing N-terminal domain in stabilizing dCTCF binding at specific genomic sites in Drosophila [58].

Crucially, we demonstrate that the ZAD, or a functionally equivalent ZAD-like domain capable of homodimerization, is indispensable for the insulator function of Pita. This requirement is evident in the BX-C model system, in which the endogenous Fab-7 boundary is replaced by an artificial insulator Pita×5. Despite efficient binding to the insulator, PitaΔZ does not provide boundary function. While ZAD could theoretically mediate direct interactions with enhancer-bound proteins to block communication, the functional replacement by the heterologous ZFP276 ZAD-like domain argues against this requiring highly specific interactions unique to the Pita ZAD. Therefore, we propose that ZAD-mediated homodimerization (or equivalent interaction via the ZAD-like domain) could facilitate long-range interactions between Pita proteins bound at distant sites. Previously we demonstrated that ZAD of Pita can support distance interaction between GAL4 activator and mini-white promoter in transgenic model system [21]. However, direct demonstration of physical interaction between artificial Pita×5 sites and endogenous Pita binding regions is critical to validate this model.

The chromatin looping could physically interfere with local contacts between regulatory elements. An alternative explanation suggests that ZAD homodimerization might promote the recruitment of multiple Pita molecules and partner proteins (including CP190) to the insulator site, forming a more stable complex that acts as a barrier. Although our qChIP data showed no difference in CP190 recruitment to the Pita×5 insulator between the PitaΔZ and Pitawt lines, CP190 interaction remains essential for Pita insulator activity [26]. Further investigation is needed to dissect the precise role of ZAD in assembling or stabilizing functional insulator complexes.

The ability of the human ZFP276 ZAD-like domain to effectively replace the Pita ZAD in specific binding contexts and for insulator activity highlights a degree of functional conservation. This raises the question of why such N-terminal dimerization domains are prevalent in insect C2H2 proteins, such as Pita, but less common in mammals. While mammalian C2H2 proteins often lack analogous N-terminal dimerizing domains, they frequently possess large clusters of zinc fingers [6]. We hypothesize that specific C2H2 domains within these clusters may have evolved to mediate homodimerization or heterodimerization, fulfilling a role analogous to the insect ZAD in stabilizing protein-DNA interactions and facilitating higher-order complex formation at regulatory elements.

Methods

Generation of pita1attP and F72attP platforms

The pita1attP and F72attP deletions were obtained by CRISPR/Cas9-induced homologous recombination. As a reporter, we used pHD-DsRed vector (Addgene plasmid # 51434), which contains multiple cloning sites (mcs)-attP-loxP-3×Р3:DsRed-PAS-loxP-mcs (Fig. 1A). Arms for homologous recombination were obtained by DNA amplification from the y1w1118 line and then inserted into mcs. The primers used for generation of the pita1attP and F72attP deletions are presented in Additional File 2: Table S1. The guide RNAs were selected using the program “CRISPR optimal target finder” (http://targetfinder.flycrispr.neuro.brown.edu/, O’Connor-Giles Lab) (Additional File 2: Table S2). The 5’- and 3’-targets were cloned into the vector pCFD4- U6:1U6:3 (Addgene # 49411), using BbsI. To generate the desired deletions, the plasmid constructs were injected into Act5C-Cas9 (#58492, Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center) embryos together with two gRNAs. The concentrations used were as described previously [59].

The F0 progeny of pita1attP was crossed with the y1w1118; CyO balancer line. In five independently obtained flies with dsRed reporter deletion was confirmed by PCR and by sequencing of one of the PCR products. Next, to check the stage of lethality the y1w1118; pita1attP males were crossed with y1w1118; CyO, GFP balancer females, where CyO, GFP was obtained from the w1118; snaSco /CyO, P[ActGFP.w-]CC2 line (#9325, Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center).

The F0 progeny of F72attP were selected on the dsRed pigmentation in the eyes and/or the posterior part of their abdomens and these flies were crossed with y1w1118; TM6/MKRS flies. In five independently generated flies with the dsRed reporter deletion was confirmed by PCR and sequencing of one of the PCR products. The breakpoints of the pita1attP: 2R: 23554440.23554180, and of the F72attP: 3R: 16900022.16897839 according to FlyBase Genome release r6.53.

Generation of transgenic lines

The Pita wt strains used are Oregon (#5, Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center) or y1w1118. To express 3×Flag-tagged Pita and its derivatives, the wt cDNA of Pita or its variants were cloned in frame with 3×Flag in a vector containing the attB site for φC31-mediated recombination, the Ubi-p63E promoter with its 5′UTR, 3′UTR with SV40 polyadenylation signal and the mini-white gene as a reporter for the detection of transformants. Corresponding primers are listed in Additional File 2: Table S1. Details of the cloning procedures, primers, and plasmids used for construction are available upon request. Integration of the plasmids in the landing platforms was achieved by injecting a plasmid and a vector expressing the фC31 recombinase into pita1attP embryos. For each construct, three independent events were obtained. As a result of crossing with y1w1118; CyO, homozygous lines for transgenes were obtained. Flies from independently derived lines expressing one of the Pita variants had comparable phenotypes.

For the F72attP replacements the Pita×5 fragment [52] was fused with RT fragment from λ-phage DNA and cloned into the plasmid with the attB site, mCherry and mini-yellow reporters [60]. Successful integrations were selected on the expression of the yellow reporter in the abdominal segments. Integration of the plasmids in the landing platforms was achieved by injecting the plasmid and a vector expressing the фC31 recombinase into embryos of the corresponding attP lines. The yellow and mCherry reporters were excised by Cre-mediated recombination between the loxP sites.

Combinations of the pita mutants with [F72attP] RT-Pita×5 were obtained using CyO/If; TM6/MKRS double balancer line.

All stocks are available upon request.

Cuticle preparations

Adult flies were collected in 1.5 ml tubes and stored in 70% ethanol for at least 24 h. Ethanol was then replaced with 10% KOH, and the samples were incubated at 70 °C for 1 h. Following digestion, flies were washed twice with distilled water and incubated in water at 70 °C for approximately 45 min. Then the samples washed with 70% ethanol and stored in the same solution. The abdominal cuticles of the fly bodies were dissected using fine tweezers and an insulin syringe needle. Each cuticle was transferred to a drop of glycerol on a glass slide. The abdomens were bisected longitudinally through all tergites using the syringe needle. Additional cuts were made between tergites as needed to ensure the cuticle lay flat. Cuticles were flattened under a coverslip, and images were captured using a Nikon SMZ18 stereomicroscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Ri2 digital camera. Image processing was performed using ImageJ version 1.50c4 and the Fiji bundle (version 2.0.0-rc-46).

Protein extraction

Twenty adult flies were homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer (Wheaton) in Buffer A (15 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.6; 60 mM KCl; 15 mM NaCl; 10 mM EDTA; 0.5 mM EGTA; 0.5% NP-40; 1 mM DTT; 1 mM PMSF; and 1:500 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail). The homogenate was centrifuged at 3,000 × g, and the pellet was resuspended in Buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 10 mM KCl; 10 mM MgCl2; 2 mM EDTA; 10% glycerol; 1% Triton X-100; 1 mM DTT; 1 mM PMSF; and 1:500 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail).

The suspension was sonicated twice for 10 s at 5 W and centrifuged at 3,000 × g. The resulting pellet was washed three times with Buffer B, resuspended in the same buffer, and mixed with 4× SDS-PAGE loading buffer supplemented with 2 M urea and 300 mM NaCl. Samples were boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min, and supernatants were loaded onto 8% or 10% SDS–PAGE gels for electrophoresis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin was isolated from 8 to 16 h Drosophila embryos and 2- to 3-day-old adult flies. One gram of embryos was collected and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde as previously described [61]. For adult flies, 500 mg of tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle, then resuspended in 20 mL of Buffer A (15 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.6; 60 mM KCl; 15 mM NaCl; 13 mM EDTA; 0.1 mM EGTA; 0.15 mM spermine; 0.5 mM spermidine; 0.5% NP-40; 0.5 mM DTT) supplemented with 0.5 mM PMSF and 1:500 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail V.

The suspension was homogenized using a Potter and Dounce homogenizer with a tight-fitting pestle, filtered through a 100 μm nylon cell strainer (Miltenyi Biotec), and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Crosslinking was quenched with glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM. Nuclei were washed three times with wash buffer (15 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.6; 60 mM KCl; 15 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA; 0.1 mM EGTA; 0.1% NP-40; 0.5 mM PMSF and 1:500 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail V), then washed once with nuclear lysis basic buffer (15 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.6; 140 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA; 0.1 mM EGTA; 1% Triton X-100; 0.5 mM DTT; 0.1% sodium deoxycholate; 0.5 mM PMSF and 1:500 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail V) and finally resuspended in 1 mL nuclear lysis buffer (same as above with the addition of 0.5% SLS and 0.1% SDS).

Chromatin was sonicated using a Covaris ME220 focused-ultrasonicator (40 cycles of 15 s ON, 45 s OFF; peak power 75; duty factor 25%). Sonication efficiency and DNA concentration were assessed on 30 µL aliquots. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C.

For immunoprecipitation, specific antibodies were pre-bound to 20 µL Protein A (rabbit antibodies) or G (rat antibodies) Dynabeads in PBST (rabbit anti-Pita, 1:100; rabbit anti-FLAG, 1:200; rabbit IgG; or rat anti-CP190, 1:100; rat IgG). After equilibration in nuclear lysis buffer, the antibody-bead complexes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with chromatin containing 10–20 µg DNA in 200 µL nuclear lysis buffer. Input samples (2 µL pre-cleared chromatin) were retained.

Beads were washed three times with nuclear lysis buffer containing 500 mM NaCl, followed by a wash with TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA). DNA was eluted in elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA; 1% SDS), and crosslinks were reversed. DNA was purified using the ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research).

Enrichment of specific regions was assessed via qPCR using a StepOne Plus Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences are provided in Additional File 2: Table S1. For normalization, genomic regions with reproducible binding (e.g., CP190 at 62D) were used. The RpL32 locus served as a negative control.

ChIP-seq libraries were generated with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit per the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were quantified with a DS-11 fluorometer (DeNovix) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), clustered on a paired-end flow cell, and sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina).

ChIP-seq data processing and sequence analysis

ChIP-seq analysis was performed for 6 samples: Pita in Pitawt, PitaΔZ and PitaZ276 lines with antibodies against Pita protein and against FLAG epitope. Each sample was represented by two biological replicates. Reads were processed as described previously [62], the main steps were trimming Illumina adapters with cutadapt [63], low quality ends were trimmed with sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) (with -q 20 option)), alignment with bowtie2 [64] against version dm6 of the Drosophila melanogaster genome, filtration of PCR duplicates and non-unique mapping with picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) and blacklist filtration with bedtools [65]. Then reproducible peaks calling was conducted against corresponding preimmune controls with IDR pipeline (https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/idr). A soft p value threshold for MACS2 [66] in IDR of 0.01, the IDR reproducibility threshold was set to 0.05 for true replicates and 0.01 for pseudoreplicates. All samples showed good reproducibility (the rescue ratio or/and the self-consistency ratio were < 2).

ChIP-Seq and ATAC-Seq public data were obtained as fastq files and processed with the same procedures as newly generated data. In case of single-end public data, bowtie (with -m 1 option) was used instead of bowtie2. We used the following public data: CP190 [67], dCTCF [67], Zipic [21], M1BP [29], GAF [68], and chromatin accessibility profile (whole body ATAC-Seq Actin5c-Gal4 drivers) [40]. Also, we used public embryo Hi-C data in the analysis [45]. Fastq files were trimmed with cutadapt and proceeded to juicer conveer version 2.09 [69] to obtain a contact matrix on different levels of resolution. Obtained.hic file was converted to.cool file with 2000 bps resolution using hic-straw [70]. Contact matrix was visualized using cooltools [71]. Boundaries were identified based on insulation score calculated with cooltools using a 6000 bps window.

All data used in analysis are provided in Additional File 2: Table S2.

De novo motif discovery was performed using ChIPMunk [72, 73]. During motif discovery for endogenous Pita (y1w1118 line) and transgenic Pitawt the top-200 peaks per sample were narrowed to ± 200 bp around the summit, motif length was set to 20. In case of motif discovery for the groups of sites (WT, WT_Z276, WT_Z276_ΔZ, ΔZ) all sites were used for each group. Motif scores in binding sites were identified using sarus (https://github.com/VorontsovIE/sarus) (-log10 p value). We considered a site to have a binding motif if sarus p value was lower than 1 × 10−4. ChIP-Seq coverage tracks were obtained using deepTools [74] bamCoverage functions, with a bin width of 50 bps and RPKM normalization.

Downstream analysis was performed in R, version 4.2.2 [75]. Peak annotation was performed using ChIPseeker [76] and GenomicRanges [77] packages; promoter segments were considered as ± 200 bp from the TSS. Analysis of the overlapping peaks was performed with ChIPpeakAnno [78] and visualized using the UpSetR package [79]. Heatmaps showing ChIP-Seq signal in binding sites were produced using deepTools [74] computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions.

To obtain groups with distinct Pita binding changes under PitaΔZ and PitaZ276 modifications we firstly merged all binding sites obtained in different lines: Pitawt, PitaΔZ and PitaZ276. Then we calculated log2FC between the mean signal in each mutated line and wt in each binding site centered around the summit ± 200 bps. The groups were defined as follows:

WT group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)< −1;WT_Z276 group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;WT_Z276_ΔZ group: −1 < log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < 1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;ΔZ enhanced group: log2(PitaΔ/Pitawt) > 1.

WT group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)< −1;

WT group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)< −1;

WT_Z276 group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;

WT_Z276 group: log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < −1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;

WT_Z276_ΔZ group: −1 < log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < 1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;

WT_Z276_ΔZ group: −1 < log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < 1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)> −1;

ΔZ enhanced group: log2(PitaΔ/Pitawt) > 1.

ΔZ enhanced group: log2(PitaΔ/Pitawt) > 1.

No sites were found for the option: −1 < log2(PitaΔZ/Pitawt) < 1 and log2(PitaZ276/Pitawt)< −1.
