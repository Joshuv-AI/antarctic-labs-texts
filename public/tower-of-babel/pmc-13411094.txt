Dietary folate education versus once-weekly iron folic acid supplementation for improving folate status in reproductive-aged women: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Kannatcha Sroypechr, Carol Hutchinson, Warapone Satheannoppakao, Mathuros Tipayamongkholgul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe folate status of women of reproductive age (WRA) in Thailand is suboptimal for reducing risk of neural tube defects (NTD). This research aimed to compare the effect of dietary folate education based on the health belief model and once-weekly iron folic acid (IFA) supplementation on folate status.MethodsThis was a pre-post-test 12-week quasi-experimental study with three groups: (1.) dietary folate education program (30-min, once-weekly), (2.) 2.8 mg folic acid + 60 mg iron (IFA supplement, once-weekly), or (3.) comparison group (no intervention). Participants were WRA recruited from three factories. Knowledge, perceptions (susceptibility, severity, benefits, and barriers), self-efficacy, and cues to action in relation to folate and NTD, together with dietary folate intake, and red blood cell (RBC) folate concentration were evaluated at pretest (week 0) and post-test (week 13). The generalized estimated equation was used to analyze within-group and between-group differences at post-test, after adjusting for covariates.ResultsTwenty-three, 25, and 18 participants completed the study in the dietary folate education, IFA supplementation and comparison groups, respectively. Perceived barriers remained unchanged in the folate education group at post-test, and the mean (95% CI) increase in dietary folate intake from pretest to post-test in the folate education group was only 53.0 µg/day, after adjustment for covariates. The change in RBC folate concentration at post-test in the folate education and comparison groups did not differ (-23.1 (-29.8, 67.5) nmol/L; p = 0.307). In contrast, the IFA supplementation group showed an increase in RBC folate concentration from pretest to post-test that was 49.2 (2.7, 95.6) nmol/L greater than the comparison group (p = 0.038).ConclusionsFolate status did not improve after a dietary folate education program for 12-weeks, which could have been partly because of the intervention’s limitations. Future education programs may consider a longer intervention duration and larger sample size. Reducing perceived barriers may also facilitate behavior change. Once-weekly IFA supplementation (2.8 mg folic acid per week) for 12 weeks might contribute to an improvement in folate status in non-pregnant women in a real-world setting. However, the findings should be interpretated with caution in view of study limitations.Trial registrationISRCTN13360510 Registered 26/11/2025 Retrospectively registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01375-4.

Background

Researchers demonstrated that folic acid supplementation prevented the occurrence and recurrence of neural tube defects (NTD) several decades ago [1]. The prevalence of NTD ranges from 0.7 per 1,000 births in Southeast Asia to 3.2 per 1,000 births in South Asia [2]. Ayutthaya province, Thailand, has 1.1 cases of NTD per 1,000 live births, which is over twice the national average of 0.5 per 1,000 live births [3]. The etiology of NTD is multifactorial, involving combined risk factors which include low folate status in the presence of genetic predispositions [4].

Red blood cell (RBC) folate concentration is indicative of long-term folate status [5], and a level > 906 nmol/L is associated with the lowest NTD risk [5, 6]. However, Kwanbunjan et al. [7] reported an average RBC folate concentration of 598.6 ± 199.8 nmol/L among 15–29-year-old women from hill tribe ethnic groups in a province in Northern Thailand. An earlier study found that women in Bangkok consumed less than half the daily recommended amount of folate, and 18% had marginal serum folate concentrations (6.8—13.6 nmol/L) [8]. These data indicate that the folate status of women of reproductive age (WRA) in Thailand is suboptimal for NTD risk reduction.

In 2019, the Thai Ministry of Public Health launched a policy and practice to offer a once-weekly iron folic acid (IFA) supplement to Thai WRA free-of-charge [9], as a continuation of the country’s Red-Cheeked Women Campaign [10]. The policy is in accordance with the WHO-suggested plan for intermittent IFA supplementation [11]. Whilst randomized-controlled trials conducted in other countries have shown that once-weekly IFA supplementation for 12 to 16 weeks is efficacious in improving the folate status of women [12, 13], this approach has drawbacks. All WRA, including adolescents, can access free IFA supplements, but they must actively register for the supplement, and compliance with IFA supplementation is often low despite educational campaigns such as Thailand’s Red-Cheeked Women Campaign [9, 10, 14]. Mandatory folic acid fortification of staple foods is effective in preventing NTD, but Thailand has not implemented it for various reasons [15].

The bio-accessibility of naturally-occurring folates and folic acid in food matrices ranges from below 20% to 100% [16]. Nevertheless, evidence indicates that naturally-occurring dietary folates can help women reach an RBC folate concentration that is associated with the lowest risk of NTD [17]. Other analyses have shown a clear dose–response relationship between total folate intake (naturally-occurring folate and folic acid fortificant) and RBC folate concentration [18]. Important sources of naturally-occurring folate in the Thai diet include rice, eggs, oranges, and native green vegetables [8]. Some dietary items, including milk drinks, breads, fruit juices, and nutricosmetic beverages, are voluntarily fortified and are widely available in Thailand. On average, they contain 35 µg folic acid per serving, according to the nutrient information shown on labels. A small number of milk drinks contain higher amounts (e.g., 90–100 µg folic acid) per serving, based on label values. Folic acid is added to these products at the discretion of the manufacturer, while taking into account government policies and regulations. A product can claim to be fortified if it contains more than or equal to 10% of the Thai RDI for a vitamin, per serving [19].

Previous intervention studies involving folate education or counseling showed small improvements in dietary folate intake [20–23], whilst another did not [24]. In one, dietary education resulted in a post-intervention increase in serum folate concentration [21], whereas the other revealed no impact on RBC folate concentration [22]. A better understanding of the potential of dietary education to increase folate status would be beneficial for public health program planners in contexts where folic acid fortification is not widespread. Therefore, our study aimed to compare the effect of a dietary folate education program, based on the Health Belief Model (HBM) [25], and once-weekly IFA supplementation on the folate status of WRA. The education intervention was learner-centered and tailored towards local food culture, like some previous dietary interventions that were successful [20, 26, 27].

Methods

Design

A pretest post-test quasi-experimental study with a comparison group was conducted (February – August 2019). Food environments were first observed to assist with the development of a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (SFFQ) for folate (Supplemental Material 1) and the dietary folate education program (Fig. 1). This preparatory phase lasted 8 months, and spanned all three seasons (cool, hot, and rainy). It entailed documenting the foods and drinks that were available in five districts. We also conducted informal interviews with 10 WRA who worked at the factories, to assess their current dietary habits. Information about the dietary items that women were consuming, including where items were obtained and cooking/preparation methods, was gathered. A quasi-experimental design was used for practical and logistical reasons. Although quasi-experimental designs have limitations, the potential to see the effects of interventions in a real-world community setting is a fundamental benefit of this design [28].Fig. 1Research design, interventions and measurements

Research design, interventions and measurements

Setting

We conducted this research in Ayutthaya province, Thailand, in three factories that were 9–43 km apart. Each factory was assigned to: 1. the dietary folate education program, 2. IFA supplementation, and 3. comparison group. Factory ‘A’ received the dietary folate education program because the managers were willing to allow the researchers to conduct activities there. Factories ‘B’ and ‘C’ were then designated as the IFA supplementation and comparison groups, respectively.

Sample size

Using a formula taken from Lwanga and Lemeshow [29] and data from Hefni et al. [30], a sample size of 28 per group was deemed sufficient to detect an increase in RBC folate concentration from pretest to post-test following the folate education program versus IFA supplementation versus no intervention, with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80%. The total target sample size was 93, or 31 per group, after adding a further 10% to account for attrition.

Participants

Participants were recruited in the same way at all three factories. Women who met the inclusion criteria (20–34 years old and worked day shifts) were invited to participate in this study. Only women aged 20–34-years were invited because most NTD cases affect this age group [31], as it accounts for the majority of pregnancies [32]. Women who were willing to participate in the study attended a screening session. They were provided with further information about the study, and a screening questionnaire was administered to determine their health status and specific lifestyle habits. Women who were pregnant or lactating, or had metabolic, hereditary, or chronic infectious diseases were excluded from taking part, as were women who had previously given birth, smokers, supplement consumers, or consumers of medicines known to interfere with folate metabolism. Women who met the eligibility criteria were invited to take part.

All participants gave written informed consent, participated voluntarily, and all data were anonymized.

Interventions

Dietary folate education program

This group participated in 12 once-weekly sessions, each lasting around 30 min, between pretest and post-test measurements. The first author led the sessions. We developed the program using information gathered from observations of local markets and convenience stores nearby the factory, together with information about the dietary items that WRA at the study setting were consuming, including where items were obtained and cooking/preparation methods. Each intervention session was tailored around the constructs of the HBM [25]. The topics were as follows: 1. health benefits of folate, including NTD prevention; 2. naturally folate-rich food; 3. foods fortified with folic acid and nutrition label information; 4. dietary reference intake for folate and serving sizes of folate-rich food; 5. folate-rich foods in the vicinity of the factory; 6. choosing ready-to-eat meals or folic acid fortified products; 7. varying folate-rich meals from ready-to-eat foods and folic acid fortified products to meet the recommended intake [33]; and 8. making folate-rich meals. Topics 4, 5, 7 and 8 were repeated at weeks 9, 10, 11 and 12, to reinforce the topics covered. Activities were designed to enhance knowledge, perceptions (susceptibility, severity, benefits), self-efficacy, and cues to action, and reduce perceived barriers in relation to dietary folate, folate status and NTD. For example, a game centered around the Thai nutrition flag was intended to reduce barriers to folate deficiency prevention. The sessions were interactive, involving short presentations, small group activities, idea-sharing, food tasting, a market assignment and meal making (Table 1). The sessions in weeks 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, 10, 11, 12 focused on choosing folate-rich ready-to-eat foods that were locally available, in addition to designing and making folate-rich items which utilized locally sold ingredients. The local market-sourced folate-rich ingredients and ready-to-eat foods were generally lower priced compared with items from supermarkets, and included a wide variety of produce. Practical guidance on making affordable folate-rich choices was also covered in the intervention sessions. Dietary folate education program topics, activities and materials

IFA supplementation

The IFA group was provided with 12 tablets that contained 2.8 mg (2,800 µg) synthetic folic acid and 60 mg elemental iron as ferrous fumarate (Ferrofolic, Government Pharmaceutical Organization, Bangkok, Thailand). This once-weekly IFA supplement regimen was used because it is one of the NTD prevention measures implemented by the Thai Ministry of Public Health [9, 10]. The WHO suggest once-weekly IFA supplementation containing 2.8 mg folic acid for menstruating women where the population prevalence of anemia is ≥ 20% [11]. The justification for this once-weekly dose of folic acid is based on seven times the daily recommended supplemental dose to prevent NTD (400 µg or 0.4 mg daily × 7 = 2,800 µg or 2.8 mg) [11]. Participants were asked to consume one tablet once weekly on the same day for 12 weeks, in between pretest and post-test measurements. Participants completed a diary to document whether they followed instructions and to note any adverse effects. Participants received once-monthly reminders to take the supplements. They also returned the supplement packaging and diary at post-test.

Comparison group

These participants did not receive any intervention in between the pretest and post-test measurements.

Measurements

Socio-demographic information

A researcher-administered questionnaire was used to obtain details of participants’ age, marital status, education level, monthly income, and the number of family members in their households at pretest.

Knowledge, perceptions, self-efficacy and cues to action in relation to folate and NTD

These variables were evaluated at pretest and post-test, using a researcher-administered questionnaire (Supplementary Material 2). The knowledge section contained 16 items, each with three answer options (yes, no, don’t know). One point was awarded for a correct answer and zero for an incorrect answer and don’t know. Knowledge level was categorized as good (more than 80%), moderate (between 60–80%) and need to improve (lower than 60%). The perceptions section had 14 items, categorized as: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers. For self-efficacy there were 10 items. The cues to action section had four items. All were rated on a three-point Likert scale of strength of agreement (agree, disagree, not sure). Three experts evaluated the questionnaire’s content validity. Reliability testing was also conducted, and initial internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient) was 0.68.

Dietary folate intake

A folate SFFQ was designed for this study and used at pretest and post-test to estimate dietary folate intakes (Supplementary Material 1). The performance of the SFFQ was evaluated by correlating daily folate intakes estimated using the SFFQ with RBC folate concentration at pretest. The FFQ incorporated items which were naturally folate-rich or fortified with folic acid and were available within the three study districts and two neighboring districts. Items included ready-to-eat and made-to-order meals, snack items, fruit and drinks. The SFFQ had 163 dietary items: (1.) rice, noodle, bread and breakfast cereal fortified with folic acid, n = 17; (2.) egg, organ meat, meat, fish, shellfish, n = 12, (3.) legumes and soy products, n = 7, (4.) tuber or root crops, n = 3, (5.) vegetables, n = 52, (6.) fruits, n = 20, (7.) animal and plant milks fortified with folic acid, n = 49, and (8.) nutricosmetic beverages fortified with folic acid, n = 3. Frequency of consumption answer options were divided into 13 scales, e.g., 1 time/day, 2 times/day, etc. The first author and one research assistant were responsible for all dietary data collection using the SFFQ, together with household measures, food models and a picture book of products fortified with folic acid to estimate portion sizes. Both data collectors were experienced public health nutritionists. To standardize and ensure data collection quality, the first author trained the research assistant to use the SFFQ, including estimation of portion sizes, and observed their practice for one week. The administration of the SFFQ and estimation of portion sizes were done in the same way across all three factories. Frequency of consumption was multiplied by portion size to get the amount consumed per day. We calculated daily folate intakes using food folate data obtained from two published sources [34, 35] and label values for folic acid enriched items.

RBC folate concentration

RBC folate concentration was measured at pretest and post-test using the ARCHITECH i2000 chemiluminescent folate immunoassay (Abbott, Northern Illinois, USA), which is a commonly used automated assay that measures total folate and has good analytic performance [36, 37]. Duplicate analyses were performed, and RBC folate concentration was corrected for hematocrit [36]. RBC folate concentration is a good biomarker of folate status because it is correlated with liver folate concentration and is representative of long-term folate intake [5].

Analysis

After cleaning and coding, data were analyzed using PASW Statistics for Windows, Version 18.0. (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, U.S.A.) and STATA version 16 software (STATA Corp, College Station, TX, U.S.A.). The correlation between RBC folate concentration and dietary folate intake at pretest was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The Generalized Estimated Equation (GEE) model was applied, and used to adjust for between-group differences at pretest which can occur in intervention studies [38]. This model was used to analyze within-group differences from pretest to post-test and between-group differences at post-test in knowledge, perceptions, self-efficacy, cues to action, dietary folate intake, and RBC folate concentration, after adjusting for age, education level, and pretest values of the outcomes as covariates. Data were analyzed following the “intention-to-treat” principle.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Committee for Human Research, Faculty of Public Health, Mahidol University, Thailand (COA: MUPH 2019–011), and the study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Our study aimed to compare the effect of a dietary folate education program and once-weekly IFA supplementation on the folate status of WRA. Folate status did not improve after the 12-week dietary folate education program. Only a small increase in dietary folate intake was observed in the group that received the folate education program. This was in spite of the program being theory-based, learner-centered, and tailored for the local food environment and dietary habits of the WRA who worked at the factories.

The program promoted the consumption of local foods containing naturally-occurring folates and foods fortified with folic acid, and it was designed around the theoretical constructs of the HBM [25]. Suggestions for dietary change were informed by information gathered from observations and WRA at the study settings during the design phase. The education intervention featured topic reinforcement, discussion sessions, and practical skill-building sessions. However, there are possible explanations for the minimal rise in dietary folate intake in the group that received the education program.

Elements of our intervention focused on increasing perceived benefits and decreasing perceived barriers towards improving folate status. Although the dietary education group’s perception of the benefits of improving folate status improved, perceived barriers in the dietary education group did not decrease. According to the HBM, perceived benefits must outweigh perceived barriers for a marked shift in dietary behavior to occur [25]. The intervention featured dietary items and meals that were locally available, and it took into account the baseline eating habits of WRA at the study settings. Practical guidance on making affordable folate-rich choices was also covered in the intervention sessions. Nevertheless, it is possible that barriers related to participants’ access to these items remained, given that perceived barriers in the dietary education group did not decrease. Economic barriers towards changing eating behaviors, or possibly barriers related to taste preferences [39], could have persisted. Remaining barriers may have deterred dietary behavior change.

Several activities were intended to enhance self-efficacy, an important determinant of health behavior change that affects the effort invested in a task and its outcome [40]. The improvement in self-efficacy within the nutrition education group was greater than in the supplementation and comparison groups, showing that the practice, social, and diet modeling elements of the program’s sessions had some benefits. However, of the four sources of self-efficacy [41], the program activities did not address the emotional state of the participants. This could have influenced their self-efficacy, or confidence, in their own abilities to consume more dietary folate to improve their folate status [41]. This may also partly explain why folate status did not improve in the group who received the folate education program.

We chose the HBM as our theoretical model, firstly, because it is an effective framework for shaping folate interventions for high-risk women [42]. A previous education intervention based on the HBM also reported improvements in the dietary folate intakes of pregnant women [23]. Drawbacks of the HBM include its assumption that health actions are the primary goal in decision-making [43]. Other factors can also significantly influence a person’s food choice decision-making, which is driven by a complex interplay of many considerations such as family and social network [39].

However, the lack of improvement in folate status in the folate education group should be interpreted with caution as it may reflect study limitations rather than evidence of ineffectiveness. Possible constraints which could be implicated in the poor response in this group are related to the intervention design, including its duration, and the sample size. Our education intervention covered 12 weeks. Previous short behavioral interventions have successfully improved biological outcomes [21, 27, 44]. Venn et al. reported increases in dietary folate intake and serum folate concentration and a decrease in plasma homocysteine concentration after a 4-week dietary counseling intervention targeting folate intake [21]. A HBM-based education intervention to improve the intake of iron-rich food and IFA supplements in anemic pregnant women increased dietary iron intake, IFA supplement compliance, and hemoglobin concentration, over a period of 15 weeks [27]. Similarly, there was a reduction in the number of pregnant women with anemia following a 6-week HBM-based nutrition education intervention to increase the consumption of iron-rich food and IFA supplement compliance [44]. Although these behavioral change interventions were conducted over 4 to 15 weeks and successfully resulted in changes in biological outcomes, their education interventions either involved one-to-one dietary counseling [21] or aimed to improve both dietary intake and IFA supplement compliance [27, 44]. It is possible that longer education interventions are necessary to improve folate status, if the education intervention involves group sessions and targets only dietary folate intake. Effects of behavioral change are observed with time and folate status may have shown a more positive response if the testing window had been closer to the average lifespan of a red blood cell of around 17 weeks. Two of the interventions which successfully improved biological outcomes targeted dietary folate intake alongside IFA supplement compliance [27, 44], therefore it may be also reasonable to suppose that an intervention which uses a combination of approaches might be optimal for improving folate status. Moreover, the number of participants was relatively small. The statistical power of the study might have been reduced, which could have limited the ability to detect an effect.

RBC folate concentration improved in the group that received once-weekly IFA supplementation for 12 weeks. RBC folate concentrations were 451.6 ± 153.1 nmol/L at pretest and 599.8 ± 250.3 nmol/L following once-weekly (2.8 mg) IFA supplementation for 12 weeks. Two recent randomized-controlled trials showed that once-weekly (2.8 mg) IFA supplementation for 12 weeks [12] and 16 weeks [13] significantly improved RBC folate concentrations in non-pregnant women. The trials demonstrated the efficacy of this supplementation regimen in non-pregnant women [12, 13], whereas our quasi-experimental approach tested the effectiveness of a supplementation regimen in a group of non-pregnant women in a real-world setting.

Although folate status improved in the once-weekly IFA supplementation group, RBC folate concentration remained below the threshold recommended to prevent NTD (906 nmol/L) [6]. This might have been partly because compliance with the supplementation regimen was only 70%. Our study participants received just three indirect group reminders to take their supplement over the duration of the 12-week intervention. Consequently, the level of supplement compliance in our study might closely reflect compliance in a community context. Half of the supplementation group reported experiencing an adverse effect, most commonly nausea. This was probably attributable to its iron content (60 mg elemental iron in the form of ferrous fumarate). Several quasi-experimental studies have demonstrated improved compliance with IFA supplementation in pregnant women who received counseling in conjunction with the IFA supplements [44–47]. One was a family-based counseling intervention [47] and another emphasized expectant adverse effects and ways of ameliorating them [46]. Additionally, Rahmiwati et al. [48] reported improved compliance with IFA supplementation in non-pregnant adolescents who received local culture-based education delivered via social media. These suggest that including tailored-counseling regarding measures to minimize adverse effects could improve compliance with IFA, which would be expected to increase its effectiveness.

Polymorphisms in genes involved in folate metabolism may affect the response to a folate intervention. The C677T polymorphism of the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene is the most widely studied, and its frequency varies geographically and ethnically. In northern China and southern China, respectively, the frequency of homozygosity (TT) for this genetic polymorphism is 19.8% and 8.1%, while the frequency of heterozygosity (CT) is 49% and 53% [49]. Data from several small studies suggest that TT and CT genotypes occur in 1.4–3.9% and 21.4–34.9% of the healthy Thai population, respectively [50–53]. Heterozygosity and homozygosity of this genetic polymorphism reduce MTHFR enzyme activity by 29% and 64%, respectively, and may reduce red cell folate concentrations [54]. It is possible that participants who carried MTHFR polymorphisms might have responded less favorably to our interventions compared to those without. However, the frequency of the C677T MTHFR gene polymorphism among the Thai population appears to be relatively low, and we would anticipate a similar frequency across all three groups because all participants were ethnically Thai.

The limitations and strengths of our research study are discussed as follows. Each factory expressed its preference to participate as either the dietary education group, the supplementation group, or the comparison group. Factories were assigned to groups in this way after the first author had provided them with details of the research, especially in terms of its activities and time commitment. This could have introduced some selection bias, but we can justify our approach as it was especially important that the factory which received the dietary folate education program allowed the researchers to conduct activities onsite once-a-week for 12 consecutive weeks. Also, those who volunteer to participate in research often have characteristics which differ from non-participants [55], but there was no reason for us to suspect that the level of volunteer bias differed between the groups.

Prior to our study, there were no available data for RBC folate response following a folate education program. Consequently, our sample size calculation was based on the effect size of an intervention which provided participants with folate-rich foods [30] rather than an education program that focused on dietary behavioral change to improve folate status. The effect size used for the sample size calculation might have been overestimated because the response in RBC folate concentration after a folate-rich food intervention could be greater than after an education-based intervention. Moreover, ten participants were withdrawn from the study between enrollment and pretest. The reduced sample size might have undermined the statistical power of the study. This is noteworthy because it can limit the ability to detect a real effect when one actually exists, or can exaggerate effect sizes. With this in mind, our findings should be treated with caution, and we recommend that future studies include a larger sample size.

The duration of our education intervention (12 weeks) could be another constraint. Other behavioral change interventions were conducted over 4 to 15 weeks and successfully improved biological outcomes, but their education interventions either involved one-to-one dietary counseling [21] or aimed to improve both dietary intake and IFA supplement compliance [27, 44]. For community-based education interventions that focus solely on dietary behavioral change, a longer intervention period may be needed to observe significant improvements in RBC folate concentration.

Although this was a workplace-based intervention study, our findings are relevant to Thai WRA in general because there are shared characteristics between the sample and the general population. Study participants were aged 20–34-years, which is the age group accounting for most pregnancies [32], and they were full-time employees. Women make up 46% of Thailand’s labor force, and they hold a high share of the employment in the country’s manufacturing sector [56].

This quasi-experiment aimed to improve the folate status of a group of women in a province of Thailand which has a prevalence of NTD above the national average [3]. Conducting an intervention to improve folate status in a locality with higher than average NTD burden is highly warranted because the need for optimizing folate status is greatest. However, the effect of an intervention in populations with a higher-than-average disease burden can be greater than the effect in low burden populations.

In our study, age, level of education attainment and pretest values differed between groups. All experimental designs carry a risk of imbalance between groups at baseline in terms of differences in baseline characteristics and outcome measures [38], but attributing any effect to the intervention relies on the groups being similar at pretest [57]. Statistical techniques are among the strategies used to manage pretest differences between groups [58] and the GEE is one statistical approach that can be used to adjust for baseline differences between-groups [38]. In short, although the study groups differed at pretest, this was partially controlled for by analyzing within-group differences from pretest and between-group differences at post-test using the GEE model, adjusting for age, education level and pretest values as the covariates. Nevertheless, given the relatively small sample size, residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Changes in the HBM constructs were observed in the supplementation and comparison groups after adjusting for covariates. Although these changes were smaller than those observed in the folate education group, possible reasons are discussed. These changes might have occurred because the process of answering the study questionnaire increased participants’ awareness of folate [59]. In turn, this might have led to slight increases in knowledge, perceptions, self-efficacy and cues to action towards changing behavior from pretest to post-test in the supplementation and comparison groups. The groups were situated 9–43 km apart and the three factories were independent of each other, so the risk of contamination from the folate education group to the other two groups was unlikely. To the authors’ knowledge there were no public health events in the vicinities of the groups during the study period, but we cannot rule out the possibility that some participants could have been coincidentally exposed to other external events, such as social media posts related to folate. These factors have implications for the interpretation of the results. Overall, the magnitude of the observed effects of the education intervention on the HBM constructs may be confounded by other factors such as questionnaire-related awareness and possible external exposures.

Likewise, dietary folate intake increased slightly in the supplementation and comparison groups from pretest to post-test, after adjustment for covariates, and RBC folate concentration increased in the comparison group from pretest to post-test. The contexts were similar across the three settings based on information gathered from pre-study observations of factories’ canteens, local markets and convenience stores. An alternative explanation could be the Hawthorne or observer effect, whereby participants modify their behavior because they are taking part in a study [60]. All participants were given detailed information about the purpose of the study and the SFFQ administered at pretest and post-test contained a comprehensive list of folate-containing items. These factors could have inadvertently increased participants’ awareness of sources of dietary folate, which might have prompted some participants in the supplementation and comparison groups to increase their intake of folate-containing foods. We excluded individuals who were current supplement users at the time of recruitment. All participants in the two groups who were not provided with the IFA supplement were instructed to not consume supplements during the study period. Nevertheless, unreported use of supplements containing folic acid in the comparison group could be an additional possible explanation for the unexpected increase in RBC folate concentration in this group. These issues make it harder to conclude with certainty that the education intervention resulted in a slight improvement in dietary folate intake or that the IFA supplement caused an increase in RBC folate concentration. Any observed improvements could be partly caused by mere participation in the study, increased awareness, or unaccounted for external confounding factors.

The areas in which the factories were situated were comparable in terms of social, cultural, economic and environmental factors. During the planning stages of the study, the first author made observations of the workers’ lifestyles and the social environments of the factories. Most employees worked 8–10-h shifts with 1–2 days off per week, and most of their homes were situated in the local area. Therefore, it is unlikely that contextual factors confounded the outcomes.

An IFA supplement was used (rather than a supplement containing only folate) because it is in line with the Ministry of Public Health policy and practice of distributing free-of-charge folic acid in a supplement which also contains iron [9, 10]. Our education intervention focused specifically on improving RBC folate concentration because it was not financially feasible to include biochemical assessments of iron status as an additional outcome measure. However, many dietary items that were promoted in our education intervention were sources of iron and vitamin B12, as well as folate. We were only able to focus on folate status, but multiple micronutrients are important in shaping reproductive health outcomes. Future similar education interventions, particularly those in other country settings which have multiple micronutrient deficiencies and higher incidences of adverse birth outcomes, may consider designing an education intervention that simultaneously targets several micronutrients.

Strengths of our research include the use of RBC folate concentration. RBC folate concentration is closely correlated with liver folate concentration and is representative of long-term folate intake [5]. Dietary folate intakes were evaluated using an SFFQ. The SFFQ is subject to errors, such as under- and over-reporting, social desirability bias, and recall error, and this should be considered when interpreting our dietary folate data. Nonetheless, our approach to dietary assessment likely reduced some measurement error. The SFFQ was administered by experienced public health nutritionists. Household measures, food models and a picture book of products fortified with folic acid were used to assist participants in estimating portion sizes. Additionally, the SFFQ was specifically designed for this study. Thai cuisine varies from region to region, and there are unique dishes and differences in recipes and ingredients between provinces within a region. Developing an SFFQ that was tailored for the study areas’ local dietary folate sources was therefore a necessity to accurately capture intakes. Our SFFQ was created after a comprehensive review and observations of foods and beverages in the study site districts and it featured 163 dietary items. Although the reliability of the SFFQ was not tested, we tested its level of agreement with RBC folate concentration at pretest. RBC folate concentration is a robust biomarker of folate status [5], and based on the Pearson correlation coefficient, the estimates of folate intake obtained from the SFFQ correlated well with this biomarker. This indicates that the SFFQ provided a relatively valid estimate of intakes of folate-containing foods that were available within the study setting. The strengths of our education intervention creation process and format are also noteworthy. Our education intervention program was informed by an 8-month preparatory phase that included observations of the local food environment and informal interviews of WRA who worked at the factories to assess their baseline dietary habits. It was composed of learner-centered weekly sessions and covered 8 topics, 4 of which were repeated to reinforce these topics. Lastly, to the authors’ knowledge, our study is the first to investigate and compare the effect of a theory-grounded dietary folate education program versus a once-weekly IFA supplement on RBC folate concentration.

Conclusion

Folate status did not improve after a dietary folate education program for 12-weeks, which could have been partly due to the intervention’s limitations. Future education programs may consider a longer intervention duration and include a larger sample size. Reducing perceived barriers towards changing dietary folate behavior may also facilitate behavior change. Once-weekly IFA supplementation (2.8 mg folic acid per week) for 12 weeks may contribute to an improvement in folate status in non-pregnant women in a real-world setting. However, the findings should be interpretated with caution in view of the study limitations.
