Diffusion through complex confining environments: Motion in fluctuating porous membrane structures
Authors: Jakob Mihatsch, Andreas M Menzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transport of individual entities through interconnected structures is a process of practical relevance in both biology and technology. Examples are given by diffusive dynamics of molecules in porous structures. In soft environments, this transport can be strongly influenced by fluctuations of the porous structure itself. Here, we focus on triply periodic membrane structures found in both cell organelles and synthetic amphiphilic systems. We theoretically study the effect of a complex three-dimensional fluctuating environment on the diffusive motion of a test object using a phase-field approach. The rigid spherical test object is energetically forced not to penetrate the membrane. Generally, the pores of the membrane structure can be smaller than the diffusing object. Yet fluctuations in the membrane can intermittently widen its pores, still allowing for the larger particles to move through them. Thus, the object stays trapped for a while inside one cavity formed by the membrane, before an appropriate fluctuation event widens a membrane pore at the right moment, allowing the object to jump into the next cavity. The process is reflected by a pronounced plateau in the time evolution of the mean squared displacement. We think that the described scenario should be directly observable, for instance, in protein diffusion through biological environments.

Significance

Objects such as proteins diffuse through narrow constrictions formed by fluctuating lipid membranes. Such structures can feature three-dimensional periodicity. The pores inherent in these structures, if not fluctuating, can be too narrow to let the objects pass. Still, under fluctuations, passage can become possible. We analyze this process and demonstrate that fluctuations, which repeatedly widen the pores, facilitate passage of the diffusing objects. In this way, diffusivity of proteins and other components is substantially enhanced compared with nonfluctuating environments.

Introduction

The diffusive transport of molecules is of interest in biophysics and beyond. Often, this transport happens in porous, heterogeneous environments. Transport processes in biological cells take place in crowded environments,1 and diffusion through nanopores has technological applications such as filtration via membranes or in catalysts.2 Thermal fluctuations, however, not only lead to the diffusive motion of a molecule but can also dynamically change the confining environment of this molecule. There have been experimental,3 numerical,4,5 and analytical6,7,8 efforts to characterize the effect of a fluctuating environment on a diffusing particle. Depending on the specific setup and the theoretical assumptions, some authors found that fluctuations in the environment decrease the effective diffusion coefficient,7,8 whereas others found that it was increased.3,4,5 The latter was especially the case when trapping the diffusing particle in cavities of the porous medium. Fluctuations in a heterogeneous environment can cause non-Gaussian diffusion.4 There, the mean squared displacement (MSD) increases linearly in time, while the displacements do not follow a normal distribution. In many studies, including those mentioned above, the movement is usually limited to effectively one- (1D) or two-dimensional (2D) geometry.

Here, we study diffusion in a three-dimensional (3D) fluctuating environment. Examples of porous structures that inherently exist only in three dimensions are cubic membranes. These are complex 3D periodic structures formed by lipid membranes in cell organelles.9,10 It is also possible to create cubic phases in vitro using amphiphilic molecules.

Mathematically, these structures can be described by periodic surfaces of constant mean curvature (CMC) H.11 In the special case of H = 0, we observe a so-called triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS). CMC surfaces are also obtained by minimizing surface area for a given periodicity while prescribing a fixed ratio of the volumes on the two sides of the surface.12 The TPMS is found in the case of equal volume on both sides of the surface. CMC surfaces are often thought of as deformed TPMSs and are characterized by the TPMS they share their symmetry with. Important examples of TPMSs are the Schwarz P- and D-surfaces, as well as the gyroid (G-surface) discovered by Schoen.12,13 TPMSs and related surfaces separate space into two unconnected volumes that form intertwined channels. The P-surface can be imagined as a cubic lattice of cavities connected by channels in each coordinate direction. That means it forms a network of junctions of six different pipes (see Figure 1). Similarly, the D-surface and the gyroid form channels with four- and three-pipe junctions, respectively. Apart from lipid membranes, TPMSs are found in the chitin skeletons of insects, the wings of butterflies, and block copolymers.14 They also find technological applications, for example in the design of scaffolds for growing tissue.15 An exact mechanism behind the formation of those structures in biological systems is not yet known. The reason they are energetically favorable is not that they minimize surface area, since lipid membranes do not have a significant surface tension. Instead, the shape of the membrane molecules dictates a spontaneous curvature c0 that is energetically optimal.12,16 An appropriate energy functional for lipid membranes is therefore the Helfrich free energy17

Diffusive motion in the periodic network of channels formed by TPMSs has already been investigated in the case of static surfaces.18 It was found that bottlenecks, if they are present, have an important influence on the transport properties. The dynamics of a diffusing particle then may correspond to a hopping process. It may be trapped in a cavity for a long time before escaping. In fluorescence correlation spectroscopy measurements of the diffusion coefficient of a protein confined in lipid cubic phases, it was found that the impact of the confinement on the diffusion coefficient was less than that of a comparable confinement with hard walls. This suggests that the softness of the confinement and thermal fluctuations play an important role.19 We here investigate how diffusion is influenced when a TPMS is undulated by thermal fluctuations, which will lead to a widening and narrowing of the bottlenecks corresponding to the channels formed by the surface. An example of the geometry under investigation is shown in Figure 1. We focus on the P-surface because its structure is easy to visualize and grasp, facilitating interpretation of the results. Our approach, however, is straightforward to extend to other CMC surfaces.

Materials and methods

Dynamics of the phase field

We use a phase-field approach to track the location of the surfaces. This method describes a surface as the zero-level set of a continuous scalar function, the phase field ϕ(r). It is obtained by minimizing a free energy Fϕ, which is a functional of ϕ. TPMSs were generated from the phase-field model for microemulsions,20 but this model has many free parameters and could result in a profile of the phase field ϕ that is difficult to analyze. Yang et al.21 showed that by minimizing the free energy associated with the Cahn-Hilliard equation starting from appropriate initial conditions, one can generate periodic surfaces of CMC. We use their approach to generate the initial conditions for our simulations. This free energy, however, is not suited to simulate the dynamic behavior of those surfaces. As explained above, the energy should not depend on the surface area of the membrane. Instead, the energy should depend on the mean curvature. Therefore, we use a free energy developed for the modeling of biological membranes and vesicles,22,23,24,25,26

Numerical implementation

We measure energies in units of κeff = κϵ3, lengths in units of ϵ, and times in units of Mϕ−1κ−1. This means we effectively set ϵ = 1, κ = 1, and Mϕ = 1 in the above equations. Typically, kBT is found in a range from 0.1 to 0.01 κeff in experiments.32 If not specified otherwise, we set kBT = 0.033 and γ = 0.01. In the following, we present detailed results for α = 10. We have verified that the observed effects show analogous trends and are of similar magnitude when we vary this parameter, in our case to values of α = 5 and 20. The dynamical equations are implemented using an Euler scheme, where the linear term in δFbend/δϕ containing ∇4ϕ is treated implicitly to increase stability. The spatial derivatives are calculated using spectral methods. We set the integration timestep to Δt = 0.1 and ν = (ΔtΩ)−1, where Ω is the volume of the computational domain, to exactly conserve ∫Ωϕ d3r. We discretize ϕ on a grid with grid constant Δx = 1.

For the following measurements of diffusion through triply periodic membranes, the phase field describing the initial, unperturbed membrane is generated using the approach of Yang et al.21 The computational domain consists of 3 × 3 × 3 unit cells of the membrane, where one cell has a length of dc = 40. A unit cell is therefore about dc/2≈28 times larger than the thickness of the transition layer of the phase field from one side of the membrane to the other. This proportion is comparable to cubic membranes found in living systems, where structures with unit cells of 50–550 nm can be formed by lipid bilayers with a thickness of less than 10 nm.10 For each set of parameters, we simulate 192 independent diffusion processes. Since the presence of the diffusing object influences the membrane, we need to generate a separate realization of the membrane for each realization of the diffusion process. For each realization, we evolve the membrane surface for at least teq = 300,000 so that the shape fluctuations equilibrate. This state is saved and used as a starting condition for the subsequent measurements. We generate 64 such starting configurations. The starting position of the diffusing object is in the center of a cavity of the membrane structure. For each membrane starting configuration, we simulate three different particle starting positions.

Results and discussion

We investigate diffusion through a P-surface as shown in Figure 1. First, we keep MR constant while varying l. A larger radius of the diffusing spherical object makes it more difficult to fit through narrow pores. Figure 4 shows the MSD for the three different radii of the diffusing particle, l = 6, 7, and 8, with MR = 1 (MR has the dimension of Mϕϵ2). There are three different regimes. For short times, the MSD is linear with the diffusion coefficient of a free particle, D0 = MRkBT. At intermediate times, there is a subdiffusive regime in which the confinement by the membrane structure becomes apparent. This regime starts a bit earlier at larger radii because, for larger particles, the accessible volume inside a cavity is effectively smaller. Similarly, the decrease in slope is more pronounced and resembles a plateau in Figure 4 for larger radii. After some time, the particle escapes through a fluctuating pore into the next cavity. The larger the particle, the longer it takes for this escape to happen. We note that without the membrane fluctuations, this escape would be substantially hindered. Although the narrowest point of a channel confined by the P-surface with a unit cell size of 40 has an approximate radius of 10, which is larger than all radii considered here, we need to take into account the transition layer of the phase field describing the membrane ϕ and the particle k(r). From Equations 4 and 10, we know that it is approximately 2 wide but not sharply defined. Thus, in order to determine the effective size of the pores exactly, we instead directly calculate the energy barrier for the particle. As Figure 5 shows, the energy barrier at the narrowest point of a channel is larger than kBT for the particle radii considered in Figure 4. The energy values in this figure were calculated numerically from Equation 9 by artificially placing the particle in the middle of a pore of the unperturbed P-surface, at its narrowest point. Finally, at longer times, the motion appears diffusive again (see Figure 4).

For further analysis, we keep the radius l constant and change the mobility MR. Figure 6 shows the resulting MSD.

Averaged mean squared displacement (MSD) of the particle over the normalized time tMR. Shaded areas indicate standard deviations of the means over 192 independent realizations. The three different lines are for different mobilities MR, while l = 7 is constant.

The time axis in this figure displays the time normalized to the timescale of free diffusion, tMR, so that we can compare measurements at different particle speeds. We note that the MSD on small timescales therefore collapses on one common curve for all particle radii, as it is equal to that of a free particle. However, when the dynamics of the particle is much faster (MR = 10) than the dynamics of the membrane, the long-term MSD is lower than when the two timescales coincide (MR = 1) or when the particle is slower (MR = 0.1). We suspect that this effect is related to the rate of jumping to the next cavity. As an underlying reason, due to the particular geometry of the confinement with pores as “bottlenecks,” the passage through pores has a strong impact. We expect it to be the dominant factor determining how fast diffusive transport can be. Widening of pores due to thermal fluctuations makes slipping through a pore more likely. For MR = 10, the membrane becomes effectively static from the point of view of the particle, so that no new pathways can open. At the other extreme of MR = 0.1, deformations of the membrane relax quickly compared to the speed of the particle.

Finally, we wish to consider the possibility of another effect that may facilitate diffusion of objects through pores that, in equilibrium, are of insufficient diameter. The object itself, when on its way into and through the pore, may deform the membrane and widen the pore to enable passage. This process goes beyond the fact that pores can open due to fluctuations.

To investigate the relevance of these two different contributions in more detail, we introduce two variations of the dynamics for comparison. In the first scenario, the membrane is frozen in its deformed state and does not fluctuate. The motion of the particle is confined by this frozen membrane. In the second variation, the membrane is not affected by the presence of the particle at all. That is, there is no deformational effect of the particle on the membrane. However, the particle is still confined by the membrane. Consequently, the system is no longer in equilibrium in both cases, as it no longer follows the stationary probability distribution (Equation 13). Nevertheless, in our theoretical approach, we can simply introduce these modifications and compare the results to conclude the importance of the underlying processes.

In Figure 7, we compare the MSDs obtained from the two modifications with the unmodified equilibrium dynamics. All other parameters are kept the same, including the random seeds for the fluctuations in our simulations. In a frozen environment, we find that the particle moves more slowly than in a fluctuating environment. This is in line with our previous results in Figure 6, as it essentially corresponds to the limit MR/Mϕ → ∞. Interestingly, when we deactivate the action of the particle on the membrane, we see no decrease in long-time diffusivity within our standard deviations. This result confirms that the speed-up is significantly caused by the membrane fluctuations rather than by the force exerted by the particle on the membrane, which may contribute to widening the pores.

In the case of a frozen membrane, the long-time MSD is significantly decreased when compared to the full dynamics.

The full dynamics are compared to the scenario of suppressed deformational action of the particle on the membrane, with no notable deviation within the standard deviations. Moreover, we compare to a scenario of a frozen membrane. The latter situation leads to notably smaller diffusion coefficients, especially for large diffusing objects.

Interestingly, existing fluorescence correlation spectroscopy measurements19 of protein diffusion in lipid cubic phases observe a similar order of magnitude of the effect of confinement. For a ratio of particle diameter to the diameter of the bottleneck of the confinement of about 0.8–0.9, they mention D/D0 ≃ 0.02–0.06. In our theoretical approach, we find D/D0 ≃ 0.02–0.1 for a particle diameter-to-bottleneck diameter ratio of about 1.0–1.2. The slightly stronger effect of confinement in experiments could, for example, stem from deviations in the periodic surface formed by lipids in reality or from additional interactions between the diffusing particle and membrane that go beyond the simple volume exclusion considered here.

Conclusion

We have investigated the motion of a diffusing object through fluctuating triply periodic membrane structures. Our investigation was accomplished using a phase-field approach to describe the confinement of the object by the membrane, including fluctuations of the membrane. We have shown analytically and numerically that adding a stochastic term to the equations of motion of the phase-field membrane indeed leads to the correct statistics of interface displacements.

In the MSD of the diffusing object, we identified two linear diffusive regimes separated by a plateau-like subdiffusive intermediate region that corresponds to the trapping of the particle inside a cavity in the periodic membrane structure. This region becomes broader when the size of the object is increased. The fluctuations of the membrane, particularly of the diameter of the pores, allow for the escape of a particle that would be trapped inside a given pore if the membrane surface were unperturbed. This effect should be directly observable, for instance, in protein diffusion through biological environments.

Our theoretical approach allows us to separate the effect of fluctuations in membrane and pore size from the effect the diffusing object has on the membrane. When we suppressed by hand the deformation of the membrane caused by the diffusing object, the long-time diffusion coefficient remained virtually unchanged. We thus conclude that, for the chosen parameters, the dominant effect is the opening of the pores caused by fluctuations in the membrane rather than deformations of the soft membrane induced by the diffusing object.

In the future, the dynamics of the phase field and the object should be coupled to the Navier-Stokes equation of hydrodynamic fluid flow to further include the role of the liquid environment.24 Long-range hydrodynamic interactions of the membrane with itself and with the diffusing object could be included in this way. Such couplings may play an important role in transport through biological systems as well.
