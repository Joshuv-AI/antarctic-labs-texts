Finding Clustering Algorithms in the Transformer Architecture
Kenneth L Clarkson, Lior Horesh, Takuya Ito, Charlotte Park, Parikshit Ram

Abstract
The invention of the transformer architecture has revolutionized Artificial Intelligence (AI), yielding unprecedented success in areas such as natural language processing, computer vision, and multimodal reasoning. Despite these advances, it is unclear whether transformers are able to learn and implement precise algorithms. Here, we demonstrate that transformers can exactly implement a fundamental and widely used algorithm for k k -means clustering: Lloyd’s algorithm. First, we theoretically prove the existence of such a transformer architecture, which we term the k k -means transformer , that exactly implements Lloyd’s algorithm for k k -means clustering using the standard ingredients of modern transformers: attention and residual connections. Next, we numerically implement this transformer and demonstrate in experiments the exact correspondence between our architecture and Lloyd’s algorithm, providing a fully neural implementation of k k -means clustering. Finally, we demonstrate that interpretable alterations (e.g., incorporating layer normalizations or multilayer perceptrons) to this architecture yields diverse and novel variants of clustering algorithms, such as soft k k -means, spherical k k -means, trimmed k k -means, and more. Collectively, our findings demonstrate how transformer mechanisms can precisely map onto algorithmic procedures, offering a clear and interpretable perspective on implementing precise algorithms in transformers.

Introduction

Clustering is one of the most fundamental and widely studied problems in computer science, dating back to the mid-20th century
[

Classic algorithms (such as,

Despite their widespread utility, clustering problems are computationally challenging and have been shown to be an NP-hard problem, even when the number of clusters is two

The combination of their widespread impact and inherent hardness of these algorithms has driven decades of research into their underlying theory and methodology.

Despite the historical prevalence of clustering algorithms, the rise of deep learning has significantly altered the recent landscape of machine learning research.
Deep learning architectures — the

However, exactly how transformer mechanisms — attention, normalization, feed-forward layers, residual connections and such — contribute to precise algorithmic capabilities remains largely unclear.

The opaque internal mechanisms of modern AI models, compounded by their inherently statistical training and inference processes, present substantial interpretability challenges that limit their reliability in critical applications.
This lack of transparency underscores the critical importance of characterizing the algorithmic capabilities of modern neural architectures to provide a foundation for reliable and interpretable AI systems.
Recent work in the related area of mechanistic interpretability — the research area concerned with understanding the behaviors of neural networks through their internal mechanisms — has begun to shed light on the internal workings of neural networks and their behaviors
[

,

Key findings have highlighted how large language models exhibit complex behaviors via in-context learning and polysemantic neurons, providing tools to steer and control the behaviors of these models

While these studies primarily focused on behaviors in large language models, they highlight the potential to directly map architectural features of transformers onto explicit operations of classical algorithms (such as

In this study, we bridge classically studied discrete clustering algorithms with modern neural architectures, offering a clear and interpretable approach to understanding how transformer mechanisms precisely map onto discrete operations of a widely used and influential classic algorithm: Lloyd’s algorithm (
Figure

1
).
By providing an explicit mapping between circuit mechanisms of the transformer — self-attention, cross-attention, residual connections — and algorithmic procedures, we demonstrate how architectural modifications bring about algorithmic variations in Lloyd’s algorithm (and vice-versa).
Given a set of data points and random initial centers (corresponding to input tokens), we show that each layer of a handcrafted yet interpretable encoder-decoder transformer exactly performs a single iteration of Lloyd’s algorithm in its forward pass.
This formulation of a clustering algorithm as a neural architecture enables the discovery of variations of clustering algorithms in terms of interpretable alterations to the transformer architecture.
We provide both theoretical results detailing this equivalence, as well as numerical simulations that solidify this correspondence empirically.
Together, these results provide a concrete and interpretable approach to understanding how formal algorithms map onto architectural components of ubiquitous, modern deep learning architectures.

2.2
A transformer implementation of Lloyd’s algorithm

The original encoder-decoder transformer
[
6
]
takes a sequence of tokens as input and decodes an output sequence of tokens.
The main components of the transformer architecture are (i) the self-attention within the encoder and decoder, (ii) the cross-attention between the two, (iii) residual connections, (iv) per-token normalization (such as LayerNorm
[
18
]
), and (v) per-token nonlinear transformation via a single hidden-layer feed-forward network (FFN).
Here we simplify the original transformer architecture to implement Lloyd’s algorithm — the primary ingredients only require residual connections, self-attention, and cross-attention.
(Token-wise normalization and nonlinear transformations are necessary for specific variations of
k
-means clustering, which we detail later on.)
Though the parameters of the transformer are typically learned from data, here we demonstrate an exact correspondence between Lloyd’s algorithm and a transformer with chosen parameters.
In other words, given a set of instances and initial centers, each layer of our proposed transformer exactly performs a single iteration of Lloyd’s algorithm (lines 3-5,
Algorithm

1

We also find that as the number of points and clusters increases, divergence between Lloyd’s and

While the previous demonstrations make use of the negative squared Euclidean distance as the attention scores for Euclidean

Robust

While trimmed

3
Discussion

The encoder-decoder transformer was developed to solve sequence-to-sequence tasks
[
6
]
.
One such task is text summarization, where the input sequence is a (large) text chunk and the output sequence is a faithful summary of the input.
We analogize the
k
-means problem as clustering (summarizing) a large set of points into a small set of cluster centers.
This led us to the development of an encoder-decoder transformer with chosen parameters that exactly implements Lloyd’s algorithm for
k
-means clustering,
without any training
.
Importantly, the development of this neural implementation of
k
-means provides a general approach for developing alternative and novel clustering algorithms through interpretable architectural modifications.

Transformers are uniquely suited for clustering.

There has been recent interest in theoretically understanding the in-context learning capabilities of transformers
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
However, most prior analyses have been limited to supervised learning problems and simplifying assumptions on the transformer
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
For example, one common assumption is the use of linear attention (a form of a weighted average) rather than the standard soft-max attention, which is an approximation of the selective arg-max.
In contrast, our work explicitly leverages this crucial aspect (i.e., selective attention), in addition to residual connections, normalization, and nonlinear transformations, to instantiate exact
unsupervised
clustering algorithms.
Specifically, we leverage the selective nature of the (limiting) soft-max attention in combination with residual connections and self-attention to exactly emulate cluster assignment and center updates, as required in Lloyd’s algorithm.
Other theoretical studies investigating in-context learning primarily focus on self-attention
[
20
]
.
Here we leverage both self- and cross-attention in a encoder-decoder architecture, demonstrating how their interplay can simulate alternating updates key for
k
-means clustering (cluster labels vs. centers).
And though some other related works have previously demonstrated where input tokens can “self-cluster” in-context
[
36
,
37
]
, we instead specify an architecture that
exactly
reproduces the widely-used Lloyd’s algorithm for
k
-means clustering.
Finally, we demonstrate the generality of our proposed architecture by instantiating alternative algorithms, such as soft
k
-means and trimmed
k
-means, via architectural modifications, highlighting how the ingredients of transformers are uniquely suitable for clustering.
An interesting implication from our analytic results is that, for any clustering algorithm that we instantiate as a transformer, there are infinitely many available configurations of its parameters (particularly the
Q,K,V
weight matrices).
This implies the existence of infinitely many variations of the transformer that are capable of instantiating the same algorithm; see
Remark

1
in §
A.2
.

Neural computation for discrete algorithms.

Our work provides a neural implementation to understanding clustering algorithms, providing a broader framework for understanding how discrete algorithms can be implemented through neural computation.
This is in line with several recent studies aimed at understanding transformers through traditional imperative programming languages (e.g., RASP
[
38
]
or ALTA
[
39
]
).
These studies have designed higher-level programming abstractions that allow imperative programs to be compiled into transformer weights and mechanisms, providing opportunities to study how transformer mechanisms map onto programming abstractions.
However, due to the limitations of the current programming abstractions employed, the range of programs these “transformer languages” can express are limited to the intermediate abstractions employed in these languages.
In our study, we take a complementary approach.
Rather than building intermediate programming abstractions, we directly map architectural components to algorithmic steps.
Though this approach provides increased flexibility (e.g., over RASP), such an approach is harder to generalize across a variety of algorithms, as it requires detailed knowledge of transformers.
Nevertheless, both of these approaches provide mechanistic insight into exactly how precise algorithms can be implemented through neural computations, providing the opportunity to easily leverage AI accelerator hardware for a variety of discrete algorithms.

Conclusion.

In this study, we introduce a handcrafted neural implementation of a widely-studied clustering algorithm: Lloyd’s algorithm for
k
-means clustering.
Through rigorous proofs and numerical simulations, we demonstrate the robustness of this implementation, while demonstrating how interpretable architectural alterations lead to unique algorithmic implementations, some of which are novel.
This provides a neural framework to studying clustering algorithms, paving a path to designing novel clustering algorithms using the tools of neural networks.
However, as mentioned earlier, generalizing this framework across different classes of algorithm is nontrivial.
Furthermore, though our work shows the existence of precise clustering algorithms in the transformer architecture, we leave the question of their learnability to future work, given the vastness of the space of learning environments and the likely dependence of learnability on experimental variables such as training data and inductive biases.

Appendix A
Methods

In this section, we will cover the theoretical results that establish the equivalence between the clustering algorithms (such as Lloyd’s) and in-context behavior of our proposed transformer architecture. We will begin by first demonstrating how the selective attention mechanism is especially well-suited for clustering. Then, we will show how the different elements of the transformer, namely, self-attention and residual connections, are utilized to perform the exact steps present in the clustering algorithms.

A.2.1

Remark 1
.

In
Theorem

2
, the query/key parameters are given by
K_{1}=Q_{1}=K_{2}=Q_{2}=I_{d+k}^{:d}
. However, note that Euclidean distances between points
x,x^{\prime}
are rotation invariant — that is,
\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|^{2}=\left\|Rx-Rx^{\prime}\right\|^{2}
for any rotation matrix

. Thus,
K_{1}=Q_{1}=K_{2}=Q_{2}=RI_{d+k}^{:d}
would be valid parameters for any rotation matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+k)\times(d+k)}
. As there are infinite such rotation matrices, there are thus infinite such query/key parameters that give us the desired behaviour.

A.2.2
Dot-product attention and quadratic activations

Here we show how to apply dot product (usual) attention, rather than Euclidean attention.
The algorithm uses much the same scheme as for Euclidean attention, but with points “lifted” to higher dimension using squared norms, so that squared Euclidean distance is expressible as a dot product.
We lift the input points in preprocessing, and augment the system with a quadratic activation function, to compute the updated lifting of updated cluster center points.

Definition 4

Lemma 2

Since

While the LSMA
\sigma_{\infty}
applied to the transposed cluster assignment matrix gives us the necessary weights to compute the updated cluster centers in Lloyd’s algorithm (

), it does not give us the desired update in the trimmed

-means as we need to filter instances before computing the center updates. For this, we turn our attention to the
\alpha

defined as
[

,
24
]

{\mathtt{NM}}_{\gamma}^{\alpha}(x)=\arg\max_{z:z^{i}\in[0,1],\sum_{i}z^{i}=1}\left\langle\gamma x,z\right\rangle-\|z\|_{\alpha},

. This norm-max activation has the desired property of filtering certain entries based on a threshold (setting their values to zero), and then assigning equal values to all the non-zero entries.

Algorithm 5

Robust
k
-means

This transformer architecture can be also be expressed as an algorithm as shown in
Algorithm

5
. After the standard cluster assignment step (line 5), each point
i
assigned to a cluster
j
is weighed based on its distance to the current center
c_{j}^{(t)}
(line 7), and these weights are normalized in line 8 using some form of a normalizing activation (denoted by “NAct”) such as soft-max or sparse-max
[
25
]
. Then the new cluster center is
c_{j}^{(t+1)}
is obtained by computing a weighted mean of the points assigned to the cluster.

This algorithm is different from the standard Lloyd’s algorithm in that the cluster center is not the exact mean of the assigned points, but rather a weighted mean, with points farther away from the previous center being weighed down. This reduces the effect of outliers assigned to the cluster, thereby making the algorithm more robust than standard Lloyd’s (
Algorithm

1
). This is different from the trimmed
k
-means algorithm (
Algorithm

4
) since trimmed
k
-means uses a threshold to remove the outliers completely before computing the unweighted mean of the remaining assigned points. This makes the algorithm susceptible to the choice of the threshold, and can be significantly influenced by outliers if the threshold is not appropriately selected (and thus, the outliers are not appropriately removed from consideration). In contrast, this new robust algorithm (
Algorithm

5
) weighs points assigned to the cluster, and thus will weigh an outlier low as it would be far away from its closest cluster center.

While the soft
k
-means (
Algorithm

2
) also makes use of weighted means, there is a significant difference between
Algorithm

5
and soft
k
-means — while soft
k
-means computes a weighted mean of all the points
\{x_{i},i\in[n]\}
,
Algorithm

5
computes a weighted mean of points assigned to the cluster
\{x_{i},i\in[n],a_{i}^{(t)}=j\}
, thereby retaining the discrete nature of the standard
k
-means clustering problem.

In our transformer architecture, the desired behavior is obtained by weighing all points for a given cluster
j\in[n]
based on the negative squared Euclidean distance and a large additive offset
\delta
for points assigned to this cluster. For a large enough
\delta
, the points not assigned to this cluster will have zero normalized weights, while the normalized weights for the points assigned to the cluster will solely depend on their negative squared Euclidean distance (with no depedence on
\delta
). This is because normalized activations such as soft-max and sparse-max are translation invariant — that is,
\text{NAct}(\{p_{i},i\in[m]\})=\text{NAct}(\{p_{i}+\delta,i\in[m]\})
for some
m
unnormalized weights
\{p_{i},i\in[m]\}
and an additive translation/offset
\delta
.

A closely related clustering problem is that of Euclidean
k
-medoids, defined as:

\min_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in X}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{j=1,\ldots,k}\left\|x_{i}-c_{j}\right\|^{2},

(35)

where it differs from the Euclidean
k
-means clustering problem in

3

to be actual points in the set

A critical difference between the Lloyd’s style alternating optimization and other existing
k
-medoids algorithms (such as PAM
[
30
]
) is that, while the alternating algorithm selects candidates for a cluster medoid update from within the cluster, PAM allows the selection of candidates for cluster medoid update from outside the cluster. We will instantiate this behaviour in our transformer architecture as follows:

Continuing with the transformer updates as considered in
Theorem

2

where we will discuss the cross-attention activation
\xi

Thus, for the

•
