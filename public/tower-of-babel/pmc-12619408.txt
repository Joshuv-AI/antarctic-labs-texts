Prediction of complications from pediatric multiple magnet ingestion: development and validation of radiologic and clinical-radiologic nomograms in a large retrospective study
Authors: Xiaohui Lai, Yizhen Luo, Songyu Teng, Yijiang Zhuang, Chenrui Zheng, Jianli Zhou, Xiongjian Cui, Qiru Su, Zhiyong Li, Hongwu Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent clinical models for pediatric multiple magnet ingestion lack accuracy in asymptomatic patients and in cases with unreliable histories, while radiologic predictors remain unvalidated. This study aimed to develop and validate two nomograms integrating radiologic (± clinical) features for individualized risk stratification.MethodThis retrospective study analyzed data from 146 pediatric patients treated at Shenzhen Children’s Hospital between 2017 and 2023. Risk factors were identified using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariable logistic regression. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics, with internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples.ResultsComplications occurred in 57 patients (39.0%; 65.1% male; median age, 4.6 years). Nomogram R (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]: 0.85, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.79–0.92; internally validated AUC: 0.86) included magnets size > 25 mm, blurred magnet edges, gap between magnets, and lack of progression on serial radiographs. Nomogram C&R (AUC: 0.95, 0.92–0.99; internally validated AUC: 0.96) incorporated multiple ingestions, vomiting, abdominal pain, magnets size > 25 mm, and lack of progression on serial radiographs. Both models demonstrated good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test), good calibration in calibration plots, and high clinical utility. Nomogram C&R demonstrated superior net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were 82.5%, 70.8%, and 75.3% for Nomogram R, and 87.7%, 92.1%, and 90.4% for Nomogram C&R. Web-based tools were created for clinical use.ConclusionNomogram C&R enables accurate complication risk stratification and supports personalised clinical decision-making in pediatric multiple magnet ingestion. Future prospective validation and integration into clinical pathways are warranted to further establish its role in routine practice.Keywords: Children, Foreign body ingestion, Radiology, Risk assessment, Nomograms

Introduction

The increased prevalence of high-powered neodymium magnets in consumer products has led to a rising incidence of pediatric multiple magnet ingestion, posing a significant yet often underestimated global health risk [1–4]. A U.S. nationwide survey reported that annual cases of high-risk multiple magnet ingestion more than doubled from 350 in 2013–2016 to 797 in 2017–2019 [3]. Ingestion of multiple magnets can result in interloop attraction, causing sustained mechanical pressure and potential bowel injury within eight hours [5]. Severe complications, including perforation, intestinal obstruction, and sepsis, occur in 32.5% to 75% of cases and account for approximately 80% of all foreign body ingestion-related complications in children [4, 6]. Despite established guidelines recommending urgent intervention, clinical decision-making remains challenging due to the dual risks of unnecessary procedures in low-risk cases and delayed intervention in high-risk cases, largely owing to the lack of precise risk-stratification tools [2, 6, 7].

The current clinical prediction model, incorporating vomiting, abdominal pain, abdominal tenderness, age, and multiple ingestions, demonstrates excellent discrimination (AUC, 0.94) in developmental cohorts [8]. However, two major limitations restrict its clinical applicability: (1) approximately 20% of children with perforation are asymptomatic before intervention, and (2) in younger children, particularly those under three years of age, unreliable ingestion history compromises risk assessment—ironically, this group faces the highest risk for severe complications and mortality [8]. These shortcomings limit the model’s generalizability to some extent and may result in missed opportunities for timely intervention.

Abdominal radiographs remain the most commonly used imaging modality for assessing magnet ingestion, primarily to determine the number and location of magnets [2, 5, 9]. However, their diagnostic sensitivity for perforation is very low, with a missed detection rate exceeding 95% [8, 10]. Case reports have suggested that radiologic markers, such as gaps between magnets and lack of progression on serial radiographs, may be associated with complications [6, 11–14]. However, these markers have not yet been systematically validated in cohort studies or integrated with clinical parameters for comprehensive risk assessment.

Nomograms are well-established predictive tools integrating multiple variables into intuitive, user-friendly risk models [15, 16].໿ To address these limitations, we aimed to (1) systematically characterize radiologic features of multiple magnet ingestion—a critical gap in current evidence—and (2) develop and validate two nomograms: a radiologic-only model (Nomogram R) for imaging-based risk stratification and triage in asymptomatic/history-limited cases and a clinical-radiologic model (Nomogram C&R) for individualized complication risk prediction and clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent

This retrospective study was approved by institutional hospital’s ethics committee (Approval No. 202200402). Given the study’s retrospective nature, informed consent was waived.

Participants

We retrospectively analyzed clinical and radiologic data from children aged 0–18 years who were treated for multiple magnet ingestion at a tertiary pediatric specialty hospitals in southern China between January 2017 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (i) a history of ingesting multiple magnetic foreign bodies, or (ii) suspected multiple magnet ingestion on abdominal radiographs, subsequently confirmed by conservative management, gastroscopy, or surgery. Patients with incomplete data were excluded.

Data collection

Patient data collected included the following: (i) demographic information (sex, age, symptoms, and signs); (ii) clinical information (characteristics of foreign body ingestion [witnessed or self-reported, multiple ingestions, and number and type of magnets] and hospitalization status); (iii) abdominal radiograph findings were carefully reviewed and interpreted through consensus by two pediatric radiologists with 10 years of experience (blinded to patient outcomes); and (iv) treatment modality(conservative management, gastroscopy, or surgery) and complications. For patients managed conservatively, outcomes were verified either through documented follow-up records (radiographic or clinical) or, if unavailable, by telephone follow-up with caregivers to confirm magnet passage and symptom resolution.

Definitions and grouping

Multiple magnet ingestion was defined as the ingestion of more than one magnetic foreign body or a combination of magnet and metallic objects. Multiple ingestions referred to instances where more than one ingestion occurred. Without witness reports or self-reporting, ingestion was assumed to involve a single event.

Radiologic features were defined as follows: Multiple magnet clusters referred to magnets visibly distributed at different locations on a radiograph. The diameter of individual ingested magnets, measured from radiographs, was typically 5–6 mm, with a few larger ones up to 28 mm. Magnets size >25 mm was characterized by their maximum overall length of the cluster, regardless of the shape, or more than four 6-mm buckyballs arranged in a straight line. This threshold was selected based on published guideline recommendations that objects ≥ 25 mm are unlikely to pass through the pylorus or ileocecal valve [17, 18]. Usually, closely positioned magnets should appear in contact on radiographs; any visible spaces between them indicate the ‘gaps between magnets’ sign. Magnet boundaries are typically sharply defined; blurred edges represent the ‘blurred magnet edges’ sign. Normally, magnets are expected to show forward positional advancement on sequential radiographs. The absence or inadequacy of such movement compared to prior imaging was defined as “lack of progression on serial radiographs.” The interval between radiographs was not fixed but determined based on clinical presentation and existing guideline recommendations [5, 19]. In most cases, follow-up occurred within 6–12 hours. In cases without follow-up radiographs, magnet progression was assumed by default.

Based on the outcomes, patients were categorized into no-complication and complication groups, with the latter group including both present and potential complications. Present complications included ulcers, perforation, fistula formation, bowel infarction, bowel torsion, internal hernia, and infection. Potential complications were defined as magnets in different intestinal loops attracting each other, compressing the bowel and posing a high likelihood of progression to complications over time.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on the events-per-variable (EPV) principle, which recommends a minimum of 10 EPVs [20]. With 57 complication cases, up to five predictors could be included in the final models.

Statistical analyses

The study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [21]. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Free Statistics software (version 1.9, Beijing, China). Statistical tests were two-tailed with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were compared using t-tests and non-normally distributed data using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and are compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Predictors of complications from pediatric multiple magnet ingestion were identified using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, which is well-suited for high-dimensional data. The optimal λ value was selected through five-fold cross-validation, with Lambda = min used for feature selection. These variables were then included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors. Two models were developed: (i) a radiologic model (Model R), using radiologic risk factors, and (ii) a clinical–radiologic model (Model C&R), which integrated clinical and radiologic factors. Nomograms were constructed for both models, and web-based dynamic versions were created to facilitate real-time clinical application.

Model performance was assessed by discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated by DeLong’s method. Calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test and calibration plots. Internal validation was performed with 1,000 bootstrap resamples to assess model stability and to obtain bootstrap-corrected AUCs with 95% CIs. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA).

Additional evaluation included the net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and conventional classification metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Optimal classification thresholds were selected based on clinical considerations and model performance. For Nomogram R, a relatively lower cutoff was chosen—rather than the Youden index—to prioritize sensitivity and improve its clinical applicability for early risk stratification. This threshold was determined based on clinical considerations to minimize the risk of missed complications. In contrast, for Nomogram C&R, the Youden index was applied to identify the threshold that maximized the combined sensitivity and specificity, balancing both detection and precision.

To assess the incremental value of radiologic features, the combined model (Nomogram C&R) was compared with our previously published clinical-only model (Nomogram C) [8] using the same dataset and evaluation metrics.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 148 patients with multiple magnet ingestion were reviewed. Two patients were excluded because they were discharged against medical advice and could not be contacted for follow-up, leaving their treatment outcomes undetermined. Thus, 146 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 95 males (65.1%) and 51 females (34.9%), aged 0.6–12.0 years (median, 4.6 years; IQR, 3.0–6.7 years). The patient selection flowchart, grouping, management and complications are shown in Fig. 1, and Table 1 summarizes baseline demographics [8]. Overall, 89 patients (61.0%) were included in the no-complication group and 57 (39.0%) in the complication group.

Among all patients, 81.5% were hospitalized with a median stay of 3 days, which increased to 10 days in complicated cases (Table 1). A total of 63% underwent gastroscopy and/or surgery, and 39% experienced complications, most commonly perforation and bowel obstruction (Fig. 1).

Radiologic findings

Abdominal radiographs confirmed the presence of foreign bodies in all patients. Incidental detection of magnetic foreign bodies occurred in 38.4% of patients with complications and 4.5% of those without complications (Table 1).

Lack of progression on serial radiographs was observed in 29 patients (19.9%) and was associated with larger magnets size (median, 28.0 mm [IQR, 17.0–39.0] vs. 20.0 mm [IQR, 12.0–30.0]; P = 0.03). Among these, 20 patients were in the complication group, and 10 showed magnets dispersed in different intestinal loops.

Gap between magnets was radiographically detected in 23 of 57 (40.4%) patients in the complication group, and in 11 of 89 (12.4%) in the no-complication group (Table 1). Endoscopic and surgical findings revealed dispersed magnets across multiple intestinal loops in 36 patients from the complication group; however, gap between magnets was visible on radiographs in only 14 of these patients (38.9%).

Patients with blurred magnet edges experienced significantly longer consultation delays (median, 2.5 days [IQR, 1.0–7.0] vs. 0.2 days [IQR, 0.1–0.9]; P < 0.01). Furthermore, 89.3% (25/28) of those with this radiographic sign developed complications.

Development of the risk prediction nomograms

The LASSO logistic regression analysis identified four features for Model R: magnets size > 25 mm, blurred magnet edges, gap between magnets, and lack of progression on serial radiographs (Fig. 2A, B). For Model C&R, 11 characteristics were selected: vomiting, abdominal pain, abdominal tenderness, fever, dispirited mood, age, multiple ingestions, magnets size > 25 mm, blurred magnet edges, gap between magnets, and lack of progression on radiographs (Fig. 2C and D). These characteristics were subsequently incorporated into the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2), which informed the development of two nomograms, Model R and Model C&R (Fig. 3). Higher scores corresponded to a greater risk of complications. Representative cases with radiologic findings and nomogram-predicted outcomes are shown in Fig. 4.

Performance and validation of the nomograms

The ROC analysis showed that the AUC was 0.85 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.79–0.92) for Nomogram R and 0.95 (0.92–0.99) for Nomogram C&R. Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap samples confirmed the discriminative ability of both models, with AUCs of 0.86 (0.79–0.92) for Nomogram R and 0.96 (0.92–0.98) for Nomogram C&R (Fig. 5A).

In addition, the HL test showed that both models demonstrated goodness-of-fit (P = 0.41 and 0.78). The calibration curves from internal validation showed a high degree of agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 5B, C).

DCA showed that both nomograms provided significant net clinical benefit, with C&R showing a superior net benefit across a broader range of threshold probabilities compared to R (Fig. 5D). Nomogram C&R demonstrated significantly better reclassification, with a categorical NRI of 0.36 (95% CI: 0.17–0.55, P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.27 (95% CI: 0.18–0.35, P < 0.001).

The optimal threshold for Nomogram R was set at 0.30, yielding a sensitivity of 82.5% (95% CI: 72.1%–91.3%), a specificity of 70.8% (95% CI: 61.8%–79.9%), a PPV of 64.4% (95% CI: 57.5%–71.8%), an NPV of 86.3% (95% CI: 79.5%–91.8%), and an accuracy of 75.3% (95% CI: 66.2%–82.9%). In comparison, Nomogram C&R, with a threshold of 0.37, demonstrated superior performance, with a sensitivity of 87.7% (95% CI: 78.2%–94.9%), a specificity of 92.1% (95% CI: 85.7%–96.8%), a PPV of 87.7% (95% CI: 79.6%–93.6%), an NPV of 92.1% (95% CI: 86.7%–95.9%), and an accuracy of 90.4% (95% CI: 84.6%–94.2%). The corresponding 2 × 2 confusion matrices are presented in Table 3. These results indicated that Nomogram C&R exhibits enhanced predictive power and diagnostic accuracy compared to Nomogram R.

Comparison with previous clinical-only model

Nomogram C&R demonstrated modest but clinically relevant improvements compared with the clinical-only model (Nomogram C) [8]. Specifically, it achieved higher AUC (0.95 vs. 0.94), sensitivity (87.7% vs. 82.5%), and accuracy (90.4% vs. 89.0%), while maintaining comparable specificity (92.1% vs. 93.3%). Net reclassification and discrimination indices showed numerical improvement (categorical NRI 0.08, 95% CI − 0.03 to 0.19, P = 0.17; IDI 0.05, 95% CI − 0.01 to 0.10, P = 0.10), although these were not statistically significant.

Web-based access to the nomogram TOOLKITS

To facilitate clinical and research applications, we developed two web-based nomogram toolkits(Nomogram R and Nomogram C&R), which are freely accessible worldwide. These interactive platforms allow users to enter patient data and generate visualised predictions of complication risk.

Discussion

In our cohort, hospitalization and complication rates were relatively high, but consistent with those reported in previous studies [22, 23], underscoring the substantial risks associated with pediatric multiple magnet ingestion and the importance of timely diagnosis and management. To our knowledge, this represents the largest single-center study to systematically evaluate both clinical and radiologic predictors in this setting. We developed two novel nomograms—Nomogram R and Nomogram C&R—demonstrating robust discrimination and potential clinical utility. These tools complement existing guidelines by providing individualized, imaging-informed risk assessment to support early decision-making and potentially improve patient outcomes.

Importantly, we included both confirmed and potential complications in our outcome definition. Potential complications referred to cases in which multiple magnets were attracting across separate intestinal loops without an immediate complication, but with a high likelihood of progression if left untreated. Based on pathophysiological evidence and clinical experience, these cases share the same underlying mechanism as overt complications. Including them may facilitate earlier identification of high-risk patients before complications arise. While this approach may limit direct comparability with studies that only consider clinically confirmed complications, it more closely reflects real-world risk assessment and the need for timely intervention.

Abdominal radiography is critical in detecting magnetic foreign body ingestion, particularly in cases without a confirmed history of ingestion [24]. In our study, incidental detection occurred in 5% of patients without complications and 40% with complications, highlighting its diagnostic value. This may be particularly relevant in younger children and those with cognitive impairments who cannot reliably report symptoms. Small, high-density buckyballs (6 mm in diameter) accounted for 85% of ingestions. Given their small size, these may be overlooked in clinical practice, particularly by less experienced clinicians. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion magnet ingestion when identifying multiple high-density, chain-like spherical objects (5–6 mm diameter), even without a reported ingestion history. Early recognition and classification of such findings are important for timely intervention and may help prevent severe complications.

Conventional radiographic indicators, such as pneumoperitoneum and intestinal necrosis, may underestimate the severity of complications when compared with surgical findings. Incorporating novel imaging signs could improve the accuracy of complication prediction and offer a more comprehensive radiographic evaluation. Larger and more numerous magnets were associated with complications, consistent with previous findings [25]. Magnet size >25 mm (equivalent to more than four 6-mm buckyballs) was a significant predictor of complications, aligning with prior findings that ingestion of >3 buckyballs increases surgical risk [26]. The risk of injury appears to increase with magnet size and number, likely due to dispersion throughout the gastrointestinal tract or impaction at anatomical narrowings such as the pylorus and ileocecal valve [11, 27].

Lack of progression on serial radiographs emerged as an important predictor of complications. While progression does not preclude the possibility of complications [28], non-progression should raise clinical concern for potential complications. Our findings are consistent with current guidelines, which recommend considering intervention when magnets fail to progress [5]. A plausible mechanism is that multiple magnets in separate intestinal segments may resist peristalsis due to magnetic attraction [27]. The Royal College of Emergency Medicine further advises repeat radiographs after 6–12 h in the same supine position, with formal radiologic assessment of progression [19].

The presence of gap between magnets was observed in 40% of complicated cases but only 12% of uncomplicated cases, suggesting potential value as a predictive marker. These subtle separations may reflect bowel wall entrapment and damage [11–13]. However, the radiographic sign of gap between magnets was absent in over 60% of patients with surgically or endoscopically confirmed dispersed magnets, likely due to factors such as intestinal wall compression, overlapping loops, or suboptimal radiographic angles. Notably, some patients in the no-complication group also exhibited this sign, possibly due to intervening bowel contents. Recognition of this sign was sometimes challenging when separations were subtle, however, careful review with image magnification and contrast adjustment improved detectability. Therefore, although informative, this marker should be interpreted in conjunction with other radiologic and clinical indicators rather than relied upon in isolation.

We identified the blurred edge sign as a potential novel predictor of complications. This sign was present in nearly half of the complicated cases and was associated with a complication rate of approximately 90% (PPV, 90%). In multivariable analysis, it remained an independent risk factor (adjusted OR, 10.0, 95% CI, 2.6–38.7, P = 0.001). The blurred edges may reflect prolonged exposure to digestive fluids, leading to magnet erosion—a phenomenon observed intraoperatively and consistent with previous case report [29]. This finding may resemble the corrosive changes seen in button batteries [30]. Given its high predictive value, clinicians and radiologists are advised to carefully examine radiographs and consider adjusting contrast and magnification to improve detection accuracy [13].

Building on these radiographic predictors, we developed Nomogram R to integrate multiple imaging features into a unified risk assessment model. A relatively lower cutoff (0.30) was selected based on clinical considerations to optimize sensitivity (82.5%) and improve applicability for risk stratification. With good discriminative performance (AUC, 0.85), Nomogram R may provide an objective, imaging-based assessment that complements clinical evaluation, particularly in asymptomatic patients and those with uncertain ingestion histories. It may serve as an adjunctive tool for early risk identification when symptom-based assessment is limited. Although it is not intended to function as a standalone decision-making instrument, its enhanced sensitivity and potential utility in broader clinical contexts warrant further validation.

Nomogram C, a previously developed clinical-only model, demonstrated excellent performance and remains useful for risk stratification in pediatric multiple magnet ingestion [8]. By incorporating radiologic predictors, Nomogram C&R modestly improved overall performance, with higher sensitivity and accuracy while maintaining specificity. In this high-risk setting, even incremental improvements in early risk identification can be clinically meaningful, as they may reduce the likelihood of missed complications. Although the observed improvements in NRI and IDI did not reach statistical significance, they suggest a potential trend toward better discrimination that warrants confirmation in larger, prospective cohorts. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating clinical and radiologic data to improve predictive accuracy and support timely, individualized decision-making.

Freely accessible online, these nomograms may serve as practical decision-support tools. Nomogram C&R stratifies patients into high-risk (≥ 0.37) and low-risk (< 0.37) groups, offering a clinically actionable framework for decision-making. For high-risk patients, surgical or endoscopic removal may be considered to prevent serious complications. Conversely, conservative management—including outpatient follow-up or short-term hospitalization with close observation and serial radiographs—might be appropriate for selected low-risk patients, potentially reducing unnecessary procedures and anaesthesia-related risks [7]. The model’s high sensitivity (87.7%) and NPV (92.1%) suggest utility in early identification of at-risk patients, while its high specificity (92.1%) and PPV (87.7%) may help minimize overtreatment. This stratification approach may facilitate more efficient clinical decision-making and support rational use of healthcare resources in managing this high-risk pediatric population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce information bias due to reliance on documented medical records. Second, although our single-center cohort was larger than those in prior studies, generalizability remains limited. As a tertiary referral center, our institution may have admitted a higher proportion of severe or complicated cases, potentially leading to overestimation of complication rates. Third, for patients without serial radiographs, magnet progression was assumed to ensure consistent classification, which may have introduced classification bias, particularly in patients requiring urgent intervention; however, such patients typically exhibited other high-risk clinical or radiologic features that were captured by the model, limiting the potential impact of this assumption. Fourth, the interval between radiographs (typically 6–12 h) was not systematically recorded, but this reflects routine practice and aligns with guideline recommendations, thereby maintaining clinical relevance. Fifth, although radiographic signs were reviewed by two experienced pediatric radiologists in consensus, interpretation—particularly of subtle features such as the “gaps between magnets” sign—may still involve subjectivity. Finally, prospective multicenter validation is required to enhance feasibility and generalizability, and to confirm the clinical utility of these predictive models and their integration into diagnostic pathways.

Conclusions

This study provides initial evidence for a radiologic assessment framework in pediatric multiple magnet ingestion. We identified key imaging predictors and developed two complementary nomograms: Nomogram R, designed for imaging-guided triage in asymptomatic or history-limited cases, and Nomogram C&R, which integrates clinical and radiologic data for individualized risk stratification. These tools may assist in supporting timely intervention in high-risk patients and reducing unnecessary procedures in low-risk patients, thereby contributing to more individualized clinical decision-making. Future prospective multicenter validation is required to confirm their clinical utility and integration into practice.
