Investigation of changes caused by Rapid Maxillary Expansion in mandibular bone and temporomandibular joint trabecular structure using fractal analysis
Authors: Gül Sümeyye Özkaymaz, Esra Çi̇fçi̇ Özkan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to evaluate the changes in the trabecular structure of the mandibular bone and temporomandibular joint following Rapid Maxillary Expansion.MethodsThirty-nine patients who received Rapid Maxillary Expansion treatment in the Department of Orthodontics of the Faculty of Dentistry were selected from the archieve for the study. Trabecular structure changes of the angulus mandible, corpus mandible, and condyle regions were examined using fractal analysis on the dental panoramic radiographs. Radiographs taken before the treatment (T0) and at the end of the 3-month retention period (T1) of the patients.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference between T0 and T1 in the condyle, ramus mandible, and corpus mandible regions depending on time and sex. (p < 0.05)ConclusionThe results of this study showed that Rapid Maxillary Expansion treatment does not have a significant impact on the trabecular structure of the mandible and temporomandibular joint depending on time and gender.Keywords: Fractal analysis, Rapid maxillary expansion, Panoramic radiography, Trabecular bone

Introduction

The treatment of maxillary narrowness often involves the separation and transverse widening of the median palatal suture, a longstanding fundamental approach in orthodontics [1]. During the expansion process using tooth-supported appliances, the heavy forces applied in the range of 900–4500 g are first transmitted to the teeth, then to the periodontal ligaments, and finally to the bones [2, 3]. As a result, with force distributed over the entire maxilla, the midpalatal suture is opened and expansion is achieved [4]. Due to the connections of the maxilla with other bones, studies have shown that the effects of Rapid Maxillary Expansion (RME) extend beyond the maxilla to encompass the surrounding structures such as the nasal cavity, mandible, pharyngeal structures, and temporomandibular joints (TMJ) are affected by this procedure [3, 5–12].

Following the separation of the suture, changes occur not only in the transverse dimension but also vertically and anteroposteriorly. One of the commonly observed effects of RME is the clockwise rotation of the mandibular plane, leading to a more inferior and posterior positioning of the mandible [9, 13]. These alterations influence other components of the craniofacial complex to which the maxilla is connected, such as the temporomandibular joint [14].

With maxillary expansion, the mandible rotates downward and posteriorly, accompanied by tilting and extrusion of the maxillary posterior teeth [3–5, 15–17]. Additionally, the overjet increases due to the forward displacement of the maxilla relative to the skull base and the downward and posterior rotation of the mandible [18]. These changes in dentition change the occlusal load. It is thought that the positional changes occurring in the maxilla and indirectly in the mandible due to occlusal changes and neuromuscular effects will impact the TMJ and adjacent structures, and these positive or negative effects may influence the bone and cartilage components of the joint morphologically in the long term [19].

In this study, Fractal Analysis (FA) was used to investigate potential changes in bone structure. This analysis utilizes mathematical techniques to assess irregular and complex anatomical structures [20, 21]. In recent years, fractal dimension (FD), which is the quantitative result of FA, has become a tool that allows estimating the quality of bone tissue in many studies where bone structures are analyzed. In dental clinical practice, dental radiographs are requested as complementary examination tools and FA is frequently used to evaluate jaw bone patterns in dental radiographs [22–25]. Techniques such as FA, which provide objective measurements of changes in bone patterns, are essential and irreplaceable in both clinical and scientific research [26].

Fractal analysis is frequently used in dentistry, especially in orthodontics, endodontics, periodontics and dental surgery [27–29]. Recent studies have investigated the changes in the mandible and TMJ in Class II patients undergoing functional orthopedic treatments, as well as in Class III patients receiving face mask therapy with FA [30–32]. Similarly, the effects of RME on the maxilla have been explored [33]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the impact of RME treatment on the mandible and TMJ is being examined for the first time with fractal analysis in the present study.

The purpose of this study is to investigate the effects of the related forces on the bone structure using the FA method, considering the publications showing that the force applied to the maxilla with RME also affects the mandible and TMJ.

Material- method

A total of 39 patients, including 20 females and 19 males, aged 12.5+−2.2 years, who applied to the Department of Orthodontics at the Faculty of Dentistry and underwent RME treatment, were selected from the archive for the study.

Patients with unilateral or bilateral posterior crossbite who underwent RME treatment, and who had no systemic diseases, were included in the study. The inclusion criteria also required high-quality diagnostic imaging records, characterized by high resolution, which meant the absence of internal or external artifacts, adequate coverage of the region of interest, proper patient positioning, and optimal image density, sharpness, and contrast. Conversely, individuals with cleft lip and palate, trauma in the craniofacial region, severe facial asymmetry, congenital anomalies, bone defects, temporomandibular joint disorders, or poor-quality panoramic radiographs (related to undesired patient movement, irregularity of the exposure roller or sensor and reader errors, noise, abnormal density and contrast, etc.) were excluded from the study.

An acrylic cover splint with Hyrax screw was used for RME. The activation time of the screw was set as 2 turns per day (0.25 mm per turn). When the palatal cusps of the maxillary first molars occluded with the buccal cusps of the mandibular first molars, it was decided that the amount of maxillary expansion was sufficient and the retention procedure was started. Panoramic radiographs of the patients taken before RME (T0) and at the end of the 3-month retention period (T1) were evaluated. All radiographs were obtained under consistent conditions: in the natural head position, using the same clinician and machine (Dentsply Sirona, Orthophos XG5, Hessen, Germany) at 68 kVp, 8 mA dose and 14.1 s exposure time. The radiographs were converted to .tif format (tiff) due to high resolution for analysis by FA technique. ImageJ 1.53 sofware program was used for FA. The FA calculations were performed on the same computer and by the same individual, following the box-counting technique described by White and Rudolph [34]. For this method region of interests (ROI) were defined as 54 × 54 pixels. A total of 6 ROIs were selected, 3 on the right and 3 on the left, including the subcortical region of the condyle, angulus mandible and corpus mandibula (between the roots of the 2nd premolar and the 1st molar) (Fig. 1). The relevant regions were determined to exclude the cortical border, mandibular nerve, and periodontal ligament. After copying the ROI from the areas to be analyzed, the image was blurred using the Gaussian blur filter with a value of 35. This step was performed to reduce brightness variations, and the blurred image was then subtracted from the original. A value of 128 Gy shades was added to the resulting image, which helped distinguish variations in brightness, particularly in the bone marrow and trabecular architecture. The image was then processed using a series of operations: first, the “Binary” option was applied to convert the image to black and white, enhancing the visibility of trabecular structures. Subsequently, “Erode” and “Dilate” operations were sequentially used to reduce noise and smooth the image. The “Invert” function was applied to switch the white and black areas, further emphasizing the trabecular bone outlines. To prepare the trabecular structure for fractal analysis, the “Skeletonize” option was used, clarifying the skeletal framework. Finally, the “Fractal Box Count” option from the “Analyze” menu was applied to calculate the FD value, which reflects the complexity of the trabecular structure (Fig. 2).

Two weeks after the initial measurements, the same observer re-measured all 6 regions in 20 randomly chosen radiographs. Since there was no statistically significant difference between the initial and subsequent measurements, the first set of measurements was used for the research. This retrospective study received ethical approval from the ethics committee under decision number 2023/79 − 27.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using the program “G. Power-3.1.9.2” at a confidence level of 95%. As a result of the analysis, α = 0.05, the standardized effect size was calculated as 0.95 with theoretical power, and the minimum sample size was calculated as 12.

Descriptive statistics (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum) are provided in this study. The normal distribution assumption was checked by the Shapiro-Wilk test and the variance homogeneity was checked by the Levene test. In cases where the normality assumption is met, the Dependent Sample t- test was used to compare the two dependent groups; In cases where the assumption was not met, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. The analyses were carried out using the IBM SPSS 25 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) program.

Results

A total of 39 patients, 20 females and 19 males, aged 12.5 ± 2.2 years, who applied to the Department of Orthodontics of the Faculty of Dentistry and underwent RME treatment, were included in the study.

As a result of the analysis, no statistically significant differences were found between the condyle, angulus, and corpus measurements on the right and left sides according to the measurement times (p > 0.05). Discussion

In this study, changes in the trabecular structure of the mandible and repositioned TMJ were investigated using FA following RME treatment. FA allows for the quantitative determination of the shape and size of structures, providing numerical insights into their characteristics [35, 36]. To date, many studies have used FA to evaluate bone structure [32, 33, 35].

While the literature extensively examines the effects of RME on the mandible and TMJ structure, our study represents the first investigation into potential changes in trabecular bone structure using FA [37, 38].

Although cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) offers three-dimensional analysis capabilities for bone tissue, it is not advisable to rely solely on it for scientific purposes or as a routine orthodontic examination due to ethical considerations. CBCT is primarily indicated in cases where adequate diagnostic data cannot be obtained using low-dose conventional radiographs [39, 40]. Therefore, the use of FA on panoramic radiographs is a convenient technique to determine bone trabecular configuration at different time points of treatment [41].

Magat et al. reported that dental panoramic radiography and reconstructed panoramic images from CBCT demonstrated comparable image quality in their study on peri-implant alveolar bone changes, utilizing trabecular microstructure analysis for evaluation.The use of CBCT for FA prevents the overlap of cortical bones on trabecular bones, resulting in more precise FD values; however, they said that given the radiation dose, image resolution, and cost of CBCT devices, it would be more appropriate to use dental panographic radiography instead of CBC T [42]. Thus, dental panoramic radiographs were utilized for FA in this study.

RME has been shown to influence the zygomatic and sphenoid bones as well as surrounding structures such as the TMJ, in addition to affecting the mid-palatal suture [43]. During the expansion process, peripheral structures such as the zygomatic and sphenoid bones, along with the TMJ, are notably affected [12, 44, 45]. In addition, the intensity and application rate of the separating force applied to the midpalatal suture can cause functional loading and adaptive remodeling of the condyles. With the effect of this remodeling, the jaw position changes, altering the occlusion [46–48]. Torres et al. documented that RME impacts the condyle-fossa relationship, modifies the structure or morphology of the articular disc and preserves bilateral condylar symmetry [49].

Arslan et al. conducted a study comparing a treatment group RME combined with reverse headgear to a control group. They reported that the RME combined with reverse headgear treatment did not have an adverse effect on the temporomandibular condyles, as the FD of the temporomandibular condyles did not show significant changes following the treatment [32].

Amuk et al. reported that the Herbst appliance significantly impacted the trabecular configuration of the TMJ compared to the control group, with notable FD changes in the superoposterior and central condyle regions during the T0-T1 period. This FD change caused by the Herbst appliance occurs in a different direction in the superoposterior and central condyle; stating that this supports the induction of endochondral bone formation as well as the Herbst appliance reshapes the bone tissue in the condyles [30]. Similarly, Cesur et al. performed a study using panoramic X-rays and FA to assess the effects of functional orthopedic appliances on mandibular bone structure, finding significant trabeculation changes in both the condyle and mandibular corpus in the treatment group relative to the control group [50]. Conversely, Gümüş et al. examined the impact of functional orthopedic appliances on mandibular trabecular structure using FA and observed that while these appliances did not alter FD values in the mandibular condyle region, they did result in significant changes in the mandibular corpus region. The results of their study showed that changes in occlusion forces and local force applications can affect mandibular trabecular structure FD values more effectively [31]. In our study, no alterations were detected in the TMJ trabecular structure with RME, which may be attributed to the short duration of treatment and retention period to cause a change.

While significant changes in the TMJ structure and condylar position are often observed in studies involving functional orthopedic forces, which directly impact these structures, our study did not detect significant changes. This lack of observed change may be attributed to the indirect effects of RME on the TMJ and mandible Additionally, the duration of treatment and the force applied in treatments using RME alone were shorter compared to those involving RME combined with a face mask or functional treatments. Therefore, no significant change in the trabecular bone structure of the temporomandibular condyle and mandible could be detected in our study.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, no significant effect of RME on the mandibular and TMJ trabecular structure was observed. However, the relatively short follow-up period and small sample size may have limited the ability to detect subtle or delayed changes. Individual variations, particularly in different age groups or skeletal maturation stages, may also influence the response to RME. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that while RME may not have an immediate impact on mandibular trabecular structure, clinicians should consider patient-specific factors, such as age and skeletal maturity, when evaluating RME outcomes. This highlights the importance of personalized treatment planning and the potential need for extended follow-up in younger patients or those at different stages of skeletal development. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and advanced imaging techniques are recommended to better understand the long-term impact of RME on trabecular bone structure.
