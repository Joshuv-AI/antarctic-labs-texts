Dimension-decaying diffusion processes as the scaling limit of condensing zero-range processes
Johel Beltrán, Kyuhyeon Choi, Claudio Landim

Abstract
. In this article, we prove that, on the diffusive time scale, condensing zero-range processes converge to a dimension-decaying diffusion process on the simplex Σ = { ( x 1 , … , x S ) : x i ≥ 0 , ∑ i ∈ S x i = 1 } , \Sigma=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{S}):x_{i}\geq 0,\;\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}=1\}, where S S is a finite set. This limiting diffusion has the distinctive feature of being absorbed at the boundary of the simplex. More precisely, once the process reaches a face Σ A = { ( x 1 , … , x S ) : x i ≥ 0 , ∑ i ∈ A x i = 1 } , A ⊂ S , \Sigma_{A}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{S}):x_{i}\geq 0,\;\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}=1\},\qquad A\subset S, it remains confined to this set and evolves in the corresponding lower-dimensional simplex according to a new diffusion whose parameters depend on the subset A A . This mechanism repeats itself, leading to successive reductions of the dimension, until one of the vertices of the simplex is reached in finite time. At that point, the process becomes permanently trapped. The proof relies on a method to extend the domain of the associated martingale problem, which may be of independent interest and useful in other contexts.



1.
Introduction

Metastability is a physical phenomenon that is ubiquitous in
first-order phase transitions. Early attempts at a precise description
can be traced back, at least, to Maxwell
[
30
]
. Following the
seminal work of Cassandro, Galves, Olivieri, and Vares
[
12
]
,
and building on the foundational contributions of Lebowitz and Penrose

[
25
]
, numerous rigorous mathematical theories have been
developed to describe metastable phenomena
[
6
,
31
,
24
,
9
]
. We refer to
[
32
,
10
,
22
]
for recent monographs.

These theories have been applied and further developed in a wide range
of contexts, including statistical mechanics
[
26
,
20
]
,
neural networks
[
11
,
28
]
, molecular dynamics
[
27
]
,
finance
[
29
]
, population dynamics
[
35
]
, and dynamical
systems
[
13
]
, to mention a few.

Many of these theories were developed from the study of specific
examples of stochastic dynamics, with the goal of formalizing the
metastable behavior observed in such systems. The model reduction
approach
[
6
,
8
,
24
]
, for instance, originated from the
analysis of condensing zero-range processes.

This stochastic dynamics, introduced by Evans
[
14
]
, describes
the evolution of particles on a finite set
S
and is conservative, in
the sense that the total number of particles is preserved. Its
stationary states—one for each fixed number of particles—exhibit a
peculiar structure known in the physics literature as

condensation
. Mathematically, this means that, under the
stationary distribution and above a certain critical density, a
macroscopic number of particles concentrates on a single site

[
14
,
19
]
. This phenomenon has been observed and studied in a
variety of contexts, including shaken granular systems, growing and
rewiring networks, traffic flows, and wealth condensation in
macroeconomics. We refer to
[
15
]
for a comprehensive review.

Condensation in a class of zero-range dynamics was first established
rigorously in
[
17
]
by proving the equivalence of ensembles in
the thermodynamic limit. This result was later refined in
[
3
,
4
,
2
]
and shown to remain valid even when the total number of
sites is fixed while the number of particles diverges
[
7
]
.

Once the presence of a condensate in the stationary state is
established, it is natural to investigate its time evolution

[
16
]
. This problem was addressed in
[
7
]
for
super-critical reversible dynamics, where the authors proved that, on
an appropriate time scale, the position of the condensate evolves as a
random walk whose jump rates are proportional to the capacities of the
underlying random walks. This result was subsequently extended to
super-critical totally asymmetric dynamics on a finite discrete torus
in
[
21
]
, to the general super-critical case in

[
34
]
, and to the critical symmetric case in
[
23
,
24
]
.

To describe the problem we examine in this article, fix a finite set

S
, and jump rates
r\colon S\times S\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Assume that the
continuous-time
S
-valued Markov chain associated to the jump rates

r(i,j)
is irreducible. Denote by
(m_{i}:i\in S)
its unique
stationary state. Fix the jump rate
g\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,\infty)

given by
g(0)=0
,
g(n)=1+(b/n)
,
n\geq 1
, where
b>0
is a
fixed parameter. The zero-range dynamics associated to the pair

(r,g)
is the
{\mathbb{N}}^{S}
-valued Makov chain in which a particle at
site
i
jumps to
j
at rate
g(p)\,r(i,j)
if there are
p

particles at site
i
.

In this article, we investigate the mechanism through which a
condensate is formed. In this model, two relevant nucleation time
scales arise. Starting from an initial configuration with positive
particle density at each site, the process evolves on a linear time
scale according to the solution of an ordinary differential equation,
converging to a state in which all particles concentrate on the sites
where the invariant measure
m
attains its maximum
[
1
]
.

We consider here the next time-scale. The dynamics is said to be
super-critical if
b>1
, and critical if
b=1
because in the first
case the condensate evolves is the time-scale
N^{1+b}

[
7
,
21
,
34
]
, while it in the second one it evolves in the
time-scale
N^{2}\ln N

[
23
,
24
]
. If
b<1
there is no
condensation.

Assume, for simplicity, that the stationary state
of the the underlying
S
-valued Markov chain is the uniform measure,
and that the process starts from an initial configuration with
positive particle density at each site.

In
[
5
]
, the authors showed that in the super-critical case on
the diffusive time scale, the evolution of the particle density
— defined as the number of particles at each site divided by the total
number of particles — converges to an absorbed diffusion process on the
simplex

\Sigma=\{\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{S})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}:x_{i}\geq 0\,,\,\;\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}=1\,\}
. The generator
{\mathfrak{L}}
of this
diffusion is given by

({\mathfrak{L}}F)(x)\,=\,\sum_{i\in S}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{b}}_{i}}F)(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in S}m_{i}\,r(i,j)\,[\,(\partial_{x_{j}}-\partial_{x_{i}})^{2}F]\,(x)\,,\quad x\in\Sigma\,,

where

(\nabla_{{{\boldsymbol{b}}}_{i}}F)(x)\,=\,b\,{\mathtt{1}}\{x_{i}>0\}\left(\frac{{m}_{i}}{x_{i}}\right)\sum_{j\in S}r(i,j)\,[(\partial_{x_{j}}-\partial_{x_{i}})F]\,(x)\,,\quad x\in\Sigma\,.

Note that the drift diverges as the diffusion approaches the boundary,
and that the parameter
b
appears only as a multiplicative constant
of the drift.

As mentioned in the abstract, this limiting diffusion has the
distinctive feature of being absorbed at the boundary of the
simplex. More precisely, once the process reaches a face

\Sigma_{A}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{S}):x_{i}\geq 0,\;\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}=1\}
,
A\subset S
, it remains confined to this set and evolves in
the corresponding lower-dimensional simplex according to a new
diffusion whose parameters depend on the subset
A
. This mechanism
repeats itself, leading to successive reductions of the dimension,
until one of the extreme points of the simplex is reached in finite
time. At that instant, the process becomes permanently trapped. We
named such a process a
dimension-decaying
diffusion process.

One might be tempted to attribute the absorption at the boundary to
the divergence of the drift. This interpretation, however, is
incorrect, since for
b<1
the process is expected to be reflected at
the boundary. Thus, the multiplicative parameter
b
, which may appear
innocuous at first glance, plays a fundamental role in determining the
qualitative behavior of the diffusion.

Although multidimensional diffusions with boundaries have been
extensively studied since the seminal work of Wentzell
[
36
]
(see
also
[
33
]
and
[
18
, Section V.6]
), we are not aware of
examples in the literature exhibiting this type of behavior, nor of a
theoretical framework that adequately accounts for it. A distinctive
feature of the process considered here is the divergence of the drift
at the boundary, which implies that
{\mathfrak{L}}u
fails to be continuous up
to the boundary even for smooth functions
u
, a regularity assumption
that is typically imposed in the study of diffusions with boundaries.

The proof presented in
[
5
]
is divided into two main steps. First,
the authors showed that any limiting distribution of the process
solves an associated martingale problem. Second, they proved that this
martingale problem admits a unique solution. While the argument in the
second step is fairly general and extends to the critical case, the
first step relies on the construction of a superharmonic function
belonging to the domain of the generator. This part of the argument is
specific to the supercritical case and, in fact, contains a flaw, which
is corrected in the present work.

In this article, we propose a method for extending the domain of
generators that is particularly useful for the study of
dimension-decaying diffusions. This extension allows the inclusion in
the generator’s domain of functions that are discontinuous at the
boundary of the simplex. More precisely, functions for which

\lim_{n}F(x^{(n)})
may be different from
F(x)
for sequences

x^{(n)}=(x^{(n)}_{1},\dots,x^{(n)}_{S})
such that
x^{(n)}_{i}>0
,

\lim_{n}x^{(n)}=x
,
x_{i}=0
. Such an extension considerably
simplifies the construction of functions with prescribed properties in
the domain of the generator, in particular superharmonic
functions. Therefore, the interest of this article lies both in the
specific result on nucleation for critical condensing zero-range
processes and in the general methodology proposed to address such
problems.

To conclude this introduction, let us reinforce that for
b<1
, there
is no condensation, and one expects reflection at the boundary with a
positive local time at the boundary. Proving the convergence of
zero-range dynamics to the corresponding diffusion is an interesting
open problem.

Remark 2.5
.

In Section
8
we introduce and alternative martingale problem
and show in Theorem

that any solution of the

({\mathfrak{L}},{\mathcal{D}}_{S})
-martingale problem is also a solution of the
alternative one. The uniqueness part in Theorem

is proved through the alternative martingale problem.

The statement of the theorem is identical to
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]

and
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
, and we adopt a similar strategy. We
begin by showing that the solution to the martingale problem is
boundary dimension-decaying, or, equivalently, absorbing at the
boundary (Theorem

). Using this property, we then
establish uniqueness, following the argument in
[
5
, Section 6]
.
Finally, we prove that the sequence

is tight and
converges to the unique solution of the martingale problem, adapting
the method from
[

, Section 7]
.

Although the overall structure of the proof follows that of

[
5
]
, the case

introduces a difficulty in establishing
the absorbing property of the solution to the martingale problem. In
particular, this step requires constructing a super-harmonic function
that lies in the domain of the generator, and this construction
becomes delicate when

To construct such a function, we introduce a method, presented in
Section

, which we refer to as the

. The core idea of this approach is Theorem

whose proof is given in Section

-martingale

contains

and

therefore extends the domain of the generator

construct super-harmonic functions which do not belong to

but only to

A boundary dimension-decaying diffusion

For each

Organization:
The article is organized as follows. In

. This process provides the

Fix a subset

It is well-known that the solution

has a stochastic

u^{B}_{k}(j)\,=\,{\mathbb{P}}_{j}[T_{k}=T_{B}]\,,\quad j\in S\,.

We turn to the formula (

\lambda_{i}\,u^{B}_{k}(i)\,=\,\lambda_{i}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{i}[T_{k}=T_{B}]\,=\,\sum_{j\in S}r(i,j)\,{\mathbb{P}}_{j}[T_{k}=T_{B}]\,=\,\sum_{j\in S}r(i,j)\,u^{B}_{k}(j)\,.

As

\lambda^{B}(j)\,=\,-\,({\mathcal{L}}_{S}u^{B}_{j})(j)\,.

),

(3.10)

\begin{gathered}{}_{k}=u^{B}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}={\mathcal{L}}_{S}u^{B}_{k}(j)=r^{B}(j,k),\;\;j\,,\,k\in B\,,\;\;k\neq j.\\
[\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})]_{j}=u^{B}_{j}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}=({\mathcal{L}}_{S}u^{B}_{j})(j)\,=\,-\,\lambda^{B}(j),\;\;j\in B\,.\end{gathered}

Thus, the vectors
\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})
,
j\in B
, relate to the
generator
{\mathcal{L}}^{B}_{S}
in the same way that the vectors
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}
,

i\in S
, relate to the generator
{\mathcal{L}}_{S}
.

On the other hand, as
u^{B}_{k}
,
k\in B
, is
{\mathcal{L}}_{S}
-harmonic on

A
,

[\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})]_{k}\,=\,u^{B}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}={\mathcal{L}}_{S}u^{B}_{k}(i)=0\;\;\text{ for all $i\in A=S\setminus B$}\,.

In conclusion,

(3.11)

\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})={\boldsymbol{v}}^{B}_{j},\;\;j\in B\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=0\,,\;\;i\in A=S\setminus B\,.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following properties hold:

(1)

For
\varnothing\neq B\subsetneq S
,

\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i},i\in B\}
are linearly independent.

(2)

For all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
,
\gamma_{B}(x)-x
is a
linear combination of
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}
for
k\in A=S\setminus B
.

Proof.

For the first part, fix a vector
{\boldsymbol{f}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
. By definition of

{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{f}}=0
if and only if

({\mathcal{L}}_{S}{\boldsymbol{f}})_{i}=0
. As the chain is irreducible,

{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{f}}=0
for all
i\in S
implies that
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is
constant. Hence,

\text{dim}(\text{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:k\in S\})=|S|-1
. Therefore,
together with the fact that

\sum_{k\in S}m_{k}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}=0\,,

for any
i\in S
the vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j},\;j\in S\setminus\{i\}\}
are
linearly independent. This proves the first part.

We turn to the second assertion of the lemma.
Recall that

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=x_{i}+\sum_{j\in A}u^{B}_{i}(j)x_{j},\;\;i\in B.

Thus,
\gamma_{B}(\gamma_{B}(x))=\gamma_{B}(x)
so that

\gamma_{B}(\gamma_{B}(x)-x)=\gamma_{B}(x)-\gamma_{B}(x)=0.

Therefore, to prove the assertion we need
to analyze the kernel of
\gamma_{B}
.

We claim that

\text{ker}(\gamma_{B})=\text{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:k\in A\}
. On the one
hand, by (
3.11
),

\text{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:k\in A\}\subset\text{ker}(\gamma_{B})
, and by
the first part of the lemma,

\text{dim\,}(\text{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:k\in A\})=|A|
. On the
other hand, since
\gamma_{B}
preserves

{\mathbb{R}}^{B}\times\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
,

\text{dim}(\text{Im}(\gamma_{B}))\geq|B|
. Therefore,

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\gamma_{B}))\leq|S|-|B|=|A|
, so that

\text{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:k\in A\}=\text{ker}(\gamma_{B})\,,

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next result is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\varnothing\neq B\subsetneq S
. Let
A=S\setminus B
. For

x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
, let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}x_{A}}
be the cannonical projection of
x
to

{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
defined by
[x_{A}]_{i}=x_{i}
for
i\in A
. Then, there exists
a linear map

such that

(3.12)

\gamma_{B}(x)-x=\sum_{i\in A}[L_{A}(x_{A})]_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.1
(2), there exists a linear map

L:{\mathbb{R}}^{S}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
such that

\gamma_{B}(x)-x=\sum_{i\in A}[L(x)]_{i}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\,.

Denote by
\pi_{A}:{\mathbb{R}}^{S}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
the canonical projection
defined by
\pi_{A}(x)=x_{A}
, we need to show that there exists a
factorization map

such that

. This is equivalent to

\text{ker}\,\pi_{A}\subset\text{ker}L\,.

It is clear that
\text{ker}\,\pi_{A}={\mathbb{R}}^{B}\times\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. By
definition,
\gamma_{B}
preserves
{\mathbb{R}}^{B}\times\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, which
implies that
{\mathbb{R}}^{B}\times\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\subset\text{ker}L

because, by Lemma
3.1
.(1), the vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i},i\in A\}
are
linearly independent.
∎

An important property of the map
\gamma_{B}
is that the function maps
an interior of a subsimplex of
\Sigma
into the one of

\Sigma_{B}
. This is stated as follows:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
B,C\subset S
. For each
i\in B
, either
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=0

for all
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
or
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}\neq 0
for all

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
. Therefore, there exists
D\subset B
such
that
\gamma_{B}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{C})\subset\mathring{\Sigma}_{D}
.

Proof.

Let
A=S\setminus B
. By (
3.9
), for every
i\in B
,

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
,

(3.13)

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=x_{i}+\sum_{k\in A}u_{i}^{B}(k)\,x_{k}=x_{i}+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}u_{i}^{B}(k)\,x_{k}\,.

Since
x_{j}>0
for all
j\in C
and
u_{\ell}^{B}(k)\geq 0
for
all
\ell\in B
,
k\in S
,

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=0
if, and only if,
x_{i}=0
(that is

)
and
u_{i}^{B}(k)=0
for all
k\in A\cap C
. This condition does not
depend on the point

, but only on the
indices
i
and
u^{B}_{i}(k)
. Therefore, either it holds for all points in

\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
or it holds for none. This is the first
assertion of the lemma.

Let

\displaystyle G\,=\,\{i\in B:u_{i}^{B}(k)=0\text{ for all }k\in A\cap C\}\,,

\displaystyle D\,=\,B\setminus\big\{\,[B\setminus C]\,\cap\,G\,\big\}\,=\,[B\cap C]\,\cup\,[B\setminus G]\,,

so that
D\subset B
.

We claim that

\gamma_{B}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{C})\subset\mathring{\Sigma}_{D}
. Fix

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
. To prove the assertion, we have to show
that
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}>0
if
i\in D
and
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=0

otherwise.

Consider first the case
i\in D
. If
i\in B\cap C
, then
x_{i}>0

because
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
. Thus, by (
3.13
),

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}>0
. If
i\in B\setminus G
, then,
u_{i}^{B}(k)>0
for
some
k\in A\cap C
. As
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
,
x_{k}>0
. Thus,
by (
3.13
),
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}\geq u_{i}^{B}(k)\,x_{k}>0
. In conclusion,

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}>0
for all
i\in D
.

Fix
i\not\in D
. If
i\not\in B
, by (
3.9
),

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=0
. Suppose that
i\in B\setminus D
, so that

i\in(B\cap G)\setminus C
. As
i\not\in C
and

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}
,
x_{i}=0
. Since
i\in G
,
u_{i}^{B}(k)=0
for
all
k\in A\cap C
. Thus, by (
3.13
),
[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}=0
, as
claimed. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We conclude this section by presenting a formula for the composition
of projection maps associated with distinct simplices. This identity
plays a central role in establishing the absorbing structure of the
process, as stated in Proposition
2.8
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
\varnothing\neq B\subset C\subset S
. Then,

\gamma_{B}\circ\gamma_{C}=\gamma_{B}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\Sigma
, and recall that
A=S\setminus B
. By
(
3.9
), for
j\in B
,

\displaystyle[\gamma_{B}(\gamma_{C}(x))]_{j}

\displaystyle=[\gamma_{C}(x)]_{j}+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}u^{B}_{j}(k)\,[\gamma_{C}(x)]_{k}

\displaystyle=x_{j}+\sum_{i\in S\setminus C}u^{C}_{j}(i)\,x_{i}+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}u^{B}_{j}(k)\,\Big(x_{k}+\sum_{i\in S\setminus C}u^{C}_{k}(i)\,x_{i}\Big)\,.

We restricted the second sum in the first line to
k\in A\cap C

because
[\gamma_{C}(x)]_{k}=0
for
k\not\in C
.

On the other hand,

[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{j}=x_{j}+\sum_{k\in A}u^{B}_{j}(k)\,x_{k}\,.

Hence, deleting the common terms, to complete the proof it remains to
show that

\sum_{i\in S\setminus C}u^{C}_{j}(i)\,x_{i}+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}u^{B}_{j}(k)\sum_{i\in S\setminus C}u^{C}_{k}(i)\,x_{i}=\sum_{k\in A\setminus C}u^{B}_{j}(k)\,x_{k}\,.

Since
B\subset C
, in the last sum we may rewrite
A\setminus C
as

S\setminus C
. Comparing the coefficient of

x_{i},\;i\in S\setminus C
, the equation is equivalent to

u^{C}_{j}(i)+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}u^{B}_{j}(k)\,u^{C}_{k}(i)=u^{B}_{j}(i)\,.

By (
3.3
), this identity can be rewritten as

{\mathbb{P}}_{i}[T_{B}=T_{j}]={\mathbb{P}}_{i}[T_{C}=T_{j}]+\sum_{k\in A\cap C}{\mathbb{P}}_{i}[T_{C}=T_{k}]\,{\mathbb{P}}_{k}[T_{B}=T_{j}]\,.

which can be directly verified probabilistically.

We just proved that
[\gamma_{B}(\gamma_{C}(x))]_{j}=[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{j}
for
all
x\in\Sigma
,
j\in B
. By (
3.9
), this identity holds
trivially for
j\not\in B
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.
Extension of the martingale problem

In this section, we introduce a relaxed version of the martingale
problem. This modification is motivated by the need to construct an
appropriate superharmonic function, which is essential for
establishing the absorbing property and, in turn, for proving the
uniqueness of the solution to the martingale problem.

Unlike in
[
5
]
, the construction of the superharmonic function
in Section
7
cannot be carried out using only
functions from the original domain
{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
of the martingale
problem. Consequently, it becomes necessary to enlarge the domain to
include functions with weaker regularity. The purpose of this section
is to develop this extended framework.

To motivate this extension, we begin by examining the operator

{\mathfrak{L}}
from a different perspective. Recall from (
2.13
) and
(
2.14
) the definition of the domains
{\mathcal{D}}^{B}_{A}
for

\varnothing\subsetneq A\subset B\subset S
,
|B|\geq 2
and the
operator
{\mathfrak{L}}^{B}
. The next result provides an alternative formula
for the value of
{\mathfrak{L}}F
on the set
\Sigma_{B}
in terms of the
generator
{\mathfrak{L}}^{B}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
. Then, for every subset
B
of
S
with at least
two elements, the restriction of
F
to
\Sigma_{B}
, denoted by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F|_{\Sigma_{B}}}
, belongs to the domain
{\mathcal{D}}^{B}_{B}
. Moreover,

({\mathfrak{L}}F)|_{\Sigma_{B}}={\mathfrak{L}}^{B}(F|_{\Sigma_{B}})
.

To prove this second-order identity, we first establish a few auxiliary
lemmata controlling the second-order derivatives of
F
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
k\in S\setminus B
,
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Sigma_{B}
,

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{w}}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}F=0\;\;\text{ \rm on }\;\;\Sigma_{B}\,.

Proof.

Fix
k\in S\setminus B
,
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
. As
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
,

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k}}F
is a continuous function. Therefore,

(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}F)(x)=(x_{k}/m_{k})\,H(x)
for some continuous
function
H\in C(\Sigma)
which vanishes if
x_{k}=0
. As
k\not\in B
,

H|_{\Sigma_{B}}=0
.

For
x\in\Sigma_{B}
, since
(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}F)(x)=0
,

\displaystyle(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{w}}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}F)(x)\,=\,\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}F)(x+t({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{w}}))}{t}\,.

As
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Sigma_{B}
,
[{\boldsymbol{w}}]_{k}=0
so that

[{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{w}}]_{k}=1
. Hence, By definition of

H
, as
H
is continuous and
x_{k}=0
, this expression is equal to

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\,H(x+t({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{w}}))\,=\,\frac{1}{m_{k}}\,H(x)\,=\,0\,,

as claimed.
∎

The following lemma describes how the trace process and the projection
map are related through the Hessian term.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
F\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{S})
,

(4.1)

\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})}F=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}m_{i}\,r^{B}(i,j)\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})^{2}F\,.

Proof.

By the definition (
3.9
) of
\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})}F

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in S}\sum_{k\in B}a_{ij}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\Big\{\delta_{j,k}+\sum_{\ell\in A}u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,\delta_{j,\ell}\,\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{k\in B}a_{ik}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\,+\,\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{k\in B}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\sum_{\ell\in A}a_{i\ell}\,u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,,

where
A=S\setminus B
,

If
i\in A
, by the definition (
2.7
) of
a_{i\ell}
, and
(
3.4
),

\sum_{\ell\in A}a_{i\ell}\,u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,=\,-\,a_{ik}\,.

The right-hand side of (
4.2
) is thus equal to

\sum_{i,k\in B}a_{ik}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\,+\,\sum_{i,k\in B}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\sum_{\ell\in A}a_{i\ell}\,u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,.

On the other hand, by the first identity in (
3.5
) and (
3.7
),
if
i\in B
,
i\neq k
,

\sum_{\ell\in A}a_{i\ell}\,u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,=\,-\,a_{ik}\,-\,m_{i}\,r^{B}(i,k)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\ell\in A}a_{k\ell}\,u^{B}_{k}(\ell)\,=\,-\,m_{k}\,[\,\lambda_{k}-\lambda^{B}(k)\,]\,.

The right-hand side of (
4.2
) can be further simplified to

\sum_{k\in B}m_{k}\,\lambda^{B}(k)\,\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\,-\,\sum_{i\in B}\sum_{k\in B\setminus\{i\}}m_{i}\,r^{B}(i,k)\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}}F\,.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

A direct use of Lemmata
4.2
and
4.3
does not yield full
control of

D_{\tilde{a}}
, since the left-hand side of
(
4.1
) is not symmetric in

and

. In effect, we need to
swap the indices, and this is achieved by introducing the adjoint

{\mathcal{L}}_{S}^{\dagger}
and
({\mathcal{L}}_{S}^{B})^{\dagger}

Let
r^{\dagger}
be the adjoint of

with respect to

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}r^{\dagger}(i,j)}\,:=\,m_{j}\,r(j,i)/m_{i}\,,\quad i\neq j\in S\,.

Denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}_{S}}
the generator of the Markov chain
associated to the jump rates

, and let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\dagger}_{i}}\,:=\,\sum_{k\in S}r^{\dagger}(i,k)\,({\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})\,,\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a^{\dagger}_{ij}}\,:=\,-\,m_{i}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\dagger}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j},\;\;i,j\in S\,,

so that

on

Corollary 4.4
.

For any

\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\nabla_{\gamma_{B}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})}F\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}m_{i}\,r^{B,\dagger}(i,j)(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})^{2}F.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\nabla_{\gamma_{B}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})}F=\sum_{i,j\in S}a^{\dagger}_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\nabla_{\gamma_{B}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})}F\,.

By the previous lemma with the adjoint rates in place of the rates,

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}m_{i}\,r^{B,\dagger}(i,j)\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})^{2}F\,,

as claimed.

Since

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

We first claim that
F|_{\Sigma_{B}}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{B}_{B}

A=S\setminus B
. Fix
i\in B

\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{B}_{i}}F(x^{n})}{(x^{n})_{i}}\to 0\,.

By (

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{B}_{i}}F(x^{n})=\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})}F(x^{n})=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}F(x^{n})-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})}F(x^{n})\,.

{\mathfrak{L}}^{B}(F|_{\Sigma_{B}})=({\mathfrak{L}}F)|_{\Sigma_{B}}

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{L}}F(x)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}F(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}m_{i}\,r(i,j)\,[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}F)](x).

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}F(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\tilde{B}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}}F(x)\,=\,b\,\sum_{j\in\tilde{B}}m_{j}\,\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}}F(x)}{x_{j}}\,=\,b\,\sum_{j\in\tilde{B}}m_{j}\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{j}-\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})}F(x)}{x_{j}}\,+\,b\,\sum_{j\in\tilde{B}}m_{j}\frac{\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{j})}F(x)}{x_{j}}\,\cdot

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}F(y)=0

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\tilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}m_{i}\,r^{B,\dagger}(i,j)\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})^{2}\tilde{F}(x)\,+\,\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-\gamma^{\dagger}_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})}\tilde{F}(x)\,.

Since

, the second term
is equal to

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{i\in S}m_{i}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-\gamma^{\dagger}_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})}\,\tilde{F}(x)\,=\,-\,\sum_{i\in A}m_{i}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-\gamma^{\dagger}_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})}\,\tilde{F}(x)

Since
x\in\Sigma_{B}
, by Lemma
4.2
, this expression vanishes.

Therefore,

(4.4)

\sum_{i,j\in S}a_{ij}\,\partial_{x_{i}}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\tilde{F}(x)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}m_{i}\,r^{B,\dagger}(i,j)\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})^{2}\tilde{F}(x)\,.

As
m_{i}\,r^{B,\dagger}(i,j)=m_{j}\,r^{B}(j,i)
, combining
(
4.3
) and (
4.4
) yields that

(4.5)

{\mathfrak{L}}F(x)={\mathfrak{L}}^{B}F(x)\,,

which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

In light of Proposition
4.1
, we redefine the operator

{\mathfrak{L}}
on the domain
{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
, then further extend the domain of the
operator. To do so, we need the space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C_{\rm pc}(\Sigma)}
(pc
for piecewise), the space of functions
F\colon\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}
such
that, for each subset
B
of
S
with at least two elements, the
function

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}}\colon\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
continuous. To differentiate the new operator (with extended domain)
from the original one, represented by
{\mathfrak{L}}
, we denote it by

{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}
.

Definition 4.5
.

For
F\in C(\Sigma)
satisfying

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}}\in C^{2}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{C})
for all
nonempty
C\subset S
,
|C|\geq 2
, define the operator

{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}
as follows: For
x\in\Sigma
, let

B=\{i\in S:x_{i}\neq 0\}
. Then

{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}F\in C_{\rm pc}(\Sigma)
is defined by

(4.6)

{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}F(x)=\begin{cases}[{\mathfrak{L}}^{B}(F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}})](x),&\text{ if }B=\{i\in S:x_{i}\neq 0\},\;|B|\geq 2\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Here,
{\mathfrak{L}}^{B}
is the operator defined in (
2.14
), considered
as a local second order differential operator on

\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}
, as mentioned in Remark
2.9
.

Note that
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}
equals
{\mathfrak{L}}
on the domain
{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
by
Proposition
4.1
. The functions introduced in Definition

4.5
can have pathological behavior near the boundary of the
domain, as the value of
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}F
may diverge as
x

approaches the boundary of
\Sigma_{B}
, causing the martingale problem
to be ill-defined. For this reason, it is necessary to introduce
further conditions.

Condition
\mathfrak{E1}
:
For each
i\in S
,

F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E1}(i)
if the map

(4.7)

x\mapsto\frac{1}{x_{i}}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}F(x),

is bounded on
\{x\in\Sigma:x_{i}>0\}
. If
F
satisfies condition

\mathfrak{E1}(i)
for all
i\in S
, then we say that
F
satisfies
condition
\mathfrak{E1}
.

Similar to the definition in (
2.11
), for
A\subset S
with

|A|\geq 2
, let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{A})}\,:=\,\big\{\,f\in C^{2}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{A}):f\text{ has bounded
second derivatives}\,\big\}\,.

The expression“
f
has bounded second derivatives” means that for
all tangent vectors
{\boldsymbol{V}}
and
{\boldsymbol{W}}
in
T_{\Sigma_{A}}
, the second
derivative
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{V}}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}f)
is bounded on

\mathring{\Sigma}_{A}
. In other words,
there exists a finite constant
C_{0}>0
such that

(4.8)

\big|\,[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{V}}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{W}}}f)](x)\,\big|\,\leq\,C_{0}\,\|{\boldsymbol{V}}\,\|\,\|{\boldsymbol{W}}\,\|

for all
{\boldsymbol{V}},{\boldsymbol{W}}\in T_{\Sigma_{A}}
and

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{A}
.

Condition
\mathfrak{E2}
:
We say that

F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E2}(A)
,

A\subset S
with
|A|\geq 2
, if

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{A}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{A})
. If
F

satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E2}(A)
for all
A\subset S
with

|A|\geq 2
, then we say that
F
satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E2}
.

Remark 4.6
.

By Lemma
A.3
, a function
F\in C(\Sigma)
such that

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma})
belongs to

C^{1}(\Sigma)
, and, for any
B\subset S
with
|B|\geq 2
,

F|_{\Sigma_{B}}
belongs to
C^{1}(\Sigma_{B})
.

Definition 4.7
.

For each
i\in S
, denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
the collection of functions

F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
satisifying condition

\mathfrak{E1}(i)
and condition
\mathfrak{E2}
. In addition, let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{E}}_{A}}\,:=\,\bigcap_{i\in A}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\quad\textrm{ for each nonempty $A\subseteq S$.}

The next result is a consequence of Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition 4.8
.

It holds that
{\mathcal{D}}_{S}\subset{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
. Moreover,
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}F={\mathfrak{L}}F
for all
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
.

Proof.

Fix
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
. By Definition
2.1
,
F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
,
and
F
satisfies condition
{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}
. Fix a subset
A
of
S
with at
least two elements. By the same definition,

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{A}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{A})
. This proves
that
F
belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
. On the other hand, by (
4.6
)
and Proposition
4.1
,
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}F={\mathfrak{L}}F
, which
completes the proof of the proposition..
∎

We finally define the extended martingale problem. Let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C_{\rm pc}^{b}(\Sigma)}
be the space of bounded functions in

C_{\rm pc}(\Sigma)
. It is clear that the operator
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}

maps
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
to
C_{\rm pc}^{b}(\Sigma)
. Since

C_{\rm pc}^{b}(\Sigma)
is a subset of bounded Borel functions, we can
consider the following martingale problem.

Definition 4.9
.

A probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
on

C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\Sigma)
is a solution for the

({\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}},{\mathcal{E}}_{S})
-martingale problem if, for any

H\in{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
,

{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}H(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}({\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}}H)(X_{s})\,ds\;,\quad t\geq 0

is a
{\mathbb{P}}
-martingale with respect to the filtration

({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the same as in Definition
2.10
.

In the next section, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.10
.

For each
x\in\Sigma
, denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
a probability measure on

D({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\Sigma)
which starts at
x
and is a solution of the

({\mathfrak{L}},{\mathcal{D}}_{S})
-martingale problem. Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
also solves
the
({\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathcal{E}}},{\mathcal{E}}_{S})
-martingale problem.

4.1.
Extension map

Since our extended domain is defined by combining information from
subsimplices, we may want to extend functions defined on subsimplices
to the entire simplex
\Sigma
in a way that the extended function
belongs to the domain of the generator
{\mathfrak{L}}^{{\mathscr{E}}}
. This approach
plays a crucial role in Sections
6
and

7
.

Given a function
f\colon\Sigma_{B}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, define the function

\gamma^{*}_{B}f\colon\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

(4.9)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\gamma^{*}_{B}f)(x)}\,:=\,f(\gamma_{B}(x))\,,\;\;x\in\Sigma\,.

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose that
F
satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E2}
. Fix

\varnothing\neq B\subsetneq S
, and let
F_{B}
be the restriction of

F
to
\Sigma_{B}
:
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F_{B}=F|_{\Sigma_{B}}}
. Then,
\gamma_{B}^{*}F_{B}

belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{A}
, where
A=S\setminus B
.

Proof.

We need to check condition
\mathfrak{E1}(A)
and condition

\mathfrak{E2}
. First, note that
F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
.
By Remark
4.6
,
F_{B}=F|_{\Sigma_{B}}\in C^{1}(\Sigma_{B})
.

(1)
\gamma_{B}^{*}F_{B}
satisfies condition

\mathfrak{E1}(A)
.

Fix
i\in A
. By Lemma
A.2
, as
F_{B}\in C^{1}(\Sigma_{B})
,

\frac{1}{x_{i}}\,[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}(\gamma_{B}^{*}F_{B})](x)\,=\,\frac{1}{x_{i}}\,(\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})}F_{B})(\gamma_{B}(x))\;\;\text{ on the
set}\;\;\{x\in\Sigma:x_{i}>0\}\,.

By (
3.11
), as
i\in A
,
\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=0
. The
right-hand side thus vanishes. In particular, it is bounded as
required.

(2)
\gamma_{B}^{*}F_{B}
satisfies Condition

\mathfrak{E2}
.

We need to check condition
\mathfrak{E2}(C)
for all sets

C\subset S
,
|C|\geq 2
. As
F
belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{A}
,
it satisfies condition
{\mathfrak{E}}{\mathfrak{2}}(B)
. Hence,

F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{B})
, so that

F_{B}|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{B})
.

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.3
,

\gamma_{B}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{C})\subset\mathring{\Sigma}_{D}
for some

D\subset B
. Thus
(\gamma_{B}^{*}F_{B})|_{\mathring{\Sigma}_{C}}
belongs
to
C^{2}_{b}(\mathring{\Sigma}_{C})
, as claimed.
∎

The next Lemma states that some particular directional
derivatives of the extension map vanish.

Lemma 4.12
.

Fix
\varnothing\neq B\subset C\subset S
and

G\in C^{1}(\Sigma_{B})
. Then,

[\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{C}_{i}}(\gamma_{B}^{*}G)](x)=0
for all

i\in C\setminus B
,
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in C
. By Lemma
A.2
, and since, by (
3.11
),

{\boldsymbol{v}}^{C}_{i}=\gamma_{C}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})
,

[\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{C}_{i}}\,(\gamma_{B}^{*}G)\,]\,(x)\,=\,(\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}^{C}_{i})}G)\,(\gamma_{B}(x))\,=\,(\nabla_{\gamma_{B}\circ\gamma_{C}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})}G)\,(\gamma_{B}(x))

for all
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}
. By Lemma
3.4
, this
expression is equal to

(\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})}G)\,(\gamma_{B}(x))
. As
i\not\in B
,
by (
3.9
),
\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=0
, which completes the proof
of the lemma.
∎

5.
A norm map and related Lemmas

This section is devoted to the construction of maps

J_{A}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
A
a nonempty subset of
S
, which
mimic the norm of
{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
and whose directional derivatives at the
boundary of the simplex
\Sigma
behave nicely (see Lemmata

5.2
,
5.3
). These functions play a
crucial role in Section
6
to approximate functions
in
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
by functions in
{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
(Lemma
6.6
) and in
the construction of a superharmonic function in the domain
{\mathcal{E}}_{S}
,
see Lemma
7.2
.

For each nonempty
A\subseteq S
, endow
{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
with the norm

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\|x\|_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}|x_{i}|
and consider the
cone
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A}:x_{i}\geq 0,\forall i\in A\}
with
vertex
{{\boldsymbol{0}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For each nonempty subset
A
of
S
there exists a function

J_{A}\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\to[0,\infty)
such that

a)

J_{A}(\alpha x)=\alpha\,J_{A}(x)
, for all
\alpha\geq 0

and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}
.

b)

There exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty
so that

c_{1}\|x\|_{A}\leq J_{A}(x)\leq c_{2}\|x\|_{A},\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}.

c)

J_{A}
admits a
C^{\infty}
extension on a open subset of

{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
containing
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{{\boldsymbol{0}}}\}
.

d)

Assume that
|A|\geq 2
. For all

x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{{\boldsymbol{0}}}\}
, and
i\in A
,

x_{i}=0\quad\implies\quad\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}}J_{A}\;\;\textrm{vanishes on a neighborhood of $x$},

where each
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}
is the canonical projection (restriction) of
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
, i.e.,

[{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}]_{j}=[{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}]_{j},\;\;j\in A\,

Proof.

For
A\not=S
, this follows from the proof of Lemma 4.1 in

[
5
, Section 8]
. For
A=S
, it is clear that

J_{S}(x)=\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}_{+}
, satisfies all the
stated properties.
∎

We shall use each function
J_{A}
as a suitable perturbation of

\|\cdot\|_{A}
satisfying the above properties. When
A
is a
singleton, properties
a)
and
b)
imply that
J_{A}(x)=\lambda x
, for
some
\lambda>0
.

Let us now add some properties derived from Lemma
5.1
. Fix a
nonempty set
A\subseteq S
. It follows from property
a)
that for
every
\alpha>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
,

(5.1)

(\nabla J_{A})(\alpha x)\,=\,(\nabla J_{A})(x)\quad\textrm{and}\quad({\rm Hess}\,J_{A})(\alpha x)=\alpha^{-1}({\rm Hess}\,J_{A})(x).

Since
\nabla J_{A}
and
{\rm Hess}\,J_{A}

are continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{0\}
, then, by (
5.1
),

(5.2)

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}}\|\nabla J_{A}(x)\|_{A}<\infty\;\;\;\text{ and }\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}}\|x\|_{A}\|\text{Hess}\,J_{A}(x)\|_{A}<\infty.

Lastly, assume that
|A|\geq 2
, fix an arbitrary
i\in A
and recall
the vector
{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{i}
from property
d)
. In virtue of this property,

(5.3)

{\mathtt{1}}\{x_{i}>0\}\,\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}}J_{A}(x)}{x_{i}},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\,,\;\;\text{ is continuous.}

Then, by (
5.1
),

(5.4)

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{A}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\},\;x_{i}>0}\;\|x\|_{A}\;\frac{|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}}J_{A}(x)|}{x_{i}}<\infty\,.

Let
{\mathfrak{J}}_{A}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{S}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be given by

(5.5)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)}\,:=\,J_{A}(x_{A})\,.

To keep the notation simple, we define that
\|x\|_{A}=\|x_{A}\|_{A}
,
where
x_{A}
stands for the canonical projection of
x
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{A}
,

A
is a nonempty subset of
S
and
x\in\Sigma
. The next result is
the estimate (
5.4
) stated in terms of this new notation

{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all nonempty
A\subseteq S
and
i\in A
we have

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}_{+}:\|x\|_{A}>0,\,x_{i}>0}\;\|x\|_{A}\;\frac{|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)|}{x_{i}}<\infty.

Next result is a corollary of Lemma
5.2
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
x\in\Sigma
and let
C=\{i\in S:x_{i}\neq 0\}
. Suppose

B\subsetneq C
. For
A=S\setminus B
,
{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)>0
and

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)=0,\quad\forall k\notin C.

Proof.

We first show that
\|x\|_{A}>0
. Suppose not. Then,
\|x\|_{A}=0

implies
x_{i}=0
for all
i\in A
. This implies
A\subset C^{c}
,
therefore
A^{c}=B\supset C
. This contradicts the assumption that

B\subsetneq C
.

Fix
k\not\in C
, and let
(x^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\Sigma
be a
sequence such that

x^{n}\to x\;\;\text{ and }\;\;(x^{n})_{k}>0,\;\;\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By Lemma
5.2
,

|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x^{n})|\to 0,

which implies the assertion of the lemma.
∎

Using the auxiliary function
{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}
, we derive estimates of
functions in
{\mathcal{E}}_{A}
. For
U\subset\Sigma
, let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}BC(\Sigma,U)}\,:=\,\big\{f:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}\text{ is
Borel measurable, bounded,
and }f|_{U}\text{ is continuous}\big\}\,.

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix
\varnothing\neq B\subsetneq S
, and let
A=S\setminus B
.
For any
G\in{\mathcal{E}}_{A}
with
G|_{\Sigma_{B}}=0
,

(1)

G=H{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}^{2}
for some

H\in BC(\Sigma,\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{B})
.

(2)

For all
i,j\in S
,

(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})G=K{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}
for some

K\in BC(\Sigma,\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{B})
.

Proof of (1).

By condition
\mathfrak{E1}(i)
,
i\in A
,
G
belongs to

C^{1}(\Sigma)
and there exists a bounded Borel
function
h_{i}\colon\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}
, such that

\frac{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}G(x)}{x_{i}}=h_{i}(x)\quad\text{for}\;\;\{x_{i}>0\}\,.

Let
C>0
be an upper bound of the functions
|h_{i}|
, that is,

\max_{i\in A}\sup_{x\in\Sigma}|h_{i}(x)|\leq C
, so that

(5.6)

|\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}G(x)\,|\,\leq\,C\,x_{i}\quad\text{for all}\;\;x\in\Sigma\,.

We claim that there exists a finite constant
C_{0}
such
that
|G(x)|\leq C_{0}{\mathfrak{I}}^{2}_{A}(x)
for all
x\in\Sigma
. This
inequality trivially holds on
\Sigma_{B}
because both functions vanish
on this set. Fix
x\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{B}
. Since

G
is of class
C^{1}(\Sigma)
,
G|_{\Sigma_{B}}=0
and
\gamma_{B}(x)\in\Sigma_{B}
, by Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
A.1
,

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x-\gamma_{B}(x)}G(\gamma_{B}(x)+t(x-\gamma_{B}(x)))\,dt

\displaystyle=\,-\,\sum_{i\in A}\int_{0}^{1}[L_{A}(x_{A})]_{i}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}G(\gamma_{B}(x)+t(x-\gamma_{B}(x)))\,dt\,.

By definition of the constant
C
, the absolute value of the previous
expression is bounded by

\displaystyle C\sum_{i\in A}\big|\,[L_{A}(x_{A})]_{i}\,\big|\,\int_{0}^{1}\big|\,[\gamma_{B}(x)+t(x-\gamma_{B}(x))]_{i}\,\big|\;dt

\displaystyle\quad\leq\,C\sum_{i\in A}\big|\,[L_{A}(x_{A})]_{i}\,\big|\,\Big\{\,|\,[\gamma_{B}(x)]_{i}\,|\,+\,|\,x_{i}\,|\,\Big\}\,\leq\,C^{\prime}\,\|x_{A}\|^{2}

for some new finite constant
C^{\prime}
. Thus,

|G(x)|\leq C_{0}{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}^{2}(x)
for some finite constant
C_{0}>0
, as
claimed.

To complete the proof of assertion (1), it remains to
define
H(x)
as
G(x)/{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}^{2}(x)
for

x\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{B}
and
H(y)=0
for
y\in\Sigma_{B}
. Clearly,
G=H{\mathfrak{I}}^{2}_{A}
,
H
is bounded and
measurable, and
H
restricted to
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{B}
is
continuous because so are
G(\cdot)
and
{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(\cdot)
on this set.

Proof of (2).
Fix

i\neq j\in S
. We claim that there exists a finite constant
C
such
that

(5.7)

|\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})G(x)\,|\leq C\,{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)

for all
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}
.

Rewrite
(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})G
as

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}}G
, so that

(5.8)

|\,(\partial_{x_{i}}-\partial_{x_{j}})G(x)\,|\,\leq\,|\,\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})}G(x)\,|\,+\,|\,\nabla_{\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})-({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})}G(x)\,|

By Lemma
3.1
.(2), (
5.6
), and the definition
(
5.5
) of
{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}
,
the second term is less than or equal to

C\,\sum_{k\in A}|\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}}G(x)\,|\,\leq\,C\,{\mathfrak{I}}_{A}(x)

for some finite constant
C
, which may change from line to line.

We turn to the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.8
). As

x\in\mathring{\Sigma}
, the interior of the line segment between

x
and
\gamma_{B}(x)
is contained in
\mathring{\Sigma}
.

For convenience, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}=\gamma_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})
. By
(
3.9
),
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=0
for
k\in A
. Thus, as
G
vanishes on

\Sigma_{B}
, and
\gamma_{B}(x)\in\mathring{\Sigma}_{B}
,

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}G(\gamma_{B}(x))=0
. By condition

{\mathfrak{E}}_{2}(S)
,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}G(x)|=|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}G(x)-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}G(\gamma_{B}(x))|=\Big|\,\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x-\gamma_{B}(x)}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}G(\gamma_{B}(x)+t(x-\gamma_{B}(x)))\,dt\,\Big|\,.

As
G
satisfies condition
\mathfrak{E2}(S)
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}
,

x-\gamma_{B}(x)
belong to
T_{\Sigma}
, and

\gamma_{B}(x)+t\,(x-\gamma_{B}(x))
to
\mathring{\Sigma}
, by
(
4.8
), there exists a finite constant
C>0
such that

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose we have a
F\in C(\Sigma)
,
V\in C(\Sigma,T_{\Sigma})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
and
\nabla^{\Sigma}F=V
.

(2)

For all

, we have

F(y)-F(x)=\int_{0}^{1}V(x+t(y-x))\cdot(y-x)dt,

where
\cdot
is the standard inner product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Since
F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
, we have
F|_{\mathring{\Sigma}}\in C^{1}(\mathring{\Sigma})
.
Therefore, for
p,q\in\Sigma
, we have

F(q)-F(p)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla F(p+t(q-p))\cdot(q-p)dt,

For arbitrary
x,y\in\Sigma
, we take a sequence
p_{n}\to x
and
q_{n}\to y
with
p_{n},q_{n}\in\mathring{\Sigma}
, the equation

F(q_{n})-F(p_{n})=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla F(p_{n}+t(q_{n}-p_{n}))\cdot(q_{n}-p_{n})dt

converges to the equation

F(y)-F(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla F(x+t(y-x))\cdot(y-x)dt.

Here, we used the uniform continuity of
\nabla F
on
\Sigma
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): Fix
x\in\mathring{\Sigma}
. The equation implies

F(y)-F(x)-(y-x)\cdot V(x)=\int_{0}^{1}(V(x+t(x-y))-V(x))\cdot(x-y)dt.

Using the uniform continuity of
V
, we have

\lim_{y\to x}\frac{F(y)-F(x)-(y-x)\cdot V(x)}{|y-x|}=0.

This implies that
F
is differentiable at
x
and
\nabla F(x)=V(x)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Fix
F\in C^{1}(\Sigma_{B})

\gamma^{*}_{B}F\in C^{1}(\Sigma)
and for any

∎

Recall the definition of

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose

Proof.

Take

For
x\in\Sigma

\displaystyle=|x_{n}-x_{m}|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\frac{x_{n}-x_{m}}{|x_{n}-x_{m}|}}\nabla F((1-t)x_{n}+tx_{m})dt.

for some
