Influence of the oil phase type and volume fraction on the properties of plant-based creams based on pea protein-pectin emulsion gels
Authors: Yu Chen, Qingqing Cao, Pei Wang, Fusheng Chen, Lifen Zhang, Wenjun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPea protein-high methoxyl pectin (PP-HMP) emulsion gels were used to replace 20 % cocoa butter substitute for plant-based cream. This study investigated the effects of oil type and volume fraction on the properties of pre-whipping cream emulsions and the characteristics of a plant-based cream based on PP-HMP emulsion gels. Compared with the control, no significant differences were observed in the properties of the pre-whipping cream emulsion with lower oil volume fractions (20–30 %) of the PP-HMP emulsion gels. Plant-based cream containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20–30 % soybean or peanut oil exhibited comparable overrun, optimal whipping time, adhesiveness, and foam collapse rate to the control. Additionally, the hardness, bubble size, and distribution of whipped cream containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20 % soybean oil were not significantly different from those of the control. The fatty acid composition, density and viscosity of the oils in the PP-HMP emulsion gels were closely associated with the properties of the pre-whipping cream emulsions and the characteristics of the plant-based cream. These findings suggest that PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20–30 % soybean oil are viable alternatives for replacing 20 % hydrogenated oil in plant-based creams, providing a theoretical basis for the application of protein-polysaccharide emulsion gels in plant-based cream alternatives.Keywords: Oil phase, Pea protein, Pectin, Emulsion gel, Plant-based cream

Introduction

Plant-based cream is primarily composed of vegetable fat, emulsifiers, thickeners, proteins, sugar, salt, and water (Yan et al., 2025), and is widely used in the preparation of desserts, cakes, milk tea, and other products (Xu et al., 2025). Traditionally, hydrogenated vegetable oils such as hydrogenated palm kernel oil—partially containing trans fats and rich in saturated fats—have been commonly utilized in plant-based creams (Zhang et al., 2024). However, excessive consumption of saturated fatty acids has been associated with obesity and cardiovascular diseases (Gafour & Aly, 2020). The World Health Organization's recommendations also advise reducing the consumption and use of saturated fatty acid-rich products to avoid potential health risks (Briggs et al., 2017; WHO & Consultation, 2003). To address these concerns, researchers have focused on identifying fat substitutes.

Emulsion gels, which resemble animal fats in appearance and texture while being rich in unsaturated fatty acids, can mimic saturated fatty acids to produce low-fat foods. In recent years, studies have investigated the application of emulsion gels as fat replacers in ice cream, cream, etc. For example, walnut oil-based blueberry pectin-calcium chloride emulsion gels exhibit melting rates similar to those of traditional butter-fat ice cream (Huang et al., 2024). Reports indicate that protein-based emulsion gels exhibit desirable textural properties, favorable rheological behavior, and enhanced storage stability (Li et al., 2025). Pea protein (PP) has been used to prepare emulsion gels and as a saturated fat replacement for its antihypertensive, anti-inflammogenic, cholesterol-lowering, intestinal flora-regulating, and antiallergenic effects (Auer et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2024). However, its poor solubility and weak emulsifying properties limit its application in emulsion gels. High methoxyl pectin (HMP) can improve the emulsion gel properties of proteins (Yu & Zhang, 2025). Researchers reported that emulsion gels with pea protein isolate as a stabilizer and pectin as a cold gelling agent had appropriate physico-chemical and textural properties and could replace pork backfat in beef burgers (Cîrstea et al., 2023). Martin et al. (Martin, Müller and van der Schaaf, 2025) also reported that the addition of pectin enhanced the lubrication in pea protein yogurt alternatives. Therefore, pea protein-high methoxy pectin (PP-HMP) was used in this study to prepare emulsion gels to replace the hydrogenated oil of the plant-based whipped cream.

Emulsion gels can be obtained through physical methods, chemical methods and biological methods. As a biological method, transglutaminase (TG) can catalyze protein cross-linking under mild processing conditions and strengthen the protein network, thereby increasing water retention, heat resistance, and elasticity of emulsion gels (Wei et al., 2025; Kuang et al., 2024). Moreover, the composition and proportion of the oil phase are pivotal in determining the functional properties of emulsion gels. An increasing amount of the oil phase has been shown to enhance both the mechanical strength and water-holding capacity of emulsion gels (Yao et al., 2025). According to Gao et al. (2023), low-oil Pickering emulsion gels demonstrated reduced environmental stability, yet their incorporation as fat replacers in ice cream yielded products with visual and textural qualities comparable to those of conventional products. Adjusting the oil phase—both in type and quantity—offers a strategic means to tailor the microstructure and rheological behavior of emulsion gels (Torres et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2021). These physicochemical properties are especially critical in shaping the performance of emulsion gels within cream (Li et al., 2025).

Fat also plays a critical role in the emulsification and foaming stability of plant-based creams. Before whipping, plant-based cream exists as an O/W emulsion. The fat source, fat globule size, and oil phase volume fraction could affect the stabilization of emulsions (Wang et al., 2025). During the whipping process, partial coalescence of fat plays a crucial role in determining the physico-chemical properties of cream (Mao et al., 2025). The partial coalescence of fat was related to the fatty acid composition, fat crystallization behavior, and oil content. An increase in unsaturated fatty acid can produce a more stable foam in whipping cream (Shamsia et al., 2002). Wu et al. (2023) reported that an increase in the oil content was beneficial to the cream structure. Our previous study revealed that PP-HMP emulsion gels with soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil had various textural properties, water/oil-holding capacities, freeze-thaw stabilities, rheological properties, and microstructures. However, how PP-HMP emulsion gels with different oil type and volume fraction influence the characteristics of plant-based creams is still unclear. Investigating the impact of oils on the properties of plant-based creams is essential for identifying suitable unsaturated vegetable oils that are both health-promoting and functionally effective in emulsion gels.

Above all, the effects of the oil type and volume fraction of the PP-HMP emulsion gels on the properties of the pre-whipping cream emulsions and the characteristics of the plant-based cream were studied. Additionally, this study examined the interfacial protein concentration in pre-whipping cream emulsions, the partial coalescence of fat, and the rheological behavior and microstructure of the plant-based cream to clarify the mechanisms of both stabilization and destabilization in plant-based creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels. The broader aim of this research was to explore the potential of PP-HMP emulsion gels as viable fat replacers and to provide a theoretical basis for the utilization of highly unsaturated vegetable oils in creams.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pea protein (PP, protein content, 80.98 %) was obtained from Yuwang Group (Shandong, China). High methoxyl pectin (HMP, galacturonic acid content, 75.1 %, degree of esterification, 61.0 %) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil were purchased from a local supermarket (Henan, China). Cocoa butter substitute (BL-41) was obtained from Yihai Kerry Southseas Oils & Fats Industry (Chiwan) Co., Ltd. Sodium caseinate, sucrose fatty acid ester (HLB 11), glucose, and other compositions of plant-based cream were all food grade, and were purchased from were obtained from Jiahe Xuri Food Additives Company (Henan, China). All remaining reagents were supplied by Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Oil property determination

The fatty acid composition method was adapted from Xu et al., 2025a, Xu et al., 2025b. The gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 8860, Santa Clara, USA; BXP-70 capillary column, 30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm, SGE Analytical Science, Ringwood, Australia) conditions were as follows: column temperature, 250 °C; flame ionization detector (FID) temperature, 260 °C; and nitrogen carrier gas flow rate, 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: 170 °C for 5 min, then increased to 250 °C at 2 °C/min.

The viscosity of the oils was determined according to Yalcin et al. (Yalcin et al., 2012) with a HR10 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA). The parameters were set as follows: parallel plates diameter, 40 mm; gap, 0.5 mm; temperature, 25 °C; shear rate range, 0.1–200 1/s. The viscosity was calculated as the slope of shear stress vs. shear rate curve. The density of oil was determined according to GB/T 5526-2024 Animal and vegetable fats and oils—Determination of relative density.

Preparation of plant-based cream

Preparation of the PP-HMP emulsion gel

PP (5 g) was dispersed in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred overnight at 25 °C to form a uniform suspension. The solubility of PP was 85.26 %, and its particle size distribution ranged from 100 to 1000 nm. HMP (0.3 g) was then incorporated into the PP suspension and mixed for an additional 4 h. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 6.0 using 1 mol/L HCl, followed by stirring for 1 h to serve as the aqueous phase. Soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil were employed as the oil phase, with volume fractions set at 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, 50 %, and 60 %, respectively. The emulsions were prepared by blending the aqueous and oil phases through high-speed shearing at 13,000 rpm for 2 min. The emulsions were subjected to transglutaminase treatment at 40 °C for 2 h, followed by heating at 85 °C for 30 min. The resulting mixtures were stored at 4 °C overnight to obtain PP-HMP emulsion gels.

Plant-based cream

The composition of the plant-based cream is provided in Table 1. PP-HMP emulsion gels were used to replace 20 % cocoa butter substitute. The preparation process of plant-based cream is outlined in Fig. 1, and the details are as follows: Cocoa butter substitute (16 %, w/w), PP-HMP emulsion gel (4 %, w/w), sodium caseinate (0.50 %, w/w), sucrose fatty acid ester (0.15 %, w/w), span-60 (0.10 %, w/w), xanthan gum (0.12 %, w/w), and guar gum (0.03 %, w/w) were stirred until completely dissolved, and kept at 70 °C, which was the oil phase. Mixtures of glucose (18 %, w/w), maltose syrup (10 %, w/w), disodium hydrogen phosphate (0.06 %, w/w), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (0.04 %, w/w), sodium chloride (0.30 %, w/w), granulated sugar (2.50 %, w/w), and vanillin (0.05 %, w/w) were dissolved in the water to obtain the aqueous phase. Then, the aqueous phase was incrementally added to the oil phase, followed by high-shear mixing at 13,500 rpm for 2 min to produce a preliminary emulsion. This emulsion was further homogenized twice using a high-pressure homogenizer at 50 MPa (APV 2000, SPX Flow, Germany). All samples were immediately cooled and frozen at −18 °C. Prior to whipping, the emulsions were thawed at 4 °C for 12 h. A sample containing 20 % cocoa butter substitute without the addition of PP-HMP emulsion gel was used as the control. Three parallel experiments were conducted for all the samples.

Properties of pre-whipping cream emulsions

Viscosity

The viscosity of the pre-whipping cream emulsion was analyzed according to Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2024) with an HR10 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA). The parameters were set as follows: parallel plates diameter, 40 mm; gap, 0.5 mm; temperature, 25 °C; shear rate range, 0.1–200 1/s. The viscosity data for the emulsions were derived by measuring the shear stress at these different shear rates.

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size and zeta potential of the pre-whipping cream emulsion were analyzed using a nanoparticle size analyzer (BeNano 90, Dandong Bettersize Instruments Ltd., Dandong, Liaoning, China). Prior to measurement, the emulsion was diluted 1:1000 with distilled water. The refractive indices applied for the dispersed phase and the aqueous medium were 1.59 and 1.33, respectively. All analyses were carried out at a constant temperature of 25 °C.

Texture

The plant-based cream samples were placed in aluminum containers measuring 50 mm × 30 mm for texture analysis. The testing parameters were set as follows: P25 probe, trigger force of 1 g, testing speed of 1.0 mm/s, and compression depth of 20 mm (Zhang et al., 2024).

Rheological properties

The fresh plant-based cream was placed onto the bottom plate of the HR10 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA). The diameter of the aluminum plate was 40 mm. A gap of 0.5 mm was set for the oscillation frequency sweep. The strain was determined by a strain sweep. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded with a frequency sweep (1–10 Hz), strain of 1 %, and temperature of 25 °C, and data points were recorded at 5 s intervals during the frequency sweep (Hu et al., 2025).

Optical microstructure

The microstructure of the plant-based cream was examined using an optical microscope (ML31, Mshot, Guangzhou, China) equipped with a 10× objective lens (Ma et al., 2023). The samples were placed on glass slides and covered with cover slips prior to observation.

Statistical analyses

Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. Graphical representations were created using Origin 2021. Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 21.0. Duncan's multiple range test was applied to identify significant differences at a confidence level of P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Oil characterization

The fatty acid compositions of the oils are presented in Table S1. The polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content was the highest in soybean oil (57.01 %), followed by peanut oil (34.81 %) and olive oil (21.05 %). Notably, soybean oil presented a relatively high linoleic acid content and relatively low oleic acid content. The degree of fatty acid saturation in oils plays a critical role in determining their physical properties—such as density, polarity, and viscosity—which in turn influences the adsorption behavior of interfacial proteins and the overall stability of the resulting emulsions (Feng et al., 2023).

The viscosity and density of the oil are critical factors influencing the stabilization of emulsions (Zhang et al., 2024). The density and viscosity of the oils are presented in Table S1. The density values of the oils were as follows: soybean oil > peanut oil > olive oil, while soybean oil had the lowest viscosity. Studies have shown that emulsions prepared with higher-density oils exhibit better stability. Low-density oils tend to induce oil droplet flotation, leading to oil-water separation and reduced emulsion stability (Shi et al., 2022).

Fat crystallization behavior, which is affected by oil properties, greatly affects the partial coalescence of fat and the characteristics of plant-based creams. Moreover, it is well known that the oil phase type and volume fraction are dominant factors in determining the stability of protein emulsions (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), and the emulsifying and foaming properties of proteins are critical determinants of emulsion stability and the characteristics of whipped cream (Xu et al., 2025). Based on the aforementioned findings, variations in fatty acid content, density, and viscosity were observed among the three oils. Therefore, the subsequent sections focused on investigating how the volume fractions of these three oils in the PP-HMP emulsion gels influence the properties of the pre-whipping cream emulsions and the characteristics of the plant-based cream.

Properties of the pre-whipping cream emulsions

Viscosity

The viscosity of emulsions is one of the pivotal roles in their stability and whipping behavior. The effects of the oil phase type and volume fraction of the PP-HMP emulsion gels on the viscosity of the pre-whipping cream emulsions are illustrated in Fig. 2. The viscosity of all the samples tended to decrease with increasing shear rate, which demonstrated shear-thinning behavior. Compared with the control, pre-whipping cream emulsions containing PP-HMP emulsion gels exhibited enhanced viscosity, which further increased with increasing oil phase volume fractions of olive oil. For soybean oil and peanut oil, the viscosity of the 20 % oil phase volume fraction was the lowest. Increasing the oil phase volume fraction increased oil and protein interactions, which contributed to the tightly packing of the oil droplets in the emulsion gels network (Sun & Gunasekaran, 2009; Matsumura et al., 1993). The close packed oil droplets lowering the gravitational separation rate (Loffredi & Alamprese, 2025), thus, the increase in viscosity might be related to the compact networks of emulsion gels with higher oil volume fractions. Moreover, a relatively high viscosity limits the mobility of dispersed droplets, thereby contributing to improved emulsion stability (Wang et al., 2025). The viscosity of pre-whipping cream emulsions containing soybean oil was lower than that of those containing peanut oil, and there was no apparent difference between soybean oil and olive oil. Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2024) reported that the stability of emulsions is related not only to the oil's density and viscosity but also to their fatty acid composition, components, etc. Researchers reported that higher viscosity could inhibit fat coalescence and enhance emulsion stability during storage. However, excessive viscosity could also impede air entry, suppress partial coalescence of fat and affect the stability of foam structures (Wang et al., 2023). Thus, the viscosity of pre-whipping cream emulsions must reach the appropriate level to effectively entrap air to create a foamy texture (Rezvani et al., 2020).

Effect of PP-HMP emulsion gel oil phase type and volume fraction on the viscosity of pre-whipping cream emulsions upon 20 % replacement of the fat phase by an emulsion gel. (A) soybean oil, (B) peanut oil, and (C) olive oil.

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size and zeta potential also affect the stability and whipping performance of emulsified systems (Hu et al., 2025). Smaller emulsion particle sizes correlate with higher pre-whipping cream emulsion stability. Fig. 3 shows the effects of oil phase type and volume fraction of the PP-HMP emulsion gels on the particle size and zeta potential of the pre-whipping cream emulsions. No significant differences in particle size were observed between the control and the samples containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with oil phase volume fractions ranging from 20 % to 30 %. The particle sizes of the pre-whipping cream emulsions decreased first and then increased with increasing oil phase volume fraction for all three oils. The smallest particle sizes were shown with oil phase volume fractions ranging from 40 % to 50 % soybean oil, 30 %–50 % peanut oil, and 40 % olive oil, and no obvious differences were observed among the three oils. Oil droplets not only acted as “active fillers” but also participated in the formation of the gel network as structural units with higher oil phase volume fractions (Ye & Taylor, 2009), which might be more prone to aggregation during the formation of pre-whipping cream emulsion. Meanwhile, Sun et al. reported that less protein was adsorbed at the droplet surface with increasing oil phase volume fractions, thus leading to larger droplets (Sun & Gunasekaran, 2009).

Effect of PP-HMP emulsion gel oil phase type and volume fraction on the average particle size and zeta potential of pre-whipping cream emulsions upon 20 % replacement of the fat phase by an emulsion gel. (A) soybean oil, (B) peanut oil, and (C) olive oil.

Particles with high surface charges could suppress their aggregation and enhance their stability. Thus, the zeta potential values of emulsions are closely related to their stability. As shown in Fig. 3, the variations in the absolute zeta potential values of the pre-whipping cream emulsions mirrored the trends observed in particle size. At higher oil phase volume fractions (≥50 %), pre-whipping cream emulsions with soybean oil resulted in both smaller particle sizes and higher absolute zeta potential values than those with peanut or olive oil. These findings might be related to the higher density and lower viscosity of the soybean oil. Oils of a higher density decreases the buoyancy and motion speed of emulsion droplets (Tan et al., 2021). Moreover, these findings might also be attributed to the higher levels of PUFA in soybean oils. Researchers reported that increasing the degree of unsaturation oil weakened the hydrophobic binding between the interfacial protein and the oil phase, thus impeding the formation of a strong interfacial layer and increasing the emulsion stability (Han et al., 2021).

Interfacial protein concentration

The interfacial protein concentration influences protein adsorption behavior at the O/W interface and regulates the whipping properties of non-dairy cream (Zhao et al., 2013). The effects of the oil phase type and volume fraction of the PP-HMP emulsion gels on the interfacial protein concentration (Γ) in the pre-whipping cream emulsions are shown in Fig. 4. Γ increased with increasing oil phase volume fraction for all three oils in the pre-whipping cream emulsions. When the oil phase volume fraction increased from 20 % to 60 %, the Γ values of the pre-whipping cream emulsions containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil increased from 1.12 mg/m2, 1.13 mg/m2, and 1.20 mg/m2 to 1.32 mg/m2, 1.47 mg/m2, and 1.44 mg/m2, respectively.

Effect of PP-HMP emulsion gel oil phase type and volume fraction on the interfacial protein concentration of pre-whipping cream emulsions upon 20 % replacement of the fat phase by an emulsion gel. (A) soybean oil, (B) peanut oil, and (C) olive oil.

The observed differences in the Γ values of the three oils are likely linked to variations in their unsaturated fatty acid contents. Gu et al. (2009) also highlighted that the degree of unsaturation of fatty acids could influence interfacial protein interactions. The molecular structure of the higher degree of unsaturation oil was more bent, which increased the interfacial area, thus decreasing the interfacial protein concentration (Gomes et al., 2018). Hydrophobic interactions took place between the proteins and the nonpolar end of linolenic acid, and more unfolded proteins with more exposed active groups could penetrate and rearrange faster at the higher degree of unsaturation interface. Emulsion with a relatively high degree of unsaturation oil afforded relatively strong short-term storage stability. However, emulsions with higher degrees of unsaturation oil were less stable in the long-term than those with lower degrees of unsaturation oil. This might be due to bridging flocculation through inter-droplets hydrophobic interaction when proteins are largely unfolded to expose more hydrophobic residues at the higher degree of unsaturation interface (Zhang et al., 2025). Above all, the relatively higher degree of unsaturation of soybean oil imparted to rapid stabilization but long-term instability of its emulsions, which made it suitable for use in plant-based creams.

Whipping characteristics of the plant-based cream

Partial coalescence of fat during whipping

During whipping, the merger of fat droplets at the surface of the air bubbles facilitated the incorporation of air into the plant-based cream and supported the texture of the plant-based cream (Wang et al., 2022). Fig. 5 shows the effects of the oil phase type and volume fraction in the PP-HMP emulsion gels on the partial coalescence of fat during cream whipping. Compared with that of the control, the partial coalescence of fat decreased with increasing oil phase volume fraction. The maximum values of partial coalescence of fat were 55.44 %, 52.88 %, and 41.71 % for soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil, respectively, with an oil phase volume fraction of 20 %. The rate and extent of partial coalescence are also related to the solid-to-liquid fat ratio, which affects the amount and strength of fat destabilization (Liu et al., 2021; Sung & Goff, 2010). At lower oil phase volume fractions, the Γ in the pre-whipping cream emulsions was lower. A smaller protein load led to lower steric stabilization of the droplet, which renders it more prone to partial coalescence (Sung & Goff, 2010). Therefore, greater partial coalescence with lower oil phase volume fractions might be due to the synergistic effects of Γ and the solid-to-liquid fat ratio.

Effect of PP-HMP emulsion gel oil phase type and volume fraction on the partial coalescence of fat of plant-based cream upon 20 % replacement of the fat phase by an emulsion gel.

Notably, the partial coalescence of fat in creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with low volume fractions of soybean oil and peanut oil was markedly greater than that of the control (48.16 %). Oil crystallization affects the partial coalescence of fat (Gao et al., 2023), and fatty acid composition is one of the primary factors governing the partial coalescence of fat. The greater partial coalescence of fat in the soybean oil might be related to its fatty acid composition characteristics. Liu et al. reported that oils containing relatively low melting fractions (such as oleic acid) led to the lowest solid fat content at low temperatures, thus resulting in the formation of a weak crystal network and decreasing partial coalescence of fat (Liu et al., 2021). In addition, oils with a lower degree of unsaturation strengthened the viscoelastic interfacial film of the emulsion, and decreased the occurrence of partial coalescence (Zhang et al., 2024). Han et al. also reported that a high degree of unsaturation deceased the binding ability of interfacial protein membranes and that fat particles coalesced (Han et al., 2021). Thus, soybean oil resulted in greater partial coalescence of fat.

Whipping time, overrun, and foam collapse rate

Whipping time and overrun are key indicators used to evaluate the whipping characteristics of plant-based cream, whereas the foam collapse rate reflects cream stability by indicating the system's ability to preserve the bubble structure. The optimal whipping time, overrun, and foam collapse rate are summarized in Table 2. As the oil phase volume fraction increased, the overrun of plant-based creams prepared with soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil decreased from 279 % to 247 %, 292 %–217 %, and 271 %–227 %, respectively, accompanied by a notable increase in the optimal whipping time. The emulsion gel network structure becomes more compact as the oil phase volume fraction increases (Lin et al., 2020). This might be related to the formation of a thicker viscoelastic film around fat globules, which prevents partial coalescence of fat and decreases the degree of whipping (Rezvani et al., 2020). Moreover, the lowest overrun of whipping cream with olive oil might be attributed to its higher viscosity, which hindered air entrainment and foam formation (Huang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Compared with the control, no significant differences were detected in the overrun of plant-based creams with soybean oil (20–50 %), peanut oil (20–50 %), and olive oil (20 %).

Textural properties

The effects of the oil phase type and volume fraction of PP-HMP emulsion gels on the texture properties of whipped creams are shown in Table 2. The hardness of the plant-based cream decreased from 47.06 g to 25.68 g (soybean oil), 39.02 g–17.15 g (peanut oil), and 33.68 g–14.50 g (olive oil) with increasing oil phase volume fractions. The adhesiveness and hardness exhibited parallel trends. Compared with those with peanut or olive oil, plant-based cream containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with soybean oil presented the highest values for both hardness and adhesiveness. The hardness of the plant-based cream containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20 % soybean oil did not significantly differ from that of the control cream, whereas the hardness of the other samples decreased. This decrease could be attributed to greater protein distribution at the O/W interface, which facilitated the formation of uniform yet loosely bound networks with fat globules. Such structural arrangements hinder extensive fat coalescence, thereby altering the network architecture of the plant-based cream and diminishing its overall hardness and adhesiveness (Cao et al., 2020; Zhan et al., 2025). Researchers also reported that the decrease in hardness ascribed to insufficient fat crystals and the lack of partial drop interface coalescence during the whipping process (Han et al., 2023).

Rheological properties

The mouthfeel, texture, and spread ability of cream are closely related to its viscoelasticity. Fig. 6 shows the effects of the oil phase type and volume fraction in PP-HMP emulsion gels on the viscoelastic properties of the plant-based cream. The storage modulus (G′) surpassed the loss modulus (G″) in the considered frequency range for the all samples, and all the samples displayed gel-like characteristics. The plant-based creams presented lower G′ and G″ values, suggesting a reduction in strength with increasing oil phase volume fraction. This was consistent with the hardness results of the whipped creams. Increasing the oil phase volume fraction reduced the partial coalescence of fat, thus attenuating the network structure fat globules in the whipped cream. These findings collectively indicated that emulsions containing partially coalesced fat droplets formed more structurally stable networks than those with unbound oil droplets (Chen et al., 2025). Across all the samples, the G″ curves exhibited negative slopes, reflecting weak viscous characteristics. At the same oil phase volume fractions, the G′ values decreased in the following order: soybean oil > peanut oil > olive oil. Elevated G′ values were indicative of greater structural integrity and stability in the plant-based cream. The increased G′ observed in the plant-based cream with soybean oil could be attributed to increased partial coalescence of fat and the formation of a denser network.

Microstructure

The microstructure, melting behavior at ambient temperature, and appearance were analyzed to assess the stability of the plant-based cream (Fig. 7). The control sample exhibited smaller and more uniformly distributed bubbles. The stability of plant-based creams strongly depends on the air bubble size (Chen et al., 2025). Han et al. (Han et al., 2023) reported that large bubbles have less resistance against internal and external pressure differentials than small bubbles do, thus leading to foam disproportionation. As the oil phase volume fraction increased across all three oils, the air bubbles in the plant-based cream became noticeably larger, with a more uneven and disorganized distribution of bubble diameters. The samples containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20 % soybean oil presented bubble sizes and uniformities that were most comparable to those of the control, followed by those made with peanut oil and olive oil.

Effect of PP-HMP emulsion gel oil phase type and volume fraction on the microstructure of plant-based cream upon 20 % replacement of the fat phase by an emulsion gel.

Decoration appearance and shape retention are important for the use of cream in food products. All the samples formed stable decorative shapes with clear ridges and peaks. All the samples collapsed at the top after 2 h of storage, whereas the collapse of the control and lower oil phase volume fraction samples (soybean oil ≤50 %, peanut oil≤ 30 %, and olive oil 20 %) was slight. Moreover, the bottom exhibited slight moisture, and the surface was soft for peanut oil, olive oil, and high volume fractions of soybean oil (≥50 %). These results might be related to the higher oleic acid content and weak fat agglomeration network of the oil (Liu et al., 2021).

Stabilization and destabilization mechanisms of plant-based creams

The characteristics of pre-whipping cream emulsions with soybean oil, peanut oil, and olive oil have the same variation tendency with increasing oil phase volume fractions, as shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4. Taking soybean oil as an example, Fig. 8 shows a diagram of the stabilization and destabilization mechanisms of plant-based creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with different soybean oil volume fractions.

Schematic diagram of the stabilization and destabilization mechanisms of plant-based creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with different volume fractions of soybean oil.

For lower oil phase volume fractions (20–30 %), the solid-to-liquid fat ratio was greater, cocoa butter substitute was more likely to dominate the foaming process, while the emulsion gel mainly plays a regulatory role. Moreover, the oil droplets formed a thin interfacial membrane and provided a more accessible approach for the ice crystals to puncture the interfacial film during the freezing and thawing process. Therefore, greater partial coalescence of fat facilitated the entrapment of air bubbles during whipping, contributing to the formation of higher-quality plant-based cream as a control. At higher oil phase volume fractions (40–50 %), both decreasing the solid-to-liquid fat ratio and the formation of a thicker interfacial membrane inhibited the penetration of ice crystals, which adversely affected the quality of the plant-based cream. Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2022) similarly reported that the ability of ice crystals to penetrate the interfacial film was influenced by its thickness, which in turn played a key role in determining the freeze-thaw stability of emulsions. Meanwhile, the flow resistance and the interfacial protein concentration of oil droplets increased, which enhanced the stability of the pre-whipping cream emulsions. The formation of a stable emulsion corresponded to a difficulty in whipping emulsion with good foaming stability (Fu et al., 2020). The pre-whipping cream emulsion became unstable with further increasing in soybean oil volume fraction (60 %). This might be related to the incomplete coverage of droplet surface that leaded to the coalescence of oil droplets. Meanwhile, the increasing in liquid oils decreased the solid fat content, which played a crucial role in the partial coalescence behavior among fat globules (Sung & Goff, 2010). The fatty acid composition, viscosity, and density of the oils are strongly associated with the stability of pre-whipping emulsions and the behavior of fat crystals (Zhang et al., 2025), all of which directly influence partial coalescence of fat and ultimately affect the quality of the plant-based cream.

Conclusion

The properties of pre-whipping cream emulsions in which 20 % cocoa butter substitute was replaced with PP-HMP emulsion gels with different oil phase type and volume fraction were studied. The results revealed that the viscosity, and interfacial protein concentration increased with increasing oil phase volume fraction. The lowest particle sizes were observed with 40 %–50 % soybean oil, 30 %–50 % peanut oil, and 40 % olive oil. There were no notable differences in the pre-whipping emulsion properties between the control and the PP-HMP emulsion gel samples with lower oil phase volume fractions (20–30 %). The performance of plant-based creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20–30 % soybean or peanut oil was similar to that of the control in terms of overrun, optimal whipping time, foam collapse rate, and adhesiveness. Similarly, the hardness, bubble size, and distribution of the plant-based creams containing PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20 % soybean oil did not significantly differ from those of the control. These results highlight the influence of oil fatty acid composition, viscosity, and density on the stability and whipping behavior of pre-whipping emulsions. PP-HMP emulsion gels with 20–30 % soybean oil present a viable alternative to hydrogenated oils in plant-based creams, offering a pathway toward healthier products rich in unsaturated fats and plant-based proteins. Future investigations should aim to further enhance the textural properties, sensory attributes, and nutritional profiles of such reformulated cream products.
