The variable stiffness orthosis: customizable mechanics for assistance and rehabilitation
Authors: Nikko Van Crey, David Lam, Emily Bywater, Max Shepherd, Elliott Rouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChallenges with community mobility are among the most prevalent disabilities worldwide, yet the passive orthoses available for daily use have remained largely unchanged for centuries. In contrast, the powered orthoses developed by researchers have advanced rapidly, but without a matching rate of user impact. To date, there are no powered ankle-foot orthoses available for daily use, stemming from challenges with reliability, safety, weight, noise, and cost. This work aims to explore the untapped potential of passive mechanisms, as a pathway that could more rapidly translate improved functionality to the people who rely on orthoses. To this end, we developed an orthosis with passive and quasi-passive mechanisms that render capabilities typically attributed to powered devices, blurring the line between powered and passive functionality.MethodsIn this work, we present the Variable Stiffness Orthosis, an ankle-foot orthosis that strikes a balance between powered and passive orthoses in terms of functionality and daily-use practicality. The Variable Stiffness Orthosis has torque-angle relationships that can be customized with a cam-based transmission, interchanged between gait phases with a cam-switching mechanism, and softened or stiffened between activities with a motorized spring support. These mechanisms enable precise control over the torque-angle relationship, including continuously variable stiffness, decoupled energy storage and return, step-to-step adjustment of stiffness magnitude, exchanging energy between gait phases, changing equilibrium angle between gait phases, negative stiffness, and extreme stiffness. These capabilities were validated on a rotary dynamometer and pilot tested on participants with and without sciatic nerve injury.ResultsDynamometer testing verified the capabilities of the VSO. In pilot testing, the participants had activity-dependent stiffness preferences spanning a large range. Using the VSO, the participant with sciatic nerve injury had reduced foot drop, increased total ankle moments, reduced biological ankle moments, reduced toe striking, and reduced steppage on their AFO side compared to walking with and without their daily-use device.ConclusionsThis work demonstrated that the passive and quasi-passive mechanisms within the VSO can replicate many powered functions, extending the capabilities of unpowered devices. The VSO also showed promise as a daily-use device, a clinical tool for orthotic prescription, and a research tool for investigating unexplored passive mechanics.

Background

Mobility impairments, including difficulty walking and climbing stairs, are among the most prevalent disabilities worldwide. In the United States alone, these challenges affect millions of people [1] and greatly impact physical and mental aspects related to quality of life [2]. These difficulties are often attributed to dysfunction at the ankle joint, which plays an important role in balance and propulsion, providing the majority of the mechanical energy added during walking [3, 4]. Without proper ankle function it can be difficult to support body weight during terminal stance [5], propel the body forward during push off [5], and lift the foot to maintain ground clearance during swing phase (i.e. foot drop) [6, 7]. To address these deficits, people with ankle impairment often rely on ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs); however, both the established standard-of-care devices and research prototypes fall short of fully addressing user needs due to limited customization, lack of real-time adaptability for different activities, and practical barriers that hinder translation.

The current standard-of-care AFOs have remained largely unchanged for centuries [8–10]. AFOs are typically classified as passive, using mechanical elements (springs, dampers, etc.) to store or dissipate energy, or powered, using actuators (typically electric motors and batteries) to add energy during the gait cycle [11–14]. A few powered AFOs are commercially available for rehabilitation or research use, but none are available for daily use, stemming from challenges with reliability, safety, weight, noise, and cost. Consequently, passive AFOs — commonly known as passive-dynamic AFOs (PD-AFOs) — are more prevalent. PD-AFOs are typically lightweight structures intended to support body weight during stance phase, return elastic energy to facilitate forward propulsion at push off, and resist excessive plantarflexion to prevent ground contact during swing phase. PD-AFOs achieve these functions by acting as spring elements in parallel with the ankle (akin to the Achilles tendon), providing stiffness resisting dorsiflexion to compensate for weakness in the plantarflexors (e.g. triceps surae) and stiffness resisting plantarflexion to compensate for weakness in the dorsiflexors (e.g. tibialis anterior). Previous work has demonstrated that PD-AFOs can provide mobility benefits to individuals with hemiparesis [15–18], cerebral palsy [19–21], spinal-cord injury [22], multiple sclerosis [16, 23], and sciatic/peroneal nerve injury [24]. When compared to shoes-only conditions, PD-AFOs can increase self-selected walking speed [16, 25–27], increase push-off power [5], decrease gait compensations [5], decrease metabolic rate [16, 28–30], and reduce foot drop [16–18, 24] in impaired populations. Although PD-AFOs have demonstrated benefits over shoes-only conditions for level-ground walking, they can be a burden during other activities [31, 32] and fall short of restoring able-bodied gait patterns [16, 29, 33].

Existing PD-AFOs are unable to fully restore able-bodied gait patterns because they cannot behave like the able-bodied ankle joint, which provides net-positive power and adjusts stiffness (and quasi-stiffness) within and across activities of daily living [34–39]. Since it is difficult to change or customize the stiffness of PD-AFOs, they conventionally have a single, fixed stiffness (linear torque-angle relationship) [40–46] that is tailored for level-ground walking. This stiffness is less suitable for other activities (ramps, stairs, etc.), cannot be easily adjusted for different stages of rehabilitation, and is a compromise of several competing factors. For example, PD-AFOs intended to support body weight or combat spasticity are typically stiff for the entire range of motion (RoM), which decreases ankle mobility [29, 33, 47]. Decreased ankle mobility inhibits forward progression of the body over the foot from heel strike to terminal stance and reduces plantarflexion at push off (e.g. similar to wearing a ski boot). Over time, decreased ankle RoM can lead to abnormal shortening of tendons and muscles (contracture), which can cause pain, increase joint stiffness, and sometimes require corrective treatment [48, 49]. PD-AFOs also decrease ankle velocity at push off [29] and have poor energy efficiency (∼ 60% [50, 51]), which reduces push-off power compared to able-bodied gait [16, 29, 33, 52]. This reduced push-off power can increase the dissipative collision losses of the leading leg at heel strike [53], increase metabolic expenditure [54–57], and redistribute mechanical power generation from the ankle to less efficient proximal joints [57]. PD-AFOs often have a plantarflexed equilibrium angle (position with zero torque) [29, 58] for standing stability, but this increases functional leg length during swing phase [7] and requires gait compensations (such as hip hiking [59, 60], vaulting, steppage, leg circumduction [60], etc.) to avoid the unbalance and steep metabolic penalty of unexpected ground contact during mid-swing [61]. The limitations of PD-AFOs create an undesirable trade-off between the gait impairments imposed by the user’s condition and those associated with use of the AFO itself.

Addressing the limitations of the PD-AFOs available for daily use will require orthoses with more adjustable and customizable mechanical behavior — an area where powered devices have shown potential, but where passive alternatives remain underexplored. Several commercially-available devices and research prototypes feature adjustable stiffness magnitude, but further work is needed to tailor the stiffness for different individual needs, rehabilitation stages, gait phases, and activities. The Nexgear Tango (Ottobock, Duderstadt, DE), Neuro Swing (Fior & Gentz, Lüneburg, DE), Triple Action (Becker, Troy, MI, USA), and Ultraflex AFO Joint (Ultraflex Systems, Pottstown, PA, USA) are articulated AFOs that permit independent adjustment of the stiffness magnitude in dorsiflexion relative to plantarflexion. These devices, however, can only be adjusted via benchtop reassembly with different compression springs, making them impractical to adjust between activities. There are several research prototypes that allow step-to-step adjustment of overall stiffness magnitude [62–66], but most of these prototypes have substantial mass [63, 64] or a narrow range of stiffness adjustment [64–66]. Despite these advancements, little work has explored the ability to customize stiffness within a gait cycle or across different gait phases. To date, only one known orthosis can passively customize the shape of its torque-angle (or stiffness-angle) relationship within a gait cycle [62] and none can passively customize the mechanics for different gait phases. These capabilities are crucial, as orthotists must currently trade off between gait interventions with competing stiffness requirements. In this work, we present the Variable Stiffness Orthosis (VSO), a quasi-passive AFO that strikes a balance between powered and passive AFOs in terms of functionality and daily-use practicality. The design is based on the Variable-Stiffness Prosthetic Ankle-Foot developed by Shepherd et al. [67] and the cam-switching strategy developed by Quraishi et al. [68]. The VSO has torque-angle relationships that can be customized with a cam-based transmission, interchanged between gait phases with a cam-switching mechanism, and softened or stiffened between activities with a motorized spring support. These mechanisms enable precise control over the torque-angle relationship, including continuously variable stiffness, decoupled energy storage and return, step-to-step adjustment of stiffness magnitude, exchanging energy between gait phases, changing equilibrium angle between gait phases, negative stiffness, and extreme stiffness greater than constituent springs within the design. In this work, we introduce the VSO design (Section 2) and torque-angle relationships (Section 3), characterize its mechanical performance (Section 4), and perform pilot testing on participants with and without sciatic nerve injury walking over level ground, ramps, and stairs (Sections 5, 6). Details of a preliminary design were also included in the proceedings of the 2023 IEEE International Conference on Rehabilitation Robotics [62].

Variable stiffness orthosis

Design overview

The Variable Stiffness Orthosis (VSO) is a quasi-passive AFO with capabilities that are typically not possible for unpowered orthoses, striking a balance between the functionality of powered orthoses and the daily-use practicality of passive orthoses (Figure 1A – B, Supplementary Video 1). The VSO includes three variable-stiffness mechanisms: a passive cam-based transmission (Section 2.2), a passive cam-switching mechanism (Section 2.2.2), and a quasi-passive motorized spring support (Section 2.3). These mechanisms allow the torque-angle relationship to be customized in several ways. The shape of the torque-angle relationship encodes an angle-dependent variable stiffness, which is mechanically programmable via the cam-based transmission. Regions of the torque-angle relationship can vary with gait phase by interchanging cams with magnetic forces at desired gait events via the cam-switching mechanism. The torque-angle relationship can also be softened or stiffened between activities by using a small electric motor to drive a lead screw, which relocates the support position beneath a simply-supported cantilever leaf spring. Stiffness adjustments occur when the leaf spring is unloaded (i.e. during swing phase) which enables the use of a small motor and battery. These mechanisms enable precise control over the torque-angle relationship, including continuously variable stiffness, decoupled energy storage and return, step-to-step adjustment of stiffness magnitude, exchanging energy between gait phases, changing equilibrium angle between gait phases, negative stiffness, and extreme stiffness.

The VSO resides on the lateral aspect of the shank, weighs 987 g (Figure 1C), and interfaces with the body via an adjustable shank attachment and interchangeable aluminum-reinforced shoes. The custom shoes weigh ∼ 370 g (EU33.5: 320 g, EU44: 420 g), which includes ∼ 135 g of 7075-T6 aluminum reinforcement. Reinforcement was integrated directly into the shoes to minimize backlash and improve mechanical coupling between the orthosis and footwear; however, the VSO remains compatible with standard carbon fiber insoles, provided they offer matched attachment stiffness. Cam-based transmission

The VSO includes a cam-based transmission that enables customizable, nonlinear torque-angle relationships with continuously variable stiffness. During gait, external moments — primarily from body-weight-induced ground reaction forces — rotate a cam with the ankle, deflecting a simply-supported cantilever leaf spring via interaction with a cam follower (FPL412, Nadella, Milan, IT) at the free end of the leaf spring (Supplementary Video 2). Deflecting the leaf spring provides a restorative torque at the ankle joint, which can support body weight during stance phase, contribute to ankle push-off power, and lift the foot during swing phase. The shape of the cam determines the path traveled by the cam follower and resulting spring deflection, enabling precise control over the torque-angle relationship. The VSO is compatible with two cam-based modules that either load and unload the leaf spring along the same cam (single-cam module) or along two distinct cams with different torque-angle relationships, which decouples energy storage and return (DESR). These modules have nearly identical mass, with the single-cam and DESR modules weighing ∼ 26 g and ∼ 38 g, respectively.

The cams are machined from D2 tool steel, hardened to 60 Rockwell C, and designed to achieve a desired torque-angle relationship (primary relationship) at a specified spring-support position (primary-support position). The shape of the torque-angle relationship can be nonlinear, where the instantaneous stiffness of the passive system is equivalent to the slope of its torque-angle relationship [70]. This nonlinearity allows the cam profile to encode an angle-dependent, continuously variable stiffness. Both modules can be quickly interchanged for testing any torque-angle relationship within the tested peak torque of 80 Nm and RoM of ±40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}, with minimal constraints on the passive mechanics (Appendix A.4).

Single-cam module

The simplest implementation of the cam-based transmission comprises a single cam profile that encodes a customizable torque-angle relationship with continuously variable stiffness. This functionality allows the torque-angle relationship to be tailored to user-specific needs while minimally impacting reliability, safety, weight, noise, and cost compared to the current standard of care. With the single-cam module, the torque-angle relationship is identical in loading and unloading because the cam follower retraces the same geometry. The torque-angle relationship can be distinct in each loading direction, however, enabling different mechanics between plantarflexion and dorsiflexion. Decoupled energy storage and return module

A module was developed that extends the capabilities of the single-cam module by enabling a region of the torque-angle relationship to vary between gait phases, including changing equilibrium angle (position with zero torque) and exchanging energy between gait phases. This module is based on the cam-switching strategy in Quraishi et al. [68] and decouples energy storage and return by loading and unloading the leaf spring along two distinct cams, each encoding a different torque-angle relationship (Figure 2). The DESR module is a completely passive mechanism that switches between cams at predetermined ankle angles based on the alignment of small (4 mm diameter) passive permanent neodymium iron boron (NdFeB) magnets (D0505, K&J Magnetics Inc., Pipersville, PA, USA).

Switching between cams is accomplished by dividing the cam profile into dynamic and static parts, which correspond to regions of the torque-angle relationship. The dynamic cams correspond to interchangeable regions of the torque-angle relationship that are phase dependent with the cam-switching mechanism, whereas the static cam corresponds to regions of the torque-angle relationship that remain constant (Figure 2A – C). The cam follower can only load one region of the cam profile at a time, so when the surface of the static cam is loaded by the cam follower, the dynamic cams are unloaded, allowing magnetic forces to slide them from one side of the chassis to the other (Figure 2D, Supplementary Video 3). This movement switches which dynamic cam is positioned along the path of the cam follower. Magnets are housed in both the dynamic cams and interchangeable fixtures on the chassis, allowing the ankle angle at which switching occurs to be adjusted for different DESR modules. The switching action takes ∼ 12.5 ms and occurs ∼ 0.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document} after the cam follower transitions onto the static cam to ensure the dynamic cams are unloaded at the switching angles. At each switching angle, the magnet polarities are configured to pull the dynamic cams to either the medial or lateral side of the chassis. As a result, the dynamic cams do not switch again when the ankle passes back through the same switching angle in the reverse direction, as the magnets will continue to pull the dynamic cams toward the side they already occupy. In normal operation, switching occurs twice per gait cycle, such that both dynamic cams are traversed each gait cycle. If the angle required for switching is not reached, normal operation resumes when the switch is successful in the next gait cycle. The design also incorporates 20 mT Hall effect sensors (DRV5056A1QLPG, Texas Instruments, Dallas, TX, USA) to detect switching actions, and foam to make switching actions inaudible.

The cam-switching mechanism enables functionality beyond the single-cam module. Each dynamic cam encodes a torque-angle relationship with different equilibrium angles, so switching between cams allows the equilibrium angle to change between gait phases. Additionally, the energy stored by loading along one dynamic cam may not be fully released after unloading along the other, resulting in energy harvested as preload in the leaf spring and orthotic structure — components under load in series with the torque-generating element (spring, motor, etc.), including the chassis and human attachments. This places one of the dynamic cams in a higher-preload state, with the leaf spring deflected at the equilibrium position. The harvested energy can be discharged (i.e. recycled) at another gait phase by switching back to the dynamic cam with a lower-energy equilibrium. The specific gait phases involved in this energy exchange depend on the shape of the torque-angle relationships (as they influence kinematics) and the position of the magnets (as they influence where switching occurs), whereas the directionality of the energy exchange — whether energy is harvested or recycled — is governed by the reversible polarity of the permanent magnets.

Depending on the specific gait phases involved and the directionality of the energy exchange, the ability to harvest and recycle energy can support a variety of functions. These include, but are not limited to: 1) harvesting energy from early stance to supplement push off, 2) harvesting energy from terminal stance to pull the user forward during tibial progression of the next gait cycle, and 3) harvesting energy from terminal stance to actively dorsiflex the foot for ground clearance during swing.

The ability to interchange ankle mechanics can support a variety of additional functions. These include, but are not limited to: 1) differing the rates of energy storage and return in late stance, 2) changing equilibrium angle for energy storage, foot clearance, and standing stability, and 3) decreasing stiffness resisting plantarflexion to avoid hindering push off, while simultaneously increasing it during swing phase to prevent foot drop.

In general, there are many possible implementations of the DESR module to support different biomechanical objectives, with the primary limitation being that the torque-angle relationships of each dynamic cam are coupled. This limitation stems from the DESR module being a passive system, requiring the energy harvested to equal the energy recycled. Furthermore, the cam-switching mechanism requires the torque-angle relationships to converge before the switching angles.

Negative and extreme stiffness

The cam-based modules in the VSO can utilize preload energy to render mechanics that are typically reserved for powered systems, including negative stiffness and extreme stiffness greater than constituent springs within the design. Mathematical descriptions of negative and extreme stiffness are provided in Appendices B.1, B.2, and the underlying principles are outlined here.

Compressing a standard torsional spring generates a restoring moment that increases with spring displacement (positive stiffness), following a torque-angle relationship with positive slope. However, passive systems can also generate moments that decrease with displacement or even drive motion in the direction of displacement, such that the torque-angle relationship features negative slope (negative stiffness). Moments can decrease with displacement when the force line of action approaches the center of rotation — governed by the concavity of the cam profile — reducing the lever arm of the spring force. Under these conditions, the moments decrease without energy release and the energy is retained as preload. Moments can drive displacement by drawing on preload energy, whether present at equilibrium or retained from previously traversed torque-angle mechanics. In this way, the intelligent use of preload energy in the leaf spring and orthotic structure can function as a mechanical analog to the batteries in powered systems.Fig. 3Simplified model demonstrating negative stiffness. An example that illustrates the fundamental principles contributing to negative stiffness is a cam with a sinusoidal torque-angle relationship (Figure 3). This cam has stable equilibria at the valleys and unstable equilibria at the summits. The cam follower starts at a stable equilibrium (Figure 3, left). As the cam rotates clockwise, resistance increases with deflection until the first peak of the sinusoid. Afterwards, resistance decreases with continued deflection until reaching an unstable equilibrium at the summit (Figure 3, middle). At the summit, there is preload energy from traversing the first half of the sinusoid, but there is zero torque because the line of action goes through the center of rotation. This equilibrium is unstable and perturbation will result in torque acting towards one of the stable equilibria on either side. If the cam continues rotating in the same direction (clockwise), it heads towards a new stable equilibrium (Figure 3, right), and torque will reverse to act in the same direction as cam rotation. This example is energy conservative and illustrates how a passive cam-spring mechanism can utilize preload energy to exhibit torque-angle relationships with negative stiffness.

Moreover, while series springs with positive stiffness will have an overall stiffness that is softer than any spring individually, combining elements with positive and negative stiffness makes it possible to render extreme stiffness greater than constituent springs within the design [72, 73]. In the context of the VSO, the orthotic structure serves as a positive stiffness element and the cam-spring mechanism can serve as either. If the cam-spring mechanism renders negative stiffness, extreme stiffness is achievable because the orthotic hinge and structure deflect in opposite directions, such that torque changes substantially with minimal change in total deflection. Extreme stiffness leverages the property that the total angular deflection of a hinged orthosis (Figure 10A – B) is the sum of its rotation at the orthotic hinge and its assembly deflection (combined angular displacement from structural deflection and axis misalignment).

Extreme stiffness is more intuitive in the context of powered devices. For example, if a powered AFO is mounted to a rigid statue such that the ankle is fixed, commanding torque will lead to infinite stiffness. This occurs because the motor drives the orthotic hinge in one direction, whereas the orthotic assembly will deflect equally in the opposite direction, causing torque to change without a change in total deflection.

The examples in this section and Appendices B.1, B.2 are intended to demonstrate the underlying principles of negative and extreme stiffness, and not to serve as practical implementations. These capabilities are useful because they reduce constraints on the torque-angle relationships, allowing more customizable mechanics. Negative and extreme stiffness also allow the VSO to partially emulate the powered and isometric functions of the biological ankle joint.

Negative and extreme stiffness are possible with both the single-cam and DESR modules, since both allow the strategic use of preload energy. The DESR module simply augments this capability by allowing differing mechanics in loading and unloading, reducing the kinematic constraints on the energy exchange.

Leaf spring and motorized support

The shape of the torque-angle relationship is governed by the cam-based modules, but the relationship can be softened or stiffened for different activities with a motorized spring support. A small DC motor (DCX16L, Maxon Motors, CHE) with a 6.6:1 planetary transmission moves the spring support along a custom lead screw (Acme thread, 1.27 mm lead, 6.35 mm major diameter, 13.67:1). The spring support and chassis both have sliding plates constructed from TC4 titanium and coated with graphite dry lubricant. The chassis is constructed from 7075-T6 aluminum and designed with manufacturing tolerances that leave a shimmable gap (∼ 100 microns) between the sliding plates. A steel shim is sandwiched between the spring support and its sliding plate to eliminate backlash with minimal spring preload. The VSO is quasi-passive since the motor only adjusts the effective length of the leaf spring (to change stiffness) without providing net-positive mechanical work to the body. Stiffness adjustments are made when the transmission is unloaded during swing phase, and the lead screw is non-backdrivable, so the motor does not provide a holding torque. The controller uses feedback from the joint encoder to avoid commanding current when the transmission is loaded. The spring forces that caused the motor to stall were characterized across the range of spring-support travel, allowing the kinematics over which the motor can reposition to be calculated for each cam module based on model-predicted loads.

The leaf spring is constructed from TC4 titanium and designed to have a large range of available stiffness. Torque-angle relationships

Section 2 outlined how the torque-angle relationship can be customized with the cam-based transmission, interchanged between gait phases with the DESR module, and softened or stiffened between activities with the motorized spring support. These mechanisms enable a level of control over the torque-angle relationship that is not possible with other unpowered orthoses; consequently, this work serves as a starting point to study the clinical outcomes of unexplored passive mechanics. The first iteration of the VSO [62] was presented with a concave-up torque-angle relationship that mimicked the stance phase of able-bodied gait, which is largely passive prior to push off [34]. The ideal passive mechanics in parallel with an intact joint may not be biomimetic, however, and may depend on the type of mobility impairment or user-specific functional needs.

The cam profiles govern the shape of the torque-angle relationship and are quickly interchangeable, allowing many torque-angle relationships to be tested with the same VSO. In this way, the cam-based modules serve a similar role to the controllers in powered devices, providing a mechanical means of reprogramming orthosis behavior. In this work, we tested six single-cam modules and two DESR modules. These modules can encode any torque-angle relationship within the tested peak torque of 80 Nm and RoM of ±40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}, with minimal constraints (Appendix A.4). All the modules in this work were designed with the primary-support position at 50% of spring-support travel to minimize distortion of the torque-angle shape at other spring-support positions. The average stiffness of the primary relationships was chosen to span the softest to stiffest commercially available AFOs across the range of spring-support travel.

The torque-angle relationships were constructed with piecewise cubic spline interpolation for all single-cam modules, and non-uniform rational basis splines (NURBS) [74] for all DESR modules. Points along a piecewise cubic spline can be defined explicitly, which simplifies manual construction of single-cam modules. NURBS are more suitable for the DESR modules, however, as they allow fine control of endpoint tangents, which must be identical where the dynamic and static cams meet.

Rationale pertaining to all modules

The torque-angle relationships of the single-cam and DESR modules were designed based on mechanical limits and biomechanical objectives, including body weight support in stance, propulsion at push off, foot clearance in swing, and stability while standing. The torque-angle relationships were softened past the peak ankle angle observed in the kinematics of users with [16, 24] and without [37–39] locomotor impairment across many activities of daily living (±25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}) to prevent high internal loads in atypical operation. The torque-angle relationships were also designed with a stiffness in plantarflexion intended to resist foot drop and foot slap, without hindering tibial progression during mid-stance or reducing stability with prolonged heel-only contact. Throughout this work, positive angles indicate dorsiflexion and negative angles indicate plantarflexion.

Single-cam torque-angle relationships

Six versions of the single-cam module were designed to showcase the ability to customize the shape of the torque-angle (or stiffness-angle) relationship (Figure 4A). The equilibrium angles of the single-cam modules are shown at neutral angle, but the equilibrium angle experienced by the user can be placed anywhere within the RoM limits of the VSO via adjustment of the shank attachment.

The concave-down (light blue), linear (green), and concave-up (purple) modules were designed to demonstrate torque-angle relationships with different concavity (Figure 4A). These modules have the same torque-angle relationship in plantarflexion but have torque-angle relationships of different concavity in dorsiflexion, with the same average stiffness at +25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}.

The zero module (orange) was designed to demonstrate strategic placement of zero stiffness (Figure 4A). The zero module has the same torque-angle relationship in dorsiflexion as the concave-down module but with zero stiffness in plantarflexion, such that it does not reduce plantarflexion at push off. The zero module is intended for testing with participants that do not require stiffness opposing plantarflexion and to facilitate comparisons between the VSO and passive ankle exoskeletons that only provide unidirectional stiffness in dorsiflexion [77–79].

The small-wave (pink) and large-wave (brown) modules were designed to demonstrate highly nonlinear torque-angle relationships with negative and extreme stiffness (Figure 4A). The small- and large-wave modules have the same stiffness in plantarflexion and a similar shape in dorsiflexion. The primary difference between their torque-angle relationships is the amplitude of the trough, which remains above the x-axis for the small-wave module and dips below the x-axis for the large-wave module, resulting in positive energy at an unstable equilibrium (similar to Figure 3, middle). The large-wave module was also designed to produce extreme stiffness exceeding that of the VSO assembly. At the stiffest spring-support positions, the large-wave module is theoretically capable of infinite device stiffness, as the torque-angle relationship becomes vertical as it transitions between extreme negative and extreme positive stiffness (similar to Figure 10G).

DESR torque-angle relationships

Two versions of the DESR module were designed to showcase the ability to have regions of the torque-angle relationship vary with gait phase. These modules were designed to demonstrate a subset of the DESR module’s capabilities — including supplementing push off with energy recycled from early stance (energy module) and changing equilibrium angle to simultaneously promote swing-phase foot clearance and standing stability (angle module). The torque-angle relationships of the DESR modules were designed using a custom optimization framework since they are coupled and subject to energetic constraints (Appendix A.4), which make manual construction difficult. The torque-angle relationships (Figure 4B) were optimized at the primary-support position (50% spring-support travel) using a genetic algorithm approach [80] outlined in Bywater et al. [81]. The parameters and bounds used in the optimization are listed in Appendix D.

The switching angles were chosen during controlled plantarflexion (i.e. early stance, heel strike to foot flat, a → b in Figure 2) and controlled dorsiflexion (foot flat to push off, b → d in Figure 2). To avoid premature switching during push off, the first switching angle was set between the peak plantarflexion observed during early stance and the peak plantarflexion observed during push off. Peak plantarflexion typically occurs during push off [37–39] (Figure 2B, light gray), but the stiffness of the VSO can oppose the plantarflexors and reduce plantarflexion at push off. At high VSO stiffness, peak plantarflexion can instead occur during early stance (Figure 2B, colored), when moments generated by body weight may be sufficient to overcome the stiffness of the orthosis. In contrast, during push off and swing phase, body-weight-induced moments are small and loading the VSO past equilibrium is only possible via the weight of the foot and shoe, spasticity, or voluntary effort from the plantarflexors. Since peak dorsiflexion typically occurs in controlled dorsiflexion — with and without the VSO — the second switching angle was set near the peak dorsiflexion reliably reached during stance phase [37–39] (Figure 2B, light gray).

In terms of the gait events shown in Figure 2, the energy and angle modules were designed to operate such that energy is stored from heel strike to foot flat (a → b), partially released from foot flat to mid-stance (b → c), and then recycled to augment push off (e). This progression is shown in Figure 2B, with colors indicating which region of the torque-angle relationship is active. The energy module was optimized to maximize energy exchange (area of regions 1 and 2). The angle module was optimized to maximize swing-phase dorsiflexion on the blue dynamic cam while maintaining a plantarflexed equilibrium for stable standing on the red dynamic cam.

There are several clinical use cases for the DESR modules outside of the strategies presented for the energy and angle modules. Other PD-AFOs force orthotists to trade off between gait interventions with competing stiffness requirements. For example, people with spasticity are often prescribed AFOs with high stiffness near neutral ankle angle to prevent foot drop during swing phase, at the expense of reduced plantarflexion at push off. A DESR module having one cam with high stiffness near neutral angle during swing and another with low stiffness at push off could mitigate this trade-off. The torque-angle relationships for this module would be similar to the energy module, except the polarity of the magnets in the chassis would be reversed, causing switching to occur at push off instead of during early stance. Reversing the polarity causes the cam follower to traverse the red dynamic cam during push off, which requires less energy to reach the plantarflexed switching angle than the blue dynamic cam, thereby allowing switching to occur at push off. The direction of energy recycling would also be reversed, harvesting energy from controlled dorsiflexion and recycling it to raise the foot for swing clearance after push off. This module may also be beneficial for users who toe strike instead of heel strike, as their peak plantarflexion is more likely to be at push off rather than during early stance.

Benchtop characterization

Stiffness reporting and literature comparisons

The methods commonly used to report the stiffness of exoskeletons and orthoses are often inaccurate because they overlook major sources of angular displacement and assume that the torque at the orthotic joint acts about the same axis as the ankle, motivating this work to use different methods. This section is dedicated to clarifying inconsistencies in existing methods and establishing guidelines for accurate stiffness measurement and reporting.

The testing of hinged devices typically reports stiffness with methods that exclude the angular displacement and torque transfer inefficiency that result from structural deflection and misalignment of the orthotic joint axis. These methods often involve rigid modeling [64, 65, 77–79] or estimating stiffness from on-board joint encoders and load cells [66], but they greatly overestimate peak stiffness (Table 1, [82]). Joint encoders only measure the rotation about the orthotic hinge (hinge angle) and exclude the additional rotation from deflection of the orthotic structure — components under load in series with the torque-generating element (spring, motor, etc.), including the chassis and attachments. Structural deflection also causes relative displacement between the orthotic joint axis and the ankle axis (or dynamometer axis), causing the angles about each axis to diverge. This misalignment causes torque transfer inefficiency, as only a component of the torque about the orthotic joint acts about the ankle-sagittal axis. This results in the load cells on devices overpredicting the applied torque, whereas the load cell on a rotary dynamometer measures the torque about the dynamometer axis (acting as a proxy for the ankle-sagittal axis) and more accurately captures the component of orthotic torque that would be applied to the user. In this work, we will refer to the torque transmitted to the sagittal (or dynamometer) axis as applied torque and the combined angular displacement from structural deflection and axis misalignment as assembly deflection/stiffness according to equations (1) and (2). At peak stiffness, assembly deflection is often most of the angular displacement experienced by the user, so neglecting it generates stiffness estimates that are biased and difficult to faithfully reproduce. Without knowledge of device-specific assembly stiffness, it is typically not possible to convert reported stiffness metrics into a regime that allows device-to-device comparisons or accurate interpretation of study results. For these reasons, the stiffness of the VSO is reported as device stiffness calculated from applied torque and device angle about the sagittal (or dynamometer) axis. Device angle includes the combined contributions of rigid angle (hinge angle under rigid assumptions) and assembly deflection to the overall angular displacement (equation (3), Figure 10A – B). Device stiffness should be used for device-to-device stiffness comparisons because it captures the device-specific mechanics prior to the inter-user variability introduced by tissue deflection.

The stiffness metrics reported in the ankle exoskeleton/orthosis literature were compiled in Table 1 and converted to device stiffness to allow device-to-device comparisons. This analysis encompassed all known quasi-passive AFOs [63–66, 83], several notable research-based passive AFOs [77–79], several commercial articulated AFOs (Nexgear Tango, Neuro Swing, Triple Action, and [46]), and a large set of commercial solid PD-AFOs [69]. The stiffness range for the solid PD-AFOs was determined by averaging the stiffness measurements of multiple methods [40, 58, 84, 85] because existing methods do not corroborate each other. For example, the four dynamometers compared by Shuman et al. [69] had an average relative percent difference (range/mean) of 39%, ranging from 13 – 89% depending on the AFO tested, which exceeds the 12% change in stiffness that people can reliably perceive in seated postures [86] .

To make comparisons between devices possible, the reported stiffness metrics were converted to device stiffness with two methods. First, stiffness estimated from rigid models or joint encoder measurements were placed in series with the assembly stiffness measured for the VSO (5.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document}) according to equation (4). Second, device stiffness measurements from testing without shoes were decreased by 12% according to literature that measured the impact of shoes on the stiffness of PD-AFOs [51], which closely matches the 11% reduction measured in our testing. The assembly stiffness of the VSO was used because the assembly stiffness of other devices was not reported. Although the hinge stiffness estimated from the on-board joint encoder and torque sensor in Hopkins et al. [66] includes the component of assembly stiffness associated with the transmission stiffness between hinge rotation and torque generation at the orthotic joint, the transmission stiffness measured for the VSO was very stiff (45.6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document}) and had little impact on the overall assembly stiffness. The hinge stiffness reported in Hopkins et al. Dynamometer testing

Dynamometer validation and protocol

The Neurobionics Lab Rotary Dynamometer (Figure 5) was used to experimentally verify the torque-angle relationships of the VSO. The custom dynamometer includes a frame mounted motor (BSM90N-3150AF, Baldor, Fort Smith, AR, USA) with a 2500 ppr incremental encoder, and a 6-axis load cell (45E15A M63J, JR3, Inc., Woodland, CA, USA) that measures forces and moments from a rotating platform. As differences in methodology impact the reproducibility of dynamometer measurements [69], we validated our dynamometer with multiple methods not reported for the other dynamometers. To ensure the torque measurements were accurate, we validated them with known torques applied to the rotating platform. To ensure deflection of the dynamometer did not affect the accuracy of stiffness measurements, we characterized the combined rotary stiffness of the dynamometer structure and load cell (676.8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document}) using finite element analysis and the reported stiffness of the load cell (9424.8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document}). The overall stiffness of the dynamometer guarantees a measurement error less than 0.9% for any stiffness less than 6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document}. We deemed this error negligible, as it is over an order of magnitude less than the stiffness differences perceivable by users in seated postures [86].Fig. 5The VSO mounted in the Neurobionics Lab Rotary Dynamometer. The VSO mounted in the Neurobionics Lab Rotary Dynamometer. The dynamometer measures torque-angle relationships with respect to applied torque and device angle. Measurements of applied torque capture the torque transfer inefficiency between the torque about the orthotic joint axis and the torque measured about the dynamometer axis (proxy for ankle-sagittal axis). The VSO was rigidly mounted to the dynamometer at the shank attachment, and the shoe was secured to the platform with clamps that applied a downward force to emulate body weight. The orthotic joint axis was aligned with the dynamometer’s rotational axis using a concentric laser. Eleven spring-support positions were tested, starting at the softest configuration (0% spring-support travel) and increasing in ten percent intervals until reaching the stiffest configuration (100% spring-support travel). At each spring-support position, the dynamometer rotated the platform in both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion with a mean angular velocity of 10 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {deg}}{\text {s}}$$\end{document} to minimize inertial effects and increase the sample density. Device angle was measured by the dynamometer’s encoder at 1 kHz, orthotic hinge angle was measured by the VSO’s on-board joint encoder (AS5048b, ams AG, Premstatten, AUT) at 300 Hz, and applied torque was measured by the dynamometer’s load cell at 1 kHz. The inertial and gravitational torques were measured in a test without orthotic torque (no leaf spring) and subtracted from the torque data. The torque data was additionally filtered with three tenth-order notch filters and a second-order Butterworth filter with a 12 Hz cutoff frequency. These filters reduced high frequency noise and were informed by fast Fourier transform analysis. Efficiency was calculated by dividing the energy stored during loading by the energy released during unloading. To ensure the measurements were minimally impacted by changes in clamping, speed, and inertia, additional tests were conducted at different clamping forces and at slower and faster speeds. Model accuracy is reported as a normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), normalized with the peak torque magnitude measured for each torque-angle relationship. Standard deviations are reported to show the variance in model accuracy and energy efficiency between modules and across spring-support positions.

The torque-angle relationships are reported with respect to device angle, but empirical mappings between orthotic hinge angle and torque were also determined for each module. Mappings between hinge angle and torque isolate the cam-spring mechanism and are accurate with any attachments, whereas mappings between device angle and torque are attachment dependent. The mapping from hinge angle to torque was used to estimate the applied torque during walking experiments, and was validated with dynamometer tests including different attachments. A mapping from hinge angle to device angle was used to estimate device angle during the stairs experiments.

The assembly stiffness of the VSO was additionally characterized with a cam that locked leaf spring deflection, preventing rotation at the orthotic hinge joint. The cam was machined to slide over the cam follower with a diametral clearance of ∼ 60 microns. Locking the leaf spring in this way effectively converts the VSO into a solid PD-AFO because the VSO can only produce torque and angular displacement through assembly deflection. The assembly stiffness measured in this test was corroborated by the other dynamometer tests, where assembly stiffness (excluding transmission stiffness) was estimated by dividing applied torque by the difference between the hinge and device angle measurements.

The measured torque-angle relationships were compared against model predictions for the single-cam (Figure 6A – D) and DESR modules (Figure 6E – F). The model accuracy had low variance between modules with an average NRMSE of 3.66 ± 1.44% for the single-cam modules and 4.80 ± 2.04% for the DESR modules. The energy efficiency had low variance between modules with an average energy efficiency of 91.0 ± 2.2% for the single-cam modules and 91.6 ± 3.3% for the DESR modules, with each module demonstrating 1.5 times the ∼ 60% efficiency exhibited by articulated AFOs [50, 51]. The model accuracy and energy efficiency for each module also had low variance across spring-support positions (Figure 6A, 6F). The VSO was capable of rendering device stiffness from -52.4 to 5.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document} across modules, and the stiffness of the linear module could vary by a factor of 7.2 between the softest and stiffest spring-support positions.

The single-cam modules demonstrated that the cam-based transmission allows the shape of the torque-angle (or stiffness-angle) relationship to be customized. The concave-down, linear, and concave-up modules demonstrated that the VSO can produce torque-angle relationships that differ in shape but achieve the same average stiffness (Figure 6C). The small- and large-wave modules demonstrated that the VSO can produce highly nonlinear torque-angle relationships with negative and extreme stiffness. Measurements from the encoders on-board the dynamometer and VSO confirmed that the orthotic structure and hinge can deflect in opposite directions, enabling extreme stiffness. The peak negative stiffness of -52.4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document} measured for the large-wave module was greater in magnitude than the stiffness of 5.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\text {Nm}}{\text {deg}}$$\end{document} measured for the VSO assembly (Figure 6D). However, this extreme stiffness is likely underestimated — particularly at stiffer spring-support positions — where the modeled torque-angle relationships include narrow regions with infinite and extreme positive stiffness that were difficult to test with the dynamometer. The dynamometer enforces a device angle trajectory, which forced the device angle to increase when it might have otherwise remained constant or decreased to render infinite or extreme positive stiffness. Measuring these properties may require a different dynamometer setup.

The energy and angle modules demonstrated that the cam-switching mechanism allows regions of the torque-angle relationship to vary with gait phase. These modules also demonstrated that the VSO can exchange energy and change equilibrium angle between gait phases. The recycled energy and swing angles of the measured torque-angle relationships were compared against model predictions for both modules (Figure 6F). The energy module exchanged up to 2.02 J between gait phases, and the energy recycled in both modules exceeded the energy lost to hysteresis for all spring-support positions. These results validate the mathematics used to generate the cam profiles and to predict torque-angle relationships at spring-support positions other than the primary-support position (Appendix A).

Methods: participant testing

Participants

One female (age: 28, height: 1.60 m, weight: 55 kg, condition duration: three years, daily-use AFO: Ottobock WalkOn Flex weighing 236 g) with sciatic nerve injury (SNI) and one able-bodied male (age: 28, height: 1.81 m, weight: 87 kg) without SNI performed several activities of daily living with the VSO outfitted unilaterally on the right leg. This work was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board, and the participants gave informed, written consent.

Acclimation

Before data collection, the participants had ∼ 110 min of training with the VSO, in 1 – 2 min walking bouts spanning three days. During training, the participants could freely change the step-to-step stiffness of the VSO with a rotary dial while walking over level-ground, ramps, and stairs. For each activity, the participants self-selected a walking speed that was comfortable and safe for both their daily-use AFO and the full stiffness range of the VSO. This training included all modules, with the most time spent on the concave-down module.

Measurement and filtering

Level-ground, decline (-8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}), and incline (+8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document}) walking kinematics were measured at 250 Hz with a 26-camera motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd, Oxford, UK), and kinetics were measured at 1 kHz with a split-belt instrumented treadmill (Bertec, Columbus, OH, USA). The participants were outfitted with the lower-body Plug-in Gait marker set [88], with additional markers on the greater trochanters, medial ankles, and medial knees. Motion capture kinematics and ground reaction forces were filtered with 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\text {th}}$$\end{document}-order Butterworth low-pass filters with 6 Hz [89] and 20 Hz cutoff frequencies, respectively. A modified version of the Vicon Plug-in Gait model was used to calculate ankle angles and total ankle moments. The modified version locates the knee and ankle axes with the lateral and medial joint markers, rather than the thigh and shank markers. All markers were placed directly on the participants, rather than on the orthosis, to avoid the kinematic and kinetic errors caused by relative movement between the orthosis and the body. The mass of the shoes and orthosis was manually allocated to the foot and shank segments to prevent the Plug-in Gait model from distributing their mass across the entire body. The ankle angle of participants was aligned with the equilibrium angle of each torque-angle relationship by adjusting the shank attachment according to feedback from the on-board joint encoder, motion capture, and visual inspection. Rotation at the inversion/eversion joint and shank pad were locked to the standing posture of each participant to provide mediolateral ankle support during walking. The position of the dynamic cams was measured with the Hall effect sensors at 300 Hz.

Stair descent and ascent were performed on a 6-flight staircase (79 steps total) with 180 mm risers and brief level-ground transitions between flights. For stair trials, the orthotic hinge kinematics were measured with the on-board joint encoder at 300 Hz and filtered using a low-pass filter with a 6 Hz cutoff frequency. Measurements from on-board inertial measurement units (3DMCX5-AR, MicroStrain, VT, USA), sampled at 200 Hz, were used for gait segmentation, but these sensors are not required in typical operation. Steady-state data excluded the initial and final three steps of each flight. For all activities, the torque applied by the VSO was estimated with an empirical mapping (hinge angle → torque, Section 4.2.1), and device angle was estimated with an empirical mapping (hinge angle → device angle, Section 4.2.1). The dynamometer measurements already account for the imperfect torque transfer between the VSO and the person, so biological ankle moments were calculated by subtracting the applied torque from the total ankle torque measured with motion capture and the treadmill.

Baseline

Prior to testing with the VSO, baseline data was collected for 2 min at self-selected speeds across level-ground, decline, and incline walking. Baseline data was collected for both participants in shoes-only conditions, the participant with SNI wearing their daily-use AFO, and the participant without SNI wearing the VSO with no spring (no stiffness). For stair descent and ascent, videos were recorded of the participant with SNI walking with their daily-use AFO as baseline data, but the shoes-only condition was not conducted because they indicated that stairs would be difficult without an AFO. The participant with SNI also performed baseline RoM testing, volitionally moving both ankles to their maximum comfortable dorsiflexion and plantarflexion angles when seated with their foot off the ground. The participant with SNI wore their own shoes for daily-use AFO and shoes-only conditions, and the participant without SNI wore an unreinforced pair of the same shoes used with the VSO.

Protocol

Participants were outfitted with the VSO and completed three phases of testing for each activity: 1) an initial exploratory period of two minutes, during which they could freely explore the full stiffness range of the VSO with a rotary dial, 2) three walking bouts, where they were seeded at the softest spring-support position and could explore the full stiffness range in search of their preferred stiffness, and 3) one minute walking bouts at the softest, preferred, and stiffest spring-support positions. Participants were required to rest for at least 1 min between walking bouts but could request more rest time as needed. The average kinematics and kinetics from phase three are reported in Section 6.

The three phases of testing were completed with the concave-down module for all activities and at multiple speeds for level-ground walking. The linear, concave-up, zero, energy, and angle modules were tested with one or both of the participants for only level-ground walking at their self-selected speed. This testing protocol was chosen to explore the dimensions of activity, stiffness, speed, and torque-angle shape, while also enabling at least one module (concave down) to be tested across all dimensions and both participants.

Discussion

In this work, we presented the design of the Variable Stiffness Orthosis (VSO), a quasi-passive ankle-foot orthosis that balances the functionality of powered AFOs with the daily-use viability of passive AFOs common in the standard of care. Numerous torque-angle relationships were customized with a cam-based transmission and validated using a rotary dynamometer. The VSO was then tested over level ground, ramps, and stairs on participants with and without sciatic nerve injury (SNI).

Dynamometer testing demonstrated that the VSO has several capabilities typically not possible for passive orthoses, while operating at 150% of the energy efficiency of articulated AFOs [50, 51]. These capabilities include: 1) customizable, nonlinear torque-angle relationships with continuously variable stiffness, 2) decoupled energy storage and return, 3) step-to-step adjustment of ankle stiffness spanning the softest to stiffest commercially available AFOs [69], 4) exchanging energy between gait phases, 5) changing equilibrium angle between gait phases, 6) negative stiffness, and 7) extreme stiffness greater than constituent springs within the design. Many of these capabilities are attributed to powered devices, showcasing the potential for versatile passive mechanisms to blur the line between powered and passive functionality.

The VSO showed promise in participant testing, revealing activity-dependent stiffness preferences and clinically-relevant biomechanical outcomes. Using the VSO, the participant with SNI had reduced foot drop, increased total ankle moments, reduced biological ankle moments, reduced toe striking, and reduced steppage on their AFO side compared to daily-use AFO and shoes-only conditions. These results are promising because the participant with SNI is at the lower end of the weight range for which the VSO was designed, meaning they experienced benefits despite increased relative device mass compared to heavier participants. Both participants had different stiffness preferences across activities; thus, prescribing an AFO with a fixed stiffness can necessitate a compromise that is undesirable to the user. Notably, the participant with SNI commented that their stiffness preference tended to be softer than their daily-use AFO, suggesting that their stiffness preference may differ from that of their orthotist. However, the torque-angle relationship of the participant’s daily-use orthosis (Ottobock WalkOn Flex) is unknown, limiting direct comparison between the mechanics of the two devices. The stiffness preferences of the participant with SNI also appeared related to their ankle kinematics and total ankle moments, as they were nearly identical at preferred stiffness for modules of the same type (single cam versus DESR). The maximum stiffness preferred by both participants was 260–800% stiffer than their minimum preferred stiffness — far exceeding the 12% change in stiffness that people can reliably perceive in seated postures [86]. This large range of stiffness preferences provides evidence that there is a clinical need for orthoses with variable stiffness.

The absence of standardized stiffness reporting makes it difficult to compare devices and interpret the results of studies [82], motivating this work to clarify inconsistencies in existing methods and establish guidelines for accurate stiffness measurement and reporting. Table 1 shows that existing methods greatly overestimate the peak stiffness of the VSO, highlighting the need to report device stiffness (Section 4.1) for accurate device-to-device comparisons. All known quasi-passive AFOs are reported in Table 1, showing that the VSO likely has the largest stiffness range and specific stiffness. Despite the mass of the mechatronics, the specific stiffness of the VSO was also on the upper end of commercially available PD-AFOs, suggesting that it may be possible to scale the peak stiffness of the VSO up or down to be mass competitive with the current standard of care.

Despite the broad capabilities of the VSO, there are still kinematic and energetic constraints that limit functionality when compared to powered devices. For example, the DESR modules must reach the switching angles to function, but increasing stiffness reduces ankle movement and can eventually prevent switching. Placing the switching angles closer together can mitigate this issue, but doing so limits the capabilities of the DESR module, necessitating a careful balance. A separate device demonstrated DESR functionality with reduced kinematic constraints by utilizing a quasi-passive mechanism with computer-controlled solenoids to clutch in and out the energy stored in passive springs [83]. However, unlike the DESR module, it could not precisely customize its torque-angle relationship and required over 618 g of hardware and electronics — rivaling the bulk of many powered devices — to replicate functionality that our cam-switching mechanism accomplishes with 38 g of passive components. Another limitation of the VSO compared to powered devices is the lack of a supplemental energy reservoir (i.e. battery). This limitation requires recycled energy to be harvested from gait and prevents rendering negative and extreme stiffness without constraints, coupling these capabilities to previously traversed torque-angle mechanics.

This work highlights the untapped potential of passive mechanisms to expand the functionality of daily-use orthoses. The VSO demonstrates that passive and quasi-passive mechanisms can replicate capabilities typically reserved for powered devices, offering an alternative strategy to translate improved functionality to the individuals that rely on orthoses. The cam-based transmission and moving spring support can be implemented independently and without motors or electronics, enabling spin-off devices that could advance the orthoses available for daily use, with minimal increases in reliability, safety, weight, noise, and cost. The VSO could also serve as a research tool for studying the effects of stiffness customization on mobility, or as a clinical tool for orthotic emulation and prescription.
