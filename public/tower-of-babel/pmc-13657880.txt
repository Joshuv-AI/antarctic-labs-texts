A T-cell exhaustion-related signature for predicting response to immunotherapy among patients with lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Chengcheng Yang, Zefeng Wang, Jintao Xie, Huanhuan Zhao, Tianqi Song, Shiqi Luo, Xinyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, and although immunotherapy has improved outcomes for a proportion of patients, reliable predictive biomarkers of response are lacking. T-cell exhaustion-associated transcriptional states are increasingly recognized as relevant to antitumor immunity, yet their prognostic and predictive associations in LUAD remain incompletely defined. The aim of this study was to evaluate transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion and to construct a prognostic gene signature for stratifying patients with LUAD and estimating potential benefit from immunotherapy.MethodsTen transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion were identified from the literature. Using LUAD transcriptomic data, we evaluated their prognostic relevance and identified four factors associated with overall survival (OS). Based on these, patients were divided into high- and low-risk exhaustion groups, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were derived. Univariate Cox regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox analysis were applied to screen prognosis-related DEGs, through which a six-gene T-cell exhaustion-related prognostic score (TEXRGPS) was constructed. The expression of these six genes was validated via reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) in 20 pairs of LUAD and adjacent normal tissues from our hospital. Associations of TEXRGPS with clinical characteristics, immune cell infiltration, and predicted response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) were also assessed.ResultsFour of the ten transcription factors were associated with prognosis and were used to stratify patients. Six key DEGs—CCL19, CD79A, CHRDL1, MS4A1, S100P, and SFTPC—were selected to construct the TEXRGPS. RT-qPCR confirmed differential expression between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. A high TEXRGPS was associated with adverse clinical features and poor prognosis, whereas a low TEXRGPS was associated with prolonged survival. The TEXRGPS was negatively correlated with the estimated infiltration of multiple immune cell types, including CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells. Low-risk patients had higher computationally predicted sensitivity to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapies than high-risk patients. These retrospective associations require prospective validation before clinical application.ConclusionsThe TEXRGPS was associated with prognosis, computational estimates of immune infiltration, and predicted immunotherapy response in retrospective LUAD cohorts. The signature may support future risk-stratification research, but it does not establish a direct functional relationship between the selected genes and exhausted T cells. Prospective multicenter validation, together with single-cell, protein-level, and functional studies, is required before clinical use.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We constructed a six-gene T-cell exhaustion-related prognostic score (TEXRGPS) for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) using The Cancer Genome Atlas and validated it in six Gene Expression Omnibus cohorts. High TEXRGPS was associated with male sex, advanced stage, nodal metastasis, and worse overall survival. Low TEXRGPS correlated with stronger infiltration of CD8+ T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells; higher expression of PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3, and CTLA-4; and elevated immunophenotype scores. Analysis of the IMvigor210 cohort confirmed that low TEXRGPS predicted greater sensitivity to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy.

What is known and what is new?

• T-cell exhaustion impairs antitumour immunity, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) benefit only a subset of LUAD patients. Transcription factors (IRF4, BATF, NFATC1, TOX) regulate exhaustion, but their utility for patient stratification is unclear.

• This study is the first to integrate four prognosisassociated transcription factors (IRF4, NFATC1, EOMES, TOX) into TEXRGPS—a quantitative tool for stratifying patients and predicting immunotherapy response, with rigorous external validation across multiple datasets.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• TEXRGPS offers a practical tool for personalised ICI treatment. Low-risk patients may be prioritised for immunotherapy, whereas high-risk patients may require combination regimens or agents targeting component genes (e.g., S100P). Prospective trials are essential to validate its clinical utility and to explore therapeutic targeting of these genes to reinvigorate antitumour immunity in non-responders.

Introduction

Lung cancer is broadly categorized into two primary histological subtypes: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). In 2022, lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and continues to be the predominant driver of cancer-associated mortality, accounting for approximately 18.7% of all cancer-related deaths (1). Although advances in therapy have markedly extended both the progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of patients with lung cancer, this disease remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. Critically, most cases of advanced NSCLC ultimately develop resistance to treatment regimens—particularly immunotherapy and targeted agents—leading to disease progression (2). Recent research indicates that reversing T-cell exhaustion may amplify the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy. For instance, Zhou et al. reported that N-acetylcysteine impedes the differentiation of T cells into a terminally exhausted state, thereby potentiating the response to PD-1 blockade in colorectal cancer model (3). Similarly, Cheng et al. identified ETV7 as a promoter of T-cell exhaustion that accelerates tumorigenesis (4). Furthermore, Zhu et al. demonstrated that KLF2 plays a key role in preserving T-cell effector capacity while suppressing exhaustion, supporting it as a novel target for immunotherapeutic intervention (5). Consequently, mitigating T-cell exhaustion to restore functional effector activity is being recognized as a priority for optimizing outcomes among patients treated with immunotherapy.

In 1998, a state of T-cell suppression was first identified in a mouse model of chronic lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection (6), and this immunological process is now known as T-cell exhaustion. When under chronic infection and antitumor immunity, T cells are exposed to persistent antigenic stimulation which leads to the progressive loss of effector functions, the expression of multiple inhibitory receptors, dysregulated metabolism, poor memory recall response, and homeostatic proliferation (7). A number of mechanisms can cause T-cell exhaustion, and several studies have suggested that the transcription factor network exerts significant regulatory functions in this regard. For example, Martinez et al. demonstrated that NFAT controls CD8+ T-cell exhaustion by binding directly to regulatory regions of many exhaustion-associated genes, including the Pdcd1 (PD-1) and Havcr2 (TIM3) promoters and cis-regulatory regions (8). Another study found that T-cell exhaustion during chronic infection is driven by a high abundance of T-cell receptor-induced transcription factors IRF4, BATF, and NFATc1, which promote the expression of PD-1 and mediate impaired cellular metabolism (9). Moreover, it was reported that NFAT can induce TOX, TOX2, and NR4A transcription factor family proteins, all of which upregulate the expression of inhibitory receptors, with a positive feedback regulation existing between NR4A and TOX (10). Other research demonstrated that BATF can directly bind to regulatory regions near Tbx21 and Klf2, modulating their enhancer accessibility to facilitate the development of CD8+ T cells during chronic viral infection (11). One study found that other transcription factors, such as T-bet, can directly compete with eomesodermin at gene promoters, such as Pdcd1, to promote T-cell homing and activation during PD-1 blockade (12). These findings suggest that the regulation exerted by transcription factors can be applied clinically and clarify, to some extent, the nature of the transcription factor network; however, further research is necessary to refine the use of these transcription factors and guide the next generation of treatment.

Few studies have investigated transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion in LUAD, particularly their associations with the immune microenvironment and treatment response. We therefore evaluated 10 transcription factors reported in the literature (8-10,12-16)—IRF4, BATF, NFATC1, TCF7, NR4A1, NR4A2, NR4A3, TBX21, EOMES, and TOX—and examined their prognostic and immune-microenvironment associations in bulk LUAD transcriptomic datasets. We further developed a prognostic score and evaluated its association with computational estimates of immunotherapy response. Because these analyses are associative, they do not establish a direct exhausted-T-cell phenotype or causal function for the selected transcription factors in LUAD. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-1101/rc).

Methods

Collection of data on patients with LUAD

Transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion were selected from the relevant literature. The Cancer Genome Atlas LUAD (TCGA-LUAD) dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) served as the training cohort; transcriptomic data reported as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads, clinical data, somatic mutation data, and proteomic data were used. Six datasets with follow-up information (GSE72094, GSE31210, GSE68465, GSE26939, GSE42127, and GSE37745) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and analyzed as independent validation cohorts rather than pooled into a single expression matrix. Cohort-specific gene identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols, and overlapping genes were retained for cross-cohort evaluation. GSE43458, GSE32863, GSE75037, and GSE40791, which contained adjacent non-tumor and LUAD tissues, were used to evaluate expression of the signature genes. For GEO datasets, the data were downloaded as Series Matrix Files. These datasets were generated based on the Expression profiling by array platform. Unnormalized datasets were normalized using log2(X+1) transformation, while already normalized datasets were used without further processing.

Identification of T-cell exhaustion-related transcription factors

First, we used the R package RCircos to display the genomic locations of transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion. A correlation network was then generated using the training-set expression data. Univariate Cox regression was used to evaluate associations between transcription-factor expression and prognosis, and forest plots were generated using the reshape2 (v1.4.4), survival (v3.2-13), and survminer (v0.4.9) R packages. These analyses assess statistical association and do not demonstrate cell-specific function or causality.

T-cell subpopulation and exhaustion analysis

T cells were extracted from the integrated scRNA-seq dataset and subjected to secondary clustering using Seurat. After normalization, identification of highly variable genes, scaling, and PCA, the first 20 principal components were used for UMAP visualization and unsupervised clustering. T-cell subsets were annotated based on differentially expressed genes and canonical markers. Exhausted T cells (Tex) were subsequently extracted based on the expression of established exhaustion markers. The expression and co-expression patterns of exhaustion-related transcription factors (IRF4, NFATC1, EOMES, and TOX) and canonical exhaustion markers (PDCD1, CTLA-4, LAG3, and HAVCR2) were evaluated, and their associations were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis.

Consensus clustering analysis

By using the ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.54.0) and scatterplot3d (v0.3-41) R packages, we performed a consensus clustering analysis of the training group based on four transcription factor genes that regulate T-cell exhaustion and that were associated with prognosis. From this, we determined the optimal number of types and drew principal component analysis (PCA) maps. Next, a heatmap with clinical features was drawn via the pheatmap R package. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were used to analyze the differences in OS, PFS, and disease-specific survival (DSS) of patients with high and low T-cell exhaustion via the survival and survminer R packages.

Analysis of the TME

First, we obtained the immune score, stromal score, ESTIMATE score, and tumor purity score of the patients with LUAD in the training set using the ESTIMATE algorithm. The ESTIMATE score was calculated as the sum of the immune score and stromal score. To analyze the degree of immune cell infiltration in LUAD tumor tissues, seven algorithms from the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) database were used: TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCPCONT, XCELL, and EPIC (http://timer.cistrome.org) (17-22). The estimate (v1.0.13), limma (v3.46.0), ggpubr (v0.4.0), reshape2, pheatmap (v1.0.12), scales (v1.4.0), ggplot2 (v3.3.5), ggtext (v0.1.2), and tidyverse (v1.3.1) R packages were used for these analyses.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the different risk groups of T-cell exhaustion and functional enrichment analysis

Using |log fold change (FC)| >1 and false-discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 as filter conditions, we identified DEGs between different clusters of T-cell exhaustion and visualized them in a volcano plot using the limma R package. Next, the clusterProfiler (v4.0.5), org.Hs.eg.db (v3.13.0), enrichplot (v1.10.2), and ggplot2 R packages were used to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses, with P<0.05 and FDR <0.05 being the selection criteria.

Construction of a prognostic risk score associated with T-cell exhaustion

To identify DEGs reproducibly observed across cohorts, we intersected cohort-specific gene lists without merging the six GEO expression matrices and visualized the overlap with an UpSet plot, yielding 19 common DEGs. Univariate Cox regression was then used to evaluate their associations with prognosis. Subsequently, LASSO Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation (nfolds =10, family = “cox”, standardize = TRUE) was used to establish the T-cell exhaustion-related prognostic score (TEXRGPS), with the optimal λ selected as lambda.min (λ=0.0108773), which was calculated as follows:

We divided patients into high- and low-TEXRGPS groups based on the median TEXRGPS obtained. Risk curves, survival status plots, and risk heatmaps were drawn to visualize the distribution of the TEXRGPS, the relationship between the TEXRGPS and survival status, and the expression of T-cell exhaustion-related genes, respectively.

The KM curve was used for survival analysis, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to verify the ability of TEXRGPS to predict patient survival. In addition, the correlation between the TEXRGPS and clinical information was analyzed via bar charts and boxplots. Following this, KM survival analysis was performed to assess the association between the expression levels of the six signature genes and patient survival. To ensure the suitability of the model, we performed a layered analysis. Finally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to determine whether the TEXRGPS was an independent prognostic factor. The above-mentioned analyses were completed via the UpSetR, survival, survminer, glmnet, ggpubr, ggExtra, reshape2, pheatmap, timeROC, limma, and ggplot2 R packages.

Validation of the TEXRGPS

To demonstrate the stability of the TEXRGPS, six data sets were selected for validation. Applicability was demonstrated by the KM curve, ROC curve, clinical correlation, and independent prognostic analyses. Next, the six genes that constituted the signature were validated in four data sets. We used the UALCAN tool (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) to analyze the protein expression levels in normal tissues and LUAD specimens obtained from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database. Finally, the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was used to further verify the protein expression of the six genes in normal and LUAD tissues. Analyses of the validation set was conducted with the same R package as those used for the training set analyses.

Single-cell analysis

The GSE131907 single-cell dataset was analyzed via the Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub (TISCH; http://tisch.comp-genomics.org) database. The single-cell dataset included 208,506 single-cell transcriptomic profiles from 58 LUAD specimens. Importantly, the dataset included tumor, stromal, and immune cell information.

Correlation of the TEXRGPS with response to immunotherapy

The expression levels of multiple immune checkpoint genes in the high- and low-TEXRGPS groups were visualized via boxplots. Subsequently, the expression level of the PD-L1 (CD274) protein was determined in the two groups, and the immunophenotype score (IPS) was obtained from The Cancer Immune Atlas (TCIA; https://tcia.at/) database to predict the response to immunotherapy. In addition, the submap algorithm was used to further verify whether there was a difference in response to immunotherapy between the two groups. Finally, the results were validated in an independent immunotherapy cohort (IMvigor210). The above-mentioned analyses were completed via the limma, ggplot2, ggpubr, reshape2, pheatmap, survival, and survminer R packages.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To analyze the response of patients in the high- and low-TEXRGPS groups to specific antitumor agents, we accessed the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) in each patient was calculated to predict sensitivity to certain agents. The pRRophetic (v0.5), ggplot2, limma, and ggpubr R packages were used to implement these analyses and draw related boxplots.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

To investigate the different biological functions of genes in the high- and low-TEXRGPS groups, we conducted GSEA and GSVA. GSEA was performed with 1,000 permutations (nPerm =1,000) to ensure robust statistical inference. Moreover, heatmaps were used in the GSVA to visualize the differential pathways of the two groups. Analyses were conducted via the clusterProfiler, org.Hs.eg.db, enrichplot, and limma R packages.

Construction of the nomogram

By using the survival, survminer, timeROC (v0.2), regplot (v0.1), and rms (v6.2-0), we developed a nomogram based on patients’ clinical characteristics and the TEXRGPS to predict patients’ 3-, 4-, and 5-year OS probabilities. ROC and calibration curves were plotted for further validation.

Clinical specimens and reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Patient selection and study design

This was a single-center, retrospective study conducted at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Twenty paired LUAD and adjacent non-tumor tissue specimens were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection between December 2022 and March 2023. Patients were included if they had histologically confirmed primary LUAD, underwent curative-intent surgical resection, had available paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue specimens with adequate tumor cellularity (>70%) for RNA extraction, were treatment-naïve with no prior immunotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or radiotherapy before surgery, and had complete clinical data. Patients were excluded if they had received neoadjuvant therapy, had a history of other malignancies within the past 5 years, had incomplete clinical data, had poor specimen quality (tumor cellularity <70%), or had recurrent or metastatic disease. Pathological staging was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition criteria. Histological subtypes were classified according to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors by two independent pathologists. The cohort comprised 20 patients (16 females, 4 males) with a median age of 59 years (range, 43-69 years). All patients were of Han Chinese ethnicity. Nineteen patients were non-smokers and 1 patient was a smoker. Pathological stages included stage IA1 (n=1), IA2 (n=11), IA3 (n=5), and IB (n=3). Histological subtypes included acinar (n=13), papillary (n=2), lepidic (n=2), solid (n=2), and micropapillary (n=1). A summary of patient characteristics is provided in Table S1. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics of these 20 patients are provided in Table S2. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval No. KY2023-139), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Specimens were preserved in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (cat. no. SM129-02; Sevenbio, Beijing, China). Complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse-transcription kit (cat. no. 1119ES60; Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (cat. no. 11184ES03; Yeasen Biotechnology). GAPDH was used as the candidate reference gene, and relative target-gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3. The RT-qPCR raw Ct values are provided in Table S4.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were analyzed with R software version 4.1.2. The nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used to determine the differences between groups, while the paired nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used to compare normal and tumor samples from the same patient. Spearman analysis was applied to evaluate correlation coefficients, and the chi-squared test was used to analyze categorical data. Survival data were compared via KM analysis and the log-rank test, while the ROC curve was used to evaluate diagnostic performance. The calibration curve was used to assess the agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcome. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to analyze hazard ratios and identify whether the TEXRGPS was an independent risk factor. P<0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Construction of the risk score based on T-cell exhaustion

We selected 19 DEGs that overlapped in the six data sets (Figure 4D), 13 of which were found to correlate with survival according to univariate Cox regression analysis (Figure 4E). LASSO regression ultimately identified six eligible genes (Figure 4F,4G), including CCL19, CD79A, CHRDL1, MS4A1, S100P, and SFTPC that were incorporated into the TEXRGPS.

Based on the median TEXRGPS in the training cohort, patients with LUAD from the training (TCGA) and validation (GEO) cohorts were divided into low- and high-TEXRGPS groups, respectively. In the training cohort, the distribution of TEXRGPS was determined via the risk curve, and survival status was compared between the different groups (Figure 5A). We observed that S100P was highly expressed in the high-TEXRGPS group, while the other five genes were highly expressed in the low-TEXRGPS group; further analysis was applied to determine the association of the TEXRGPS with the OS, PFS, and DSS among patients with LUAD. It was found that the high-TEXRGPS group had a significantly worse prognosis (Figure 5B-5D). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the TEXRGPS in predicting 3-, 4-, and 5-year survival was 0.649, 0.642, and 0.650, respectively (Figure 5E). We additionally analyzed the correlation between clinical characteristics and the TEXRGPS. We found that patients in the high-TEXRGPS group had a higher proportion of males and patients with stage Ⅲ–Ⅳ disease, stage T3–4, disease, and stage N1–3 disease, while there was no difference in age or M stage (Figure 5F). The TEXRGPS was significantly higher among males and patients with advanced disease, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis (Figure 5G). Moreover, a higher expression of S100P was associated with lower survival probability, but this was not the case for the other five genes (Figure 5H).

Prognostic validation of the TEXRGPS in LUAD

To ensure the stability of TEXRGPS, we assessed it in the validation cohorts, and the results showed good consistency with the previous analyses (Figure 7, Figures S1,S2). We then examined the expression levels of the six genes that constitute the TEXRGPS. For CCL19, CD79A, and CHRDL1, the TCGA dataset revealed that CCL19 and CD79A were significantly upregulated, while CHRDL1 was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure 8A). These mRNA-level differences were further validated by RT-qPCR in 20 pairs of clinical samples from our hospital (Figure 8B). At the protein level, CPTAC database analysis showed that CD79A was upregulated and CHRDL1 downregulated in LUAD primary tumors compared to normal tissues, while CCL19 showed a borderline difference (P=0.06) (Figure 8C). Immunohistochemistry data from the HPA database corroborated these proteomic findings (Figure 8D). As for MS4A1, S100P, and SFTPC, the TCGA dataset demonstrated that MS4A1 and S100P were significantly upregulated, whereas SFTPC was downregulated in tumor tissues (Figure 9A). These results were also confirmed by RT-qPCR in our clinical samples (Figure 9B); however, the expression pattern of MS4A1 was not consistent in the GSE32863 and GSE75037 datasets (Figure S3). Protein-level analysis from CPTAC revealed that MS4A1 and S100P were upregulated and SFTPC was downregulated in primary tumors (Figure 9C). These findings were further supported by HPA immunohistochemistry data (Figure 9D). Collectively, these results supported differential expression of the six genes in LUAD. Some genes did not reach statistical significance in every validation cohort, which may reflect limited sample size, platform differences, and biological heterogeneity. To determine the differences in the infiltration of immune cells in the TME between the low- and high-TEXRGPS groups, 208,506 single-cell LUAD transcriptomic profiles from 58 specimens in the GSE131907 dataset were clustered and defined into 14 cell types, including B lymphocytes, CD4+ conventional T (CD4Tconv) cells, CD8+ T cells, exhausted CD8+ T (CD8Tex) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, monocytes/macrophages, oligodendrocytes, and plasma cells (Figure 10A). We observed that SFTPC had a significantly higher expression in several cell types, including CD8+ T cells and CD8Tex cells; CD79A and MS4A1 had a significantly higher expression in B lymphocytes and plasma cells; and S100P and CHRDL1 had a significantly higher expression in epithelial cells and fibroblasts, respectively (Figure 10B,10C). Results from immunohistochemistry and analysis of the TCGA further indicated that CD79A and IRF4 had a significantly higher expression in LUAD tissues, and there was a significant correlation between them (Figure S4A-S4C). Furthermore, the correlation between the TEXRGPS and immune cell distribution was determined (Figure 11).

Association of the TEXRGPS with response to antitumor therapy

First, we evaluated several immune-checkpoint genes and found that TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2, PDCD1, and CTLA-4 were more highly expressed in the low-TEXRGPS group than in the high-TEXRGPS group (Figure 12A). Proteomic data similarly showed higher PD-L1 expression in the low-TEXRGPS group (Figure 12B). The low-TEXRGPS group also had a higher immunophenotype score, suggesting greater predicted sensitivity to immune-checkpoint inhibition (Figure 12C). Subclass mapping indicated a stronger predicted response to anti-PD-1 therapy in the low-TEXRGPS group (Bonferroni-corrected P<0.02; Figure 12D). The association between TEXRGPS and immunotherapy outcome was further examined in IMvigor210, an independent urothelial-cancer immunotherapy cohort; because this cohort is not LUAD, it provides supportive cross-tumor rather than disease-specific validation (Figure 12E). Drug sensitivity was estimated using the GDSC database (Figure S5).

Second, we performed GSEA and GSVA to evaluate the biological pathways in the low- and high-TEXRGPS groups and visualized differential pathway enrichment in a heatmap. We found that pathways related to immune cell function, such as IL-2/STAT5 signaling and IL-6/JAKSTAT5 signaling pathways, were enriched in the low-TEXRGPS group, and pathways correlated with inhibitory factors in the TME, such as glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and cell proliferation, were enriched in the high-TEXRGPS group (Figure 13A-13C).

Development of a nomogram for predicting the survival of patients with LUAD

A nomogram consisting of the TEXRGPS and clinicopathological parameters was developed to predict the 3-, 4- and 5-year OS rates. (Figure 13D). The AUCs of the nomogram in predicting 3-, 4- and 5-year OS were 0.724, 0.706, and 0.724, respectively (Figure 13E). The calibration curve indicated good agreement between the predicted and observed survival probabilities, indicating that the nomogram was well calibrated (Figure 13F).

Discussion

Among malignancies, LUAD is one of the most common and is associated with high global mortality. Consequently, research on effective treatments, especially anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy, is considered a particularly high priority. In contrast to conventional therapies such as surgical treatment, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and chemotherapy, immunotherapy exerts its effect by remodeling the immunosuppressive TME, but more capable agents are needed in this regard. Although a considerable volume of research has identified an array of important regulatory factors in the TME and provided the theoretical basis for next-generation immunotherapy therapy, how transcription factors related to T-cell exhaustion affect the TME and their influence on the response to immunotherapy remain unclear. T-cell exhaustion-related immunity is considered to be a key factor that can modulate antitumor treatment; for example, it has been found that chimeric antigen receptor T-cell exhaustion is a major limitation to this type of therapy (23). Therefore, characterizing T-cell exhaustion status may help predict the survival of patients with LUAD and identify populations amenable to cancer immunotherapy. In our study, we sought to clarify if transcription factors related to T-cell exhaustion influence immune infiltration features, prognosis, and immunotherapy sensitivity in patients with LUAD.

We developed a LUAD prognostic risk score (TEXRGPS) using TCGA-LUAD and evaluated it in six independent GEO cohorts. TEXRGPS was associated with prognosis and computational estimates of immunotherapy sensitivity. To explore potential clinical utility, we integrated TEXRGPS with age, sex, and tumor-node-metastasis stage in a nomogram. These retrospective findings support further validation but do not establish clinical utility or treatment-selection benefit.

The GSEA and GSVA results showed that nutrient metabolism and cell cycle pathways, which are recognized targets for immunotherapy, were enriched in the high-TEXRGPS group. The drug sensitivity analysis we performed may provide insight into developing targeted drugs. By contrast, pathways associated with inflammatory response were enriched in the low-TEXRGPS group, and this we had a higher degree of immune cell infiltration, particularly for T cells and B cells. We further confirmed the ability of the TEXRGPS to predict response to immunotherapy via an analysis of gene expression, proteomics, and IPS, with the TEXRGPS demonstrating satisfactory performance.

The TEXRGPS includes six genes, namely CCL19, CD79A, CHRDL1, MS4A1, S100P, and SFTPC. CCL19, CD79A, MS4A1, and S100P were more highly expressed in LUAD tissues, whereas CHRDL1 and SFTPC were less expressed. These genes, except for S100P, were all highly expressed in the low-TEXRGPS group and correlated with a positive prognosis. S100P belongs to the S100 family and is recognized for its role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Its biological functions are mediated through several signalling pathways and regulatory molecules, including calcium-dependent signalling, the receptor for advanced glycation end products-dependent pathway, ezrin, calcyclin-binding protein/Siah1-interacting protein and cathepsin D (24), with its biological effect being particularly evident in LUAD (25,26). Moreover, the expression of S100P may be closely related to tumor size and tumor staging (27). As to how S100P promotes the progression of lung cancer, previous studies suggest that it may be through increasing its interaction with integrin α7 or interferon beta (IFN-β) (28,29). Other studies have indicated that S100P is an important link in multiple pathways related to lung cancer invasion such as the Keap1-Nrf2 axis, the SIX3-Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis, the PI3K/AKT pathway, and the YAP pathway (30-33). Analyses of the TCGA and GEO suggest that S100P is an effective prognostic gene for LUAD, correlating with immune cell infiltration, and may also predictive response to immunotherapy (34,35).

CCL19 exerts tumor-suppressive functions through the induction of antitumor immune responses and inhibition of angiogenesis but may also promote tumor development by inducing inflammation, cell growth, and metastasis. CCL19 dysregulation has been reported to be a tumor biomarker for diagnosis and prognosis in several cancers, including colorectal, breast, pancreatic, and lung cancer (36). For example, Itakura et al. demonstrated that chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7) and its ligand CCL19 are prognostic factors and that CCR7 is strongly related to Crk-like adaptor protein (CrkL) and c-ABL kinase messenger RNA expression, which play a role in malignant cell transformation in LUAD (37). In one study on the immunosuppressive TME, CCL19 was found to function as a molecular rheostat, balancing immune activation and suppression within the TME (38). However, a study using a murine model of spontaneous bronchoalveolar cell carcinoma revealed that CCL19 can induce CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration into the tumor, increasing the secretion of antitumor cytokines and decreasing the expression of immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β (39).

SFTPC has also been extensively investigated. Although it remains unclear whether it exerts a similar inhibitory effect on lung cancer progression to that of other surfactant proteins, the study by Li et al. suggested that the miR-629-3p-meditated downregulation of SFTPC may promote the LUAD progression and that low SFTPC expression correlated with the poor OS of patients with LUAD (40). Another study showed that SFTPC was positively correlated with the infiltration of six immune cell types (CD8+ T cells, B cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and DC) both in LUAD and lung squamous cell carcinoma; moreover, its low expression was associated with shorter OS in patients with lung cancer (41). CHRDL1 is a specific antagonist of bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), and BMP signaling participates in many cancer processes, including cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. A previous analysis of the TCGA and GEO databases suggested SFTPC to be a signature of endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes and potentially correlated with the expression level of immunomodulatory molecules including CD28, CD27, and IDO2, among others (42).

CHRDL1 is an inhibitor of BMPs, and, according to Deng et al., is related to processes governing the homeostasis of cell proliferation, including cell cycle checkpoint, protein translation, and G2M phase checkpoint; moreover, it may suppress the cancer invasion through innate immunity and improve the effectiveness of immunotherapy by regulating immune infiltration in LUAD (43). Although its specific mechanism of action in LUAD remains unclear, database analyses indicated that CHRDL1 is an important prognostic factor for patients with LUAD (43-45).

CD79A and MS4A1 are the specific markers of B cells, and the different expression of these two genes in our study implies a distinct degree of infiltration of B cells between the low- and high-TEXRGPS groups of LUAD. Furthermore, it suggests that B cells may play an important role in the emergence and development of tumors and may be used to predict antitumor efficacy. The favorable clinical outcomes provided by tumor-infiltrating B cells for patients with LUAD was demonstrated in a study that analyzed RNA-sequencing data from the TCGA. It was found that the expression levels of B-cell and plasma cell signature genes were associated with improved OS in patients with LUAD (46).

Research has established that B cells are important components of the TME, with approximately two-thirds of lung tumor-infiltrating immune cells being composed of T and B cells (47-49). B cells have different subtypes that exerts different immunological effects on lung cancer. For example, Wu et al. found that the abundance of tumor-infiltrating B cells is positively associated with delayed cancer progression, improved therapeutic response, and extended survival in patients lung cancer (50). Another study reported that B-cell infiltration is positively correlated with PD-L1 expression and that PD-L1 acts as a negative regulator to influence B-cell infiltration-mediated tumor immunity in LUAD, which suggests that B-cell infiltration may be involved in the effect that anti-PD-L1 therapy exerts (51). In contrast, regulatory B cells have been shown to inhibit T cell-mediated immunity through inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10, IL-35, and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) (52,53). Moreover, certain type of B cells, by activating several STAT3 downstream proangiogenic genes, may promote tumor progression through angiogenesis (54). However, the exact role of B cells in LUAD and their effect on immunotherapy have not been sufficiently clarified.

After further analysis of the TCGA database, we found a significant correlation between CD79A and IRF4. In addition, through further immunohistochemical analysis, we found that the expression of CD79A and IRF4 in tumor group was significantly higher than that in the normal group. Other studies have shown that CD79A is highly correlated with the prognosis of patients with lung cancer and is an effective predictor of response to immunotherapy (55-58). Transcription factor IRF4, an important regulator of a variety of immune cells, has been demonstrated to promote B-cell differentiation and maturation (59). Moreover, by enhancing the suppressive effect of regulatory T cells, it can promote the exhaustion of T cells and thus tumor development (60). Other research found that a subset of DCs with low IRF4 expression can restore immunotherapy resistance (61). Other studies have shown that IRF4-associated signaling pathways are important predictors of immunotherapy efficacy and immune-related adverse events (62,63). Although our immunohistochemical analysis did not indicate a significant relationship between CD79A and IRF4, the literature and our analysis suggest that they may be nonetheless connected.

The TME, a key link in T-cell exhaustion, not only plays an important role in antitumor immunity but is also being increasingly regarded as marker of immunotherapy efficacy, although it is still not used in clinical practice. In our study, the low-TEXRGPS subgroup had higher immune and stromal scores but a higher ESTIMATE score and survival rates. In addition, there was a significant difference in the degree of immune cell infiltration between the high- and low-TEXRGPS group. Patients in the low-TEXRGPS subgroup showed a relatively high infiltration of immune cells, especially different subtypes of T cells and B cells. The low-TEXRGPS had highly similar results to those of the high-TEXRGPS subgroup with T-cell exhaustion. Moreover, the low-TEXRGPS group not only expressed a variety of inhibitory receptors but had an elevated expression of a variety of T-cell exhaustion-related DEGs. The differences between these groups were closely related to the changes in the composition of the microenvironment caused by the dynamic progression of the tumor (64,65). Finally, we found that T-cell exhaustion was associated with poor response to treatment.

Thus, considering these differences, we deemed that it may be productive to assess the effect of ICI treatment in the two groups of patients with LUAD and subsequently analyzed the relationship between the TEXRGPS and both PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 expression via proteomics and the IPS. We found that the proteomics and IPS scores of PD-1/CTLA-4 or PD-L1 were higher in the low-TEXRGPS group than in the high-TEXRGPS group, suggesting that immunotherapy may be more effective in the low-TEXRGPS group. This is consistent with the results of subsequent subclass mapping analysis and validation in an independent cohort. Compared to the high-TEXRGPS, the low-TEXRGPS group had a higher degree of immune cell infiltration and a greater response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy, suggesting that the TEXRGPS is associated with the inhibition of antitumor immunity in LUAD. Therefore, the TEXRGPS may serve as tool for effectively evaluating the prognosis of patients with LUAD receiving immunotherapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, standardized markers and criteria for T-cell exhaustion remain incomplete, and the bulk-transcriptomic and deconvolution analyses used here cannot directly establish a dysfunctional T-cell phenotype or a causal role for NFATC1, EOMES, TOX, or the TEXRGPS genes. The available single-cell analysis described cell-type expression patterns but did not provide functional validation. Second, sequential feature filtering and model fitting may introduce selection instability and TCGA-specific overfitting. Although the signature was evaluated in six independent GEO cohorts, these retrospective public datasets may share platform-, processing-, and selection-related biases. Third, IMvigor210 is a urothelial-cancer rather than LUAD immunotherapy cohort, so its findings represent cross-tumor supportive evidence. Fourth, while GAPDH was selected as the reference gene for RT-qPCR normalization based on its widespread use in the literature and its demonstrated stability in our preliminary experiments, we acknowledge that GAPDH is a glycolytic enzyme and may be subject to expression alterations in cancers with metabolic reprogramming. Formal validation of GAPDH expression stability across LUAD tissues—potentially using multiple reference genes or algorithmic approaches—would further strengthen the normalization strategy. Finally, the clinical RT-qPCR series was small and conducted at a single center. Prospective multicenter validation using prespecified coefficients and thresholds, clinically defined treatment endpoints, and single-cell, protein-level, and functional experiments is required before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Our study examined associations between transcription factors previously linked to T-cell exhaustion, the tumor microenvironment, and clinical outcomes in LUAD. We constructed the six-gene TEXRGPS and evaluated it in six independent GEO cohorts—GSE72094, GSE31210, GSE68465, GSE26939, GSE42127, and GSE37745. TEXRGPS was associated with overall survival and with computational estimates of immune infiltration and immunotherapy response. These retrospective results support further investigation but do not establish a direct functional relationship with exhausted T cells or justify clinical treatment selection. Prospective multicenter and mechanistic validation is required.
