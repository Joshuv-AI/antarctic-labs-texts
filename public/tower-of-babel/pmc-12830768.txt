Experimental investigation of mechanical properties in cryogenically damaged shale
Authors: Xuelin Zheng, Guangqing Zhang, Dongying Wang, Feng Yang, Mingjing Lu, Liyu Song, Dawei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
During cryogenic fracturing, the low thermal conductivity of rock results in varying freezing rates within shale formations, leading to significant differences in mechanical behavior. This study employs innovative experimental methods to examine the effects of cryogenic freezing rates on shale’s mechanical properties and thermal damage capacity. Results demonstrate that when the freezing rate is below 2.5 °C/s, shale exhibits enhanced mechanical properties under cryogenic conditions. Conversely, freezing rates exceeding 5 °C/s cause mechanical deterioration. A nonlinear relationship was established between shale’s compressive strength/elastic modulus and cryogenic freezing rate. The primary damage mechanisms were identified as micro-fracturing induced by cryogenic thermal stress and ice expansion. This study proposes a novel method for quantifying shale’s thermal damage capacity using the average peak thermal stress gradient. This finding provides a direct guidance for optimizing cryogenic fracturing operations in shale oil and gas reservoirs, where maximizing the freezing rate can promote more complex fracture networks and enhance stimulation efficiency.

Introduction

Research on rock mechanical properties under cryogenic conditions has gained considerable attention in recent years, particularly for applications in cryogenic fracturing techniques1–5, geothermal energy extraction, and liquefied natural gas (LNG) storage6–9. Current investigations primarily examine how temperature, water saturation, rock type, and freeze-thaw cycles influence rock mechanical parameters.

The technical challenges of conducting mechanical experiments in cryogenic environments have led many researchers to study property changes as specimens warm from cryogenic to ambient conditions10–14. These studies consistently demonstrate that cryogenic treatment degrades physical and mechanical properties, causing microporosity damage, induced microfractures, increased permeability, reduced wave velocity, and diminished strength and elasticity. These changes correlate strongly with rock type, saturation state, and freeze-thaw cycle frequency15–18.

During shale cooling, differential contraction among mineral particles generates interparticle tensile or compressive stresses. At sufficiently low temperatures, two damage mechanisms emerge: (1) cryogenic thermal stress creates new microfractures between mineral particles, and (2) contraction-induced stress concentrations at natural microfracture tips activate and propagate existing fractures19–21. Water presence significantly alters cryogenic mechanical behavior through ice crystallization pressures that damage microstructure during freezing, with repeated freeze-thaw cycles accelerating this damage22–26. The combined effects of heterogeneous mineral strength/thermal expansion coefficients and ice expansion in pores/fractures degrade the rock’s physical and mechanical properties.

Some researchers have successfully conducted in-situ cryogenic rock mechanics experiments, generally finding that low temperatures enhance strength and elastic modulus while reducing ductility. Quasi-static uniaxial compression and Brazilian splitting tests on dry/saturated rocks at −160 °C showed substantial increases in mechanical parameters: compressive strength (20–70%), tensile strength (50–100%), and elastic modulus (~ 20%)27–34. Uniaxial compression tests on shale across 20 °C to −196 °C revealed significant matrix contraction during freezing, producing elastic-brittle strengthening behavior without plastic yielding. Both compressive strength and elastic modulus increased with decreasing temperature, establishing clear temperature-property relationships35–37. These strength enhancements primarily derive from rock matrix contraction and strengthened pore ice under cryogenic conditions31,32,38,39.

During liquid nitrogen fracturing, injected LN₂ creates extreme thermal gradients: surface shale cools rapidly while interior portions cool gradually. These rapid freezing rates generate intense thermal stresses that may exceed tensile strength35–37,40–42. This thermal shock effect differs fundamentally from conventional slow freezing, potentially creating more extensive fracture networks43–48. Although prolonged exposure eventually equalizes temperatures throughout the formation, significant freezing rate variations exist during the process (Fig. 1). Therefore, systematic investigation of cryogenic freezing rate effects on shale mechanical properties and thermal damage capacity is critically needed.

Mechanical experiments under different cryogenic freezing rates

An innovative experimental apparatus was designed to achieve controlled liquid nitrogen freezing rates. Following treatment at specified freezing rates, mechanical tests were conducted on shale specimens under both cryogenic and room-temperature conditions to investigate the relationship between freezing rate and mechanical property evolution.

Experimental methods and design

The samples were taken from continental shale oil in western China, with a burial depth of approximately 2,500–2,800 m. The core porosity is 4.5 ± 0.8%, and the permeability is 0.1 ± 0.05 mD. XRD analysis revealed the mineral composition: quartz (27.3%), potassium feldspar (3.7%), plagioclase (7.7%), pyrite (6.8%), clay minerals (45.1%), and TOC (9.4%). The clay minerals consist of illite (62%), montmorillonite (15%), chlorite (13%), and kaolinite (10%). Following ISRM standards, samples were machined into 50 mm × 25 mm cylinders.

Specimens were dried at 50 °C until mass stabilization (consecutive unchanged measurements over 48 h). Sonic testing screened specimens with consistent wave velocities to minimize internal defect interference. Select specimens were saturated via vacuum impregnation: (1) Specimens placed in a sealed chamber evacuated to −0.1 MPa for > 2 h to remove air from pores; (2) Distilled water introduced under continuous vacuum for 4 h until bubble cessation; (3) Specimens submerged for ≈ 1 month until mass stabilization (saturation water content: 1.8%).

Freezing employed a custom liquid nitrogen rate-control device (Fig. 2a-b). Specimens within an insulated chamber received liquid nitrogen from a self-pressurizing tank, with flow regulated by valve adjustment to control freezing rate. Temperature was monitored throughout using a Data Taker 80 collector. After reaching − 196 °C at target rates, specimens were held in liquid nitrogen for ≈ 1000 s to ensure complete freezing, then transferred to liquid nitrogen storage for 2 h prior to testing.

Mechanical response of dry shale

Stress-strain curves and mechanical properties under cryogenic conditions are shown in Fig. 3. Initial strength and modulus were 44.59 MPa and 16.26 GPa. At freezing rates ≤ 2.5℃/s, strength and modulus increased by 2.3–13.4% and 1.1–7.4%, respectively. Rates ≥ 5℃/s caused decreases, reaching maximum reductions of 44.2% (strength) and 36% (modulus) at 100℃/s. Thus, cryogenic mechanical properties strengthen below 2.5℃/s but weaken above 5℃/s.

Mechanical response of saturated shale

Cryogenic properties (Fig. 6) showed initial values of 41.47 MPa (strength) and 14.88 GPa (modulus). Rates ≤ 2.5℃/s caused increases (6–18.3.3% strength, 6–15.9.9% modulus), while rates ≥ 5℃/s caused decreases, maximized at 48.1% and 39.1% (100℃/s). The strengthening/weakening threshold aligns with dry shale.

It should be noted that the post-thaw re-drying process may cause irreversible changes in clay minerals, which could affect the mechanical properties. However, the comparison between the saturated cryogenic tests and the post-thaw dried tests allows us to decouple the effects of ice-related damage from the temporary strengthening effects. In actual water-bearing formations, the shale would remain saturated, and the mechanical behavior would be similar to the saturated cryogenic tests, but without the re-drying effect. Therefore, the post-thaw dried condition is a laboratory method to evaluate the permanent damage, while the saturated cryogenic condition represents the in-situ state during cryogenic treatment.

The power-law relationship between mechanical parameters and freezing rate can be derived from the theory of thermal elasticity and damage mechanics. The thermal stress induced by rapid freezing causes microcracking, which accumulates nonlinearly with the freezing rate. The observed microcrack initiation and propagation under cryogenic conditions can be explained by the Griffith theory of brittle fracture. The thermal stresses induced by rapid freezing act as the driving force for crack growth, and when the stress intensity factor at a crack tip exceeds the fracture toughness, the crack propagates. Our results show that the critical freezing rate for significant damage (5 °C/s) corresponds to the condition where the thermal stress exceeds the tensile strength of the shale, which is consistent with the Griffith criterion.

This study focused on single freeze-thaw cycles; future work will include cyclic freeze-thaw experiments to investigate the cumulative damage and the long-term effects on shale mechanical properties.
