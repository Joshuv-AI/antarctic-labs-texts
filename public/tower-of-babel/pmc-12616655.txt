Association of CXCL16 in aseptic loosening following total hip arthroplasty and its effect on bone metabolism: a case-control study
Authors: Langfeng Zhu, Kai Wang, Peijie Li, Jiankang Zeng, Yongjie Qiao, Xiaoyang Song, Jiahuan Li, Zhiqiang Lin, Shenghu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAseptic loosening commonly leads to surgical failure and revision arthroplasty after total hip arthroplasty (THA). C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16), a chemokine involved in various inflammatory and immune responses, has been implicated in bone metabolism. This single-institution study investigated the association between CXCL16 and aseptic loosening following THA.MethodsWe compared 100 patients who underwent revision THA due to aseptic loosening (experimental group) to 100 patients who underwent primary THA for femoral neck fracture or congenital hip dysplasia (control group). Immunohistochemistry and RT-PCR were used to assess CXCL16 expression at the protein and mRNA levels. Double immunofluorescence staining determined the RANKL/OPG ratio in periarticular synovial tissues. Statistical analysis included a chi-square test for CXCL16 protein expression differences and Pearson correlation for the relationship between CXCL16 mRNA expression and RANKL/OPG.ResultsProteomics analysis revealed significantly higher CXCL16 expression in the experimental group compared to the control group (p < .01). Immunohistochemistry confirmed that 90% of samples in the experimental group showed positive CXCL16 staining compared to 7% in the control group (p < .01). RT-PCR demonstrated a 3.4-fold higher CXCL16 mRNA expression in the experimental group relative to controls (p < .01). TRAP staining identified that 72% of CXCL16+ multinucleated giant cells in the experimental group were macrophages. Immunohistochemical staining showed that 73% of CXCL16+ macrophages were M2 type, and 27% were M1 type. Dual-immunofluorescence staining indicated an increase in the RANKL/OPG ratio in the experimental group, with a positive linear correlation between CXCL16 mRNA expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio (r = .919, p < .01).ConclusionOur findings reveal a significant association between elevated CXCL16 expression and an increased RANKL/OPG ratio in periprosthetic synovial tissues, suggesting a role for CXCL16 in promoting osteoclast differentiation and activity. These findings offer preliminary insights into CXCL16 as a biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for managing bone resorption in aseptic loosening.Keywords: CXCL16, prosthesis, aseptic loosening, osteoclasts, arthroplasty

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is one of the most effective treatments for end-stage hip degeneration [1]. Since the 1970s, artificial arthroplasty has carried out a great deal of clinical research and practice, especially for the repeated radiological appearance of artificial prostheses. Currently, the development of this procedure has become so mature that it has become a standard treatment in orthopaedic therapy, greatly improving the quality of life of patients [2].

A very important issue is that since the clinical application of total joint replacement, reducing and preventing postoperative complications has been the main focus of clinical and research. Aseptic loosening is the most serious complication that affects the recovery after resurfacing [3]. The mechanism of aseptic loosening is various, on the one hand, it is a mechanical factor, due to the poor fixation of the prosthesis during the operation, resulting in micromovement between the prosthesis and the surrounding bone, which affects the fit between the articular prosthesis and the corresponding bony surfaces, which prevents the prosthesis from being stabilized in the first few months of the postoperative period. Over time, the prosthesis eventually loosens aseptically [4]. On the other hand, micromotor friction between the articular prosthesis and the prosthesis or between the prosthesis and the bone surfaces generates wear particles, which can cause osteolysis through the occurrence of biological reactions related to the macrophage multicellularity and inflammatory factors such as tumour necrosis factor(TNF) and interleukin [5]. Valladares et al. [6] found that wear particles stimulate macrophages to trigger an inflammatory response that can lead to periprosthetic osteolysis. Pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF and IL-6 constitute an aging-related secretion phenotype, in which senescent backbone cells have a reduced potential for bone formation and an increased tendency to drive inflammation and reabsorption [7], which further leads to decreased bone density and ultimately to aseptic loosening [8].

However, these markers, while useful, are not always specific to aseptic loosening and may not fully capture the complex inflammatory processes involved in bone remodelling and osteolysis. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16), a chemokine involved in both inflammation and bone metabolism, has recently been implicated in aseptic loosening. Unlike TNF-α and IL-6, which are primarily inflammatory cytokines, CXCL16 also plays a role in osteoclast differentiation and activity [9–11]. This dual function makes CXCL16 a promising biomarker for aseptic loosening, potentially offering both diagnostic and therapeutic insights.

In this study, we aim to explore the role of CXCL16 in bone metabolism, specifically its influence on osteoclast differentiation and its potential as a more specific marker for aseptic loosening.

Materials and methods

Patient enrolment

Patients who underwent revision THA at The 940th Hospital of Logistics Support Force of Chinese People’s Liberation Army between 30 June 2015 and 30 June 2023 were assigned to the experimental group. All patients signed an informed consent form. The ethical code of this study was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and The 940th Hospital of Logistics Support Force of Chinese People’s Liberation Army approved all procedures (Ethics Number: 2023KYLL228). Hundred patients completed clinical data and met the study inclusion criteria, of which 47 were men and 53 were women (age range: 46–79 years, mean: 63). The mean time (SD) from the initial THA to the revision THA was 11.5 ± 3.24 years (range, 5–17 years) (Table 1). Hundred patients who underwent an initial THA for a femoral neck fracture or congenital hip dysplasia during the same period were selected as the control group. These patients were matched to the experimental group based on age, gender and clinical diagnoses. Additional factors, including BMI and comorbidities, were also considered to ensure comparability and minimize selection bias; 53 were men and 47 were women (age range: 45–77 years, mean: 57).

Selection of specimen sites and inclusion and exclusion criteria

The experimental group consisted of patients diagnosed with aseptic loosening of either the acetabular component, the femoral stem, or both components of the THA. Patient samples in the experimental group were selected from around the femoral head and acetabular prosthesis during surgery and the adjacent osteolytic lesion tissue. Inclusion criteria: ① Patients who underwent previous THA surgery for revision due to aseptic loosening. ② Patients aged ≥45 years and ≤80 years, patients over 80 years old were excluded to avoid potential confounding factors related to advanced age, such as frailty, comorbidities and altered immune responses that might impact the generalizability of the findings; ③ Those meeting the diagnostic criteria of aseptic loosening of hip prosthesis after evaluation of clinical symptoms, physical examination and imaging examination (Including standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral X-rays of the hip joint. Additional imaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were used when necessary to confirm the diagnosis and assess the severity of the loosening); The diagnostic criteria for aseptic loosening are based on the parametric criteria summarized by Reinartz [12], which have received wide clinical acceptance. ④ >3 years after the primary artificial hip arthroplasty. Exclusion criteria: combined with prosthesis dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, periprosthetic infection, other inflammatory joint diseases, immune disease, malignant tumour and incomplete clinical data.

Patient samples in the control group were obtained from the periacetabular fascia, including joint capsule and synovial tissue during the primary hip arthroplasty procedure. Bone samples from the femoral canal were not collected in the control group due to the surgical protocol. Inclusion criteria: ① patients who had their primary artificial hip arthroplasty in the Department of Joint Surgery of The 940th Hospital of Logistics Support Force of Chinese People’s Liberation Army during the same period and patients with the same clinical diagnosis as those in the experimental group; ② patients aged ≥45 years old and <80 years old. Exclusion criteria: patients with other inflammatory joint diseases, systemic immune diseases and malignant tumours.

Quantitative parallel reaction monitoring (PRM)-targeted proteomics

For PRM-targeted proteomics, four samples were randomly selected from each group (experimental and control) to ensure feasibility due to the high cost and technical complexity of the assay. These samples included periarticular synovial tissues from the experimental group and corresponding joint capsule tissues from the control group. Similar sample sizes have been used in other proteomics studies due to resource constraints in exploratory analyses [13]. Paraffin samples from four cases of the experimental group and four cases of the control group were selected for dewaxing, protein extraction, reductive alkylation and enzymatic digestion and then mixed in equal amounts into one library for mass spectrometry to obtain high-resolution mass spectrometry. Then, liquid tandem mass spectrometry PRM assay was performed separately on each sample on the machine. Skyline was used for peak extraction of the raw data of PRM; ions with a high abundance of daughter ions were selected for quantitative analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Considering the small amount of samples included in PRM-targeted proteomics, Immunohistochemistry and RT-PCR analyses were conducted on 100 samples from each group to validate the proteomics findings on a larger scale. Immunohistochemistry was conducted using the streptavidin-peroxidase (SP) method. Primary antibodies included CXCL16 (Bio-Techne, HKB0419011), CD86 (Shanghai Jingfeng Biotechnology, BB08212678) and CD163 (Fuzhou Maixin Bio-Technology, 2210260869e). The staining procedure followed the manufacturers’ protocols. Under the microscope, cells were judged positive when brown or yellow-brown granules were visible in the cytoplasm. CXCL16 positivity was assessed in multinucleated giant cells, CD86 in macrophages and CD163 in macrophages. For quantitative analysis of CXCL16 expression, 100 cells per sample were analysed across five randomly selected high-power fields (400× magnification) to ensure consistent sampling. This method was standardized across all samples to minimize variability in staining assessment.

RT-PCR

Fresh specimens from 20 patients were selected from the experimental and control groups for RT-PCR. In total, 200 mg of tissue was clipped; after nucleoprotein dissociation, RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis, the reverse transcription reaction was performed in a PCR amplifier. The cDNA was pre-denatured, denatured and annealed using a three-step method to obtain the results. Primer sequences used for CXCL16 and GAPDH amplification were as follows:

Then the data were analysed by the 2 − ΔΔCT method, statistically analysed and plotted by the software.

TRAP staining method

Staining was performed on 100 samples from the experimental group to assess the distribution of CXCL16-positive multinucleated cells and osteoclast activity. Fixative solution was added to the sections of 100 patients in the experimental group, which were subsequently fixed at room temperature for a duration of 1 min. Following water washing and gentle drying, staining solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalogue #387 A-1KT) was dropwise added to cover the tissues. The sections were then incubated at 37 °C in a thermostat for a period of 60 min and subsequently washed with water. The sections on slides were stained using the methyl counterstaining solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalogue #M8884) for 3 min, followed by washing, drying and microscopic examination.

Immunofluorescence double staining

For RT-PCR analysis, 20 specimens from the experimental group were fixed in a 4% neutral formaldehyde solution. The specimens underwent routine paraffin embedding and were sectioned into slices with a thickness of 4 um. After dewaxing and hydration, EDTA antigen retrieval was performed followed by serum blocking. Added the primary antibody (OPG, batch no. 1016181-1) and incubated overnight. The next day incubated fluorescent secondary antibody. Added the secondary antibody (RANKL, batch no: YD3898962A) and incubated at a constant temperature of 37 °C for 2 h. After adding the fluorescent secondary antibody and incubating at an additional hour at 37 °C, the slices were cleaned, sealed and observed under a fluorescence microscope. RANKL and OPG are key regulators of bone remodelling. RANKL binds to its receptor RANK on osteoclast precursor cells, stimulating their differentiation into active osteoclasts, which are responsible for bone resorption. In contrast, OPG acts as a decoy receptor for RANKL, binding to it and preventing RANKL from activating RANK, thus inhibiting osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. This balance between RANKL and OPG is crucial for maintaining bone homeostasis.

Statistical methods

Experimental data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. The count data were expressed as number of cases with percentage. The chi-square test was used to compare the differences in the expression of CXCL16 protein between the experimental group and the control group. A total of 100 cases were included in each group based on feasibility and the anticipated effect size for CXCL16 expression differences. This sample size was determined to provide sufficient statistical power (≥80%) to detect a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with a significance level of 0.05. The data satisfied the normality test, which was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test to ensure that statistical test assumptions for Pearson correlation coefficients were met. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between CXCL16 mRNA expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio. A p value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of experimental and control groups

The clinical and demographic characteristics of the experimental and control groups are detailed in Table 1. The groups were balanced for sex distribution, with 47% male and 53% female patients in the experimental group compared to 53% male and 47% female patients in the control group. The mean age of the experimental group was higher, with 64% of patients aged ≥60 years compared to 41% in the control group. BMI distributions were comparable across groups, with the majority of patients in the normal BMI range (57% in the experimental group vs. 51% in the control group). The Harris hip score differed significantly between groups, with 90% of experimental group patients scoring <70 compared to only 2% in the control group, reflecting the clinical status of patients undergoing revision surgery. Additionally, prosthesis material and duration varied, as expected, given the study design. For instance, all patients in the experimental group had biological femoral stems, and the duration of hip prostheses ranged from 5 to ≥15 years. These characteristics ensure the comparability of the groups while highlighting key clinical differences relevant to the study.

Identification of CXCL16 markers

PRM-based proteomics assay and the quantitative analysis of CXCL16 were performed to investigate the potential role of CXCL16 in aseptic loosening in patients undergoing THA revision. Comparison of CXCL16 expression levels in tissue samples

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significant differences in CXCL16 protein expression between the experimental and control groups. In the experimental group, 90% (90/100) of samples were positive for CXCL16, compared to only 7% (7/100) in the control group. Conversely, 10% (10/100) of samples in the experimental group were negative for CXCL16, compared to 93% (93/100) in the control group. The chi-square test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant (χ2 = 137.904, p < .01) (Figure 3). To confirm differences in CXCL16 expression levels further, RT-PCR was used to measure CXCL16 mRNA expression in the tissues surrounding the aseptic loosening prosthesis and the necrotic femoral head. The results showed that CXCL16 mRNA expression in the tissues surrounding the aseptic loosening prosthesis was significantly higher (mean ± SD: 3.4 ± 0.8-fold increase relative to GAPDH) compared to tissues surrounding the necrotic femoral head (1.0 ± 0.2 relative to GAPDH, p < .01) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Identification of the type of multinucleated macrophages in the experimental group

Antibodies against CD86 and CD163 were used for immunohistochemistry. Quantitative analysis revealed that 73% ± 5% of CXCL16+ macrophages in the experimental group were CD163+ M2-type macrophages, while 27% ± 4% were CD86+ M1-type macrophages. These percentages were derived from the immunohistochemistry analysis of 20 randomly selected fields per sample under high magnification. The results showed that CXCL16-positive macrophages in the experimental group were predominantly CD163+ M2-type macrophages; CD86+ M1-type macrophages were also widespread (Figure 4).

Relationship between CXCL16 mRNA expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio

In the experimental group, dual-immunofluorescence staining was used to determine the expression of RANKL and OPG in periprosthetic synovial tissues. RANKL was visualized with green fluorescence, OPG with red fluorescence, and nuclei were labelled with DAPI (blue fluorescence) to facilitate signal localization. The RANKL/OPG ratio was calculated based on the intensity and coverage of the fluorescence signals (Figure 5). Additionally, the relationship between CXCL16 mRNA expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio was analysed in these patients. A positive linear correlation was observed between CXCL16 mRNA levels and the RANKL/OPG ratio (r = .919, p < .01), as shown in Supplementary Figure 3.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a significant association between elevated CXCL16 expression and aseptic loosening following THA. Our findings reveal that CXCL16 expression is markedly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group, correlating strongly with an increased RANKL/OPG ratio. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis identified CXCL16 as predominantly expressed by M2-type macrophages, suggesting its role in promoting osteoclast differentiation and activity. These findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying aseptic loosening and highlight CXCL16 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target. The remainder of the Discussion interprets these findings within the broader context of existing literature.

Macrophages and chemokines

Our findings suggest that CXCL16 plays a central role in the inflammatory and osteoclastic activity associated with aseptic loosening. Elevated CXCL16 expression in the experimental group was predominantly localized to M2-type macrophages, which are known for their dual roles in resolving inflammation and promoting tissue remodelling. However, our results align with recent evidence suggesting that M2 macrophages may have higher osteoclastogenic potential, directly contributing to periprosthetic osteolysis. This is further supported by the strong correlation between CXCL16 expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio observed in this study. These findings indicate that CXCL16 may act as a mediator linking macrophage activity and osteoclastogenesis in aseptic loosening.

While prior studies have established the involvement of other chemokines in periprosthetic osteolysis, such as TNF-α and IL-6, our study highlights the specificity of CXCL16 in driving these processes [14–18]. Unlike broad-spectrum inflammatory markers, CXCL16 may offer a more targeted pathway for therapeutic intervention. Future research should investigate whether targeting CXCL16 in M2 macrophages could mitigate osteoclast formation and reduce the progression of aseptic loosening.

Mechanisms of CXCL16 and macrophage activity in aseptic loosening

The CXCL16+ cells in the experimental group were giant multinucleated cells, so TRAP staining was used to identify the cell type. TRAP staining confirmed that the multinucleated giant cells expressing CXCL16 were predominantly macrophages with few osteoclasts. M1-type macrophages promote osteoclastogenesis by secreting tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-1β [19], whereas M2-type macrophages inhibit osteoclastogenesis by secreting IL-4 and IL-10 [20–22]. However, concerning the macrophage subtypes and induction of osteoclasts, the M2-type macrophages inherently have greater osteoclast differentiation potential and are more likely than M1-type macrophages to fuse to form osteoclasts [23–27]. Given the different functions of M1- and M2-type macrophages, we used immunohistochemistry (CD86 and CD163) to determine the types of macrophages in the periprosthetic synovial tissues. CXCL16+ macrophages were predominantly of the M2 type, while some M1-type macrophages were also present.

Currently, the known pathological mechanisms responsible for aseptic loosening include: (1) wear particles that stimulate macrophage conversion to osteoclasts, disrupt the equilibrium between osteoclasts and osteoblasts and lead to osteolysis and (2) wear particles affect bone formation by stimulating the secretion of chemokines by macrophages. We observed numerous CXCL16+ M2- and M1-type macrophages in the periprosthetic synovial tissue. While M2-type macrophages were traditionally thought to attenuate inflammatory responses and inhibit osteoclast formation through the secretion of IL-4 and IL-10, recent studies suggest that M2-type macrophages have a higher potential to directly fuse into osteoclasts [28,29]. Our findings of CXCL16 expression in M2-type macrophages, combined with these observations, indicate a potential role for CXCL16 in promoting osteoclast differentiation, contributing to periprosthetic osteolysis. Additionally, we found that periprosthetic M1-type macrophages also expressed CXCL16. M1-type macrophages, known for their pro-inflammatory effects, likely exacerbate the local inflammatory environment by secreting TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β, which are established mediators of osteoclastogenesis [30–32]. These findings collectively highlight the complex interplay between CXCL16 expression, macrophage subtypes and osteoclast activity in periprosthetic tissues.

Osteolysis is an outcome of an imbalance of CXCL16 and RANKL/OPG signalling

The discovery of the RANKL/OPG system has made a significant contribution to osteoimmunology and bone biology, providing a robust theoretical foundation for studying the pathogenesis of aseptic loosening [33,34]. Our study specifically focused on patients undergoing hip revision for aseptic loosening or primary THA for hip fracture and degenerative changes and did not include an osteoarthritis-specific cohort. As such, direct comparisons between CXCL16 and osteoarthritis-associated RANKL/OPG signalling were not assessed. Previous studies have demonstrated that the RANKL/RANK ratio is significantly higher in aseptic loosening periprosthetic synovial tissue of patients with osteoarthritis compared to healthy individuals, while OPG levels remain largely unchanged in this context [35]. However, those studies also reported no significant differences in RANKL expression in aseptic loosening tissues compared to osteoarthritis, with OPG expression being significantly reduced in osteoarthritic tissues [36]. In contrast, our findings show a strong positive correlation between CXCL16 mRNA expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio in aseptic loosening tissues, suggesting that CXCL16 may drive osteoclastogenesis in the periprosthetic environment by influencing this key signalling pathway [37]. Although changes in RANKL and OPG expression in these studies are inconsistent, both result in the same outcome of an elevated RANKL/OPG ratio.

Our results also showed that the expression of CXCL16 in synovial tissues of patients in the experimental group was significantly higher than those in the initial THA group, suggesting that CXCL16 may be a major chemokine involved in developing aseptic loosening. To further validate the effects of CXCL16 on bone metabolism in aseptic loosening pathogenesis, dual-immunofluorescence staining was used to determine RANKL levels and OPG expression in the synovial tissue of patients in the experimental group. Additionally, the surface area with a positive RANKL/OPG ratio was calculated. The RANKL/OPG ratio gradually increased with increasing expression of CXCL16 mRNA; the relationship between the two indicators showed a positive linear correlation. A retrospective analysis of the clinical data in the experimental group revealed that patients with ceramic-ceramic prostheses exhibited significantly lower CXCL16 expression compared to those with other types of prosthetic coupling. Patients with ceramic-ceramic prostheses displayed lower CXCL16 expression levels in the periprosthetic synovial tissues, likely due to the reduced generation of wear particles compared to metal or polyethylene components. Wear particles are known to stimulate macrophage activation, leading to inflammatory cytokine production, including CXCL16. This observation highlights the potential role of prosthetic material selection in mitigating inflammatory responses and reducing the risk of aseptic loosening. CXCL16 was not expressed in synovial tissue; the RANKL/OPG ratio change was insignificant. Our data demonstrated a positive correlation between CXCL16 mRNA levels and the RANKL/OPG ratio, suggesting that CXCL16 may indirectly influence osteoclastogenesis by modulating RANKL/OPG signalling in the periprosthetic environment. While this supports a potential role for CXCL16 in bone metabolism, additional in vitro or in vivo experiments are required to establish a causal relationship between CXCL16 expression and osteoclast activation or bone resorption.

CXCL16 expression and other aseptic revisions

While this study specifically focused on aseptic loosening as a consequence of inflammatory responses triggered by wear particles, it is plausible that similar mechanisms involving CXCL16 could occur in other types of aseptic revisions, such as those resulting from instability or polyethylene wear. Both conditions can generate inflammatory responses due to mechanical stress or particulate debris, which are known to activate macrophages and promote cytokine release.

Polyethylene wear particles, for instance, are well-established triggers of macrophage recruitment and activation, leading to the secretion of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6. These mediators play overlapping roles with CXCL16 in promoting osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption. Although direct evidence linking CXCL16 to instability or poly wear is currently limited, the presence of macrophage-driven inflammatory cascades in these conditions suggests that CXCL16 expression could be similarly upregulated.

However, the magnitude and impact of CXCL16 expression may vary depending on the underlying aetiology. Unlike poly wear or instability, aseptic loosening involves sustained mechanical and biological interactions between wear particles and periprosthetic tissues, potentially amplifying CXCL16-related pathways. Future studies comparing CXCL16 levels across different types of aseptic revisions could clarify whether this mechanism is unique to loosening or shared across other inflammatory conditions.

Limitations and outlook

Aseptic loosening remains a significant challenge in orthopaedic practice, partly due to the complexity of its pathophysiology and the limited availability of biomarkers for early prediction and diagnosis. This study specifically focused on the role of CXCL16 in aseptic loosening, highlighting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target. However, one limitation of our study is the absence of long-term follow-up to validate CXCL16 as a predictive marker for aseptic loosening. Additionally, the findings are limited to the selected cohort of patients undergoing revision THA or primary THA for fracture or degenerative changes, which may not fully generalize to other populations or conditions, such as osteoarthritis. Future research should explore broader cohorts and integrate longitudinal designs to address these gaps [38].

CXCL16 is a factor that plays a crucial role in inflammation [39], bone destruction [40] and the movement of osteoblasts [41]. While this factor has been extensively studied, most research has focused on individual factors rather than the complex interactions between surgical procedures, daily physical activities and perioperative care. As a result, recreating these complex interactions remains an ongoing challenge. And Potential confounding factors such as patient age, comorbidities and concurrent medication use (e.g. corticosteroids or anti-inflammatory drugs) were not explicitly controlled in this study. aseptic loosening patients have shown the presence of other factors, such as MMP-13 [42], p53/BAK [43] and ADAM9 [44], in the periprosthetic area. However, patients with known inflammatory conditions, autoimmune diseases, or recent infections were excluded during the selection process. However, statistical robustness is lacking due to the small sample size. Future studies should include a stratified analysis to further explore the influence of these factors on CXCL16 expression.

Over eight years, we obtained tissue samples from aseptic loosening patients who had undergone joint prostheses. Through clinical sample analysis, we established a connection between CXCL16 and aseptic loosening after total hip arthroplasty, which had not been previously explored. The study examined the mechanisms of aseptic loosening in humans, filling a notable research gap.

Although the study used primarily paraffin-embedded tissue samples with limited fresh samples, it highlighted the significant role of CXCL16 in the development of aseptic loosening. One limitation of this study is the small number of samples (four per group) analysed using PRM-targeted proteomics. This was due to the high cost and technical complexity of this advanced proteomics method. Although this limited sample size provided exploratory insights into CXCL16 expression, it restricts the generalizability of the findings. Subsequent validation of these results through complementary techniques, such as immunohistochemistry and RT-PCR on larger sample sizes, partially mitigates this limitation. Future studies should aim to include a larger cohort in proteomics analyses to enhance statistical robustness and better represent the molecular mechanisms underlying aseptic loosening. Although our study identified a correlation between CXCL16 expression and the RANKL/OPG ratio, the mechanistic role of CXCL16 in osteoclast differentiation and bone metabolism was not directly tested, and we also should evaluate whether elevated CXCL16 levels could serve as a risk determinant for repeat revisions and its potential role in osseointegration defects. Future studies utilizing functional assays, such as co-culture experiments of macrophages and osteoclast precursors, are needed to validate these hypotheses and clarify the molecular pathways involved.

The findings of this study also have significant potential for clinical translation. Elevated CXCL16 expression in periprosthetic tissues correlates strongly with the RANKL/OPG ratio, which is a key regulator of osteoclast activity and bone resorption. This suggests that CXCL16 could serve as a biomarker for the early detection of aseptic loosening. Although specific expression thresholds were not established in this study, future research could focus on defining clinically relevant CXCL16 cutoff values using larger patient cohorts and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Such thresholds could improve the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic tools for aseptic loosening. Moreover, therapeutic interventions targeting CXCL16, such as monoclonal antibodies or small molecule inhibitors, could be explored as strategies to mitigate osteoclast-driven bone resorption in patients at risk of aseptic loosening. These findings pave the way for both diagnostic and therapeutic advances, potentially extending the lifespan of prosthetic implants and reducing the need for revision surgeries.

CXCL16 has potential as a therapeutic target for aseptic loosening. Compounds such as Palmatine (Pal) [45,46] and metformin [47] have reduced inflammation, pain and bone destruction. Clinical trials that used monoclonal antibodies targeting CXCL16 have shown promise in mitigating periarticular inflammation and bone damage [48]. Future research should explore the use of anti-CXCL16 monoclonal antibodies or CXCL16 inhibitors to extend prosthesis lifespan and prevent aseptic loosening.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant role of CXCL16 in the pathogenesis of aseptic loosening following THA. Elevated CXCL16 expression in periprosthetic synovial tissues was strongly correlated with an increased RANKL/OPG ratio, indicating its potential involvement in promoting osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed that CXCL16 was predominantly expressed by M2-type macrophages, which may directly contribute to osteoclastic activity, as well as by M1-type macrophages, which exacerbate local inflammatory responses.

These findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying aseptic loosening, suggesting that CXCL16 could serve as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target for preventing or managing aseptic loosening. However, this study is limited by the lack of direct quantitative assessments of osteoclast activity and bone turnover and by its exclusive focus on CXCL16 without exploring other inflammatory mediators.

Future research should incorporate in vivo models, quantitative bone turnover analyses and a broader evaluation of cytokine interactions to further elucidate the role of CXCL16 in bone metabolism and its potential as a therapeutic target. By addressing these gaps, we can advance our understanding of aseptic loosening and improve the longevity and efficacy of prosthetic implants.
