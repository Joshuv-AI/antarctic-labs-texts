Non-infectious occlusive retinal vasculitis following switching from aflibercept 2 mg to aflibercept 8 mg in neovascular age-related macular degeneration: a case report
Authors: Ermal Simaku, Ahmed Nour Eddien, Megi Popa, Ahmed El Shafei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnti–vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy represents the standard of care for neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Aflibercept 8 mg was recently introduced with extended dosing intervals and favorable safety outcomes in phase III trials. Although no confirmed cases of occlusive retinal vasculitis were reported in pivotal studies, rare inflammatory vascular events may emerge during broader real-world exposure.Case presentationA 74-year-old pseudophakic male with active nAMD in the left eye demonstrated significant anatomical and functional improvement following intravitreal aflibercept 2 mg. After switching to aflibercept 8 mg, the patient presented 26 days later with painful visual deterioration. Fundus examination revealed mild vitritis, segmental arterial sheathing, venous narrowing, and peripheral intraretinal hemorrhages. Fluorescein angiography demonstrated capillary non-perfusion and late vascular leakage. Optical coherence tomography confirmed cystoid macular edema. Extensive infectious and autoimmune investigations were negative. Systemic and local corticosteroid therapy resulted in regression of inflammation with partial visual recovery.ConclusionsNon-infectious occlusive retinal vasculitis may rarely occur following switching to aflibercept 8 mg. Early recognition and prompt anti-inflammatory treatment are critical to minimize irreversible ischemic damage. Continued post-marketing pharmacovigilance is warranted.

Introduction

Intravitreal anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy represents the gold standard in the management of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) [1]. Over the past decade, multiple anti-VEGF agents including ranibizumab, aflibercept 2 mg, brolucizumab, and faricimab have been widely adopted in clinical practice [2–4].

Inflammatory adverse events, including retinal vasculitis and retinal vascular occlusion, have been reported in association with certain anti-VEGF agents, particularly during post-marketing surveillance [5–8]. These events have highlighted the importance of ongoing pharmacovigilance even when clinical trials demonstrate reassuring safety profiles.

Aflibercept 8 mg was introduced following the PULSAR phase III trial, which demonstrated non-inferiority to aflibercept 2 mg with extended dosing intervals and favorable early fluid control [9]. Importantly, no confirmed cases of occlusive retinal vasculitis were reported in the clinical trial population.

As real-world use expands, rare adverse inflammatory events may become detectable. We report a case of non-infectious occlusive retinal vasculitis occurring shortly after switching from aflibercept 2 mg to aflibercept 8 mg.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old male presented with progressive blurred vision in his left eye over a two-month period. He was pseudophakic in both eyes. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measured 20/25 in the right eye and 20/100 in the left eye at baseline. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated dry age-related macular degeneration in the right eye and active neovascular AMD in the left eye with intraretinal and subretinal fluid (Fig. 1A, B).

The patient received one intravitreal injection of aflibercept 2 mg in the left eye. Four weeks later, BCVA improved to 20/40 with significant anatomical improvement on OCT, including marked reduction of intraretinal and subretinal fluid (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Post-treatment OCT of the left eye four weeks after intravitreal aflibercept 2 mg demonstrating marked anatomical improvement with near-complete resolution of intraretinal and subretinal fluid and restoration of foveal contour

Based on emerging evidence supporting extended dosing intervals, treatment was switched to aflibercept 8 mg.

Twenty-six days after the first aflibercept 8 mg injection, the patient returned with painful blurred vision in the treated eye. BCVA had deteriorated to counting fingers. Slit-lamp examination revealed no conjunctival hyperemia and no anterior chamber inflammatory reaction. Intraocular pressure was within normal limits.

Dilated fundus examination demonstrated mild vitritis, segmental arterial sheathing, venous narrowing, and new peripheral intraretinal hemorrhages (Fig. 3).

Fluorescein angiography revealed capillary non-perfusion along the infratemporal arcade with late-phase vascular leakage (Fig. 4A, B).

OCT confirmed cystoid macular edema (Fig. 5).

Comprehensive laboratory investigations were undertaken to exclude infectious and systemic inflammatory etiologies. Microbiological testing was negative. Serologic testing for syphilis, hepatitis B and C, HIV, and toxoplasmosis was negative. Autoimmune screening including ANA, ANCA, rheumatoid factor, and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels was unremarkable. QuantiFERON testing and chest radiography showed no evidence of tuberculosis. CT angiography of the head and neck demonstrated no vascular stenosis.

A diagnosis of non-infectious occlusive retinal vasculitis was established. Treatment was initiated with oral methylprednisolone (1 mg/kg/day), topical dexamethasone, and sub-Tenon’s triamcinolone acetonide. Due to persistent macular edema, intravitreal dexamethasone was subsequently administered. At two-month follow-up, OCT demonstrated partial resolution of macular edema (Fig. 6), and BCVA improved to 20/125.

Discussion and conclusions

Occlusive retinal vasculitis is an uncommon but potentially vision-threatening complication of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. Although most prominently reported in association with brolucizumab, inflammatory vascular events have been described across multiple biologic agents in post-marketing surveillance [5–8, 10].

In this case, the temporal association between aflibercept 8 mg administration and symptom onset, combined with the absence of prior inflammatory episodes under aflibercept 2 mg, supports a possible treatment-related inflammatory mechanism. Extensive infectious and systemic investigations were negative, and clinical findings were not consistent with infectious endophthalmitis.

Interestingly, this case differs from the “first-injection phenomenon” described with some anti-VEGF-associated inflammatory vasculitis syndromes, in which inflammation may occur after initial exposure to the offending agent [5–8]. Our patient had previously tolerated standard-dose aflibercept 2 mg without intraocular inflammation before developing occlusive retinal vasculitis after dose escalation to aflibercept 8 mg. This observation suggests that factors beyond simple first exposure, including dose-related immune activation, formulation-related factors, or altered pharmacodynamic effects, may have contributed to the inflammatory response.

Several mechanisms may be considered. Immune-mediated hypersensitivity reactions related to anti-drug antibody formation have been proposed in similar contexts [6–8]. Alternatively, sustained and potent VEGF suppression may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and vascular instability [11, 12]. While these mechanisms remain theoretical, they provide biologically plausible explanations for inflammatory occlusive phenomena.

No confirmed cases of occlusive retinal vasculitis were reported in the PULSAR trial [9]. However, rare events may only become apparent during broader real-world exposure. Early recognition and prompt corticosteroid therapy are essential to limit irreversible ischemic damage. In our patient, despite timely intervention, permanent visual decline persisted, highlighting the potential severity of occlusive ischemic complications.

Although causality cannot be definitively established from a single case, documentation of such events contributes to ongoing pharmacovigilance and may assist clinicians in early detection and management. Follow-up fluorescein angiography was not available; therefore, follow-up assessment was based on clinical examination and OCT findings.
