Comparison of pulmonary ventilation/perfusion tomography and conventional diagnostic techniques in the diagnosis of pulmonary thromboembolism
Authors: Xue Lv, Fei Du, Jie Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) is a common and potentially fatal cardiopulmonary vascular disease. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for improving patient prognosis. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is widely used as a first-line imaging modality, while the clinical value of pulmonary ventilation/perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography (V/Q SPECT) in specific patient populations is increasingly recognized. However, systematic evidence comparing the diagnostic performance and corresponding patient clinical phenotypes across different imaging techniques remains limited. This study aimed to compare the application differences between V/Q SPECT and CTPA in diagnosing PTE, along with their associated clinical characteristics, physiological functional status, and risk stratification features, thereby providing evidence-based support for optimizing imaging diagnostic pathways.MethodsA retrospective case-control study was conducted, enrolling hospitalized patients diagnosed with PTE between January 1, 2017, and April 30, 2022, at a tertiary hospital in Shandong Province. Initially, 1,124 cases were retrieved via electronic medical record (EMR) and imaging databases. After screening, 726 patients were finally included: 312 in the V/Q SPECT group and 414 in the CTPA group. Demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, laboratory indicators [D-dimer, troponin I, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), partial pressure of arterial oxygen to the fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2), etc.], lower limb venous ultrasound, and echocardiographic parameters were systematically collected. Propensity score weighting was employed to balance baseline differences. Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed.ResultsAfter weighting, baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups. Patients in the V/Q SPECT group more frequently presented with central or lobar emboli, accompanied by more significant hypoxemia and right ventricular dysfunction (RVD). Their levels of D-dimer, NT-proBNP, and myocardial injury markers were higher than those in the CTPA group. The CTPA group in our cohort predominantly consisted of patients with relatively milder clinical symptoms and biomarker profiles. Multivariable analysis revealed that elevated NT-proBNP, RVD, and lower limb deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were significantly associated with the use of V/Q SPECT. The multi-parameter combined model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.832 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.801–0.862].ConclusionsV/Q SPECT and CTPA are associated with distinct clinical phenotypes in real-world practice, largely reflecting systematic selection patterns driven by patient-specific contraindications and baseline risk profiles. In our cohort, V/Q SPECT was more commonly utilized in patients with higher clinical risk and biomarker levels, reflecting a clinical selection pattern where this modality was assigned to a more physiologically compromised population, while CTPA was applied to a relatively lower-risk cohort. The selection of imaging modality should integrate comprehensive assessment of clinical probability, biomarkers, and cardiopulmonary function to achieve individualized diagnostic decision-making.Keywords: Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), pulmonary ventilation/perfusion tomography, computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), imaging diagnosis, right ventricular function

Highlight box

Key findings

• Ventilation/perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography (V/Q SPECT) and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) are applied to pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) patients with distinct clinical and pathophysiological profiles, largely as a result of clinician-driven selection.

• CTPA more frequently detects central or lobar emboli associated with significant hypoxemia, right ventricular dysfunction, and higher cardiac biomarker levels.

• V/Q SPECT predominantly identifies peripheral or low-burden emboli with milder clinical impact.

• A combined model integrating biomarkers and cardiopulmonary function demonstrated good discriminatory performance.

What is known and what is new?

• CTPA is the first-line imaging modality for PTE. V/Q SPECT is an alternative diagnostic tool, particularly in selected patient populations, but comparative clinical phenotype data are limited.

• This study systematically compares V/Q SPECT and CTPA from a clinical phenotype and physiological burden perspective. Imaging modality selection is shown to correlate with embolic burden, cardiopulmonary dysfunction, and biomarker profiles rather than imaging alone.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Imaging strategies for PTE should move beyond a one-size-fits-all approach.

• Integrating clinical probability, biomarkers, and cardiopulmonary function can guide more individualized and rational selection between V/Q SPECT and CTPA in real-world practice.

Introduction

Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), as one of the primary lethal phenotypes of venous thromboembolism (VTE), remains a significant cause of preventable mortality worldwide. Longitudinal analyses of multinational mortality registries reveal considerable cross-country heterogeneity in the burden of PTE-related deaths. These differences are closely associated with varying levels of economic development and healthcare system capacity. The observed trends are largely driven by the combined effects of population aging, the obesity epidemic, and high-risk exposures such as malignancy (1). Real-world inpatient database studies further indicate that PE is not a rare event. An analysis of China’s National Hospital Quality Monitoring System (HQMS) recorded approximately 200,000 hospitalizations for PTE in 2021 alone. Both incidence and mortality rates increased markedly with age (2).

A population-level study of hospital records in England demonstrated a continuous increase in first-hospitalization PTE rates from 2008 to 2023, with marked structural fluctuations during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. This suggests that public health events and changes in healthcare accessibility can substantially influence clinical diagnostic burden and case identification patterns (3). Furthermore, a US national mortality data trend study found persistently elevated mortality risk with significant gender and ethnic disparities among PTE patients with obesity, indicating that risk clustering and outcome inequities related to PTE persist even within established healthcare systems (4). In summary, existing evidence consistently indicates that the overall disease burden of PTE has not declined substantially despite advances in diagnosis and treatment. Against a backdrop of expanding high-risk populations and increasingly complex comorbidity profiles, balancing safety with improved diagnostic accuracy and efficiency has become a pivotal challenge affecting clinical outcomes (5).

However, the early identification of PTE remains significantly hampered by the non-specific nature of its clinical presentation and the high heterogeneity of its risk spectrum (6). To mitigate radiation exposure, healthcare resource consumption, and potential complications from unnecessary imaging, several diagnostic strategies combining clinical probability assessment with D-dimer thresholds have been proposed and validated across various clinical settings in recent years (7). For instance, a prospective cohort study conducted in a primary care environment demonstrated that a risk stratification strategy based on the YEARS clinical decision rule algorithm could safely exclude PTE in a considerable proportion of suspected cases, thereby significantly reducing referral needs and reliance on imaging (8). Nevertheless, such “imaging reduction” strategies do not equate to replacing imaging studies. In patients presenting with hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, increased right heart strain, or multiple comorbidities, imaging assessment remains indispensable for definitive diagnosis, quantification of thrombus burden, and guidance of risk stratification (9). Concurrently, the choice of imaging strategy is also constrained by practical safety considerations. For example, while computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is widely regarded as the first-line imaging modality, its reliance on iodinated contrast agents creates a significant trade-off between feasibility and safety in patients with renal insufficiency or a history of severe contrast allergy (10,11). Further clinical studies indicate that acute kidney injury following CTPA is not uncommon in patients with acute PTE. Its risk is closely associated with disease severity and right ventricular dysfunction (RVD), suggesting that the choice of imaging modality may be intertwined with the patient’s underlying risk profile (12). Consequently, in real-world diagnostic pathways, the selection of an imaging technique is not merely a technical decision, but rather reflects a complex process of risk-benefit balancing. Without quantifiable and clinically interpretable evidence, this process may lead to greater variability in test utilization and increased uncertainty in diagnostic pathways.

Against this backdrop, pulmonary ventilation/perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography (V/Q SPECT) and its hybrid strategies with low-dose computed tomography (CT) have regained attention in both clinical and research arenas. Unlike CTPA, which is based on the anatomical principle of directly visualizing intravascular filling defects, V/Q SPECT focuses on evaluating the matching relationship between ventilation and perfusion. This approach theoretically aligns more closely with the pathophysiological hallmark of PTE, which is the redistribution of pulmonary perfusion (13). Recent empirical studies provide further support for its clinical application. For example, integrating low-dose CT information into the V/Q SPECT interpretation workflow for suspected PTE patients significantly improves diagnostic specificity and reduces false-positive rates, thereby enhancing its interpretability in complex clinical scenarios involving underlying lung disease or structural abnormalities (14).

Recent comparative studies have increasingly focused on the diagnostic agreement between simplified protocols—such as perfusion-only SPECT/CT—and CTPA. These studies systematically evaluate diagnostic performance using various interpretation criteria, thereby providing methodological foundations for establishing alternative diagnostic pathways under different resource constraints (15-17). Evidence regarding the benefit-risk balance of imaging in special populations is also evolving. A study using clinical dose data quantified the lifetime cancer risk from maternal and fetal radiation exposure associated with CTPA and V/Q scanning for suspected PTE during pregnancy. The results showed that both modalities entail low overall fetal doses, but differences exist in maternal organ dose distribution and long-term risk composition. These findings offer actionable guidance for refining imaging strategies in highly sensitive populations (18). In summary, current evidence consistently indicates that V/Q SPECT is not merely an alternative technology. It holds significant value in specific clinical contexts where contrast agents are contraindicated, functional information is prioritized, or specific risk exposures need to be mitigated. Nevertheless, its real-world utilization patterns—along with the corresponding patient phenotypes and disease severity profiles—remain inadequately characterized by systematic comparative studies.

More importantly, the choice of imaging modality may be largely driven by the patient’s pathophysiological burden, which can be objectively quantified using biomarkers, blood gas parameters, and cardiac function indices (19,20). Previous and recent studies consistently demonstrate that increased right ventricular pressure overload and secondary myocardial injury are key pathological pathways determining adverse outcomes in PTE. Elevated plasma B-type natriuretic peptide-related markers are significantly associated with all-cause and disease-related mortality in acute PTE, providing independent incremental predictive information for clinical risk stratification (21). Meanwhile, impaired oxygenation, as a comprehensive reflection of ventilation-perfusion mismatch, has also been confirmed to possess prognostic value. Recent studies indicate that the partial pressure of arterial oxygen to the fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ratio holds important reference significance in risk stratification and therapeutic pathway selection for acute PTE (22). Therefore, from a pathophysiological evolution perspective, “increased thrombus burden → impaired pulmonary perfusion → decreased oxygenation → elevated right heart strain → abnormal biomarkers” constitutes a continuum of observable dysfunction. If systematic preferences for specific imaging tools exist at different stages of this continuum in clinical practice, then the observed differences in the application of V/Q SPECT vs. CTPA should be explainable through patient phenotypes and quantitative measures, and further translate into modifiable evidence for pathway optimization. Conversely, without systematically clarifying these selection mechanisms, it becomes difficult to distinguish whether variations in imaging strategy reflect rational individualized decision-making or non-rational variations stemming from resource allocation, operational inertia, or disparities in healthcare access.

Based on the above rationale, this study retrospectively analyzed hospitalized patients with confirmed PTE. Employing a case-control design, patients were categorized into V/Q SPECT and CTPA groups according to the primary imaging modality used for definitive diagnosis. We systematically compared the differential profiles of these two diagnostic technologies across multiple dimensions, including clinical presentation, laboratory biomarker levels, blood gas oxygenation status, and right heart function parameters. We also assessed their application characteristics and diagnostic alignment across different clinical risk spectra. To mitigate selection bias (specifically confounding by indication) related to the choice of imaging test, we introduced propensity score weighting to statistically adjust for the clinical factors that initially dictated the selection of one modality over the other. Furthermore, we constructed a multivariable model to identify key factors independently associated with the choice of imaging modality. This comprehensive approach aims to elucidate the application differences and underlying mechanisms between V/Q SPECT and conventional imaging techniques in real-world clinical practice. This study seeks to address the gap in systematic comparative evidence regarding patient phenotype and disease severity distributions across different imaging diagnostic pathways. It aims to provide interpretable, quantifiable evidence to support the rational selection of V/Q SPECT or CTPA in complex clinical scenarios, thereby optimizing the imaging diagnostic strategy for PTE and enhancing overall diagnostic quality and patient safety. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0382/rc).

Methods

Patient population

This retrospective study enrolled hospitalized patients diagnosed with PTE between January 1, 2017, and April 30, 2022, at a municipal tertiary general hospital in Shandong Province, China. All cases were sourced from the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system and medical imaging/ultrasound databases. A systematic search using relevant diagnostic codes, imaging reports, and clinical notes was conducted to initially identify eligible patients. Included cases possessed relatively complete basic clinical data, imaging results, and laboratory test indices, sufficient for subsequent statistical analysis and model construction. Cases with severely missing or incomplete key information were excluded during data compilation to ensure data reliability and the stability of analytical results.

Study design

This study employed a retrospective case-control design to systematically analyze patients with a prior PTE diagnosis. After strictly applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, patients were grouped based on the primary imaging modality used for definitive diagnosis. Patients with a PTE diagnosis confirmed by V/Q SPECT constituted the case group. Those diagnosed primarily by CTPA formed the control group. This grouping allowed for a systematic comparative analysis of the diagnostic differences between these two imaging modalities in clinical practice.

The overall study workflow sequentially involved case screening and confirmation, extraction of raw clinical data, standardized data processing and variable coding, univariate comparative analysis, multivariable logistic regression modeling, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. All steps followed standardized operating procedures to minimize the potential impact of information bias and selection bias on the findings, thereby enhancing the scientific rigor and reproducibility of the conclusions.

Ethics and informed consent

As a retrospective observational study based on pre-existing medical records, this research did not involve any prospective interventions or additional diagnostic/therapeutic procedures. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University Dezhou Hospital (approval No. 2025122; date: October 13, 2025). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Study procedures

Case retrieval and preliminary screening

A systematic retrospective search was performed across the hospital’s EMR system, Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), and related clinical information management platforms for all inpatients within the study period. Case identification utilized International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes and standardized clinical diagnostic terms, supplemented by cross-referencing with Chinese and English keywords including “pulmonary thromboembolism”, “pulmonary embolism”, “pulmonary artery thrombosis”, and “pulmonary embolism”. For initially identified cases, complete EMRs, original imaging studies, and radiology reports were reviewed. The timing, image quality, and diagnostic conclusions of CTPA and V/Q SPECT exams were systematically verified. Cases with incomplete imaging data, suboptimal image quality for diagnosis, or ambiguous imaging conclusions were excluded from further analysis. All cases passing initial screening underwent manual verification by two independent researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy in the selection process.

Group assignment and consistency control

Group assignment was primarily based on the formal radiology diagnostic report. V/Q SPECT imaging was performed following a standard two-day or one-day protocol. Ventilation imaging was obtained after inhalation of 99mTc-labeled diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (99mTc-DTPA) aerosol (740–1,110 MBq). Perfusion imaging was performed following the intravenous injection of 99mTc-macroaggregated albumin (99mTc-MAA) (74–148 MBq) with the patient in a supine position. SPECT data were acquired using a dual-head gamma camera with a low-energy high-resolution collimator, using a 128×128 matrix and 6° angular steps.

Patients with CTPA demonstrating definite intravascular filling defects in the main or branch pulmonary arteries meeting diagnostic criteria were assigned to the control group. For patients who underwent both CTPA and V/Q SPECT during the study period, a predefined assignment rule was applied uniformly. The principle was to use the first imaging modality that led to the clinical diagnosis for grouping. If the initial study was non-diagnostic, the primary modality that ultimately established the clinical diagnosis was used for categorization. In cases of discrepant findings between the two modalities, a senior radiologist reviewed the studies to reach a consensus judgment, thereby minimizing grouping bias.

Clinical data extraction and database creation

Clinical data for finally included cases were extracted independently by two researchers from the original EMR system and related examination databases. Extracted information encompassed demographics, past medical history, exposure to risk factors, clinical signs and symptoms, laboratory results, imaging data, and echocardiography findings. All data were sourced from original records to avoid information bias from secondary summaries. A standardized electronic case report form (eCRF) was developed based on study objectives, and a dedicated research database was established. All variables were defined and coded according to a predefined data dictionary. After initial data entry, a thorough review of all data points was conducted. Omissions, logical inconsistencies, or anomalous records identified were traced back to source documents for verification and correction, ensuring data completeness and consistency.

Variable definition, standardized coding, and consistency management

Study variables were constructed and processed per a predefined analysis plan. Risk factor variables were defined uniformly based on core items common to the simplified Wells score and revised Geneva score: prior history of PTE or deep vein thrombosis (DVT), immobilization/surgery within the preceding 4 weeks, and active malignancy. Presence of any of these conditions was defined as risk factor positive; absence of all was defined as negative. Categorical variables, such as specific clinical symptoms, presence of lower limb DVT, pulmonary hypertension (PH), and RVD, were coded dichotomously (yes/no). Continuous variables retained their original measurement values during data entry, without arbitrary grouping or truncation, to preserve their statistical information. For indicators from different testing platforms or time periods, units were harmonized and values were adjusted according to laboratory quality control standards where applicable.

Data cleaning and quality control

Prior to statistical analysis, a systematic data cleaning and quality assessment was performed. The database was first screened for duplicates to identify multiple hospitalization records or duplicate entries for the same patient. According to a predefined rule, only the first hospitalization with a definitive PTE diagnosis was retained. Subsequently, the extent of missing data for key variables was assessed. Individual cases with a high proportion of missing data for crucial indicators that could not be retrieved were excluded. Exploratory distribution analysis was conducted for numerical variables to identify outliers and extreme values falling outside clinically plausible ranges. All flagged records were verified by consulting original lab reports, imaging reports, and medical charts. Values confirmed as genuine were retained; data entry errors were corrected. The entire data cleaning and correction process was documented to ensure traceability and procedural rigor.

Outcome measures

Laboratory biomarkers

Arterial oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2)

Calculated from arterial blood gas results as the ratio of PaO2/FiO2. This objectively assesses the degree of impaired ventilation-perfusion function, reflecting the impact of pulmonary blood flow redistribution due to PTE on oxygenation efficiency. It served as a key quantitative parameter for respiratory dysfunction.

Plasma D-dimer level and age-adjusted threshold interpretation

Plasma D-dimer results during hospitalization were systematically collected. Interpretation followed an age-adjusted threshold principle: for patients aged ≥50 years, the threshold was set at “age ×10” μg/L (or equivalent unit); for those <50 years, the conventional reference threshold was used. All results were converted to standard units before statistical analysis to reflect the degree of in vivo thrombosis and fibrinolysis activation.

Myocardial injury marker—cardiac troponin I (cTnI)

Plasma cTnI levels were collected to evaluate right ventricular strain and secondary myocardial injury in acute PTE. This marker reflects right ventricular ischemia and myocardial stress, and its association with imaging diagnostic findings was analyzed.

Cardiac strain marker—N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)

Plasma NT-proBNP levels were collected to reflect right ventricular pressure and volume overload. As a key biomarker of right heart dysfunction, it was used to assess potential diagnostic performance differences of the two imaging modalities in patients with impaired cardiac function.

Composite biomarker-derived parameters

Beyond individual biomarkers, derived parameters were constructed, such as the D-dimer to NT-proBNP ratio and a combined score incorporating cTnI and NT-proBNP. These aimed to comprehensively reflect the overall state of thrombotic burden, myocardial injury, and cardiac dysfunction, and to explore their potential value in stratifying imaging diagnostic findings.

Echocardiography and vascular assessment parameters

Lower limb DVT assessment

Systematic evaluation of the deep venous system via compression venous ultrasound of the lower limbs, documenting the presence and anatomical distribution of thrombus. This parameter reflected peripheral venous thrombus burden and its correlation with pulmonary embolism imaging findings was analyzed.

PH assessment

Estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) was derived from Doppler echocardiography, primarily using the peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity, supplemented by right heart structural parameters. This screened for PH, reflecting the degree of increased pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular afterload.

RVD assessment

Comprehensive echocardiographic assessment of right ventricular systolic function and structural changes, including right ventricular dilation, septal motion abnormality, and impaired right ventricular systolic function. This was used to define the presence of RVD and analyze its influence on imaging diagnostic performance.

Quantitative right heart structure and function parameters

Further specific parameters were collected: right ventricular end-diastolic diameter, right ventricle/left ventricle diameter ratio (RV/LV ratio), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), and peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity. These enabled a more refined quantitative assessment of right heart strain and were used to model the association between imaging findings and the degree of cardiac functional impairment.

Composite derived indices and risk stratification parameters

Multi-parameter combined risk assessment index

Indices combining laboratory biomarkers and echocardiographic parameters were constructed to provide a comprehensive quantitative assessment of overall thrombus burden, right heart functional status, and disease severity. These were used for risk stratification analysis across different diagnostic strategies.

Imaging diagnostic prediction index construction parameters

Based on results from multivariable regression analysis, statistically significant independent influencing factors were weighted and integrated to construct a prediction index related to imaging modality choice. This index aimed to comprehensively evaluate the differential diagnostic performance of V/Q SPECT vs. CTPA across patient subgroups with varying risk levels, providing a quantitative basis for optimizing individualized diagnostic pathways.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed on data undergoing rigorous quality control. Routine statistical analyses were primarily conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0) and FengRui Statistical Software. Advanced modeling, model validation, and visualization were implemented using relevant statistical packages in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-sided, with the significance level (α) set at 0.05.

Continuous variables were first assessed for distribution normality. Data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group comparisons employed the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range), and group comparisons used non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages [n (%)], with group comparisons using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

During the variable selection stage, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for dimensionality reduction among candidate predictor variables. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined via k-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients selected by LASSO were entered into the subsequent multivariable analysis model to mitigate multicollinearity and overfitting risks.

For multivariable analysis, with the imaging modality (or diagnostic outcome) as the dependent variable, variables selected by LASSO were incorporated into a binary logistic regression model to identify independent influencing factors. Odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. Model goodness-of-fit was assessed via the likelihood ratio test and residual analysis.

Model discriminative ability was evaluated using the ROC curve, calculating the area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% CI. Comparisons of AUCs between different models or indicators were performed using DeLong’s test.

To assess the stability and generalizability of the prediction model, internal validation was conducted using the Bootstrap resampling method with no fewer than 1,000 repetitions. For each resampled dataset, the model was refitted and discriminative metrics were recalculated. Bias-corrected AUC values were computed. Model calibration performance was evaluated via calibration curves and the Brier score, supplemented by the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

To further evaluate the potential clinical utility of the prediction model, decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to calculate the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. This net benefit was compared against traditional diagnostic strategies (e.g., “treat all with CTPA” or “treat all with V/Q SPECT”) to assess the model’s practical value in supporting clinical decision-making.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), under the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR). Multiple imputed datasets were generated, and results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Sensitivity analyses comparing results from complete-case analysis and multiple imputation analysis were performed to verify the robustness of the findings.

To address confounding by indication inherent in this retrospective, non‑randomized design, we employed inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores. The propensity score—the predicted probability of undergoing V/Q SPECT rather than CTPA—was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model that included 16 a priori selected covariates covering demographic characteristics (age, sex, smoking history), major comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease), a documented contraindication to iodinated contrast media, established risk factors for VTE (prior history of PTE or DVT, immobilization or surgery within the preceding 4 weeks, active malignancy), clinical presentation (dyspnea, admission heart rate), and key physiological and laboratory parameters (D-dimer, NT-proBNP, and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio). All continuous variables were entered without categorization to preserve statistical information. After estimating the propensity score, IPTW weights were calculated as 1/propensity score for patients in the V/Q SPECT group and 1/(1-propensity score) for those in the CTPA group; weights were then truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to enhance stability. Balance in baseline characteristics between the two weighted groups was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD <0.10 predefined as indicating negligible residual imbalance. Weighted comparisons were further evaluated using weighted t‑tests or chi‑square tests as appropriate.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 1,124 inpatient cases initially suspected or diagnosed with PTE were retrieved through the EMR and imaging databases within the study period. Following a detailed review of each medical record and original imaging data, 176 cases (15.7% of the initial 1,124 retrieved) were excluded due to suboptimal image quality for diagnostic purposes, primarily resulting from respiratory motion artifacts, severe image noise, or incomplete imaging archives, 128 cases were excluded for severe missing critical clinical or laboratory data, and a further 94 cases were excluded for comorbid conditions such as chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease or other underlying pathologies that could confound the analysis. Consequently, 726 cases were ultimately included for analysis. Among these, 312 patients, whose diagnosis was primarily established by V/Q SPECT, constituted the case group. After adjusting for demographic characteristics and potential clinical confounders, the multivariable analysis indicated that elevated NT-proBNP levels were significantly associated with the use of V/Q SPECT as the primary diagnostic modality (OR =1.87, 95% CI: 1.34–2.61, Wald χ2=13.42, P<0.001). RVD was also an independent correlate (OR =2.43, 95% CI: 1.62–3.65, Wald χ2=17.86, P<0.001), suggesting a greater propensity for selecting V/Q SPECT in patients with evident right heart strain. Additionally, the presence of lower limb DVT was significantly associated with an increased probability of V/Q SPECT application (OR =1.58, 95% CI: 1.11–2.25, Wald χ2=6.29, P=0.01).

The PaO2/FiO2 ratio showed a significant inverse association with the likelihood of V/Q SPECT use (OR =0.74, 95% CI: 0.58–0.95, Wald χ2=5.47, P=0.02), indicating a clinical tendency towards V/Q SPECT as the first-line imaging choice as oxygenation function deteriorates. In contrast, age, D-dimer level, and prior thrombosis history did not demonstrate independent statistical significance in the multivariable model (all P>0.05).

The overall model fit was satisfactory, as indicated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ2=6.21, P=0.62), showing no significant lack of fit. The C-statistic of the regression model was 0.79, reflecting good discriminative ability. These results suggest that, even after comprehensively considering clinical severity, biomarker levels, and cardiac functional status, the choice between V/Q SPECT and CTPA in clinical diagnostic pathways exhibits distinct and significant differentiating features.

Diagnostic performance and ROC analysis

A predictive model for imaging diagnostic pathway selection was developed based on the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Its discriminative ability was systematically evaluated using ROC curves. The AUC for the comprehensive predictive model was 0.832 (95% CI: 0.801–0.862; Figure 4A), indicating good ability to distinguish the clinical preference for a V/Q SPECT-based vs. a CTPA-based diagnostic pathway. Using the Youden index to determine the optimal cut-off point, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73–0.82), specificity of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.71–0.79), positive predictive value of 0.71, negative predictive value of 0.81, and an overall accuracy of 0.76 at this threshold. These findings indicate that the multi-parameter combined model not only significantly enhances overall discriminative performance but also possesses utility in terms of clinical interpretability and classification performance.

The comprehensive predictive model demonstrated clear superiority compared to single-biomarker models (Figure 4B). To further delineate the comparative focus, subgroup predictive models were constructed with V/Q SPECT-related and CTPA-related pathways as respective outcomes, and their discriminative abilities were compared. Internal validation, calibration, and DCA

To assess the stability and generalizability of the comprehensive predictive model, internal validation was performed using the Bootstrap resampling method with 1,000 repetitions. The model was refitted, and discriminative metrics were recalculated in each resampled dataset. The bias-corrected AUC was 0.824 (95% CI: 0.792–0.854), closely approximating the original model’s AUC of 0.832. The mean optimism bias was 0.008, indicating that the model maintained relatively stable discriminative performance across different sampling datasets without evidence of substantial overfitting.

Model calibration performance was evaluated comprehensively using calibration curves and multiple quantitative metrics (Figure 5A). The calibration curve demonstrated good overall agreement between predicted probabilities and the actual probability of diagnostic pathway selection across the entire risk spectrum, with no systematic deviation observed in either low-risk or moderate-to-high-risk ranges. The Brier score was 0.142, suggesting satisfactory overall prediction accuracy. The calculated calibration-in-the-large (intercept) was 0.03 and the calibration slope was 0.96, both close to their ideal values of 0 and 1, respectively, indicating no marked systematic overestimation or underestimation tendency. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test result (χ2=7.84, P=0.57) further supported good model calibration (Figure 5B).

Building upon this, DCA was employed to evaluate the potential clinical utility of the comprehensive predictive model (Figure 5C). The results showed that across a threshold probability range of 0.18 to 0.72, the net benefit of using the comprehensive predictive model consistently surpassed that of the two extreme strategies (i.e., performing CTPA on all patients or performing V/Q SPECT on all patients). In summary, the results from internal validation, calibration analysis, and DCA collectively indicate that this multi-parameter combined predictive model is not only statistically stable and reliable but also holds considerable value for clinical decision support. Guiding the choice between V/Q SPECT and CTPA based on individualized patient risk profiles can help further refine the differential application of these two imaging diagnostic strategies across varied clinical contexts, thereby enhancing the overall appropriateness and precision of the diagnostic pathway for PTE.

Discussion

PTE represents a common and potentially fatal acute cardiopulmonary condition for which timely diagnosis is paramount to improving outcomes. However, diagnostic challenges persist due to the non-specific and variable clinical presentation of PTE (14). CTPA, widely employed in clinical practice, is recognized as the diagnostic gold standard for PTE owing to its capacity for direct visualization of intravascular filling defects within the pulmonary arteries and its ability to concurrently identify other thoracic abnormalities (23). Nonetheless, CTPA is not without limitations. Its reliance on iodinated contrast media restricts its applicability in special populations, such as those with renal impairment, contrast allergy, or pregnancy. Additionally, its relatively higher radiation dose raises safety concerns, particularly for younger patients (24). V/Q SPECT, an imaging modality that identifies perfusion defects based on functional lung mismatch, requires no contrast agent, delivers a lower radiation dose, and has virtually no absolute contraindications. Its role in pulmonary embolism screening has gained increasing prominence in recent years (25). Numerous studies and international guidelines indicate that V/Q SPECT offers diagnostic accuracy comparable to CTPA, particularly for patients with low-to-intermediate risk PTE or those with contraindications to contrast media (14,25). Against this backdrop, the present study systematically compared the imaging characteristics, clinical phenotypes, physiological parameters, and cardiopulmonary functional indices between V/Q SPECT and CTPA in the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism. The aim was to explore the patient profiles associated with different imaging pathways and their underlying mechanisms, thereby providing evidence-based support for optimizing PTE diagnostic strategies.

Our findings reveal distinct differences in the imaging manifestations of acute pulmonary embolism between patients undergoing V/Q SPECT vs. CTPA, which likely reflect the clinical selection of these modalities for different disease severities rather than purely intrinsic diagnostic differences. V/Q SPECT identifies ventilation-perfusion mismatch, which resulted in a higher detection rate of segmental perfusion abnormalities within its assigned patient cohort. In this study, some patients exhibited multiple segmental or subsegmental perfusion defects on V/Q SPECT, while CTPA failed to demonstrate definitive vascular filling defects, implying a potential limitation of CTPA in detecting small peripheral emboli. This observation aligns with existing literature. A multicenter study by Wang et al. [2020] on CTEPH reported that V/Q SPECT demonstrated 85% sensitivity at the segmental level for detecting vascular obstruction, significantly higher than the 67% sensitivity of CTPA (26). Furthermore, a prospective study by Naydenska et al. [2023] found that among suspected PE patients, V/Q SPECT or SPECT/CT yielded positive findings in 74.7% of cases, whereas CTPA was positive in only 52.8% of concurrently examined patients (27). Our cohort of acute PE inpatients showed a similar trend, with V/Q SPECT identifying a greater number of positive cases than CTPA. However, this increased sensitivity comes at the cost of some reduction in specificity. We observed instances where small perfusion defects were present on V/Q SPECT with no corresponding abnormality on CTPA. Subsequent analysis integrating clinical and follow-up information suggested that some of these represented false positives, potentially attributable to non-thrombotic factors such as atelectasis, chronic inflammatory changes, or respiratory motion artifacts. Literature corroborates this point. The aforementioned study reported V/Q SPECT specificity at the segmental level ranging from 42% to 51%, lower than the 60% specificity of CTPA (26). The diagnostic findings in the V/Q SPECT cohort showed a different profile of concordance with clinical outcomes compared to the CTPA cohort, highlighting that modality-specific findings must be interpreted strictly within the context of the patient’s selected diagnostic pathway. This underscores the need for caution regarding potential overdiagnosis risk when interpreting V/Q images, especially in patients with atypical symptoms or complex underlying lung disease. This finding affirms the important role of V/Q SPECT as a highly sensitive tool for detecting peripheral PE, while simultaneously emphasizing that image interpretation must be integrated with the clinical context to avoid misleading diagnoses.

Our study further elucidates a fundamental divergence between V/Q SPECT and CTPA in image representation and physiological information interpretation, epitomizing the dichotomy between functional and anatomical imaging pathways. CTPA relies on the direct visualization of intravascular filling defects, providing excellent anatomical localization, particularly for identifying large emboli in the main or lobar arteries. Its imaging findings are straightforward and unambiguous, and a positive finding carries high specificity (25). In contrast, V/Q SPECT provides indirect evidence by detecting a pattern of segmental perfusion defects with preserved ventilation. It captures the functional consequences of hemodynamic alteration without directly visualizing the thrombus itself, conferring particular sensitivity for minute thrombi lodged in subsegmental or even more distal small arteries (26). This distinction reflects a clinical selection pattern in our hospital where patients with potentially higher-burden or central lesions were more frequently directed toward the V/Q SPECT pathway, possibly due to specific contraindications or the need for functional assessment in sicker individuals (28). Nonetheless, the clinical significance and management implications of such V/Q-positive, CT-negative small foci remain debated. Existing research suggests that patients with isolated subsegmental pulmonary embolism (SSPE) have an exceedingly low short-term recurrence rate even without anticoagulation therapy, implying that some imaging findings may represent clinically low-risk lesions (29). International guidelines recommend that such findings undergo review by experienced imaging specialists and be interpreted within a comprehensive clinical framework to avoid misinterpreting artifacts or non-thrombotic perfusion abnormalities as definitive diagnostic evidence. In our study, a small number of cases with V/Q SPECT-positive but CTPA-negative findings did not demonstrate typical PTE progression on follow-up, possibly representing inactive perfusion defects or chronic emboli. However, other patients in this category presented with significantly elevated D-dimer levels or evidence of lower limb venous thrombosis, suggesting that the perfusion defects had a thrombotic etiology missed by CTPA (27). Therefore, while V/Q SPECT enhances diagnostic sensitivity, vigilance against potential false-positive interpretations is warranted. Conversely, although CTPA boasts higher specificity, it may harbor a blind spot for minute peripheral lesions. Prospective multicenter studies have shown no significant difference in VTE recurrence rates at three months between diagnostic pathways predominantly based on V/Q SPECT vs. those based on CTPA, indicating similar overall levels of diagnostic safety and efficacy for the two strategies (17). Our findings support a flexible approach to imaging modality selection based on individual patient risk profiles and imaging contraindications. In specific clinical scenarios, sequential or combined application of both modalities may be considered to maximize diagnostic value and enhance clinical decision-making precision.

Our study also noted significant differences in the capability of V/Q SPECT and CTPA to provide ancillary imaging findings. CTPA can simultaneously deliver detailed anatomical information of the lung parenchyma, pleura, and mediastinum. It often identifies alternative diagnoses explaining the patient’s symptoms even in the absence of detectable pulmonary artery embolism. In our cohort, some patients with a high clinical suspicion of PE but negative CTPA findings had imaging suggestive of alternative diagnoses, such as lobar consolidation, pleural effusion, or pulmonary tumors, thereby preventing misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment. Previous studies have similarly reported that in patients with SSPE, over 80% of CTPA scans reveal other abnormalities, such as pleural effusion, pulmonary inflammatory consolidation, or cardiomegaly, suggesting that some small emboli may be incidental findings, with symptoms primarily driven by other pathological processes (30). In comparison, while V/Q SPECT exhibits a high negative predictive value for excluding PE, its capacity to provide clues for alternative diagnoses is more limited. Unless the scan shows matched ventilation and perfusion defects (suggesting atelectasis or consolidation) or extensive perfusion abnormalities (indicative of altered pulmonary blood flow distribution), V/Q SPECT is less adept at pinpointing non-thrombotic etiologies (25). It is noteworthy that the ventilation component of V/Q SPECT can also reflect certain pulmonary physiological abnormalities. Guidelines indicate that diffuse matched ventilation-perfusion defects may signal pulmonary blood flow alterations due to conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or chronic heart failure (25). One patient in our study presented with V/Q SPECT findings of extensive bilateral lower lobe perfusion reduction with corresponding ventilation decrease. Combined with clinical and follow-up data, this led to a diagnosis of cardiogenic pulmonary congestion rather than PTE, demonstrating that V/Q SPECT can also offer insights into cardiopulmonary dysfunction in specific contexts. Thus, in the imaging diagnosis of PE, CTPA augments the ability for alternative diagnosis through its revelation of anatomical structural abnormalities, while V/Q SPECT, though primarily reflecting perfusion functional changes, retains clinical value in assessing pulmonary blood flow redistribution and certain non-PTE cardiopulmonary states. The two modalities can be complementary in specific scenarios, supporting an optimization of imaging strategy tailored to individual characteristics.

Our study further compared the clinical features and laboratory parameters of patients diagnosed with PTE via V/Q SPECT vs. CTPA, revealing systematic phenotypic differences associated with each modality. Patients undergoing V/Q SPECT typically presented with central or lobar large emboli and higher clinical risk features. In contrast, patients in the CTPA group predominantly manifested as a lower-risk phenotype with relatively milder biomarker elevations (31-33). Additionally, we noted that among patients with V/Q SPECT-positive but CTPA-negative results, some, despite lacking typical imaging manifestations, frequently exhibited concomitant lower limb thrombosis or a hypercoagulable state. This suggests that these abnormalities possess a biological basis and should not be simply dismissed as false positives. These results underscore the necessity of interpreting imaging findings within a clinical probability framework. Diagnostic confidence is highest when the pre-test probability aligns with the imaging result; discrepancies warrant further evaluation with laboratory markers and follow-up verification (34,35). Therefore, the choice of imaging modality not only influences detection sensitivity but also reflects the inherent risk stratum of the identified PE, necessitating integrated interpretation with clinical parameters to guide individualized management.

PTE directly impairs gas exchange by inducing ventilation-perfusion mismatch, leading to arterial hypoxemia. Comparing the oxygenation status, we found that patients in the V/Q SPECT pathway had significantly lower mean PaO2 levels than those in the CTPA group, reflecting a higher pathophysiological burden in the cohort selected for functional imaging. This aligns with literature noting that hypoxemia is prevalent in high-risk or intermediate-to-high-risk PTE patients, whereas oxygenation remains relatively preserved in low-risk cases (35). This discrepancy is attributed to a substantial reduction in gas exchange surface area and increased physiological shunt resulting from extensive perfusion blockage (36). Notably, while some cases identified via V/Q SPECT showed near-normal oxygenation (implying low-burden micro-emboli), the overall V/Q SPECT cohort in our study represented a more clinically compromised population, including isolated cases where V/Q SPECT revealed multiple subsegmental defects explaining significant hypoxemia despite negative CTPA findings. Thus, incorporating oxygenation indices aids in identifying high-risk patients and guiding management when imaging evidence is discordant.

However, isolated cases presented with significant hypoxemia despite negative CTPA, while V/Q SPECT revealed multiple subsegmental perfusion defects, supporting functional derangement due to pulmonary micro-embolism or microcirculatory disturbance. This concurs with prior observations; case reports describe PE patients presenting with unexplained hypoxemia, negative CTPA, but positive V/Q scan leading to diagnosis (37). Thus, the stratification of oxygenation function can provide risk context for divergent imaging results, suggesting that incorporating oxygenation indices can aid in identifying potentially high-risk patients and guiding further management strategies in situations with discordant imaging evidence or clinical-CT symptom mismatch.

Acute pulmonary embolism increases right ventricular afterload by obstructing the pulmonary circulation, precipitating right ventricular dilation and dysfunction. Comparing cardiac function parameters, we observed more pronounced signs of right heart impairment in the V/Q SPECT group compared to the CTPA group, evidenced by both echocardiography and myocardial biomarkers. Among patients assigned to the V/Q SPECT pathway, morphological abnormalities such as right ventricular enlargement and septal motion changes were more frequent, accompanied by significantly higher NT-proBNP and troponin I levels. This contrasts with some typical reports where large emboli are often prioritized for CTPA; however, in our cohort, the systematic selection of V/Q SPECT for patients with contraindications (such as renal impairment) likely clustered more severe cardiopulmonary strain into this group. UK multicenter prospective study reported median troponin I levels of approximately 100 ng/L in patients with main pulmonary artery embolism, far exceeding levels in those with subsegmental PE (38). In our cohort, nearly half of the CTPA-positive patients also showed mild-to-moderate elevations in cardiac enzymes, indicating that the right heart was subject to both mechanical strain and ischemic stress. In contrast, cardiac function indices in V/Q SPECT-positive but CTPA-negative patients largely remained within normal ranges, with echocardiography showing no right heart dilation or PH, suggesting minimal circulatory impact from micro-emboli. Cardiac function impairment is a core metric for assessing PTE severity and guiding therapeutic decisions. The moderate-to-large emboli typically revealed by CTPA are often associated with significant right heart strain, warranting risk classification as intermediate-high or high, necessitating close monitoring or even thrombolytic therapy (39). Conversely, emboli independently identified by V/Q SPECT are mostly small and peripherally distributed, imposing no substantive load on the right heart, conferring lower risk for adverse events, and potentially allowing for conservative management. Long-term observations support this distinction: some CTPA-positive patients exhibited persistent right heart structural abnormalities post-discharge, with potential progression to CTEPH, a phenomenon not observed in the V/Q SPECT-positive group. This is consistent with previous research indicating that CTEPH development is often associated with unresolved large emboli (40,41). Therefore, from a cardiac functional perspective, PE identified by CTPA tends to be associated with higher clinical risk, whereas V/Q SPECT often signals occult, low-burden lesions with limited pathophysiological impact. Incorporating cardiac function indicators aids in the clinical contextualization of imaging results and guides refined stratification of anticoagulation strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of this study introduces a significant selection bias (confounding by indication); since the choice between V/Q SPECT and CTPA was dictated by clinical factors—such as renal impairment, contrast allergy, or pregnancy—the observed differences in patient phenotypes may represent the selection criteria for the imaging modality rather than the inherent diagnostic performance of the tests themselves. Not all participants underwent both V/Q SPECT and CTPA; our comparative analysis primarily focused on patients who completed both examinations or had diagnoses confirmed via supplementary tests. This selection approach may underestimate the actual false-negative rates of each modality when used in isolation. Furthermore, due to ethical and feasibility constraints, we were unable to validate the veracity of all V/Q or CTPA abnormalities using an invasive reference standard such as digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Consequently, for cases with discordant findings between the two modalities, we relied predominantly on clinical follow-up and comprehensive judgment to infer their true status. This, to some extent, limits the definitiveness of our conclusions and warrants verification in future prospective studies. Second, patient enrollment in this study was primarily focused on hemodynamically stable cases. Acutely ill, high-risk, unstable patients, often unable to tolerate transport for imaging, mostly underwent bedside ultrasound and were managed based on clinical diagnosis; they were thus not included in this comparison. Therefore, our findings are primarily applicable to the hemodynamically stable subgroup, and the utility of both imaging modalities in patients with shock or massive PTE requires further investigation. Third, partial data were missing for some oxygenation and cardiac function indicators during comparative analyses, which may have affected the precision of these comparisons. Additionally, we did not perform quantitative scoring of perfusion defect extent on V/Q SPECT. Incorporating such quantitative metrics with prognostic analysis would lend greater persuasiveness to the conclusions, representing an area for improvement in future research.

Conclusions

This study, based on real-world inpatient data, systematically compared the clinical phenotypes, physiological functional impact, and risk profiles associated with PTE diagnoses established by V/Q SPECT vs. CTPA. The results demonstrate clear differences between the patient cohorts assigned to each imaging technique in terms of lesion distribution, clinical presentation, and cardiopulmonary strain levels. CTPA more frequently detects central or lobar pulmonary emboli, and the corresponding patient cohort tends to exhibit more severe oxygenation impairment and increased right heart strain, indicative of a higher clinical risk stratum. Conversely, V/Q SPECT more readily identifies peripheral or minute emboli, and its associated patient profile typically manifests as a low-risk phenotype with relatively limited cardiopulmonary functional impact. These findings suggest that the clinical choice of imaging modality serves as an indirect indicator of the patient’s pathophysiological burden, highlighting how clinical selection logic shapes the observed diagnostic landscape of pulmonary embolism.

Further analysis reveals a close interplay between imaging findings and clinical probability assessment, biomarkers, and cardiac function indices. Interpreting imaging discoveries within a comprehensive evaluation framework enhances diagnostic judgment accuracy and rationality. This study highlights that while imaging choice is inherently biased by patient baseline characteristics and contraindications, understanding these selection mechanisms is crucial for optimizing individualized diagnostic pathways, to optimize the imaging assessment pathway for PTE. Future research should focus on multi-center prospective studies to further validate the impact of different imaging strategies on clinical outcomes, thereby continually refining an evidence-based, individualized diagnostic decision-making system.
