Effectiveness of a psychoeducational intervention for people with suicidal behavior in the prison environment. N’VIU project: a randomized control trial
Authors: Quintí Foguet-Boreu, Manel Capdevila-Capdevila, Judit Pons-Baños, Lola Riesco- Miranda, Ana Sanjuan Torres, Marian Martínez García, Berta Framis-Ferrer, Saray Valdivieso Muñoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide rates are higher in prison environments than those in the general population. Therefore, preventive activities are crucial. We aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a psychoeducational group intervention in decreasing the number of suicidal behaviors in prisons.MethodsWe conducted a randomized controlled trial in nine prisons in Catalonia. Main outcome: total number of suicidal behaviors. Other variables: suicidal ideations, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and quality of life. The intervention group carried out 17 psychoeducation sessions and the control group received information.Results171 individuals participated in the study, mean age of 34.9 years, 84.8% males. The intervention group (n = 85) reduced anxiety and depression and increased quality of life post-intervention (p < 0.05), with no changes in the control group (n = 86). Both groups reduced all suicide items except for the number of interrupted/aborted attempts and the percentage of people with non-suicidal self-injurious behavior, which improved only in the intervention group. In both groups, the absolute number of suicide attempts decreased (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe psychoeducational intervention had an impact on suicidal ideation observed in both groups, with a non-significant reduction in the number of suicide attempts. Furthermore, the intervention improved levels of anxiety, depression, and quality of life.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on January 19, 2022 (NCT05195554).

Introduction

Suicidal behavior is a complex phenomenon that generates great concern in today’s society and constitutes a serious public health problem. According to the World Health Organization, suicide is one of the leading causes of death in prisons (World Health Organization, 2023). Worldwide, suicide rates in prison vary substantially, ranging from 10 (Croatia) to 180 suicides per 100,000 prisoners (Norway). Northern and Eastern European countries have the highest rates, with most of them exceeding 100 suicides per 100,000 prisoners (Fazel et al., 2017). In 2020, 626 suicide cases were reported in 35 countries cases (World Health Organization, 2023). The rate of suicide in prisons is significantly higher than the general population rate: seven times higher in remand prisoners and six times higher in sentenced prisoners (Rabe, 2012). In Catalonia (Spain), in 2020, the suicide rate for prisoners was 83 per 100,000 inmates, whereas the overall population rate is 7.2 per 100,000 inhabitants (Catalan Penitentiary Services, 2022; Statistical Institute of Catalonia 2022). The number of deaths in penitentiary centers in Catalonia has shown an upward trend in recent years, from seven in 2019 to 14 in 2022 (7.9 million inhabitants).

People in prison exhibit suicidal behaviors and thoughts throughout their lives. There are several factors that converge to suicide in remand prisoners: people who breach legislation often have various suicide risk factors associated with them (“import” the risk); and the suicide rate is higher among the group of people who have committed offences, even after release from prison. The risk factors for prisoners are broken down into those related to imprisonment (process, environment, and personnel), characteristics of the inmate, and history of self-injury. A recent systematic review analyzed risk factors for suicide in 35,351 cases in 27 countries and concluded that the five factors most strongly associated with the risk of suicide are: (a) suicidal ideation during the stay in prison, (b) previous suicide attempts, (c) history of self-injury, (d) occupation of a single cell, and (e) the presence of a current psychiatric diagnosis (Zhong et al., 2021). These findings underscore the responsibility of prison services in addressing and mitigating inmates’ suicidal behaviour. Moreover, these vulnerable people are given the option to receive treatment while in a closed regime. Being imprisoned is also a stressful event, even for healthy prisoners (World Health Organization, 2023; Cramer et al., 2017). Given the high prevalence of suicide, as well as the fact that it is a decision taken by the individuals themselves (and therefore preventable if detected and intervened in time), it is no wonder that recently, different programs and measures have been promoted to prevent suicide. General actions to prevent suicide in Catalan prisons were guided by the 1991 Program of Prevention and Intervention of Serious Self-Harming Behavior. However, more than a quarter of a century later, a review, assessment, and update on the question of suicide prevention was necessary.

In 2019, the prisons of Catalonia introduced the N’VIU Framework Program, a psychoeducational program for suicide prevention which stems from the need to ensure the life, integrity, and health of inmates with the aim of reducing mortality by suicide, increasing the survival of those treated for suicidal behavior, and preventing the repetition of suicide attempts. It is called N’VIU for two reasons: first, because it is a program that wishes to keep people alive (viu means ‘alive’ in Catalan); and second, because it aims to be a highly experiential program in which inmates actively participate in the sessions and acquire coping skills and strategies. In a pilot program carried out in 2020, the program was tested, some functional changes were made, and a new randomized control trial was designed to determine the efficacy of the program.

The scientific community shows that suicide can be prevented through strategies to promote mental health, early diagnosis, treatment, and identification of precipitating factors and protective actors. Suicide prevention in prison settings requires a comprehensive approach that addresses mental health promotion, early detection, and the identification of both risk and protective factors. Incarcerated individuals face disproportionately high suicide rates, often associated with psychiatric disorders, social isolation, and institutional stressors (Fazel et al., 2017). Systematic reviews have identified key risk factors such as prior self-harm, psychiatric morbidity, and lack of social support (Zhong et al., 2021; Favril et al., 2020), underscoring the need for tailored interventions. Evidence from randomised trials and systematic reviews supports the effectiveness of behavioural health interventions—particularly those based on cognitive-behavioural and dialectical behaviour therapy frameworks—in reducing suicidal ideation and self-harming behaviours among prison populations (Pratt et al., 2015; Winicov, 2019). The N’VIU program will be structured as a psychoeducational intervention for people at greater risk of suicide in prisons. Prisons must be open to these new preventive strategies, involving all agents in the institution with the aim of supporting people. To achieve this, we must begin talking openly and fight the stigma that it generates.

The main purpose of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a psychoeducational group intervention performed by rehabilitation professionals to decrease the number of suicidal behaviors in a penitentiary environment. Other secondary objectives were to: (a) show the impact on the severity of suicidal ideation; (b) determine how intervention affects symptoms of anxiety and depression; (c) describe the impact of intervention on quality of life; (d) check the feasibility of intervention in terms of participant satisfaction and adherence to it; and (e) identify whether intervention decreases problematic behaviors in prisons.

Methods

Study design and population

The study was a multicenter, randomized, controlled trial with two parallel groups, with blind assessment of the response variables and post-intervention. We used the SPIRIT guidelines (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) to implement the study protocol (Chan et al., 2013).

The study population comprised inmates of nine different prisons in Catalonia (Spain), a region of northeastern Spain with a global population of 7,722,203 inhabitants (2020 census) (Statistical Institute of Catalonia, 2022).The Catalan prison service consists of 13 prisons (closed regime and open custody measures) with a total population of 7,884 inmates (2020) (Catalan Penitentiary Services, 2022).

Eligibility criteria

We included all participants who presented with some kind of suicidal or high-risk behavior based on the criterion of self-directed violence according to RisCanvi. The risk assessment was conducted using the RisCanvi protocol, a validated instrument developed by the Catalan Department of Justice for evaluating the risk of violent and self-harming behavior in prison populations (Subdirectorate General for Rehabilitation Programs and Health, 2019). This instrument is used by penitentiary professionals, based on the individualized and structured assessment of a set of pre-established variables, to manage the probabilities of increased and decreased risk of prison inmates for further episodes of violent behavior. The full RisCanvi scale consists of 43 risk factors grouped into the following areas: criminological, personal, biographical, social, family, clinical, and personality. These 43 factors were added to the assessment of the future risk of the emergence of four behaviors: self-directed violence, intra-institutional violence, repeat violence, and prison-breaking. We excluded individuals with the following mental disorders: decompensation, intellectual disability, and cognitive impairment. In some cases of isolation, the management team was assessed individually to determine the suitability of participation in the program.

Interventions

The participants who met the inclusion criteria were divided into two groups:

intervention group and control group. The intervention group was involved in a psychoeducational intervention (N’VIU programme) and consisted of between ten and 12 participants led by two professionals, one of whom must be a prison psychologist, trained in the N’VIU program, supported by a second professional acting as co-therapist—either another psychologist or a social educator—ensuring continuity and therapeutic support throughout the intervention. In some sessions, other professionals such as psychiatrists, educators, and social workers, among others, were invited to participate, acting as external observers or leading the session. There were 17 sessions, with one or two sessions per week, with a duration of 90 min. Before the start of the psychoeducational intervention, these professionals received training that consisted of one in-person session and two online sessions, approximately 4.5 h per session; 13.5 h in total. The aim of the training was to provide specialized training on the phenomenon of suicide by two mental health specialists specialized in suicide (a psychologist and a mental health nurse) and training and guidance in the following areas: conduction and observation of groups; behavioral problems and activation techniques; improving skills in leading psychoeducational group sessions in these population groups; how to administer the different scales and evaluation instruments; and the purpose and methodology of the investigation. The N’VIU programme is a structured, multi-session intervention designed to address suicidal behaviour among incarcerated individuals. It begins with the establishment of group norms and a commitment to self-care, followed by psychoeducational modules that explore myths about suicide, its conceptualisation as a continuum, risk and protective factors, and the suicidal process using metaphors and warning signs. Subsequent sessions focus on emotional awareness and regulation, healthy lifestyle habits, and problem-solving skills. The programme also includes the development of a personalised safety plan and a “hope kit,” alongside training in help-seeking and self-protection strategies. It concludes with a survivor testimony and a closing session to reflect and consolidate learning (Table 1).

The possibility of telephone or video supervision was also offered to professionals who applied to the program to allow comments on the experiences, difficulties, or concerns that arose during the group interventions. This supervision was conducted by psychologists from the research team and experts leading psychoeducational groups.

Participants in the control group received information on suicide and advice if suicidal ideation had increased.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the total number of suicidal behaviors, including both suicide attempts and suicides, registered during the first year after inclusion in the study.

All instruments and cut-off points for psychometric scales used are detailed in the published protocol article. (Foguet-Boreu et al., 2022). The variables and instruments used are as follows: (i) Suicide risk was evaluated using the International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) (Sheehan et al., 1998); (ii) Severity of suicidal ideation was evaluated with The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) (Posner et al., 2011; Al-Halabí et al., 2016); (iii) Anxiety and depression were measured with the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HARS) and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) (Lobo et al., 2002; Bulbena et al., 2003); (iv) Quality of life was scored using the EuroQoL-5D Health Questionnaire (EQ-5D) (Badia et al., 1998). Other outcomes were the sociodemographic variables. The variables that form part of the Catalan Penitentiary Information System (CPIS) include personal variables, criminal variables, prison variables, and activity variables. Other recorded variables included the number of psychoeducational sessions performed by study participants, self-reported medical background, and self-reported medication. A survey on satisfaction with the psychoeducational group intervention was also administered at the end of the study.

Participant timeline

The researchers provided the following data for the data collection questionnaire: sociodemographic and medical variables, suicide risk and ideation, anxiety and depression scales, EQ-5D, and the number of suicide attempts at baseline. After the intervention period (three months later), participants were interviewed to collect all the variables listed above, except for the sociodemographic and medical variables. The study was conducted in two phases: a baseline assessment from August 1, 2021 to February 28, 2022, and a post-intervention evaluation from March 1, 2022 to September 30, 2022.

Sample size

Accepting an alpha risk of 0.05 and a beta risk of 0.95 in bilateral contrast, 74 subjects were required in each group to detect a difference in the effect size of 0.6, using a t-test for independent groups (Pratt et al., 2015). The sample was extended to 89 people in each group, owing to a predicted loss of 20%, with a total of 178 individuals.

Recruitment

Nine penitentiary centers participated in this study. In each center, pre-enrolment professionals made an initial proposal to include participants in the clinical trial who: (a) had been in the suicide attempt register in the previous year; (b) had been labelled in the prevention program as high or moderate suicide risk in the previous year; (c) had been reported in the RisCanvi instrument to have had an episode of risk of self-directed violence in the previous year; and (d) had participated in the suicide risk prevention program (low risk) in the previous year in the prison. Those previously selected were given an international neuropsychiatric MINI interview (Sheehan et al., 1998) All cases with a light and moderate risk were included in the study, and those with high risk of suicide were excluded and derived to psychiatric services from the prison for more intensive monitoring. Each prison recruited a minimum of 22 participants, with half assigned to the control group and the other half to the intervention group. Recruitment was simultaneously performed for each prison. Participants were randomly assigned to either the control or intervention group with 1:1 allocation based on computer-generated random numbers. This assignment was performed after the selection criteria were checked and the patient signed a consent form. Intention-to-treat analysis was also performed. All individuals who agreed to participate, signed an informed consent form, and underwent their first interview were included in the study.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistical analysis of the data was performed to evaluate homogeneity between the intervention and the control group for the variables studied. To evaluate the main objective (decreasing the number of suicidal behaviors), a multivariate analysis was performed. The number of suicidal behaviors was considered a dependent variable, and the group to which the participant belonged was considered an independent variable. Crude odds ratios (ORs) were calculated, and an analysis of paired data (Wilcoxon) was performed to determine the evolution of the dependent variables during follow-up. The difference between the baseline and follow-up values of the C-SSRS and the other scales was calculated, and Student’s t-test was used to compare the differences between variables of the two groups. The level of statistical significance used by the evidence of hypotheses was fixed at 5%, and the analysis was performed using the SPSS program for Windows, version 27.

Ethical issues

The Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Vic University Hospital approved the study Protocol No (Protocol No: 2021059) on November 20, 2021. An information sheet was designed for participants with comprehensible language, and a form for written informed consent was provided. At all times, data anonymity was guaranteed in accordance with national and international standards according to the Helsinki and Tokyo Declaration on Ethical Aspects and Standards of Good Practice in Clinical Research.

Results

A total of 171 individuals were included in the clinical trial, with a median age of 34.9 years (SD: 10.4), 84.8% of whom were male. They were distributed into 85 participants in the intervention group and 86 participants in the control group. Of the 209 inmates initially selected, 171 were included, yielding a participation rate of 81.8%,

Regarding judicial status, 80.0% of participants were serving sentences of five years or more, 12.7% between three and five years, and 7.3% less than three years. In terms of prison classification, 66.7% were assigned to second grade, 14.8% to first grade, 4.3% to third grade, and 4.2% were unclassified. Additional data beyond the judicial status presented here are available in the CEJFE’s evaluation report (Centre d’Estudis Jurídics i Formació Especialitzada, 2021).

In terms of sociodemographic characteristics, 64.0% of the sample had a primary education and lived mostly with their parents (32.3%) or with their families (30.5%). The most prevalent toxic habits were smoking (82.5%), cannabis (49.7%), and alcohol (20.5%). The most common self-referred mental disorders were substance use disorder (28.7%) and depressive disorder (27.5%). The most frequent non-psychiatric illness was infectious diseases (9.9%). The mean number of regular medications was 2.0 drugs (SD: 1.8). The mean score on the Hamilton Depression Scale was 14.0 (SD: 7.7) and on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale 17.9 (SD: 12.2). Quality of life was scored as 61.7 (SD: 23.7) (Table 2).

Session attendance among participants in the intervention group was generally high: 59.0% completed all 17 sessions of the program, while the remaining participants attended between 3 and 14 sessions.

In the results of the psychiatric rating scales comparing the baseline (pre-intervention) and post-intervention results, the intervention group showed a statistically significant reduction in anxiety (18.4 vs. 14.0, p = 0.022) and depression (14.5 vs. 10.2, p = 0.010) and a statistically significant increase in the quality of life score (62.0 vs. 67.9, p = 0.013. The control group did not experience statistically significant changes on any of the three scales (Table 4).

The MINI score did not show statistically significant differences in the pre-intervention and post-intervention scores in either the intervention group (6.29 vs. 5.49, p = 0.417) or in the control group (6.67 vs. 8.04, p = 0.364).

Among the odds ratios calculated for the items in Table 5, only the item “Had you thought about how you might do this? Had you started to work out or worked out the details of how to kill yourself? Did you intend to carry out this plan?” yielded a crude odds ratio of 0.430 (95% CI: 0.171–1.080), with an upper confidence limit close to 1, suggesting a potential effect that falls just short of statistical significance.

Discussion

The psychoeducational intervention had an impact in both groups on suicidal ideation and a trend towards a statistically nonsignificant reduction in the number of suicide attempts. In addition, the intervention group improved their health in terms of lower levels of anxiety, fewer depressive symptoms, and increased perceived quality of life after the intervention, while the control group did not change these parameters after the intervention.

Few studies have assessed the effectiveness of suicide intervention programs in prisons. Most studies on prisons have focused on the analysis of the prevalence of suicide and on the determination of the risk factors that lead to suicidal behavior (Favril et al., 2020, 2022). A recent review analyzed behavioral health interventions, including six studies with a range of participation from 9 to 76 individuals. Modalities of psychological interventions vary widely and include cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavioral therapy, peer prevention programs, staff intervention training, and uniquely designed courses that incorporate various aspects of other treatment modalities. However, the absence of a control group in most studies, the lack of relevant evaluation studies, and the inconsistency of behavioral outcome measurements compromise the conclusions of the review. Even so, the results suggested that cognitive behavioral therapy and uniquely tailored intervention programs could be effective in the prevention of suicidal behaviors (Winicov et al., 2019). Another study tested the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) for major depressive disorder among 181 prisoners of various US prisons. The intervention consisted of 20 90-minute group therapy sessions over ten weeks, with four individual sessions. The authors concluded that this program reduced depressive symptoms, hopelessness, and posttraumatic stress disorder symptoms and increased rates of major depressive disorder remission compared to the control group, with good cost-effectiveness results (Johnson et al., 2019). In addition, recent suicide prevention initiatives have been implemented in prison settings, such as the VigilanS program—an adaptation of a broader suicide prevention strategy originally developed for the French general population. In the prison context, VigilanS aims primarily to reduce the recurrence of suicidal behavior by fostering social connectedness (e.g., through letter exchanges) and facilitating contact with healthcare professionals. Unlike group-based psychoeducational interventions, VigilanS focuses on individual follow-up and post-crisis support (Eck et al., 2019).The fact that both groups showed a reduction in the total number of suicides after the intervention, although not statistically significant, could be partially explained by indirect exposure to the intervention within the highly contained prison environment. This fact can also be observed in some suicide ideation items. However, self-injury experienced an increase in absolute value in both groups (although this was not a statistically significant difference). The effects of the program would therefore spread beyond the intervention subjects. The results reinforce the well-known idea that talking about suicide does not lead to higher rates of suicide attempts (Dazzi et al., 2014).

Other studies have evaluated group-based interventions in prisons for severe mental disorders. One of these studies, conducted in the USA, showed an improvement in cognitive ability and motivation at discharge, and participants considered it very useful 88% of the time. The authors reported a decrease in psychopathology and psychiatric improvement after the program (Leidenfrost et al., 2017).

One of the possible explanations for why the program has failed to impact the main outcome of reducing the number of suicides could be explained by the fact that the sample is young (around 30 years old). Younger prisoners make more attempts, while suicidal ideation is more prevalent among older prisoners. Patterns of suicidal thinking and behavior differ with age. For example, the presence of physical illness is associated with suicidal history in younger prisoners, whereas in older prisoners it is mediated by depression (Stoliker et al., 2020). It is also worth noting that the overall number of suicide attempts in both groups was low, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant between-group differences.

Among the strengths of this study is its design; the evaluation of suicide prevention programs in correctional institutions is not common. This method allows for a higher level of evidence and makes it possible to determine the impact of an intervention. Another strength is the inclusion of 175 individuals in the randomized clinical trial, practically the same number as the sample size calculation indicated. The complexity of the penitentiary environment is high, with multiple changes in the penitentiary modules and permanence in prison, which makes follow-up difficult. In this sense, it should be noted that participation was quite high considering the kind of participants included (59%). One weakness is the low inclusion of women, which is due to the fact that the male/female ratio is 6:1. As a result, the impact of the intervention could have been different in this subgroup of prisoners. For reasons of confidentiality and the different organizational dependence of the health and prison systems, it was not possible to record the mental disorders presented by prisoners more objectively. Only self-reported information from the participants was considered. Moreover, the presence of mental disorders is an important risk factor for suicide (Favril et al., 2020). However, the mere presence of a self-reported diagnosis of a mental disorder confers a high risk (Favril, and O’Connor, 2021).

Suicide risk prevention programs are necessary to reduce suicide and self-harm among inmates. In line with the recommendations made by Biddle et al., based on their experience in prisons in northern England, we also believe that project implementation needs to have a clear focus, realistic budgets and timescales; be multicentered, well evaluated, and promote a commitment from prison staff; be implemented by professionals familiar with prisons; and facilitate a “champion role” (leaders who drive the projects forward) (Biddle et al., 2018).

Most countries have standardized protocols for identifying and assisting people at risk of suicide and/or self-harm (75% in the WHO European region) (World Health Organization, 2023).Programs are usually evaluated in a political sense, but there is no comprehensive evaluation of their effectiveness using quantitative methodology with a high level of evidence, for example with a randomised clinical trial-type methodology. Although the study did not reach statistical significance for its primary endpoint, the intervention was associated with substantial reductions in anxiety and depression levels—factors closely linked to suicidal ideation and behaviour. These improvements, along with the positive trends observed in the C-SSRS scores, suggest that the programme may have had a meaningful clinical impact.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial tested the effectiveness of a psychoeducational group intervention performed by rehabilitation professionals to decrease the number of suicidal behaviors in a penitentiary environment. The proposed program involves a multidisciplinary team of professionals in the fields of psychology, social work, psychiatry, and mental health nursing. The psychoeducational intervention had an impact on the intervention group and the control group, reducing suicidal ideation in a statistically significant way and absolute values of the number of suicides in both groups, albeit not significantly. In addition, the intervention group significantly improved their levels of anxiety, depression, and quality of life, parameters closely related to suicide.
