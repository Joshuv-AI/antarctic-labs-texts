Regional priorities in implementing forestation and wind energy as climate solutions in facing their trade-offs
Authors: Pengyi Zhang, Fen Gou, Zhaorong Zhu, Wei Liang, Han Zhang, Ziyan Li, Chao Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Both forestation and wind-energy upscaling are crucial climate solutions, but they may have conflicts as forestation can increase surface roughness and reduce wind speed. Here, based on coupled land-atmosphere simulations with contrasting forest areas, we report a notable trade-off between wind energy and carbon sequestration across a range of potential forestation areas. A maximum global forestation area of 1508 Mha is estimated to enable a biomass carbon sequestration of 90.7 GtC but also contribute to a 19.5% reduction in global onshore wind energy technical potential. The strength of such carbon-energy trade-offs, however, differs among different regions. Tropics could provide large forestation-induced carbon sequestration but have a small loss in wind energy potential. Large-scale forestation in Europe and US mainland may diminish not only wind energy potential but also energy generation of their current wind installations. Our results highlight the importance of maximizing the collective benefits of potentially competing climate solutions.

Introduction

The UN Climate Change Conference (COP28) urges Parties to take actions aimed at achieving the objectives of the Paris Agreement, aiming for attaining net-zero carbon dioxide emissions by 2050 and limiting global warming to 1.5 °C1. In particular, it reaffirmed the critical role of renewable energy in meeting the objectives of the Paris Agreement and in advancing the global move towards energy systems free of fossil fuels. In addition, COP28 delivered a global agreement to accelerate efforts towards the phase-down of coal power, phasing out inefficient fossil fuel subsidies, a tripling of renewable energy capacity and doubling energy efficiency by 20301. This decision implies an accelerated transition in energy systems with rapid upscaling of bioenergy and renewable energy, including solar, wind, hydro and geothermal energy2. Wind energy is expected to play a key role in this transition. The global installed capacity for wind energy has grown from 181 GW in 2010 to 733 GW in 20203, covering 5.9% of global electricity demand in 20204. Looking ahead, it is projected that by 2050 the global installed wind-energy capacity will increase by a factor of 3 to 5, to 2870–5806 GW, with wind energy contributing 18%–36% of the projected global electricity demand then5,6.

On the other hand, realizing the 1.5 °C target also necessitates large-scale deployment of carbon-dioxide removal technologies, including carbon capture and storage (CCS) and afforestation and/or reforestation2,7. In this study, based on a literature review (Supplementary Table 1), afforestation refers to the establishment of forests, whether naturally or artificially, on lands not recently forested, and reforestation refers to the re-establishment of forests, whether naturally or artificially, on lands that were historically forested. According to the Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (INDCs) to implement the Paris Agreement, 25% of planned emissions reductions globally will be realized by land use and, in particular, by forests8. Forestation, here including both afforestation and reforestation but excluding enhancing carbon storage of existing forests, has been identified as a leading nature-based climate solution9,10, due to its potential for carbon sequestration11 and the multiple co-benefits of biodiversity conservation, soil protection, hydrological regulation, and other ecosystem services12,13. Although forest protection—such as by halting wood production in currently managed forests—can increase carbon storage, we deliberately exclude this option from our study as large-scale, global cessation of wood production appears implausible given rising global wood demand (see “Methods”).

However, the dual climate solutions of wind-energy upscaling and large-scale forestation may have internal conflicts, as boundary-layer climate processes dictate that forestation will increase surface roughness and reduce wind speed14. Although there is some controversy surrounding the exact mechanisms15, widespread surface wind speed reductions in the northern hemisphere around the turn of the 21st century have been partly ascribed to increases in surface roughness due to vegetation restoration16. Therefore, there may be potential trade-offs between wind energy and the amount of carbon that can be sequestered through forestation. To the best of our knowledge, however, such trade-offs have not been quantified, nor accounted for in the current Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs) used to lay out future deployment of forestation and wind energy for different regions across the globe.

This study used coupled land-atmosphere simulations from eight Earth System Models (ESMs) from the Land Use Model Intercomparison Project (LUMIP, a contributory project to CMIP617) to investigate local surface wind speed changes in response to potential forest expansion. Potential trade-offs between wind-energy technical potential and biomass carbon sequestration were then examined using different forestation scenarios. The implications of future forestation on global onshore wind installations, and the potential for technological development in the wind turbine fleet to offset the resulting reductions in wind energy, were also explored. For method details, please refer to the “Methods” section. The results show a clear trade-off between wind energy and forestation-induced carbon sequestration that must be accounted for in formulating future climate mitigation plans when both wind-energy upscaling and forestation are to be used as climate solutions.

Results

Trade-off between wind-energy technical potential and carbon sequestration

To examine the relationship between local wind-energy potential and carbon sequestration through worldwide forest expansion, four different forestation scenarios are designed (S1 to S4). Additionally, five auxiliary scenarios are derived by interpolating from the four main scenarios (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Under the maximum forestation scenario (S4), all locations with potential forest distribution, except for those currently occupied by forest, cropland, or human settlement (i.e., urban land), are allowed to be forested. Moving through the scenarios from S4 to S1, the forestation area becomes progressively smaller with the gradual introduction of more constraints based on increasingly realistic considerations. In S3, pastures were excluded from forestation for safeguarding the provision of livestock products (e.g., milk and meat). Then, in S2, boreal regions are further excluded to avoid unintended climate warming by forestation-induced surface albedo reduction in these regions18. Our analysis suggests increasing biomass carbon sequestration potential as the forestation area increases across different forestation scenarios (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). The biomass carbon uptake is projected to increase from 43.8 Gt C under the minimum forestation scenario S1 to 90.7 Gt C under the maximum forestation scenario S4, when the forestation area increases from 709.9 Mha (Mha = 106 ha) to 1508.5 Mha. This corresponds to an increase of 17.5%–37.1% in current forest area and could store an equivalent of 5.2%–12.8% of the current atmospheric carbon pool. However, on the other hand, mean annual global surface wind speed is projected to decrease with increasing forestation area, from −0.017 m s-1 under the minimum forestation scenario (S1) to −0.038 m s-1 under the maximum forestation scenario (S4) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The decrease in the wind energy capacity factor (CF) ranges from −0.007 (the minimum forestation scenario, S1) to −0.015 (the maximum forestation scenario, S4) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The technical potential indicates the annual amount of electricity that could be generated over the land area suitable for wind turbine installation under the limitations of turbine technology, turbine spacing and system loss (see “Methods”). Correspondingly, a larger decline in wind-energy technical potential is also found with increasing forestation area, rising from a 50.0 PWh (PWh = 1015 Wh) (10.6%) decline under S1 to a substantial decline of 87.3 PWh (19.5%) under S4 (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 4). Hence, a notable trade-off relationship exists between wind energy technical potential and forestation-induced carbon sequestration potential (Fig. 1c).

The spatial patterns of forestation-induced biomass carbon sequestration potential and wind-energy technical potential loss largely follow those of forestation potential (Fig. 2b, c). However, there are some exceptions. Eastern and Southeast Asia exhibit reasonable forestation potential, but show a high carbon sequestration potential (likely because of their high biomass carbon stock density) and surprisingly low levels of wind-energy technical potential loss. Similar patterns are also present for South America, where pronounced carbon sequestration is found, corresponding to large areas of forestation potential but with a relatively low wind-energy technical potential loss. In contrast, for the US mainland, notable areas of forestation and carbon sequestration potential are accompanied by a pronounced wind-energy loss.

The clear carbon-energy trade-off reported in this study calls for prioritizing forestation activities among different regions based on the different strengths of the trade-off relationship. We propose a framework aimed at maximizing carbon sequestration potential while minimizing total wind-energy technical potential reduction. The arctangent of the ratio between forestation-induced total wind-energy technical potential loss and carbon sequestration potential is used to measure the forestation efficiency as a potential climate solution while accounting for the carbon-energy trade-off (Fig. 2d–f). Forestation efficiency represents the reduction in wind energy per unit of carbon sequestration achieved through forestation (a negative number). A higher efficiency indicates a greater chance for the co-success of the dual climate solutions of wind-energy upscaling and forestation, and hence assigns a higher priority to the deployment of forestation to a given region (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Of the thirteen regions of the globe, Southeast Asia exhibits exceptional efficiency, with a large amount of biomass carbon gain (15.4 Gt C) but a negligible reduction in wind energy technical potential (−1.1 PWh) (Fig. 2d). In fact, the top five regions in terms of high forestation efficiency, i.e., Southeast Asia, South Asia (−0.7 PWh vs. 2.0 Gt C), Eastern Asia (−2.2 PWh vs. 4.8 Gt C), Central America (−1.6 PWh vs. 2.7 Gt C), and South America (−17.5 PWh vs. 29.0 Gt C), together provide 56.2% of the global total biomass carbon sink potential. These regions should be prioritized when deploying the dual climate solutions of wind energy and forestation. In contrast, Central Asia (−2.8 PWh vs. 0.8 Gt C) and Canada and Alaska (−6.1 PWh vs. 2.5 Gt C) are regions where a strong carbon-energy trade-off exists, and caution should be taken when implementing forestation-based climate solutions, or a refined evaluation of forestation and wind farm location should be performed. The same caution should be applied for Russia, Europe and the US mainland, which have relatively large values of carbon sequestration potential (3.1 Gt C; 3.9 Gt C; 6.5 Gt C), but also the large reduction in wind-energy technical potential (−7.2 PWh; −6.1 PWh; −11.6 PWh). The reduction in wind-energy technical potential in South America (−17.5 PWh) and Tropical Africa (−12.1 PWh) is the greatest and second greatest of all the regions, but they remain comparatively high forestation efficiency because of their extremely large carbon sequestration potential.

Loss in energy generation for current onshore wind installations

Forestation-induced reduction in local surface wind speed and the consequential loss of wind-energy technical potential reported in this study also suggest unavoidable reductions in energy generation for currently installed onshore wind turbines. Current onshore wind installations are widely distributed globally but their density is greatest in Eastern Asia, Europe, the US mainland, and South Asia. However, under the minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation scenarios, the global average CF of onshore wind installations falls to 0.267 and 0.251, or by 4.0% and 9.7%, respectively (Fig. 3b, c). Of the thirteen regions of the globe, Eastern Asia, Canada and Alaska, and South Asia all show less than 5% reduction in CF under both minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation potential (Fig. 3b, c, and T1 under S1 and S4 scenarios in Fig. 4). In contrast, South America, Australia, Central Asia, Tropical Africa and the US mainland all show greater than 5% reductions in CF either under the minimum forestation scenario (S1) or the maximum forestation scenario (S4). Europe, Central America, North Africa and the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and Russia exhibit less than 5% reductions in CF under the minimum forestation scenario (S1) but greater than 5% reductions under the maximum forestation scenario (S4).

Potential changes in annual wind-energy generation for current onshore wind installations are further estimated by multiplying regional wind-energy installation capacity by the change in CF and the number of hours in a year (8760 hours) (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The current global wind-energy generation, without further forestation for a given total installation capacity of 838.1 GW, is estimated at 2079.3 TWh (TWh = 1012 Wh). However, under the minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation scenarios, global wind-energy generation is projected to decline by 77.7 TWh (3.7%) and 171.7 TWh (8.2%), respectively (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 3).

Of the thirteen regions of the globe, Eastern Asia, although it has the highest installation capacity of all regions (340.3 GW), exhibits the lowest wind-energy generation loss due to potential forestation (−8.1 TWh for S1; −13.9 TWh for S4), due to the small reduction in CF. In contrast, in Europe and the US mainland, where the installation capacity ranks second (225.2 GW) and third (144.2 GW), respectively, there is a substantial reduction in wind-energy generation. In Europe the values are −11.9 TWh for S1 and −81.1 TWh for S4 while in the US mainland they are −22.8 TWh for S1 and −28.0 TWh for S4, consistent with the strong carbon-energy trade-off for potential forestation in these regions (Fig. 2d). In addition, there is a notable reduction in wind-energy generation in South America (−24.6 TWh for S1, −34.2 TWh for S4) which is mainly due to a large reduction in CF (−23.6% under S1 and −32.7% under S4). The discrepancy between the large reductions in wind-energy generation by currently installed wind turbines and a low carbon-energy trade-off strength in South America (Fig. 2d) is mainly because current onshore wind installations there tend to be located in coastal areas subject to large reductions in CF (Fig. 3b, c). In other regions, forestation-caused reductions in wind-energy generation for currently installed wind turbines are negligible, with reductions no greater than 10.0 TWh even under the maximum forestation scenario (S4).

Influence of potential technological improvements on the global wind turbine fleet

The energy loss in current onshore wind turbine installation sites following forestation implies that to maintain their current levels of energy generation, technological improvements in wind turbines are necessary. To assess to what extent the negative impacts of forestation could be offset by wind turbine technological improvements, the CF of current onshore installation sites under two forestation scenarios of S1 and S4 is further evaluated using two projected pessimistic (T2) and optimistic (T4) technological development scenarios developed in a recent study25 (see “Methods”). When the whole globe is considered, both the pessimistic (T2) and optimistic (T4) wind turbine developments enable the post-forestation mean CF of global onshore wind installation sites to recover to or exceed the current CF level, irrespective of the forestation scenario. More specifically, under the minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation scenarios, the pessimistic technological development scenario T2 is projected to increase the global mean CF of current wind installation sites to reach 0.298 and 0.281, respectively, exceeding the present CF value (0.278) with no forestation. The optimistic technological development scenario T4 is projected to increase the global CF even further, to reach 0.373 and 0.353, under the S1 and S4 forestation scenarios, respectively, approximately 1.35 and 1.27 times of the present CF value. These results indicate that, when considering the global average value, wind turbine technology improvements of current installation sites could largely compensate for the forestation-induced energy loss.

As for regional scale, all the thirteen regions, except South America, exhibit higher CF with the optimistic technological development scenario T4 than the present CF value (current wind turbine technology with no forestation), irrespective of the forestation scenario (Fig. 4). For South America (Fig. 4m) with the optimistic T4 scenario, the CF value under the minimum (S1) forestation scenario (0.295) could exceed the present CF (0.273), but the maximum (S4) forestation scenario is projected to reduce the CF to a level (0.258) below the present CF. For the same continent but with the pessimistic technological scenarios (T2), irrespective of the forestation scenario, the CF values (0.233 and 0.204 for S1 and S4, respectively) are projected to be both lower than the present CF.

Given that future wind installations may not follow the spatial distribution of existing sites and that future regional wind energy installation capacity remains uncertain, we further assess the influence of wind turbine technological improvements on wind-energy technical potential for the globe and for different regions (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similar to the influence of technological improvements on the CF of current onshore installation sites, both the pessimistic (T2) and optimistic (T4) technological development enable the post-forestation global total wind-energy technical potential to recover to or exceed the pre-forestation level. Under the minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation scenarios, global total wind-energy technical potential with pessimistic technological development (T2) is projected to reach 735.3 PWh and 684.3.1 PWh, respectively, both exceeding the pre-forestation level (596.9 PWh). The global total wind-energy technical potential with optimistic technological development (T4) under the minimum (S1) and maximum (S4) forestation scenarios could even reach 2003.3 PWh and 1863.7 PWh, respectively.

At the regional scale, the forestation-induced loss in wind-energy technical potential could be compensated for by the pessimistic technological development scenario T2 for almost all regions, irrespective of the forestation scenario. Exceptions are Europe, the US mainland, Southeast Asia, South America, and Central America, where the wind energy technical potential loss under the maximum forestation (S4) could only be compensated by the optimistic T4 scenario but not the pessimistic T2 one. In addition, for South America, the wind-energy technical potential under the minimum forestation (S1) and pessimistic technological development (T2) scenario is also projected to be below its pre-forestation level.

Discussion

Our forestation area for the S1 scenario (709.9 Mha), which accounts for food security, local biophysical warming, and biodiversity conservation, is consistent with the estimate (678.0 Mha) made in previous work9 with similar considerations. Our estimated living biomass carbon sequestration potential of 90.7 Gt C with a maximum forestation area of 1508.5 Mha (S4), is consistent with previous estimates. Mo et al.19 reported a carbon sequestration potential in living tree biomass of 91.4 Gt C when existing forest, urban and cropland areas were excluded from forestation, similar to our scenario of S4. Our study and the previous studies all indicate substantial biomass carbon sequestration potential dependent on forestation area. But our results go a step further to suggest clear trade-offs between wind-energy potential and forestation-induced carbon sequestration.

Our reported forestation-induced reductions in surface wind speed and the associated reductions in wind energy are both of meaningful magnitudes. Previous studies have reported pronounced surface wind stilling since the 1980s, possibly in response to vegetation thickening or decadal ocean-atmosphere oscillations15,16, with the reduction in global surface wind speed reaching −0.26 m s-1 over 1978–201015. Our results indicate a surface wind speed reduction in forestation areas of 0.21–0.27 m s-1 (Supplementary Fig. 7) in response to future forestation: a value of comparable magnitude to previously reported surface wind stilling. This result highlights the important impact of future forestation on surface wind speed through increasing land surface roughness20 and by altering regional-scale circulation driven by orography, land-sea contrasts, and thermally-driven circulation contrasts between the open land and forested area21,22. Although global surface wind speed recovered after 2010, increasing at a rate of 0.24 m s−1 per decade, such a recovery may not be sustained in forestation areas because it’s driven by ocean-atmosphere circulations23. Furthermore, our results show that the positive effect of recent wind speed recovery on wind energy could be canceled out by future forestation.

On the other hand, surface wind speed is projected to decrease over the northern hemisphere, being partly offset by slight increases in the southern hemisphere under mid-to-high anthropogenic warming in the future23,24. Nonetheless, the global average CF of current onshore wind installations is projected to decrease by 0.005–0.010 between 2020 and 2060 in response to different extents of anthropogenic climate warming25. We find a reduction of 0.007–0.015 in the CF of current onshore wind installations caused by future forestation, a reduction comparable with, or even exceeding, the CF reduction caused by the worst climate warming. At the same time, the reductions in global onshore wind-energy technical potential range between −10.6% under our minimum forestation scenario and −19.5% under our maximum forestation scenario, both of which exceed the reduction in global onshore wind energy (−4.1%) under future climate change corresponding to mid-to-high level warming (RCP6.0) projected by a recent study26. However, technological improvements in the wind turbine fleet could offset the reductions in wind energy caused by either climate change25 or forestation.

The trade-off between forestation-induced carbon sequestration and reduced wind energy potential implies that mitigating forestation’s negative impact on wind power requires either deploying more turbines or advancing turbine technology, as shown by our analysis. Since wind energy avoids CO2 emissions that could otherwise occur from fossil fuel generation of the same electricity5, forestation-induced wind slowdown indirectly diminishes this emissions-avoidance benefit, thereby partly offsetting the carbon removal gained from forestation. A rough calculation (Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Table 4) indicates that the resulting loss of avoided CO2 emissions could offset only 3–6% of forestation’s carbon uptake under today’s global wind capacity (6.2% of electricity generation27). However, if installed wind capacity grows elevenfold by 2050—as projected in the IEA Net Zero roadmap, when wind could supply ~30% of global electricity28 the offset rises to approximately 20%.

For tropical regions with high forestation efficiency and substantial biomass carbon density potential, the substantial biomass carbon created by forestation in these regions could partly offset the associated wind-energy reduction. These regions should therefore be prioritized for integrated climate strategies that combine wind energy development with forestation. For regions with substantial installed wind-energy capacity and relatively low forestation efficiency, particularly Europe and the United States, widespread forestation may already be reducing wind power generation by increasing surface roughness. Forestation projects in these regions should explicitly consider potential spatial overlap between wind installations and forest expansion, ensuring that new forests do not compromise the wind energy resource of existing or planned turbines. Likewise, strategic placement of wind turbines in locations of low forest cover could effectively mitigate the wind energy reduction arising from increased surface roughness.

The intricate but critical interaction whereby forestation reduces wind energy potential and, in turn, diminishes wind generation partly offsets forestation’s carbon-sequestration benefit arises from complex Earth-system feedback processes that must be incorporated into climate mitigation policy. Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs), which simulate energy–economy–land–climate interactions to design pathways that limit warming well below 2 °C, consistently project both massive forestation (500–1000 Mha) and a roughly tenfold increase in wind capacity—scales comparable to those examined here—as essential measures. Yet, to our knowledge, current IAMs do not account for the negative forestation–wind interaction identified in this study. Our findings, therefore, underscore the urgent need to enhance IAMs by including such trade-offs and synergies29, potentially through better representation of Earth-system feedbacks, to improve the reliability of future mitigation pathways. Concurrently, future Earth-system model research should explore not only the impacts of land-based mitigation on wind resources but also the reciprocal effects of large-scale wind deployment on forest dynamics and surface–atmosphere exchanges30,31.

Several potential caveats in our analysis should be kept in mind when using our results for the optimization of forestation activities and wind-energy upscaling across the globe. First, the forestation-induced changes in surface wind speed were calculated by using coupled climate model simulations, which assume climate conditions in equilibrium with contrasting forest areas. Consequently, the forestation-caused wind-energy changes assessed here represent the biophysical effects of a fully developed forest, which may take years to decades after stand establishment. In addition, the biophysical effects of forest protection (allowing existing forests to restore to a mature or old-growth state) are not included in our study. Basic forest ecophysiology indicates that forest protection or releasing currently managed forests by halting rotational wood harvest could enable recovery of tree height, leaf area index and biomass32, thereby increasing surface roughness33 and further reducing near-surface wind speed. However, these biogeophysical effects are likely incremental compared to those from establishing new forests. Indeed, rare modeling studies investigating the biogeophysical effects of current global wood harvest reveal only minor, nearly negligible impacts on surface roughness34 or land surface temperature35, relative to deforestation effects. Moreover, although several studies explored the potential of carbon removal through halting wood harvest36,37, this approach conflicts with rising global wood demand38,39, making widespread harvest cessation an unlikely large-scale strategy for enhancing terrestrial carbon storage. We acknowledge that this study focuses on carbon storage as the primary metric for evaluating forest ecosystem benefits, without explicitly assessing other important services such as biodiversity conservation, habitat provision, or soil protection—benefits that forest protection can also deliver. These aspects fall outside the scope of our study and warrant investigation in future research.

Second, widespread forestation might alter atmospheric circulation patterns at mesoscales or larger, thereby affecting the wind speed of non-forestation areas22. However, our results show that wind speed change in grid cells without forest change, i.e., the nonlocal effect, has been largely insignificant (Supplementary Fig. 8). Hence, in this study, our methodology focused on the local surface wind effect of forestation and ignored the nonlocal effect. It should be noted, however, that the local surface wind effect of forestation simulated by ESMs reflects the well-mixed conditions of the lower atmosphere, and hence applies over the whole grid cell rather than being differentiated among vegetation types within it. It is thus reasonable to apply the simulated wind speed to wind installations in forestation grid cells that still contain some non-forest areas suitable for wind turbine installation. Third, wind energy was estimated on a monthly time scale, being limited by the temporal resolution of the LUMIP model output, whereas in reality, wind speed fluctuates at much higher temporal frequencies. Although using monthly wind speed leads to an underestimation of wind energy compared to using hourly wind speed (Supplementary Fig. 9), which is in line with previous reports24,40, the reported reductions in wind-energy potential as well as the relative strengths of carbon-energy trade-offs are highly robust and independent of the temporal resolution of the wind speed (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Fourth, although previous studies24 showed that using coarse-grid wind speed data yields wind-power estimates comparable with those obtained by using site-level wind speed data, our results are suboptimal for direct application to carbon-energy optimization on a local scale. Instead, we argue that deploying both forestation and wind energy on a local to regional scale needs careful planning. Fifth, although climate model simulations using preindustrial atmospheric CO2 were used, we show that the derived sensitivity of wind speed to forest cover change and the associated changes in wind energy following different forestation scenarios, are very close under preindustrial climate (including CO2 concentration) and the SSP1-2.6 future climate (see “Methods,” Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). This increases the relevance of our results for future climate scenario where large-scale employment of forestation and wind energy are mostly likely, because the SSP1-2.6 scenario was designed for future warming below 2 °C which is consistent with the goal of Paris Agreement41.

Despite these limitations, our results provide a benchmark and an overarching framework for managing potential carbon-energy conflicts when deploying forestation for global climate mitigation. Although on a global scale, the reported carbon-energy trade-off seems an unavoidable constraint, regional opportunities remain when such trade-offs are less strong. Primarily, tropical regions, including Southeast Asia, South Asia, Eastern Asia, Central America, and South America (56.2% of the global total), experience relatively small decreases in wind-energy technical potential. In contrast, for Europe, the US mainland and Australia, large-scale forestation could not only reduce wind-energy technical potential but also reduce the amount of energy generated by currently installed wind turbines. Forestation in these regions is thus suboptimal, and science-based planning is essential. The wind energy infrastructure, such as road construction, turbine installation, and operational noise, can have negative effects on wildlife, including birds, even in unprotected regions42. The rollout of renewable energy infrastructure required to meet climate objectives should proceed in a way that does not present a major threat to local conservation efforts, either within or outside protected regions. We also stress that a holistic approach, incorporating careful planning at both regional and local scales, is essential in international climate mitigation efforts to maximize the collective benefits of diverse climate solutions.

Methods

An overview of the procedure and how the various datasets and analysis steps are connected is shown in Supplementary Fig. 13.

Potential forestation area under different forestation scenarios

We conducted an extensive literature review to define key relevant terms related to forestation—afforestation, reforestation and forest restoration (Supplementary Table 1). The review examined about 40 usages of these three terms in academic journals and reports from international organizations (e.g., FAO, UNFCCC). Based on the review, in this study, “afforestation” refers to the establishment of forest (naturally or artificially) on land that has not recently been forested but where local climate and soil conditions can support forest growth, and “reforestation” refers to the re-establishment of forest (naturally or artificially) on land that was historically forested and where local climate and soil conditions still support forest growth. Thus, both afforestation and reforestation involve converting land from an unforested to a forested state and do not necessarily entail establishing monoculture plantations.

Following the mainstream of the literature (Supplementary Table 1), we define “forestation” as encompassing both afforestation and reforestation. Given that “forest restoration” carries multiple meanings—including (a) recovering lost forest (i.e., reforestation)43, (b) increasing tree cover regardless of land-use history (thus including afforestation44, (c) restoring degraded forests to their original undisturbed state45, and (d) enhancing ecological integrity and diversity of an existing forest by recovering native species composition46, we excluded it from “forestation.” We emphasize that forestation here does not imply unmanaged, single-species, or unguided planting; rather, we strongly advocate careful, science-based planning and management to ensure positive ecological outcomes and prevent unintended long-term harm. This could be realized through, for example, forest establishment guided by ecological suitability and effective management practices, including planting native species, promoting mixed-species forests and maintaining proper spacing.

Although several studies explore the potential of carbon removal by increasing carbon stock in existing forests—primarily through halting wood harvest36,37—this approach conflicts with rising global wood demand38,39, making widespread harvest cessation an unlikely large-scale solution for enhancing terrestrial carbon storage. Moreover, site-level quantitative studies on how enhancing carbon stocks of existing forests (i.e., post-harvest recovery) affects local surface wind speed are scarce, and idealized simulations of large-scale wood harvest impacts on wind speed remain absent (see the introduction about LUMIP below), due in part to inadequate representation of harvest processes in current models34,35. Accordingly, this study excludes carbon-stock enhancement in existing forests and focuses solely on forestation, defined as the expansion of forest area.

To obtain the spatial distribution of potential forestation areas, we used global potential tree cover distribution under current climate and environmental conditions as predicted by a recent study47 (Supplementary Fig. 14). In that study, tree-cover measurements were made using direct photo interpretation in globally distributed, protected nature reserves, where vegetation is considered little affected by human activity, and hence “potential” tree cover as determined by environmental conditions is assumed to be reached. With tree cover measurements spanning the entire range of environmental and climatic gradients, potential tree cover was predicted using random forest modeling for each 30-arcsec (~ 1 km) pixel across the world. In our study, pixels that could be potentially forested are defined as those with a potential tree cover >25%, consistent with the definition used in previous studies48. We assume that this approach maps areas suitable for forest growth by including both places where forests have historically been distributed, and where recent changes in climate and other environmental conditions make it possible for forests to grow. Fire disturbance, which is reported as critical for maintaining low tree cover in savanna biomes where forests are climatically suitable49, was not included in Bastin et al.35 for predicting tree cover, thus likely resulting in an overestimation of potential forest area. But on the other hand, widespread woody encroachment in African dryland over the past decades was reported in response to changes in precipitation, population and burned area50, reflecting the possibility of forest distribution. Nonetheless, we urge that active afforestation should be avoided at places that are naturally unsuitable for forest growth, for example, the savanna biomes that are critical to maintain biodiversity, including many animals.

Spatially explicit information about existing land cover types was used to preclude certain land cover types (e.g., cropland and urban land) from potential forestation opportunities and to design different forestation scenarios (detailed below). The current distribution of forest, agricultural, grazing, and urban land (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Table 5) was retrieved from the ESA CCI land cover dataset. The ESA CCI product was re-gridded from 300-m resolution to 1-km according to the dominant land cover type (i.e., the most frequent land cover type) of all 300-m pixels within a 1-km grid cell. For grassland, a distinction was made between more intensively managed “pasture” and extensively managed “rangeland” (Supplementary Fig. 16), following a similar method to that used in the widely adopted HYDE product51. Specifically, grassland is defined as rangeland if one of two criteria is met: (1) the aridity index (calculated as annual precipitation divided by annual potential evapotranspiration) is below 0.5; (2) the aridity index exceeds 0.5, but the population density is less than 5 inhabitants per km2. Otherwise, grassland is considered pasture.

Forestation scenarios are subject to constraints regarding the source land cover type, with various considerations of food security, net biophysical warming, and biodiversity conservation9,12. Four different forestation scenarios were designed, ranging from potential forestation with the maximum constraint (the S1 scenario, corresponding to the smallest forestation area) to that with the minimum constraint (the S4 scenario, corresponding to the largest forestation area). More specifically, the maximum forestation scenario (S4) assumes forestation of all areas of potential forest distribution, excluding those currently occupied by existing forests, cropland, or human settlements. In this scenario, it is assumed that all grassland (pasture and rangeland) can be reforested. In the S3 scenario, forestation over pasture was excluded, to ensure the provision of livestock products such as meat and milk. In Scenario S2, boreal regions obtained from a climate-zone dataset9 (Supplementary Fig. 17) were additionally excluded from forestation, to avoid the less advantageous or even detrimental biophysical warming effect of forestation at high latitudes, which is caused by the associated surface albedo reduction52. Under the minimum forestation scenario of S1, currently protected nature reserve areas, as retrieved from the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA) (Supplementary Fig. 18), were additionally excluded from forestation for the purpose of biodiversity conservation.

To ensure consistency in spatial resolution with the surface wind speed data (detailed below), all forestation scenarios were aggregated to 0.25° resolution, with the value for each grid cell indicating the fraction of ground area suitable for forestation. To better visualize the relationship between forestation-induced wind-energy change and carbon sequestration potential, five additional auxiliary forestation scenarios (V1 to V5) were interpolated using S2 and S3, and S3 and S4, respectively. This procedure resulted in equal intervals of global forestation area of approximately 100 Mha (106 ha) between each consecutive forestation scenario (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Forestation-induced changes in local surface wind speed derived using multiple coupled climate models

Multiple coupled climate model simulations were used to derive forestation-induced changes in local surface wind speed at a height of 10 m. To diagnose the sensitivity of surface wind speed to forest coverage (i.e., the percentage of ground area covered by forest), paired simulations with different global forest areas are needed. For this purpose, the piControl simulation of CMIP6 was used as a forestation scenario53, and the idealized global deforestation simulation (“deforest-glob”) of the LUMIP was used as a reference scenario. In part due to inadequate model representation, idealized simulations of forest wood harvest were not included in LUMIP, preventing us from using it for investigating harvest impacts on wind speed. The piControl simulation is a long-term (≥500 years) fully coupled simulation53. It was forced with a background volcanic aerosol close to the mean value for 1850–2014 and a solar constant fixed at its mean value for 1850–1873. Orbital parameters, atmospheric composition (including CO2 concentration), and land use were fixed at their 1850 levels to represent the period prior to the onset of large-scale industrialization. The deforestation simulation branches off from the piControl simulation, with a total area of 20 × 106 km2 of forest being removed in a temporally linear fashion over a period of 50 years and replaced by grassland. Nine ESMs carried out the “deforest-glob” experiment: MPI-ESM-1.2.0, IPSL-CM6A, CESM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, CanESM5, BCC-CSM2-MR, MIROC-ES2L, UKESM1-0LL, and EC-Earth3-Veg (for details of the models, refer to Supplementary Table 6; for the spatial pattern of deforestation area for each model, refer to Supplementary Fig. 19). We used output from all of these models except MIROC-ES2L, which was excluded from our analysis because its land cover type was fixed as woody vegetation following deforestation, and forest recovery started soon after the completion of deforestation54. All external forcings in the idealized “deforest-glob” and piControl simulations are identical except for forest cover54. The difference in simulated surface wind speed between these two simulations can then be ascribed to forest coverage change.

Current global wind turbine technical features and scenarios for future development

The calculation of forestation-induced wind energy change requires information on the current technical features of global wind turbines. We adopt the technical feature that is representative of the average rated power of the global current wind turbine fleet according to a recent study by Jung and Schindler25 (the T1 scenario, see below). In that study, a detailed onshore wind turbine fleet dataset mainly including those in the United States, Germany, and Canada was compiled, which together accounted for 26.6% of global onshore installed capacity in 2020. They found an average rated power of 1.69 MW in these countries, which closely matches that of turbines in China (1.7 MW) but also worldwide (1.71 MW).

Jung and Schindler25 further classified the current wind turbines into eight power classes (PC) according to their rated power (Pr): PC1000 (600 kW <Pr ≤1400 kW); PC1800 (1400 kW <Pr ≤2200 kW); PC2350 (2200 kW <Pr ≤2600 kW); PC2700 (2600 kW <Pr ≤2900 kW); PC3000 (2900 kW <Pr ≤3500 kW); PC3600 (3500 kW <Pr ≤3900 kW); PC4350 (3900 kW <Pr ≤5200 kW) and PC6200 (Pr >5200 kW). The power classes represent different levels of wind turbine technical advancement, as more recent, advanced technology enables turbines with greater Pr values. In addition, the wind turbines are also categorized into six hub height classes (HC): HC60 (hub height ≤60 m), HC80 (60 m <hub height ≤90 m), HC100 (90 m <hub height ≤110 m), HC120 (110 m <hub height ≤130 m), HC140 (130 m <hub height ≤150 m) and HC160 (hub height >150 m). In general, wind turbines with higher Pr are associated with greater HC. To describe a wind turbine by both its rated power and hub height, 29 power-height combinations (PHC) are defined.

Based on the classification of wind turbines, the technical features of currently installed global onshore wind turbines, as well as their potential evolution in the future, can then be described by different shares of 29 power-height combinations (PHC)25. Jung and Schindler25 defined the share of the 29 power-height combinations (PHC) classes of wind turbine (denoted as T1, Supplementary Table 8), which is representative of the mean Pr value (about 1.7 WM) of the global wind turbine fleet, and three additional scenarios (denoted as T2, T3 and T4), which represents the pessimistic, middle and optimistic developments of the global wind turbine fleet up to 2060, respectively.

We also examined how potential technological developments in the global wind turbine fleet counteract the forestation-induced negative impacts on wind energy by adopting the T2 and T4 technical scenarios of Jung and Schindler25. Specifically, the technical development scenarios are based on the trends of the past and the expected increase in the deployment of modern wind turbine types with high Pr values and rotor heights. The T2 scenario assumes a linear development of wind turbine type shares in line with the development during 2014-2020. The T4 scenario assumes a more ambitious modernization of the global wind turbine fleet with higher rated power. The shares of high PC increase more strongly compared to T2, which is mapped with a two-degree polynomial for PC3600 and a three-degree polynomial for PC4350 based on the wind turbine share for 2014–2020. In addition, it considers a massive integration of the most powerful onshore turbine (PC6200). Starting at 10% in 2021, the share of PC6200 in the combined share of PC4350 and PC6200 increases by 2.0% every year until 2060. For details, please refer to Jung and Schindler25. For both T2 and T4, the wind turbine type shares in 2060 were used (Supplementary Table 8).

Wind-energy technical potential change under different forestation scenarios and technical scenarios

To quantify wind energy change, we used the concept of “technical potential,” which was widely adopted by international agencies and extensively used in previous research56–60. Technical potential refers to the amount of electricity that can be generated over the land area suitable for wind turbine installation for a given region, known as geographical suitability, combined with the limitations of turbine technology, turbine spacing and system loss26,56,57. The use of “technical potential” enabled us to account for land areas suitable for wind turbine installation when comparing different regions.

To estimate the wind energy technical potential, we first identified the global land area suitable for onshore wind turbine installation at a 1-km resolution, consistent with the resolution at which our forestation potential maps were derived (see above). Consistent with previous studies56–60, the global land area suitable for onshore wind turbine installation was obtained after accounting for geographic and environmental constraints. The following lands are considered as unsuitable for wind turbine installation: existing forests, water bodies (ESA CCI land cover dataset), high elevation areas (more than 3000 m, 1-km SRTM digital elevation database version 4.1), steeply sloped areas (>20%) (derived from 1-km SRTM digital elevation database) and urban areas (ESA CCI land cover dataset). When potential forestation (scenarios of S1–S4) areas are considered in our study, the respective forestation areas were further excluded from the suitable land for wind turbine installation. Subsequently, the global land area suitable for onshore wind turbine installation in different forestation scenarios was aggregated from the 1-km resolution to 0.25° resolution (Supplementary Fig. 22). Using global climate model simulations imposed a coarse spatial (0.25°) and temporal (monthly) resolution on the surface wind speed data used to estimate wind energy in this study. This practice, however, is the state of the art for evaluating large-scale wind energy changes in response to future climate change24,40. Karnauskas et al.24 reported that using coarse-grid wind speed data yields wind-power estimates comparable with those obtained by using site-level wind speed data, which include the temporal variability of wind power. However, using monthly average wind speed results in an underestimation of monthly total energy compared to using hourly data, but the global spatial pattern of wind energy is little influenced24. Their results demonstrate the feasibility of using climate model outputs of coarse-grid monthly surface wind speed to investigate spatial-temporal changes in global wind energy, lending credibility to the approach taken here.

We also explored the effect of using monthly average wind speed on derived wind-energy change in response to forestation. The time series of global monthly mean wind energy derived by using hourly wind speed were compared with those derived by using the same hourly wind speed but aggregated to a monthly scale before computing wind energy, using ERA reanalysis climate data for 1980–2020 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Our results confirm previous reports40 that using a monthly average wind speed results in an underestimation of wind energy. However, the spatial pattern of global mean wind energy is quite similar between those derived using monthly average wind speed and those calculated using hourly wind speeds (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Thus, a persistent low bias due to using monthly average wind energy should be kept in mind when interpreting the absolute wind power values in this study. In addition, we compared wind-energy reductions following different forestation scenarios between those derived from monthly wind speed and hourly wind speed but applying the monthly-scale wind-speed model (M2). Comparable reductions in wind-energy potential for different forestation scenarios were found between those derived by using monthly and hourly wind speed data (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The strengths of the carbon-energy trade-off across the 13 regions, derived by using monthly wind speed, show almost no changes in comparison to those derived by applying the monthly wind speed model (M2) on hourly wind speed (Supplementary Fig. 10b). These results demonstrate the robustness of our conclusions to different temporal resolutions of wind speed data.

Changes in wind-energy generation by current onshore wind installations due to potential forestation

To estimate changes in wind-energy generation by currently installed onshore wind turbines due to future forestation, we used a dataset of global wind installation distribution63. The dataset contains the geographical coordinates of 266,672 onshore wind installations, but contains no information on installation capacity or wind turbine type. Hence, capacity factor (CF) changes under different forestation and technical scenarios for each wind installation were derived by extracting the CF changes obtained in the previous section for the corresponding 0.25° grid cell where the wind installation is located. To explore changes in energy generation by current wind installations under future forestation and technical development scenarios for different regions, information on wind-energy installation capacity for different countries in 2022 was obtained from previous reports64,65 and used to calculate regional wind-energy installation capacities (Supplementary table 2). The annual wind-energy generation for the 13 regions of the globe was calculated by multiplying the regional average capacity factor (CF) of current wind installations by the total regional installation capacity and the number of hours in a year (8760 hours).

Biomass carbon sequestration potential for different forestation scenarios

Biomass carbon sequestration potential following forestation was estimated as the carbon accumulation in forest biomass when restored forests reach an old-growth stage, i.e., when their biomass stock reaches equilibrium. The equilibrium biomass carbon density for different grid cells was obtained using global potential living tree biomass under the natural potential as predicted by a recent study19.

In that study, the global forest carbon density potential under hypothetical natural conditions was predicted by two sets of models. The first set of models (GS1 and SD1) combined aboveground forest carbon measurements and satellite-derived biomass products with spatially explicit data reflecting climate, soil conditions, topography, forest canopy cover and human disturbance, using random-forest machine-learning models to interpolate biomass carbon density across the globe. The global potential living tree biomass density under the natural potential in the first set of models was estimated by statistically accounting for the impact of human disturbance, setting all variables directly reflecting human disturbance to zero. The second set of models (GS2 and SD2) differs from the first set of models (GS1 and SD1) in a reduced number of observations (only pixels with minimal human disturbance) and a reduced number of predictors (no human-disturbance variables). The global potential living tree biomass density under the natural potential in the second set of models was estimated using aboveground forest carbon measurements and satellite-derived biomass products from protected areas with minimal human disturbance.

To account for uncertainties in canopy-cover estimates from ground-sourced data, this study incorporated upper and lower boundaries of canopy cover in each pixel, resulting in a total of four GS models: GS1_MAX, GS1_MEAN, GS2_MAX and GS2_MEAN. To assess the uncertainty in biomass density potential across various satellite-derived biomass products, this study included three satellite-derived biomass products (ESA-CCI66, Walker et al.10 and harmonized67), resulting in a total of six SD models: SD1_ESA-CCI, SD1_Walker, SD1_Harmonized, SD2_ESA-CCI, SD2_Walker and SD2_Harmonized. In this study, forestation-induced biomass carbon sequestration was subsequently calculated using global potential living tree biomass density derived from a full combination of models under the natural potential (Supplementary Fig. 28) and the forestation area under the different forestation scenarios.
