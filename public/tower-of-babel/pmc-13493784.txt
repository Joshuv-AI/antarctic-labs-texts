Probabilistic deep learning framework for dynamic carbon emission accounting of electric buses under grid uncertainty
Authors: Shaoxuan Zhu, Guolin Lü, Chenhao Zhi, Shuhong Liu, Chenhao Li, Kai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrification of public transit has emerged as a pivotal pathway for deep urban decarbonization. However, existing carbon accounting methods predominantly rely on static grid emission factors and deterministic energy models, often overlooking spatiotemporal variability and uncertainty propagation. To address this limitation, this study establishes a dynamic, uncertainty-aware framework for the carbon accounting of electric bus systems. A hybrid deep learning architecture integrating Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks, and an Attention mechanism is developed to capture multi-scale temporal dependencies in energy consumption. In parallel, a time-varying probabilistic grid emission model is formulated using period-specific distributions across diurnal intervals, and Monte Carlo simulation is employed to propagate uncertainty throughout the accounting chain. Using high-frequency operational telemetry from ten electric buses in Shenzhen, the proposed model achieved an of 0.9610 and an RMSE of 0.0523, outperforming ensemble learning methods, conventional deep learning baselines, and ablation variants. Leave-one-bus-out cross-validation further confirmed robust cross-vehicle generalizability, with a mean of 0.9618 ± 0.0146. The results reveal pronounced heteroscedasticity in carbon emission profiles, with uncertainty expanding substantially during high-power transient events, while the time-varying emission factor model yields wider confidence intervals than static approaches. These findings demonstrate the importance of uncertainty-aware dynamic accounting and provide a robust data-driven basis for probabilistic carbon footprint estimation in urban public transport.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In response to the escalating crisis of global climate change, nations worldwide have pledged to achieve carbon neutrality, a commitment strongly reinforced by the landmark synthesis report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)1. Given that the transportation sector accounts for approximately 23% of global energy-related CO2 emissions and remains one of the most “hard-to-abate” sectors2, the electrification of public transport—particularly electric buses3—has emerged as a pivotal pathway for deep urban decarbonization4. This transition is not only technologically feasible but also yields substantial environmental benefits due to the immense scale of urban public transit systems5. Notably, China has pioneered this shift, with Shenzhen establishing the world’s first fully electrified bus fleet6. Empirical evidence indicates that, when accounting for regional grid structures and operational contexts, the life-cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions of electric buses can be up to 52% lower than those of conventional diesel counterparts6,7.

Despite these advancements, significant uncertainty persists regarding the real-world carbon footprint of electric bus operations8. A critical challenge lies in the dynamic and stochastic interplay between vehicle energy consumption—which is modulated by driver behavior, traffic conditions, and environmental factors9—and the fluctuating carbon intensity of the electricity grid. However, prevailing carbon accounting methodologies in both academic literature and policy frameworks predominantly rely on static, region-wide average grid emission factors10 and simplified estimations of vehicle energy consumption11,12. Such static approaches fail to capture spatiotemporal variations, potentially biasing life-cycle assessments and consequently misinforming policymakers and fleet operators.

Methodologies for modeling vehicle energy consumption generally fall into two primary categories: physics-based models13 and data-driven approaches. While physics-based models offer theoretical precision in controlled environments, they face significant practical limitations, including complex parameterization and a heavy reliance on detailed vehicle specifications14,15. Consequently, these models often struggle to generalize or capture the fine-grained, instantaneous fluctuations inherent in real-world driving11.

In contrast, data-driven methods bypass the need for explicit physical formulations16, instead leveraging historical data to identify consumption patterns. Early implementations relied on traditional statistical techniques such as ARIMA and SARIMA17 or standard machine learning algorithms, including multiple linear regression18, SVM, and ensemble methods19–21. More recently, deep learning has advanced the field22, with hybrid architectures employing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs/LSTMs) and Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) demonstrating superior accuracy in handling time-series data23,24. However, existing literature remains predominantly focused on deterministic prediction, frequently overlooking the critical roles of uncertainty propagation and dynamic feature importance.

Furthermore, a growing body of literature highlights the spatiotemporal volatility of grid carbon intensity25,26, driven largely by the increasing penetration of intermittent renewable energy and load shifting12,27. As Sugano et al.27 demonstrated, carbon intensity within urban environments can fluctuate significantly on an hourly basis, challenging the validity of using annual average emission factors. To address this, recent studies underscore the necessity of probabilistic approaches, such as Monte Carlo simulations28,29. These methods are essential for rigorously quantifying and propagating the uncertainties inherent in both operational energy predictions and grid carbon factor distributions30,31. Methodology

Framework

The central aim of this work is to establish a dynamic, stochastic framework for electric bus carbon accounting that moves beyond the determinism of conventional static methods. Figure 1 delineates the system architecture, which functions as an end-to-end pipeline: it processes high-granularity operational telemetry to yield a probabilistic carbon inventory, explicitly quantifying the uncertainties inherent in real-world operations.

The proposed framework unfolds in four sequential stages. The process initiates with Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering, where raw, encrypted hexadecimal telemetry from the vehicle’s CAN bus is parsed, sanitized, and synchronized in strict accordance with the standard. Through this process, a multi-dimensional feature set—encompassing vehicle dynamics and battery status—is constructed.

At the framework’s (Fig. 2) core lies the Energy Consumption Prediction module. Here, we deploy a hybrid deep learning architecture that integrates a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for extracting local temporal features, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network for capturing long-term dependencies, and an Attention mechanism to dynamically weigh critical driving events.

The overall architecture of the proposed probabilistic carbon emission accounting framework.

To overcome the rigidity of deterministic accounting, the third stage introduces Probabilistic Carbon Factor Modeling. Instead of relying on a static regional average, the grid emission intensity is reformulated as a time-varying probability distribution whose parameters are modulated by the diurnal characteristics of the power generation mix, thereby capturing the inherent stochasticity of the power grid across different operational periods. Finally, the Uncertainty-Aware Emission Accounting stage synthesizes these components. Utilizing a Monte Carlo simulation approach, the framework propagates uncertainties by convolving the predicted energy values with sampled carbon factors. This yields a full probability distribution for the final carbon footprint, enabling the reporting of not just a mean estimate, but a robust confidence interval for more reliable assessment.

Stage 1: Decryption and protocol decoding

The raw data was originally transmitted as encrypted hexadecimal streams from each vehicle’s on-board terminal to the Shenzhen Bus Group’s remote monitoring platform via TCP/IP, following the communication protocol defined in the national standard GB/T 32960.3-2016 32. Each data packet comprises a fixed header (ASCII start bytes, command unit, 17-character VIN), an AES-128 encrypted data unit, and a BCC (Block Check Character) checksum byte for integrity verification.

Custom decoding software was developed to decrypt the payloads and parse the binary data units according to the byte-level offset and scaling rules specified in the standard. This procedure extracted 46 physically meaningful parameters organized into six information categories defined by the protocol:

Vehicle status data (Section 7.2.3.1 of GB/T 32960.3): vehicle speed (0.1 km/h resolution), cumulative mileage, total pack voltage (0.1 V resolution), total pack current (0.1 A resolution, with 1000 A offset), State of Charge (SOC, 1% resolution), DC-DC status, gear position, and insulation resistance;

Stage 2: Data cleaning

A rigorous data cleaning pipeline was implemented to ensure the physical validity of the dataset. Protocol-defined exception flags (0xFE for anomalous readings and 0xFF for invalid data) were identified and replaced with null values. Sensor-specific linear transformations—such as the C offset for temperature readings and the 1,000 A offset for current measurements—were applied in strict accordance with the scaling rules defined in the standard. Outliers were detected and removed based on the protocol-specified physical validity bounds (e.g., speed: 0–220 km/h, total voltage: 0–1,000 V, total current: -1000 to +1000 A). For motor-related parameters, which exhibited approximately 11% missing values due to intermittent transmission during motor-off states, forward-fill imputation was applied to maintain temporal continuity. The cleaned data was subsequently resampled to a uniform 30-second frequency, consistent with the maximum reporting interval specified in GB/T 32960.3-2016 (Section 5.2.4: “the maximum reporting interval shall not exceed 30 s”).

This systematic feature engineering process yielded a high-quality, multi-dimensional feature space that integrates real-time vehicle status, historical operational context, and dynamic driving patterns, serving as the direct input for the subsequent deep learning model. To ensure temporal integrity, the dataset was chronologically partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) sets without shuffling, strictly preserving the temporal order to prevent information leakage from future time steps into the training process.

Energy consumption prediction with TCN-BiLSTM-attention

At the core of the proposed framework is a novel hybrid deep learning architecture (presented in Fig. 3), specifically engineered to predict electric bus energy consumption with high fidelity. Real-world driving data involves complex, multi-scale temporal dependencies, where traditional models often struggle to simultaneously resolve short-term high-frequency events and long-term operational trends. To address this, we propose an integrated TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model. This architecture synergistically combines a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for efficient local feature extraction, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network for capturing sequential dependencies, and an attention mechanism to dynamically weigh critical driving events.

The architecture of the proposed TCN-BiLSTM-Attention hybrid model for energy consumption prediction.

The proposed model employs a hierarchical architecture designed to capture the multi-scale temporal dynamics inherent in real-world electric vehicle operations. The processing pipeline begins with a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) configured with a look-back window of 10 time steps. Utilizing causal, dilated convolutions, the TCN acts as a feature preprocessor that encodes local high-frequency transients—such as instantaneous acceleration surges and gear shifts—while strictly preventing future information leakage. Its exponentially expanding receptive field (covering approximately time steps) effectively avoids the vanishing gradient issues common in deep RNNs.

The locally enriched feature maps are subsequently propagated into a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network. While the TCN captures short-range variations, the BiLSTM leverages its internal gating mechanisms to model the long-range sequential evolution of the vehicle’s state. This allows the network to account for cumulative factors that progressively influence future energy demand, such as battery thermal buildup, prolonged high-speed cruising, and SOC decline.

To further enhance interpretability and predictive accuracy, an Attention mechanism operates on the BiLSTM outputs. By assigning dynamic weights, it identifies and prioritizes the most salient time steps—effectively suppressing the contribution of steady-state cruising periods while amplifying distinct, high-power transient events (e.g., sharp acceleration on an incline). The resulting context vector is decoded by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with dropout regularization to yield the final scalar energy consumption prediction. The necessity of this hierarchical design—local feature extraction, sequential state modeling, and adaptive temporal weighting—is empirically validated in the subsequent ablation study.

Model Optimization The entire architecture is trained end-to-end using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate = 0.0005, weight decay = 0.01) and a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function. Training was conducted with a batch size of 32, gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1.0, and an early stopping patience of 15 epochs on the validation loss to ensure robust generalization. All experiments utilized a fixed random seed for reproducibility. Systemic evaluations of key hyperparameters, including input sequence length sensitivity, are detailed in the Results section.

Probabilistic carbon emission accounting

The final stage of our framework transitions from deterministic energy prediction to probabilistic carbon emission accounting. This advanced approach addresses a critical limitation of conventional methods, which rely on a single, static grid emission factor. In reality, the carbon intensity of a power grid exhibits inherent volatility due to the fluctuating mix of energy sources (e.g., coal, gas, renewables) and variations in grid load. Using a fixed average value fails to capture this real-world uncertainty.

To overcome this, our methodology integrates uncertainty into the accounting process. While the TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model provides a highly accurate, deterministic point prediction for energy consumption at each time step, denoted as , we reformulate the grid emission factor F as a time-varying stochastic variable. Ideally, carbon accounting should be coupled with real-time power dispatch data; however, given the lack of publicly available sub-hourly generation mix data for the Shenzhen grid, we adopt a stochastic sensitivity approach informed by the well-documented diurnal patterns of power grid operations.

These parameters are constructed as informed estimates based on the documented diurnal characteristics of regional power grid operations. The scaling factors applied to the baseline emission factor are grounded in the following empirical evidence from the literature. Khan 25 demonstrated that hourly grid carbon intensity can deviate from the annual mean by ±15–30% depending on the generation dispatch schedule, with nocturnal base-load periods exhibiting systematically lower intensities and evening peak periods showing elevated values due to the activation of peaking generation units. Sugano et al. 27 further quantified intra-day carbon intensity fluctuations of up to ±20% in urban environments, with the highest variability occurring during midday hours when intermittent solar photovoltaic generation reaches its peak output. Zhang et al. 26 reported that provincial-level emission factors in China’s regional power grids exhibited coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 8% to 18% over the 2005–2020 period.

Based on this evidence, we adopt conservative scaling factors within the ±10% range relative to the official baseline of 0.4224 kgCO/kWh, and assign higher coefficients of variation (up to 15%) to periods with greater renewable intermittency. The weighted daily average of across all periods is constrained to approximate the official annual average, thereby ensuring consistency with the regulatory baseline.

Importantly, the framework is designed to be modular: as real-time grid dispatch data becomes publicly available, the assumed distributions can be readily replaced with empirically calibrated parameters, further enhancing the fidelity of the carbon accounting.

Results

The methodology presented above establishes three interconnected hypotheses that the following empirical analysis is designed to validate. First, we test whether the hybrid TCN-BiLSTM-Attention architecture captures multi-scale temporal dependencies more effectively than standalone models—a claim motivated by the literature gap identified in the Introduction regarding deterministic prediction limitations. Second, we examine whether the time-varying emission factor model produces meaningfully different uncertainty estimates compared to the conventional static approach, directly addressing the spatiotemporal volatility highlighted by Sugano et al. 27 and Khan 25. Third, we assess the practical significance of heteroscedastic emission uncertainty for carbon accounting, which constitutes the core policy-relevant contribution of this work. The following subsections present the experimental setup, model performance benchmarking, sensitivity analyses, and dynamic carbon emission results in this logical sequence.

Experimental setup

The empirical validation of our model is grounded in a real-world, high-resolution dataset collected from ten pure electric buses operating within the Shenzhen Bus Group’s public transport network. The data spans a continuous 9-day period from September 11 to September 19, 2024, yielding a total of 236,575 valid data records. Following the decryption and decoding process compliant with the national standard, a rich feature set of 46 key parameters was extracted for each timestamp. The raw data was subsequently resampled to a uniform 30-second frequency to form a consistent time-series dataset. To ensure a robust evaluation, the dataset was chronologically partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets, allowing the model’s forecasting capability to be tested on unseen future data.

To rigorously benchmark the predictive fidelity of the proposed TCN-BiLSTM-Attention architecture, we employed a comprehensive set of baselines, ranging from traditional machine learning algorithms to deep learning variants. Specifically, we introduced ensemble learning methods, including XGBoost and Random Forest, which were trained with engineered rolling-window features to serve as strong non-deep learning baselines. Additionally, ablation models derived from the proposed architecture (e.g., standalone TCN and LSTM) were evaluated to verify the contribution of each module. For all models, the input consisted of the past 10 time steps across all 46 features to predict the energy consumed in the subsequent 30-s interval (kWh). The predictive accuracy was quantified using three standard regression metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination (). The proposed model was implemented in PyTorch and trained with early stopping to mitigate overfitting. Key architectural and training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 4.

Ablation study

To validate the contribution of each architectural component, we conducted a systematic ablation study by selectively removing one module at a time from the proposed TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model. Three variants were evaluated: (1) TCN-BiLSTM, which removes the Attention mechanism and uses only the last time step output; (2) BiLSTM-Attention, which bypasses the TCN and feeds raw features directly into the BiLSTM; and (3) TCN-Attention, which replaces the BiLSTM with a direct connection from TCN to the Attention layer. All variants were trained under identical conditions (same dataset, hyperparameters, and random seed) to ensure a fair comparison.

As shown in Table 5, removing any single component from the full architecture leads to measurable performance degradation, confirming the necessity of the hybrid design. The BiLSTM component exhibits the largest individual contribution, with its removal causing R to decrease from 0.9610 to 0.9327 (ΔR = 0.0283). This indicates that the gated recurrent mechanism is critical for capturing long-range sequential dependencies, such as cumulative battery thermal effects and prolonged driving patterns. The TCN module contributes the second-largest improvement (ΔR = 0.0096), validating its role in extracting high-frequency local features through dilated causal convolutions. The Attention mechanism provides a smaller but still significant enhancement (ΔR = 0.0081), confirming that dynamic temporal weighting improves the model’s ability to prioritize informative time steps during transient events.

Notably, all three ablation variants outperform the standalone LSTM and TCN baselines, demonstrating that even partial combinations of the proposed architecture yield superior performance. Furthermore, the full model’s advantage is not attributable to increased model complexity alone: the GRU baseline, with 457,345 trainable parameters, achieves only R = 0.9274, whereas the proposed model’s architectural synergy—rather than parameter count—drives its superior predictive fidelity.

Cross-vehicle generalizability analysis

To further assess the inter-vehicle generalizability of the proposed model beyond the chronological train-test split, we conducted a leave-one-bus-out cross-validation analysis. In each of the 10 folds, the TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model was trained on data from 9 buses and evaluated on the held-out bus under identical hyperparameter settings (Table 4).

As summarized in Table 6, the model achieved a mean R of 0.9618 (±0.0146) across all 10 folds, with individual fold R values ranging from 0.9362 to 0.9794. The mean RMSE was 0.0521 (±0.0083), the mean MAE was 0.0316 (±0.0049), and the mean MAPE was 8.47% (±2.91%). Notably, 9 out of 10 folds achieved R, indicating highly consistent performance across vehicles. The cross-validation mean R (0.9618) closely matches the chronological test set R (0.9610 in Table 5), providing strong evidence that the model captures generalizable energy consumption patterns rather than vehicle-specific idiosyncrasies. The low standard deviation across folds confirms that the proposed architecture generalizes consistently across different vehicles of the same type, rather than overfitting to individual bus characteristics.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the proposed framework to key parameter choices, we conducted a systematic sensitivity analysis examining three aspects: the coefficient of variation (CV) of the grid emission factor, the Monte Carlo iteration count, and the input sequence length.

Sensitivity to grid emission factor CV

The probabilistic carbon accounting framework relies on the assumed CV of the grid emission factor distribution. To evaluate the impact of this assumption, we systematically varied the CV by applying uniform scale factors (0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, and 2.0) to the baseline period-specific CV values defined in Table 3. The analysis utilized full-day operational data comprising 1,112 thirty-second samples from a representative bus, spanning four diurnal periods (Morning, Midday solar, Afternoon, and Evening peak).

As shown in Fig. 6, the average 95% confidence interval width exhibits a strictly linear relationship with the CV scale factor, expanding from 6.7 gCO at ×0.5 to 27.0 gCO at ×2.0 (Table 7). This linearity is theoretically expected, as the CI width is proportional to , and confirms that the emission uncertainty is predictable and controllable. Crucially, the mean emission remains invariant across all CV settings (28.12 kgCO), verifying the unbiasedness of the Monte Carlo estimator.

Sensitivity to input sequence length

The look-back window determines how much historical context the model uses for prediction. We evaluated four window sizes by retraining the TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model under identical hyperparameters and data splits (Table 7). The results reveal a characteristic inverted-U pattern: the optimal performance is achieved at 10 time steps (5 minutes, R = 0.9610), with performance degrading for both shorter and longer windows. A window of 5 time steps (2.5 minutes) yields R = 0.9487, as the truncated history fails to capture sufficient operational context such as cumulative acceleration patterns. Conversely, extending the window to 15 or 20 time steps (7.5–10 minutes) reduces R to 0.9564 and 0.9539, respectively, likely due to the inclusion of temporally distant information that introduces noise rather than predictive signal. This analysis validates the selection of 10 time steps as the optimal sequence length for the proposed architecture.

Dynamic carbon emission accounting results

Having established the superior predictive fidelity of the TCN-BiLSTM-Attention architecture (, Table 5)—and confirmed its robustness through ablation analysis and cross-vehicle validation—we now leverage these validated energy predictions as the quantitative foundation for the central objective of this study: dynamic, uncertainty-aware carbon emission accounting. This transition from energy prediction to emission quantification operationalizes the probabilistic framework introduced in the Methodology section, where the time-varying grid emission factor model (Table 3) is coupled with Monte Carlo simulation to propagate uncertainty through the assessment chain. The following analysis demonstrates how the integration of these two components—high-fidelity energy prediction and stochastic emission modeling—yields insights that are unattainable through conventional static accounting methods.

These predictions serve as the quantitative basis for dynamic carbon accounting. The conversion from energy to carbon emissions follows the probabilistic formulation established in Equation (2), where each time-step emission is computed by multiplying the predicted energy consumption with a grid emission factor sampled from the period-specific distribution defined in Table 3. This formulation captures the diurnal variability of grid carbon intensity, rather than relying on a single static annual average. For reference, the official annual-average emission factor for the Shenzhen power grid (), published in the Guidelines for Greenhouse Gas Emission Accounting and Reporting for Enterprises in Shenzhen, is retained as a deterministic baseline for comparison in subsequent figures.

Limitations and discussion

While the proposed framework demonstrates strong predictive performance and provides a principled approach to uncertainty-aware carbon accounting, several limitations must be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future research.

Input data uncertainties

The empirical foundation of this study relies on CAN bus telemetry, which is inherently subject to sensor measurement uncertainties. Specifically, the total current sensor exhibits an accuracy of ±1 A, the voltage sensor ±0.5 V, and the State of Charge (SOC) estimation carries an inherent error of ±2–3%. These uncertainties propagate through the feature engineering pipeline: for instance, the computed instantaneous power (P = V × I) inherits the combined measurement errors of both voltage and current sensors, with the relative error amplified during low-power operating conditions where the signal-to-noise ratio is reduced. Furthermore, GPS positioning drift (±5–10 m) may introduce noise into the longitude and latitude features, though this effect is mitigated by the 30-second resampling which spatially averages positional fluctuations. The motor-related parameters (controller temperature, RPM, torque) exhibited approximately 11% missing values in the raw dataset, which were addressed through forward-filling imputation. While this is a standard approach, it may introduce artificial smoothing during periods of motor state transitions.

Framework assumptions

The proposed framework incorporates several simplifying assumptions that should be considered when interpreting the results:

Normal distribution of emission factors. The grid emission factor is modeled as a normal distribution within each diurnal period. In practice, the actual distribution may exhibit skewness or multi-modality, particularly in grids with high renewable penetration where sudden drops in solar or wind generation can cause abrupt shifts in carbon intensity. Future work could explore non-parametric or mixture distributions to better capture such behavior.

In reality, systematic factors such as time-of-day may simultaneously affect both driving patterns (and hence prediction accuracy) and grid carbon intensity, introducing correlations that are not captured by the current Monte Carlo formulation.

Data and generalizability constraints

The evaluation is based on a dataset of 10 BYD E6 electric buses operating over 9 days (September 11–19, 2024) in Shenzhen. While the high-frequency sampling yields 236,575 individual records and the dataset covers both weekdays and weekends, several constraints limit the generalizability of the findings:

Fleet homogeneity. All 10 buses are of the same model (BYD E6) with identical specifications (Table 2). The model’s transferability to heterogeneous fleets with different battery capacities, motor configurations, or vehicle sizes remains to be validated through transfer learning or domain adaptation techniques. Nevertheless, the leave-one-bus-out cross-validation (Table 6) demonstrates that the model generalizes well across the 10 vehicles within the current homogeneous fleet (mean R = 0.9618 ± 0.0146, with all folds exceeding R = 0.93). Extending this validation to heterogeneous fleets with different battery capacities, motor configurations, or vehicle sizes remains an important direction for future work. For scaling to heterogeneous fleets, transfer learning—where the model is pre-trained on a large homogeneous source fleet and subsequently fine-tuned on limited data from a target fleet with different specifications—offers a promising pathway to reduce the data requirements for new vehicle types 19.

Geographic specificity. The emission factor parameters (Table 3) are calibrated for the Shenzhen (Guangdong) power grid, which has a specific generation mix dominated by coal and natural gas with increasing renewable penetration. Deployment in regions with fundamentally different grid compositions would require recalibration of both the emission factor distributions and potentially the energy prediction model. Despite these limitations, the proposed framework represents a significant advancement over static carbon accounting methods by providing: (1) a principled mechanism for uncertainty quantification through Monte Carlo propagation; (2) dynamic emission factor modeling that captures diurnal grid variability; and (3) a modular architecture that can readily incorporate improved data sources and emission factor models as they become available. We frame the current study as a methodological proof-of-concept that establishes the validity and superiority of the probabilistic framework, while acknowledging that operational deployment would benefit from extended validation across diverse operational conditions.

Policy and operational implications

The granular, uncertainty-aware carbon accounting enabled by the proposed framework yields several actionable implications for fleet operators and urban transport authorities.

Eco-driving feedback systems. The heteroscedastic emission uncertainty identified in Fig. 12 provides a quantitative basis for targeted driver behavior interventions. Our experimental results demonstrate that the 95% confidence interval width during high-power transient events (e.g., rapid acceleration at minute 19, 23.15 gCO) is 2.8 times larger than during steady-state cruising (minute 3, 8.18 gCO). This empirically validated relationship between driving aggressiveness and emission uncertainty suggests that real-time driver coaching systems—which issue alerts when instantaneous energy consumption enters high-variance regimes—could simultaneously reduce mean emissions and narrow the uncertainty bounds of fleet-level carbon inventories.

Emission-sensitive scheduling. The hourly emission profiles derived from our validated energy predictions (Fig. 10) reveal a peak-to-trough emission ratio of approximately 13.9× between the morning rush hour (8:00, ∼0.19 kgCO) and off-peak periods (21:00, ∼0.013 kgCO). This pronounced temporal concentration, which reflects the empirically observed variation in driving intensity and traffic conditions, suggests that targeted scheduling adjustments during identified peak-emission windows—such as deploying higher-efficiency vehicles or adjusting service headways—could yield disproportionate carbon reductions relative to uniform operational strategies.

Carbon-aware charging strategies. While the specific emission factor values assigned to each diurnal period in this study represent informed estimates rather than empirical measurements (Table 3), the framework itself establishes the methodological infrastructure for carbon-aware charging optimization. As real-time grid dispatch data becomes publicly available, the modular emission factor model can be directly calibrated to quantify the actual carbon benefit of shifting charging loads from high-intensity to low-intensity grid periods. The literature suggests that such temporal arbitrage of grid carbon intensity could yield meaningful emission reductions 25,27, and our framework provides the necessary accounting mechanism to rigorously evaluate these benefits on a case-by-case basis.

Risk-adjusted carbon budgeting. The probabilistic confidence intervals generated by the Monte Carlo framework enable a shift from deterministic to risk-adjusted carbon quota allocation. Rather than assigning emission budgets based on static annual averages, transport authorities can adopt approaches informed by the full emission distribution—for example, setting operational carbon targets at the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval to ensure compliance with high probability. This is particularly relevant for cities participating in emissions trading schemes, where the financial consequences of underestimating emissions necessitate conservative yet defensible accounting practices 29.

Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) integration

Beyond the unidirectional charging model assumed in the current framework, a critical avenue for future research is the carbon implication of Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) interactions, which represents a paradigm shift in how electric bus fleets interact with the power grid. V2G technology enables bidirectional energy flows, allowing buses to discharge stored energy back to the grid during periods of high demand 33,34.

This bidirectional capability fundamentally alters the carbon accounting paradigm by introducing “negative emission credits.” When a bus discharges energy during a high-carbon-intensity period (e.g., the Evening peak, kgCO/kWh in Table 3), it displaces fossil fuel generation and effectively reduces the fleet’s net carbon footprint. Conversely, if the same bus charges during a low-carbon period (e.g., Midday solar, kgCO/kWh), the net carbon balance becomes highly favorable. Our time-varying emission factor framework is inherently well-suited for this extension: the Monte Carlo simulation can be adapted to model V2G discharge events as negative energy consumption terms in Equation (2), with the carbon credit determined by the period-specific emission factor at the moment of discharge.

Recent studies have demonstrated the substantial potential of V2G in public transit applications. Elliott and Kittner 35 showed that a fleet of 14,000 V2G-enabled electric school buses could avoid up to 1,130 tonnes of CO emissions per day by displacing natural gas peaker plants through peak-load shaving. In an urban bus fleet context, an integrated optimization framework incorporating V2G operations, photovoltaic generation, and energy storage systems achieved a 58% reduction in total operational costs compared to unmanaged charging 36. Furthermore, Kumar et al. 33 conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of 16,457 V2G studies and identified that research is rapidly shifting from fundamental battery technology toward smart grid integration and greenhouse gas mitigation, underscoring the growing relevance of V2G to carbon accounting frameworks such as the one proposed in this study.

From a practical perspective, the integration of V2G into our framework would enable fleet operators to implement carbon-aware smart charging schedules, where charging is preferentially scheduled during periods of low grid carbon intensity (e.g., midday solar peaks) and V2G discharge is triggered during high-carbon periods (e.g., evening peaks). This temporal arbitrage of carbon intensity, enabled by the diurnal emission factor profiles established in our framework (Table 3), could yield substantial additional reductions in the net carbon footprint beyond simple electrification, though the precise magnitude would depend on fleet-specific charging patterns, battery degradation characteristics, and local grid composition. However, the realization of this potential requires careful consideration of battery degradation costs associated with increased charge-discharge cycling 37, as well as the development of regulatory frameworks for compensating bidirectional energy transactions 38.

We therefore identify V2G-aware probabilistic carbon accounting as a high-priority direction for future research. Our current framework provides the necessary methodological foundation—particularly the time-varying emission factor model and Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation—for the rigorous assessment of these bidirectional carbon flows.

Conclusion

This study established a dynamic, uncertainty-aware carbon emission accounting framework for electric bus fleets, bridging the gap between static estimation methodologies and the stochastic reality of urban transit operations. By integrating a hybrid deep learning architecture with a probabilistic grid emission model, we quantified the high-resolution spatiotemporal distribution of carbon footprints.

The proposed TCN-BiLSTM-Attention model demonstrated superior predictive fidelity in capturing the multi-scale temporal dependencies of energy consumption, achieving an R of 0.9610 on the chronological test set, with cross-vehicle generalizability confirmed through leave-one-bus-out cross-validation (mean R = 0.9618 ± 0.0146 across 10 folds).

The empirical results from the Shenzhen case study yielded critical insights into the micro-structure of carbon emissions. A key finding is the heteroscedastic nature of prediction uncertainty: the confidence intervals for carbon emissions expand significantly during high-power transient events, such as rapid acceleration and gradient climbing. This observation underscores the inadequacy of conventional deterministic approaches, which utilize static annual averages and consequently fail to capture the magnified risks associated with peak-load operations. By propagating uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulations, our framework provides a robust probabilistic range that enhances the reliability of life-cycle assessments.

From a practical perspective, the transition from coarse-grained to fine-grained accounting offers a rigorous foundation for precision decarbonization strategies. The granular visibility enabled by this framework allows transport authorities and fleet operators to identify specific carbon-intensive micro-behaviors and operational segments. This facilitates the development of targeted interventions—such as dynamic eco-driving feedback systems and emission-sensitive route scheduling—that address the most volatile periods of the driving cycle.

Future research will extend this work in two critical directions. First, we aim to incorporate battery state-of-health degradation models into the accounting framework, as the declining energy efficiency of aging batteries may introduce long-term drift in carbon intensity. Second, as detailed in the Discussion section, we will explore the integration of bidirectional Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) interactions, which represents a paradigm shift in net carbon accounting.
