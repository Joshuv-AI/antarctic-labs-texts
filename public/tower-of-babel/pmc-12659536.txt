Serum lipid metabolic characteristics and potential biomarkers in first-episode schizophrenia
Authors: Suzhen Zhang, Mengyi Luo, Xinping Kuai, Xuan Li, Tianhao Gao, Yuke Dong, Chang Lu, Kaiming Zhuo, Qiong Xiang, Dengtang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLipids play a vital role in health and disease, but changes to their circulating levels and the link with schizophrenia remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to investigate the pathological lipid profiles in patients with first-episode schizophrenia (FES) and explore potential biomarkers for the early identification of FES.MethodsThis study recruited 99 drug-naive FES patients and 60 healthy controls (HC). Serum samples were analyzed using an absolute quantitative lipidomics with ultra-high performance liquid chromatography. Psychiatric symptoms were assessed using Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) scale. Data were analyzed with regression analysis and XGBoost-based machine learning to develop biomarkers and classification of FES.ResultsIn FES patients, 33 lipid metabolites exhibited significantly altered levels independent of BMI, age, and sex. A predictive model was constructed using 14 lipid metabolites to accurately distinguish individuals with FES from healthy controls, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.967, F1 score of 0.918, an accuracy of 0.894, a specificity of 0.824, and a recall of 0.933. Bioinformatic analyses indicated changes in serum lipids co-expression correlated with disease state.ConclusionOur findings indicate dysregulated lipids implicated in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia and suggest potential lipid biomarkers may serve as indicators for the differential diagnosis of schizophrenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07588-4.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Lipid, Biomarker, Diagnosis, Machine learning

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe and chronic psychotic disorder, which affects about 1% of general population globally and is associated with excess mortality, reducing the lifespan by up to 15 years [1, 2]. The disorder is characterized by high heterogeneity and complex clinical symptoms, encompassing positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive impairments, which are also observed in other medical conditions such as bipolar disorder and autism [3]. Due to the unclear pathophysiology, the current psychiatric classification systems may lack accuracy, potentially leading to costly diagnostic delays and impeding effective treatment, despite physicians’ best efforts. Identifying biomarkers associated with schizophrenia would facilitate both clinical diagnosis and the elucidation of its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [4].

For long, schizophrenia has been widely viewed as a brain disorder. Researchers have employed a variety of imaging methods in the search for biomarkers that may be sensitive to schizophrenia diagnosis. Brain structural abnormalities are widely reported in schizophrenia with smaller hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and larger pallidum and lateral ventricle volume [5]. In a transdiagnostic, multimodal meta-analysis, schizophrenia patients exhibited specific substantial dysconnectivity in insula, lateral postcentral cortex, striatum, and thalamus compared to controls [6]. At the same, studies using the magnetic resonance spectroscopy, reduced cortical GABA and Glu levels have been found in schizophrenia [7]. Although neuroimaging techniques holds promise, its costs and challenges are significant, and some patients experiencing acute episodes may find it difficult to undergo examinations. This has prompted researchers to explore alternative approaches that can be effectively scaled for broad clinical application.

Lipids play essential roles in cellular transport, energy metabolism, neuroprotection, and maintenance of membrane composition [8]. Lipidomics, a rapidly evolving field within high-throughput metabolomics, has enabled the direct measurement of metabolic shifts associated with disease. This approach has emerged as a valuable tool for elucidating the pathophysiology of schizophrenia and identifying potential biomarkers for its early detection and diagnosis [9]. In schizophrenia, dysregulation of lipid metabolism has been observed in both the brain and biofluids. Yu et al. evaluated the lipidomics of the gray matter in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) and found that 10.4% of the detected lipids (522 out of 5024) were significantly altered in the patient group [10]. Similarly, the lipid content of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) is found to be reduced in schizophrenia patients compared to controls [11]. At the same time, Chie et al. found that 17 lipid species in the corpus callosum that can help discriminate between schizophrenia and control samples, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.751 [12]. In a plasma lipidomics study, one elevated lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs), 16 reduced phosphatidylcholines (PCs) and one reduced sphingomyelin (SM) were identified in unmedicated FES patients [13]. Moreover, Cai et al. also investigated plasma lipidomics and found that the levels of PC, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) were decreased, while the levels of LPCs were increased in first-episode unmedicated schizophrenia [14]. Notably, in research on individuals with clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR), lower levels of ether phospholipids may predate the onset of psychosis [15].

The aforementioned findings suggest abnormal lipidome alterations in peripheral blood are linked to schizophrenia, and thus hold promise for the diagnosis of schizophrenia. In the present study, we performed non-targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) for lipid analysis on serum samples collected from drug-naive FES patients and healthy controls (HC). The aim of this study was to characterize clinically relevant lipid profile associated with FES, and to evaluate potential biomarkers that may aid in diagnosing FES.

Methods

Participants

This study is based on the Sequential Multiple-Assignment Randomized Trials to Compare Antipsychotic Treatments (SMART-CAT) trial [16] and was carried out from January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2021. Ninety-nine patients fulfilling Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-V) criteria for schizophrenia were recruited from Shanghai Mental Health Center (SMHC). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 16 and 45 years; (2) inpatients; (3) first episode with illness duration of less than 3 years (4) drug-naïve, or continuous use of antipsychotic drug < 14 days; (4) a total PANSS score greater than 70, with at least one of the following symptom items scoring 4 or higher: delusions, conceptual disorganization, hallucinatory behavior, grandiosity, or suspiciousness/persecution. Exclusion criteria included an unstable clinical condition, significant medical comorbidities, concomitant psychiatric or neurological disorders, substance abuse, prior modified electroconvulsive therapy, pregnancy, or lactation.

During the same period, a sex- and age-matched control cohort (n = 60) was recruited through community advertisements. Healthy controls underwent structured diagnostic interviews and comprehensive evaluations to confirm the absence of any personal or family history of psychiatric disorders [16]. Demographic and anthropometric data, including age, sex, height, and weight, were collected following enrollment. Clinical symptoms were evaluated with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) in FES patients [17]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of SMHC (Reference no.:2018-37R), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Serum absolute quantitative lipidomics

Fasting blood samples were collected using standard venipuncture technique after fasting for more than 8 h. A volume of 5 mL of blood was collected into anticoagulant-free tubes and allowed to stand for 1 h at 4 °C prior to serum isolation via centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Serum samples were then frozen and stored under identical conditions at − 80 °C until further processing. We adopted the untargeted lipidomics absolute-quantitative analysis reported in previous study [8]. An ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) Nexera LC-30 A (SHIMADZU, Japan) with a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) was used in this study. The raw data obtained from the Q-Exactive Plus instrument were processed using LipidSearch software version 4.2 (Thermo Scientific™). The k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) algorithm was applied to impute missing lipidomics values, while features with more than 50% missing data were excluded from subsequent analysis. To eliminate the impact of variable scale differences, all lipid data were standardized using Z-score transformation. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to evaluate overall differences between groups. Lipid species with variable importance in projection (VIP) >1, fold change (FC) >1.2 or < 0.67, and adjusted p-values (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) were identified as differentially expressed lipids (DELs) [18]. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to construct signed co-expression networks.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2). Baseline demographic comparisons between study groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the associations between lipid levels, diagnostic categories, and potential confounders, in order to determine whether group differences in lipid levels were independent of age, sex, and body mass index (BMI).

The XGBoost algorithm was employed to carry out both classification and prediction tasks, with the dataset divided into training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. To ensure model stability, five-fold cross-validation was implemented within the training set for iterative evaluation and parameter optimization, facilitating the identification of key hyperparameters and the generation of the optimal model configuration. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curves with AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. The SHAP method was utilized to improve model interpretability through the quantification of feature contributions and the ranking of feature importance.

Altered serum lipid metabolite profiles in FES patients

A total of 1892 lipid metabolites were detected in our study. Of these, 51 serum lipid metabolites were identified as DELs following VIP > 1, FC > 1.2 or FC < 0.67 and pFDR < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 1). After correcting for potential confounders such as age, sex, and BMI, 33 DELs distinguished FES from healthy controls (p < 0.05; Supplementary Tables 2–3, Fig. 1). Of these, FES patients demonstrated 23 elevated levels of lipid metabolites at baseline compared with controls, including 5 diglycerides (DGs), 5 PCs, 4 phosphatidylglycerols (PGs), 4 SMs, 3PSs, 1phosphatidic acid (PA) and 1 phosphatidylinositol (PI), while 10 lipids metabolites were deregulated in the FES group.

Association between DELs and psychiatric symptoms

A total of 33 DELs were identified as serum characteristic metabolites associated with FES. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant correlation between the levels of PC(27:1_6:0) (r = 0.245, p = 0.018), ChE(22:6) (r = 0.242, p = 0.020) and PANSS total scores, Fig. 2A-B. The levels of ChE(18:1D7) (r=-0.211, p = 0.042), PC(26:0_6:0) (r=-0.215, p = 0.038) correlated negatively with PANSS-P scores, Fig. 2C-D. Additionally, serum levels of ZyE(18:2) (r = 0.251, p = 0.015), PS(22:3e) (r=-0.221, p = 0.033) correlated with PANSS-N scores, Fig. 2E-F. Three lipid metabolites, ChE(22:6) (r = 0.250, p = 0.018), PC(27:1_6:0) (r = 0.232, p = 0.025), and ChE(20:5) (r = 0.224, p = 0.031) correlated positively with PANSS-G scores, Fig. 2G-I. However, none of the correlations remained statistically significant after applying the FDR correction.

Serum lipid biomarkers for FES diagnosis

The XGBoost-based machine learning identified 14 lipid signatures related to FES, including 1PE, 1DG, 1 cholesterol ester (ChE), 1PS, 3SMs, 3PCs, and 4PGs. All the 14 metabolites were confirmed by ROC analysis to distinguish FES and healthy controls (AUC: 0.377–0.826, all p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). And sensitivities and specificities of the best cut-offs reached 40.0–100.0% and 43.4–82.7%. Finally, four metabolites, namely DG(30:2e), PS(22:3e), ChE(20:4), and PG(28:6) exhibited AUC values exceeding 0.8 for the diagnosis of FES.

ROC curve analysis of XGBoost model subjected to five-fold cross-validation are shown in Fig. 3A. The average performance of XGBoost models across five-fold cross-validation were AUC (0.930 ± 0.014), and F1-score (0.864 ± 0.051), precision (0.850 ± 0.086), and recall (0.885 ± 0.059). Test ROC analysis for XGBoost model exhibited the best prediction performance, with an AUC of 0.967, F1 score of 0.918, a precision of 0.903, an accuracy of 0.894, a specificity of 0.824, and a recall of 0.933 (Fig. 3B). The mean SHAP value, gain value and cover value of DELs in XGBoost-based model are summarized in Fig. 3C-E.

Perturbation of lipid co-expression modules in FES patients

WGCNA was adopted to explore changes in serum lipids co-expression in FES, which resulted in the identification of nine modules. The yellow module (r=-0.53, pFDR < 0.001), the black module (r = 0.277, pFDR = 0.002), green module (r = 0.257, pFDR = 0.003) correlated with disease state (Fig. 4B). Neither the red module nor the turquoise module exhibited detectable DELs in our system analysis. On the contrary, the grey module exhibits the highest total number of DELs compared to other modules (30.30%, 10/33). However, the proportion of DELs to module lipids (1.60%, 10/625) remains relatively low, indicating a limited contribution of grey module to disease-associated lipid co-expression dysregulation. With the highest feature importance in our machine learning module, PG(28:6) emerged as the sole DEL within the green module (1.12%, 1/89), and contributed to the relationship between green module and disease state. Similarly, a total of 3 DELs were identified in the black module, accounting for 4.76% (3/63) of its module lipids, while 6 DELs were detected in the yellow module, representing 3.73% (6/161) of its total lipids (Fig. 4C). Discussion

This study explored the lipid metabolomics of FES and preliminarily identify potential biomarkers for distinguishing FES using serum samples from 99 FES patients with FES and 60 HC. By integrating serum lipid profiling with machine learning techniques, this study has successfully developed a lipid biomarker panel, which has demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing FES from healthy controls, with an AUC of 0.967, F1 score of 0.918, and a recall of 0.933. Our findings suggested that FES may be associated with dysfunctional lipid metabolism, shedding new light on the potential role of lipids in the etiology and diagnostic development of FES.

Alterations in lipid concentrations have been reported for specific lipid classes across various mental disorders, including schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [10]. Tessie et al. observed alterations in certain membrane lipids of red blood cells (RBCs) in patients with schizophrenia, and further animal studies have suggested that these metabolic changes are associated with dopamine dysfunction [19]. Genetic variations, including those in ABCA13 and the rs253 SNP within the lipoprotein lipase gene, highlight the involvement of lipid metabolism in schizophrenia [20, 21]. In this study, we employed XGBoost-based machine learning to analyze serum absolute quantitative lipidomics data, aiming to identify potential biomarkers for FES. Encouragingly, the biomarker panel identified in our study proves a personalized index for accurately discriminating schizophrenia. Furthermore, WGCNA analysis reinforced the association between changes in serum lipid co-expression and disease states, thereby verifying the dysregulated lipids involved in disease pathogenesis. Most of these lipid biomarkers belong to the PGs, SMs, and PCs.

PC, SM and PG are major components of cell membrane phospholipids, which are not be ignored for the maintenance of cell membrane fluidity and structural integrity [22]. Alterations in phospholipids might reflect disturbances in cell membrane metabolism, which could potentially affect neuronal development and differentiation, leading to impaired interneuronal conduction in schizophrenia [23]. Significantly lower quantities of PC and SM were found in corpus callosum from patients with schizophrenia [12]. Using 31P magnetic resonance spectroscopy, alterations in PC in cerebrospinal fluid were found in schizophrenia spectrum disorder patients, and psychotic symptoms correlated with PC levels in the cerebral cortex [24]. In addition, Leppik et al. (2020) found that the decreased levels of PCs observed in drug-naïve schizophrenia patients were normalized following antipsychotic treatment [13]. PG is a mitochondrial phospholipid essential for the structural integrity of mitochondrial cristae and the biosynthesis of cardiolipin [25]. Recently, Kawatani et al. investigated the impact of ABCA7 deficiency on neuronal metabolism and function, revealing a reduction in PG levels in iPSC-derived cortical organoids with ABCA7 deficiency [26]. This evidence suggest that ABCA7 loss-of-function contributes to AD risk by modulating mitochondria lipid metabolism, indicating that PG may represent novel therapeutic targets for addressing neuronal dysfunction in AD [26]. However, the potential function of PGs in the development of schizophrenia requires further investigation.

SM is the most abundant sphingolipid species in the central nervous system. Studies have indicated alterations in the expression of genes involved in sphingolipid metabolism within the prefrontal cortex [27], thalamus [23] and skin [28] from schizophrenia patients. In agreement, changes in SM levels have been reported in RBCs membranes and serum in schizophrenia patients [13, 19]. Moreover, alterations in sphingomyelin metabolism are associated with cognitive impairments in patients with schizophrenia [29]. Our findings revealed PE level is down-regulated in FES patients. A study conducted by Song et al. demonstrated lower levels of PE in the plasma of patients with schizophrenia and these changes was reversed following treatment with risperidone or olanzapine [30]. In line with our study, significantly reduced levels of PE were observed in both postmortem brain tissues [31] and RBCs [32] obtained from patients with schizophrenia. Antipsychotic treatment has been shown to regulate abnormal levels of PE. A study demonstrated that the reduced levels of PE observed in patients with schizophrenia were restored following administration of risperidone and olanzapine [33] A previous study by our team revealed baseline PE level in FES was associated with treatment response [8]. Together, these aforementioned findings suggested the potential function of phospholipids in the pathology and diagnosis of schizophrenia.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although XGBoost has proven effective in delivering reliable outcomes even when utilized with limited sample sizes, the clinical applicability of lipid-based biomarkers in the diagnosis of schizophrenia remains to be validated through large-scale involving multicenter cohorts. Second, our findings are predominantly based on a young and middle-aged cohort (16–45 years), and therefore, careful interpretation is warranted when extrapolating these results to older populations. Third, the patients recruited in our study were experiencing an acute episode, and therefore, further investigation is required to optimize the lipid tool for use in treated and stable patient populations.

Conclusion

The results of the present study indicate dysregulated lipids implicated in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia and suggest potential lipid biomarkers may serve as indicators for the differential diagnosis of schizophrenia.
