Electrocardiogram Abnormality Classification From Summary Statistics With Patient‐Level Validation and Process‐Focused Evaluation
Authors: Sungjoon Hong, Christina Hartnett, Michael Ruane, Jason Tagliarino, Michael Nizich, M Toma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMachine learning (ML) applications in clinical medicine are vulnerable to data leakage, particularly temporal leakage from post‐diagnostic features and patient‐level leakage from improper partitioning, compromising electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormality detection systems. This study addresses these vulnerabilities through patient‐level data splitting and systematic evaluation across multiple classification scenarios.MethodsECG data from 4419 observations representing 2180 unique patients were analyzed using Random Under‐Sampling Boosting (RUSBoost). Introduction

Electrocardiography remains the cornerstone of cardiac diagnostic assessment, providing non‐invasive evaluation of cardiac electrical activity guiding treatment decisions across diverse clinical settings. ML integration into electrocardiogram interpretation has emerged as a promising avenue, with numerous studies demonstrating computational approaches achieving diagnostic accuracy comparable to or exceeding human interpreters in specific classification tasks (Chong et al. 2024; Dey et al. 2019; Hannun et al. 2019; Nasef et al. 2025; Rajpurkar et al. 2017). Recent ML research has predominantly focused on training models using raw waveform data captured directly from recording devices, leveraging full information content of multi‐lead voltage time‐series to extract complex temporal and spatial patterns indicative of cardiac pathology (Acharya et al. 2017; Oh et al. 2018).

Waveform‐based approaches typically operate on high‐dimensional signal data sampled at 250–1000 Hz across 12 standard leads (Clifford et al. 2006), with deep learning architectures demonstrating remarkable capability in learning hierarchical feature representations directly from raw signals without manual feature engineering (LeCun et al. 2015; Schmidhuber 2015), establishing raw waveform analysis as the dominant paradigm supported by benchmark datasets including the MIT‐BIH Arrhythmia Database and PTB Diagnostic ECG Database (Bousseljot et al. 2009; Kligfield et al. 2007; Merdjanovska and Rashkovska 2022; Moody and Mark 2001; Wagner et al. 2020). It should be noted that these benchmark databases are referenced here to contextualize existing research paradigms; the present study does not utilize these waveform repositories but instead analyzes summary‐statistic ECG data from an independent clinical source, as described in the Methods section.

Many healthcare institutions store electrocardiogram data not as continuous waveform recordings but rather as summary statistics and clinically‐derived measurements (ventricular rate, atrial rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, electrical axis orientations) extracted during initial interpretation (Cuevas‐Gonzalez et al. 2022; Dugas et al. 2024; Jacobsson et al. 2023), creating substantial disconnect between research methodologies and operational realities where only parameterized representations are available (Clifford et al. 2006; Yoo et al. 2023).

This storage pattern reflects deliberate institutional decisions: electronic health record vendors often implement electrocardiogram modules storing measurement parameters as primary data objects rather than archiving underlying voltage time‐series (Chung et al. 2018), rendering raw waveforms unavailable for retrospective research (Basco et al. 2025). Clinical validity of summary‐statistic‐based classification derives from recognition that cardiologists utilize these quantitative measurements as primary diagnostic features during standard interpretation protocols emphasizing systematic evaluation of rate, rhythm, axis, and interval measurements (Hancock et al. 2009). These measurements reflect established diagnostic criteria that have undergone extensive clinical validation, such as PR interval thresholds for atrioventricular conduction abnormalities, QRS duration criteria for intraventricular conduction delays, and QT interval boundaries for repolarization disorders (Rautaharju et al. 2009a), potentially facilitating more transparent predictions than black‐box deep learning models.

The present study addresses electrocardiogram abnormality classification when only summary‐statistic data are available by developing and evaluating Random Under‐Sampling Boosting models trained on clinically‐derived measurement parameters and patient demographic characteristics. Unlike previous research emphasizing raw waveform analysis with deep neural networks, this investigation demonstrates that meaningful classification performance can be achieved using the limited feature set typically available in summary electrocardiogram databases. The study implements systematic data partitioning strategies to prevent temporal and patient‐level information leakage (Kapoor and Narayanan 2023; Toma 2025, 2026), evaluates model generalization across multiple classification scenarios reflecting different clinical conceptualizations of electrocardiogram abnormality, and provides comprehensive assessment of learning dynamics and calibration characteristics extending beyond conventional single‐point accuracy metrics. By demonstrating effective classification using summary statistics, this work establishes feasibility for retrospective ML research utilizing archived electrocardiogram data where complete waveform recordings are unavailable, potentially enabling development of clinical decision support systems deployable in healthcare environments constrained to parameterized data representations.

Study Objectives

This investigation aimed to develop and validate ML classifiers for ECG abnormality detection using only summary statistics available in clinical database systems. The study evaluated performance across three classification scenarios reflecting different clinical conceptualizations of borderline findings, implementing patient‐level data partitioning to prevent information leakage and assessing model behavior through learning dynamics, generalization patterns, and calibration stability rather than aggregate metrics alone.

Methods

ML applications in clinical medicine are vulnerable to data leakage, which fundamentally compromises model validity. Temporal leakage occurs when features derived from post‐diagnostic events (such as clinical impressions or treatment responses) are included as predictors of initial diagnosis. Patient‐level leakage arises when multiple measurements from the same individual are distributed across training and test sets, allowing models to learn patient‐specific patterns rather than generalizable disease characteristics, thereby overestimating performance on truly novel patients.

These vulnerabilities are frequently overlooked in implementations developed without adequate clinical domain expertise, as data scientists unfamiliar with temporal sequence of clinical workflows and hierarchical structure of medical data may inadvertently include features representing consequences rather than predictors of outcomes. The present study was conducted in close collaboration with clinical domain experts to prevent data leakage through careful feature selection (temporal safeguards) and appropriate data partitioning strategies (patient‐level protections), ensuring both accurate performance estimation and genuine clinical decision support.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

The electrocardiogram (ECG) dataset contained 4466 observations across 17 variables from anonymized patient records, including demographic information, ECG measurement parameters, clinical diagnoses, and classification results. Three columns were removed: anonymized medical record number (column 1), procedure order identifier (column 2), and detailed diagnostic text description (column 16). While the first two represented administrative identifiers without clinical predictive value, column 16 removal warranted particular methodological attention as it contained the interpreting clinician's textual diagnosis, representing post‐interpretation clinical knowledge that directly indicates the classification outcome. Including such a feature would constitute fundamental data leakage, wherein information from the diagnostic process itself becomes available to the predictive model (see Figure 1).

Missing values totaling 63 (1.05% of dataset) were identified across remaining variables. Given the minimal proportion, complete case analysis was deemed appropriate. Consequently, 47 observations containing missing values were removed, resulting in a final preprocessed dataset of 4419 observations ensuring all subsequent analyses were performed on complete data without requiring imputation methods that might introduce bias. This complete case deletion was performed prior to any data partitioning. As described in Section 2.4, subsequent quality control procedures for detecting computational artifacts employed partition‐aware imputation where statistics were computed exclusively from training data to prevent information leakage from validation or test partitions.

A critical consideration in medical ML applications is the potential for data leakage when individual patients contribute multiple measurements to the dataset. The original dataset did not contain stable anonymized patient identifiers suitable for longitudinal tracking; therefore, a composite key approach was adopted as an approximation. Classification Scenario Definitions

To comprehensively evaluate the ML model's performance across different clinical decision‐making contexts, three distinct binary classification scenarios were defined. These scenarios reflect different clinical perspectives on what constitutes an abnormal versus normal ECG reading, accounting for the presence of borderline cases in the original dataset.

Scenario 1 represented a conservative classification approach where only unambiguously normal and abnormal ECG readings were considered. In this scenario, observations classified as either “Borderline” or “Neither” in the original dataset were excluded from analysis. The binary classification task distinguished between observations labeled as “Abnormal” (positive class) and those labeled as “Normal” (negative class). This scenario yielded 3661 observations from 1928 unique patients.

Scenario 2 adopted a more inclusive definition of abnormality by combining “Abnormal” and “Borderline” cases into a single positive class, contrasted against “Normal” cases as the negative class. Observations classified as “Neither” were excluded. This approach reflected clinical situations where borderline findings warrant similar attention as clearly abnormal readings. This scenario included 4308 observations from 2156 patients.

Scenario 3 employed an alternative perspective where only clearly “Abnormal” cases constituted the positive class, while both “Normal” and “Borderline” cases were grouped into the negative class. Again, “Neither” observations were excluded. This scenario was designed to identify only the most clinically significant abnormalities while treating borderline findings conservatively. Like Scenario 2, this configuration included 4308 observations from 2156 patients.

The exclusion of “Neither” observations across all scenarios reflects an assumption that the classification model operates downstream of clinical quality assurance procedures. In the intended deployment context, ECG recordings classified as uninterpretable due to technical artifacts, lead disconnection, or excessive noise would be filtered by existing clinical workflows before algorithmic classification is attempted. The model does not incorporate an abstention mechanism for borderline interpretability; rather, the scope of application is explicitly limited to ECG recordings that have passed institutional quality control standards and received definitive or borderline clinical classifications. This operational boundary should be documented in any deployment implementation to prevent application of the model to technically inadequate recordings.

Patient‐Level Data Partitioning

To prevent data leakage, patient‐level stratified data splitting was implemented. Rather than randomly partitioning individual observations, patients were assigned to training, validation, and test sets such that all observations from a given patient were contained within a single partition, preventing scenarios where multiple measurements from the same patient could appear in both training and test sets, which would artificially inflate performance metrics (see Figure 2).

Patient identifiers for each scenario were extracted and randomized using a fixed random seed (seed = 42) to ensure reproducibility, then partitioned according to a 60:20:20 ratio for training, validation, and test sets respectively. This allocation strategy was justified by the substantial dataset size (over 2000 unique patients contributing more than 4000 ECG observations across scenarios): the 60% training allocation provided sufficient data volume to enable the RUSBoost algorithm to learn robust decision boundaries while performing iterative random undersampling of the majority class, a process that inherently reduces effective training sample size at each boosting iteration; the 20% validation set allocation ensured adequate statistical power for hyperparameter tuning and model selection decisions; and the 20% test set provided a sufficiently large held‐out sample for reliable performance estimation with narrow confidence intervals. Alternative ratios such as 75:15:10 or 80:10:10 (Basco et al. 2025; Nasef et al. 2025) would reduce validation and test partition sizes below levels necessary for stable performance estimation given the patient‐level (rather than observation‐level) partitioning employed, where effective sample sizes correspond to unique patients rather than total observations.

RUSBoost Algorithm for Imbalanced Classification

To address the class imbalance observed in the datasets, the Random Under‐Sampling Boosting (RUSBoost) algorithm was employed. RUSBoost is an ensemble learning method specifically designed for imbalanced classification problems, combining the principles of boosting with random undersampling of the majority class (Seiffert et al. 2010). While alternative oversampling approaches such as SMOTE (Chawla et al. 2002) and hybrid methods (Husain et al. 2025) exist for imbalanced classification, RUSBoost was selected for its combination of random undersampling with ensemble boosting, which addresses class imbalance without generating synthetic minority class samples that may introduce noise or unrealistic feature combinations (Rawat and Mishra 2024). Unlike oversampling techniques that can lead to overfitting through replication or synthetic data generation (Wongvorachan et al. 2023), RUSBoost iteratively creates balanced training subsets through majority class undersampling while building a robust ensemble classifier (Chen et al. 2024). This hybrid sampling and boosting approach has demonstrated effectiveness for learning from skewed training data, making it particularly suitable for the moderately to severely imbalanced scenarios observed in the present ECG classification tasks.

Discussion

Generalization Performance and Metrics Evolution

The confusion matrices and performance evolution across three classification scenarios reveal distinct model generalization patterns reflecting both effective patient‐level partitioning and varying task difficulty. Scenario 1 (excluding borderline cases) achieved the most stable patterns across partitions. True negatives (720, 276, 269) and true positives (827, 291, 281) maintained consistent proportions, with low false positive rates (93, 38, 42) and modest false negative increases (451, 188, 185). Figure 6 exhibits ideal monotonic decline across all metrics: accuracy decreased 73.98%→71.50%→70.79% (3.19 percentage points total degradation), sensitivity declined 64.71%→60.75%→60.30%, and specificity decreased 88.56%→87.90%→86.50%. This pattern confirms patient‐level splitting prevented leakage, with modest degradation indicating the model learned generalizable ECG abnormality patterns rather than memorizing training characteristics.

Scenario 2 (combining abnormal + borderline) showed substantially different patterns. Confusion matrices revealed markedly higher false negatives (650, 226, 216) reflecting increased difficulty detecting the heterogeneous positive class, while false positives (112, 67, 55) also increased. Figure 7 displays the most pronounced degradation: accuracy declined 6.17 percentage points (71.43%→65.26%). All metrics except specificity exhibited monotonic decline. Specificity showed an anomalous pattern, declining sharply 87.47%→77.05% before recovering to 78.17%. While overall training‐to‐test degradation remained monotonic (9.3 percentage points), the validation‐to‐test recovery deviates from ideal patterns, though substantially less than Scenario 3.

Scenario 3 (abnormal vs. normal + borderline) exhibited highest true negative counts (1104, 336, 305) and elevated false positives (198, 75, 67), indicating difficulty distinguishing clearly abnormal cases when borderline findings expand the negative class. Figure 8 reveals concerning non‐monotonic patterns across all metrics. Accuracy declined 71.84%→67.71% before recovering to 69.36% (4.13 percentage point decline, 1.65 percentage point recovery). Sensitivity, specificity, and F1‐score demonstrated identical patterns. This non‐monotonic evolution deviates from expected progressive decline patterns, suggesting the validation set may have presented anomalously challenging patient distribution rather than intermediate difficulty.

Comparative Analysis Across Scenarios

Cross‐scenario comparison reveals how borderline case treatment influences classification difficulty. Scenario 1 achieved optimal sensitivity‐specificity balance (60.30%/86.50% test), while Scenario 2 showed lowest specificity (78.17%) and Scenario 3 lowest sensitivity (57.84%), reflecting inherent tradeoffs when borderline cases aggregate with either abnormal or normal classes.

Training‐to‐test degradation differed markedly: Scenario 1 exhibited smallest decline (3.19 percentage points) with ideal monotonic patterns; Scenario 2 showed largest decline (6.17 percentage points) but maintained monotonicity reflecting genuine task difficulty; Scenario 3 demonstrated non‐monotonic patterns (declining 71.84%→67.71% before recovering to 69.36%) diminishing deployment confidence despite superficially small overall degradation (2.48 percentage points). All scenarios favored specificity over sensitivity (training: 84.79%–88.56% vs. 59.49%–64.71%), indicating conservative decision boundaries despite random undersampling.

Learning Dynamics and Training Convergence

The learning curves presented in Figures 9, 10, 11 provide insights into the training dynamics and convergence characteristics of the RUSBoost models across the three classification scenarios. The convergence patterns reveal substantial differences in learning difficulty that correlate with class imbalance severity and task complexity.

Learning Curves

Learning curves revealed distinct convergence patterns across scenarios reflecting task complexity and class balance effects. Scenario 1 demonstrated healthy learning dynamics with gradual accuracy improvement over 20 epochs followed by stable plateau, maintaining a persistent 3–4 percentage point training‐validation gap throughout 100 cycles without divergence, confirming appropriate training duration and successful learning of generalizable decision boundaries between unambiguous abnormal and normal ECG patterns. Scenario 2 exhibited markedly different dynamics requiring ~50 learning cycles to reach plateau versus 20 in Scenario 1, with substantially wider training‐validation gap (5–6 vs. 3–4 percentage points) and persistent validation accuracy oscillation indicating genuine difficulty generalizing the heterogeneous abnormal + borderline class to novel patients, likely stemming from 2:1 class imbalance combined with inherent ambiguity of borderline ECG findings; this wider generalization gap directly corresponds to the steeper performance degradation (6.17 percentage points training‐to‐test) documented in confusion matrix analysis.

Scenario 3 exhibited unusually abrupt learning plateau with both training and validation accuracy rising sharply within 10–15 cycles before reaching completely flat plateaus persisting unchanged for remaining 85–90 iterations (virtually zero improvement beyond epoch 10), contrasting sharply with gradual sustained improvement in Scenario 1 (20 cycles) and Scenario 2 (50 cycles) and suggesting the model identified a simple separating boundary early and ceased exploring more complex feature interactions; moreover, the 4 percentage point training‐validation gap in Scenario 3 exceeds Scenario 1's 3 percentage point gap despite balanced classes, and the flat plateau across 90 subsequent cycles provides no evidence of decision boundary refinement, raising questions whether different hyperparameters might have enabled continued improvement or whether the task definition itself presents fundamental ambiguity preventing deeper pattern learning—collectively, the combination of rapid plateau, wider training‐validation gap than Scenario 1, and non‐monotonic accuracy pattern indicates Scenario 3 presents the most problematic learning dynamics among all scenarios despite the superficial advantage of balanced class distribution.

P‐R Curves

Precision‐recall curve analysis revealed distinct calibration generalization patterns across scenarios reflecting task complexity and class definition coherence. Scenario 1 demonstrated tight overlap across training, validation, and test sets, indicating the model maintains consistent precision‐recall tradeoffs when applied to novel patients and confirming robust calibration where confidence scores generalize appropriately to held‐out data, though precision declined steeply at recall values above 0.85, reflecting the universal challenge in medical classification where capturing nearly all positive cases inevitably increases false positive rates as decision thresholds become more permissive. Scenario 2 exhibited greater separation with validation and test curves shifted downward relative to training across the entire recall range, indicating that predictions achieving a given precision level on training data require accepting lower recall on held‐out data and reflecting genuine difficulty in generalizing the heterogeneous abnormal + borderline class to new patients where borderline cases may exhibit greater inter‐patient variability; this precision‐recall curve separation provides more granular evidence of generalization challenges than single‐point metrics, demonstrating that difficulty pervades the entire range of classification thresholds rather than being isolated to a particular operating point. Scenario 3 demonstrated precision‐recall curves with separation resembling Scenario 2 rather than Scenario 1's tight overlap, with the training curve maintaining systematically higher precision across most of the recall range while validation and test curves track each other at lower precision values, indicating the model's learned decision function does not generalize precision‐recall tradeoffs as tightly as Scenario 1 despite the near‐balanced class distribution and suggesting that grouping borderline cases with normal findings introduces classification ambiguity affecting model calibration across the full range of decision thresholds, not merely at the default 0.5 threshold where non‐monotonic accuracy was observed.

The systematic differences underscore that Scenario 1's clearly defined class boundaries facilitate both efficient learning and robust generalization, Scenario 2's aggregation of heterogeneous clinical presentations increases both learning difficulty and generalization challenges despite potentially being justified by clinical decision‐making requirements, and Scenario 3's rapid convergence belies underlying generalization difficulties evidenced by separated precision‐recall curves, highlighting that convergence speed and class balance alone do not guarantee robust generalization; the precision‐recall curve patterns provide critical complementary information to single‐point accuracy metrics, revealing that generalization challenges in Scenarios 2 and 3 affect model calibration across the full spectrum of classification thresholds rather than being artifacts of a particular decision threshold.

Calibration analysis revealed an unexpected inverse relationship between classification performance and probability calibration quality. Scenario 1, despite achieving superior classification metrics, exhibited the highest uncalibrated ECE (0.0799), indicating that RUSBoost probability outputs showed moderate systematic deviation from true class frequencies. This miscalibration likely reflects the aggressive undersampling inherent to RUSBoost, which equalizes class frequencies during training but produces probability estimates biased toward the originally majority class. Platt scaling reduced Scenario 1 ECE to 0.0353, demonstrating that a simple logistic transformation fitted on validation data effectively corrects this systematic bias without altering test set evaluation integrity. Scenarios 2 and 3, despite inferior classification performance, demonstrated substantially better baseline calibration (ECE 0.0295 and 0.0387 respectively), suggesting that the heterogeneity introduced by borderline cases, while complicating classification boundaries, paradoxically produces more calibrated probability estimates in the uncalibrated model. Post hoc calibration improved all scenarios, with Platt scaling generally outperforming isotonic regression except in Scenario 3 where isotonic regression achieved the lowest ECE (0.0167). The isotonic regression performance degradation in Scenario 2 (ECE increasing from 0.0295 to 0.0346) suggests overfitting of the nonparametric isotonic mapping to validation set characteristics that did not generalize to the test set. For clinical deployment, Platt scaling represents the preferred calibration approach given its consistent improvement across scenarios and resistance to overfitting compared to isotonic regression.

The non‐monotonic pattern in Scenario 3 is methodologically problematic because it contradicts the fundamental premise of progressive unfamiliarity in properly partitioned datasets. When patient‐level splitting is correctly implemented, performance should decline monotonically (train→val→test). Validation‐to‐test recovery indicates either: (1) validation set contained unrepresentatively difficult patient distribution due to stochastic sampling variation, (2) test set contained unrepresentatively easy distribution, or (3) subtle information leakage occurred, though this is less likely given verification procedures confirming no patient overlap.

While overall training‐to‐test degradation in Scenario 3 remains modest at 2.48 percentage points (less than Scenario 1's 3.19 percentage points), this superficially favorable comparison masks fundamental uncertainty introduced by the intermediate validation performance anomaly. The non‐monotonic pattern creates ambiguity about which held‐out set provides more reliable real‐world generalization estimates. If validation set performance (67.71% accuracy) represents true clinical generalization capability, then test set performance (69.36% accuracy) provides false optimism potentially leading to deployment of systems performing worse than anticipated. Conversely, if test set accurately represents true generalization, then validation set provided unnecessarily pessimistic estimates that might have led to premature abandonment or misdirected hyperparameter optimization efforts. This ambiguity undermines confidence in deployment‐readiness assessments.

The convergence of multiple concerning signals in Scenario 3 (i.e., non‐monotonic accuracy, premature learning plateau, wider training‐validation gap than better‐performing Scenario 1 and separated precision‐recall curves) collectively indicates the most problematic methodological profile among all scenarios evaluated.

In contrast, Scenario 1's monotonic pattern across all metrics provides confidence that both validation (71.50% accuracy) and test (70.79% accuracy) sets represent progressively challenging but properly independent patient populations, with performance estimates bracketing expected real‐world deployment performance within a narrow 0.71 percentage point band. The gradual learning convergence over 20 epochs, modest stable training‐validation gap, and tight precision‐recall curve overlap collectively demonstrate that clear separation of borderline cases from both positive and negative classes facilitates learning of robust, well‐calibrated decision boundaries generalizing reliably to unseen patients. Although Scenario 1 exhibited highest uncalibrated ECE requiring post hoc correction, Platt scaling effectively addresses this limitation, reducing ECE from 0.0799 to 0.0353 while maintaining classification performance on the held‐out test set.

Scenario 2, while exhibiting steeper performance degradation (6.17 percentage points) and requiring extended training (50 epochs), nonetheless maintains mostly monotonic decline and demonstrates superior baseline calibration (ECE 0.0295), indicating that generalization difficulties stem from genuine complexity of the heterogeneous positive class rather than methodological artifacts. The slow convergence and separated precision‐recall curves indicate genuine challenge in generalizing borderline cases, but the overall pattern remains interpretable, and performance estimates remain reliable even if less favorable than Scenario 1. Scenario 3, despite achieving the best isotonic regression calibration (ECE 0.0167), remains the most problematic scenario due to non‐monotonic performance patterns and premature learning plateau, highlighting that calibration quality alone does not compensate for fundamental uncertainty in generalization estimates.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First, regarding data partitioning methodology, we employed a single three‐way split (60% training, 20% validation, 20% test) with patient‐level grouping rather than repeated grouped splits or grouped cross‐validation procedures such as GroupKFold. We note that k‐fold cross‐validation is most critical when employing two‐way data splitting (training and test only), where the same held‐out partition serves both for hyperparameter tuning and final performance estimation, creating risk of optimistic bias through indirect test set leakage. Our three‐way splitting approach, where the validation set was used exclusively for monitoring learning curves and hyperparameter selection while the test set remained strictly held out for final unbiased evaluation, mitigates this concern. With 2156 unique patients in Scenarios 2 and 3 (1928 in Scenario 1), the 20% allocation yielded 385–431 patients per held‐out partition, providing reasonable statistical power for performance estimation. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a single split cannot fully characterize sampling variance, and the non‐monotonic pattern observed in Scenario 3, where test accuracy exceeded validation accuracy, could arise from stochastic sampling variability rather than representing a true methodological concern or information leakage. Future work employing repeated grouped splits with different random seeds or GroupKFold cross‐validation could provide confidence intervals around performance estimates and help determine whether the observed non‐monotonic patterns represent true phenomena or sampling artifacts.

Second, the composite patient identifier constructed from weight, sex, diabetes status, and smoking history, while providing reasonable approximation to patient‐level grouping in the absence of true anonymized identifiers, may not perfectly capture patient uniqueness across longitudinal observations. Third, the exclusion of “Neither” (uninterpretable) ECG recordings limits generalizability to clinical workflows where such recordings would require separate quality control filtering prior to algorithmic classification. Fourth, the study analyzed summary‐statistic ECG features rather than raw waveform data, precluding assessment of whether the classification patterns would generalize to end‐to‐end deep learning approaches operating directly on voltage time‐series.

Conclusion

This study evaluated ECG abnormality detection across three classification scenarios using RUSBoost with systematic patient‐level data partitioning. The findings underscore a critical methodological imperative: aggregate performance metrics alone provide insufficient evidence for clinical deployment decisions. Medical AI publications, regulatory submissions, and clinical deployment decisions should require complete reporting of learning curves, performance evolution across partitions, precision‐recall curves for all sets, and explicit assessment of monotonic decline patterns. Journal and regulatory evaluators should reject submissions reporting only test metrics without demonstrating stable learning dynamics and appropriate generalization patterns. This shift from metric‐focused to process‐focused evaluation represents necessary maturation of medical AI methodology, transitioning the field toward comprehensive validation standards appropriate for life‐critical clinical decisions.

Class Imbalance Analysis

As described in Section 2.5, the imbalance ratio (IR, Equation (5)) was calculated for each classification scenario to quantify class asymmetry. The three scenarios exhibited substantially different imbalance patterns due to differential treatment of borderline ECG cases.

Scenario 1 (excluding borderline cases) exhibited an imbalance ratio of 1.55:1, with 2223 abnormal cases (60.72%) constituting the majority class and 1438 normal cases (39.28%) representing the minority class. This ratio exceeded the 1.5:1 threshold defined in Methods, indicating significant class asymmetry requiring specialized algorithmic handling. The moderate imbalance reflected natural prevalence patterns in the clinical dataset, where clearly abnormal ECG findings outnumbered unambiguously normal findings by approximately 3:2.

Scenario 2 demonstrated more pronounced imbalance with a ratio of 2.00:1, comprising 2870 abnormal cases (66.62%) and 1438 normal cases (33.38%). This two‐fold imbalance resulted directly from including 647 borderline cases within the abnormal class definition, expanding the positive class from 2223 cases (Scenario 1) to 2870 cases while the normal class count remained constant at 1438. This produced the highest imbalance ratio among all scenarios, with abnormal cases comprising approximately two‐thirds of the dataset.

Scenario 3 presented the most balanced distribution with an imbalance ratio of 1.07:1, containing 2223 abnormal cases (51.60%) and 2085 normal cases (48.40%). The near‐parity resulted from the conservative classification approach grouping the 647 borderline cases with the normal class, increasing the negative class from 1438 normal cases (Scenario 1) to 2085 combined normal and borderline cases (Scenario 3), producing a distribution closely approaching ideal 1:1 balance. With an imbalance ratio below the 1.5:1 threshold, Scenario 3 represented the only classification task where class imbalance was not considered significant.

The systematic variation in imbalance ratios (1.55:1, 2.00:1, and 1.07:1) demonstrated that the 647 borderline cases constituted a sufficiently large subset (approximately 15% of observations after excluding neither cases) to substantially alter the statistical properties of the classification task depending on their categorical assignment.

To address class imbalance, the RUSBoost algorithm was employed for model training across all three scenarios for consistency. RUSBoost addresses class asymmetry by randomly undersampling the majority class at each boosting iteration to achieve 1:1 parity with the minority class during weak learner training. In Scenario 1, this procedure randomly sampled 813 abnormal cases at each training iteration from the 1278 total abnormal cases in the training set. In Scenario 2, undersampling was more aggressive, selecting 894 abnormal cases from 1773 total at each iteration. In Scenario 3, minimal undersampling was required, with only 1302 abnormal cases needing to be matched against 1365 normal cases, reflecting the near‐balanced nature of this scenario's class distribution.

The training set imbalance ratios closely mirrored overall dataset imbalance for each scenario: 1.57:1 for Scenario 1 (1278 abnormal vs. 813 normal), 1.98:1 for Scenario 2 (1773 abnormal vs. 894 normal), and 1.05:1 for Scenario 3 (1365 abnormal vs. 1302 normal). This consistency between overall and training set imbalance ratios confirmed that patient‐level data splitting preserved natural class distribution across partitions rather than introducing artificial imbalance through sampling artifacts.
