Flashbacks to Harmonize Stability and Plasticity in Continual Learning Note: Manuscript submitted to Neural Networks (Elsevier) in August 2024; and accepted in May 2025 for publication. Journal: Neural Networks
Leila Mahmoodi, Peyman Moghadam, Munawar Hayat, Christian Simon, Mehrtash Harandi

Abstract
We introduce Flashback Learning (FL), a novel method designed to harmonize the stability and plasticity of models in Continual Learning (CL). Unlike prior approaches that primarily focus on regularizing model updates to preserve old information while learning new concepts, FL explicitly balances this trade-off through a bidirectional form of regularization. This approach effectively guides the model to swiftly incorporate new knowledge while actively retaining its old knowledge. FL operates through a two-phase training process and can be seamlessly integrated into various CL methods, including replay, parameter regularization, distillation, and dynamic architecture techniques. In designing FL, we use two distinct knowledge bases : one to enhance plasticity and another to improve stability. FL ensures a more balanced model by utilizing both knowledge bases to regularize model updates. Theoretically, we analyze how the FL mechanism enhances the stability-plasticity balance. Empirically, FL demonstrates tangible improvements over baseline methods within the same training budget. By integrating FL into at least one representative baseline from each CL category, we observed an average accuracy improvement of up to 4.91% in Class-Incremental and 3.51% in Task-Incremental settings on standard image classification benchmarks. Additionally, measurements of the stability-to-plasticity ratio confirm that FL effectively enhances this balance. FL also outperforms state-of-the-art CL methods on more challenging datasets like ImageNet. The codes of this article will be available at https://github.com/csiro-robotics/Flashback-Learning .

Our brain excels at learning new information without significantly disrupting or forgetting previously acquired knowledge. In contrast, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) often struggle with Catastrophic Forgetting (CF)
[
1

, where new information can overwrite and interfere with existing knowledge. This phenomenon poses a significant challenge when continuous learning of new tasks is required. Continual Learning (CL) methods have been developed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by enabling DNNs to retain prior knowledge while acquiring new skills.

Deploying AI models in realistic settings demands that models function effectively when encountering data with varying distributions over time. Therefore, updating the model based on newly received data after training on previous tasks is necessary. However, storing all previous data and retraining the model on the entire dataset from scratch is not feasible due to storage, processing, or privacy constraints. Consequently, many studies have focused on developing methods to learn from new data while avoiding CF. The primary challenge in this endeavor is to achieve a delicate balance between the model’s

In this study, we build upon our initial research

The encouraging results from that investigation motivated us to develop a mechanism that can be adapted to various categories of CL methods. We demonstrate the benefits of this approach across a broad range of CL techniques. To be more specific, we formulate FL for distillation, memory rehearsal, parameter regularization, and dynamic architecture methods. The first phase of FL focuses on compiling plastic knowledge as the new task commences and its data becomes available. We update the old model with new task data throughout this phase, prioritizing the swift acquisition of new information over preserving the old one. This phase creates a primary version of the new model, which is essential for accumulating plastic knowledge.
In the second phase, termed “flashback," the old model revisits new task data once more. This phase guides model updates through a bidirectional knowledge flow, in which one side utilizes stable knowledge to enforce stability while the other employs plastic knowledge to drive plasticity. Theoretically, we show that the bidirectional knowledge flow moves the model weights towards maintaining an interpolation between old and new tasks’ representations, directly contributing to stability-plasticity balance.

We conduct comprehensive experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the FL mechanism in improving CL methods. Our evaluation covers several established CL image classification benchmarks, including Split CIFAR-10, Split CIFAR-100, and Split Tiny ImageNet, across two primary settings: Class-Incremental (CI) and Task-Incremental (TI) Learning.
We assess at least one method from each CL category on all benchmarks under both CI and TI settings, including LUCIR

We observe FL significantly improves CL methods’ average accuracy up to 4.9% and 3.5%, respectively, under CI and TI settings within the same training budget. Moreover, FL effectively reduces average forgetting and preserves performance on old tasks in CL methods. In summary, this work makes the following contributions:

1.

We propose flashback learning, providing a mechanism for existing CL methods to improve their stability-plasticity balance. FL functions in two phases: building an expert knowledge base from new task data to promote plasticity and utilizing it to counteract the unidirectional stability regularization in common CL methods.

2.

We theoretically show how bidirectional regularization in the second phase of our algorithm enhances the stability-plasticity balance in previous CL methods.

3.

We develop and demonstrate the efficacy of FL across four main categories of CL methods: 1) parameter constraint-based methods, 2) knowledge distillation methods, 3) replay memory methods (including both partial old sample reuse and generative memory), and 4) architecture expansion methods. Our experiments show that FL provides tangible improvements in all these categories.

4.

We conduct comprehensive experiments to benchmark FL against several distinct CL methods across multiple datasets. Our results consistently indicate that FL enhances performance, demonstrating substantial improvements even when integrated with advanced CL strategies.

Flashback Learning

Figure 2
:

Phase 2
-flashbacks from primary to the old model

CL methods update the model sequentially on tasks
\mathbb{T}=\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
; within this sequential update, at each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, the model should ideally learn new information from task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and keep what it has learned from previous tasks
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
. Each method stores some information from previous tasks—we denote it as
stable knowledge
in this study — and develops a strategy to maintain this information within the model while updating on task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Stable knowledge can range from a copy of the old model to a memory of past samples, and the corresponding strategy varies from distillation to replay accordingly. However, the fundamental pattern is the same - stable knowledge is transferred from previous tasks to the new model, and the main focus is on model stability by enforcing previously acquired knowledge to remain within the model throughout updates on task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. While success for CL models relies on balancing stability and plasticity, overemphasizing one side can compromise the other. Most current strategies in CL methods dominantly focus on maintaining stability during updates on the new task, which can consequently hinder the model’s plasticity, and the model cannot learn effectively from new data. Therefore, it is crucial to give adequate emphasis to both stability and plasticity in the model. To this end, we introduce
Flashback Learning (FL)
, a two-phase plugin mechanism compatible with various CL methods.

FL formulates a bidirectional knowledge transfer to concurrently promote model stability and plasticity, improving their balance in CL methods. When integrating FL with CL methods, we aim to refine some knowledge from task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
to create a new source promoting plasticity, named “
plastic knowledge
". Then, stable and plastic knowledge makes bidirectional knowledge transfer feasible in a subsequent step. In this bidirectional transfer, one side works towards stability and the other towards plasticity, ultimately achieving a balance. FL operates in
two phases
:
Phase 1
is designed to extract plastic knowledge from task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
by which we control plasticity in the next phase, and
Phase 2
aims to harmonize stability-plasticity balance by leveraging stable and plastic knowledge. When task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
commences, we define
stable model
by
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}^{*})
— model learned in prior tasks— and denote stable model by
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
in FL algorithm, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}^{*}
.

During
Phase 1
(see Figure
2
), we start from
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}^{*}
and update the model on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
to obtain a primary version of the new model, denoted
primary model

f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
, here
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
shows the model parameters after seeing
\mathcal{D}_{t}
in
Phase 1
. This initial phase prioritizes the rapid acquisition of task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
information, deferring the incorporation of previously learned knowledge. Consequently, the number of training iterations is limited, and the transfer of stable knowledge to the new model is not pursued at this stage. Different categories of CL methods can act as a host for the FL algorithm. These categories include
1.
distillation,
2.
memory replay,
3.
parameter regularization, and
4.
dynamic architecture methods. The stable knowledge that a host method carries from past tasks depends on its strategy for maintaining stability. For example, a distillation-based method has access to a copy of the old model during task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, which distills knowledge into the new model and preserves stability. In FL, we keep the stable knowledge carried by the host method in the
Stable Knowledge Base (SKB)
. We identify what information a host method from different CL categories holds after task
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
for its stability retention strategy in task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and store the same information in SKB without incurring additional expenses (see §
3.1
.)

In
Phase 1
, primary model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
gains new insights of task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. This model plays the main role in compiling plastic knowledge. We ensure that plastic knowledge is compatible with the stable knowledge that the host CL algorithm has retained from the past. We store this plastic knowledge in the
Plastic Knowledge Base (PKB)
. PKB may vary within different CL methods to maintain consistency with stable knowledge. For instance, distillation-based methods hold a copy of the old model; accordingly, we keep a copy of the primary model in PKB. We provide further details on how to derive plastic knowledge for each CL category in §
3.2
.

Phase 2
(see Figure
2
) involves a flashback to the starting point, which means resetting the model to the stable model (
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
) and revisiting
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. FL aims to balance stability and plasticity in this phase through a bidirectional regularization process. FL establishes a two-way flow of knowledge by using the information compiled in the former phase’s SKB and PKB to regularize the model. The bidirectional regularization process includes two loss components. The first component uses knowledge from SKB to encourage model stability, while the second component enhances model plasticity by utilizing knowledge from PKB to learn new tasks effectively. For more information on the FL phases, please refer to §
3.3
.

3.1.3
Parameter Regularization

Regularization methods often assume past tasks’ distributions are captured in the Fisher Information Matrices (FIMs) calculated on previous tasks. This assumption leads to different approaches to approximate
\mathbf{F}_{i}
. The FIM can be used to regularize the training objective as:

\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{i})\right)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\right).

(3)

Here, each component
\mathcal{L}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{i})\right)
represents the negative log-likelihood
-\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\big(-\log p\left(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\big)
and
\lambda_{i}
regulates the importance of the i
th
task among the seen tasks. EWC
[
28
]
estimates FIM of task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
by:

\mathbf{F}_{i}\approx\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\bigg[\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*}}\Big)^{\top}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*}}\Big)\bigg]\quad i=\{1,\dots,t-1\},

(4)

where
p\left(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
is the probability distribution parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and its gradient is evaluated at
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}

Keeping one FIM per task imposes extra complexity. The online EWC approach
[
5

proposes an average FIM with a recursive update:

\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i}=\gamma\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1}+\mathbf{F}_{i},

(5)

where

is the average FIM, and

is a hyperparameter to adjust the contribution of the former task’s average FIM,

the average FIM,

t-1
number of FIMs (
i.e
.

\{\mathbf{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}

\mathcal{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\,\mathbf{F}_{s}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}^{*},\,\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{t-1}\}.

3.3
Flashback Learning Phases

With a clear definition of stable and plastic knowledge in §
3.1
and §
3.2
, we explain how SKB and PKB are incorporated in FL phases to harmonize the stability-plasticity balance.

3.3.1
Phase 1

This phase plays a preparation step for bidirectional regularization; we update the model here to rapidly learn
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and then refine the plastic knowledge. We update the model by the same loss function as the host CL method for a limited number of epochs
E_{1}
. The loss function is split into two components;

\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;.

(14)

The first component
\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the task objective function (
e.g
.
classification loss), which helps the model to learn task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Stability loss
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
takes stable knowledge
\mathcal{S}
from SKB to regularize model updates in a unidirectional manner, and
\alpha_{s}
is its scaler. Here, we explain how CL methods engage their available stable knowledge in the stability loss:

1.

Distillation methods
’ stability loss utilizes stable knowledge (
1
) to preserve new model representations close to stable model at feature embeddings, logits, or even intermediate features. For instance, in LUCIR
[
21
]
, the stability loss keeps the similarity between
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta})
’s and
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
’s normalized outputs by cosine embedding loss. The general form (See
B
) of stability distillation loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big\rVert^{2}\Big]\;.

(15)

2.

Replay methods
consider a stability loss component in their objective function that takes the memory samples into joint training. For example, in DER
[
26
]
,
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the mean squared error (MSE) loss between the model’s response
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
to samples in memory (
i.e
.

\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
) and the corresponding old logits
\mathbf{o}_{s}
in SKB (
2
). So, the stability loss forces the model response to memory samples to remain the same as old responses,

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{o}_{s})\sim\mathcal{S}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{o}_{s}\big\rVert^{2}\Big].

(16)

3.

Parameter regularization methods
put their stable knowledge (
6
) into a stability loss component to impose constraints on model updates so old critical parameters are preserved. The stability loss is the distance between the parameters of the model under training and the stable model weighted by the stable FIM as:

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}}\right).

(17)

4.

Dynamic architecture methods
update task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
’s expanded feature extractor (
7
), while its stable feature extractor
h(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s})
(
8
) is frozen. The stability loss formulation depends on how they devise the method to compatibly integrate representation learned by the new module
m(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{t})
into
h(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s})
; various forms of distillation loss are commonly used to maintain the similarity between probability distribution modeled by current model (
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
after expansion) and stable model
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
. Let
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathrm{Softmax}\big(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})=\mathrm{Softmax}\big(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big)
be the output of current and stable models after
Softmax
layer, respectively. FOSTER
[
23
]
employs the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss between
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
as:

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big].

(18)

Assuming a linear classifier,
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=g(h(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\phi});\boldsymbol{\psi})=\boldsymbol{\psi}h(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\phi})
and the expanded feature extractor (
7
); we decompose task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
’s model to
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\psi}\begin{bmatrix}h(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s})\\
m(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\varphi})\end{bmatrix}
; and its stable model to
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}h(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s})
to detail trainable parameter set as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\}
. Stability loss (
18
) attempts to reduce the distance between the predicted distribution parameterized by
f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
.

To recap,
Phase 1
updates the model on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
for limited epochs
E_{1}
with objective function
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(
14
). We define this objective function per CL category in (
15
)-(
18
). We obtain primary model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
at the end of this phase, from which we derive plastic knowledge
\mathcal{P}
(
9
)-(
13
) for the next phase.

3.3.2
Phase 2

Having refined plastic knowledge
\mathcal{P}
compatible with the stable knowledge
\mathcal{S}
kept by the host CL method, we access two required sources, SKB and PKB, for bidirectional knowledge transfer. In this phase, we re-initialize the model to stable model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
; and proceed with training on task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
for a sufficient number of epochs
E_{2}
by below loss function;

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{p}\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(19)

The first two terms of
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in (
19
), task-specific objective function
\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and stability loss
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, are analogous to
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in (
14
). While stability loss benefits from stable knowledge in SKB to enhance model stability, the additional term
\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is introduced to take plastic knowledge
\mathcal{P}
from PKB and adjust the model’s plasticity;
\alpha_{p}
is its scaler. Therefore, stability-plasticity balance is harmonized by the counteract between stability and plasticity loss components in a bidirectional flow. Here, we explain how the FL mechanism takes plastic knowledge into the plasticity loss:

1.

Distillation methods
attempt to keep model representations close to the stable model (
1
) in the stability loss (
15
); so, we formulate the plasticity loss to concurrently keep model representation close to the primary model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
kept in the PKB (
9

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\big\rVert^{2}\Big]\;.

(20)

2.

Replay methods’
stability loss (
16

\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
and their corresponding old logits
\mathbf{o}_{s}
in SKB (
2

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{o}_{p})\sim(\mathcal{M}_{t-1},\mathcal{P})}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{o}_{p}\big\rVert^{2}\Big].

(21)

to reduce the distance between model response to memory samples and their primary logits
\mathbf{o}_{p}
kept in its PKB (
10
).

3.

Parameter regularization methods
’s stability loss (
17

\mathcal{T}_{t}
’s primary parameters and their FIM from PKB (

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{p}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}\right),

(22)

with a focus on critical parameters to task

4.

In

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big];

in which
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})=\mathrm{Softmax}\big(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\big)
is the output after softmax layer in primary new model

f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{p}\begin{bmatrix}h(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s})\\
m(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{p})\end{bmatrix}
; so it has absorbed task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
knowledge in its new module kept in PKB (

To summarize FL’s

by optimizing

).

3.3.3
Training and Memory Budget

We design FL to maintain a training budget comparable to that of the host CL method. Given that the host CL method trains for
E_{\text{CL}}
epochs, we structure FL training in two phases with the same number of epochs. In
Phase 1
, the model is trained on the current task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
for a small number of epochs (
E_{1}\ll E_{\text{CL}}
), allowing it to quickly adapt.
Phase 2
then resumes training from the old checkpoint for
E_{2}=E_{\text{CL}}-E_{1}
epochs. This ensures that the total effective training epochs for FL remain the same as the host CL method, i.e.,

E_{\text{FL}}=E_{1}+E_{2}=E_{\text{CL}}.

In terms of memory overhead, FL approximately doubles the memory requirement of the host CL method, requiring a total memory budget of:

\mathbf{M}_{\text{FL}}=\mathbf{M}_{\text{PKB}}+\mathbf{M}_{\text{SKB}}=2\mathbf{M}_{\text{CL}}.

For the
SKB
, FL follows the memory strategy of the host CL method. If the host CL method allocates
\mathbf{M}_{\text{CL}}
for storing old model checkpoints, parameters, or a subset of past samples, FL uses the same amount for SKB, ensuring

\mathbf{M}_{\text{SKB}}=\mathbf{M}_{\text{CL}}.

For the
PKB
, FL extracts plastic knowledge from the new model in a manner consistent with how the host CL method preserves stable knowledge. If the host method requires
\mathbf{M}_{\text{CL}}
to store stable knowledge, an equal amount is allocated for PKB, leading to

\mathbf{M}_{\text{PKB}}=\mathbf{M}_{\text{CL}}.

Thus, while FL introduces additional memory overhead, it does so in a structured and predictable manner, ensuring that training remains efficient without exceeding the computational constraints of the host CL method.

4
Stability-Plasticity Analysis

This section demonstrates how FL can affect stability and plasticity in continual learning. Consider a model with a parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The aim is to update the model for task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. We distinguish between two cases. In the CL setting, the updated model is obtained by minimizing:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;.

In contrast, and as alluded to earlier, for FL, we perform:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{p}\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;.

We show that including
\alpha_{p}\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
will enhance the balance between stability and plasticity. Our theoretical development is based on the analysis of the gradient behavior in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{s}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\alpha_{p}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{p}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;.

4.1
Distillation Methods

In distillation methods, the form of stability (
15
) and plasticity (
20
) loss are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big\rVert^{2}\Big]\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\big\rVert^{2}\Big]\;;

which leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Gradient Decomposition in Distillation Methods)
.

Let
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
, and
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
denote a model, its stable version from SKB (
1
), and its primary new version from PKB (
9
) respectively; then the gradient of distillation-based methods at a sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\alpha_{s}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\left(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}\;.

(24)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\left(\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}\right)\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\left(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\alpha_{s}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})+\alpha_{p}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})}{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}\right)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}\;.

(25)

See §
C.1
for the proof.

The interpolation term in gradient (
25
) drives the output
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
toward an interpolation between the stable response
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and the primary new response
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
. The stable response encapsulates information from the old tasks
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
, while the primary model contains information from task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Therefore, the interpolation term guides the model output toward a balance between
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
and will enhance the stability-plasticity trade-off.

4.2
Replay Methods

We turn our attention to replay methods and show similar behavior to what we observed in distillation methods that can emerge with FL. With stability (
16
) and plasticity (
21
) loss in the form of:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{o}_{s})\sim\mathcal{S}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{o}_{s}\big\rVert^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{o}_{p})\sim(\mathcal{M}_{t-1},\mathcal{P})}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{o}_{p}\big\rVert^{2}\Big]

the following theorem formalizes the behavior.

Theorem 2

(Gradient Decomposition in Replay Methods)
.

Let
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be a sample from memory
\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
, and
\mathbf{o}_{s}
and
\mathbf{o}_{p}
be its corresponding stable logit kept in SKB (
2
) and primary new logits kept in PKB (
10
), respectively.
The gradient of replay methods can be decomposed into two terms for CL and FL as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\alpha_{s}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\left(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{o}_{s}\right)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}\;.

(26)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\left(\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}\right)\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\left(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{o}_{s}+\alpha_{p}\mathbf{o}_{p}}{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}\right)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}\;.

(27)

See §
C.2
for the proof.

Once again, we note that in FL, an interpolation term is used during training. Unlike distillation methods, this gradient interpolation term in replay methods is determined using the logits of the SKB (
2
) and PKB (
10
). If the logits
\mathbf{o}_{s}
and
\mathbf{o}_{p}
are equal, the gradient term simplifies to that of the CL methods. If the stable model is sufficiently rich to generate the logits for the new task accurately, then a simple regularization is all we need in FL. However, if this is not the case, the gradient updates are governed by both SKB (
2
) and PKB (
10
) logits. The contributions of SKB and PKB are adjusted by the hyperparameters
\alpha_{s}
and
\alpha_{p}
, providing the designer with enough flexibility to update the model.

4.3
Parameter Regularization Methods

In this part, we will develop the behavior of FL for regularization methods, focusing on the role of the FIMs kept in SKB (
6
) and PKB (
12
). The following theorem will materialize this, considering stability (
17
) and plasticity (
22
) loss of form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{p}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}\right).\

Theorem 3

(Gradient Decomposition in Parameter Regularization Methods )
.

Let
\mathbf{F}_{s}
and
\mathbf{F}_{p}
be the FIM kept in SKB (
6
) and PKB (
12
), respectively. Furthermore, let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
denote the parameters of the stable and primary new version of model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta})
, kept in SKB (
6
) and PKB (
12
), respectively.
Then the gradient can be decomposed into two terms for CL and FL as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\underbrace{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}\;.

(28)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\underbrace{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)+\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}\;.

(29)

See §
C.3
for the proof.

This implies that the parameter update for CL and FL has the following recursive forms (See §
C.5
for details):

\displaystyle\mathrm{CL~Setting:~}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}_{\text{Initial Term}}-\eta\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)}_{\text{Task-specific Term}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)^{j}}_{\text{Stability Term}},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FL~Setting:~}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}_{\text{Initial Term}}-\eta\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)}_{\text{Task-specific Term}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\Gamma_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)^{j}}_{\text{Interpolation Term}}.

(31)

where
\Gamma_{s}=\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}
and
\Gamma_{p}=\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}
. The initial term in Eq. (
30
) and Eq. (
31
) controls how much of the initial condition
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
remains over time. When eigenvalues of
\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}
(or
\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}
) are between 0 and 1, the initial term decays to zeros as
k\to\infty
. The task-specific term stabilizes learning by a sum over past gradients of
\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The influence of past gradients also diminishes over time due to multiplication with
\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}
(or
\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}
). The third term which includes
\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)
(or
\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\Gamma_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)
) determines the steady state solution; and if
\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}
(or
\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}
) decays over time, the final steady-state solution depends mainly on
\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)
in CL setting while on
\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\Gamma_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)
in FL setting. Thus, the SGD trajectory in the CL setting (
30
) shows how the parameter updates remain centered around
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
. Compared to the CL setting, the FL update rule (
31
) incorporates the interpolation term, balancing the influence of both
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
during learning.

4.4
Dynamic Architecture Methods

Recall the model expands its feature extractor (
7
) to learn the new task in dynamic architecture methods. The new module is updated, while the probability distribution parameterized by the expanded model is controlled by KL divergence loss against the model with stable feature extractor (
8
). In the FL case, the probability distribution is concurrently controlled by KL divergence loss against the model with primary new module (
13
). Using the stability (
18
) and plasticity (
18
) loss in the form of KL loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big];

the following theorem can explain the gradient of CL and FL settings for dynamic architecture methods.

Theorem 4

(Gradient Decomposition in Dynamic Architecture Methods )
.

Let
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
be the probability distributions parameterized by a model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta})
after expansion (
7
), its stable version
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
with feature extractor (
6
) and its primary version
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
incorporating the primary new module (
13
), respectively. The gradient for a sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
can be decomposed into two terms for CL and FL as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}\;.

(32)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\frac{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\bigg(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\alpha_{s}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})+\alpha_{p}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})}{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}\bigg)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}\;;

(33)

where
\tau
is the temperature parameter of the Softmax, see §
C.4
for the proof.

The interpolation term in gradient (
33
) drives the probability distribution
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
toward an interpolation between probability distributions parameterized by the stable model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
with the stable feature extractor (
8
) and the primary model
f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
with the primary new module (
13
). The stable output
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
represents old tasks distribution
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
, while the primary output
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
encapsulates
\mathcal{T}_{t}
’s distribution. Therefore, the interpolation term guides
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
toward a balance between probability distributions modeled by stable model
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and primary new model
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
and will enhance the stability-plasticity trade-off.

5
Related Work

We divide CL methods into four categories
[
30
]
and discuss their
recent advancements
here. Moreover, we explain
how

FL

mechanism enhances the balance between stability and plasticity
in each CL category. In the initial phase, the FL mechanism compiles new task information to a knowledge base, promoting plasticity. FL aligns the content of the plastic knowledge base with retained information from previous tasks in the CL methods. This alignment is pivotal, as the subsequent phase entails competing for stability and plasticity using equivalent information from each side. We outline how FL shapes the plastic knowledge base and implements bidirectional regularization across four categories of CL methods.

5.1
Distillation

Distillation-based CL methods
[
6
,
21
,
25
,
31
,
32
]
typically preserve a copy of the old model as their stable knowledge. Distillation occurs at different model representations; for instance,
[
6
]
proposed minimizing the mean square error at logits between the model under training and the stable model.
[
33
,
8
]
perform knowledge distillation on final feature maps before the classification layer. PODNet
[
25
]
extended knowledge distillation to intermediate feature maps, and AFC
[
34
]
incorporated gradient information to weigh the feature distillation loss and preserve essential features for old tasks. Some approaches limit their access only to the new task’s dataset
[
35
]
, while others
[
11
,
36
]
combine distillation with memory replay, focusing only on a selected subset of old samples, or they use some synthesized old samples
[
37
]
for distillation. Some methods approach distillation without keeping the stable model by utilizing encoded old knowledge into gradients
[
38
]
, or gradient norm through a warm-up step for a balanced gradient distillation
[
39
]
; however, the final aim is the same, ensuring stability by transferring knowledge from the previous tasks to the new task.

A successful distillation strategy should ideally balance stability and plasticity in the model, although plasticity has yet to receive as sufficient attention as stability in the literature
[
15
]
. Recent advancements in distillation methods attempt to achieve a better stability-plasticity balance by new features’ projection onto the old ones within several approaches.
[
40
]
uses a predictor network to project the representations from the new feature space (new model’s feature embeddings) to the old one to ensure this new feature space is at least as powerful as the old one by self-supervised learning.
[
41
]
also allows more plasticity to learn new features by projected functional regularization. Although these projection ideas provide more plasticity for the new model, they cannot explicitly take control over plasticity. Even with learnable linear transformation
[
42
]
to simultaneously allow the separability of old classes and provide enough space for a new direction, we don’t have explicit control over plasticity. After adding
FL
to distillation methods, we train the model on new data and conclude
Phase 1
by integrating this model as plastic knowledge. In
Phase 2
, the model is reinitialized to its old weights and trained on new task data again. During this phase, knowledge is distilled from two sources into the model: one side distills from the stable model and the other transfers from the new model stored in the plastic knowledge base. Two-way distillation avoids the overemphasis on stability and balances the focus on stability and plasticity equally.

5.2
Memory Replay

Replay methods
[
11
,
26
,
22
]
guide model updates on the new task using a kept memory from the previous tasks.
[
26
,
22
,
43
,
44
]
maintain limited selected samples of the earlier tasks with their corresponding logits or feature embeddings in memory. Various memory management strategies are presented in the literature to choose old samples for memory. Some methods like Rainbow memory
[
45
]
use classification uncertainty to ensure diversity of selected samples, while others like
[
46
]
and
[
47
]
pick more representative samples by using prototypes from previously seen classes. Moreover, some
[
48
]
prioritize complex samples with high errors in memory selection. Some replay methods enhance their sample selection strategy by incorporating current task information. MIR
[
49
]
selects and replays memory samples whose predictions are most negatively impacted by the current parameter updates. This is achieved by computing the gradient of the loss with respect to the model parameters before and after the update. However, the focus on revisiting knowledge at the highest risk of being forgotten may come at the expense of not learning new knowledge.

Recent methods prefer keeping processed information in memory for replay; this information might include features
[
50
]
,
[
27
]
, or logits by previous models. The obvious advantage is reducing the storage cost; ALIFE
[
51
]
refines and memorizes features of earlier tasks, while
[
52
]
uses features processed by data compression. Some methods
[
53
]
,
[
54
]
, and
[
13
]
leverage generative models to eliminate storage costs; they train a generative model on previous tasks, then synthesize old samples at the beginning of the new task and use them for replay. Replay methods use different types of generative models for this purpose, from variational autoencoders
[
55
]
,
[
56
]
to generative adversarial networks
[
57
]
,
[
58
]
.

Despite the variety of memory content in replay methods, the principle remains the same: leveraging stable knowledge kept in memory in the training objective of task
t
to retain what the model has learned from
\left\{\mathcal{D}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
. DER/DER++
[
26
]
and X-DER
[
22
]
utilize the memory samples in cross-entropy and distillation loss during task
t
training.
[
25
]
and
[
21
]
employ a distillation loss at the feature level on memory samples. GEM
[
59
]
and A-GEM
[
60
]
impose constraints on task
t
optimization by memory samples. As the model learns the new task, replay methods ensure that its responses to memory samples remain consistent with the old predictions. The loss component taking memory content into the objective function directly influences the stability but not plasticity. However, the new model offers more accurate predictions at new logits. In
FL
,
Phase 1
learns the new task and integrates the new model’s predictions for memory samples into the plastic knowledge base. In
Phase 2
, the two-way regularization mechanism adjusts to align model responses to memory samples with the old predictions at old logits while being consistent with the new model’s predictions at new logits. This bidirectional regularization on memory samples brings balance to the stability-plasticity competition.

5.3
Parameter Regularization

Regularization methods aim to understand how modifications to model parameters affect task losses; they restrict updates on critical parameters to previous tasks to avoid forgetting. One notable approach is EWC
[
28
]
introducing the Fisher information matrix to calculate the importance of model parameters. A distance-based penalty term is defined between new and old parameters by applying the calculated Fisher information matrix. When updating the model on task
t
, essential parameters in tasks
\left\{i\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
incur a more significant penalty, resulting in less modification.
[
61
]
proposes an adaptive approach to measure how crucial is each network node for carrying out the previous tasks.
[
62
]
extends the Kronecker-factored approximation of the Hessian matrix to weigh the penalty term between new and old parameters. SI
[
63
]
, and MAS
[
64
]
utilize estimated path integrals and gradient magnitudes to regulate model parameter changes. RWalk
[
3
]
combines the Fisher information matrix and online path integral for approximating parameter importance. Online EWC
[
5
]
presents a solution for efficiently compiling the Fisher information matrix. Meta-NC
[
65
]
leverages the importance of previously learned parameters to compute and preserve the optimal inter-class margin. This computation relies on parameters learned during the meta-training phase. By retaining these parameters, the model ensures that the margin remains optimal, even as new classes are introduced.

Regularization methods rely on old parameters and their importance in encouraging stability and preserving the learned information from
\left\{\mathcal{D}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
within the model. However, their strategy limits the model’s ability to learn task
t
and does not promote plasticity. Some recent approaches
[
66
]
attempt to enhance learning capacity by employing richer architectures like vision transformers, but the objective function incorporates unidirectional regularization on attention layers for stability, not plasticity.
Temporal ensembles
[
67
]
regularize the model weights by computing the exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights during training. This technique smooths out updates, providing a more stable set of weights for evaluation. By mitigating abrupt changes in the current model weights, temporal ensembles ensure more stable performance over time, focusing on stability rather than plasticity. Recent methods, such as La-MAML
[
68
]
, introduce meta-learning into the regularization-based category and aim to improve the stability-plasticity balance through a look-ahead step in the optimization process. La-MAML
[
68
]
anticipates the impact of current updates on future learning and adjusts the learning rates of individual parameters accordingly. This approach leverages prior knowledge of the current task to optimize model performance across all tasks. While using information from the current task does help improve CL performance, fine-grained control is required to modulate the learning rate for each parameter. This makes the algorithm computationally intensive and challenging to scale to more complex sequential tasks. Having learned task
t
quickly in
Phase 1
,
FL
provides regularization methods with the new model parameters and their importance.
Phase 2
leverages old plus new parameters and their importance in regulating parameter updates. FL introduces a bidirectional regularization to control model updates, forcing the model parameters to stay at a proper distance from old and new parameters.

5.4
Dynamic Architecture Method

Architecture-based methods assign a portion of the backbone to each task. This allocation typically occurs either by expanding the architecture at the start of a task
t

[
29
]
,
[
23
]
or learning a suitable mask
[
71
]
,
[
72
]
to activate a part of the model per task. The second approach often requires the task identifier
\left\{\ i\right\}_{i=1}^{\mathcal{T}}
at the inference time to decide which mask to select, making this approach inapplicable to more challenging CL settings where a task identifier is unavailable. Some works extend the model plasticity by adding new neurons;
[
73
]
evaluates loss function and decides to add a specific number of units to each layer when the task begins; or
[
74
]
applies neural architecture search on a given topology, then optimizes the neuron connections to activate neurons gradually.

Some methods
[
29
]
duplicate the entire backbone to expand the architecture, integrating a new learnable feature extractor into the old backbone and using channel-wise masking for pruning. Similarly, FOSTER
[
23
]
expands the backbone and then applies distillation to prevent redundancy in feature extraction and unused dimensions. BEEF
[
24
]
outperforms FOSTER by independently training expanded modules and fusing them for bidirectional compatibility using an energy-based objective function. While dynamic expansion increases plasticity by providing additional capacity, model plasticity is not encouraged in the objective function. They usually freeze the old backbone and update the expanded module on task
t
by knowledge transfer from old representations to maintain performance on tasks
\left\{i\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
; however, this approach may hinder learning task
t
effectively. In contrast,
FL
allows the extended module to learn task
t
without constraints in
Phase 1
and keeps the updated module. In
Phase 2
, FL distills from the updated module into the model to counteract regularization by old representations and encourage plasticity in the objective function.

5.5
Bidirectional Knowledge Transfer for Stabililty-Plasticity Balance

Conceptually close to our approach, P&C
[
5
]
, AFEC
[
69
]
, CAF
[
70
]
, and
[
75
]
make use of bidirectional knowledge transfer to mitigate the stability-plasticity dilemma. However, they differ in how effectively they balance the two opposing forces. P&C
[
5
]
alternates between two distinct phases: Progress and Compress. In the Progress phase, only the active module is trained on the new task while linearly connected to the knowledge base. In the Compress phase, the knowledge base is updated via distillation from the active module and regularization against its previous state. While P&C enables bidirectional knowledge flow, the interaction between the two modules is asymmetric, making it unclear how effectively each side influences the other.

AFEC
[
69
]
addresses the rigidity of parameter preservation by introducing an active forgetting mechanism. Instead of strictly maintaining past knowledge, AFEC expands the model with new parameters, trains them independently on the new task, and then selectively merges them with existing parameters. Although designed as a plug-in method for CL, AFEC’s active forgetting mechanism operates independently of the host method, relying solely on parameter regularization rather than integrating with the host strategy’s knowledge transfer framework. CAF
[
70
]
extends AFEC by employing a weighted average of multiple learners, each trained with a different forgetting rate. However, like AFEC, it primarily focuses on parameter regularization without explicitly incorporating the host CL strategy into its method.

Lin
et al
.

[
75
]
takes a different approach by balancing stability and plasticity through two separately optimized networks. One network learns the current task via standard SGD, while the other preserves past knowledge through null-space projection. Unlike AFEC and CAF, this method is not a plug-in solution but a standalone framework, making it suitable for direct comparison against other CL baselines.

In contrast to these approaches, our FL algorithm is explicitly designed as a plug-and-play mechanism that integrates seamlessly with existing CL baselines. Unlike prior work, which either treats bidirectional knowledge flow asymmetrically or relies solely on parameter regularization, FL formulates knowledge transfer in alignment with the host method’s strategy. This ensures that both stability and plasticity counteract each other at the same level, achieving a better performance in the CL setting, which is evident in a head-to-head average accuracy comparison after augmentation with EWC
[
28
]
(Figure
11
).

A CL method achieves a better stability-plasticity balance if the model trained by it demonstrates lower forgetting in old classes (indicating its stability) while effectively learning new classes (indicating plasticity). To monitor this balance, we define

Conclusion

We formulated the FL algorithm to smoothly integrate into prior methods from four main CL categories. Aiming to effectively guide the model to swiftly incorporate novel knowledge while actively retaining its old knowledge, the FL mechanism introduces a bidirectional form of regularization into existing methods. Our inspiration comes from biological studies, where memory refinement through integrating new information is vital in effective adaptation to new tasks. On the computational side, reverse distillation (where a student model can enhance its teacher) challenges the common belief in unidirectional knowledge transfer. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to demonstrate that bidirectional knowledge flow can benefit CL, showing that knowledge transfer in both directions is helpful.
FL operates in two phases: the first builds a knowledge base from new task data to promote model plasticity, and the second utilizes this knowledge base to counteract the unidirectional stability regularization in existing CL methods. Given the efficacy of our method across four main categories of CL methods, future research will explore FL’s integration with emerging CL techniques, including parameter-efficient methods: prompt learning (
e.g
.

[
90
]
,
[
91
]
) and Low-rank adaptation (
e.g

[

]
,
[
93
]
). By integrating FL into parameter-efficient methods, we can leverage large pre-trained models and move toward multi-modal continual learning. Additionally, we aim to extend the FL mechanism to more challenging CL settings, including online learning and task-agnostic scenarios. By pursuing these directions, we aim to advance the field of CL and contribute to developing more robust and adaptable learning systems.

Cosine Embedding Loss

Consider the cosine embedding loss in LUCIR
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[1-\langle\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\rangle\Big].

Let
\bar{f}=f/\lVert f\rVert_{2}
represent the
\ell_{2}
-normalized vector of the output vectors and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product between two vectors. The dot product between two outputs (
i.e
.

\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}))
can be rewritten in terms of the squared difference between them. Use the identity:

\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right\|^{2}=\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right\|^{2}-2\langle\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\rangle.

(38)

Since embeddings are normalized we have
\lVert\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\rVert=1
,
\lVert\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\rVert=1
, and:

\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right\|^{2}=2-2\langle\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\rangle\quad\implies\quad\langle\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\rangle=1-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right\|^{2}

(39)

Substitute (
39
) in the stability loss, we have the general form:

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right\|^{2}\Big]\;.

(40)

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
as follows
[
94
]
:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}.

(57)

Eq. (
57
) presents the gradient in the CL setting, where
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
represents the probability distribution parameterized by the model with stable feature extractor (
8
).

In
FL case
, the objective function is constructed using stability (
18
)
and plasticity (
23
) KL losses:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Loss}}-\alpha_{s}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\bigg(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg)}_{\text{Stability Loss}}-\alpha_{p}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{t}}\bigg(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})^{\top}\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg)}_{\text{Plasticity Loss}}.

(58)

With the same notation as the previous scenario, we write the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\big)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{p}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})\big)}_{\text{Plasticity Gradient}};

(59)

then rewrite the gradient as:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\frac{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}{\tau}\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\bigg(\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\alpha_{s}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})+\alpha_{p}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})}{\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{p}}\bigg)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}.

(60)

Eq. (
60
) presents the gradient in the FL setting, where
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
represent the probability distributions parameterized by the stable model with stable feature extractor (
8
) and the primary model incorporating primary new module (
13
).

C.5
SGD Trajectories in Parameter Regularization Methods

Consider gradient expression from (
28
) for the loss function in the
CL case
under regularization methods:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\underbrace{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)}_{\text{Stability Gradient}}.

Substituting it in the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) update rule yields:

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}\Big)-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big);

(61)

and we rewrite it in the following form:

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}\Big)+\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s};

(62)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}
denotes the parameter value at training iteration
k
. Starting from
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
, we derive a recursive formula to express
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}
based on its initial value:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}\Big)+\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}}_{\text{A}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}}_{\text{A}}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}}\Big)+\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+\sum_{j=0}^{1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{1-j}\big(\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)^{j}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)^{j}

(63)

Let denote
\Gamma_{s}=\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}
in the Eq. (
63
) to obtain:

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}=\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)^{j}.

(64)

Eq. (
64
) shows the SGD trajectory in the CL setting.

In
FL case
, the gradient is given by (
29
):

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{Task-specific Gradient}}+\underbrace{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\big)+\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)}_{\text{Gradient Interpolation}}.

Substituting this into the SGD update rule results in:

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\big(I-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}\Big)+\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}+\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}.

(65)

From Eq. (
65
), we derive a recursive formula for
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}\Big)+\eta\big(\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)}_{\text{A}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}}_{\text{A}}-\eta\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)}}\Big)+\eta\big(\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{1-j}\big(\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)^{j}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}-\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)^{j}

(66)

Define
\Gamma_{s}=\eta\alpha_{s}\mathbf{F}_{s}
and
\Gamma_{p}=\eta\alpha_{p}\mathbf{F}_{p}
in the Eq. (
66
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\Big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(j)}}\Big)+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{p}\big)^{k-1-j}\big(\Gamma_{s}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}+\Gamma_{p}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}\big)^{j}

(67)

Eq. (
67
) characterizes the SGD trajectory in the FL setting.
