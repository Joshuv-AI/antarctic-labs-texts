A Tribute to Richard Askey, and an Expansive View of Some of his Favorite Beta Integrals
Donald Richards

Abstract
This article represents a personal tribute to Richard Askey together with a new look at some of his favorite integrals, including the Cauchy beta integral. The article also provides some new multidimensional extensions of Cauchy’s beta integral in which the domain of integration is the space of real symmetric matrices, and these multidimensional integrals are used to obtain some special cases of the Cauchy–Selberg integrals. Keywords and phrases . Bessel function of matrix argument, Cauchy’s beta integral, Gaussian hypergeometric function, positive definite matrices, q q -series, Student’s t t -distribution, symmetric cone, Wishart distribution. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification . Primary: 33C05, 62H10. Secondary: 33C15, 33C67.

An expansive view of Cauchy’s beta integral

We now consider a variety of integrals of a genre that have become attached to Askey’s name. In all of these integrals, it is understood that fractional powers are assigned their principal values and are continued through continuity.

We remark that the latter result can also be obtained by expanding the
{}_{2}F_{1}
as a hypergeometric series and applying (
2.1
) to integrate term-by-term. More generally, the result (
2.9
) can be extended to arbitrary
{}_{p}F_{q}
generalized hypergeometric series.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the probability density function of a renormalized Student’s
t
-distribution with
2\nu
degrees-of-freedom,

(2.11)

f_{\nu}(x)=\dfrac{(1+x^{2})^{-(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}}{B(\nu,\tfrac{1}{2})},

x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\nu>0
. Denoting by
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
the modified Bessel function (of the second kind) of order
\nu
, the Fourier transform of
f_{\nu}
is

(2.12)

\widehat{f}_{\nu}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{|t|^{\nu}K_{\nu}(|t|)}{2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)},&t\neq 0\\
1,&t=0\end{cases},

a result that follows from
Basset’s integral
: For
t>0
,

K_{\nu}(t)=\pi^{-1/2}\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{1}{2})(2/t)^{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+x^{2})^{-(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}\cos(tx)\,\mathrm{d}x.

We refer to Olver and Maximon
[
35
, eq. (10.32.11)]
and Watson
[
40
, p. 172]
for more details about Basset’s integral and to Hurst
[
29
]
for an alternative derivation of (
2.12
).

Suppose that
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}>0
, and consider the probability density functions
f_{\nu_{1}}
and
f_{\nu_{2}}
, as defined by (
2.11
). To verify that each
f_{\nu_{j}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, we simply note that

[f_{\nu_{j}}(x)]^{2}=\dfrac{(1+x^{2})^{-(2\nu_{j}+1)}}{[B(\nu_{j},\tfrac{1}{2})]^{2}}=\dfrac{B(2\nu_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}{[B(\nu_{j},\tfrac{1}{2})]^{2}}f_{2\nu_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}}(x);

this shows that
f_{\nu_{j}}^{2}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, equivalently,
f_{\nu_{j}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Consequently
\widehat{f}_{\nu_{j}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
so, by Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{|t|^{\nu_{1}}K_{\nu_{1}}(|t|)}{2^{\nu_{1}-1}\Gamma(\nu_{1})}\dfrac{|t|^{\nu_{2}}K_{\nu_{2}}(|t|)}{2^{\nu_{2}-1}\Gamma(\nu_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle\widehat{f}_{\nu_{1}},\widehat{f}_{\nu_{2}}\rangle=\langle f_{\nu_{1}},f_{\nu_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{B(\nu_{1},\tfrac{1}{2})B(\nu_{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(1+x^{2})^{-(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+1)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\dfrac{B(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}{B(\nu_{1},\tfrac{1}{2})B(\nu_{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}.

In summary, we obtain the integral,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}K_{\nu_{1}}(t)K_{\nu_{2}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\pi\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}K_{\nu_{1}}(|t|)K_{\nu_{2}}(|t|)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}-2}\pi\Gamma(\nu_{1})\Gamma(\nu_{2})\dfrac{B(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}{B(\nu_{1},\tfrac{1}{2})B(\nu_{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle=2^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}-2}\pi^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+1)}\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}),

and, by analytic continuation, this result remains valid for all
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\nu_{1})>-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\Re(\nu_{2})>-\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
\Re(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})>-1
. We also note that the final result agrees with Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
[
25
, p. 660, 6.511(13); p. 684, 6.576(4)]
.

The methods used above can be extended to calculate more general integrals of the form
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda}K_{\nu_{1}}(at)K_{\nu_{2}}(bt)\,\mathrm{d}t
. This leads to final expressions involving the Gaussian hypergeometric function.

In closing this section, we consider probability density functions that are proportional to
(1-x^{2})^{\nu_{j}-1}
,
-1<x<1
,
\nu_{j}>0
,
j=1,2
. Then we recover integrals of the type,
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{a}J_{\nu_{1}}(t)J_{\nu_{2}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
, the well-known integrals of Weber-Schafheitlin type
[
40
, p. 398]
.

Example 2.5
.

For
\nu>\tfrac{1}{2}
, let

f_{\nu}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}},

-1<x<1
. Then

\widehat{f}_{\nu}(t)=\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{1}{2})(|t|/2)^{-\nu}J_{\nu}(|t|),

t\neq 0
.
For
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}>\tfrac{1}{2}
we apply Parseval’s identity to
f_{\nu_{1}}
and
f_{\nu_{2}}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\pi^{1/2}

\displaystyle\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2})(|t|/2)^{-\nu_{1}}J_{\nu_{1}}(|t|)\cdot\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})(|t|/2)^{-\nu_{2}}J_{\nu_{2}}(|t|)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle\widehat{f}_{\nu_{1}},\widehat{f}_{\nu_{2}}\rangle=\langle f_{\nu_{1}},f_{\nu_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=2^{2(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})-1}B(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2},\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}).

Equivalently,

\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|t|/2)^{-\nu_{1}}J_{\nu_{1}}(|t|)\cdot(|t|/2)^{-\nu_{2}}J_{\nu_{2}}(|t|)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=2^{2(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})-1}B(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2},\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})=2^{2(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})-1}\frac{\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})}{\Gamma(2(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}))},

so

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}}J_{\nu_{1}}(t)J_{\nu_{2}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}-1}\frac{\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})}{\Gamma(2(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}))\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})}{2^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\nu_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\nu_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2})},

where the latter equality follows by applying the duplication formula for the gamma function. Further, by analytic continuation, the final result remains valid for
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})>0
, and it agrees with Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
[
25
, p. 683, 6.575(2)]
.

3
Multidimensional Cauchy-beta integrals

Denote by
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
the vector space of
d\times d
real symmetric matrices, and let
{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
be the cone in
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
of positive definite matrices. For
X\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(X)
and
\det X
the trace and determinant, respectively, and also denote
\exp(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}X)
by
\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(X)
. For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\alpha)>(d-1)/2
, define the gamma function for the cone
{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}

(3.1)

\Gamma_{d}(\alpha)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}}(\det X)^{\alpha}\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(-X)\,(\det X)^{-(d+1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}X,

where
\,\mathrm{d}X
denotes Lebesgue measure on
{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
. A well-known result, due to Wishart, and later to Ingham and Siegel, is that

(3.2)

\Gamma_{d}(\alpha)=\pi^{d(d+1)/4}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Gamma(\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2}(j-1)).

Wishart’s integral is now prominent in aspects of multivariate statistical analysis (see Muirhead
[
33
]
) and has been generalized to the setting of the symmetric cones (see Ding,
et al.

[
20
]
).

For
\Sigma\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
and
\alpha>(d-1)/2
, the function

(3.3)

f_{\alpha,\Sigma}(X)=(\det\Sigma)^{-\alpha}(\det X)^{\alpha-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1)}\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(-\Sigma^{-1}X)/\Gamma_{d}(\alpha),

X\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
, is a probability density function, and the corresponding probability distribution is called the
Wishart distribution on

{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
. To prove that the function
f_{\alpha,\Sigma}
integrates to
1
, i.e., that

(3.4)

\int_{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}(\det X)^{\alpha}\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(-\Sigma^{-1}X)\,(\det X)^{-(d+1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}X=\Gamma_{d}(\alpha)(\det\Sigma)^{\alpha},

one makes the change-of-variables
X\mapsto\Sigma^{1/2}X\Sigma^{1/2}
in the integral, where
\Sigma^{1/2}
denotes the unique positive definite square-root of
\Sigma
, apply the invariance of the measure
(\det X)^{-(d+1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}X
under such a substitution, and use the definition (
3.1
) of
\Gamma_{d}(\alpha)
.

Given a function
f\in L^{1}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})
, define the Fourier transform of
f
to be

\widehat{f}(T)=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(-iTX)f(X)\,\mathrm{d}X,

T\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
. Applying classical Fourier transform theory, it follows that
\widehat{f}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
if
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})
; moreover, for
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})\cap L^{2}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})
, the Parseval identity implies that

(3.5)

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\widehat{f_{1}}(T)\overline{\widehat{f_{2}}(T)}\,\mathrm{d}T\\
=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}}\langle\widehat{f_{1}},\widehat{f_{2}}\rangle=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}f_{1}(X)\overline{f_{2}(X)}\,\mathrm{d}X.

Our first example of a Cauchy beta integral on the cone
{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
extends (
2.1
). In deriving this result, we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{d}+i\mathcal{S}_{d}=\{T_{1}+iT_{2}:T_{1},T_{2}\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\}
the vector space of complex symmetric
d\times d
matrices.

Example 3.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})>d
, and let
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}+i\mathcal{S}_{d}
such that
\Re(\Sigma_{1}),\Re(\Sigma_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
. Then

(3.6)

\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\det(I_{d}+i\Sigma_{1}T)^{-\alpha_{1}}\det(I_{d}-i\Sigma_{2}T)^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T\\
=(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}\frac{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1))}{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1})\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{2})}\frac{(\det\Sigma_{1})^{\alpha_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1)}(\det\Sigma_{2})^{\alpha_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1)}}{(\det(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}))^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1)}}

and

(3.7)

\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\det(I_{d}+i\Sigma_{1}T)^{-\alpha_{1}}\det(I_{d}+i\Sigma_{2}T)^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T=0.

Proof.
For
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{d}+i\mathcal{S}_{d}
, the right-hand side of (
3.4
) is analytic in
\Sigma
for
\Re(\Sigma)\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
. Therefore (
3.4
) remains valid for the larger region
\alpha>(d-1)/2
and
\Re(\Sigma)\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
. Consequently, it is a straightforward consequence of (
3.4
) that the Fourier transform of
f_{\alpha,\Sigma}
is

\widehat{f}_{\alpha,\Sigma}(T)=\det(I_{d}+i\Sigma T)^{-\alpha},

T\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
. Further, by the same argument that proves
f_{\alpha,\Sigma}\in L^{1}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})
, we find that if
\alpha>d/2
and
\Re(\Sigma)\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
then
f_{\alpha,\Sigma}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}})
, in which case we also obtain
\widehat{f}_{\alpha,\Sigma}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
.

Now suppose that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>d/2
. For
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
, we apply the Parseval identity (
3.5
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\det(I_{d}+i\Sigma_{1}T)^{-\alpha_{1}}\det(I_{d}-i\Sigma_{2}T)^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}}\langle\widehat{f}_{\alpha_{1}},\widehat{f}_{\alpha_{2}}\rangle=\langle f_{\alpha_{1}},f_{\alpha_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(\det\Sigma_{1})^{-\alpha_{1}}(\det\Sigma_{2})^{-\alpha_{2}}}{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1})\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}(\det X)^{\alpha+\alpha_{2}-d-1}\mathop{\mathrm{etr}}(-(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{2}^{-1})X)\,\mathrm{d}X.

On applying (
3.4
) to evaluate the latter integral, and then simplifying the resulting expression, we obtain (
3.6
).

Next, we apply the analyticity of the right-hand side of (
3.6
) as a function of
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
to deduce that the result remains valid for
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}+i\mathcal{S}_{d}
such that
\Re(\Sigma_{1}),\Re(\Sigma_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
.

For complex
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, it follows from (
3.2
) that the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is analytic in
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
for
\Re(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})>d
. Therefore (
2.1
) remains valid on the stated region, i.e., for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})>d
, and
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}+i\mathcal{S}_{d}
such that
\Re(\Sigma_{1}),\Re(\Sigma_{2})\in{\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}}
.

As for (
3.7
), that result follows by an argument entirely analogous to the derivation of (
2.2
).

∎

The next result, a beta integral of Cauchy–Selberg type, is a special case of an integral due to Selberg. See Morris
[
32
, p. 92]
for more general instances of these integrals, an account of some of their history, and applications to constant term identities.

Example 3.2
.

(Selberg.)

Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})>d
and
\Re(\sigma_{1}),\Re(\sigma_{2})>0
. Then

(3.8)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq d}|t_{j}-t_{k}|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+i\sigma_{1}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{1}}(1-i\sigma_{2}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}\\
=d!\,2^{d(d+1)/2}\pi^{d/2}\frac{\Gamma_{d}(\tfrac{1}{2}d)\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1))}{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1})\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{2})}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{\alpha_{2}d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}\sigma_{2}^{\alpha_{1}d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}}{(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2})^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}}

and

(3.9)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq d}|t_{j}-t_{k}|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+i\sigma_{1}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{1}}(1+i\sigma_{2}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}=0.

Proof.
Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>(d-1)/2
. Also suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Re(\sigma_{1}),\Re(\sigma_{2})>0
. Letting
I_{d}
denote the
d\times d
identity matrix, we return to (
3.6
) and substitute
\Sigma_{1}=\sigma_{1}I_{d}
and
\Sigma_{2}=\sigma_{2}I_{d}
. Then we obtain

(3.10)

(2\pi)^{d(d+1)/2}\frac{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(d+1))}{\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{1})\Gamma_{d}(\alpha_{2})}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{\alpha_{2}d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}\sigma_{2}^{\alpha_{1}d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}}{(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2})^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})d-\tfrac{1}{2}d(d+1)}}\\
=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}\det(I_{d}+i\sigma_{1}T)^{-\alpha_{1}}\det(I_{d}-i\sigma_{2}T)^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T.

Let
O(d)
denote the group of
d\times d
orthogonal matrices. For generic
H\in O(d)
, denote by
\,\mathrm{d}H
the corresponding Haar measure, normalized to have total volume
1
. Each
T\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
can be expressed in the form

(3.11)

T=H\,\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\,H^{\prime}

where
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
T
. By Muirhead
[
33
, p. 103]
, the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
decomposes as

(3.12)

\,\mathrm{d}T=\frac{\pi^{d^{2}/2}}{d!\,\Gamma_{d}(\tfrac{1}{2}d)}\,\,\mathrm{d}H\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq d}|t_{j}-t_{k}|\,\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}t_{d}.

Applying (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) to the integrand in (
3.10
), and integrating over
H
using the fact that the Haar measure has volume
1
, we find that the integral in (
3.10
) equals

\frac{\pi^{d^{2}/2}}{d!\,\Gamma_{d}(\tfrac{1}{2}d)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq d}|t_{j}-t_{k}|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+i\sigma_{1}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{1}}(1-i\sigma_{2}t_{j})^{-\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}.

On equating the latter expression with the left-hand side of (
3.10
) and simplifying the normalizing constant, we obtain (
3.8
).

To complete the proof, we again observe from (
3.2
) that the function
\Gamma_{d}(\alpha)
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, is analytic on the region
\Re(\alpha)>(d-1)/2
; then it follows that the right-hand side of (
3.8
) is analytic in
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
for
\Re(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})>d-1
.

Finally, (
3.9
) is proved by starting with (
3.7
) and proceeding analogously to the proof of (
3.8
).

∎

4
Concluding remarks

The results in Sections
2
and
3
can be extended in numerous directions, and at the heart of each such extensions lies an analog of the Parseval identity. For instance,
q
-series variations on the results of Section
2
can be obtained using
q
-analogs of the Fourier transform and their corresponding Parseval identities; see, e.g., Rubin
[
39
]
.

The analysis in Section
3
on the cone of positive definite matrices can be extended
via
the results of Gindikin
[
24
]
to some general homogeneous domains. As a consequence, we would obtain generalizations of Example
3.1
in which the integrands extending (
3.6
) involve products of powers of principal minors of the matrices
I_{d}+i\Sigma
and
I_{d}-i\Sigma_{2}T
. On specializing such extensions to the case in which the domains of integration are the symmetric cones
[
20
]
, we will obtain generalizations of Example
2.1
in which the integrand contains zonal (spherical) polynomials. Further, we will obtain generalizations of Examples
2.3
–
2.5
involving the spherical functions, the Bessel functions, or the confluent hypergeometric functions defined on the symmetric cones.

In other directions, analogs of the results given here can be obtained using other integral transforms, such as the Mellin and Hankel transforms, then applying Parseval-Plancherel identities for those transforms. We have already noted that Askey and Roy
[
15
]
and Marichev
[
31
]
derived several integral formulas using the Parseval identity for the classical Mellin transform, and the possibility exists that the Parseval identity for the classical Hankel transform can be applied similarly. Moreover these results suggest that numerous multivariate integrals over matrix spaces can be evaluated using multidimensional Parseval identities for the Hankel transforms of functions of matrix argument and for the spherical Mellin transforms on the symmetric cones
[
20
]
.

In conclusion, we see these results as evidence of Dick Askey’s foresight and vision; he saw in the seemingly simple Cauchy beta integral an enormous breadth and depth of mathematics.
