Exposure and Sensitivity of Terrestrial Vertebrates to Biological Invasions Worldwide
Authors: Clara Marino, Boris Leroy, Guillaume Latombe, Céline Bellard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile biological invasions continue to threaten biodiversity, most of current assessments focus on the sole exposure to invasive alien species (IAS), without considering native species' response to the threat. Here, we address this gap by assessing vertebrates' vulnerability to biological invasions, combining measures of both (i) exposure to 304 identified IAS and (ii) realized sensitivity of 1600 native vertebrates to this threat. We used the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species to identify species threatened by IAS, their distribution, and the species' range characteristics of their associated IAS. We found that 38% of worldwide terrestrial lands are exposed to biological invasions, but exposure alone was insufficient to assess vulnerability since we further found that most of the world hosted native species sensitive to biological invasions. We delineated areas highly vulnerable to biological invasions, that is, combining areas of high exposure and high sensitivity to IAS, located in Australia and coastal states of North America with a high confidence level, but also—depending on the group—in Pacific islands, Southern America, Western Europe, Southern Africa, Eastern Asia, and New‐Zealand with a medium confidence level. Assessing the completeness in exposure data, we revealed strong biases in the global description of the well‐known invasion hotspots, with limited areas being assessed with a medium to high confidence level. The completeness of sensitivity was overall very high, for the three studied taxonomic groups. We also demonstrated that coldspots of vulnerability to biological invasions were areas of low confidence in terms of data completeness, which coincided with biodiversity hotspots. There is thus a critical need to address these knowledge shortfalls which jeopardize efficient conservation initiatives, regarding the threats to well‐known vertebrate taxa.

Introduction

Invasive alien species (hereafter, IAS) continue to threaten biodiversity (Purvis et al. 2019). More than a quarter of threatened terrestrial vertebrates assessed by the IUCN are in peril because of biological invasions (Bellard, Genovesi, and Jeschke 2016), with over 3500 IAS documented worldwide according to the last IPBES report (IPBES 2023). During COP15 in 2022, a new set of targets to reverse biodiversity loss by 2030 was promulgated, including a specific target dedicated to the mitigation of IAS (Secretariat of the United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity 2021). Expressly, 196 countries agreed to decrease by 50% the introduction and establishment of IAS around the world and to reduce their impacts. To achieve this, there is a need to determine where the consequences of IAS could be the highest to identify spatial priorities.

The potential spread of IAS and their associated anthropogenic causes has been explored already (Bellard et al. 2016; Bradley et al. 2019; Early et al. 2016; Latombe et al. 2023; Pyšek et al. 2010). For instance, Early et al. (2016) mapped different spatial indicators related to IAS, such as international trade or environmental disturbance, while other studies have documented the potential distribution of invasive and established alien species (Bellard et al. 2017; Dawson et al. 2017; Van Kleunen et al. 2015) and identified future climatic suitable areas of IAS (Bellard et al. 2013; Li et al. 2016; Pili et al. 2020; Polaina et al. 2021). Despite improvements in geographic predictions of invasions, those studies only indicate the potential exposure to IAS presence. However, the presence of an alien species in an area—even for species with a history of impacts elsewhere—does not necessarily mean impacts on native communities. In parallel, recent studies explored areas where native species are more sensitive to biological invasions, highlighting hotspots of species that are extinct or threatened because of biological invasions (Bellard, Cassey, and Blackburn 2016; Bellard, Genovesi, and Jeschke 2016; Evans et al. 2021; Harfoot et al. 2021). Yet, efforts to assess the threat posed by IAS are currently limited either to the exposure of native communities to known IAS or to the sole sensitivity of native species to this threat but fail to consider both components within the same spatial framework. Because biological invasion impacts are context‐dependent, and depend on alien species characteristics but also native species features and environmental conditions (Pyšek et al. 2020), it is crucial to assess the threat posed by IAS accounting for both exposure to threat and sensitivity to the threat.

Vulnerability assessments (VA) integrate both intrinsic (species sensitivity) and extrinsic (species exposure) factors related to species vulnerability, permitting a detailed description of what could imperil a system facing a threat (Foden et al. 2019). VA allow to determine areas the most at risk of a given threat, enabling us to define priority zones for conservation purposes (Ameca y Juárez et al. 2012). Yet most existing VA have focused on climate change (Butt et al. 2016; de los Ríos, Watson, and Butt 2018; Foden et al. 2019; Leclerc, Courchamp, and Bellard 2020), and none has been conducted specifically for biological invasions (but see Butt et al. 2022 for multiple stressors).

Here, we introduce a biological invasion vulnerability assessment framework (BIVA framework) that combines measures of (i) exposure to IAS threats and (ii) realized sensitivity of native species to this threat (Figure 1). Exposure describes the level of biophysical changes induced by the threat, while sensitivity represents the realized susceptibility of the system's components to harm. Inspired by Parker et al.'s impact formula (Parker et al. 1999), we define exposure to IAS threats as the combination of three metrics: (i) the documented taxonomic richness of alien species, (ii) the area of their global alien range, and (iii) their impact at a given location. We further define the realized sensitivity to IAS at a location as the number of native species that are documented to be affected by biological invasions. We apply the BIVA framework to global terrestrial vertebrates and we assessed areas vulnerable to biological invasions by combining the distribution of 1614 native birds, mammals, and reptiles with their associated 304 IAS. Finally, we assess the uncertainty of final vulnerability by evaluating the data completeness for exposure and sensitivity.

(B) Exposure is the sum of three metrics related to IAS occurrences and characteristics, while sensitivity is composed of one metric related to native species. Materials and Methods

Biological Invasion Vulnerability Assessment (BIVA) Framework

The BIVA framework is designed to estimate the vulnerability associated with biological invasions worldwide (Figure 1). Here, we define exposure to biological invasions as the level of IAS threat that may occur at a location, i.e., the potential impacts IAS may have at this location based on known impacts. To measure exposure, we summed three metrics reflecting the presence and characteristics of IAS in this location: IAS richness, median alien range size, and alien impact breadth (Figure 1A). IAS richness scales with introduction effort and colonization pressure, thus reflecting how much an area is subject to alien introductions, but also has environmental conditions allowing introduced alien species to establish viable populations and spread (Blackburn et al. 2016; Dawson et al. 2017). Moreover, species with a larger global alien range would have greater invasive capacities and thus represent a greater threat to native systems than alien species established in a limited range of locations (Bernery et al. 2023). Finally, alien species differ in their ability and strength to impact native biota (Bradley et al. 2019; Doherty et al. 2016). Thus, the number of native species an alien species is known to impact globally informs on the possible alteration the IAS can have on a biotic system. Here, we consider alien species have an impact on native species if an impact is reported in the IUCN Red List, which indicates if they impair the way native species can thrive, causing a stress on the ecosystem, a direct stress (e.g., causing mortality), or an indirect stress (e.g., competition with the native species) on the species (IUCN and CMP 2012).

In parallel, we defined realized sensitivity to IAS as the species' intrinsic response to the threat, using the number of species documented to have at least one population affected by one or several IAS according to the IUCN Red list (Figure 1A). Native species are exposed to many different threats globally; however, their response to threats depends on many factors, including intrinsic characteristics like traits (Chichorro, Juslén, and Cardoso 2019). We considered that a location containing many species impacted by IAS somewhere in their range would likely show a high sensitivity to this threat. Sensitivity makes two major assumptions: (i) IAS‐impacted species are sensitive in their whole range; (ii) only documented affected species by IAS are sensitive. Finally, the overall vulnerability to biological invasions at a given location was the combination of both exposure and sensitivity to IAS and defined in five categories: very low, low, medium, high, and very high (Figure 1C).

BIVA of Terrestrial Vertebrates

We performed the biological invasion vulnerability assessment of terrestrial vertebrates for units of equal‐area cells of 110 km resolution (dimension of 1° cells at the equator) for terrestrial lands in the Mollweide system of projection (n = 17,257 cells in total). We chose this resolution as the best compromise between clarity of the visualization of results, computing time, and data quality (preliminary analyses at a finer resolution generated similar results).

Studied Native and Alien Species

We identified all native birds, mammals, and reptiles that are known to be affected by IAS in at least some part of their distribution range thanks to the IUCN Threats Classification Scheme (version 3.2), as well as the specific IAS species involved when available (IUCN 2022). We extracted the list of IAS species, removing taxa not identified at the species level (e.g., “Unspecified Rattus” were not considered) and life forms requiring a host to spread (i.e., removing bacteria, parasites, pathogenic fungi, and viruses). We collected the occurrences of the selected IAS from GBIF, using the occ_download function from the rgbif package (Chamberlain et al. 2023). Then, we cleaned the downloaded occurrences with the clean_coordinates function from the CoordinateCleaner package (Zizka et al. 2019). We removed occurrences that had no informed coordinate uncertainty and kept all the occurrences with an uncertainty below 55 km, i.e., half the resolution of our study. Finally, we selected all the occurrences that fell into the non‐native range of each IAS. To do so, for IAS with a native range described in the IUCN Red List, we removed occurrences falling into their native range. For IAS not assessed by the IUCN Red List, we kept all the occurrences occurring in the introduced areas, defined with alternative sources (details in Supporting Information). We ended up with a total of 304 IAS with at least one occurrence outside their native range, with a coordinate uncertainty below 55 km, and 1614 native species for which IAS were cited as a threat.

Exposure Metrics

To calculate the exposure to invasion threat, we combined information related to IAS occurrences and characteristics at the cell level. First, we calculated alien species richness as the number of alien species that have at least one exotic occurrence in the cell. Second, for each IAS in our list, we calculated its alien range size as the total number of equal‐area cells where the alien species has at least one occurrence, out of its native range. Median alien range for a given cell was calculated as the median alien range size of all IAS occurring in the cell. Third, we extracted the total number of native species impacted by each IAS present in the cell from the IUCN Red List. This was compiled for each class separately (birds, mammals, and reptiles). We calculated median impact breadth as the median number of natives impacted by all IAS occurring in the cell.

Sensitivity Metric

We defined the realized sensitivity to IAS of each cell as the number of native species impacted by at least one of the 304 IAS, occurring in the cell. We collected the native range of the 1614 mammal, bird, and reptile species from Birdlife International for birds (BirdLife International and Handbook of the Birds of the World 2020) and from the IUCN for mammals and reptiles (IUCN 2022). Sensitivity was calculated for each taxonomic group separately as the number of native species whose range intersected with each cell. Globally, 668 birds, 292 mammals, and 654 reptiles were impacted at least by one of the 304 IAS and occurred in 16,187 out of the 17,257 cells evaluated.

Vulnerability to Biological Invasions

We normalized the exposure and sensitivity components using three methods: max‐min rescaling, natural log before max‐min rescaling, and cumulative ranking (Figure 1B, Data S1) (Leclerc, Courchamp, and Bellard 2020). All normalizations resulted in metrics ranging between 0 and 1, but the log transformation minimized the influence of extremely high values while cumulative ranking resulted in a uniform number of values for any level of values. For exposure, we normalized the three metrics, summed them, and normalized the final obtained metric with a max‐min rescaling. As sensitivity had only one component, we simply applied the three normalizations. Robustness analyses demonstrated that exposure and sensitivity metrics were consistent for the three alternative normalization methods (Spearman's coefficient > 0.60 for exposure and > 0.88 for sensitivity, Table S2, Figure S6). We thus present results for the max‐min normalization (but see Figures S7–S10 for results with other normalization methods).

Exposure and sensitivity values were then separated into three quantiles (i.e., low, medium, and high values) using the bi_class function with the “fisher” method of the biscale package (Prener, Grossenbacher, and Zehr 2022). Vulnerability to biological invasions is the result of the combination of exposure and sensitivity and is defined by five classes ranging from very low to very high (Figure 1C). Zones of very high vulnerability are the cells of high value for both exposure and sensitivity, following the conceptual model of Ameca y Juárez et al. (2012).

Uncertainty Assessment

Both exposure and sensitivity were obtained by collecting data from large‐scale datasets (i.e., GBIF, IUCN Red List) that are spatially and taxonomically biased (Bachman et al. 2019; Bowler et al. 2022). We propose a metric of completeness that considers, at the same time, the sampling effort for detecting the presence of species and the effective detectability of IAS threat (Figure 1D), applicable to both alien and native species. Sampling effort depicts research effort, while effective detectability accounts for the capacity to detect the threat caused by IAS in a given place.

For exposure, we first extracted the density of GBIF records per cell of 110 km for terrestrial vertebrates from Meyer et al. (2015). Then, we log‐transformed and normalized the density to obtain the sampling effort in GBIF that ranged between 0 (no GBIF record for terrestrial vertebrates in the cell) and 1 (maximum density of species records in the world). Second, we used the Global Register of Introduced and Invasive Species (GRIIS) checklists to assess how much our data effectively detect the presence of known alien species in a given country (Pagad et al. 2022). Those checklists are based on regional or local inventories and should reference all IAS present in a given administrative unit. For each administrative unit (n = 267), we calculated (i) the number of species referenced in GRIIS but not in our database, (ii) the number of species referenced in our database but not in GRIIS, and (iii) the number of species present in both GRIIS and our database. Effective detection was the number of species present in both lists divided by the total number of species in the two lists.

Sensitivity data were based on IUCN Red List assessments, containing some Data Deficient (DD) species for their conservation status, or lacking information regarding IAS threat. Completeness for sensitivity accounted for (i) the information on the conservation status by the IUCN and (ii) the ability to describe precisely the threat caused by IAS on native species. First, we collected the extinction risk and the native range of all birds (n = 10,965), mammals (n = 5755), and reptiles (n = 9776) from the IUCN and calculated sampling effort for each group at the cell level as the percentage of species with a documented conservation status. Second, effective detection was based on the 1614 native species for which IAS were cited as a threat and corresponded to the proportion of species with documented information on the scope and severity of IAS threat.

Final completeness was the product of sampling effort and effective detection. Completeness was calculated for all 304 IAS together for exposure, but separated by taxonomic group for sensitivity.

Lastly, we applied cutoff values for completeness to display the final vulnerability results with increasing confidence levels. For each completeness (exposure and sensitivity), we defined a cutoff at 1/9, ensuring that sampling effort > 1/3 and effective detection > 1/3 (hereafter, medium confidence level) and a cutoff at 4/9 (hereafter, high confidence level) for which sampling effort > 2/3 and effective detection > 2/3. The confidence level for final vulnerability is medium (high) when both exposure and sensitivity have a medium (high) confidence level.

All analyses were performed with R (R Core Team).

Results

Vulnerability to Biological Invasions

We found that 38% of terrestrial surfaces with the studied native vertebrates were exposed to biological invasions worldwide. Native birds were exposed to 212 IAS while mammals were exposed to 75 IAS and reptiles to 119 IAS. Spatially, birds were slightly more exposed to IAS (36% of areas with native birds contained at least one alien occurrence) than reptiles (27%) and mammals (25%). Hotspots of exposure were spatially congruent across the three taxonomic groups, with North and Central America (including Hawaii Islands), Western Europe (including Azorean and Canary Islands), South‐Eastern Africa, India, Eastern Australia, and New Zealand as the main areas (Figure 2A). Ninety‐four percent of terrestrial lands contained at least one species qualified as sensitive to one of the 304 IAS (92% for birds, 76% for mammals, and 25% for reptiles). Most of the mainland coastal areas and islands showed high values of sensitivity, but there was a strong spatial divergence between taxa (Figure 2B). For instance, highly sensitive areas for birds included most Pacific islands and coastal areas of Western North American and European countries, as well as coastal areas of South Africa, Australia, Tasmania, and New Zealand. By contrast, the highest sensitivity values for mammals were concentrated in coastal areas of Australia. For reptiles, Caribbean islands and Japan also showed high sensitivity values in addition to Northern and Eastern Australia. Sensitivity was scaled with total native species richness for the three groups, but hotspots of sensitivity remained consistent while corrected by total species richness (Figure S1, Data S1).

By combining exposure and sensitivity (Figure 1C), we assessed the overlap between both markers of vulnerability to biological invasions for each taxon (Figure 2C). Very high vulnerability zones (i.e., overlap of high sensitivity and high exposure) for birds mostly occurred in coastal areas (e.g., North and South America, South Africa, Australia, and North‐East Asia), in addition to many oceanic islands, New Zealand, and some places in Europe. Mammals and reptiles displayed similar patterns of vulnerability, with very high vulnerability in Eastern Australia, and high vulnerability in North America and Southeastern Asia. Mammals also had high vulnerability in Argentina, African rift, and Madagascar, while reptiles had high vulnerability in Pacific and Caribbean islands. Very low vulnerability zones (i.e., low sensitivity and low exposure), which also coincided with zones of low completeness (Figure 3), were similar between the three taxa and included latitudes above both polar circles, the Amazonian region, most of Africa, and Central Asia.

Uncertainty in Vulnerability Components

We measured uncertainty separately for exposure and sensitivity by assessing data completeness from information on sampling effort and effective detectability of IAS threat. Exposure completeness ranged from 0 to 0.73, with sampling effort ranging from 0 to 1 and effective detection from 0 to 0.77. Locations with the highest completeness for exposure were Hawaii, the Galapagos islands, French Polynesia, North America, Western Europe, Australia, and New Zealand (Figure 3A, Figure S2). The lowest completeness values were located in North Andes, most of Africa, Central Asia, Siberia, and Antarctica, with more than 50% of areas with null completeness, and thus the highest uncertainty related to vulnerability to biological invasions (Figure S2). Exposure was positively correlated with completeness (Pearson's correlation coefficient = 0.33, p < 0.001), especially for mammals and reptiles (Figure 3B). Yet, we found zones of medium to very high vulnerability for birds in central Russia despite the very low completeness in this country (Figures 2C and 3A).

Sensitivity had a much higher completeness than exposure. Birds showed no spatial variation in sensitivity completeness (Figure 3C), this group being almost completely assessed for conservation status (> 99%) and IAS threat (> 97%). The conservation status of mammals and reptiles was assessed for 86% and 85% of their respective groups, but only 43% and 69% were correctly informed regarding the IAS threat, respectively. Zones of low completeness for sensitivity included North‐West America, the Amazonian region, Europe, Central Asia, India, and Indonesia for mammals, and Central and South America for reptiles (Figure 3D,E). For reptiles those zones were associated with a low vulnerability, but, for mammals, areas like North America, Western Europe, and India harbored a median to high vulnerability, mostly because of a high exposure (Figure 2).

Applying thresholds of data completeness for both exposure and sensitivity revealed an important spatial variation of vulnerability values for the three taxa. For instance, we observed that vulnerability can be assessed with very high confidence only in few areas, including Southeastern Australia and Tasmania for the three taxa as well as some restricted coastal areas in North America for birds, mammals, and reptiles (Figure 4). A less conservative value for completeness cutoff suggested that most of North America, Central America, Europe, Australia, restricted areas of India, and Eastern coasts of Africa were vulnerable to biological invasions (Figure 4B). By contrast, most of the Pacific and Caribbean islands, as well as Africa and Asia, tend to have completeness below the lower cutoff value. (Figures 2A,B and 4A). Overall, confidence in vulnerability was limited by poor completeness in exposure (Figure S3) while patterns of sensitivity were very similar even when removing areas of low confidence, especially for birds (Figure S4).

Discussion

We found that 38% of terrestrial surfaces with native vertebrates contained at least one of the 304 IAS known to impact them. This proportion is very high, yet it remains a gross underestimate considering that we assessed exposure to less than 10% of the 3500 documented IAS worldwide (IPBES 2023). The vast majority of terrestrial lands contained vertebrate species qualified as sensitive to one of these 304 IAS. Note that species might not be sensitive to IAS in their whole range, but we were not able to distinguish, within the spatial range, which part was actually sensitive to IAS. For birds, we identified expected vulnerability hotspots on oceanic islands and, notably, revealed previously overlooked high vulnerability of mainland coastal areas to IAS (Dawson et al. 2017). For mammals and reptiles, we found high vulnerability in East Australia and in multiple other continental and oceanic areas.

By contrast, we found that many tropical areas in South America, Africa, Central Asia, and Indonesia were areas of low vulnerability to biological invasions. However, this was most likely due to low completeness for both exposure and sensitivity, especially for mammals. These areas are potentially false negative because knowledge on IAS is insufficient (Bellard and Jeschke 2015). They should therefore be considered as “Data Deficient” until further knowledge is acquired. In this regard, our global assessment is a conservative estimate of the overall vulnerability of the world to biological invasions, given the lack of data in inaccessible and understudied regions of the world (Feeley, Stroud, and Perez 2017). This lack of data is likely reinforced by linguistic barriers: most GBIF data rely on English language, and a broader inclusion of other languages would increase the scientific knowledge on biological invasions (Angulo et al. 2021).

Assessing Global Exposure to IAS

Patterns of exposure revealed that more than one quarter of lands containing native tetrapods were exposed to IAS threats. This proportion is likely underestimated given that many areas had incomplete data for IAS occurrences, leading to no exposure. Plus, among areas with exposure values, many had a low completeness, inducing only a few areas like North America, Argentina, Western Europe, Southeastern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand with medium to high confidence in their exposure (Figure S3). Overall, hotspots of exposure complemented previous studies on global alien distribution (not only restricted to IAS). Invasion hotspots, identified based on alien species richness and drivers of IAS richness, are mainly located on oceanic islands and continental coastal countries, with a predominance of North and Central America, Europe, South Africa, South‐East Asia, and Oceania (Dawson et al. 2017; Early et al. 2016). Yet, those identified hotspots of invasions also suffer from low completeness (Figure S3), and only small areas in Australia and North America had very high completeness values. This result revealed important biases in our knowledge of global exposure to biological invasions due to the lack of data. However, because we included additional information on IAS besides their richness, our exposure index accounts for the impacts of IAS present at a location and their ability to spread (Figure S5) and enabled us to also identify inland North America or India as zones exposed to IAS threats. This supports the importance of integrating multiple metrics for assessing exposure to threats in vulnerability assessments, as already applied for climate change (Leclerc, Courchamp, and Bellard 2020) or advocated to assess IAS impact (Parker et al. 1999). For instance, Latombe et al. (2023) proposed a way to measure IAS impacts for multiple species on a system by generalizing the impact formula designed by Parker et al. (1999), where the total impact of a given IAS would be the product of its range size, local abundance, and per‐unit effect. However, such a framework is not yet feasible at the global scale because of knowledge shortfalls and is more applicable at a local scale. Our BIVA framework follows the same principles as the original impact formula, but it is more easily applicable at larger scales, therefore providing an overall picture of vulnerability to biological invasions.

Sensitivity Unveiled Previously Unidentified Hotspots of Vulnerability to Invasions

Considering each taxon separately revealed sensitivity hotspots never identified before (Bellard, Genovesi, and Jeschke 2016). Our results confirmed the previous geographic patterns of native species impacted by IAS, with islands being more sensitive than mainland, especially for birds (Russell et al. 2017). However, we found that coastal areas had high sensitivity and thus high vulnerability for native birds, with a high confidence given the good data completeness for bird sensitivity (Figure S4). Although it is acknowledged that coastal areas are more prone to species introductions than interior mainland locations (Van Kleunen et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2012), the global geographic distribution of native species associated with IAS threat confirms that these introductions induce strong ecological impacts on avian communities.

Further, we found that mammal sensitivity was highest in South‐Eastern Australia, supported by a high completeness, thus a high confidence level (Figure S4). This contrasts with spatial patterns of globally endangered mammals, which are concentrated in tropical areas (Ceballos and Ehrlich 2006; Jenkins, Pimm, and Joppa 2013). Tropical areas (including the Amazonian basin, Central Africa, and Southeast Asia) were even invasion sensitivity coldspots in our study (Figure 2B, Figure S1). However, the tropics also suffer from low completeness except for African mammals, possibly blurring the real sensitivity of mammals to IAS in the tropics (Chong et al. 2021). Australia was also an area of high sensitivity for reptiles facing IAS, again contrasting with hotspots of globally threatened reptiles that include the Andes, the Caribbean, West Africa, Madagascar, and Southeastern Asia (Cox et al. 2022). Contrary to mammals, these regions were of high completeness for reptiles (Figure 3E, Figure S4), thus confirming these discrepancies are not due to a lack of data but rather to the specificity of IAS threat compared to other global change anthropogenic pressures.

Assessing Uncertainty is as Important as Assessing Vulnerability

Exposure completeness revealed that well‐known hotspots of invasions are spatially biased. Current exposure highlights wealthier countries of the globe, where intensive research programs have been conducted for a long time and where the threat caused by IAS motivates stakeholders to act to improve knowledge (Chiu et al. 2023). This is even more evident when applying cutoffs on data completeness, as the only zones with medium to high confidence levels were North America including Hawaii islands, Western Europe and North Atlantic islands, localized areas in South America and Asia, South Africa, the East African rift, Australia, Tasmania, and New Zealand (Figure S3). Yet, even for those areas, we could not reach a very high confidence level in the results due to a lack of data for exposure. Alien occurrences are still hardly collected and standardized, remaining scarce for most taxa, in spite of recent advances on a global scale (Seebens and Kaplan 2022). Thus, the Wallacean shortfall (i.e., lack of knowledge about species distribution, Hortal et al. 2015) is also verified for alien species. It is even amplified, because in addition to bias in survey effort, interest in biological invasion threat is country‐dependent. For example, Australia and New Zealand were the hotspots of highest exposure and also the regions of highest completeness. This is not surprising given the tremendous research efforts on threats to Australian and Novozealandic biota, ranking alien species as the top cause of population declines (Kearney et al. 2019; Simberloff 2019). Interestingly, the regions of highest completeness for sensitivity differed from regions of high exposure completeness, pointing out the differences in current knowledge and research effort between native and alien species (Figures S3 and S4).

Besides the lack of completeness in IAS distribution data, BIVA also faces biases regarding interactions between IAS and native species. This could refer to the Eltonian shortfall, i.e., the lack of knowledge about interactions among species (Hortal et al. 2015). Compared to terrestrial vertebrates, whose threats are well recorded in the IUCN Red List, information is scarce for invertebrates or plants (Bachman et al. 2019), which are nonetheless highly impacted by biological invasions (Bellard, Cassey, and Blackburn 2016). Moreover, the IAS mentioned in the IUCN Red List are only a subsample of all the IAS that effectively affect native species, leading to an underestimation of total exposure and sensitivity. Including other databases containing IAS impacts on a global scale, such as the Environmental Classification of Alien Taxa (EICAT) (Kumschick et al. 2023), could help in identifying the native species associated with specific IAS. However, existing EICAT assessments are far from complete, even for major alien taxa, and require large data and human resources to be implemented (Volery et al. 2020), limiting their use in global BIVA. Profiling potential sensitive species using known characteristics that make them more vulnerable to IAS such as an herbivorous diet for mammals, an animal‐based diet for birds, or a ground‐level foraging strategy for insular birds, mammals, and reptiles (Marino, Leclerc, and Bellard 2022), could be a way to identify potential vulnerability. Thus, as for climate change vulnerability assessments, a mechanistic BIVA framework would benefit from the inclusion of species traits (Butt et al. 2022; Pacifici et al. 2017). Although data quality might be lacking at global scale, BIVA can also be conducted at much lower resolution on specific systems.

Finally, the measure of completeness itself was also biased by data availability and knowledge on biological invasions. We advocate for a better consideration of data completeness and associated uncertainty in global vulnerability assessments, which obviously suffer from biodiversity knowledge shortfalls. Although impossible to avoid when conducting global scale studies, bias in data must be identified, if possible measured, and used to correct or at least interpret results. The implementation of cutoffs for defining confidence levels could provide conservative results, useful for conservation stakeholders or decision‐makers. This approach allowed us to quantify the potential data gaps and ensure that our exposure and sensitivity metrics are used with caution, preventing incorrect interpretations in areas where data are lacking. We emphasize the urgency to gather data on understudied systems and taxa, especially in biodiversity hotspots that are data deficient regarding the IAS threat, even for well‐known vertebrate groups.
