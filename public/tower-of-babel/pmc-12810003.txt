Comprehensive characterization of non-retroviral endogenous viral elements in the rice pest Chilo suppressalis
Authors: Jia-Bao Lu, Yu-Hua Qi, Ying Tian, Qing-Kai Feng, Peng-Peng Ren, Hai-Jian Huang, Yi-Yuan Li, Jian-Ping Chen, Jun-Min Li, Chuan-Xi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-retroviral endogenous viral elements (nrEVEs) are “molecular fossils” in eukaryotes that offer key molecular evidence into virus-host coevolution, particularly in insects. The striped rice stem borer (Chilo suppressalis), one of the most notorious chewing rice pests, is widely distributed across China. By integrating meta-transcriptome, small RNA (sRNA) sequencing, and the published C. suppressalis genomic data, a comprehensive characterization of nrEVEs in this species was revealed.ResultsFifteen nrEVEs were identified in the C. suppressalis genome, comprising 13 Orthomyxoviridae-derived and two Rhabdoviridae-derived viral sequences (designated CsOrEVE1-13 and CsRhEVE1-2). These elements are integrated in either forward or reverse orientation across multiple chromosomes, and share 49–77% sequence identity with known exogenous viruses. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that most CsOrEVEs cluster closely with the Quaranjavirus clade, suggesting historical infections by viruses of this genus. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct expression patterns: CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE5 showed developmental stage-specificity; CsOrEVE3, CsOrEVE6, and CsOrEVE11 exhibited tissue-specificity; and CsOrEVE1, CsOrEVE3, and CsOrEVE6 were expressed in a geographic population-specific manner. Twelve nrEVEs in C. suppressalis were located within transcripts predicted to contain intact open reading frames. Among these, CsOrEVE11, CsOrEVE12, and CsOrEVE13 were found within intron-containing host transcripts, suggesting their potential as candidates for molecular domestication. sRNA sequencing detected abundant piRNA-like reads derived from most nrEVEs. Furthermore, various integration-related domains such as retrotransposons in the flanking regions of these nrEVEs may serve as critical evidence to help hypothesize their origins.ConclusionsThis study presents a comprehensive characterization of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis through bioinformatic characterization, demonstrating their association with host mRNAs and their potential important roles. Our findings advance the understanding of long-term virus–host coevolution and genomic structure formation in C. suppressalis, and establish a theoretical foundation for further research on the functions of nrEVEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12426-8.

Background

During the long-term co-evolution of viruses and their hosts, viral sequences that become integrated into the germline genomes of hosts and are vertically inherited are termed endogenous viral elements (EVEs). As molecular “fossils” of ancient viral infections, EVEs are widely distributed across eukaryotic genomes and play significant roles in the evolution of both hosts and viruses [1, 2]. Studies have revealed that both DNA and RNA viruses can be endogenized in eukaryotic genomes [3–6]. Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) were the earliest and most extensively studied EVEs, owing to the inherent biological properties of retroviruses, such as the presence of reverse transcriptases (RTs) as well as integrases and the necessity to integrate their DNA into the host genome during replication [7, 8]. It was not until 2004 that non-retroviral endogenous viral elements (nrEVEs) were first identified in Aedes albopictus and Aedes aegypti mosquitoes [5]. Research suggests that the formation of nrEVEs may occur through accidental integration events facilitated by host. For instance, host retrotransposons can mediate the reverse transcription of viral mRNA into DNA and its subsequent integration into the host genome [1, 9]. Over the past decades, advances in high-quality chromosome-level genome sequencing and the widespread application of metagenomic next-generation sequencing have led to the identification of numerous RNA viruses [10, 11] and a growing number of nrEVEs across diverse taxonomic groups [12, 13]. These include plants [14], animals [15, 16], and fungi [17].

As molecular “fossils” distributed within host genomes, one reason that EVEs were ultimately retained and inherited across generations may be their beneficial roles for the host—or at least, the absence of deleterious insertion—under host selective pressures [18]. Domesticated EVEs have been implicated in antiviral immunity, helping hosts resist exogenous viruses. For instance, envelope gene-derived restriction factors can inhibit exogenous retroviruses [19]; nrEVE-derived P-element-induced wimpy testis-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) in A. aegypti mosquito limit cognate virus replication [20]. Furthermore, a very few studies have shown that not only ERVs but also nrEVEs can act as actual genes and contribute to the development and physiology of hosts, for example, a human endogenous bornavirus-like nucleoprotein, hsEBLN-2, has been actively transcribed and functionally co-opted as a host gene, where it influences cell viability by interacting with mitochondrial proteins [21]; an nrEVE (NlToEVE14) was identified in the rice pest Nilaparvata lugens and validated at the protein level. It has been shown to regulate growth, development and reproduction in this species [13]. As major viral reservoirs [11], discoveries in insects are particularly prominent [22, 23], encompassing medically important vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks, as well as key plant virus-transmitting insects with piercing-sucking mouthparts, including aphids, rice planthoppers, and bugs [13, 24, 25]. However, study of nrEVEs in insects with chewing mouthparts remains poorly understood.

The striped rice stem borer, Chilo suppressalis (Walker) (Lepidoptera: Crambidae), is one of the most notorious insect pests, causing significant damage across rice-producing regions in Asia, Oceania, and Europe [26–29]. This pest is polyphagous [30] and non-migratory [31], with a wide geographical distribution in China. The larvae bore into and feed within rice stems, leading to impaired plant growth and severe yield losses. Their intimate ecological interaction with rice and the surrounding environment provides potential opportunities for horizontal RNA virus transfer and the integration of nrEVEs into the host genome. While the study of nrEVEs has been significantly advanced by pioneering work in mosquitoes, aphids, and planthoppers [13, 24, 32, 33], our current understanding remains heavily biased towards piercing-sucking insect vectors. In contrast, as a major chewing pest of rice, C. suppressalis exhibits a distinctly different life history and feeding strategy. A systematic investigation of nrEVEs in this chewing pest has been lacking.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis by integrating genomic, meta-transcriptomic, and small RNA (sRNA) sequencing data. This integrated approach allowed us to thoroughly characterize the nrEVEs in C. suppressalis. This study is expected to significantly advance our understanding of virus-host interactions within the rice ecosystem, and establish a theoretical framework for exploring the functional significance of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis.

Results

Identification and characterization of nrEVEs in the C. suppressalis genome

A total of 15 nrEVEs were identified in the C. suppressalis genome through a tBLASTn search using reported non-retroviral RNA viruses from the Crambidae family and another sympatric species, Sesamia inferens (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). All 15 nrEVEs are integrated into the C. suppressalis genome in either forward or reverse orientation, showing 49–77% sequence identities to their homologous exogenous viral sequences. Notably, 13 of these nrEVEs originated from viruses in the Orthomyxoviridae family (designated CsOrEVE1–13). Among these, five are located on chromosome (Chr) 2 (CsOrEVE2–6) and another five on Chr27 (CsOrEVE9–13), while CsOrEVE1, CsOrEVE7, and CsOrEVE8 are situated on Chr1, Chr7, and Chr26, respectively. Of the 13 CsOrEVEs, five, four, and three share homologies with the nucleoprotein (NP), RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp: one for polymerase PB1, two for PB2, and one for PA), and hemagglutinin (glycoprotein, GP), respectively. It is noteworthy that the predicted amino acid identity is 93.72% between CsOrEVE9 and CsOrEVE11, and 93.48% between CsOrEVE9 and CsOrEVE12 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). All correspond to the GP sequence of the lepidopteran orthomyxo-related virus OKIAV178 (Crambus sp.), despite being distantly located on Chr27. Similarly, CsOrEVE7 and CsOrEVE8 show 98.70% amino acid identity (Supplementary Fig. 1b), both matching the NP sequence of the same virus, lepidopteran orthomyxo-related virus OKIAV178, even though they are located on Chr7 and Chr26, respectively. This suggests potential EVE replication events within the insect genome. In contrast, no sequence similarity was detected for CsOrEVE1, CsOrEVE2, CsOrEVE3, and CsOrEVE10, which correspond to the RdRP sequences of various orthomyxo-related viruses (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Furthermore, two nrEVEs (CsRhEVE1 and CsRhEVE2) derived from viruses in the Rhabdoviridae family were identified, originating from NP and RdRp coding sequences, and integrated into Chr9 and Chr20 in the C. suppressalis genome, respectively.

Evolutionary relationships of nrEVEs revealed by phylogenetic analysis

Most nrEVEs integrated into the C. suppressalis genome were derived from viruses belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family, suggesting a long-term coexistence between these viruses and C. suppressalis. To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of these nrEVEs with their exogenous viral counterparts, five maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were constructed based on the RdRp (PB1, PB2, and PA), GP, and NP sequences (Fig. 2). With the exception of CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE10, which are derived from PB2 coding sequences (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2), the remaining nrEVEs showed close phylogenetic affinity to Quaranjavirus (Fig. 2b-e and Supplementary Fig. 3–6). Specifically, the GP-derived CsOrEVE9, 11–12 formed a well-supported clade that remains unclassified (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3). A similar pattern was observed for the PA-derived integrations: CsOrEVE2 and the Coleopteran orthomyxo-related virus OKIAV196 together constituted a robust and distinct clade (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4). The PB1-derived CsOrEVE1 and CsOrEVE13 clustered closely with Quaranjavirus yet form two distinct subclades (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that the C. suppressalis might be infected by two different but related Quaranjavirus strains at separate points in its evolutionary history. Meanwhile, the NP-derived CsOrEVE5 clustered more closely with Quaranjavirus, while the group comprising CsOrEVE4, 6, 7, and 8 formed another unclassified group (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting the presence of previously unrecognized viral diversity. CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE10, derived from the PB2 coding sequence, clustered with viruses belonging to different genera within the family Orthomyxoviridae (including Alphainfluenzavirus, Betainfluenzavirus, Deltainfluenzavirus, Gammainfluenzavirus, and Thogotovirus). This suggests that C. suppressalis may have been infected multiple times throughout its long evolutionary history by orthomyxoviruses from distinct genera. Alternatively, these integrated nrEVEs might originate from an ancient, relatively conserved ancestral viral gene prior to the divergence of these genera. The above results indicate that Quaranjavirus likely has a long-term evolutionary history of coexistence with C. suppressalis, which may have shaped the diversity and distribution of nrEVEs in the C. suppressalis genome. Furthermore, the observed phylogenetic patterns imply that multiple viral lineages may have independently colonized the host genome, contributing to the complex evolutionary dynamics of virus–host interactions in this system.

Spatiotemporal and geographic transcriptomic patterns of nrEVEs

In order to study the potential transcription of the 15 nrEVEs in C. suppressalis, we used transcriptomes from different developmental and tissue samples, and different geographic populations of the C. suppressalis to search for these nrEVEs derived transcript reads. Results revealed that all the 15 nrEVEs were transcribed in various transcripts (Fig. 3). For the laboratory-reared population of C. suppressalis from Hunan Agricultural University, transcript reads of CsOrEVE1 were nearly undetectable across all developmental stages, while the transcript reads of CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE5 could not be detected during the larval stage. This suggests that CsOrEVE1 may be absent in this population, whereas CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE5 exhibit developmental stage-specific expression patterns. The other nrEVEs were ubiquitously expressed in the egg, larval, pupal, and adult stages (Fig. 3a). In six different tissue samples detected from C. suppressalis larva that were collected from Ningbo water bamboo fields, CsOrEVE3, CsOrEVE6, and CsOrEVE11 showed tissue-specific expression: CsOrEVE6 was transcribed only in the trachea, CsOrEVE3 was expressed in the integument and silk gland, and CsOrEVE11 was not transcribed in the integument or fat body. These results suggest that these three CsOrEVEs may play distinct tissue-specific roles in the development and physiology of C. suppressalis, while the other nrEVEs were ubiquitously expressed across all examined tissues (Fig. 3b). Among six C. suppressalis geographic populations representing different climatic regions in China, CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE6 were not transcribed in the Urumchi and Kaiyuan populations but were detectable in the other four geographic populations. The remaining nrEVEs were detected in all geographic populations (Fig. 3c). Although CsOrEVE1 was absent from the laboratory population from Hunan, it was detected in all six field populations. The widespread presence of most nrEVEs across these geographic regions suggests that these elements may have been integrated into the C. suppressalis genome in ancient times. Notably, although the majority of nrEVEs exhibit widespread expression patterns across various spatiotemporal and geographic of C. suppressalis, they still display variations in expression levels. For example, CsOrEVE4 shows higher expression during the egg and larval stages compared to the pupal and adult stages, and elevated transcript levels in the fat body and malpighian tubules relative to other tissues. It also demonstrates higher expression in geographic populations from Changchun and Kaiyuan. All source data are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

CsOrEVEs and CsRhEVEs might be integral components of the C. suppressalis mRNAs

To further investigate the presence of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis transcripts, the 13 CsOrEVE and 2 CsRhEVE sequences were used as queries to search against the C. suppressalis meta-transcriptomic databases of the above six distinct C. suppressalis geographical populations samples (Supplementary Table 3). The results revealed that all 15 nrEVEs were detected in various transcripts (Fig. 4). With the exception of CsOrEVE3, the assembled transcripts of the remaining 14 nrEVEs were longer than the nrEVE sequences themselves. Additionally, most nrEVEs exhibited relatively high coverage abundance. In contrast, for CsOrEVE2-6, which showed relatively low coverage or partial coverage, the complete or partial transcripts containing nrEVEs were verified by RT-PCR, followed by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 7). The primers used are listed in the Supplementary Table 4. Twelve nrEVE-containing transcripts, corresponding to CsOrEVE2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, CsRhEVE1, and CsRhEVE2, were predicted to contain intact open reading frames (ORFs) ranging from 393 nt to 1290 nt in length. Furthermore, CsOrEVE11-13 were contained within transcripts exhibiting exon-intron structures.

Diverse profiles of nrEVE-derived piRNA-like RNAs in C. suppressalis

In this study, sRNA sequencing was conducted using five different geographic populations of C. suppressalis (Supplementary Table 3). Analysis of nrEVE-derived sRNA profiles showed that no sRNAs were detected for CsOrEVE2, 5, and 8, despite their high transcript abundance. In contrast, most sRNAs mapped to other nrEVEs were 24–30 nt in length, a typical size for piRNAs (Fig. 5). However, their specific characteristics warranted a detailed classification: (i). CsOrEVE1 and CsOrEVE3 generated predominantly sense-stranded sRNAs. CsOrEVE1 exhibited a Ping-Pong signature (1U-sense and 10 A-antisense bias) but lacked the definitive 10-nt overlap (Fig. 5a), while CsOrEVE3 displayed only a 1U-sense bias (Fig. 5b). (ii). CsOrEVE9 produced predominantly antisense-stranded sRNAs, without Ping-Pong amplification (Fig. 5f). (iii). Strand-specific sRNAs (24–30 nt) were also identified for CsOrEVE4, 6, 7, and CsRhEVE2 (sense strand) and for CsOrEVE10-13 (antisense strand) (Fig. 5c-e, g-k), none of which showed evidence of Ping-Pong amplification. Due to the above results, we tentatively classify these sRNAs as piRNA-like RNAs. Additionally, a peak of 20 nt sRNAs was observed for CsOrEVE1, CsOrEVE10, and CsRhEVE1 (Fig. 5a, g, l), indicating the possible presence of exogenous viruses highly similar to them in C. suppressalis.

nrEVEs are located near retrotransposon or other integration-related domains in the genome of C. suppressalis

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the integration of nrEVEs into the C. suppressalis genome, we analyzed the flanking regions (± 10 kb) surrounding these nrEVEs. Notably, various retrotransposons or other integration-related domains were detected within these regions (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, RT_nLTR_like domains, which are associated with non-long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons and non-LTR retrovirus RTs, were detected near CsOrEVE2, 5, 9, 11, 12, 13 and CsRhEVE1. RTs were also found adjacent to CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE4. Additionally, Rnase_HI_RT_non_LTR, a non-LTR RNase HI domain of RTs, was identified near CsOrEVE10. Furthermore, several nrEVEs were flanked by other types of retrotransposon peptidases or transposases: a Pao retrotransposon peptidase (Peptidase_A17 super family) was located near CsOrEVE1; a pepsin_retropepsin_like domain was found near CsOrEVE8; three types of transposases (HTH_Tnp_Tc3_2, transpos_IS630 super family, and Transposase_1 super family) were detected close to CsOrEVE6; an HTH domain in Mos1 transposase (HTH_48) was identified near CsOrEVE4 and CsOrEVE7; and a ZnF_TTF domain, which functions in both transposases and transcription factors, was found near CsRhEVE2. Notably, Permutation Test revealed significant enrichment of retrotransposon and other integration-related domains in the flanking regions of these 15 nrEVEs (Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, several auxiliary factors potentially involved in integration were abundantly identified, such as the DDE_Tnp_4 superfamily and Integrase_H2C2. The former, involved in site-specific DNA cleavage, was located in the flanking regions of CsOrEVE1, CsOrEVE9, and CsRhEVE2, while the latter, an integrase zinc-binding domain, was found near CsOrEVE2 and CsOrEVE10.

Discussion

Over the past two decades, advances in sequencing technologies and bioinformatic analytical methods have led to the progressive identification of a class of “molecular fossils”, the nrEVEs, in a wide range of eukaryotic organisms [18]. Insects, with their extensive evolutionary history and remarkable species diversity, serve as key organisms for studying the diversity, genomic integration mechanisms, and biological functions of nrEVEs [13, 34]. Bidirectional viral transmission may occur between species sharing similar ecological niches or biological associations [35]. Following this framework, utilizing viral sequences from closely related species for nrEVE annotation can more accurately reveal the interactions and genetic dynamics among species, environment, and host. In this study, we systematically identified 15 nrEVEs in C. suppressalis using previously reported RNA viruses from itself, the sympatric specie S. inferens, and documented RNA viruses from other species within the Crambidae family. In contrast to the extensive range of viral families identified as sources of nrEVEs in other lepidopterans like Bombyx mori and Plutella xylostella [22], the 15 nrEVEs characterized in this study from C. suppressalis are exclusively derived from the Orthomyxoviridae and Rhabdoviridae families, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). This restricted profile may reflect species-specific histories of viral integration in different insect groups. Recent genome-wide investigations in several species such as mosquitoes [32, 33], planthoppers [13], and aphids [24] have significantly advanced our understanding of nrEVE abundance, diversity, integration mechanisms, and potential functions. For instance, Totiviridae-derived nrEVEs are ubiquitously integrated into the genomes of three major rice sap-sucking pests, N. lugens, L. striatellus, and Sogatella furcifera. Notably, one such element in N. lugens, NlToEVE14, has been experimentally verified to be translated into an authentic protein that influences the planthopper’s development and fecundity [13]. In contrast, C. suppressalis serves as a major chewing pest of rice, with a distinctly different life history from these piercing-sucking insects. However, a systematic investigation of nrEVEs has been lacking. Therefore, by comprehensively characterizing the nrEVEs in C. suppressalis, our study provides key insights into the differences in viral integration between insects with divergent feeding strategies. Furthermore, this work substantially expands our understanding of virus-host interactions within the rice ecosystem.

Among the 15 identified nrEVEs, 13 were derived from the Orthomyxoviridae family (CsOrEVE1–13). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that, exception for CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE10, the other 11 CsOrEVEs originated from NP, RdRp (PB1, PA), and GP encoding regions, respectively. These 11 CsOrEVEs all showed the closest phylogenetic relationship to viruses of the genus Quaranjavirus. Viruses in this genus are typically arboviruses, which can be transmitted by ticks and infect vertebrates, such as Wellfleet Bay virus [36]. Although research on Quaranjavirus remains relatively limited, its potential public health risks have attracted significant attention. Investigating nrEVEs derived from these viruses not only contributes to understanding the evolution of viral pathogenicity but is also crucial for elucidating the dynamics of their host shifts [37]. Recently, a clade of quaranja-like viruses was identified in spiders, and researchers have proposed that Quaranjavirus and quaranja-like viruses could be classified into a separate family, Quaranjaviridae [38]. Furthermore, a remarkable diversity of orthomyxo-like viruses has been observed within Quaranjavirus, forming multiple insect-specific lineages [10]. The abundant identification of nrEVEs closely related to Quaranjavirus in the C. suppressalis genome suggests a long-term and extensive evolutionary interaction between these viruses and C. suppressalis. Quaranjavirus and related viruses may possess significant host diversity, and their evolutionary history is far more complex than currently recognized. Thus, C. suppressalis could serve as a key model for studying long-term co-evolution between Quaranjavirus and insect hosts. Additionally, CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE10, which are derived from the RdRp (PB2) coding sequence, exhibited close phylogenetic relationships with multiple other genera within the Orthomyxoviridae, including Alphainfluenzavirus, Betainfluenzavirus, Deltainfluenzavirus, and Gammainfluenzavirus, which cause influenza in vertebrates including humans; and Thogotovirus, which can cause fatal disease in both invertebrates and vertebrates [39]. This suggests that the PB2 gene element in members of the Orthomyxoviridae may have undergone multiple independent horizontal transfer or recombination events across genera during its evolutionary history, potentially predating the divergence of these viral genera. Such transfer and recombination of key functional genes may have further enhanced the ability of Orthomyxoviridae members to adapt to new hosts, resulting in their extremely broad host range.

The remaining two nrEVEs were derived from the Rhabdoviridae family (CsRhEVE1 and CsRhEVE2). This family includes numerous important pathogens with a wide host range, encompassing humans, other vertebrates, plants, and arthropods, making it a major focus of public health concern. Well-known examples include rabies virus and vesicular stomatitis virus, and arthropods are key vectors for many viruses in this family [40, 41]. Previous studies have identified numerous integrations from viruses within both Rhabdoviridae and Orthomyxoviridae in mosquitoes such as A. aegypti, A. albopictus, and Anopheles species [42, 43]. Furthermore, Rhabdoviridae-derived nrEVEs are also widespread and abundant in non-blood insect species [44], such as aphids [24] and Drosophila [45]. Collectively, the above evidences suggest that C. suppressalis may be an important co-evolutionary host for viruses associated with Orthomyxoviridae and Rhabdoviridae, warranting greater attention in future research on viral evolution and transmission.

Previous studies have demonstrated active transcription of numerous nrEVEs in arthropod genomes, suggesting potential biological significance [13, 42]. We observed a similar phenomenon in C. suppressalis, with all 15 identified nrEVEs being actively transcribed. Notably, five of these (CsOrEVE1, 3, 5, 6, and 11) exhibited marked expression heterogeneity: CsOrEVE1, 3, and 6 showed significantly divergent expression levels across different geographic populations, aligning with reports in mosquitoes [46, 47], planthoppers [13], and aphids [24]. This finding indicates historical variations in exposure to specific exogenous viruses or a potential role for these nrEVEs in host environmental adaptation. Furthermore, the expression of CsOrEVE3, 6, and 11 varied significantly among tissues, while that of CsOrEVE3 and CsOrEVE5 dynamically changed throughout development. These distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns imply potential roles in specific physiological processes or developmental programs, highlighting the functional diversity of these nrEVEs, as exemplified by the NlToEVE14 in N. lugens [13].

12 of the 15 nrEVEs in C. suppressalis were located within transcripts predicted to contain intact ORFs. Moreover, the average amino acid identity between these 12 nrEVEs and their cognate exogenous viruses was considerably high (61.8%; Supplementary Table 1), substantially exceeding the mean amino acid identity (31%) typically observed between EVEs and exogenous viruses [22]. This high sequence conservation suggests that these nrEVEs might have integrated into the host genome relatively recently in evolutionary history. Additionally, the transcripts containing nrEVE11, 12, and 13 were predicted to possess exon-intron structures—a hallmark of eukaryotic genes [48]—suggesting their potential integration into the host’s genome. However, active transcription does not unequivocally demonstrate the production of functional proteins, as evidenced by some mosquito nrEVEs that generate piRNAs rather than being translated [42]. In addition, intron-containing transcripts could arise from readthrough or splicing artifacts. Therefore, whether these nrEVEs in C. suppressalis have been truly domesticated, ultimately leading to transcription or further protein translation, requires further validation in future studies.

Currently, it has been discovered in various arthropods (particularly mosquitoes) that nrEVEs-derived piRNAs can participate in immune defense against cognate exogenous viruses [9, 20, 49]. Similarly, this study found that the majority of nrEVEs can generate sRNAs with lengths of 24–30 nt in C. suppressalis, exhibiting typical size characteristic of piRNAs. However, a canonical Ping-Pong signature, characterized by 1U-sense and 10 A-antisense bias, was exclusively detected for CsOrEVE1. A similar piRNA profile has been previously reported in the silkworm, B. mori [50]. Notably, the production of piRNA-sized small RNAs in the absence of an active Ping-Pong mechanism is not unique to our study. A parallel phenomenon has been observed during B. mori cytoplasmic polyhedrosis virus infection [51, 52]. This recurring pattern across different species suggests that the biogenesis of these “piRNA-like RNAs” identified here may be governed by alternative mechanism, which may represent a significant and promising direction for future research. CsOrEVE2, 5, and 8 show high transcript abundance, and their respective transcripts are predicted to contain complete ORFs. However, no corresponding sRNA production was detected. This phenomenon aligns with the predictions regarding the functional differentiation of insect nrEVEs: most nrEVEs lose their coding capacity due to ORF disruption and result in piRNA generation to exert immunoregulatory functions [18, 22]. Recently identified NlToEVE14 in N. lugens is the first nrEVE functionally confirmed at the protein level. This element does not produce piRNAs but has been co-opted by the host, exhibiting a periodic expression pattern and significantly influencing host physiology [13]. Based on this, it is hypothesized that elements such as CsOrEVE2, 5, and 8 in C. suppressalis may be functional. This hypothesis warrants further investigation.

Although the integration of retroviruses or DNA viruses into host genomes mediated by RTs and integrases is a well-established phenomenon (such as approximately 8% of the human genome is composed of retroviral sequences [53]), growing evidence has revealed that non-retroviral RNA viruses-derived nrEVEs are also widely present. Since non-retroviral RNA viruses do not encode key enzymes such as reverse transcriptase, the mechanisms underlying their integration have become a major focus of current research. Since the first insect nrEVEs were identified in the genomes of A. aegypti and A. albopostictus in 2004 [5], the increasing discovery of such elements in insect genomes suggests that genomic sequences originating from non-retroviral viruses may share similar integration mechanisms across different insect species. Studies have shown that reverse transcriptases and integrases from retrotransposon may mediate the nrEVE integration [45, 54]. In line with observations in Ae. aegypti, where EVE-containing loci are predominantly associated with long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons [42, 55], our Permutation Test also revealed a significant enrichment of retrotransposon and other integration-related domains in the flanking regions of the 15 identified nrEVEs. Notably, our analysis further demonstrated that the associated retrotransposons in our system are primarily of the non-LTR type. This distinction may highlight a potential divergence in the predominant integration mechanisms between these insect species or specific viral contexts. Research has indicated that superfamily 1 helicase-encoding long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs: a class of non-LTR retrotransposons widely distributed in eukaryotic genomes) are commonly present in some lepidopteran insects such as Heliconius melpomene and P. xylostella [56], suggesting a similar characteristic may exist in C. suppressalis. Although complete non-LTR retrotransposon structures were not directly predicted in the flanking regions of other nrEVEs, the widespread detection of related functional elements, including RTs, endonucleases, and RNase H-like superfamily members [57], provides direct and important clues for elucidating the endogenization mechanism of non-retroviral RNA viruses in C. suppressalis.

Conclusion

In this study, we first systematically identified 15 nrEVEs in C. suppressalis, comprising CsOrEVE1–13 and CsRhEVE1-2. This finding suggests a potential long-term co-evolutionary history between these related viruses and C. suppressalis. Evolutionary analysis indicated that viruses in Quaranjavirus genus may play a significant role in shaping the genomic architecture of C. suppressalis. Diverse transcriptome expression patterns, abundant transcriptional coverage, and experimental validation of a subset of low-coverage transcripts collectively indicate the stable endogenization of these nrEVEs within the host genome. Furthermore, the abundant presence of nrEVE-derived piRNA-like sRNAs and the different spatiotemporal and geographic transcriptomic patterns suggest that these nrEVEs may play important functions in C. suppressalis. In summary, this study deepens our understanding of virus-insect co-evolution mechanisms and lays a solid foundation for future investigations into the functions of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis.

Materials and methods

Identification and genomic distribution of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis

The chromosome-level genome assembly of C. suppressalis was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) genome database (Accession number: GCA_902850365.2). Protein sequences of reported RNA viruses from the Crambidae family (including C. suppressalis) and the sympatric species S. inferen were retrieved from the NCBI virus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/) (Supplementary Table 6). Then these protein sequences were searched against the C. suppressalis genome using the tblastn algorithm with a cutoff e-value of 2 × e− 5. If a single query sequence aligned to multiple genomic loci, the resulting hits that were either overlapping or in close proximity (interval < 150 bp) were merged. The longest contiguous sequence from the merged region was then retained and defined as the putative nrEVE sequence. Putative nrEVEs (each longer than 200 nt) were extracted and then subjected to a blastx algorithm against the entire NCBI non-redundant protein database, applying the same e-value threshold (2 × e− 5). This analysis served two purposes: first, to eliminate false positive hits, and second, to identify their closest cognate exogenous viruses. R (4.2.3) was used to compare the genomic distribution of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis.

Phylogenetic analysis of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis

To infer the potential function and taxonomic origin of the nrEVEs, a blastx search was performed against the NCBI non-redundant protein database using the nrEVE nucleotide sequence as a query. The top three exogenous viral protein sequences exhibiting the highest homology to the nrEVEs were selected for further analysis. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with protein sequences derived from functionally analogous nrEVEs, such as RdRP, NP, and GP, along with the top three homologous exogenous viral protein sequences identified above. Besides, representative sequences from relevant virus genera of the Orthomyxoviridae family reported in the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses database (ICTV_VMR_MSL38_v3) were incorporated. All sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.505, and poorly aligned regions were removed using Gblocks v0.91b. Phylogenetic inference was conducted with RAxML-NG v0.9.0 under the best-fit protein substitution model, supported by 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Transcription profiling of nrEVEs via meta-transcriptomics

To investigate the transcriptional dynamics of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis across developmental stages, tissue types, and geographical origins, the following samples were collected: individuals from different developmental stages (egg, larva, pupa, and adult) reared under laboratory conditions at Hunan Agricultural University; various tissues dissected from larvae collected in Ningbo water bamboo fields, including trachea, integument, fat body, gut, malpighian tubules, and silk gland; whole larvae sampled from rice paddies across six major climatic zones in China [Wuhan (Central China), Changchun (Northeastern China), Dongfang (Southern China), Ningbo (Eastern China), Urumchi (Northwestern China), and Kaiyuan (Southwestern China)], representing distinct geographical populations. All samples were listed in Supplementary Table 3. Total RNA was isolated from each sample with the Trizol RNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and quantity were measured using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Ribosomal RNA was then removed from the total RNA, and the remaining RNA was broken into short fragments (250–300 bp). Using these fragments, double-stranded cDNA was generated by reverse transcription. The cDNA was then processed through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, and PCR amplification to prepare the meta-transcriptomic libraries. Trimmomatic version 0.39 [58] was used to remove adaptor contaminations and low-quality reads with the parameters: “ILLUMINACLIP: Illumina.fa:2:30:10:8:TRUE LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:150”. The resulting clean paired-end reads were assembled using Trinity version 2.14.0 [59] with default parameters. TransDecoder version 5.7.1 (Haas) was then used to annotate the coding regions of the transcriptome assemblies. Transcription profiling of nrEVEs were reflected using Transcripts Per Kilobase Millions (TPMs) by mapping clean reads to the nrEVE contigs using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5) [60] with default parameters to calculate TPMs.

Analysis of potential nrEVE transcripts in C. suppressalis

To identify transcripts containing nrEVEs in the C. suppressalis genome, the nrEVEs nucleotide sequences were used as queries to search for corresponding transcripts with the assembled contigs in the above six distinct C. suppressalis geographical populations libraries described above. Then, the nrEVE transcripts were used as queries to search against the C. suppressalis genome by NCBI BLAST Genomes algorithm (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The matched regions of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis genome were retrieved to predict ORFs using the software DNAMAN version 9. To investigate the genomic coverage of nrEVE transcripts, the adaptor- and quality-trimmed reads from corresponding datasets were mapped back to these transcript contigs using Bowtie2 (v.2.3.5) and SAMtools (1.9) [60, 61].

Characterization of conserved domains

To gain further insight into the molecular basis of nrEVE integration, a ± 10 kb flanking genomic region surrounding each identified nrEVE was isolated. These segments were then examined for the presence of conserved protein domains using the NCBI Conserved Domain Search (CD-Search) tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). The enriched analysis method of integration-related domains was revealed in Supplementary File 1.

sRNA profiling of nrEVEs in C. suppressalis

sRNA sequencing and analysis were performed on five distinct C. suppressalis geographical populations samples, except for samples from Kaiyuan City (Supplementary Table 3). The five cDNA libraries were prepared with the NEB Next® Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina®(NEB E7300L, New England Biolabs) and subjected to sequencing on an NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene, Tianjin, China). Raw reads were processed with fastp [62] to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Clean reads ranging from 18 to 30 nt in length were extracted using the seqkit toolkit [63] and aligned to nrEVEs with Bowtie2 (v2.5.2) with default parameters (retaining only the best match). Subsequent bioinformatic analyses containing the mapping statistics (read length distribution and strand specificity) were conducted using custom Python scripts. Finally, all results were visualized using R (v4.0) and ggplot2, illustrating genome-wide sRNA distributions. Sequence signatures at the 5’ ends of sRNAs were investigated by performing a 5’-end alignment, and the nucleotide composition across the first ten positions was displayed using the ggseqlogo 0.2 R package [64]. The 5’-terminal overlap distances between sense and antisense sRNAs mapped to the nrEVEs were examined. The distribution of these overlaps was evaluated against a theoretical random model employing Z-score analysis [65], with results visualized using the ggplot2 R package (v3.5.1).
