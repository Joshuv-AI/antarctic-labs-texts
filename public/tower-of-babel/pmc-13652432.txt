Introduction

Ovarian fibrothecal tumors are rare neoplasms, accounting for 1–4.7% of all organic ovarian tumors. These tumors predominantly affect postmenopausal or perimenopausal women. Although typically benign, they can be associated with hormone secretion. This case report aims to highlight the diagnostic challenges and management strategies for a large ovarian fibrothecal tumor in a postmenopausal patient, contributing to the understanding of this rare entity.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old woman of North African origin, retired, presented to the surgical outpatient department with a 5-month history of progressively worsening pelvic pain.

The patient had a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), previously diagnosed and managed with inhaled bronchodilator therapy. She reported no other chronic medical conditions such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cardiovascular disease.

No prior surgical interventions were reported.

The patient had been menopausal for 20 years. She reported no history of hormone replacement therapy, no prior gynecological malignancy, and no significant obstetric complications.

The patient was on regular inhaled therapy for COPD. She reported no other regular medications. She reported no known drug allergies.

History is noncontributory, with no reported family history of ovarian, breast, or colorectal malignancy; the patient is a nonsmoker, reports no alcohol consumption, and has no significant occupational exposures.

She presented with progressively worsening pelvic pain over the past 5 months. She reported no associated metrorrhagia or digestive transit disorders. Abdominal examination revealed a palpable, lateralized, and mobile hypogastric mass exceeding 10 cm in diameter. Tumor marker analysis showed an elevated CA-125 level of 149.5 IU/mL.

An abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed. These imaging studies identified a voluminous pelvic mass, likely of right ovarian origin, extending to the umbilical region and measuring 167 × 115 × 135 mm. The mass was in contact with the sigmoid colon, cecum, and ileal loops, but without evidence of invasion (Fig. 1). To rule out other pathologies, a colonoscopy was performed, and the results were normal.

Given the patient’s age and severe COPD, the procedure was performed under general endotracheal anesthesia with careful intraoperative ventilatory management to minimize the risk of bronchospasm and barotrauma. The surgery was conducted by a senior consultant surgeon. A midline laparotomy was performed, which immediately revealed a moderate amount of free serous ascites, which was evacuated. The voluminous right ovarian mass was identified exactly as characterized on imaging. While the tumor was densely adherent to the sigmoid colon, cecum, and adjacent ileal loops, meticulous blunt and sharp adhesiolysis confirmed the absence of any deep serosal or mural infiltration. Consequently, the mass was easily resectable in toto without the need for bowel resection or enterectomy. A bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was completed.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. She remained hemodynamically stable and afebrile throughout her hospital stay. Relevant physiological indicators normalized rapidly; gastrointestinal recovery was uneventful, with the first passage of flatus on postoperative day 2 and tolerance of a normal solid diet by postoperative day 3. The surgical incision showed no signs of infection or dehiscence.

The length of hospital stay was 4 days, and the patient was discharged in good general condition.

The definitive anatomopathological study macroscopically described an 18 × 16 × 10 cm tumor with raised, buff-colored remnants, accompanied by two satellite paratubal nodules measuring 2.2 and 0.5 cm in diameter. Microscopic examination confirmed the fibrothecal nature of the tumor, revealing spindle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm arranged within a lobulated or nodular mass separated by fibrovascular septa, which were sometimes myxoid and sometimes hyalinized. Moderate and focal calretinin binding was observed (Fig. 2). The two satellite paratubal nodules corresponded to two serous cystadenomas of the ovary with fibrous walls that were occasionally calcified. The postoperative course was uneventful. Given the definitive histological confirmation of a benign ovarian fibrothecoma, no adjuvant therapies (such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy) were indicated or administered.

The patient was enrolled in a regular outpatient surveillance program. At the time of reporting, the duration of follow-up is 12 months. Clinical evaluations and pelvic ultrasonography performed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively at the hospital outpatient clinic by the operating surgeon revealed no evidence of local recurrence or residual disease. The patient reported complete resolution of her initial pelvic pain and had fully returned to her baseline daily activities and quality of life.

Discussion

Ovarian fibrothecomas are rare benign sex cord-stromal tumors typically occurring in postmenopausal women, consistent with the 67-year-old profile of our patient[1]. However, this case is distinguished from most reports in the global literature by its purely mechanical clinical presentation. Unlike numerous documented cases that manifest with endocrine symptoms (such as postmenopausal bleeding or virilization) or paraneoplastic syndromes such as Meigs’ syndrome (ascites and pleural effusion)[2,3], our patient presented solely with progressive pelvic pain over 5 months, strictly secondary to the mass effect of a large tumor (>10 cm).

Radiologically, while CT and MRI adequately characterize the solid pelvic mass, preoperative differentiation from a malignant neoplasm (such as an ovarian sarcoma) remains impossible for lesions of this size, thereby justifying the indication for a median laparotomy[4].

In the study by Numanoglu et al[5], fibromas and fibrothecomas accounted for 4.7% of the 912 surgically managed adnexal masses (n = 43), affecting women with a mean age of 52.2 years (range: 21–80). When applying the Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI), the majority of patients (76.7%, n = 33) were categorized as low risk, though 23.3% (n = 10) were falsely flagged as high risk. Notably, the RMI demonstrated extremely poor diagnostic accuracy for identifying malignancy in these specific tumors preoperatively, yielding a sensitivity and positive predictive value of 0%, alongside a specificity of 76% and a negative predictive value of 97%.

The most striking and distinctive feature of our intraoperative management lies in the use of frozen-section analysis. The literature emphasizes the excellent reliability of this examination for ovarian stromal tumors[6]. In our case, it allowed for the immediate confirmation of the benign nature of the fibrothecoma, thereby preventing unjustified and morbid radical surgery (such as hysterectomy with lymphadenectomy) and safely limiting the procedure to a bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy.

Take-away lessons

This case serves as a vital reminder that benign ovarian tumors, such as fibrothecomas, can present with features often associated with malignancy, including massive size, ascites, and significantly elevated CA-125 levels. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for these benign entities to avoid overly radical surgery when a more tailored approach is appropriate. Furthermore, the presence of multiple, distinct adnexal pathologies in the same patient highlights the need for meticulous intraoperative exploration and thorough pathological examination of all excised tissues. The characteristic MRI findings, particularly the T2 hyosignal, can be a valuable clue in preoperative diagnosis, guiding surgical planning.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this report is the comprehensive clinical, radiological, and pathological correlation provided for a rare presentation of a large fibrothecoma. The use of both MRI and intraoperative frozen sections demonstrates a rigorous adherence to modern surgical standards, ensuring optimal patient management. Furthermore, the detailed description of the satellite nodules and the explicit exclusion of associated syndromes provide a more complete picture of the adnexal pathology encountered.

However, we must acknowledge certain limitations. As this is a single case report, the findings related to the CA-125 elevation and the specific tumor morphology cannot be generalized to all patients with fibrothecomas. Additionally, while the immediate postoperative course was uneventful, the lack of long-term follow-up data limits our ability to comment on the very long-term prognosis, although the benign nature of the tumor suggests an excellent outlook.

Conclusion

Ovarian fibrothecomas, though rare, represent an important diagnostic consideration, particularly in older women. Their clinical and paraclinical presentations, often characterized by a palpable mass and specific imaging features, typically allow for their localization to the ovary. However, the preoperative distinction between benign fibrothecomas and malignant ovarian processes remains a significant challenge, as these tumors can frequently appear suspicious during surgical exploration, necessitating intraoperative frozen-section analysis for guidance.

Ultimately, the definitive diagnosis of ovarian fibrothecomas relies on thorough histological examination. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for these benign entities, even in the presence of concerning features such as large tumor size and elevated CA-125 levels. By doing so, clinicians can ensure appropriate surgical management and avoid unnecessary radical interventions.
