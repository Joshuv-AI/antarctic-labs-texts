ArchetypeShift: An R Package Integrating KEGG-Informed Pathway Analysis and IPA-Derived Functional Predictions for Validation of Single-Cell Archetypes
Authors: Adam P. Wilson, Hala Chaaban, Kathryn Y. Burge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functional archetype analysis of single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data is important because clustering of cell types is often nuanced and inexact. Intermediate phenotypes exist that are difficult to account for in these analyses, particularly in the setting of infant development. Further, different cell types work together to achieve biological functions, thereby broadly supporting tissue function at homeostasis and through environmental challenges via phenotypic plasticity. Currently, the process of assigning archetypes to cell clusters is labor-intensive because annotation requires manual upload of expression data to multiple programs. ArchetypeShift is an R-based pipeline that integrates existing archetypal analysis methods with annotation by Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), graphics visualization, and trajectory analysis for scRNA-seq data. Generating IPA- and/or KEGG-informed dot plots, UMAPs (uniform manifold approximation and projections) of archetype weights, archetype maps, heatmaps defining the top genes for each archetype program, and trajectory analysis graphics, ArchetypeShift streamlines the biological interpretation of archetype programs within a unified and efficient analytical framework. The source code for ArchetypeShift is available on GitHub (https://github.com/Neo-NEC-Lab/ArchetypeShift).

Introduction

Characterizing cellular heterogeneity within single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) transcriptomes is a conceptually and technically daunting task. Historically, investigators have used dimensional reduction (e.g., principal component analysis [PCA]) to computationally identify highly variable genes (HVGs). Variable clustering methods (e.g., hierarchical, k-means, community-detection-based, density-based, etc.) are then combined with manual annotation to assign each cluster a specific cell type.1,2 However, variation in user-defined parameters, such as feature numbers per sample, and conflicting reports in the literature upon which manual annotation is based, often result in cell-type clusters that are not well-differentiated functionally. 3 Further, many cell-type-specific genes integral to functional identity are weakly expressed, 4 resulting in delineations among cell type clusters that are often blurred by cells transitioning between cell types (e.g., during development), displaying multiple intermediate, and potentially functionally significant, phenotypes that are difficult to cluster accurately.

Despite these hurdles, recent advances in single-cell analysis have produced numerous methods for cell-type and cell-state annotation. Supervised approaches such as scAnnotate 5 and Garnett 6 leverage reference datasets to classify cells into known populations, while semi-supervised frameworks such as HiCat 7 incorporate marker information and hierarchical relationships to improve annotation accuracy. More recently, probabilistic models such as scANVI, 8 interpretable statistical methods such as PCLDA, 9 and deep-learning-based approaches such as scSorterDL, 10 have sought to improve the identification and characterization of complex cellular states. These methods have substantially advanced single-cell annotation; however, most are designed to assign cells to predefined labels or reference-derived states.

In contrast, archetypal analysis (AA) uses Pareto optimality theory 11 to define functional identities of cells as coordinates in multidimensional space, with the extreme vertices of the phenotypic space (i.e., the convex hull, approximated by a polytope) defined by specialized functions or archetypes. Single-cell Pareto Task Interference (ParTI) 12 is used to mathematically determine the number of archetypes optimally defining a polytope. AA, then, defines cell function as a convex combination, with very specialized cells performing a singular primary function mapped adjacent to a vertex, while more generalist cells lay in continuous space performing a broader variety of cell functions. 13 AA, therefore, seeks to identify extreme transcriptional programs and represent cells as mixtures of these programs, providing a complementary framework for studying continuous biological processes and transitional cell states.

Importantly, current processes for AA are inefficient, as they involve manual upload of cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and their respective p-values and fold changes, to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) and/or KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) databases for functional annotation. A new R package, ArchetypeShift, streamlines functional, AA-based analysis of scRNA-seq data. Importantly, ArchetypeShift is not intended as a replacement for existing archetype-fitting frameworks, nor does it introduce a novel AA algorithm. Instead, the package builds upon established methods, including ParTI-based principal convex hull analysis (PCHA), integrating these approaches with downstream pathway enrichment and upstream regulator prediction, graphics visualization, and trajectory analyses in a single R-based pipeline. The primary contribution of ArchetypeShift is the reduction of analytical fragmentation, allowing users to move from archetype inference to functional, biological interpretation and condition-specific comparisons using a standardized, reproducible, and customizable workflow. Here, we demonstrate the utility of ArchetypeShift using an example dataset derived from the preterm infant small intestine.

Methods

In Vitro Treatment of Human Enteroids

Human ileal enteroids (n=3 patient lines), derived from distal bowel surgical resections from preterm infants with necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), were grown and maintained in WRNE media (containing Wnt3a, R-spondin-3, and noggin) in three-dimensional extracellular matrix (ECM; Matrigel® growth factor-reduced basement membrane matrix, Corning, Corning, NY) domes, as previously described. 14 Enteroids were differentiated for 72 h using human IntestiCult™ Organoid Differentiation Medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, British Colombia, Canada). In the last 48 h of enteroid differentiation, enteroids were simultaneously treated with 150 µg/mL hyaluronan 35 kDa (HA; Lifecore Biomedical, Chaska, MN) or vehicle control (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Following treatment, NEC and NEC_HA enteroids were removed from ECM domes using ice-cold VitroGel® Organoid Recovery Solution (TheWell Bioscience, North Brunswick, NJ), washed with PBS, and mechanically disrupted by pipette. Enteroids were then dissociated into a single-cell suspension using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C until fully dissociated. Dissociated cells were further disrupted with cold Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Ham’s F-12 (DMEM/F-12; STEMCELL Technologies) + 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA), then filtered through a 37 µm strainer (STEMCELL Technologies). Dead cells were removed from the single-cell suspension using an EasySep™ Dead Cell Removal (Annexin V) kit paired with an EasySep™ magnet (STEMCELL Technologies), per manufacturer instructions. Cell viability was then assessed for 10x Genomics scRNA-seq thresholds using 0.04% trypan blue stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Countess 3 automated cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

ScRNA-Seq

Samples were processed with the Chromium GEM-X 3′ Gene Expression Kit v4 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) and libraries were generated using the Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ Gel Bead Kit v4 (10x Genomics). Cell Ranger v 9.0.1 (10x Genomics) was used to align reads with the GRCh38-2024-A reference transcriptome, perform unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting, and process FASTQ files. Additionally, Cell Ranger was used to extract barcodes and generate a filtered-feature barcode matrix for each sample (gene-by-cell count matrix) for downstream processing in R v. 4.4.1 15 with the Seurat v. 5.2.1 16 package.

Single-Cell Data Processing, Annotation, and Visualization

To remove low quality data, data from cells representing the lowest 1% of UMI counts were removed. Additionally, data from cells with more than 9,000 detected genes, more than 60,000 UMIs, and more than 10% mitochondrial or 3% hemoglobin transcripts were removed. Data were normalized via log-normalization for standard clustering and visualization analyses and with SCTransform (regularized negative binomial regression) for integrated analyses, as indicated. HVGs were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) method, selecting 2,000 features per sample. For log-normalized analyses, data were scaled and PCA was performed (capped at 30 PCs). Data were then integrated with reciprocal principal component analysis (RPCA). RPCA was selected, rather than Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), Harmony, and other batch correction methods, due to improved integration performance with less overcorrection. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization, neighbor graph construction, and clustering were performed using the top 30 PCs (dims 1:30) for the integrated reduction. Clusters were manually annotated by comparing marker DEGs to known published reference datasets.17,18

Statistical Analysis

Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied to high-dimensional analyses involving multiple simultaneous hypothesis tests, including global and cluster-level generalized additive model (GAM) pseudotime comparisons, gene-level GAMs, IPA and KEGG pathway enrichment, and Monocle3 trajectories analyses. Adjusted p-values are reported as q-values or FDR-adjusted p-values, with significance defined as q≤0.05. Analyses involving a limited number of predefined comparisons, such as cluster-level archetype comparisons in volcano and violin plots and GAMs of Archetype 3 across pseudotime, are reported using nominal p-values, with significance defined as p≤0.05. Unless otherwise noted, statistical comparisons involving individual cells should be interpreted as cell-level associations rather than independent biological replicate comparisons.

Input Validation and Metadata Resolution

Prior to model-fitting, Seurat objects were validated using check_input(). This step confirms that the assay and required metadata fields, including group/condition (group_col), biological/sample identifier (sample_col), and cell/cluster annotation (cluster_col), are present. Columns were auto-detected from common scRNA-seq names (group: condition, group, treatment; sample: orig.ident, sample, donor; cluster: seurat_clusters, cluster, etc.). Samples with fewer than 100 cells were flagged with a warning.

Gene Universe

A gene universe was constructed from the RNA assay matrix using make_gene_universe(). Genes were filtered to optionally exclude mitochondrial genes (prefix MT-/mt-), ribosomal protein large and small subunits (RPL and RPS, respectively) and MALAT1 (metastasis associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1). To remove rarely detected features, genes expressed in fewer than 10 cells were excluded. This gene universe was used to restrict downstream feature selection.

Selection of HVGs

HVGs were selected from the RNA assay using Seurat’s VST method with select_hvgs(). HVGs were computed separately within each experimental group and then combined across groups using either union (genes variable in any group) or intersection (genes variable in all groups), with an n of 3,000. HVGs were intersected with the gene universe prior to PCA.

PCA Feature Space

PCA was performed on the log-normalized expression values from the RNA assay. PCA was computed using prcomp_irlba (v. 2.3.5.1) 20 with centering enabled and scaling disabled. The top 30 principal components were retained. PC scores were stored as a matrix of cells x PC.

Archetype Model Fitting and Selection With ParetoTI

Archetypes were fit in PC space using ParetoTI’s PCHA implementation. Model selection was evaluated across a range of archetype numbers (k=3-10) using k_fit_pch (Figure 2B). Four key complementary metrics, including archetype volume (Supplementary Figure 1A), t -ratio (Supplementary Figure 1B), convex hull volume (Supplementary Figure 1C), and the sum of squared error (SSE; Supplementary Figure 1D) were used to assess best archetype fit. As with other unsupervised learning approaches, selection of archetype number requires balancing model complexity with biological interpretability. An archetype number of 5 (k=5) represents the point at which improvements in reconstruction error (i.e., SSE; Supplementary. Figure 1D) begin to plateau. Although models with more archetypes further reduced SSE, these gains were modest and accompanied by substantial increases in archetype (Supplementary Figure 1A) and convex hull (Supplementary Figure 1C) volumes, suggesting over-partitioning of the cellular state space. Further, the t-ratio (Supplementary Figure 1B) approaches a plateau by k=5, indicating the major structure of the data has already been captured.

To further illustrate archetype number justification, we performed a sensitivity analysis for dominant archetypes k=4-6 by computing Pearson correlations between cluster-level archetypes and matching each archetype to its most correlated dominant biological program (Supplementary Figure 2). Three k=5 archetypes showed high correlation with k=4 (r2=0.981-0.988), while two k=5 archetypes did not correlate with k=4 archetypes (r2=0.654 and 0.686; Supplementary Table 1). As seen in the UMAP, k=5 captures an additional spatial program not seen in k=4 (Supplementary Figure 3). In contrast, all k=6 archetypes correlated with k=5 archetypes (r2=0.906-0.998; Supplementary Table 2). The k=6 UMAP did not reveal a new archetype, but rather subdivided an existing archetype (Supplementary Figure 3). Collectively, these metrics support k=5 in capturing most of the explainable geometry without introducing unnecessary model complexity (k=6) or underfitting (k=4).

Archetype Quality Control (Entropy and Max Weight)

Target models at specified k values were then fit using fit_pch, producing archetype vertices (XC: PCs x k) and per-cell archetype weight matrices (S: k x cells). Archetype weights (w), providing the contribution of each archetype to the transcriptional state of each cell, were configured to be non-negative and sum to one per cell, so each cell could be represented through a combination of archetype programs. To evaluate whether the fitted archetypes were interpretable, we used two per-cell diagnostics calculated from the archetype weight vector, w. Archetype dominance was summarized as the maximum archetype weight per cell, maxi, wi. A value near 1 indicates an archetype dominates the cell, whereas a value near 1/k indicates an even mixture across all archetypes. Archetype entropy was summarized using normalized Shannon entropy, H(w)/log(k), generating values from 0 to 1 (0=one dominant archetype, 1=uniform mixture of archetypes). Most cells were characterized by a dominant archetype program (max weight median: 0.62; interquartile range [IQR]: 0.50–0.76; Supplementary Figure 4A), while minor contributions from other archetype programs resulted in intermediate normalized Shannon entropy values (normalized entropy median: 0.54; IQR: 0.40–0.66; Supplementary Figure 4B). These distributions confirm most cells occupy a mixture of generalist and specialist states, rather than a single, discrete archetype program.

Spatial Separation of Archetypes

UMAPs resulting from traditional scRNA-seq analysis provide very limited cluster-specific functional information. In contrast, projection of archetype weights onto the original UMAP (Figure 2A) revealed unique spatial orientations of each of the five archetypes (Figure 2C). Each archetype is localized to a different but extreme region of the UMAP, demonstrating that each program functions uniquely within the intestine. Assigning each cell to its dominant archetype revealed further spatial organization on the UMAP (Figure 2D). To visualize overlap between cell types and archetype programs, mean archetype weights were calculated across cell clusters (Figure 2E). Along the crypt-villus axis, cell types overlapped with different archetypes, suggesting unique transcriptional programs as cells differentiate along the crypt-villus axis. Detected archetypes were visualized in principal component space, confirming each archetype occupies a unique gene program within a space confined by five archetype vertices (Figure 2F). Each vertex represents an extreme gene program characterizing specialist cells, while generalist cells, representing a mixture of archetype programs, are located between vertices.

Archetype Program Annotation

Archetype identities were assigned through a multi-step annotation procedure. For each archetype, a gene program was defined using correlation-based signatures with compute_signature_cor(). Under this custom function, gene expression values for each gene were correlated with per-cell archetype weights across all epithelial cells using Spearman’s correlations. Genes were ranked by correlation coefficient, and the top 100 correlated genes were used to define archetype-associated gene signatures. Genes expressed in fewer than 5% of cells were excluded, along with overexpressed mitochondrial and ribosomal genes. Correlation-based gene signatures derived directly from the single-cell data captured genes whose expression varied with archetype activity, allowing identification of transcriptional programs associated with each archetype rather than discrete cell-type cluster markers (Figure 3A). Ranked archetype-specific gene signatures were then analyzed with IPA for enriched canonical pathways and upstream regulator predictions using annotate_ipa(). Pathways and regulators were plotted in R using a custom dotplot function, plot_ipa_dotplot. Terms were filtered by archetype, an overlap of at least 3 genes, and significance p≤0.05. For upstream regulators, microRNA and chemicals/drugs were excluded. IPA pathway and upstream regulator enrichment results were evaluated together to define biological program annotations for each archetype. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis 21 was also performed on archetype gene lists using the R package, clusterProfiler. 22 Enrichment was assessed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) against KEGG pathways (Supplementary Figure 5A–E). Finally, we evaluated the distribution of archetype weights across annotated epithelial clusters. Archetype activity and dominance were visualized by projecting per-cell archetype weights onto the UMAP. The gene-archetype correlation coefficient was used as the module weight, and per-cell weighted module scores were calculated from normalized RNA expression values. The strongest signatures were observed in Archetypes 2-5, with Archetype 1 (A1) demonstrating a more generalist pattern (Supplementary Figure 6). The locations of cells with high archetype weights were compared with previously annotated clusters, including stem, TA, progenitor, and mature absorptive and secretory populations. This allowed visualization of where, along the crypt-villus axis, each archetype program was most active. The top genes associated with each archetype were compared with reported top epithelial markers and gene programs described in the literature.17,18 Archetype labels of terminal epithelial maturation, metabolic stress adaptation, inflammatory enterocyte stress, TA proliferation, and biosynthetic growth were assigned based on concordance among highly correlated genes, significantly enriched biological pathways, predicted upstream regulators, and the spatial localization of archetype weights across the epithelial transcriptional manifold. These archetype names should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of dominant biological features, rather than definitive cell-state classifications. Archetype 3 (inflammatory enterocyte stress) occupied a similar region in NEC and NEC_HA (Supplementary Figure 9), indicating HA treatment did not generate an entirely new transcriptional program. While the cumulative cell type composition of Archetype 3 in NEC and NEC_HA did not differ (Supplementary Figure 10; nominal p=0.2059), HA did induce cluster-specific changes within Archetype 3 (Figure 4B; Supplementary Figure 11). Trajectory Analysis

Single-cell trajectories were inferred using Moncole3. 23 A principal graph was learned to model an epithelial state progression (pseudotime), from trajectory rooted early stem/secretory progenitor regions toward more differentiated, mature states. For Archetype 3 (inflammatory enterocyte stress; Figure 5A), the merged epithelial manifold demonstrates that NEC and NEC_HA occupy the same epithelial trajectory rather than forming condition-specific branches (Supplementary Figure 12). Archetype program dynamics were assessed by plotting per-cell archetype weights as a function of pseudotime. Inflammatory enterocyte stress mapped onto the global pseudotime trajectory indicates strong association of the archetype as a function of pseudotime, with values rising from low levels in early developmental states to intermediate in late pseudotime, and declining in terminal states (Figure 5B). To evaluate condition differences in archetype dynamics, we fit GAMs with mgcv. 24 Cluster-restricted GAMs were then used to test whether pseudotime differed between NEC and NEC_HA within individual cell clusters. A GAM further confirmed significant effects of each condition (nominal p=0.0175) and pseudotime (nominal p=2 x 10-16) on Archetype 3 weight, with significant conditional smooths (NEC: nominal p=2 x 10-16; NEC_HA: nominal p=0.00268; data not shown). We then plotted cell cluster pseudotime trajectories to establish whether HA treatment alters pseudotime trajectories in a cluster-dependent manner (Supplementary Figure 13). Pseudotime shifts were most prominent in earlier and intermediate epithelial states, with immature AE showing the strongest shift in NEC_HA relative to NEC (FDR-adjusted p = 4.54 x 10-50), followed by AE progenitor (FDR-adjusted p = 8.53 x 10-7), and secretory progenitor (FDR-adjusted p = 4.44 x 10-4). In the most dominant inflammatory enterocyte cluster, mature AE2, there was no significant shift in pseudotime trajectory with HA treatment (FDR-adjusted p = 0.328). Further, inflammatory enterocyte stress weights were associated with pseudotime trajectories in a condition-dependent manner, with AE progenitor (nominal p = 0.0391) and mature AE1 (nominal p = 0.0233) clusters most significantly altered by HA treatment, and a trend towards HA-associated trajectory alterations in mature AE2 (nominal p = 0.0917). Together, pseudotime analysis suggests that HA, rather than creating a new inflammatory epithelial state, shifts the inflammatory stress archetype away from a mature absorptive focus toward earlier progenitor states.

For gene-level trajectory analysis, genes were selected from the ArchetypeShift A3 program using the top positively weighted genes identified from the correlation-based gene signature analysis for the inflammatory stress archetype (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table 3). These Archetype 3 genes were integrated with Monocle graph_test results, which quantifies graph-autocorrelated expression along the learned principal graph. The selected genes for visualization were chosen from this set as representative examples of biological processes associated with each archetype, as each demonstrates significant trajectory dependence and correlation of expression across pseudotime, but are not intended to represent an unbiased statistical ranking. For the inflammatory enterocyte stress archetype (A3), all four selected genes (ANPEP, FABP2 [fatty acid binding protein 2], KRT20, and RNF186; Figure 5C) showed trajectory dependence along epithelial pseudotime. HA treatment reduced ANPEP (FDR-adjusted p=1.15 x 10-5) and FABP2 (FDR-adjusted p=1 x 10-16) trajectory dependence compared with vehicle control, while KRT20 and RNF186 did not significantly differ by treatment.

To further validate pseudotime trajectory, we used Slingshot 25 on a subset of clusters, including secretory progenitor, immature AE, mature AE1, and mature AE2. Pseudotime was inferred in UMAP space, with trajectory rooted in secretory progenitor. Inflammatory enterocyte stress (Archetype 3) dynamics were evaluated across Slingshot pseudotime. Slingshot reproduced the Monocle3 trajectory in these Archetype 3-dominant cell clusters (Supplementary Figure 14A), with the inflammatory enterocyte stress signal rising mid-trajectory and declining at the terminal state (Supplementary Figure 14B). Global pseudotime significantly differed by condition (nominal p=2.2 x 10-16), and inflammatory stress weights were significantly different between conditions (nominal p=7.75 x 10-14) and pseudotimes (nominal p=2 x 10-16) using GAMs. At the cluster-level, HA shifts were again toward later pseudotime in immature AE, AE progenitor, and secretory progenitor clusters.

Discussion

Analyzing cellular heterogeneity in scRNA-seq data remains a complex challenge, particularly when relying on traditional approaches constrained by user-defined parameters, inconsistent reference data, and the difficulty of distinguishing between functionally similar or transitional cell states. With the rapid expansion of scRNA-seq analysis techniques,26-28 demand is increasing for novel approaches that can better interpret biological data, moving beyond discrete clustering toward functional relationships. AA addresses this need by modeling cells along a continuum of functional states, where specialized cell types lie at extremes and more generalized cells occupy intermediate positions, providing a more nuanced framework in functional cell identification.

Although a variety of tools exist for scRNA-seq analysis, most are designed for individual steps (e.g., clustering, differential expression, pathway enrichment, visualization) rather than integrated workflows. 29 As a result, annotating and comparing archetypes across samples often requires multiple packages, potentially in different environments (e.g., ParTIpy 30 in Python, ParTI 12 in MATLAB). ArchetypeShift was developed to overcome this fragmentation by integrating AA with functional pathway interpretation into a single, streamlined workflow, built end-to-end in R. ArchetypeShift combines archetype fitting, gene-archetype correlation, and pathway-based annotation using IPA and KEGG, generating a unified framework that links transcriptional programs, cell states, and condition-specific changes directly to biological pathways, with dedicated visual outputs for each analysis component. By incorporating KEGG and IPA functional analyses, ArchetypeShift enables deeper biological interpretation of archetype programs and associated changes across conditions, significantly reducing the time and complexity required for scRNA-seq analysis.

The principal innovation of ArchetypeShift is not the development of a new archetype inference method, but rather the integration of archetype fitting, functional annotation, visualization, and trajectory analyses into a unified workflow compatible with contemporary single-cell analysis workflows, such as Seurat. While ArchetypeShift builds upon several established methods/platforms (e.g., Seurat, ParetoTI/PCHA, pathway enrichment tools, trajectory inference frameworks), the pipeline develops a unified analytical framework specifically designed for biological interpretation of archetypes and archetype-associated cellular functions. While individual components of this workflow can be implemented separately through custom scripting, archetype-based analyses currently require substantial manual integration of multiple packages, custom data structures, and ad hoc strategies for interpretation. This process can be labor-intensive, difficult to reproduce, and challenging to standardize across datasets. ArchetypeShift was developed to address this methodological gap by providing a standardized and reproducible workflow that links archetype inference, pathway-based annotation, archetype-specific visualization, condition-dependent archetype comparisons, and trajectory analyses within a single framework, thereby lowering the barrier to adoption of AA in single-cell studies.

Thus, ArchetypeShift complements existing single-cell annotation approaches rather than replacing them. Methods such as scAnnotate, 5 HiCat, 7 PCLDA, 9 and scSorterDL, 10 are highly effective for assigning cells to known cell types or biologically curated states using supervised, semi-supervised, or machine-learning-based strategies, respectively. In contrast, ArchetypeShift focuses on the interpretation of archetypes derived from continuous transcriptional landscapes. Because archetypes are not necessarily equivalent to canonical cell types, biological interpretation often requires integration of pathway enrichment, regulator prediction, trajectory inference, and visualization tools. ArchetypeShift addresses this need by providing a unified workflow that links archetype inference to downstream biological interpretation, enabling investigators to characterize functional programs that may extend across conventional cell-type boundaries. Although numerous methods exist for cell-type and cell-state annotation, comparatively fewer tools focus on the biological interpretation of archetype programs identified through AA.

While an efficient and effective pipeline to explore single-cell archetype programs, ArchetypeShift is subject to several limitations. Model fitting can be computationally intensive, even with dedicated access to high-performance computing (HPC) resources. Yet, computational performance and scalability remain important considerations for efficiently implementing ArchetypeShift across a variety of datasets. Benchmark runtime for archetype fitting, using an HPC cluster of 8 CPUs per task and 64 GB of allocated memory, was analyzed in R using a linear regression model of log-transformed runtime with log10 transformed cell number, archetype number (k), and their interaction as predictors. Median runtime for k=9 and 10 increased significantly with cell number (nominal p<0.01 and nominal p<0.0001, respectively) when compared to archetype k=5 (Supplementary Figure 15). Although ArchetypeShift performed efficiently in these studies using a personal workstation, scaling beyond 5,000 cells with k=9 or any cell number with k ≥10 clearly requires HPC. Future development efforts will focus on optimizing memory usage, improving parallelization, and evaluating pipeline performance across increasingly large datasets. Future iterations of the package will address computational scalability through incorporation of parallelized implementations of key fitting steps, broadening accessibility of the package.

Despite integrated visualizations and pathway-level summaries, AA annotation can be somewhat subjective, biased toward archetypes defined by a more generalist pattern and vague KEGG and IPA pathway/regulator outputs. To address this limitation, Reactome pathway analysis with ReactomePA, 31 ranked gene set approaches, such as fgsea 32 with MSigDB-derived gene sets, 33 and complementary reference-based tools such as SingleR, 34 can be utilized for additional functional validation. Additionally, while ArchetypeShift employs linear AA via ParetoTI for computational efficiency and ease of interpretation, single-cell transcriptional states may, realistically, reside in non-linear manifolds—particularly in systems where transcriptional trajectories exhibit branching, cyclicity, or curved development (e.g., during infancy). To better model this heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and often curved data, deep learning-based extensions of AA (e.g., scAAnet) 35 have been created to escape these linear restraints. ScAAnet leverages autoencoder architectures to model non-linear structures in learned latent spaces. While these approaches can better capture complex transcriptional geometries through enhanced resolution of non-linear transitions between and among cell states, additional challenges regarding optimization complexity and direct interpretability of functional archetypes are introduced, limiting reproducibility. Further, neural archetypal models often require significant training costs, scaling inefficiently with both dataset size and model complexity. Given these considerations, ArchetypeShift prioritizes a linear AA backbone as a deliberately constrained model to ensure biological interpretability, reproducibility, and deterministic scaling behavior across datasets spanning multiple orders of magnitude in cell number. However, future studies will require systematic benchmarking of linear and non-linear archetypal frameworks, including neural AA methods, across large-scale single-cell atlases to quantify trade-offs among accuracy, scalability, and biological interpretability.

A further limitation is that the primary analyses in this study were performed at the single-cell level, with cells serving as the biological unit of analysis. Given our study included enteroid lines from only 3 independent patients, the risk of cell-level analyses overestimating significance (pseudoreplication) is real. However, our intent was to demonstrate how ArchetypeShift can characterize shifts in transcriptional programs at single-cell resolution rather than to make population-level inferences across patients. Future studies incorporating larger numbers of biological replicates will be needed to establish the generalizability of these findings across patient populations.

Recent advances in multi-omics analysis have increasingly leveraged graph-based and heterogeneous graph-based frameworks to integrate information across modalities. Examples include approaches such as scAGCI 36 for integrated scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq clustering and MOH 37 for multilayer multi-omics heterogeneous graph-based single-cell clustering. Although ArchetypeShift was not explicitly evaluated for these methods or on multi-omic datasets, AA could potentially provide an additional layer of functional interpretation by characterizing extreme transcriptional programs and continuous cellular states identified within integrated latent spaces. Future studies will be needed to determine the utility of archetype-guided annotation in these emerging multi-omics contexts. Finally, ArchetypeShift was studied only in the context of the preterm small intestinal epithelium. Future validation in disparate tissues and diseases will be important in fine-tuning the package. While the data presented here are biologically plausible, in rare cases, archetype programs may be defined by mathematically optimal, but physiologically unrealistic values. End users are urged to apply their biological expertise toward validating annotation outputs.

Conclusion

ArchetypeShift provides an R package to integrate transcriptional archetypes with IPA pathway and regulator analysis for biological interpretation in scRNA-seq analyses. The workflow identified distinct epithelial archetype programs and used IPA- and KEGG-informed analyses of condition-dependent inflammatory stress states across cell states and pseudotime. By incorporating these steps within an R-based pipeline, ArchetypeShift simultaneously improves biological interpretations and reduces the need for multiple, sequential packages in scRNA-seq analysis.
