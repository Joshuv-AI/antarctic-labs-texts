Heavy Metal Content of Water Hyacinth (Pontederia (Eichhornia) crassipes) and Its Safe Inclusion Levels in Ruminant, Poultry, and Swine Diets: A Critical Review
Authors: Getachew Assefa, Habtamu Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWater hyacinth poses significant environmental challenge in many countries. As part of the effort of reducing its negative effect, there is a great interest to use it as animal feed. However, reports of its safe inclusion level are not consistent. Since the heavy metal content of water hyacinth differed with the water quality it grows in, the safe inclusion level recommendation based on single research results can't be reliable to use widely. This study made a review of the heavy metal content of water hyacinth published to 2025 and presented widely applicable safe inclusion level of water hyacinth to ruminant, poultry, and swine based on the 95th percentile value of the heavy metals content report and the maximum tolerable limit of heavy metals reported by NRC (2005). Therefore, based on these values, water hyacinth leaf can be included in ruminants’, poultry and swine rations up to 13.5%, 9.19% and 18.4%, respectively, without posing a problem on animals due to the heavy metal intake. Water hyacinth shoot can be included in cattle, sheep and poultry ration up to 17.3%. For swine, it can be included up to 24.4%. The whole plant water hyacinth can safely be used in ruminants and nonruminants’ rations up to 6.23% and 6.15%, respectively. These recommendations are safe for 95% of the areas where heavy metal content of water hyacinth reported.Keywords: cattle, poultry, sheep, swine

Introduction

Water hyacinth (Pontederia (Eichhornia) crassipes) is a free‐floating, perennial hydrophyte (Akwukwaegbu et al. 2026). It is characterised by large, thick, shiny, ovate leaves (Ramadan et al. 2026), swollen and spongy petioles allowing buoyancy (Brito et al. 2026), and long (Elbasiouny et al. 2021) purple black fibrous roots (Ramadan et al. 2026). Water hyacinth produces daughter plants via runners and flower seeds (Mengist and Moges 2019).

Water hyacinth poses a significant environmental challenge in many countries. It quickly invades large water areas with its high rate of vegetative propagation, forming dense mats of congregated hyacinths (Elsie Erika et al. 2019). Its invasiveness is assisted by its ability to clone itself (Zhang et al. 2010).

Water hyacinths can cover the surface of water bodies, thereby reducing the amount of sunlight and air penetrating the water. The plant can cause shallowing and increase evaporation due to evapotranspiration (Yuniati et al. 2018). Because of its high evaporation‐transpiration rate water hyacinths can potentially dry up smaller lakes and ponds and minimise water flows in rivers and irrigation canals (Chala et al. 2026).

As part of the effort to reduce its negative effect on the ecosystem, there is a great interest to use water hyacinths for animal feed. As livestock feed, water hyacinth leaves contain 7.17% (Malik et al. 2025) to 14.5% (Mulu et al. 2025) DM. The CP content was reported to range from 0.93% (Mustari et al. 2025) to 17.8% (Malik et al. 2025). It also contains 8.29 MJ/kg DM metabolisable energy (Dong et al. 2020). WH produces from 100 tons (Wechakorn et al. 2024) to 400 tons (Elbasiouny et al. 2021) plant biomass per hectare per year. However, water hyacinth is also known to accumulate heavy metals (Guchhait et al. 2025) that are toxic to livestock (Tahir and Alkheraije 2023). Water hyacinth (WH) not only accumulates a wide range of heavy metals (HM) but it also enhances their uptake and translocation in the more polluted areas, demonstrating that it has a strong ability for HM removal from water (Elbasiouny et al. 2021). The toxicity of HMs in WH as part of animal feed can be managed by maintaining the inclusion level of water hyacinth in the ration so that the heavy metal content of the total ration will be below the maximum tolerable level. Since there is a great variation in the heavy metal content of water hyacinth in different locations, there is no universal and safe level of inclusion of water hyacinth in animal feeds. This study made a wide‐ranging review of the heavy metal content of water hyacinth and presented a safe inclusion level of water hyacinth to ruminant, poultry, and swine based on the 95th percentile value of the heavy metals content reported in documents published to 2025 and the maximum tolerable limit of heavy metals reported by NRC (2005).

Determination of the Maximum Inclusion Level of Water Hyacinth

The maximum safe inclusion level of water hyacinth in ruminant, poultry, or swine ration was determined based on the concentration of heavy metals reported, namely lead (Pb), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), mercury (Hg), cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), and manganese (Mn). The maximum tolerable limit (MTL; mg/kg dry matter) for each heavy metal in the complete diet of animals was taken from NRC (2005). This formula was derived from solving the question that ‘If the maximum tolerable limit of heavy metal “i” is MTL i and the 95th percentile value of heavy metal “i” content of water hyacinth harvested from different water bodies is Ci 95, what will be the inclusion level of water hyacinth in a complete feed at which the heavy metal “i” content of the complete feed is just equal to the maximum tolerable limit of heavy metal “i” when all the other feed ingredients and water were free from heavy metal “i”’. SAS 9.2 (SAS institute Inc. n.d) and Microsoft excel (Microsoft Corporation 2019) were used to calculate the 95th percentiles value and the threshold or MIP, respectivelyS1.

This procedure resulted seven heavy metals specific threshold, that is, maximum inclusion percentage (MIP) values corresponding to lead (Pb), Iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), mercury (Hg), cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), and manganese (Mn). The final maximum inclusion level of water hyacinth was defined as the lowest MIP value among all heavy metals.

The safe MIP of water hyacinth leaf, due to its heavy metal content, in cattle and sheep ration is 13.5%. For poultry and swine, it is 9.19 and 18.4% of DM, respectively. That means there will be no negative effect on animal health and production due to heavy metals if water hyacinth leaf is added to the ration of ruminants, poultry and swine ration up to 13.5%, 9.19 and 18.4%, respectively. The calculated MIP of Water hyacinth shoot for cattle, sheep, and poultry was 17.3%. For swine, it was 24.4%. For the whole plant water hyacinth, the MIP for cattle and sheep it is 6.23. For poultry and swine, it is 6.15% of their ration DM.

Discussion

In agreement with the recommendation of water hyacinth leaf in poultry and ruminants ration (i.e. 9.19% and 13.5%), Amit et al. (2019) reported that water hyacinth can be used as an alternative feed source for growing dairy bull calves at 10%–20% inclusion rate to ensure its optimal utilisation. Supporting the above recommendation, de Vasconcelos et al. (2016) after comparing the 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% inclusion reported that even at 20% inclusion dry matter intake and fibre digestibility in sheep was reduced. In agreement with the calculated maximum inclusion level of water hyacinth leaf for swine indicated in the result section (i.e. up to 18.4%), FAO (n.d) reported traditional pig feeding system use water hyacinth 5–10%. Supporting the current recommendation inclusion of 1%–3% water hyacinth leaves (dry matter basis) in goat diets as a natural feed protein protector has also reported to improve growth performance and enhances meat quality, particularly by increasing average daily gain and meat protein content (Sondakh et al. 2025).

In agreement with the calculated maximum inclusion level of water hyacinth whole plant for sheep and poultry indicated in the result section (i.e. up to 6.23% and 6.16%, respectively), Yusuf et al. (2021) reported inclusion of 5% whole plant water hyacinth in a ration of African dwarf goats gave the highest (p < 0.05) weight gain, metabolic weight and dry matter intake. Antonio et al. (2025) also reported that dietary inclusion of fermented whole plant water hyacinth meal at levels up to 5% in Hy‐Line laying hens does not compromise growth performance, feed and protein utilisation efficiency. Dumaup and Ampode (2020) also reported that feed composition with 2.5% whole plant water hyacinth meal in starter and finisher phases showed a sufficient amount for the higher final body weight and the rest of the growth performance parameters such as weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio.

Some publications reported the higher values of inclusion level water hyacinth than calculated in this review, such as Fanta (2024), who reported 75% of concentrate portion of sheep and goat ration, which is about 22.5% of the total ration at 30% of concentrate level in a diet. Al‐Aboudi and Hamodi (2023) also reported higher inclusion level (12%) of water hyacinth leaf in broilers ration than that recommended in current review (i.e. 9.19%). Mekuriaw et al. (2107) report that 75% of concentrate or 29.53% of the total ration DM of Washera sheep is higher that the safe inclusion percentage of water hyacinth leaf recommended in current review (i.e. 13.5%). Tham and Udén (2013) also reported that ensiled water hyacinth shoot (i.e. petiole and leaf) at levels of at least 50% of the diet has the potential to support ME for reasonable live weight gains in cattle. These differences are due to the difference in the heavy metal content of water hyacinth used in the feed which in turn is a result of the water quality in which the water hyacinth grew. Their report is a result of a single feeding trial with a limited experimental period and from water hyacinth collected in a very specific location and are applicable in areas where the heavy metal content of the water body where water hyacinth growing was similar to the reporter's research area. However, the current report was based on the 95th percentile value of heavy metal content of water hyacinth samples collected all over the world until 2025, and it can be applicable in 95% of areas of the world, where water hyacinth heavy metal content was reported. The other limitation of WH as livestock feed is its high moisture content. The leaf, stem and whole plant WH contain 88.70%, 93.27% and 91.13%, respectively (Mulu et al. 2025). This makes its continuous removal from water bodies requires substantial effort, resources, and careful logistical planning, which can limit its feasibility in practice (Chala et al. 2026). However, as Ralinala et al. (2026) reported water hyacinth has demonstrated potential as a cost‐effective feed supplement for various ruminant species, including goats, sheep, cattle, and calves. In agreement with this, Valk (2015) also reported that it is economically feasible to process water hyacinth into animal feed, biogas and fertiliser. Similarly, Geoffery (2024) reported that high quality, affordable and sustainable tilapia diets can be made using WH.

Conclusion

Water hyacinth contains lead, iron, zinc, mercury, cadmium, copper and manganese. The amount greatly varied from place to place. Before the use of water hyacinth, especially that has grown in polluted water for animal feed, it is advisable to test for its heavy metal content and adjust the inclusion rate accordingly. However, if there is an inability to test the heavy metal contents of water hyacinth, use the inclusion level calculated based on the 95th percentiles of heavy metal content given in this review. Using inclusion level higher than recommended below without testing its heavy metal content will pose problem in many places.

Based on the heavy metal content of water hyacinth reported till 2025 and their maximum tolerable limit (NRC, 2005), there will be no effect on animals due to heavy metal intake if water hyacinth leaf included in cattle and sheep ration up to 13.5%. For swine ration and poultry, water hyacinth leaf can be included up to 18.4% and 9.19%, respectively, without posing a problem on animals due to the heavy metal intake. Water hyacinth shoot can be included in cattle, sheep and poultry ration up to 17.3%. For swine, it can be included up to 24.4% without posing a problem on animals due to heavy metals. The whole plant water hyacinth can safely be used in cattle and sheep rations up to 6.23%. For swine and poultry, the whole plant water hyacinth can be included up to 6.15% without posing a problem on animals due to heavy metals.

These inclusion levels are merely the results of a desk‐top exercise and should be supported by in vivo safety studies. Any deliberate usage of heavy metal contenting feed ingredients in animal feed shall be viewed with great suspicion and caution. A carcass produced from animals fed heavy metal containing feeds, like water hyacinth, should go through rigorous checks for their heavy metal status as per the safety standards of the respective country.
