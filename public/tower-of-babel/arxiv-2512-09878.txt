GFH-v2 Pipeline for Searches of Long-Transient Gravitational Waves from Newborn Magnetars
Sandhya Sajith Menon, Lorenzo Pierini, Pia Astone, Cristiano Palomba, Lorenzo Silvestri, Sabrina D’Antonio, Simone Dall’Osso, Francesco Safai Tehrani, Stefano Dal Pra, Gaetano Dinatale, Sergio Frasca, Dafne Guetta, Paola Leaci, Alessio Orlandi

Abstract
This paper presents an enhanced methodology for searching long transient gravitational waves associated with a newborn magnetar using a strongly improved version of the generalized Frequency Hough Transform algorithm, called GFH-v2. We describe the main developments introduced relative to the original implementation and outline the optimized parameter-space selection used in the search. We then compute the theoretical sensitivity of the method and compare it with an empirical sensitivity estimate obtained by injecting simulated signals into LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA O4a data. The updated framework achieves improved sensitivity and computational performance. These results provide a robust basis for future directed searches for long-transient gravitational-wave signals from core-collapse supernovae and other transient events in current and upcoming observing runs.



I
Introduction

Continuous gravitational waves (CWs) are long-duration, nearly monochromatic signals typically emitted by non-axisymmetric rotating neutron stars
27
. Although still undetected, these signals will provide a powerful probe of neutron star properties, including their internal structure and spin evolution
6
. Searches for CWs have so far mainly focused on isolated or binary neutron stars, either known from electromagnetic observations or searched in all-sky surveys. However, within the broad category of CWs lies a particularly intriguing subclass: long-transient gravitational waves (tCWs)
27
;
37
;
13
. These are signals with durations ranging from thousands of seconds to days, characterized by non-linear frequency evolution due to the intense rotational energy loss of the source.

tCWs represent a promising yet challenging frontier in gravitational wave (GW) observations. Among the potential sources of such signals are newborn magnetars: rapidly rotating, highly magnetized neutron stars formed in the aftermath of catastrophic stellar events such as core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) or binary neutron star (BNS) mergers
33
;
14
;
12
;
40
;
18
. In a newborn magnetar the gravitational wave emission is due to the asymmetry induced by the strong internal magnetic field, if it is not aligned with the star’s rotation axis.

Detecting tCWs offers a unique opportunity to probe extreme physics that is otherwise inaccessible: the equation of state (EoS) of ultra-dense matter, the internal magnetic field configuration, and the dynamics of the newly formed remnant. Additionally, this information could give us significant insight on the nature of the progenitor and the physical mechanisms during and after the merger or the core-collapse.

However, these signals are difficult to detect due to their weak amplitude and broad parameter space. Hierarchical search strategies, particularly those employing semi-coherent techniques, are essential to maintain sensitivity while keeping computational costs tractable. One such approach is the Generalized FrequencyHough (GFH) pipeline
31
, which was previously applied to the post-merger remnant of GW170817, though no signal was observed at the time, as the source distance was much larger than the potential reach of the search
3
.

Building on that foundation, in this paper we introduce an improved version of the method, which we call GFH-v2. It incorporates significant enhancements of the algorithm, resulting in higher sensitivity and better management of computational resources. As a consequence, it offers more promising detection prospects for the upcoming O5 science run of the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA
2
detectors and future third-generation detectors, like ET
29
and Cosmic Explorer
19
.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Sec.
II
we briefly describe magnetars, the relevant equations describing their rotational evolution and the main characteristics of the associated gravitational wave emission. Sec.
III
provides the basic concepts of the original GFH algorithm, while the new version, GFH-v2 and its methodological improvements are discussed in detail in Sec.
IV
. Within this section, we discuss in detail the astrophysically motivated search parameter space, including the choice of coherence time and observation-time window, as well as the technical enhancements to the analysis pipeline.

Sec.

, we compare the theoretical results with an experimental computation, obtained by injecting simulated signals into data from the first part of the fourth LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA observing run. This section also includes detailed discussions on data characteristics, the injection setup, candidate selection, and efficiency analysis.

Finally, Sec.
VII
presents conclusions and future prospects of this work. Additional technical details, including the derivation of the theoretical sensitivity formula and the procedure adopted for the selection of the critical ratio threshold, are provided in Appendices
A
and
B
, respectively.

Newborn Magnetars and associated GW signal

Newborn magnetars, a type of highly magnetized neutron star, are thought to form in the aftermath of CCSNe explosions or in BNS mergers. These compact objects are characterized by extremely strong surface magnetic fields, typically of the order

The Generalized Frequency Hough (GFH) transform is a semi-coherent, hierarchical search algorithm designed to detect long-transient gravitational wave signals whose frequency evolves non-linearly over time
31
. It extends the original Frequency Hough (FH) method
25
;
7
, which was primarily developed for detecting continuous wave signals with linearly evolving frequency.

The method starts from the
Short Fourier Transform Databases
(SFDB), which contain sequences of short Fourier transforms (SFTs) generated from calibrated strain data. The data are initially divided into overlapping segments of equal length, and each segment is transformed using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm. The SFDB spans the full frequency band, with FFTs typically covering timescales of the order of thousands of seconds. We first read the SFDB and extract the desired frequency band. The FFTs are then reprocessed and subdivided into shorter segments with the desired FFT length for the analysis.

The construction of the
Peakmap
proceeds by computing, for each of the
N
FFTs, the ratio between the periodogram (i.e., the squared modulus of the FFT) and the corresponding average spectral estimate
7
. The most significant peaks in this ratio, that is, local maxima whose normalized power exceeds a predefined threshold, are identified and collected to form a time–frequency map of selected peaks.

The original FH transform maps these peaks from the time–frequency plane of the detector into the frequency–spindown parameter space of the source, assuming a linear frequency evolution. In this case, the gravitational-wave frequency can be written as a Taylor expansion around the reference time
t_{0}
:

f(t)=f_{0}+\dot{f}(t-t_{0}),

(13)

where
f_{0}
is the source frequency at
t_{0}
, and
\dot{f}
is the first-order spindown parameter. In the FH framework, each peak
(t,f)
in the detector’s peakmap is mapped into a line in the
(f_{0},\dot{f})
source plane:

\dot{f}=-\frac{f_{0}}{t-t_{0}}+\frac{f}{t-t_{0}}.

(14)

Clusters of intersecting lines in this parameter space correspond to candidate CW signals.

However, this linear approximation breaks down for newborn magnetars or other transient sources with rapid frequency decay, where the frequency evolution instead follows a power law (see Eq.
7
).

The GFH transform overcomes this limitation by introducing a change of variables,

x=\frac{1}{f^{n-1}},\quad x_{0}=\frac{1}{f_{0}^{n-1}},

(15)

which linearizes the trajectory. In this
(t,x)
space, the frequency evolution becomes a straight line,

x(t)=x_{0}+(n-1)k(t-t_{0}).

(16)

Each peak
(t,x)
in the transformed peakmap is then mapped into a line in the
(x_{0},k)
space:

k=-\frac{x_{0}}{(n-1)(t-t_{0})}+\frac{x}{(n-1)(t-t_{0})}.

(17)

As in the FH, true signals appear as clusters of intersecting lines in this parameter space, but now the method remains efficient even for rapidly evolving sources with power-law frequency decay.

An example of the GFH workflow is shown in Fig.
2
. The upper panels display the input strain and its corresponding original
peakmap
, while the lower panels show the coordinate-transformed
peakmap
and the resulting Hough map where the clusters indicate potential signal candidates.

The Hough maps are built to cover a range of
(x_{0},k)
bins according to two discrete grids. To uniformly explore the parameter space, they are then partitioned into rectangles, where significant candidates are selected as excesses of counts and are then classified using a critical ratio statistic:

\mathrm{CR}=\frac{y-\mu}{\sigma},

(18)

where
y
is the number count in the outlier bin of the map, and
\mu
and
\sigma
are the mean value and standard deviation computed in the map, to estimate the noise floor. Candidates from different detectors are compared to identify coincident events, further reducing false alarms.

After GFH identifies candidates, we refine their parameters through a follow-up by making a heterodyne correction which is explained in detail in
31
. Using the estimated parameters (initial frequency
f_{0}
, spin-down
\dot{f}_{0}
, braking index
n
, and start time
t_{0}
), we heterodyne the original strain to correct the phase evolution, making the signal nearly monochromatic. This allows longer
T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
for better frequency resolution. A refined Hough transform then checks signal consistency and updates the CR. Only candidates with improved CR are kept for further analysis, enhancing sensitivity and reducing false alarms.

The GFH algorithm was applied to the post-merger analysis of GW170817
3
, demonstrating its capability to search for signals with significant spin-down, although no detection was made in that search.

The coherence time
T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
defines the duration of data segments over which we compute Fourier transforms. The choice of
T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
depends on the expected signal evolution. It must be short enough that the signal’s frequency drift, due to
\dot{f}_{\rm gw}
, remains within a single frequency bin during that time interval, but long enough to limit sensitivity loss.
Assuming the frequency drift is approximately linear over an FFT segment, a condition largely verified for the typical segment durations we employ, the frequency change during
T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
is:

\Delta f\simeq|\dot{f}_{\rm gw}|\,T_{\mathrm{FFT}}.

(19)

To keep the signal power within a single frequency bin of width
1/T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
, we require:

\Delta f\lesssim\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{FFT}}}.

(20)

Combining the two expressions gives:

|\dot{f}_{\rm gw}|\,T_{\mathrm{FFT}}\lesssim\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{FFT}}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{\mathrm{FFT}}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\dot{f}_{\rm gw}|}}.

(21)

The spindown rate
\dot{f}_{\rm gw}
is related to the ellipticity
\epsilon
through Eq. (
2
),

\dot{f}_{gw}=-\frac{32\pi^{4}G\epsilon^{2}If_{\rm gw}^{5}}{5c^{5}},

(22)

showing that higher ellipticity or frequency results in a faster spin evolution and hence a shorter coherence time. Figure
3
left panel illustrates the resulting dependence of
T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
on both
f_{0}
and
\epsilon

IV.2
Implementation of the GFH-v2 algorithm

The GFH-v2 algorithm significantly differentiates from the old GFH and, actually, is completely new for several aspects. One big difference concerns the pre-processing of the detectors’ data. Originally, the blocks of FFTs from the SFDB files, which are typically thousands of seconds long, were reprocessed and divided in shorter Fourier-transformed segments with typical length of tens of seconds and cut to the desired frequency range. Possible injections of simulated signals for validation purposes were done on that set of reduced FFTs, as well as the creation of the
peakmap
. In the GFH-v2, the blocks from the SFDB are directly combined together and inverse Fourier transformed to create a sub-sampled, complex reduced-analytic time series that covers the desired frequency range. This format is analogue to the Band-sampled data (BSD)
35
, widely used in CW searches, with the difference that the additional cleaning applied on BSD, which is specific for CW searches, is not applied here. In this way, all the manipulations, like injection of signals or generation of the
peakmap
, can be easily done exploiting the existing libraries and the code is easily accessible to the gravitational community.

The other big change concerns the implementation of the core of the GFH-v2 transform itself. In the original GFH, each peak from the
peakmap
was transformed into a line in the Hough space through an algorithm based on a double nested loop. The first loop spans between the different times in the
peakmap
, selecting the peaks happening at those times, while the second loop writes on the Hough map spanning through all the values of the
k
parameter according to a discrete grid. The main limitations of this algorithm are its computational efficiency and the fact the loops for different peaks could access the same memory locations for writing, limiting the possibility to parallelize the operation and to exploit modern computing architectures. In the GFH-v2 implementation, the loop over time is removed. The loop now runs over the grid in
k
: each value corresponds to a different slope of the signal in the linearized
peakmap
, according to Eq.
16
. For each
k
value, the whole
peakmap
is integrated along the corresponding slope by combining one linear transformation and the computation of an histogram along the grid of transformed frequencies
x_{0}
. The result of the histogram fills one column in the Hough map at the location corresponding to the selected
k
value. This new implementation of the transform is made by means of highly-optimized functions to calculate the histograms, so that the total computing time needed to perform the whole transform is significantly reduced with respect to the original implementation, up to one order of magnitude, depending on the input dataset. Moreover, each different
k
value of the grid is mapped in a different column in the Hough map, so the loop can be performed in parallel exploiting hyper-threading or multi-core CPUs and can be easily ported to Graphical Processing Units (GPUs)
38
.

V
Theoretical Sensitivity

Theoretical sensitivity provides a baseline to evaluate the performance of the GFH-v2 pipeline and to quantify the impact of detector noise and analysis parameters. For tCW searches from newborn magnetars, the standard CW sensitivity expression can be generalized to account for the rapidly evolving frequency. The resulting minimum detectable strain is derived in
34
:

h_{0,\mathrm{min}}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm min}f_{0}^{2}\\

(23)

where,

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\rm min}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{2.4308}}\frac{N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{1/4}}{\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{FFT}}}}\left(\frac{p_{0}(1-p_{0})}{p_{1}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\\
\times\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f_{i}^{4}(F_{+}A_{+}+F_{\times}A_{\times})^{2}_{i}}{S_{n}(f_{i})}\right)^{-1/2}\\
\times\sqrt{CR_{\mathrm{thr}}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(2\Gamma)},\end{split}

(24)

Here,
N_{\mathrm{eff}}=T_{\mathrm{obs}}/T_{\mathrm{FFT}}
is the number of effective segments, with interlaced FFTs leading to
n=2N_{\mathrm{eff}}
. The quantity
p_{0}
represent the probabilities of selecting a noise peak above a threshold
\theta
(typically
\theta=2.5
), given by

p_{0}=e^{-\theta}-e^{-2\theta}+\frac{1}{3}e^{-3\theta}

(25)

and

p_{1}=\theta\bigl(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\theta}+\frac{1}{6}e^{-3\theta}\bigr)+\frac{1}{4}e^{-2\theta}-\frac{1}{9}e^{-3\theta}

(26)

The sum in Eq. (
24
) extends over all
n
time segments where
f_{i}
denotes the instantaneous signal frequency
2
2

2

In previous works
34
;
31
, the quantity
\mathcal{F}_{i}
was introduced to denote the scaling of the signal amplitude with frequency. For newborn magnetars, the signal amplitude scales as
f_{i}^{2}
, so we directly replace
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{2}
with
f_{i}^{4}
here for clarity.
. The factors
F_{+}
and
F_{\times}
are the detector response functions, while
A_{+}
and
A_{\times}
are defined as
A_{+}=\frac{1+cos^{2}(\iota)}{2}
and
A_{\times}=cos(\iota)
. The noise power spectral density is denoted by
S_{n}
.

The factor
CR_{\mathrm{thr}}
defines the critical ratio threshold for candidate selection, balancing sensitivity and false-alarm probability. In our tests with simulated signals we have adopted
CR_{\mathrm{thr}}=7
, which provides a good compromise between both (see Appendix
B
). Finally,
\Gamma
denotes the chosen confidence level, and
\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}
is the inverse complementary error function.

The corresponding maximum distance can be is obtained by equating the gravitational-wave strain, Eq.
3
, to the sensitivity, Eq.
23
, obtaining

D_{\rm max}=\frac{4\pi^{2}G}{c^{4}}\frac{I\,\epsilon}{\mathcal{A}_{\rm min}},

(27)

Figure
7
shows the theoretical sensitivity of the GFH-v2 pipeline computed using these equations, with real O4a data and actual search parameters as inputs. The left panel shows the minimum detectable strain
h_{0,\mathrm{min}}
, while the right panel shows the corresponding maximum distance

as a function of the initial frequency

for a few representative ellipticities.

The trend of these plots can be understood from the underlying sensitivity equations Eq. (
23
)–(
27
). The curve for
h_{0,\rm min}
generally increases with frequency due to the
f_{0}^{2}
scaling in Eq. (
23
). However, the overall trend results from a combination of competing effects: higher ellipticity increases the signal amplitude but also shortens the optimal coherence time

(see Sec.

), which degrades the sensitivity. The net outcome of these opposing factors is the behavior observed in the plots.

VI
Empirical Sensitivity

The theoretical sensitivity derived in the previous section assumes data follow a Gaussian distribution. Although this assumption can be reasonable to a first approximation, typical detector data often show non-Gaussianity both in time and in frequency. It is thus important to estimate the search sensitivity in a more empirical way, which takes in full consideration the characteristics of the data.

To empirically estimate the sensitivity of the pipeline, we performed simulated signal injections into O4a data across representative values of frequency and ellipticity. For each injection set, we determined the minimum detectable strain amplitude (or equivalently, the maximum distance) as that strain such that 90
\%
of the signals were successfully recovered.

VI.3
Candidate Selection

The injected datasets were analyzed with GFH-v2, and candidates were selected from the resulting Hough maps. To systematically cover the parameter space, the Hough map was divided as follows: the
x_{0}
parameter was divided into a number of blocks determined by the number of injections and width of the spindown band covered by the signal. The
k

x_{0}
and
k
. Within each block the bin with the highest map count was selected as a candidate. A second candidate was chosen only if it was sufficiently far away from the first (in this case, by 6 bins in
x_{0}
) to reasonably select candidates of different origin.

Coincidence checks were performed across detectors and with respect to the injected parameters to identify common candidates. Following
7
, candidates are considered coincident if they are close within a defined distance in the
(x_{0},k)
parameter space, computed as

d_{\mathrm{coin}}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{x_{0,2}-x_{0,1}}{\delta x_{0}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{\delta k}\right)^{2}},

(28)

where
\delta x_{0}
and
\delta k
are the grid spacings in
x_{0}
and
k

Since only
x_{0}
and
k

d_{\mathrm{coin}}=2
. In previous implementations, additional parameters such as braking index and sky position were included in the search, requiring a higher threshold.

VI.4
Detection Efficiency and Sensitivity

Following the candidate selection, we determine the detection efficiency for each signal initial frequency, defined as the fraction of injected signals that are successfully recovered with
CR\geq CR_{\mathrm{thr}}
and coincident across the detectors.

To estimate the empirical maximum detectable distance,
D_{\mathrm{max}}
, we identify the point at which the detection efficiency curve crosses the 90% recovery level. For each frequency, injections are sorted by increasing distance and their corresponding efficiencies are evaluated. The first distance value at which the efficiency falls below 90% is taken as the upper bracketing point, while the immediately preceding distance value, that yields an efficiency above 90%, is taken as the lower bracketing point. The crossing point,
D_{\mathrm{max}}
, is then obtained by linear interpolation between these two values. This procedure provides a conservative estimate of
D_{\mathrm{max}}
. The associated uncertainty on
D_{\mathrm{max}}
is derived from the spacing between these neighboring distance points, defining asymmetric error bars.

Overall, the empirical sensitivity follows the theoretical trend, but noticeable deviations are observed in certain frequency bands. In some bands, the empirical
D_{\mathrm{max}}
falls below the theoretical prediction, typically in regions affected by strong spectral features, like instrumental lines. These disturbances, often present in particular low or mid frequency bands, raise the local noise level and reduce the recovery efficiency, leading to a lower measured sensitivity relative to the theoretical expectation.

Conversely at other frequencies, the empirical sensitivity exceeds the theoretical one, sometimes by factors of 1.5-2. This behavior is consistent with the expected trend, since the theoretical sensitivity curve is derived using conservative assumptions and an average noise spectral density computed over long durations. The empirical estimates, on the other hand, are obtained by using shorter durations specific to each frequency band, which may correspond to periods of relatively lower noise, resulting in higher sensitivity.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented an enhanced methodology for the detection of tCW signals using the updated GFH-v2 pipeline. This approach introduces astrophysically motivated parameter constraints, optimized choices of coherence and observation times, and computational improvements that significantly enhance both sensitivity and computational efficiency. Using real O4a LIGO data and simulated tCW signals describing newborn magnetar emission, we demonstrated the pipeline’s ability to recover signals over a broad parameter space and to produce empirical sensitivities consistent with theoretical expectations. These results establish GFH-v2 as a robust and efficient framework for directed searches for tCW signals in current and future observing runs.

The pipeline has already been applied in a real directed search targeting the nearby core-collapse supernova SN2023ixf, detected in May 2023. This was carried out using the approved LIGO engineering run (ER15) data segments. The source localization provided by electromagnetic observations enables a well-constrained directed search using the framework developed in this study. Results from that analysis will be presented in a forthcoming publication.

In future work, we plan to extend this study in several directions. First, we will relax the assumption that all signals start at the same time and test different start times to see how this affects detection efficiency. We also plan to include a wider range of magnetar spin-down models, considering different braking indices
21
and possible fallback accretion effects, to check how robust the method is under various physical conditions
15
;
36
;
13
. Additionally, we plan to implement an adaptive Hough approach, where the contribution of each time segment is weighted according to the detector noise level and the beam pattern, following the method described in Ref.
7
;
32
.

Moreover, a promising direction is to develop a hybrid pipeline that combines GFH-v2 with a machine-learning (ML) based tCW search, as described in Ref.
8
. In this framework, the ML pipeline can rapidly scan large portions of parameter space to generate candidate triggers, which are then followed up by GFH-v2 for a detailed and sensitive analysis. This approach could enable efficient all-sky searches for long transient signals, combining speed and sensitivity.

Finally, the GFH-v2 framework can also be applied to search for other long-duration sources, such as post-merger remnants, using data from future observing runs with the advanced LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA detectors.

Overall, the results presented here demonstrate that the GFH-v2 pipeline provides a sensitive, efficient, and astrophysically informed framework for long-transient gravitational-wave searches, representing an important step toward probing the physics of newly born magnetars and other rapidly evolving compact objects with gravitational waves.

Appendix A
Theoretical sensitivity equation

In this appendix, we give some details on the derivation of the sensitivity formula, Eq.
24
, directing the reader to
34
for more details.
The sensitivity computation consists of several steps and starts from two basic quantities: the probability of selecting a noise peak (local maxima above the threshold
\theta
) in the peakmap,
p_{0}=P(\theta;0)
, given by Eq.
25
, and the probability of selecting a peak when a signal with spectral amplitude
\lambda
(in units of the equalized spectra) is present,
p_{\lambda}=P(\theta;\lambda)
. Details of the original computation, for CW signals, can be found in
7
, while its extension to long-transient signals is given in
31
. Under the assumption of Gaussian noise, the probability of selecting a peak in the peakmap in presence of a signal with spectral amplitude
\lambda
is

p_{\lambda}=\int_{\theta}^{\infty}p(x;\lambda)\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{-x^{\prime}}dx^{\prime}\right)^{2}dx

(29)

where
x
is the value of the noise spectrum.
This equation corrects an error in
7
(eq. 19), where the inner integral had as argument
p(x;\lambda)
, instead of
p(x;0)
. By computing the integral of Eq.
29
, under the small signal approximation, we find

p_{\lambda}=p_{0}+p_{1}\lambda

(30)

where
p_{0}
is given by Eq.
25
and

p_{1}=\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\theta}+\frac{1}{6}e^{-3\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{4}e^{-2\theta}-\frac{1}{9}e^{-3\theta},

(31)

which corresponds to Eq.
26
.
The expression for the minimum detectable
\lambda
now reads (cfg. eq 66 in
7
)

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}=\sqrt{\frac{p_{0}(1-p_{0})}{N_{\mathrm{eff}}\times p_{1}^{2}}}\left(CR_{\mathrm{thr}}-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(2\Gamma)\right).

(32)

By following the same steps as in
7
, and considering the case of a signal with finite duration, we arrive at the expression for the theoretical sensitivity of the GFH-v2 algorithm, given in Eq.
24
. Note that Eq.
24
replaces eq. 34 of
31
, not only because of the difference in the expression for the coefficient
p_{1}
but also because in Eq.
24
the dependence on the detector radiation pattern is explicit, while in
31
an average over time (and the source parameters) is assumed. This is not correct when the signal duration is smaller than about one sidereal day, in which case no time average can be taken.

The critical ratio threshold,

A lower threshold increases the number of recovered signals but also raises the rate of false alarms, while a higher threshold reduces false alarms at the cost of missing weaker signals.

.

We found that values below
CR_{\mathrm{thr}}\approx 5
lead to a significant rise in false coincidences, while values above 7 begin to substantially reduce the recovery efficiency for weaker signals.

Based on these tests, we adopted
CR_{\mathrm{thr}}=7
as a compromise providing a stable false-alarm rate across the full frequency range while maintaining good detection efficiency.
