Microleakage of Class II resin composite restorations restored with conventional, bulk-fill, and short fiber-reinforced composites with or without polyethylene fiber reinforcement: a comparative in vitro study
Authors: Saeede Asdagh, Mahdi Rahbar, Sajede Hoseininezhad, Aziz Kamran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroleakage remains a major concern in Class II resin composite restorations and is associated with postoperative sensitivity, marginal discoloration, secondary caries, and restoration failure. Various strategies, including the use of bulk-fill composites and fiber reinforcement, have been proposed to reduce polymerization shrinkage stress and improve marginal sealing. Background

Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent oral diseases worldwide and continues to represent a major challenge in restorative dentistry [1]. Owing to their favorable esthetic properties, ability to bond to dental tissues, and conservative tooth preparation requirements, resin composite restorations have become the material of choice for the restoration of posterior teeth [2]. Advances in filler technology, adhesive systems, and monomer formulations have substantially improved the mechanical properties and clinical performance of these materials [3]. Nevertheless, microleakage remains a major concern, particularly in Class II restorations, and continues to compromise the long-term success of composite restorations [4].

Microleakage is defined as the penetration of bacteria, oral fluids, ions, and other substances through microscopic gaps at the tooth–restoration interface. It has been associated with postoperative sensitivity, marginal discoloration, secondary caries, pulpal irritation, and restoration failure [5]. The problem is particularly pronounced in Class II restorations because the gingival margin is frequently located in dentin or cementum, substrates that exhibit lower bonding effectiveness than enamel and are more susceptible to polymerization-induced stresses.

Polymerization shrinkage is considered one of the primary causes of microleakage in resin composite restorations. During polymerization, volumetric contraction of the resin matrix generates stresses at the bonded interface. In Class II cavities, the high configuration factor (C-factor) further increases these stresses and promotes gap formation, thereby facilitating dye and bacterial penetration. Previous laboratory studies have demonstrated that polymerization shrinkage remains a major contributor to microleakage despite improvements in contemporary adhesive systems [6, 7].

Several approaches have been proposed to reduce microleakage, including incremental placement techniques, low-modulus liners, modified curing protocols, and the development of novel restorative materials. Among these materials, bulk-fill resin composites have gained considerable attention because they can be placed in thicker increments, simplifying restorative procedures and reducing clinical chair time. These materials have been specifically designed to achieve greater depth of cure and lower polymerization shrinkage stress [8, 9]. However, evidence regarding their effectiveness in reducing microleakage remains inconsistent [10]. While some studies have reported microleakage levels comparable to those of conventional composites [11], others have demonstrated significantly greater dye penetration in bulk-fill restorations [12]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that the available evidence remains inconclusive and appears to depend on the material composition and experimental conditions used [10].

Fiber reinforcement has been proposed as another strategy to reduce microleakage in composite restorations. Polyethylene fibers may decrease polymerization-related stresses by occupying part of the composite volume and modifying stress distribution within the restoration. In addition, fiber reinforcement improves stress transfer and increases resistance to crack propagation, which may contribute to reduced interfacial leakage [13, 14]. Although several in vitro studies have reported reduced microleakage following fiber reinforcement, the findings remain inconsistent and appear to be influenced by the composite type, cavity configuration, and restorative technique employed [15].

More recently, short fiber-reinforced composites (SFRCs) have been introduced as a new category of restorative materials. These materials contain randomly distributed short glass fibers within a resin matrix, creating a three-dimensional reinforcing network. The incorporation of short fibers improves fracture toughness, inhibits crack propagation, and enhances the mechanical performance of restorations. Furthermore, it has been suggested that short fibers can absorb part of the stresses generated during polymerization, potentially reducing microleakage and improving resistance to interfacial gap formation [16]. Consequently, SFRCs have emerged as promising materials for the restoration of extensive posterior cavities.

Although bulk-fill composites, polyethylene fiber reinforcement, and SFRCs have each been investigated separately, studies directly comparing these approaches under identical Class II restorative conditions remain limited. Furthermore, it is unclear whether polyethylene fiber reinforcement can reduce the microleakage of conventional and bulk-fill composites to a level comparable with that achieved by SFRCs [4, 7]. Therefore, the present in vitro study was conducted to compare the effects of composite type and polyethylene fiber reinforcement on microleakage in Class II resin composite restorations.

Materials and methods

Study design and specimen selection

This randomized in vitro experimental study was conducted to evaluate the effect of composite type and polyethylene fiber reinforcement on the microleakage of Class II resin composite restorations. Forty extracted human premolars, removed for orthodontic or periodontal reasons, were collected. Teeth presenting cracks, fractures, previous restorations, developmental defects, or extensive caries were excluded. Soft tissue remnants and calculus deposits were removed using hand scalers, and the teeth were disinfected and stored in 0.1% thymol solution at room temperature until use. Before cavity preparation, all teeth were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove any residual disinfectant. Teeth extracted more than six months before the study were excluded [17].

Sample size and randomization

The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The calculation was based on a one-way analysis of variance (fixed effects, omnibus test) for comparison among five independent groups. Assuming a large effect size (f = 0.55) derived from previous in vitro studies evaluating microleakage of resin composite restorations, a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 40 specimens (8 specimens per group). Therefore, forty extracted human premolars were included in the study and randomly allocated to the five experimental groups (n = 8).

Following cavity preparation, all specimens were assigned a unique identification code and randomly allocated to the study groups using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence. Allocation was performed by an investigator not involved in restoration placement or microleakage assessment to minimize allocation bias.

The study groups were as follows CC (Conventional composite (without polyethylene fiber)), CPF (Conventional composite + polyethylene fiber), BC (Bulk-fill composite (without polyethylene fiber)), BFC (Bulk-fill composite + polyethylene fiber), SFRC (Short fiber-reinforced composite).

Cavity preparation

Standardized Class II cavities were prepared by a single operator using a #008 diamond bur (RDT, Lustenau, Austria) mounted on a high-speed handpiece under water cooling. The bur was replaced after every eight preparations. All cavities were prepared with standardized dimensions of 4 mm buccolingual width, 3 mm occlusogingival height, and 2 mm axial depth. Cavity dimensions were verified using a periodontal probe [18].

Adhesive protocol

All cavities were rinsed and gently dried. A 37% phosphoric acid etchant (Cobalt, Tehran, Iran) was applied to all cavity surfaces for 30 s, followed by rinsing with water spray for 15 s. A universal adhesive system (Universal ELS Bond; Saremco, Rebstein, Switzerland) was actively applied using a microbrush for 20 s and gently air-thinned for 5 s. Light curing was performed for 20 s using an LED curing unit (Woodpecker iLED, Guilin, China) with an irradiance of 1200 mW/cm².

Restorative procedures

Group 2: CPF

A 1-mm gingival layer of conventional composite was placed, followed by insertion of a polyethylene fiber strip (Ribbond, USA) measuring 4 mm × 2 mm. The fiber was adapted to the gingival floor and light-cured for 20 s. The remaining cavity was restored as described for Group 1.

Group 1: CC

A 1-mm gingival layer of conventional composite resin (A2; Saremco, Switzerland) was placed and light-cured for 20 s. The remaining cavity was restored using an oblique incremental technique with increments not exceeding 1.5 mm, each cured for 20 s.

Group 3: BC

The entire cavity was restored using a single increment of bulk-fill composite (Saremco, Switzerland) and light-cured for 20 s.

Group 4: BFC

A 1-mm gingival layer of bulk-fill composite was placed, followed by insertion of a 4 mm × 2 mm polyethylene fiber strip. The remainder of the cavity was restored with bulk-fill composite in a single increment and light-cured for 20 s.

Group 5: SFRC

A 1-mm layer of conventional composite was first placed to reconstruct the cavity wall and light-cured for 20 s. The cavity was subsequently restored using EverX Posterior (GC, Tokyo, Japan) in oblique increments of 1.5 mm, each cured for 20 s. Finally, a 1-mm occlusal capping layer of conventional composite was applied and light-cured.

Thermocycling and dye penetration procedure

Following restoration, specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h and finished using abrasive discs. Subsequently, all specimens underwent 500 thermocycles between 5 °C and 55 °C with a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 10 s. This thermocycling protocol was selected according to the recommendations of ISO/TS 11405:2015 and is widely accepted as an artificial aging method for simulating thermal stresses encountered by adhesive restorations during early clinical service. Thermocycling reproduces repeated thermal expansion and contraction at the tooth–restoration interface, thereby providing a clinically relevant assessment of marginal integrity [18].

The apical 2 mm of each root was resected and sealed with utility wax. All tooth surfaces were coated with two layers of nail varnish, leaving a 1-mm window around the gingival restoration margin. Specimens were immersed in 2% methylene blue solution for 24 h at 37 °C, rinsed, embedded in polyester resin, and sectioned longitudinally through the center of the restoration in a mesiodistal direction under continuous water irrigation.

A 2% methylene blue solution was selected because of its low molecular weight and high penetration ability, which enable sensitive detection of marginal gaps. Furthermore, methylene blue remains one of the most widely used tracer dyes in laboratory microleakage studies, facilitating comparison with previously published investigations [19].

Microleakage assessment

Microleakage was evaluated under a stereomicroscope (Imatt, Tehran, Iran) at ×10 magnification using the following ordinal scoring system: Score 0: No dye penetration, Score 1: Dye penetration into the outer half of the gingival floor, Score 2: Dye penetration into the inner half of the gingival floor, Score 3: Dye penetration extending to the coronal one-third of the axial wall, Score 4: Dye penetration extending to the coronal two-thirds of the axial wall, Score 5: Dye penetration extending to the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ) [18].

Blinding procedure

Microleakage assessment was performed by a trained and calibrated examiner who was blinded to group allocation. All specimens were coded before evaluation, and the allocation key was maintained by an independent investigator until completion of data collection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Because microleakage scores represented ordinal data, results were summarized using frequencies, percentages, medians, interquartile ranges (IQR), and mean ranks. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Distribution of microleakage scores

In the Conventional composite group, all specimens (100%) demonstrated dye penetration limited to the outer half of the gingival floor (score 1). In the CPF group, two specimens (25%) showed no dye penetration (score 0), while six specimens (75%) exhibited dye penetration limited to the outer half of the gingival floor. The Bulkfill group demonstrated the broadest distribution of microleakage scores. Dye penetration was observed in the outer half of the gingival floor in two specimens (25%), the inner half of the gingival floor in two specimens (25%), one-third of the axial wall in one specimen (12.5%), two-thirds of the axial wall in two specimens (25%), and up to the dentinoenamel junction (DEJ) in one specimen (12.5%). In the BFC group, three specimens (37.5%) exhibited dye penetration limited to the outer half of the gingival floor, two specimens (25%) to the inner half of the gingival floor, and three specimens (37.5%) to one-third of the axial wall. No specimen demonstrated dye penetration extending to two-thirds of the axial wall or the DEJ. In the SFRC group, three specimens (37.5%) showed no dye penetration, whereas five specimens (62.5%) exhibited penetration limited to the outer half of the gingival floor. No deeper dye penetration was observed in this group. The distribution of microleakage scores is summarized in Table 1, and representative stereomicroscopic images are presented in Fig. 1.

Comparison of microleakage among experimental groups

Microleakage scores differed significantly among the five experimental groups according to the Kruskal–Wallis test (H = 22.02, P < 0.001). The highest mean rank was observed in the Bulkfill group (31.31), followed by the BFC group (27.75). Lower mean ranks were observed in the Conventional (17.50), CPF (13.88), and SFRC (12.06) groups. Discussion

The present in vitro study evaluated the effect of composite type and fiber reinforcement on the microleakage of Class II resin composite restorations. Significant differences were observed among the experimental groups. The Bulkfill group demonstrated the highest microleakage scores, whereas the lowest values were observed in the SFRC group. Pairwise analysis revealed significantly greater microleakage in the Bulkfill group compared with the CPF and SFRC groups. In addition, the BFC group exhibited significantly higher microleakage than the SFRC group. Interestingly, no significant differences were detected between the Conventional and SFRC groups or between the Conventional and CPF groups. These findings indicate that the evaluated restorative approaches exhibited different abilities to maintain marginal sealing at the gingival margin of Class II restorations.

Microleakage remains one of the major causes of restorative failure in resin-based restorations. The penetration of bacteria, fluids, and their by-products through the tooth-restoration interface may contribute to postoperative sensitivity, marginal discoloration, recurrent caries, pulpal irritation, and ultimately restoration failure [7, 10]. This issue is particularly relevant in Class II restorations because the gingival margin is frequently located on dentin or cementum, where adhesion is less predictable than enamel bonding [20, 21].

One of the principal findings of the present study was the favorable performance of SFRC. More than one-third of the specimens in this group showed complete absence of dye penetration, and no specimen demonstrated deep microleakage extending toward the axial wall or DEJ. Although SFRC was not statistically different from Conventional or CPF restorations, it demonstrated significantly lower microleakage than BFC and numerically lower scores than all other groups. These findings are consistent with the study by Garoushi et al., who demonstrated that restorations incorporating a short fiber-reinforced composite base exhibited significantly lower microleakage than restorations restored with other composite base materials. Their results, together with the present findings, support the potential of SFRC to improve marginal sealing in posterior restorations [22].

The improved marginal performance of SFRC may be attributed to its unique composition. Unlike conventional composites, SFRC contains short glass fibers dispersed throughout the resin matrix. These fibers occupy part of the restorative volume and reduce the relative proportion of polymerizing resin, thereby potentially decreasing volumetric shrinkage and the stresses generated during polymerization [23]. In addition, the three-dimensional fiber network may contribute to a more favorable distribution of shrinkage stresses within the restoration. Previous investigations have similarly reported favorable mechanical behavior and improved performance of SFRC restorations in posterior teeth [24]. Although the present study did not directly evaluate fracture resistance or stress distribution, the observed reduction in microleakage is consistent with the proposed stress-modifying effect of fiber-reinforced materials.

In contrast, the Bulkfill group exhibited the highest microleakage scores and was the only group in which dye penetration extended to the deepest scoring categories. These findings are in agreement with studies reporting increased gingival microleakage or reduced marginal adaptation of certain high-viscosity Bulkfill materials in Class II restorations [11, 21]. Several factors may explain this observation. First, placement of a large composite increment results in polymerization contraction occurring throughout a greater volume of material. In cavities with a high configuration factor (C-factor), the cumulative contraction stress generated within a large increment may exceed the bond strength at the adhesive interface [25]. Second, adaptation of highly filled restorative materials to deep gingival margins may be less predictable, particularly in dentin-based margins where bonding conditions are inherently less favorable [26].

Nevertheless, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously. Contemporary Bulkfill composites have demonstrated acceptable clinical performance and survival rates in several clinical studies. Therefore, the increased microleakage observed under laboratory conditions does not necessarily translate into inferior long-term clinical outcomes. Differences in material formulation, placement technique, cavity design, and aging protocols may partially explain the variability among published studies.

Another important finding of this study was the beneficial effect of polyethylene fiber reinforcement. Incorporation of fiber was associated with reduced microleakage in both Conventional and Bulkfill restorations. Although the reduction was not statistically significant in all pairwise comparisons, the overall trend was consistent across both restorative systems. Notably, deep microleakage scores observed in the Bulkfill group were eliminated following fiber incorporation.

This observation is consistent with previous investigations that reported improved marginal adaptation after placement of polyethylene or glass fiber inserts beneath composite restorations [27]. The mechanisms by which polyethylene fiber reinforcement may reduce microleakage are multifactorial. The presence of fiber inserts decreases the volume of polymerizing resin adjacent to the gingival margin and may contribute to stress redistribution during polymerization. Furthermore, fibers can act as crack-stopping structures that limit crack propagation within the restorative material and at the tooth–restoration interface. These effects may collectively contribute to improved marginal integrity and reduced dye penetration. Recent laboratory investigations have reported that fiber reinforcement can significantly improve marginal adaptation and reduce microleakage in Class II composite restorations, supporting the findings of the present study [28].

Despite the favorable influence of fiber incorporation, fiber-reinforced Bulk-fill restorations (BFC) still demonstrated significantly greater microleakage than SFRC restorations. This finding suggests that the effect of a localized polyethylene fiber insert may differ from that of short fibers distributed throughout the restorative material. In SFRC, the fibers are uniformly dispersed within the resin matrix, which has been suggested to provide a more homogeneous reinforcement throughout the restoration. Previous experimental studies have proposed that this three-dimensional fiber distribution may contribute to more favorable stress distribution and may improve resistance to crack initiation and propagation compared with a single polyethylene fiber layer placed at the cavity base. However, neither stress distribution nor crack propagation was evaluated in the present study; therefore, these mechanisms should be considered only as possible explanations for the observed differences in microleakage rather than direct findings of the present investigation [29, 30].

From a clinical perspective, short fiber-reinforced composites may also offer practical advantages over polyethylene fiber reinforcement. Polyethylene fibers require additional clinical steps, including cutting, adaptation, positioning, and stabilization within the cavity, making the restorative procedure more technique-sensitive. In contrast, short fibers are pre-incorporated into the composite material and can be placed using conventional restorative techniques, thereby simplifying clinical handling and potentially reducing chairside time while maintaining favorable marginal adaptation [14].

Interestingly, although SFRC exhibited the lowest microleakage scores, no statistically significant difference was observed between SFRC and the Conventional composite group. This finding may be related to the relatively small sample size of the present study and the ordinal nature of microleakage scoring systems. Moreover, conventional incremental placement remains an effective strategy for reducing polymerization stress and may partially explain the comparable performance observed between these two groups. Similar observations have been reported in previous laboratory studies where differences in microleakage among contemporary restorative materials did not always reach statistical significance despite numerical trends favoring fiber-reinforced materials [6].

The present findings should also be interpreted within the limitations of in vitro microleakage studies. Dye penetration methods provide a useful and widely accepted model for evaluating marginal sealing ability; however, they cannot fully reproduce the complex oral environment. Factors such as occlusal loading, biofilm activity, enzymatic degradation, salivary contamination, and long-term hydrolytic aging may influence clinical performance differently from laboratory conditions. Therefore, although the present results suggest advantages for SFRC and fiber-reinforced restorations in terms of microleakage, extrapolation of these findings to long-term clinical outcomes should be performed cautiously. Further randomized clinical studies are required to confirm whether the observed laboratory benefits translate into improved restoration longevity [30].

Collectively, the present findings suggest that both composite formulation and fiber reinforcement may influence microleakage behavior in Class II restorations. Among the evaluated materials, SFRC exhibited the most favorable overall performance, whereas Bulkfill restorations demonstrated the highest susceptibility to dye penetration. Fiber reinforcement improved the performance of both conventional and Bulkfill composites, although the extent of improvement appeared to depend on the restorative material used.

Study limitations

Microleakage was evaluated using a dye penetration method, which provides an indirect assessment of interfacial leakage and may not completely reflect clinical behavior. In addition, only one commercial product from each restorative category was investigated, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other materials with different compositions. Furthermore, microleakage was assessed using an ordinal scoring system rather than a continuous quantitative measurement. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to clinical settings. Furthermore, the present study did not evaluate fracture resistance, polymerization shrinkage stress, or crack propagation. Therefore, the mechanisms proposed to explain the differences in microleakage should be interpreted as hypotheses supported by previous literature rather than direct findings of the present study.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, significant differences in microleakage were observed among the evaluated restorative approaches for Class II composite restorations. The SFRC group demonstrated the lowest microleakage scores, whereas the Bulkfill group showed the highest levels of dye penetration. Fiber reinforcement was associated with reduced microleakage in both conventional and Bulkfill composite restorations, although the magnitude of improvement varied among the tested materials.

The findings suggest that both the restorative material and the reinforcement approach may influence marginal sealing performance at dentin gingival margins. However, because the study was conducted under laboratory conditions, the clinical significance of these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further long-term laboratory investigations and well-designed clinical studies are required to determine whether the observed differences in microleakage translate into improved clinical outcomes and restoration longevity.
