Effectiveness of a behavioral weight management intervention by race, ethnicity, income, and education: a meta-analysis
Authors: Lorraine T. Dean, Shadiya L. Moss, Gary D. Foster, Alexandra M. Lee, John W. Apolzan, Amy A. Gorin, Stanley Heshka, John M. Jakicic, David G. Marrero, Patrick M. O’Neil, Sherry L. Pagoto, Ana M. Palacios, Kelly N. B. Palmer, Renee J. Rogers, Deborah F. Tate, Stephanie L. Fitzpatrick, Michelle I. Cardel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRacially and ethnically marginalized groups experience disproportionately high obesity rates, but are underrepresented in clinical trials of obesity treatments. Obesity treatments seem less effective for marginalized groups, but studies are characterized by small samples, clinic-based treatments, and little attention to socioeconomic position. We evaluated weight loss outcomes of a widely-available behavioral weight management program (Weight Watchers; WW) by race, ethnicity, income and education.MethodsWe performed an individual participant data meta-analysis of 11 WW intervention studies published from 2000-2025. T-tests compared weight loss between intervention and control participants within race, ethnicity, income and education subgroups. Among intervention participants, fixed effects multivariable regression estimated the odds of achieving a 5% weight loss across subgroups.ResultsHere we show that among 2972 participants (19% Non-Hispanic Black, 7% Hispanic/Latine; 30% <$50,000/y) and a mean (SD) weight of 96.2 (18.1) kg, WW interventions produce significantly greater weight loss than controls in all subgroups at 3, 6, and 12 months. Within the intervention group, Non-Hispanic Black participants are significantly less likely to experience a 5% weight loss than Non-Hispanic White participants at 3, 6, and 12 months, but there are no differences by income or education.ConclusionsA widely-available weight management program is more effective than controls for weight loss in all race, ethnicity, income, and education subgroups. However, the likelihood of clinically significant weight loss varies when comparing across racial and ethnic groups. These data can assist practitioners and patients when choosing evidence-based approaches to manage obesity among racially and ethnically marginalized groups.

Introduction

Obesity is a serious, prevalent, and refractory disease that confers medical, psychosocial, and economic consequences1,2. While the overall US prevalence of obesity is high (42%)3, nearly half of non-Hispanic (NH) Black/African American (49.6%) and Hispanic US adults (44.8%) have obesity, compared with 42.2% of non-Hispanic White US adults3. Despite the high rates of obesity and obesity-related illnesses in marginalized populations3, these groups remain under-represented in obesity treatment studies4.

While the data are limited, behavioral weight management programs have produced less weight loss in NH Black and Hispanic/Latine adults compared to NH Whites5,6, although few studies have compared weight loss between Hispanic/Latine and other marginalized groups7. Due to small sample sizes, nearly all studies have focused on weight losses within the active treatment groups, comparing across racial and ethnic groups. While these are useful, they do not assess the extent to which observed differences between intervention and control groups persist within racial and ethnic groups. It is also unclear how socioeconomic position (SEP), such as income or education, is related to weight loss outcomes8. SEP is rarely reported, but among studies reporting it, participants tended to be highly educated and have high incomes8.

Thus, overarching questions remain about how behavioral weight loss intervention outcomes, especially when compared to controls, varies by race, ethnicity, and SEP7–9. Previous systematic reviews have attempted to address some of these questions; however, heterogeneity across intervention delivery modes, components, and reported outcome measures have precluded clear and consistent conclusions4,8,10. Further, studies are challenged to report weight loss outcomes by race and ethnicity, or SEP, due to small sample sizes and high attrition rates4,9.

The purpose of this study was to examine two questions; (1) do the previously published overall intervention effects of a widely available behavioral weight management intervention relative to control groups persist within race, ethnicity, and SEP groups, and (2) within the intervention group, are there differences across race, ethnicity, or SEP groups in the likelihood of experiencing a 5% reduction in weight?

Given that previously reported data demonstrates larger effect sizes for those from more advantaged populations11,12, we hypothesize that the intervention effect would not persist for racially and ethnically marginalized or low SEP participants. We also hypothesize that, within the intervention group, the odds of experiencing a 5% weight loss would be lower for racially and ethnically marginalized or low SEP participants compared with NH White or high SEP participants, respectively. We find that the program produces greater weight loss for intervention participants relative to controls at 3, 6, and 12 months; however, within the intervention arm, it is less effective at achieving clinically significant (5%) weight loss at 3, 6, and 12 months for non-Hispanic Black than White intervention participants, with no differences by income and education.

Methods

Data sources and study selection

All publications that mentioned the WW program (n = 169) were assessed according to the following inclusion criteria: an experimental evaluation of WW behavioral program published after 2000 and exclusion criteria: entire sample outside United States, comparison group includes WW, less than 50 completed participants in intervention group, participant sample selected patients with a specific disease other than obesity or type II diabetes, primary or secondary outcome did not include change in weight at 3, 6, or 12 months, or data were not made available by author. The full screening process is detailed via a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). International samples were excluded as the experiences of non-White participants outside of the US are both historically and culturally different from US-based experiences of non-White populations. Eighty-seven publications did not meet the inclusion criteria, and sixty-six met exclusion criteria, with five additional being removed because they came from the same study dataset. Most of the studies were excluded because the WW intervention was mentioned but not directly evaluated (49.7%) (e.g., reviews, registry studies, or studies of experienced stigma among WW members) or the study was conducted outside the US (18.9%). Exposures

Intervention

The WW intervention was ideal for this examination of weight loss effectiveness by race, ethnicity, and SEP because: (1) WW interventions are effective13–20at equal or greater rates than other behavioral weight management interventions21,22; (2) WW programs are standardized to be similar across sites and studies, reducing concerns about intervention heterogeneity; (3) WW is cost-effective23–25 so it is more accessible than higher cost programs; (4) the treatments were delivered by WW staff rather than specialized providers in tertiary care centers yielding greater generalizability to services provided in community settings; (5) pooled demographic data from WW members allowed for a robust analysis by racial and ethnic groups, and socioeconomic background.

Eleven studies included a WW intervention as part of an RCT18–20,26–28 (n = 6) or a single-arm trial13–16,29 (n = 5). The WW interventions varied slightly across studies, but all included a weekly curriculum with specific behavioral goals. The content included a variety of topics, including self-monitoring, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention. Using a member’s age, height, weight, and sex to estimate resting energy expenditure, a daily points value was calculated, as well as a weekly points target to produce a 750 kcal/d deficit. The point value of a food or drink is based on calories and various macronutrients. The program also includes skill-building on physical activity, mindset, and sleep health.

Race and ethnicity

Race and ethnicity were self-reported, but not collected in consistent categories across all studies. Thus, we created a single harmonized 4-category race and ethnicity variable: non-Hispanic (NH) Black, Hispanic/Latine, member of Other Racial and Ethnic group (Asian American, Native American/Alaskan), and NH White (referent)30. In cases where respondents selected multiple races (n = 28), we assigned the race of the most underrepresented group. We prioritized ethnicity over race, in which case those identifying as Hispanic/Latine in addition to a racial group were re-classified only as Hispanic/Latine.

Income

Low income was defined as annual household income of <$50,000/y (referent) compared with ≥$50,000/y, a cutoff determined by the distribution of the harmonized data. We applied multiple imputation using chained equations to missing income data, specifying a logistic model.

Education

Education was categorized as having completed: a high school diploma/GED or less; some college, associate’s degree, trade or vocational training; a college degree; or a professional, master’s, or doctoral degree. We applied MICE to missing education data, specifying a multinomial logit model.

Outcomes

Two key outcomes were assessed at 3, 6, and 12 months from baseline: (1) percent body weight change, assessed continuously, in intervention versus control groups and (2) ≥5% weight reduction, a standard for clinically meaningful weight loss31, assessed as a binary outcome in the intervention group. Histograms and scatterplots were inspected to determine outliers of excessive weight gain or weight loss. Outliers were considered as greater than the equivalent of an absolute 10-lb weight change per month. Outlier weight datapoints (n = 16) were removed (three 3-month weights, seven 6-month weights, and six 12-month weights). Other data from these participants were retained.

Statistics and reproducibility

The availability of the original data from each study enabled an individual participant data (IPD) pooled meta-analysis (N = 2972). We used a one-step analysis approach32, which consisted of combining datasets from individual studies into a single harmonized dataset and producing overall effect estimates across all pooled studies. To assess weight loss for intervention compared to control participants of the same (within) racial, ethnic, income or education group, we conducted independent samples t-tests of the mean percent change in weight at 3-, 6-, and 12-month timepoints within racial, ethnic, income, and education subgroups. For this analysis, we used the full sample of all participants. Participants in single-arm studies were included in the intervention group.

To assess differential weight changes resulting from the intervention, comparing across race and ethnicity or income and education subgroups, we applied a fixed effects multivariable regression approach only to participants in the behavioral weight management intervention (RCT intervention group or single-arm trial participants). We used logistic regression to estimate the relative odds of achieving the binary outcome of weight loss of ≥5%.

All models were controlled for age, sex, and baseline weight, accounting for clustering by study. We conducted an intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis using data from all baseline participants and with missing 3-, 6-, or 12-month outcomes imputed based on the last value carried forward approach (including baseline-only inputs). In supplemental tables, we also report results of a completers analysis of only participants who had complete data for 3-, 6-, or 12-month outcomes (respectively). All analyses were conducted in Stata (release 15.1. StataCorp. 2020).

Discussion

This study is, to our knowledge, the largest analysis of a widely available, community-based, behavioral weight management intervention’s effectiveness by race, ethnicity, income, and education. There were three principal findings from this study. The first is that the behavioral intervention produced significantly greater weight loss at 3-, 6-, and 12-months compared to control groups for all race and ethnicity subgroups. This is one of the few studies to examine the intervention versus control effects within underrepresented groups. In the only other study of which we are aware, West et al. found that the Diabetes Prevention Program lifestyle intervention produced significantly larger percent weight loss than placebo and metformin arms within all race groups33. The current findings are similar to those and suggest that the behavioral intervention was more effective than a variety of controls (do-it-yourself, RD visits, standard nutritional guidance) among those most at risk for obesity and its associated co-morbidities. Our results make an additional contribution because they come from a widely available, empirically validated community-based program, wherein people of different racial and ethnic backgrounds have access to a treatment that is effective for various racial groups. This contrasts with interventions specifically developed for a more focused population, limiting their generalizability and scalability.

The second principal finding was that, within the intervention group, underrepresented groups were less likely to achieve 5% weight loss compared to NH Whites. Specifically, NH Black participants had a 58% lower likelihood of a 5% clinically significant weight loss at 3 months, a 56% lower likelihood at 6 months, and a 41% lower likelihood at 12 months. Additionally, Hispanic/Latine participants had a 46% lower likelihood of reaching 5% weight loss at 3 months, though there were no differences at 6 or 12 months.

The finding of less weight loss among racial/ethnic minorities is consistent with previous findings of studies with smaller samples of racially and ethnically marginalized persons within a particular intervention group34–37. Previous studies have found that behavioral weight management interventions yield less weight loss for Black women than White women4,11,12,35. A recent study found that non-Hispanic Black participants experienced 2–3% less weight loss than non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic participants within three different behavioral intervention groups, although the study was powered for subgroup analysis38. Behavioral weight management interventions are not unique in their lowered benefit to marginalized populations, as these patterns are seen for some pharmacological and surgical weight loss interventions as well39–42.

The precise reasons for the lowered weight loss, within the intervention group, among racially and ethnically marginalized participants are unclear, but could arise from a variety of factors. The first is that interventions may not be properly adapted to participants’ cultural and environmental context41,43 or the participant’s attitudes and values44–46. For example, previous research has found that demographic differences in weight loss outcomes were not significant after adjusting for program attendance or physical activity level36,47,48, which could be a reflection of interventions not appropriately recognizing structural barriers that disproportionately affect racial/ethnic minorities49. Second, marginalized populations in the US have differential exposures to environments that may make weight loss more difficult, including less access to healthful and affordable foods12, and greater exposure to unhealthful food advertising50–52. Further, racial discrimination is associated with reported loss of control, eating and overeating in Black women53, and predictive of changes in waist circumference54. Finally, pre-existing physiological factors could also partially explain differences in outcomes. Evidence suggests Black women have a lower resting energy expenditure (REE) before treatment55–57, as well as greater reductions in REE during weight loss than White women58, even after adjusting for weight and fat-free mass.

Regardless, it is important to avoid explanations that blame already marginalized groups or use stigmatizing attributions that aren’t rooted in data, like “motivation,” “commitment,” or “discipline.” They are both inaccurate and unhelpful. A more useful approach is to build upon the work of recent weight management interventions that have overcome lower benefits for NH Black43,59,60 and Hispanic/Latine10 participants. Successful programs have focused on retaining racial and ethnic participants using strategies, such as church-based interventions, individual sessions and family-centered programs11. Culturally-tailored behavioral weight management programs have had mixed results due to a lack of clear standards for what constitutes meaningful cultural tailoring61. Efficacious culturally-tailored programs have addressed culturally-relevant foods and real-world barriers, and engaged community members in intervention planning and recruitment61. In contrast, interventions that only made superficial adaptations (e.g., having photos of Black or Hispanic people on materials), employed race-concordant interventionists, or involved constituents only in the later implementation stages of behavioral weight management interventions were less efficacious61. In addition to leveraging any available qualitative data to improve interventions, future research should be grounded in methodologically rigorous qualitative data that directly capture the needs and perspectives of various racial and ethnic subgroups. The use of mixed methods approaches can be useful before, during and after an intervention. Given the advent of newer generation obesity medications, such as GLP-1s, future research should also focus on race and ethnicity differences for these weight-loss approaches.

The third principal finding is that there were no differences in weight loss by income or education groups. This was true whether looking at the intervention versus control effects or just within the intervention group. A recent study showed no income or education effects on weight loss, but the sample was predominantly low-income38. A meta-analysis that focused on behavioral weight management interventions in the UK also found no differences in outcomes by income or education62. One notable exception is a recent study of an online behavioral weight management intervention that reported a lower vs higher income predicted poorer weight loss outcomes. This relationship, however, was partially explained by perceived stress63. Findings from these studies and the current one might suggest that the success of behavioral weight management interventions is less related to resources available to participants of varying income and education level to participate, but may be related to the stressors that accompany low income, including long work hours and financial stress. This reinforces the need to address the contexts in which participants are making dietary and physical activity choices64.

This study has several strengths, such as the inclusion of studies that had both intervention and control groups. It is only the second study, to the best of our knowledge, that looked at group versus intervention effects by race and ethnicity. Other strengths included the availability of original data from each study that enabled an IPD pooled meta-analysis, a large number of NH Black (n = 566) and Hispanic/Latine (n = 199) participants and a widely available, standardized, cost-effective, and well-studied behavioral intervention. There were also several limitations. First, even with the pooling, some of the racial and ethnic groups had small sample sizes later in treatment, which may have precluded our ability to obtain statistical power to detect differences. Second, women were overrepresented in the sample, composing of 80% of the sample. This has been a persistent pattern that affects the vast majority of behavioral weight loss trials65. Third, factors other than the intervention itself may influence weight loss. The extent to which participants engaged in the intervention was not available across studies. As such, we used both the ITT analysis and completer analyses to test our aims. Finally, while our quantitative approach can help identify the patterns in the data, it cannot help us to explain why patterns occur. These considerations aside, this analysis is the largest body of evidence from clinical trials of a widely available behavioral weight management program and provides valuable insights into the extent to which such programs have value for a racially, ethnically, and economically diverse range of participants.

Conclusions

The findings that a widely available behavioral weight management intervention produced significantly greater weight losses than a variety of controls among all racial and ethnic groups provide useful information for practitioners and patients when making choices about evidence-based approaches to manage obesity among marginalized groups. It is also encouraging that neither income nor education has any impact on weight loss outcomes. Less encouraging is that NH Black participants were less likely than NH White participants to achieve a 5% weight loss. Integrating more culturally and contextually relevant components of interventions, while remaining scalable, may help enable weight management interventions to achieve greater body weight loss among marginalized racial and ethnic groups. Multilevel interventions that address the cultural, social, policy and environmental contexts, in addition to addressing weight loss-promoting behaviors, should be explored41.
