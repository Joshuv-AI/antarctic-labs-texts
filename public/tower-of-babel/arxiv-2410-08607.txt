Riemannian Gradient Descent Method to Joint Blind Super-Resolution and Demixing in ISAC Thanks: Corresponding author: Jinchi Chen
Zeyu Xiang 1 , Haifeng Wang 2 , Jiayi Lv 1 , Yujie Wang 1 , Yuxue Wang 1 , Yuxuan Ma 1 , Jinchi Chen 1

Abstract
Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) has emerged as a promising technology for next-generation wireless networks. In this work, we tackle an ill-posed parameter estimation problem within ISAC, formulating it as a joint blind super-resolution and demixing problem. Leveraging the low-rank structures of the vectorized Hankel matrices associated with the unknown parameters, we propose a Riemannian gradient descent (RGD) method. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that the proposed method achieves linear convergence to the target matrices under standard assumptions. Additionally, extensive numerical experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach.



I
Introduction

ISAC represents an innovative and emerging paradigm aimed at enhancing spectral efficiency by enabling the joint utilization of spectrum resources for both communication and sensing functionalities
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. By integrating these two functions, ISAC seeks to reduce spectrum congestion and improve the overall performance of wireless systems.

In practical implementations of ISAC, several significant challenges arise. One major challenge is that the transmitted waveform in passive
[
4
,
5
]
or multistatic
[
6
]
radar systems is often unknown to the receiver. This uncertainty complicates the ability of the receiver to effectively detect and process signals, leading to a decrease in system performance.

Another critical challenge in ISAC systems is the acquisition of accurate channel state information (CSI) in communication systems, which is inherently complex. For example, in Terahertz (THz) band communication systems
[
7
,
1
]
, the channel coherence time is extremely short, making it difficult to obtain reliable CSI using conventional pilot-based methods. The rapid variability of THz channels, combined with severe path loss and molecular absorption, further complicates the acquisition process.

In this paper, we consider a generalized scenario where both the radar and communication channels, along with their transmitted signals, are unknown to the common receiver. We formulate the task of estimating channel parameters and transmitted signals as a Joint Blind Super-resolution and Demixing (JBSD) problem. Specifically, we use a subspace lifting technique to leverage the low-dimensional structures inherent in the data matrices related to the channel parameters and transmitted signals. This allows us to frame the JBSD problem as a low-rank matrix demixing problem. To solve the low-rank matrix demixing problem, we propose a novel Riemannian gradient descent framework. This approach enables computationally efficient reconstruction, with exact recovery guarantees established under standard assumptions.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider a scenario involving
K
radar or communication users transmitting signals to a common receiver. The received signal at the receiver is modeled as a superposition of convolutions from
K
users, expressed mathematically as:

\displaystyle y(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r_{k}}d_{k,p}\delta(t-\tau_{k,p})\ast g_{k}(t),

where
r_{k}
represents the number of paths for the
k
-th user,
\tau_{k,p}\in[0,1)
denotes the delay,
d_{k,p}
is the complex-valued amplitude, and
g_{k}(t)
is the waveform corresponding to the
k
-th user. By taking the Fourier transform and sampling, we obtain for
j=0,1,\cdots,n
:

\displaystyle y[j]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}e^{-\imath 2\pi(j-1)\tau_{k,p}}\hat{g}_{k}[j],

(II.1)

where
\hat{g}_{k}
represents the Fourier transform of
g_{k}(t)
. Defining
\bm{g}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{k}[0]&\cdots&g_{k}[n-1]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the objective of the JBSD problem is to estimate the parameters
\{d_{k,p},\tau_{k,p}\}
as well as the unknown signals
\{\bm{g}_{k}\}
from the observations in the equation above.

Since the number of measurements is less than the number of unknowns, JBSD is an ill-posed problem. To address this challenge, we adopt a subspace assumption inspired by prior research
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Specifically, we assume that each signal
\bm{g}_{k}
lies in a low-dimensional subspace defined by a matrix
\bm{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times s_{k}}
, such that

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{k}=\bm{B}_{k}\bm{h}_{k},\quad p=1,\cdots,r.

where
\bm{h}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{s_{k}}
is an unknown coefficient vector. This assumption implies that the waveform
\bm{g}_{k}
can be represented as a linear combination of a redundant codebook matrix
\bm{B}_{k}
.

Under the subspace assumption, the measurements can be expressed as:

\displaystyle y[j]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\bm{b}_{k,j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{X}_{k,\natural}\right\rangle,

(II.2)

where
\bm{X}_{k,\natural}=\sum_{p=1}^{r_{k}}d_{k,p}\bm{h}_{k}\bm{a}_{\tau_{k,p}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the steering vector
\bm{a}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is defined by:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{-\imath 2\pi\tau}&\cdots&e^{-\imath 2\pi\tau(n-1)}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},

and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
represents the inner product, defined as
\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\right\rangle=\trace(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B})
. Without loss of generality, we assume
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{K}=r
and
s_{1}=\cdots=s_{K}=s
.
Let
{\cal A}_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a linear operator defined by:

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{k}(\bm{X})[j]=\left\langle\bm{b}_{k,j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{X}\right\rangle,

with the adjoint operator
{\cal A}^{\ast}_{k}
given by
{\cal A}^{\ast}_{k}(\bm{y})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{y}[j]\bm{b}_{j,k}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Then the measurements in can be expressed as a more compact form:

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}(\bm{X}_{k,\natural}).

(II.3)

The problem of JBSD can thus be formulated as the task of demixing a sequence of matrices
\{\bm{X}_{k}\}
from the superposed linear measurements. Once the data matrices are recovered, the delays
\{\tau_{k,p}\}
can be estimated using spatial smoothing MUSIC
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, and the parameters
\{d_{k,p},\bm{h}_{k}\}
can be recovered using an overparameterized linear system
[
16
]
. Thus the main objective of this work is to efficiently recover the data matrices
\{\bm{X}_{k,\natural}\}
from the measurement (
II.2
).

When
K=1
, JBSD reduces to the classical blind super-resolution problem. Convex and non-convex approaches have been successfully employed to solve this problem. In particular, the theoretical guarantees of convex methods, including atomic norm minimization
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
and nuclear norm minimization
[
12
]
, have been established. However, these convex optimization-based methods are computationally expensive. To overcome this issue, non-convex optimization-based methods, such as low-rank factorization
[
20
,
21
]
and low-rank manifold-based
[
22
]
approaches, have gained attention for their efficiency in leveraging the low-rank structures of the data matrices.

Given that the measurements are a superposition of multiple users’ data, JBSD presents a greater challenge compared to blind super-resolution. Consequently, theoretical guarantees and algorithms developed for blind super-resolution cannot be directly applied to JBSD. Recent research has focused on JBSD, with notable progress achieved through methods such as ANM (Atomic Norm Minimization)
[
8
,
23
,
24
]
and vectorized Hankel lifts
[
25
,
26
]
. However, compared to blind super-resolution, computationally efficient algorithms for JBSD remain limited. Therefore, developing fast and robust algorithms for JBSD is a critical area of interest. In this work, we propose a Riemannian gradient descent method for solving the aforementioned optimization problem efficiently.

III
Algorithms

Let
{\cal H}:\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{sn_{1}\times n_{2}}
denote the vectorized Hankel lift operator defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal H}(\bm{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{0}&\bm{x}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{x}_{n_{2}-1}\\
\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}&\cdots&\bm{x}_{n_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{x}_{n_{1}-1}&\bm{x}_{n_{1}}&\cdots&\bm{x}_{n-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{sn_{1}\times n_{2}},

where
\bm{x}_{j}
is the
(j+1)
-th column of
\bm{X}
with
j=0,1,\cdots,n-1
, and
n_{1}+n_{2}=n+1
. It has shown that
\rank({\cal H}(\bm{X}_{k,\natural}))=r

[
12
]
. Consequently, we consider the following non-convex optimization problem to recover the data matrices:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{X}_{k}}~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{D}\bm{y}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}{\cal D}(\bm{X}_{k})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}~\text{s.t.}~\rank({\cal H}(\bm{X}_{k}))=r.

(III.1)

Furthermore, we define the operator
{\cal D}:\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal D}(\bm{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{w_{0}}\bm{x}_{0}&\cdots&\sqrt{w_{n-1}}\bm{x}_{n-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
w_{i}=\#\{j+k=i,0\leq j\leq n_{1}-,0\leq k\leq n_{2}-1\}
. Let
\bm{D}={\cal D}(\bm{I}_{n})
and
{\cal G}={\cal H}{\cal D}^{-1}
. Denoting
\bm{Z}_{k}={\cal G}{\cal D}(\bm{X}_{k})
, the optimization (
III.1
) can be reformulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{Z}_{k}}~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{D}\bm{y}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}{\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{Z}_{k})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}},

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{k}\in{\cal M}_{k,r},~({\cal I}-{\cal G}{\cal G}^{\ast})(\bm{Z}_{k})=\bm{0},~k=1,\cdots,K.

where
{\cal M}_{k,r}
is the Riemannian manifold of all rank-
r
complex matrix, embedded with inner product, the second constraint guarantees that
\bm{Z}_{k}
has the vectorized Hankel structure. Define
\bm{Z}=(\bm{Z}_{1},\cdots,\bm{Z}_{K})
. Let
{\cal M}_{r}={\cal M}_{1,r}\times\cdots\times{\cal M}_{K,r}
be the product manifold. We also consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{Z}\in{\cal M}_{r}}f(\bm{Z}),

where

\displaystyle f(\bm{Z}):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}{\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{Z}_{k}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|({\cal I}-{\cal G}{\cal G}^{\ast})(\bm{Z}_{k})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2},

(III.2)

We employ the Riemannian Gradient Descent (RGD) method
[
27
]
for the problem (
III.2
), which is summarized in Algorithm
1
. The RGD algorithm generates a sequence of iterates using the following update rule:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{k,t+1}={\cal R}_{k}\left(\bm{Z}_{k,t}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\cal M}_{k,r}}f(\bm{Z}_{k,t})\right),

where
\eta_{t}
represents the step size,
\nabla_{{\cal M}_{k,r}}f(\bm{Z}_{k,t})
is the Riemannian gradient at
\bm{Z}_{k,t}
, and
{\cal R}_{k}(\cdot)
is the retraction operator. By adopting the canonical Riemannian metric and employing truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) as the retraction, the RGD method becomes:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{k,t+1}={\cal P}_{r}\left(\bm{Z}_{k,t}-\alpha_{t}{\cal P}_{T_{k,t}}(\bm{G}_{k,t})\right),

where
\bm{G}_{k,t}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{k,t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}{\cal G}{\cal A}_{k}^{\ast}{\cal A}_{\ell}{\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{Z}_{\ell,t}-\bm{Z}_{\ell,\natural})+({\cal I}-{\cal G}{\cal G}^{\ast})(\bm{Z}_{k,t}),

(III.3)

and
{\cal P}_{T_{k,t}}
represents the projection onto the tangent space of the manifold
{\cal M}_{k,r}
at
\bm{Z}_{k,t}
. Let
\bm{Z}_{k,t}=\bm{U}_{k,t}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,t}\bm{V}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}
be the compact SVD. The tangent space
T_{k,t}
of
{\cal M}_{k,r}
at
\bm{Z}_{k,t}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{k,t}=\{\bm{U}_{k,t}\bm{A}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}+\bm{B}_{k,t}\bm{V}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}:\bm{A}_{k,t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times r},\bm{B}_{k,t}\in\mathbb{C}^{sn_{1}\times r}\}.

The projection
{\cal P}_{T_{k,t}}(\bm{Y}_{k})
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{T_{k,t}}(\bm{Y}_{k})=\bm{U}_{k,t}\bm{U}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Y}_{k}+\bm{Y}_{k}\bm{V}_{k,t}\bm{V}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{U}_{k,t}\bm{U}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{Y}_{k}\bm{V}_{k,t}\bm{V}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}.

Indeed, the RGD algorithm can be regarded as a generalization of Fast Iterative Hard Thresholding (FIHT) in
[
28
,
29
,
22
]
to the JBSD problem. Moreover, the RGD method can be eﬃciently implemented, where the main computational complexity in each step is
{\cal O}(K(r^{2}sn+r^{3}+srn\log n))
.

IV
Theoretical Results

In this section, we present our primary result based on the following two assumptions.

Assumption IV.1

(
\mu_{0}
-incoherence)
.

Suppose that the columns
\{\bm{b}_{k,i}\}
of
\bm{B}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
for
k=1,\cdots,K
, are i.i.d sampled from the a distribution
F
, which satisfies the following conditions for
\bm{b}\sim F
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{b}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\bm{0},\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\mathsf{H}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\bm{I},\max_{1\leq p\leq s}|\bm{b}[p]|\leq\sqrt{\mu_{0}}.

Assumption IV.2

(
\mu_{1}
-incoherence)
.

Let
\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}=\bm{U}_{k,\natural}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}\bm{V}_{k,\natural}^{\mathsf{H}}
be the singular value decomposition of
\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}
, where
\bm{U}_{k,\natural}\in\mathbb{C}^{sn_{1}\times r},\bm{V}_{k,\natural}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times r}
. Let
\bm{U}_{k,\natural,j}=\bm{U}_{k,\natural}[js:(j+1)s-1,:]\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times r}
be the
j
-th block of
\bm{U}_{k,\natural}
for
j=0,\cdots,n_{1}-1
. Suppose that for all
k=1,\cdots,K
, the matrix
\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}
obeys the following conditions:

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq n_{1}-1}\left\|\bm{U}_{k,\natural,j}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}r}{n}\text{ and }\max_{0\leq\ell\leq n_{2}-1}\left\|\bm{e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{V}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}r}{n}

for some positive constant
\mu_{1}
.

We are now prepared to formally state our main result.

Theorem IV.1
.

Assume that Assumptions
IV.1
and
IV.2
hold. If the number of measurements satisfies
n\geq C_{\gamma}K^{2}s^{2}r^{2}\kappa^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}\mu_{1}\log^{2}(sn)
, then with probability at least
1-(sn)^{-\gamma}
, the iterations produced by Algorithm
1
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{Z}_{k}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{t}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{K\mu_{0}s(1+\varepsilon)}

(IV.1)

for
t=0,1,\cdots,T
, where
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{r}^{2}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})
and
\kappa=\frac{\max_{k}\sigma_{1}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}{\min_{k}\sigma_{r}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}
.

Remark IV.1
.

Theorem
IV.1
establishes that RGD converges to the target matrices at a linear rate. Furthermore, the convergence rate is independent of the condition number of the target matrix, underscoring the efficiency of the proposed method.

VI
Proof of Main Result

We will prove our main result by induction. Notice that Lemma V.7 in
[
16
]
guarantees that (
IV.1
) holds when
t=0
. Next we assume (
IV.1
) holds for the iterations
0,1,...,t
, and then prove it also holds for
t+1
.

Recall that
\bm{Z}_{t}
is a block diagonal matrix. A direct computation yields that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{Z}_{t+1}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{Z}_{t+1}-\bm{W}_{t}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}+\left\|\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

Moreover, one can express
\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}

\displaystyle={\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\left(\bm{Z}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\nabla f(\bm{Z}_{t})\right)-\bm{Z}_{\natural}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t}){\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\left(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha_{t}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}({\cal I}-{\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A})\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha_{t}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\left({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\right)(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})

\displaystyle\qquad-\alpha_{t}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}{\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\left({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\right)(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})

\displaystyle\qquad+({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}})\left(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right),

which implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\leq

\displaystyle(1-\alpha_{t})\left\|{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\left(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left\|({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}})\left(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}}_{:=I_{1}}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{t}\underbrace{\left\|{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}{\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\left({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\right)(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}}_{:=I_{2}}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{t}\underbrace{\left\|{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\left({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\right)(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}}_{:=I_{3}}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{t}\underbrace{\left\|{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}({\cal I}-{\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A})\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}}_{;=I_{4}}.

To this end, we bound these four terms, respectively.

•

Bounding of
I_{1}
.
A direct computation yields that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{k,t}})\left(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right)\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}\left\|\bm{Z}_{k,t}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k}\frac{\left\|\bm{Z}_{k,t}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}}{\sigma_{r}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{Z}_{k,t}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{K\mu_{0}s(1+\epsilon)}}\cdot\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

•

Bounding of
I_{2}
. A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}{\cal A}^{\ast}\right\|\cdot\left\|{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\right\|\cdot\left\|\left({\cal I}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\right)(\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{1+\epsilon}\cdot\sqrt{K\mu_{0}s}\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{K\mu_{0}s(1+\epsilon)}}\cdot\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\epsilon}{2}\left\|\bm{Z}_{t}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

•

Bounding of
I_{3}
. One has

\displaystyle I_{3}

Proof.

For any block diagonal matrix
\bm{Y}
such that
\left\|\bm{Y}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}=1
, one has

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}}(\bm{Y})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\|\left({\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}-{\cal P}_{{\cal S}}\right)(\bm{Y})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}(\bm{Y})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}({\cal I}-{\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A})\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}{\cal P}_{{\cal S}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+4\epsilon+16\epsilon

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the problem of simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing in ISAC, formulating it as a low-rank matrix demixing problem. We propose an RGD method to solve this problem and establish its sample complexity, along with a linear convergence guarantee. Notably, we demonstrate that the convergence rate is independent of the condition number of the target matrices. The empirical effectiveness of our algorithm is validated through extensive numerical experiments.
