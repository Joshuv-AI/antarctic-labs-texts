Annexin A2 associated with bevacizumab resistance and infiltrative escape in glioblastoma
Authors: Taketo Ezaki, Ryota Tamura, Yohei Yamamoto, Jun Takei, Akihiko Teshigawara, Kyoichi Tomoto, Yasuharu Akasaki, Yuichi Murayama, Masahiro Toda, Hikaru Sasaki, Keisuke Miyake, Toshihide Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical benefit of bevacizumab (Bev) in glioblastoma (GBM) is typically transient, as tumors develop adaptive resistance characterized by an infiltrative shift from angiogenesis dependent growth. This study investigated Annexin A2 (ANXA2), a regulator of both angiogenesis and invasion, as a potential biomarker of Bev resistance.MethodsWe analyzed 66 tissue-specimens from 33 GBM patients using quantitative real-time PCR and immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence. The cohort included 15 Bev-naïve cases and 18 neoadjuvant Bev (neoBev) cases. 33 refractory specimens-including unique paired samples from the same patients at different treatment phases (initial resection vs. refractory stage) were evaluated to correlate ANXA2 expression levels with progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and MRI recurrence patterns.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified high ANXA2 expression as a significant independent poor prognostic factor for both PFS and OS. In the neoBev group, patients with low ANXA2 mRNA expression levels demonstrated significantly superior survival outcome compared to those with high expression. While ANXA2 mRNA levels tended to increase at the refractory stage, a significant negative correlation was observed between the number of Bev cycles and ANXA2 expression levels. Histopathological analyses revealed intense ANXA2 expression in the tumor vasculature and stroma at the GBM leading edge, co-localization with the hypoxia marker hypoxia-inducible factor-1α.ConclusionsANXA2 may serve as a predictive biomarker of the adaptive transition from an angiogenic to infiltrative phenotype with Bev therapy. These findings suggest that ANXA2 serves as a predictive biomarker for Bev clinical benefit and may represent a candidate therapeutic target requiring further validation in GBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11060-026-05772-4.Keywords: Annexin A2, Neoadjuvant bevacizumab, Invasion

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most malignant type of brain tumor, characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis [1]. Bevacizumab (Bev), a monoclonal antibody targeting the potent angiogenic protein vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), is approved as a standard treatment for GBM, particularly at recurrence. However, phase 3 trials have shown that Bev does not significantly improve overall survival (OS) in primary GBM. Despite resulting in substantial tumor volume reduction, its therapeutic effects are transient [2, 3].

In Japan, Bev is uniquely approved for use with newly diagnosed GBM cases, a strategy rarely adopted elsewhere. This approval was based on clinical trials demonstrating that while Bev did not extend OS, it significantly improved PFS and maintained quality of life, leading Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare to prioritize these clinical benefits. We previously conducted comparative analyses of VEGF and other angiogenesis-related factors, tumor oxygenation, stemness, and immunoregulatory mechanisms using samples from patients naïve to, treated with, and refractory to Bev. Paired specimens from initial and salvage surgery and/or autopsy enabled exploration of resistance mechanisms [4–10].

While Bev can effectively suppress angiogenesis, it may paradoxically promote tumor invasion, limiting long-term efficacy. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP2 and MMP9, have been identified as prognostic indicators following Bev therapy, with elevated expression levels correlating with worse survival outcomes, suggesting a mechanistic link between anti-angiogenic treatment and invasive escape [11, 12]. Radiological studies have shown that recurrence patterns, specifically contrast-enhanced T1-weighted image with gadolinium contrast (T1-Gd) lesions versus T2 diffuse infiltration, are associated with different outcomes for progression-free survival (PFS) and OS [13].

Angiogenesis and invasion are considered complementary processes in GBM biology [14]. While initial Bev administration suppresses angiogenesis and tumor growth, prolonged exposure may trigger compensatory invasive mechanisms, representing a key limitation in sustaining Bev efficacy [15, 16]. Therefore, identifying molecules involved in both angiogenesis and invasion is crucial for understanding adaptive resistance to Bev. Molecules that have been investigated in this context include MMPs and Annexin A2 (ANXA2). However, reliable biomarkers for therapeutic stratification under Bev treatment remain elusive.

ANXA2 is frequently overexpressed and has been associated with increased invasiveness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and poor clinical outcomes in GBM [17, 18]. Functionally, ANXA2 facilitates extracellular matrix degradation by activating plasminogen and downstream MMP2 and MMP9, known contributors to GBM invasion [19, 20]. Given its dual role of upstream regulation in both angiogenesis and invasion, we hypothesized that ANXA2 is potentially a more reliable predictive biomarker than well-known invasion molecules, including MMP2, MMP9, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (TIMP-1), and plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1) [21, 22], and may underlie the observed adaptive resistance to Bev in GBM. Unlike MMP2, MMP9, TIMP-1, and PAI-1, which primarily reflect individual components of extracellular matrix remodeling, ANXA2 is positioned upstream of multiple invasion-related pathways and has also been implicated in angiogenesis, stemness, and hypoxia-associated signaling. Therefore, ANXA2 may provide broader biological insight into the adaptive transition between angiogenic and infiltrative phenotypes during Bev therapy.

In this study, we examined how GBM invasiveness via ANXA2 contributes to Bev resistance and is associated with clinical outcomes. Japan’s unique approval of Bev at initial GBM diagnosis enabled collection of neoadjuvant Bev-treated specimens (neoBev), which are unavailable elsewhere globally. To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze invasion markers using paired surgical samples from the same patients during both Bev-responsive and Bev-resistant phases.

Materials and methods

Patient eligibility and sample collection

The present study used 66 GBM tissues from 33 patients obtained from three different settings: 18 tumors were removed after neoBev administration (during Bev response; neoBev group), 15 were newly diagnosed GBM without any previous treatment (naïve Bev group), and 33 were recurrent tumors after Bev administration (refractory Bev group). The refractory Bev group included 22 specimens from autopsy and 11 recurrent tumors obtained from salvage surgery (Fig. 1). Patient selection was conducted in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria of a previous phase II study [8]. As paired specimens from the same patient, 29 naïve-refractory Bev specimens from 15 cases (including 11 autopsy specimens and four salvage surgery specimens) and 18 neoBev-refractory Bev specimens from 18 cases (including 11 autopsy specimens and seven salvage surgery specimens) were obtained. The baseline characteristics and treatment profiles of the patients are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1. Because part of this cohort was collected before the implementation of the World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, 5th edition (WHO CNS5) criteria, comprehensive molecular profiling, including Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status, histone H3 alterations, O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status, and other molecular markers, was not uniformly available for all patients.

Treatment protocol

All neoBev patients were treated with preoperative neoBev at 10 mg/kg on day 0. Surgical resection was performed 3 to 4 weeks after neoBev administration. Maintenance treatment with temozolomide (TMZ) began 4 weeks after the completion of radiotherapy at a starting dose of 150 mg/m2 for five consecutive days of a 28-day cycle. All newly diagnosed GBM patients (naïve Bev) were treated with a concomitant Stupp regimen and Bev at 10 mg/kg every two weeks after surgical resection [9, 10].

Study oversight

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of Keio University School of Medicine, Jikei University School of Medicine, and Kagawa University School of Medicine. The study also received Institutional Review Board approval (approval number N20050002 for Keio, JKI 018–052 for Jikei, and 2018KH046 for Kagawa) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples using the NucleoSpin totalRNA FFPE kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed immediately after extraction using a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo, Japan). Because of the fragmented nature of RNA derived from FFPE samples, RNA quality was not evaluated by RNA integrity number. Instead, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and beta-actin (ACTβ), commonly used housekeeping genes, were employed as internal controls to normalize gene expression levels in the qRT-PCR analysis. Next, cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio Inc.) with a TaKaRa PCR Thermal Cycler (Takara Bio Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

qRT-PCR was conducted using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K.) on an Applied Biosystems ViiA™ 7 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K.). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 2 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 minute. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the comparative cycle threshold (ΔCt) method. GAPDH and ACTβ were used as internal control genes to ensure robust normalization. Gene-specific primers for ANXA2 were synthesized by Eurofins Genomics K.K. (Tokyo, Japan) [23] (Supplementary Table S2). The relative expression level of ANXA2 was calculated using the 2−ΔCt method.

Log-transformed expression values were used for all statistical analyses. The arithmetic mean of the GAPDH and ACTβ Ct values was used for normalization. ANXA2 expression levels were dichotomized into high and low groups relative to the median value.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

To assess changes in ANXA2 protein expression levels, 3-μm FFPE tissue sections were analyzed. Procedures were followed according to the manufacturers’ suggested protocols. Briefly, antigen retrieval was performed by a microwave method in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) [9]. After blocking with 2.5% normal horse serum (ImmPress Detection Systems, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) for 60 minutes at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-ANXA2 primary antibody (1:200, sc-28385, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA). The tumor core and leading-edge area were selected under high-power observational fields at 400× magnification. All experiments were assessed by three neurosurgeons and a neuropathologist [24]. The primary purpose of IHC analysis was to evaluate the spatial distribution of ANXA2 within the tumor core and leading-edge regions. Therefore, a formal quantitative scoring system was not applied.

Triple-label IF was performed to evaluate the spatial distribution of ANXA2, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α, and CD133. Briefly, the sections were blocked as described above, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ANXA2, HIF-1α (1:50, AF1935, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and CD133 (1:50, 18,470–1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). Signals were visualized using secondary antibodies (ImmPRESS HRP KIT Anti-MOUSE IgG for ANXA2, Anti-GOAT IgG for HIF-1α, Anti-RABBIT for IgG, Vector, CA, USA) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (red), 488 (green), and 647 (far-red/magenta), respectively. DAPI was applied for nuclear counterstaining. To minimize endogenous tissue autofluorescence, the sections were treated with the Vector TrueView Autofluorescence Quenching Kit (SP-8500, Vector Laboratories) for 5 minutes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Confocal images were captured with sequential laser scanning (405/488/555/647 nm) to eliminate spectral crosstalk.

Recurrence magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) pattern and modified response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) 2.0

All patients were followed up regularly with MRI. The recurrence MRI pattern was classified into three settings: cT1-flare up, T2 diffuse, and T2 circumscribed [9, 13, 25]. The reduction rates (RR) of T1 and T2/FLAIR were calculated and evaluated by neurosurgeons. The modified RANO (mRANO) 2.0 criteria were applied to assess radiological response in GBM patients, incorporating both contrast-enhancing (T1-Gd) and non-enhancing (FLAIR) MRI features, alongside clinical and steroidal parameters [26]. In this study, the clinical status and steroid dosage changes were assumed to be stable. The classification in the neoBev group is shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analyses

Baseline patient characteristics were compared using the Mann–Whitney U, Fisher’s exact, or χ2 test. Survival analyses used multivariable Cox models adjusting for age, number of TMZ and Bev cycles, extent of resection, sex, PFS, and OS.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to statistically compare the ANXA2 expression levels in paired samples from the same patients. The correlation between the number of Bev administrations and ANXA2 expression levels was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Patients were stratified into three subgroups using the number of Bev administrations: 1–10, 11–20, and ≥21.

The recurrence MRI pattern and gene expression levels in the naïve Bev and neoBev groups were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were performed to estimate PFS, OS, and RR, with differences evaluated using the log-rank test. Patients with missing survival data (NA) were not included in the corresponding statistical analyses. To identify independent prognostic factors, multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Factors that showed clinical significance or differences in baseline characteristics, such as age and the number of TMZ cycles, were included as covariates in the model. All statistical analyses and graph creation were performed using GraphPad Prism 11 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). For exploratory subgroup survival analyses, p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study was not randomized.

Results

Patient characteristics

The patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that neoBev was independently associated with longer PFS (hazard ratio (HR): 0.33, p = 0.010), but not OS (Supplementary Table S4). Independent factors for OS included male sex (p = 0.025) and longer TMZ exposure (p = 0.031). Notably, high ANXA2 mRNA expression levels remained a robust independent prognostic factor for both shorter PFS (HR: 2.85, p = 0.028) and shorter OS (HR: 3.12, p = 0.015) (Supplementary Table S4).

Comparative analyses using paired samples

To validate the ANXA2 mRNA expression level results, paired samples obtained from naïve-refractory Bev and neoBev-refractory Bev cases were compared. No significant differences in ANXA2 mRNA expression levels were observed (p = 0.244 and 0.205, respectively), although both comparisons showed a trend toward increased expression levels in the refractory setting (Fig. 2A).

Dose-dependent ANXA2mRNA expression changes with Bev treatment

Next, we performed linear regression analyses between the log-transformed ANXA2 mRNA expression levels and total number of Bev treatment cycles. Rather than separating the data into naïve and neoBev groups, all samples were analyzed as a single cohort. Notably, a significant negative correlation was found, with ANXA2 mRNA expression levels decreasing with increasing Bev exposure (r = −0.456, p = 0.009) (Fig. 2B).

To assess the impact of cumulative Bev administrations on the ANXA2 mRNA expression profiles, we stratified the patients by treatment cycle count into three groups (1–10, 11–20, ≥21 cycles). Then, log-transformed ANXA2 mRNA expression levels were observed when comparing the three groups, with expression significantly decreased in the 11–20 dose group compared with the ≤10 dose group (p = 0.017). Additionally, patients receiving more than 20 bevacizumab doses maintained lower ANXA2 mRNA expression levels compared with those receiving fewer doses (Fig. 2C). To further explore the biological relevance of ANXA2, publicly available TCGA GBM RNA-seq data were analyzed. ANXA2 expression demonstrated weak but significant positive correlations with MMP9 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.23, p < 0.01) and HIF1A (HIF-1α) (ρ = 0.21, p = 0.01), whereas no significant correlation was observed with MMP2 (ρ = − 0.09, p = 0.25) (Supplementary Fig. S3).

IHC and IF analyses

ANXA2 protein expression levels in the tumor core and leading-edge area were evaluated by IHC staining of the tumor stroma. ANXA2 showed cytoplasmic expression in neoplastic glial cells and was also detected within the stromal compartment in the tumor core, with tumor vasculatures demonstrating prominent cytoplasmic ANXA2 expression at the leading edge (Fig. 3A). Only a small subset of reactive astrocytes exhibited positive ANXA2 IHC staining. Representative single channel IF images of DAPI (nuclei; blue), HIF-1α (nuclear/cytoplasmic; green), ANXA2 (cytoplasmic with additional stromal signal; red), and CD133 (membranous/punctate; magenta) staining are shown, along with merged images from the tumor core and leading edge (Fig. 3B). Neither the IHC nor IF assays revealed notable differences in HIF-1α, ANXA2, and CD133 protein staining patterns between the naïve Bev and neoBev groups. However, HIF-1α protein expression was almost absent at the leading-edge area.

Molecular profiles vary across recurrence MRI patterns and mRANO 2.0 analysis

Within each MRI recurrence patterns (cT1-flare up, T2 circumscribed, and T2 diffuse), ANXA2 expression did not differ significantly between the naïve and neoBev groups (p = 0.911 and p = 0.882, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S1A). A total of 18 patients in the neoBev group were evaluated using the mRANO 2.0 criteria: four patients were classified as partial response, 13 as stable disease, and one as progressive disease (Supplementary Table S3). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no statistically significant differences in PFS or OS among the three response groups (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Survival outcomes and ANXA2 expression patterns

Next, ANXA2 mRNA expression levels were dichotomized relative to the median value. No statistically significant difference in PFS was observed between the high- and low-ANXA2 expression groups (6.5 months vs. 7.5 months in naïve Bev and 6.7 months vs. 9.9 months in neoBev, respectively). However, OS was statistically significantly longer in the low-ANXA2 expression group (18.0 months vs. 21.8 months in naïve Bev and 10.9 months vs. 19.9 months in neoBev; p = 0.003) (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Table S5). A subgroup analysis of PFS revealed that although no significant difference was observed between the high-ANXA2 expression neoBev and naïve Bev subgroups, the naïve Bev high-ANXA2 expression group showed a trend toward longer PFS, with a subset of patients demonstrating prolonged PFS beyond the median. In contrast, among patients in the low-ANXA2 expression group, those treated with neoBev demonstrated significantly longer PFS compared with those in the naïve Bev group (p = 0.006). Furthermore, within the neoBev cohort, low ANXA2 expression was associated with significantly prolonged PFS compared with high expression (p = 0.034) (Fig. 4A). For OS, the neoBev high-ANXA2 expression group had significantly worse OS than the naïve Bev high-ANXA2 expression group (p = 0.029). In contrast, no significant difference in OS was observed between the low-ANXA2 expression groups in the naïve Bev and neoBev patient groups. Within the neoBev cohort, patients with low ANXA2 expression achieved significantly longer OS compared with those with high expression (p = 0.004). No significant difference in OS was observed between the naïve Bev high-ANXA2 expression group and neoBev low-ANXA2 expression group (Fig. 4B). Consistent with these findings, pairwise comparisons of HRs across the treatment and expression subgroups further clarified these prognostic differences (Supplementary Table S6). Pairwise hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals are presented in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7. In a comprehensive analysis using the neoBev low-ANXA2 expression group as a benchmark, the neoBev high-ANXA2 expression group exhibited a markedly elevated risk of death (OS HR: 2.701, 95% confidence interval: 0.953–7.660, p = 0.061), whereas the risk profile of the naïve Bev group was less pronounced relative to that of neoBev low-ANXA2 expression cohort (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This study provides a globally unique perspective on GBM biology with Bev treatment, enabled by the exclusive availability of neoBev-treated specimens in Japan. Our findings provide novel insights into the molecular dynamics induced by Bev therapy.

Despite unfavorable biases in the neoBev cohort, such as older age and reduced treatment exposure, neoBev remained independently associated with improved PFS, supporting its biological and clinical contribution. Notably, while Bev treatment generally downregulates ANXA2 expression patterns, high expression levels persisted in a subset of the neoBev group, which exhibited significantly shorter PFS and OS. These results suggest that ANXA2 not only serves as a prognostic factor, but also as a key molecular indicator of Bev-resistant invasive escape.

ANXA2 regulates plasmin generation and activates downstream MMPs, such as MMP2 and MMP9, to promote angiogenesis and extracellular matrix degradation [27]. With Bev therapy, GBM may shift from angiogenesis-dependent growth toward an invasion-driven phenotype [28], a transition in which the ANXA2-plasmin-MMP axis is likely involved [14]. Its roles in neoangiogenesis and matrix remodeling further support this adaptive mechanism [29–33]. Furthermore, ANXA2 is directly regulated by HIF-1α through a hypoxia-responsive element [34]. Consistent with this biological framework, analysis of the TCGA GBM RNA-seq dataset demonstrated significant positive correlations between ANXA2 and HIF1A as well as MMP9 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Although no significant correlation was observed with MMP2, this discrepancy may reflect the biological heterogeneity of GBM and the limitations of bulk transcriptomic analyses compared with longitudinal and spatially resolved tissue-based investigations. It can also stabilize HIF-1α, creating a positive feedback loop that supports tumor progression [33]. ANXA2 is also known to activate signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, which promotes the EMT and adaptive resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy. The ANXA2–STAT3-oncostatin M receptor (OSMR) axis has been shown to drive mesenchymal phenotypic changes in GBM [35]. Given that the phenotypic balance between angiogenesis and invasion might determine the therapeutic efficacy of Bev [27, 33, 35], the present study suggested that regulation of ANXA2 and its related signaling axes, including HIF-1α and STAT3-OSMR, should be given attention.

The dynamic changes in the angiogenic and invasive phenotypes of GBM during Bev therapy are shown in Fig. 5. ANXA2 plays a potentially pivotal role in regulating the angiogenesis/invasion dependency for tumor growth and Bev therapeutic efficacy in this cancer. In addition, ANXA2 contributes to glioma stemness and resistance to radiotherapy, acting as a important molecular indicator of invasive adaptation under anti-VEGF treatment. Specifically, recent evidence indicates that lactylation-driven, ubiquitin-specific protease 4 mediated stabilization of ANXA2 promotes the maintenance and radioresistance of glioblastoma stem cells, thereby reinforcing the tumor’s survival scaffold [21].

While our paired analysis showed an overall trend of upregulated ANXA2 expression levels at recurrence, we observed a dose-dependent inverse correlation between ANXA2 expression levels and the number of Bev cycles. This suggested that maintaining low ANXA2 expression levels, thereby avoiding HIF-1α/ANXA2 axis activation, reflects higher Bev sensitivity. Thus, Bev-induced hypoxia activates HIF1-α, which directly upregulates ANXA2 to drive an infiltrative phenotype, thereby dictating patient survival outcomes. Interestingly, high ANXA2 expression patterns may counteract the benefits of neoBev. Importantly, ANXA2 remained an independent prognostic factor after adjustment for sex and TMZ exposure, indicating that its prognostic significance was not solely driven by these covariates. An apparent paradox in our findings is that ANXA2 expression levels showed a negative correlation with cumulation of Bev, whereas persistently high ANXA2 expression in the neoBev cohort was associated with poor survival outcomes. Several biological explanations may account for this observation. First, the inverse correlation may reflect a form of survivor bias, whereby patients who derive sustained benefit from Bev receive a greater number of treatment cycles and maintain relatively low ANXA2 expression levels. In contrast, a subset of tumors may exhibit intrinsically high ANXA2 activity despite Bev exposure, representing a biologically aggressive phenotype with persistent invasive potential. Second, Bev -induced hypoxia may transiently activate the HIF-1α/ANXA2 axis in selected tumors. Although the overall population trend demonstrates ANXA2 suppression with prolonged therapy, tumors that fail to downregulate ANXA2 at Bev failure may be predisposed to infiltrative escape and early treatment resistance. Therefore, the absolute ANXA2 level after Bev exposure, rather than the overall population trend, may better reflect biological resistance and clinical outcome. Furthermore, GBM exhibits substantial spatial and microenvironmental heterogeneity. Although neoBev generally promotes normalization of the TME and reduces hypoxia in responding tumors, hypoxic niches may persist within selected regions. Therefore, ANXA2 expression may vary not only between patients but also across distinct histological regions within the same tumor. Such inter-patient and intra-tumoral heterogeneity may explain why a subset of neoBev-treated tumors retains elevated ANXA2 expression despite overall trends toward ANXA2 suppression. Crucially, our data demonstrated that neoBev significantly improved PFS only in the low-ANXA2 expression subgroup. This observation was further supported by our multivariate analyses (Supplementary Tables S4, S6, and S7). Pairwise comparisons and exploratory subgroup analyses using the neoBev low-ANXA2 expression cohort as a benchmark suggested a trend toward poorer survival in patients with high ANXA2 expression, although these associations did not retain statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). These findings suggest that low ANXA2 expression may be required for neoBev to provide optimal clinical benefit by maintaining anti-angiogenic sensitivity. These findings support implementing a clinical strategy of ANXA2-guided neoBev, where patients identified via biopsy as having low tumor ANXA2 expression levels would benefit most.

The radiological response assessed by mRANO 2.0 did not significantly correlate with survival, suggesting that morphology-based imaging assessment alone may not fully reflect the underlying biological aggressiveness associated with Bev therapy. In particular, ANXA2-driven invasive adaptation may not be fully captured by conventional imaging criteria for infiltrative escape.

In addition, the ANXA2 expression patterns did not significantly differ across MRI recurrence patterns in either the naïve Bev or neoBev group. This apparent discrepancy may reflect a dissociation between radiological and pathological features. T1-Gd and FLAIR abnormalities are influenced by multiple biological factors, including vascular density, vascular permeability, blood-brain barrier integrity, tumor infiltration, and edema. Therefore, ANXA2-associated invasive biology may not necessarily correspond to conventional MRI recurrence patterns, particularly during Bev treatment.

Although greater lesion shrinkage was associated with longer PFS, previous reports have demonstrated that anti-angiogenic therapy can induce heterogeneous recurrence patterns, including diffuse T2/FLAIR progression reflecting invasive escape [9, 13, 25]. In this context, ANXA2, through its regulation of the plasmin-MMP axis and extracellular matrix remodeling, may serve as a complementary molecular indicator of invasion-driven adaptation beyond conventional radiographic response criteria.

This retrospective study has several limitations, including residual confounding factors, the relatively small sample size, incomplete molecular profiling according to WHO CNS5 criteria, and the absence of functional validation experiments. In addition, the IHC analyses were primarily qualitative and did not include formal interobserver reproducibility testing, endothelial marker validation, or quantitative spatial analyses. Therefore, the observed neoBev benefit should be interpreted primarily in terms of PFS, with OS requiring further validation. Additionally, the limited number of salvage surgical specimens may have reduced the statistical power of this work. These issues should be addressed in future studies.

In conclusion, dynamic changes in ANXA2 expression levels during Bev treatment can significantly influence patient outcomes. Reflecting Bev-induced changes, ANXA2 is associated with both angiogenesis and tumor invasion. These results identify ANXA2 as a versatile molecular indicator, serving as both a prognostic factor and a predictive biomarker for neoBev therapy.
