Re-irradiation in oligorecurrence and oligometastatic cervical cancer in modern radiotherapy era
Authors: Wiwatchai Sittiwong, Tissana Prasartseree, Pongpop Tuntapakul, Wajana Thaweerat, Nantakan Apiwarodom, Yaowalak Chansilpa, Pittaya Dankulchai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent cervical cancer remains a therapeutic challenge despite advances in primary treatment. The emerging paradigm of oligorecurrence and oligometastasis has opened avenues for curative-intent local therapies, including re-irradiation. Modern radiotherapy techniques have enabled high-dose delivery with acceptable toxicity. This study aims to assess clinical outcomes and treatment-related toxicities in patients with oligorecurrent or oligometastatic cervical cancer treated with modern re-irradiation techniques.MethodsThis retrospective study included 20 cervical cancer patients with oligorecurrence or synchronous/metachronous oligometastases (≤ 5 lesions) who underwent at least one course of re-irradiation. Survival outcomes including locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) were estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Genitourinary (GU), gastrointestinal (GI), and hematologic toxicities were assessed and graded based on CTCAE version 5.0.ResultsThe median age was 53 years (range: 33–70), with 75% initially diagnosed at FIGO 2018 stage III. The predominant histologies were squamous cell carcinoma (50%) and adenocarcinoma (45%). Recurrences most commonly involved pelvic (30%) and para-aortic (30%) lymph nodes, with 50% occurring in-field. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and MR-guided adaptive brachytherapy (MR-GABT) were the most commonly used re-irradiation modalities, employed in 95% of patients. Median times to first and second recurrence were 11.1 months (IQR: 6.0–17.3) and 13.7 months (IQR: 5.6–21.7), respectively. At a median follow-up of 33.6 months, PFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS rates after the first recurrence were were 31.8%, 33.6%, 60.5%, and 84.2% respectively. Grade ≥ 2 genitourinary (GU), gastrointestinal (GI), and hematologic toxicities were observed in 40%, 25%, and 55% of patients, respectively. Grade 3 hematologic toxiciy was 25% and mostly occurred during chemotherapy administration. No grade ≥ 3 GU or GI toxicities were reported. The mean accumulated D0.03 cc after re-irradiation to bladder, rectum, sigmoid and bowel for in-fied/out-of-field were 83.8 ± 6.7/78.5 ± 7.5), 71.2 ± 3.9/69.5 ± 5.2, 68.0 ± 5.5/61.0 ± 5.3, and 62.9 ± 4.6/62.4 ± 7.1 GyEQD2(3), respectively.ConclusionRe-irradiation with contemporary radiotherapy techniques appears to be a feasible and effective salvage option for selected patients with limited recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer, yielding favorable survival and acceptable toxicity profiles.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a major global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where screening and access to advanced treatments may be limited [1–3]. Despite definitive therapy, a substantial proportion of patients experience disease recurrence, commonly presenting as locoregional or limited metastatic relapse, even in the modern radiotherapy era with the use of MRI-guided adaptive brachytherapy [4, 5].

The concept of oligorecurrence and oligometastasis, defined by the presence of a limited number of metastatic or recurrent lesions, typically five or fewer, has gained increasing attention as a distinct clinical state amenable to curative-intent local therapies [6–8]. This paradigm shift has been reinforced by recent ASTRO/ESTRO consensus guidelines, which advocate for a standardized definition of oligometastatic disease to support consistent clinical decision-making and research design [9].

Emerging evidence suggests that a distinct subset of cervical cancer patients among those with oligometastatic or oligorecurrent disease may derive meaningful benefit from aggressive local therapies such as high-dose radiotherapy. In oligorecurrence, modern re-irradiation techniques, including intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), and magnetic resonance-guided adaptive brachytherapy (MR-GABT), can provide durable local control with acceptable toxicity in recurrent cervical and other gynecologic cancers [10, 11]. A systematic review reported pooled 2-year and 5-year overall survival (OS) of 63% and 42%, with grade ≥ 3 toxicity < 10% when SBRT or brachytherapy were used alone [12]. Single-institution series show complete response 56% and 2-year survival 44% with manageable toxicity [13]; local control 44–71% and OS 40–78% at 2–5 years with 3D-IGBT in nonsurgical candidates [14]; and superior outcomes with IMRT-based salvage compared with limited-field RT [15]. Overall, re-irradiation within previously treated fields is feasible and safe when individualized planning is employed in experienced centers [16].

In the oligometastatic setting, A systematic review and meta-analysis noted poorer outcomes in de novo stage IVB than in recurrent disease, underscoring the need for advocate stage‑specific trials integrating aggressive local therapy [17]. A phase II randomized SABR-COMET trial demonstrated an overall survival benefit from SBRT to metastatic sites versus standard palliative care, without detriment to quality of life [18]. Specific to cervical cancer, multiple studies support aggressive local therapy. In carefully selected stage IVB patients with limited metastases, 5-year OS of 25–50% has been reported, with outcomes influenced by metastatic burden and primary tumor size [19]. Population-based data show improved survival with combined chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and primary-site surgery compared with CRT alone, particularly in patients without visceral metastases [20]. Collectively, these findings support definitive, advanced radiotherapy, with or without surgery, in carefully selected patients with advanced or metastatic cervical cancer. A pattern-of-care survey conducted within the EMBRACE research network highlighted substantial variability in the management of such patients, with most centers favoring stereotactic radiation approaches and recognizing the unmet need for standardized treatment protocols [21].

Given the promising outcomes observed in patients with oligometastatic and oligorecurrent cervical cancer treated with advanced radiotherapy techniques, there is a growing interest in defining the role of re-irradiation in this setting. Despite emerging evidence supporting its feasibility and potential survival benefits, data remain limited, and treatment practices for re-irradiation vary widely. This study aims to investigate clinical outcomes, failure patterns, and dosimetric parameters in cervical cancer patients undergoing re-irradiation for oligorecurrent or oligometastatic disease.

Materials and methods

This retrospective analysis included 20 patients with histologically confirmed cervical cancer who developed either oligorecurrence or synchronous/metachronous oligometastatic disease. Oligorecurrence was defined as locoregional recurrence involving five or fewer sites in patients, occurring either within or outside previously irradiated fields. Oligometastatic disease was defined as the presence of five or fewer distant nodal or visceral metastatic sites, either at initial presentation (synchronous) or after a disease-free interval (metachronous). All patients had received definitive radiotherapy as part of their initial treatment and subsequently underwent at least one course of re-irradiation.

Patient data were extracted from institutional electronic medical records and treatment planning systems and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (COA no. Si 477/2022). Baseline characteristics recorded included age, initial FIGO 2018 staging, histological subtype, nodal status, and primary treatment modalities. Patterns of recurrence were categorized as in-field, out-of-field, or both, based on spatial correlation with prior radiation portals.

The standard primary definitive treatment consisted of concurrent chemoradiation, delivering 45 Gy in 25 fractions using the VMAT technique, administered concurrently with either cisplatin or carboplatin. All patients received MR-GABT with a dose of 7–8 Gy per fraction for four fractions, aiming to achieve a cumulative D90 to the high-risk clinical target volume (HR-CTV) exceeding 85 Gy EQD2. The total treatment time was limited to no more than 56 days. Prophylactic para-aortic nodal irradiation was considered based on the EMBRACE II protocol. The follow-up protocol to detect recurrence or metastasis included MRI at 3 and 12 months after treatment, with abdominal CT performed annually for up to 5 years. PET/CT was obtained when findings on MRI or CT were equivocal.

Patients with synchronous or metachronous oligometastatic disease typically received first-line systemic chemotherapy as initial treatment. The choice of regimen was determined at the discretion of the treating gynecologic oncologist. Commonly administered doublet regimens included carboplatin with paclitaxel or cisplatin with paclitaxel. Second-line chemotherapy was initiated upon disease progression, with the selection of agents, such as gemcitabine or topotecan, left to the physician’s clinical judgment. No patients in this cohort received targeted therapy or immunotherapy.

The first recurrence was defined as disease relapse after completion of primary treatment, and the second recurrence as relapse following therapy for the first recurrence. Re-irradiation techniques employed included SBRT, VMAT, three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), and MR-GABT. Treatment parameters such as radiation technique, dose, fractionation scheme, and the use of concurrent or sequential chemotherapy were recorded. Where applicable, cumulative dose in equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) assuming an α/β ratio of 3 for OARs and 10 for tumor was calculated for different course of radiation.

Dosimetric data were collected for both target volumes and organs at risk (OARs), including the high-risk clinical target volume (HR-CTV), rectum, bladder, bowel, and sigmoid colon. Cumulative dose between each plan was calculated by DVH summation assuming the worst-case scenario and presented as D90 for targets and D0.03 cc for OARs.

The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) after the first recurrence, defined as the time from the date of the first recurrence to disease progression at any site or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRRFS), defined as the time to locoregional recurrence or progression within the pelvis or regional lymph nodes; distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), defined as the time to the appearance of new distant metastases beyond the regional field; and overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the first recurrence to death from any cause or last follow-up. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Acute and late genitourinary (GU), gastrointestinal (GI), and hematologic toxicities were recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Results

Twenty patients were included, with a median age of 53 years (range: 33–70). The majority (90%) presented with oligorecurrence, while 5% each had synchronous and metachronous oligometastatic disease. Most patients had advanced stage disease at initial diagnosis, with 75% classified as FIGO stage III. Histologic subtypes included squamous cell carcinoma (50%), adenocarcinoma (45%), and adenosquamous carcinoma (5%). Most patients had pelvic node involvement (N1) 65% while paraoartic node involvement (N2) Was found in 20%.

Initial treatment consisted of concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) in 85% of patients, surgery in 10%, and chemotherapy alone in 5%. Median GTV volume was 46.75 Gy. (IQR: 37,65) The median total treatment duration was 47 days (IQR: 42–52 days). Prophylactic para-aortic nodal (PAN) irradiation was delivered in 45% of patients, while the remaining 55% received radiation to the pelvic field only. Cisplatin was the most commonly used concurrent chemotherapy agent, administered in 83% of patients. Adjuvant chemotherapy following CCRT was given in two patients, both of whom received a combination of cisplatin and paclitaxel. Patterns of recurrence were in-field in 50%, out-of-field in 45%, and both in 5%.

Re-irradiation was delivered using SBRT (50%), VMAT (35%), 3D-CRT (5%), or brachytherapy (5%). Concurrent chemotherapy was used in 20%, while 30% received sequential chemotherapy. Dose regimens varied, with 55% receiving 30–50 Gy in 5 fractions, 35% receiving 45–66 Gy in 25–33 fractions, and 10% receiving 30–45 Gy in 10–15 fractions. There were 20 sites of first recurrence; among these, 18 patients (90%) underwent re-irradiation as part of their treatment. In addition, 11 sites of second recurrence were identified, and 2 patients (10%) received a second course of re-irradiation as part of salvage therapy. The median time from completion of initial treatment to first recurrence was 9.8 months, and from completion of first recurrence treatment to second recurrence was 13.1 months. The first recurrence sites included pelvic lymph nodes (30%), para-aortic lymph nodes (30%), the primary tumor site (10%), distant metastases (10%), or combinations of these locations. Among those who experienced a second recurrence, the majority had distant metastases (62%), with the common metastatic sites being the lungs (60%) and supraclavicular nodes (40%). The median time to first and second recurrence was 11.1 months (IQR: 6.0–17.3) and 13.7 months (IQR: 5.6–21.7), respectively. With a median follow-up of 33.6 months, the actuarial rates of PFS, LRRFS, DMFS and OS after the first recurrence were 31.8%, 33.6%, 60.5%, and 84.2%, respectively. Progression was observed in 11 patients, with a median PFS of 23 months. Locoregional progression occurred in 10 patients, with a median LRRFS of 22.7 months. Distant progression was seen in 5 patients; the median DMFS was not reached. Death occurred in 3 patients, and the median OS was also not reached at the time of analysis. Regarding treatment-related toxicity, none of the patients had persistent ≥ grade 2 toxicity from the initial treatment. Among 18 patients who underwent the first course of re-irradiation, the actuarial rates of acute and late ≥ grade 2 GU, GI, and hematologic toxicities were 40%/27%/72% and 36%/23%/45%, respectively. No patients experienced grade ≥ 3 GU or GI toxicity. The actuarial rates of grade 3 acute and late hematologic toxicity were 36% and 22.5%, respectively, with no cases of grade 4 or higher hematologic toxicity observed. Among the two patients who received a second course of re-irradiation, both experienced acute and late ≥ grade 2 GU, GI, and hematologic toxicities, while grade 3 toxicity occurred only as late GI toxicity in one patient. The most common GU toxicity was cystitis, whereas proctitis and diarrhea were the most frequent GI toxicities.

The most common initial radiotherapy treatment was definitive CCRT using VMAT technique and the dosimetric evaluation showed the cumulative D90 HR-CTV to be 88.1 ± 3.9 GyEQD2 (10) during initial RT, increasing to 93.2 ± 11.4 GyEQD2 (10) for in-field re-irradiation, and 88.2 ± 4.2 GyEQD2 (10) for out-of-field re-irradiation. The maximum rectal dose (D0.03 cc) was higher with in-field re-irradiation (71.2 ± 3.9 GyEQD2 (3)) compared to out-of-field re-irradiation (69.5 ± 5.2 GyEQD2 (3)). Similarly, sigmoid, bowel, and bladder doses were lower during re-irradiation, indicating effective OAR sparing. Radiotherapy treatment retails for re-irradiation are demonstrated in Table 2.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that re-irradiation using advanced radiotherapy techniques is a feasible and effective salvage strategy for patients with oligorecurrent and oligometastatic cervical cancer. With a median follow-up of 33.6 months, our cohort achieved promising survival outcomes, with PFS, LRRFS, DMFS and OS after the first recurrence were 31.8%, 33.6%, 60.5%, and 84.2%, respectively. These outcomes support the growing body of evidence suggesting that selected patients with limited recurrences or metastases may benefit from aggressive local treatment, including re-irradiation.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies in this field. The PFS was 31.8% and the median PFS found in this study was 23 months which also consistent with the finding from Chopra et al. reported median PFS of 16 months in patients undergoing re-irradiation for oligometastatic and oligorecurrent cervical cancer using modern radiation techniques [10]. Another finding from Li et al. re-irradiation with VMAT in recurrent cervical cancer with curative intent reported 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 38.7% [22]. A study evaluated the outcomes and patterns of relapse after definitive radiation therapy in 38 oligometastatic cervical cancer patients. The result also showed a similar rate of median progression-free survival (PFS) was 21.7months [11]. These results suggest that re-irradiation with curative intent in cervical cancer can provide favorable disease control. However, compared with other studies using SBRT for oligorecurrent or oligometastatic gynecologic cancer, which reported 2-year progression-free survival rates of 52–82% [23, 24], the outcomes in our study were lower. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in case selection for SBRT in those studies, whereas various techniques were employed in the present cohort.

A small subset of patients with oligometastatic disease may derive significant benefit from aggressive local therapies, such as radiotherapy or surgical resection. In our study, the overall survival (OS) rate was 84.2% at a median follow-up of 33 months, which is consistent with previous reports showing a 2-year overall survival rate of approximately 75% following definitive radiotherapy to oligometastatic sites in cervical cancer [11] and recurrent gynecologic cancers treated with SBRT [25]. These results are further supported by evidence from other malignancies [26, 27], where definitive radiotherapy to sites of limited metastatic disease has been associated with high rates of local control and favorable survival outcomes in selected patients.

The majority of recurrences in our study were either in-field or nodal, highlighting the challenge of treating regions previously exposed to high radiation doses. Although the integration of SBRT and image-guided brachytherapy allowed dose escalation to the target while sparing surrounding organs at risk, the prescribed doses were often compromised to protect the OARs, as demonstrated in the dosimetric analysis. This may explain why in-field recurrence remains a clinical challenge.

Notably, in this study, where 95% of patients were treated with SBRT, VMAT, or MR-GABT, treatment-related toxicities were limited to Grade 1–2 events involving the GU and GI systems, with no Grade ≥ 3 toxicities observed. Hematologic toxicities were predominantly mild to moderate, with transient grade 3 events associated with systemic chemotherapy. This favorable toxicity profile is consistent with previous reports, such as the study by Chopra et al., which reported Grade ≥ 3 toxicities, primarily radiation cystitis and proctitis, in 19% of patients, with 55% receiving SBRT or VMAT [21]. Our results suggest that with contemporary radiotherapy techniques, treatment-related morbidity can be minimized.

Despite the encouraging results, univariate analysis did not identify any statistically significant prognostic factors for survival. Neither the number of involved lymph nodes, tumor histology, disease burden, nor the number of re-irradiation courses correlated with outcomes. This may reflect the small sample size and heterogeneity of our cohort, or it may highlight the importance of individualized treatment approaches that account for multiple overlapping clinical variables.

A key strength of this study is the uniform use of modern radiotherapy techniques in both the initial and salvage settings. All patients received definitive chemoradiotherapy with VMAT followed by MR-GABT, and re-irradiation was delivered using advanced modalities such as SBRT. The selection of radiotherapy technique depended on the site of recurrence. MR-GABT was primarily used for local recurrences, SBRT was preferred for nodal or oligometastatic disease, and VMAT was employed when large-field treatment was required. Additionally, the study provides detailed dosimetric analysis, offering insights into dose distribution and organ-at-risk sparing during re-irradiation. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study introduces potential selection and reporting biases, and the small sample size limits the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the absence of patients treated with targeted therapies or immunotherapy reflects evolving treatment paradigms and may underestimate the potential benefits of integrating systemic agents. As immune checkpoint inhibitors receive regulatory approval for recurrent and metastatic cervical cancer [19–22], ongoing research exploring their potential synergy with re-irradiation with modern radiotherapy techniques may soon offer valuable insights to guide treatment selection.

Lastly, while our findings align with previously reported data, there remains a need for prospective, multicenter trials to better define re-irradiation protocols, assess long-term outcomes, and develop validated selection criteria. The integration of molecular biomarkers and imaging-based risk stratification could further refine patient selection and help personalize treatment.

Conclusion

Re-irradiation using modern radiotherapy techniques represents a promising salvage strategy for patients with oligorecurrent or oligometastatic cervical cancer, offering favorable survival outcomes and an acceptable toxicity profile.
