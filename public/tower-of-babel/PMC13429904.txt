Genome-wide CRISPR screen identifies AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker of response to platinum-based therapies in oral squamous cell carcinomas
Authors: Lucía Acero-Riaguas, Iván López-García, Irene Posse-Alonso, Mariana Yáñez-Bartolomé, Alejandro San Lorenzo-Vaquero, Catalina Berca, César Pérez-Brotóns, Beatriz Castelo, Ana Sastre-Perona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs) are among the most frequent and lethal cancers worldwide. Advanced stage tumors are still treated with standard chemotherapy; however, most patients develop chemotherapy resistance. To uncover new functional biomarkers of cisplatin response, we performed a synthetic lethality genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen in an OSCC cell line. This led to the discovery of AMBRA1 as a regulator of cisplatin sensitivity. In all tested OSCC cell lines and oral primary culture, AMBRA1 knockout cells showed increased sensitivity to cisplatin. Mechanistically, we demonstrated an unknown function of AMBRA1 in OSCCs, where loss of AMBRA1 led to an increase in S phase population, expression of genome instability markers, and DNA damage in basal conditions, predisposing cancer cells to an increased sensitivity to cisplatin and other platinum drugs. In summary, we uncovered AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker of response to platinum-based therapies in OSCCs.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is the sixth most common and lethal cancer worldwide.1 It comprises a diverse group of tumors that arise from oral or nasal cavities, larynx, pharynx, and salivary glands.1 Over 90% of HNC are Head and Neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) that emerge from mucosal epithelium, and within these, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is among the most common types of HNSCCs with 40% of the cases. HNSCC are generally classified into two subgroups, according to their etiology: those associated with high-risk subtypes of human papilloma virus (HPV) or associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption. Oropharynx tumors are frequently related to HPV infection while oral cavity and larynx malignancies are mostly HPV-negative. The molecular characteristics of both types of tumors differ, with HPV+ having better prognosis than HPV- due to the high mutational burden of the latter.2,3,4,5

Overall survival of early stages in HNSCC patients has improved as a result of refinement in surgical techniques and prevention campaigns. On the contrary, advanced stages prognosis and survival have not changed over the last decades.6 Specifically, OSCCs are characterized by being very aggressive tumors as they are usually diagnosed at advanced stages, with a 5-year survival of 83% at early stages and 38% at advanced stages.7 First line treatment is surgery, combined with radiotherapy alone or with adjuvant chemotherapy. However, late-stage tumors, which are unresectable or unsuitable for radiotherapy or immunotherapy,6,8 are treated with standard chemotherapy, being cisplatin the gold standard. Still, 60% of these patients develop therapy resistance, so they suffer relapses, which is associated with a very poor outcome.3,5,6

In the clinical practice, chemotherapy treatment is still chosen based on histological factors and the ECOG patient status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group), and there are no established or reliable biomarkers that can predict an effective response to the treatment.3,5,6 This becomes very relevant in the case of cisplatin, since it can have a very negative impact on the quality of life of the patients, due to cytotoxic effects that, added to the side effects derived from surgery, can lead to treatment interruption or failure.6,9 Hence, identifying genes that could act either as predictive biomarkers of therapy response or as targets for complementary therapy alongside standard treatment procedures, has a major role in the improvement of prognosis, survival, and quality of life of the patients.

A tool that has been proved very useful for identifying treatment-response biomarkers are the whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 knockout (KO) screens. These screens are based on the genome editing capacity of the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 system which, combined with a lentiviral library of single guide RNAs (sgRNAs), allows the generation of individual KOs of all-coding regions of the genome and thus studying the effect of these genes under a selective pressure. Since the establishment of these genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 KO screens, multiple CRISPR screens have been conducted to identify promising biomarkers of tumor progression, survival, or therapy resistance.10,11,12

In this study, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen on an OSCC cell line to identify genes that confer cisplatin resistance, as cisplatin is the gold standard administered chemotherapy in HNSCC. Of all the candidate genes studied in the validation phase of the screen, AMBRA1-KO showed the highest sensitivity to cisplatin treatment. Further studies showed that AMBRA1 depletion sensitized the cells due to an increase in genome instability. Thus, this work reinforces the use of CRISPR-Cas9 KO screens as a useful tool for identifying predictive biomarkers of response and uncovers AMBRA1 as a potential biomarker to predict cisplatin resistance in OSCC patients.

Results

Whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen identifies AMBRA1 as a cisplatin-resistance gene

Since cisplatin is the gold standard chemotherapy in patients with advanced OSCC, we evaluated cisplatin sensitivity in seven OSCC cell lines. We choose the OSC20 cell line for the whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO synthetic lethality screen with cisplatin (Figure 1A), as this cell line derives from a lymph node metastasis of an advanced OSCC and demonstrated the highest resistance to cisplatin, with a ∼35% of live cells at the highest concentration tested (Figure S1A). To avoid intracellular differences in Cas9 expression, we generated clones of the OSC20 cell line expressing Cas9 protein and choose the one that expressed the highest levels of Cas9 protein (OSC20 clone 3, CL3, Figure S1B). Prior to the screening, we verified that clone 3 had a functional Cas9 (Figure S1C) and did not present major differences from its parental line, both in terms of cell growth and resistance to cisplatin (Figures S1D and S1E). We used a whole-genome library that targeted 19,050 genes (3 sgRNAs/gene). OSC20 cells were infected at a <0.3 multiplicity of infection (MOI) to ensure a low MOI, and after puromycin selection, cells were seeded and treated using an IC80 cisplatin concentration (50 μM) (Figure S1F) as a selective pressure for 7 days. IC80 was calculated based on the concentration of cisplatin required to kill 80% of the cells using the conditions from the screen −100 mm dishes at 80% confluency. T0 and untreated cells were collected as controls (Figure 1A). Quality control analyses showed an acceptable Gini index (Figure 1B) and a high sgRNA coverage (>80%, Figure 1C). Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 Knockout Robust Rank Aggregation (MAGeCK-RRA) analysis was used to uncover increase/decrease of sgRNAs, identifying 903 cisplatin-resistance and 909 cisplatin-sensitivity candidates (p value <0.05, Figure 1D). Considering the distribution of the sgRNAs, the value of log2 fold change (L2FC >±3), rank position and their function, we selected 5 resistance genes (AMBRA1, KEAP1, MRFAP1, TGFBR2, and PDGFD) and 5 sensitivity genes (GSK3B, MORF4L1, FAT2, MED17, and CDK8) for the validation phase of the screen (Figures 1D and 1E). After verifying the loss of AMBRA1 protein expression in OSC20 CL3 KO cells (Figure 2C), we carried out concentration-response curves and measured the percentage of metabolically active cells, confirming that the depletion of AMBRA1 significantly sensitized cells to cisplatin treatment (IC50 sgTomato 4.07, sgAMBRA1-1 3.1, and sgAMBRA1-2 2.22 μM, Figures 2D and 2E). However, when we evaluated autophagy activation through LC3B and p62 expression, no differences were observed between AMBRA1-depleted and control cells, either at basal or cisplatin-treated conditions, or after blocking autophagy with the inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Figures 4C–4E). Furthermore, treatment with Bafilomycin A1 did not recapitulate AMBRA1-KO-induced sensibilization to cisplatin in control cells; instead, it appeared to have a protective effect at high concentrations of cisplatin (Figure 4F). These data suggests that the observed effects of AMBRA1 depletion decreasing cisplatin resistance may not be mediated by a defect in autophagy as was previously shown in oropharyngeal HNSCCs.13

AMBRA1 depletion decreases cell growth despite causing cyclin D1 accumulation and increased S-phase cell accumulation

Another recently described function of AMBRA1 is cell cycle regulation through the ubiquitination and degradation of D-type cyclins.14,15,16 Based on this role, we hypothesized that AMBRA1 deficiency could lead to the accumulation of cyclin D, increasing cell proliferation. Since it is known that high proliferation is associated with higher sensitivity to chemotherapy, this could explain the increased susceptibility to cisplatin of cells that lack AMBRA1. However, while we confirmed the accumulation of cyclin D1 protein (Figures 5A and S2D), AMBRA1-depleted cells displayed an overall tendency of decreased cell growth capacity in all cell lines analyzed (OSC20 CL3, OSC20-P, Cal27, and HN5), and even in the primary cell line OSCC43. The delay in cell growth was also sustained under low serum conditions in which we remove the external proliferation stimuli (Figures 5B, S3A, and S3B). To better understand the consequences of cyclin D1 upregulation upon AMBRA1 deletion, we performed EdU/Hoechst assays to measure DNA synthesis on OSC20 CL3 cells. Intriguingly, AMBRA1-deficient cells had an increased cell number in S phase, indicating a higher transition to S phase, although we could not detect a global increase in cell growth (Figure 5C). Additionally, the increase in proliferation was confirmed by measuring an increase in E2F2 mRNA levels on OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 cells (Figures 5D and S3D). After 24 h of cisplatin treatment, both control and AMBRA1-deficient cells accumulated massively into S phase, reflecting probably S phase DNA damage checkpoint. But while control cells were able to transit to the G2/M phase, so that they can repair the DNA damage induced by cisplatin and overcome treatment, AMBRA1-deficient cells fail to reach G2/M phase and remained accumulated on S phase. Taken together, these data suggest that although AMBRA1-deficient cells accumulate cyclin D1 protein and accumulate in S phase, this is not sufficient to increase their cell number. Furthermore, it suggests that upon damage, AMBRA1-KO cells remain accumulated at the S phase DNA damage checkpoint.

AMBRA1 depletion confers sensitivity to cisplatin-induced apoptosis

To understand the consequence of the accumulation of AMBRA1-KO cells on S phase upon cisplatin treatment, we performed apoptosis assays by flow cytometry in cells cultured under serum starvation conditions, with or without cisplatin treatment. In Cal27, HN5 and OSCC43 AMBRA1-KO cells, cisplatin treatment produced a significant increase in early and/or late apoptotic cells (Figures 5E and S4A). The same 48-h treatment on OSC20 CL3 cells induced only a slight increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells after treatment (10% of apoptotic cells, Figure S4A), showing a tendency toward higher apoptosis on AMBRA1-KO cells. Nevertheless, extending the duration of the treatment to 5 days, we could detect late apoptosis that was increased on AMBRA1-KO cells even at lower concentrations of cisplatin (Figure 5E). Regarding the expression of apoptosis-related genes, we detected an upregulation of the pro-apoptotic gene BCL2L11(Figure S4B). Taken together, these data support that AMBRA1 deficiency increases cisplatin-induced apoptosis.

Depletion of AMBRA1 promotes DNA damage, thereby increasing the toxicity of cisplatin treatment

To better understand the molecular mechanisms involved in cisplatin sensitivity induced by AMBRA1 loss, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in OSC20 CL3 control and AMBRA1-KO cells with or without cisplatin treatment. The global analysis of down- or up-regulated genes (adjusted p value <0.05 and L2FC > ± 0.5) showed differences in gene expression profiles between AMBRA1-KO and control cells (Figures S5A and S5B; Table S2). While sgAMBRA1 untreated cells modified the expression of a small number of genes (Figure S5A, clusters 4 and 5), we observed that depletion of AMBRA1 regulated a greater number of genes after cisplatin treatment in comparison to control cells (757 upregulated genes) (Figure 6A, blue circle). The same was observed in downregulated genes in AMBRA1-KO cells (Figure S5D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the genes commonly upregulated in both control and AMBRA1-KO cells after cisplatin treatment (235 genes) showed an overall alteration in metabolic pathways, highlighting the increase in inflammation and DNA-binding related pathways (Figure S5C). Interestingly, KEGG analysis of the genes that were regulated exclusively or differentially expressed in AMBRA1-KO-treated condition (797 upregulated and 464 downregulated) revealed that AMBRA1 depletion induced the expression of DNA damage/repair and cell cycle-related genes and a downregulation in genes involved cell-matrix interaction, in comparison with sgTomato cells (Figures 6A and S5D). We validated the RNA-seq results by evaluating the gene expression of DNA repair, chromosome instability and cell cycle checkpoint regulator genes, both in OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 cells. We could confirm a general upward trend in the expression levels of the evaluated genes. Notably, we observed a significantly upregulated expression of XRCC1, XRCC3, RECQL4, RAD54L, LIG1, MAD2L1, AURKB, and CDC25A; with these differences being more apparent after cisplatin treatment in the case of OSC20 CL3 or under basal conditions in OSCC43 cells (Figure 6B). To confirm this phenotype, we also assessed the protein expression of phospho-p53 (p-p53) and γH2AX, two well-known markers of DNA damage (Figure 6C). In line with the gene expression results, the depletion of AMBRA1 in OSC20 CL3 and OSCC43 caused an increase in both phosphorylated and total p53 and γH2AX after cisplatin treatment. Interestingly, OSC20 AMBRA1-KO cells presented higher levels of p-p53 even in basal conditions. Furthermore, we measure the presence of micronuclei, which is a direct measurement of genome instability. AMBRA1-KO cells presented higher number of micronuclei already in basal conditions, and this increase remained constant upon cisplatin treatment (Figure 6D). Interestingly, AMBRA1-KO cells had the same levels of micronuclei as the tomato control cells treated with cisplatin. To extend our analysis to a larger and more clinically relevant cohort, we analyzed the cBioPortal online database, specifically the Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma database (The Cancer Genome Atlas, TCGA, PanCancer Atlas).17,18,19 In agreement with our in vitro data, this analysis showed that AMBRA1 low samples exhibited a higher proportion of “fraction of genome altered,” “tumor break load,” and “microsatellite instability (MSI) MANTIS score,” three clinical attributes studied as indicators of genome instability (Figures 6E and S5E). The comparison of upregulated genes in AMBRA1 low samples in cBioportal with those highly expressed in the AMBRA1-KO treated condition from our RNA-seq (clusters 1 and 2; Figure S5A), displayed a coincidence of 157 common genes. In accordance with the previous observations, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of biological processes of these shared genes showed an enrichment of DNA repair and cell cycle-related genes (Figure 6F). Aligned with these findings, cBioportal gene expression analysis revealed a negative correlation between the gene expression of AMBRA1 and the DNA damage genes UBE2T, MAD2L1BP, XRCC3, and RAD54L (Figures 6G and S5F). Depletion of AMBRA1 confers sensitivity to platinum-based therapies, but not to other chemotherapies

Since the loss of AMBRA1 caused cells to be more responsive to cisplatin, we proceeded to evaluate whether it also enhanced the sensitivity to other chemotherapies frequently administered to patients with HNSCC, regardless of whether they had the same mechanism of action. We evaluated the sensitivity of AMBRA1-depleted cells to carboplatin treatment, which, like cisplatin, is a platinum-based chemotherapy that acts as an alkylating agent that binds directly to DNA and forms DNA adducts.20 We also tested 5-fluorouracil, an analog of uracil which inhibits thymidylate synthase activity,20 and two different taxanes, paclitaxel and docetaxel, which are inhibitors of microtubule polymerization.20 Our data indicated that only carboplatin treatment reproduced the sensitivity profile in all cell lines (OSC20 CL3, OSC20-P, Cal27, and HN5) and the primary cell culture OSCC43, especially under serum starvation conditions (Figures 7A and S5G). Either 5-fluorouracil or the taxanes demonstrated major differences between AMBRA1-KO compared to control cells (Figures 7B and S5G). But docetaxel and paclitaxel-treated AMBRA1-KO cells showed a tendency toward sensitivity to both taxanes especially under serum deprivation (Figures 7C, 7D, and S5G). These results suggest that the absence of AMBRA1 may have a minor but not determinant role in chemotherapy sensitivity on non-platinum-based therapies. Discussion

Overcoming therapy resistance is still one of the major obstacles in the treatment of HNSCC. Treatments are becoming increasingly sophisticated, but the lack of consistent predictive biomarkers prevents proper personalization of therapy. In this work, our main objective was the identification of potential functional biomarkers of cisplatin response, as it is broadly used in HNSCC patients, but also has negative effects on the quality of life of the patients. Considering this, the application of a whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen offered a good approach to achieving that objective. We transduced an OSCC cell line with a lentiviral library consisting of 58,028 sgRNAs that interfered 19,050 genes and then we evaluated the enrichment or loss of the sgRNAs after cisplatin treatment. Validation experiments of the selected sgRNAs allowed us to identify one gene, AMBRA1, as a possible biomarker, as AMBRA1-depleted cells became more sensitive to cisplatin treatment. Regarding the failure to identify more response genes, we hypothesize that it could be due to a high rate of false positive sgRNAs in the screen analysis—a common challenge in CRISPR screens21—or a deficient KO efficiency of the sgRNA library, as cells are theoretically only transduced with one lentiviral particle by employing a very low MOI. It could also be attributable to the fact that there was no verification that the sgRNAs were working correctly in the validation phase, so we cannot rule out completely that those genes do not actually confer such sensitivity to cisplatin.

There is an evidence of the dual role of AMBRA1 in therapy resistance and tumor progression, as it seems to be context-dependent.22,23 For example, in melanoma, low levels of AMBRA1 promote tumor aggressiveness24 and resistance to mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inhibitors,25 while in breast and ovarian cancer, the silencing of AMBRA1 sensitizes the cells to the studied treatment (paclitaxel26 and epirubicin27 in breast cancer and cisplatin in ovarian cancer,25 respectively). In the context of HNSCC, Antonioli et al.13 demonstrated that AMBRA1 expression is decreased in HPV-associated tumors compared to HPV-negative tumors and that low AMBRA1 confers cisplatin sensitivity through the impairment of autophagy in oropharyngeal tumors, which is known to be a resistance mechanism to therapy. In our study, we assessed the role of AMBRA1 in autophagy in our models. While we confirmed that AMBRA1-depleted cells were more sensitive to cisplatin under autophagy induction conditions (serum starvation) in three cell lines (OSC20, Cal27, and HN5) and a primary cell culture (OSCC43), it did not seem to be due to autophagy. Our data showed that Bafilomycin A1 treatment did not induce higher levels of the autophagy markers LC3B-II or p62 on AMBRA1-deficient cells compared to control cells, and importantly it did not recapitulate AMBRA1-KO sensitivity profile in control cells. Overall, this suggests that AMBRA1 deficiency can cause sensibilization to cisplatin treatment through an autophagy-independent mechanism. Additionally, it suggests that HNSCC from different locations (oropharyngeal vs. oral SCC), may have different consequences upon AMBRA1 loss.

Since serum starvation conditions affect cell proliferation and AMBRA1 is involved in cell cycle regulation by ubiquitination of D-type cyclins,14,15 we hypothesized that AMBRA1-depleted cells could respond better to cisplatin due to an increased cell proliferation, a well-known factor causing therapy sensitivity. Surprisingly, overall cell growth was not increased or showed inconsistent results between our models, despite a cyclin D1 protein accumulation. More detailed cell cycle analysis indicated that AMBRA1-KO cells accumulate into S phase in basal conditions, and do not overpass the S phase DNA damage checkpoint in response to cisplatin and cannot reach the G2/M phase. This may indicate that the loss of AMBRA1 is increasing S phase, causing a replicative stress, an accumulation of DNA damage and the S phase checkpoint both in basal conditions, which prevents their faster growth, but also upon cisplatin treatment. The S phase checkpoint monitors DNA integrity, and it is triggered when the machinery detects accumulation of damaged DNA, pausing DNA replication. If the damage is severe, it causes prolonged S arrest and predisposes the cells to enter cell death. Indeed, we demonstrated in all four cell lines that the treatment with cisplatin in AMBRA1-KO cells, elicited a higher percentage of apoptotic cells. The fact that OSC20 cell line required a longer treatment to detect apoptosis can be explained by its longer doubling time, although this remains to be explored in detail.

A recently published study concluded that cisplatin resistance is linked predominantly to DNA damage induction more than to alteration on drug uptake-related pathways in HNSCC. In their transcriptomic analysis, there was an increase in DNA damage and cell cycle genes such as H2AFX, TP53, BRCA1, XRCC2, FANCD2, CDKN1A, and RAD18.28 In addition, another study analyzed cisplatin resistance genes in two tongue squamous cell carcinoma cell lines by performing a CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen, using a lentiviral library designed specifically against genes involved in the control of kinases. They identified STK19 as a potential therapeutic marker in HNSCC and observed that STK19 depleted cells promoted DNA damage through O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) regulation, dysregulating the levels of different DNA repair genes, including XRCC4, RAD51B, and FANCC, and inducing protein expression of γH2AX.29 These results are in line with our RNA-seq analysis, in which we observed an upregulation of genes involved in DNA repair, cell cycle, and chromosome stability in AMBRA1-depleted cells. The validation of the RNA-seq was demonstrated through gene expression analysis of DNA damage-related genes and protein expression of two canonical DNA damage markers, γH2AX and p-p53, and the measurement of micronuclei, a genome instability well-known marker. This result would explain why AMBRA1-KO cells cannot overcome cell-cycle arrest since they accumulate such DNA damage upon cisplatin treatment that cannot be repaired, leading to cell death.

When extrapolating the results using the TCGA database, survival curves showed no differences based on AMBRA1 expression levels (data not shown). This could be due to the pleiotropic and ubiquitous expression of AMBRA1 in normal tissue and tumor microenvironment. However, this analysis corroborated that patient samples with low AMBRA1 expression have higher fraction of genome altered, tumor break load, and MSI. In addition, we observed a negative correlation between DNA damage genes and AMBRA1 expression. Moreover, part of the upregulated genes in AMBRA1-KO-treated cells overlapped with those upregulated in AMBRA1 low samples from TCGA, reinforcing the findings of our study.

Considering all of the above, the results indicate that AMBRA1 depletion, through cyclin D1 upregulation, causes a replicative stress that increases the DNA damage in basal conditions, thus promoting genome instability and compromising the cells’ ability to respond to the genotoxic stress induced by cisplatin in OSCCs.

AMBRA1 has a wide range of molecular functions and there is no specific AMBRA1 inhibitor available, so pharmacological inhibition does not seem feasible, given the actual knowledge. Instead, this study shows that it could be tested as a promising predictive biomarker to platinum-based eligible patients, depending on its expression levels in biopsies analyses, and enabling a certain degree of therapy personalization.

Limitations of the study

Although we clearly demonstrated that AMBRA1 depletion can sensitize OSCC cells to cisplatin, some limitations to our study should be recognized. Firstly, while we could show how AMBRA1 deletion increased cyclin D1 protein, and that caused an increase in S phase, and a replicative stress-associated DNA damage than sensitized cells to cisplatin, we lack the final evidence that cyclin D1 overexpression is sufficient to reproduce the phenotype, although this has been demonstrated in immortalized human fibroblast.16 Secondly, despite we demonstrated the reproducibility of AMBRA1-loss phenotype on multiple cell lines, using 2D and 3D models, our assays were limited to in vitro models, which lack modification of cisplatin biodistribution, and availability and the effects of the tumor microenvironment provided by in vivo models. Lastly, while we demonstrated significant correlations of AMBRA1 expression with DNA-damage markers using the TCGA retrospective cohort, a validation cohort in a retrospective Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) patient samples collection to strengthen the clinical value of these correlations.
