Explainable machine learning for predicting clinical outcomes in HIV/TB co-infection: a comparative retrospective study
Authors: Qingfeng Sun, Kai Zhang, Yuanlong Xu, Mengmei Luo, Zhouzhou Yang, Qianyu Liu, Sang Liu, Aimei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV/TB co-infection presents substantial public-health challenges, showing greater treatment-failure and mortality rates than tuberculosis alone. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) provide a robust means of identifying high-risk patients early in the disease course.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 359 patients co-infected with HIV and TB at a single tertiary-care hospital. We extracted clinical and immunological data. The cohort was subsequently divided into training (0%) and test (0%) subsets, and class imbalance was addressed with the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). Six ML classifiers—Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, Support Vector Machine, Extra Trees and CatBoost—were trained after grid-search hyper-parameter tuning. Model performance was assessed with the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, recall, precision, specificity and F1-score. Multi-criteria ranking was then conducted with the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). The leading model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsOverall, 304 of 359 patients (84.7%) had favourable outcomes, whereas 55 (15.3%) had unfavourable outcomes. LightGBM achieved the best overall performance (AUC = 0.771; accuracy = 84.72%; F1 = 0.522) and was ranked first by TOPSIS. SHAP analysis highlighted age, CD4 and CD8 counts, body-mass index and occupation as key predictors. Lower BMI, pronounced immunosuppression and older age were strongly associated with unfavourable outcomes, findings that align with established clinical evidence.ConclusionA gradient-boosted model (LightGBM) combined with SHAP interpretation demonstrated reliable predictive performance in HIV/TB co-infection and highlighted clinically actionable risk factors. Incorporating this tool into routine workflows could enable healthcare providers to identify high-risk individuals earlier, allocate resources more efficiently and, ultimately, improve TB-treatment success.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11998-w.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV/TB co-infection, Machine learning, SHAP, Retrospective study

Background

HIV and TB co-infection poses a major global-health challenge owing to the synergistic effects of the two pathogens on patient outcomes [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2024 estimates that 10.8 million people developed active TB in 2023; 61%—≈662 000 cases—were among people living with HIV (PLHIV). TB remained the leading opportunistic infection among PLHIV, accounting for 161 000 deaths recorded as HIV/AIDS-related. In the same year, 436 805 new episodes of HIV-associated TB were notified, representing 6.8% of all TB notifications worldwide [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the greatest burden of HIV/TB co-infection, driven largely by high HIV prevalence and the heightened susceptibility of HIV-positive individuals to active TB [3]. Although national TB-control efforts have reduced incidence in many parts of China, HIV/TB co-infection continues to challenge public health in specific regions. The south-western province of Guangxi—home to the nation’s highest HIV prevalence—reports a persistently high burden of co-infection [4]. In a multicentre Brazilian cohort (RePORT-Brazil), the overall TB treatment-succes rate was 79% but only 64% among PLHIV, underscoring the need for earlier detection and targeted support for those at greatest risk of poor outcomes [5].

Timely, accurate risk stratification of individuals co-infected with HIV and TB is essential for directing targeted interventions. Cohort studies from Ethiopia and the United States demonstrate that mortality depends on a constellation of demographic, clinical, and immunological factors rather than on any single parameter, underscoring the need for multifactorial assessment [6, 7]. Conventional models are often less adaptable to newly emerging biomarkers or demographic variables—a limitation further compounded by patient heterogeneity [8, 9]. Recent advances in ML show promise for improving outcome prediction in complex diseases by integrating high-dimensional clinical, laboratory, and sociodemographic data [10–12]. Studies involving HIV and TB cohorts indicate that ML algorithms—especially tree-based methods such as XGBoost—can deliver robust predictive performance; for example, AUCs of 0.777 and 0.928 have been reported for HIV risk stratification and HIV/TB co-infection diagnosis, respectively [13, 14]. Consequently, ML-driven prognostic modelling specific to HIV/TB co-infection remains limited, and many reports are constrained by small sample sizes, imbalanced datasets, or limited model transparency. This issue is particularly critical, because reliance on “black-box” algorithms without interpretable outputs can impede clinical adoption [15].

Explainable-AI techniques, notably SHAP, are increasingly valued for clarifying how individual features contribute to model predictions and for supplying both global (population-level) and local (patient-specific) insights [16]. Interpretable models are essential not only for fostering clinician trust but also for enabling the ethical, effective integration of algorithmic tools into real-world therapeutic decision-making [17]. Reliance on a single performance indicator—such as accuracy—can overlook trade-offs with other clinically important dimensions, including sensitivity and interpretability. To address this limitation, the TOPSIS provides a robust multicriteria evaluation framework that integrates diverse performance metrics into one composite ranking, thereby supporting more balanced, context-sensitive model selection [18].

Against this backdrop, we systematically compare six ML classifiers for predicting clinical outcomes (favourable vs. unfavourable) in HIV/TB co-infected patients treated at a single tertiary hospital. We further employ TOPSIS to consolidate multiple performance metrics into a comprehensive ranking and apply SHAP to the top-performing model to identify the key predictors that influence patient prognosis. By coupling robust predictive modelling with interpretable outputs, our study aims to enhance current methods for recognising high-risk HIV/TB co-infected individuals and for guiding timely, individualised clinical interventions.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective single-centre study was conducted at a tertiary-care hospital dedicated to infectious diseases. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Chest Hospital, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Informed consent was waived, as all data were de-identified and used exclusively for research. Between 2021 and 2023, we enrolled 359 patients co-infected with HIV and TB. To ensure diagnostic consistency, patients were required to meet all of the following criteria: (i) confirmed HIV infection; (ii) pulmonary TB verified by sputum smear or culture, or by nucleic-acid amplification plus supporting clinical evidence (e.g., chest radiography); and (iii) a documented TB-treatment outcome—favourable or unfavourable—according to World Health Organization definitions. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete treatment records or lacked a definitive TB-outcome classification. All individuals ultimately received guideline-concordant antiretroviral therapy (AT): 34% were already on ART at TB diagnosis and the remaiing 66% initiated ART within 4 weeks, in line with national and WHO recommendations. Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records, laboratory-information systems and hospital administrative databases. Variables captured comprised (i) demographic factors—age, sex, ethnicity, occupation, smoking status; (ii) clinical characteristics—body-mass index (BMI), comorbidities, timing of antiretroviral therapy (ART) relative to TB treatment, and site of TB disease; and (iii) immunological indices—CD4 and CD8 T-cell counts, plus the CD4/CD8 ratio. Treatment outcomes were classified as favourable (cured or treatment completed) or unfavourable. All identifying information was anonymised before analysis.

Data preprocessing

To minimise imputation bias, we restricted the feature set to variables that were recorded in >80% of patients across the study period. Standard laboratory assays (e.g., liver enzymes, full blood count) were excluded because >30% of their values were missing or they were obtained at non-uniform time-points, risking temporal leakage. All variables were evaluated for missingness and plausibility. When < 5% of a continuous variable was missing, values were imputed with the mean or median, whereas missing categorical values were replaced with the mode. Variables with 5–20% missingness underwent multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Features showing >20% missingness or deemed clinically peripheral were removed. Outliers in continuous variables (e.g., age, CD4 count) were retained when cross-verification confirmed plausibility. Because unfavourable outcomes represented 15. % of the cohort, indicating a moderate class imbalance, the SMOTE was applied to the training set. This technique helps mitigate the risk of the model becoming biased towards the majority class (favourable outcomes) and improves its ability to identify the clinically crucial minority class (unfavourable outcomes), thereby enhancing the model’s sensitivity for at-risk patients [19]. The dataset was then partitioned 80:20 (training: test), while the original class distribution was preserved in the test subset. Finally, continuous variables were standardised for the Support Vector Machine (SVM), whereas tree-based algorithms were run on unscaled data. One-hot encoding was applied to nominal categories (e.g., occupation).

Machine learning model development

Six supervised algorithms were evaluated for binary classification (favourable vs. unfavourable): Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), LightGBM (LGBM), SVM, Extra Trees Classifier (ETC) and CatBoost (CB). Hyper-parameter tuning was performed through grid-search with five-fold cross-validation on the SMOTE-adjusted training set, optimising parameters including n_estimators, learning-rate, maximum depth and regularisation strength. Models were then retrained on the oversampled training set using the best hyper-parameters and subsequently applied to the held-out test set for final evaluation. For completeness, we additionally trained a simple CART decision-tree; full tuning details are provided in Supplementary materials.

Model evaluation and comparison

Model performance on the test set was evaluated using predicted probabilities for the unfavourable class, which was designated as the positive class; therefore, true positives (TP) represent correctly predicted unfavourable outcomes, with a decision threshold of 0.5. Principal metrics comprised the AUC, accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity and F1-score. Bootstrapped 9% confidence intervals were calculated for AUC and other key metrics. To visualise and compare performance, ROC curves were plotted, and a radar chart summarised multi-metric performance across models. Finally, the TOPSIS combined all six metrics into a single ranking score, where higher values denote closer proximity to an “ideal” classifier.

Model interpretability with SHAP

The top-performing model, identified through the multi-metric comparison and TOPSIS ranking, was subjected to post-hoc explainability analysis using SHAP. Mean absolute SHAP values quantified each feature’s global importance, and a beeswarm plot depicted how feature values related to their contribution to unfavourable predictions. Local explanations were generated with force or waterfall plots for individual patients, illustrating how specific variables shifted the baseline risk and thus aiding clinicians in interpreting each feature’s influence on the model output.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python v3.9. Continuous variables were summarised as median (interquartile range) and compared between groups with the Mann–Whitney U-test, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and analysed using the chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05. Model performance was evaluated on the 0% hold-out test set using accuracy, recall, specificity, precision, F1-score and the AUC, the latter computed with roc_auc_score.

Results

Patient characteristics and outcomes

Of the 359 HIV/TB co-infected participants, 304 (84. %) had a favourable outcome, whereas 55 (15. %) had an unfavourable outcome (Table 1). The cohort’s median age was 55 years (IQR 46–64), and 80. % (n = 288) were male. Occupation type (p = 0.026) and key immunological markers—CD4 (p = 0.002) and CD8 (p = 0.005)—differed significantly between outcome groups. Conversely, no significant differences were observed for gender (p = 0.382), patient type (p = 0.363) or smoking status (p = 1.000). The median BMI was 19 kg/m² (IQR 17–21), with no notable group-wise difference (p = 0.162). Comorbidity counts are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Several comorbidities were individually rare in our cohort—each occurring in ≤ 34% of patients. Modelling every low-frequency condition as a stand-alone category would fragment the data and risk unstable estimates. We therefore retained a three-level composite variable (“None”, “Electrolyte/Metabolic Disorder”, “Other”). Taken together, these findings highlight the prognostic relevance of occupational factors and immunological status in HIV/TB co-infection.

Model performance

As shown in Table 2, six machine-learning models—Random Forest, LightGBM, Extra Trees, CatBoost, SVM and XGBoost—were trained on the SMOTE-balanced dataset and evaluated on a held-out test set (n = 72). Random Forest achieved the highest accuracy (8611%) and specificity (9365%); however, it detected fewer than one-third (3.33%) of unfavourable cases. In contrast, LightGBM provided a more balanced performance, recording an AUC of 0.771 and the highest F1-score (2.17%). SVM and XGBoost each achieved an AUC of 0.723, yet their accuracies were lower—2.22% and 6.67%, respectively. Figure 2A presents the ROC curves, highlighting LightGBM’s comparatively larger area under the curve than that of the other models.

For unfavourable-outcome detection, LightGBM achieved the highest recall (66.6%), whereas Random Forest and Extra Trees both recorded recall values of 33.3%. CatBoost (accuracy 76.3%) and SVM (accuracy 72.2%) displayed moderate recalls—55.5% and 66.6%, respectively—but exhibited lower precision. Figure 2B depicts each model’s performance across five core metrics—accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1-score—using a radar chart, and underscores LightGBM’s superior recall–precision trade-off. A single-tree CART model was also evaluated (see Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1); it yielded AUC = 0.60 with markedly lower recall and F1-score, so it was not included in the main comparison. The corresponding confusion matrix for LightGBM is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, further illustrating its sensitivity–specificity trade-off at the default threshold.

Model ranking via TOPSIS

Figure 2C displays the integrated TOPSIS scores for all models, thereby condensing multi-metric performance into a single numeric value for each. LightGBM again ranked highest, reflecting a consistent balance across accuracy, recall, precision, specificity, F1-score and AUC. XGBoost, CatBoost and Random Forest followed in descending order, whereas Extra Trees and SVM received the lowest scores, indicating a comparatively weaker balance among the assessed metrics. Consequently, LightGBM demonstrated the most balanced performance of the six models and thus appears well-suited for risk stratification in this population.

Feature importance and SHAP interpretation

LightGBM was selected for additional interpretability analysis using SHAP. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, global-feature importance—expressed as mean absolute SHAP values—identified age, CD8 count, occupation, BMI and CD4 count as the five most influential predictors. Greater age, lower BMI and advanced immunosuppression shifted the model output towards unfavourable outcomes, whereas normal or elevated BMI, stronger immune markers and employed status generally favoured a more positive classification.

(B) SHAP summary plot showing the individual contribution of clinical features to predictions across all patients

The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 3B) further clarifies these directional effects at the individual level. For example, older patients (red dots for “Age” at the right) typically exhibit higher SHAP values, indicating increased risk, whereas younger patients (blue dots) shift the model towards a more favourable prediction. Similarly, low BMI (blue points) contributes to unfavourable outcomes, whereas higher BMI (red) exerts a protective influence. CD8 count displays a nuanced pattern—both very high and very low levels can elevate risk, possibly reflecting the dual effects of immune activation and immune depletion in HIV/TB co-infection. CD4 counts below ≈ 150 cells µL⁻¹ carry markedly positive SHAP values, underscoring that severe immunosuppression strongly pushes the prediction towards an unfavourable outcome. An inverted CD4/CD8 ratio (< 0.3) shows a similar risk-amplifying effect, whereas recovery towards ≥ 0.5 after ART initiation yields negative SHAP values, consistent with clinical use of the ratio as an immune-reconstitution marker. Occupation likewise functioned as a socio-economic indicator: patients classified as lower status or unemployed clustered on the higher-risk side, reinforcing that both biological and social factors influence treatment outcomes.

Figure 4 presents SHAP force plots for two representative patients, underscoring how individual features collectively drive the model prediction. In Fig. 4A, a patient with several risk factors—multiple comorbidities, low CD4 count, under-weight BMI and low occupational status—shows predominantly positive SHAP values (red), elevating the final output f(x) to 7.69, well above baseline, and yielding a predicted unfavourable outcome. Conversely, Fig. 4B depicts a younger patient with moderate immune markers, normal BMI and no significant comorbidities; these protective factors (blue) pull the prediction below baseline (f(x) = − 1.88), corresponding to a favourable outcome. Clinically, these local explanations confirm that LightGBM predictions are consistent with established risk indicators—age, immune status and nutritional state.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and compared six ML models to predict TB treatment outcomes in patients co-infected with HIV and TB, with the dual aim of achieving high predictive performance and interpretability. The results show that contemporary ML methods—particularly tree-based ensemble techniques—yield robust prognostic insights and can therefore support more targeted interventions for high-risk individuals.

LightGBM achieved a favourable balance between sensitivity (recall) and specificity, capturing a high proportion of unfavourable outcomes without generating an excessive number of false positives. Although Random Forest produced the highest overall accuracy, its limited recall meant that it failed to identify a substantial fraction of at-risk patients. Clinically, an AUC of 0.771 suggests that LightGBM holds potential as an exploratory tool for risk stratification. However, we stress that these findings should be interpreted with caution and are preliminary. Before any consideration for clinical implementation, these results require robust external validation in larger, multi-centre cohorts. Furthermore, dependence on a single metric may obscure trade-offs between critical objectives such as recall and precision. Using the TOPSIS multi-criteria decision method, we observed that LightGBM consistently outperformed Random Forest, SVM and XGBoost across several performance indicators. This comprehensive evaluation therefore supports the use of LightGBM to identify high-risk cases while minimising false alarms—a balance that is particularly important in managing HIV/TB co-infection.

Consistent with recent calls for interpretable AI in healthcare [20], we applied SHAP to explain how LightGBM generated its predictions. Global feature-importance analysis showed that age, CD4 and CD8 counts, BMI and occupation exerted the greatest influence on patient risk. For example, advanced age and low BMI consistently increased the likelihood of unfavourable outcomes, whereas higher CD4 counts and being employed were associated with a better prognosis [21]. In keeping with these findings, transcriptomic modelling of HIV/TB co-infection has highlighted CD8-related signatures as key discriminators of disease state [22]. Taken together, these observations echo established risk patterns in HIV/TB co-infection: older or severely immunocompromised individuals and those facing socio-economic disadvantages often experience worse outcomes [20, 23].

Beyond global rankings, patient-level SHAP force plots vividly illustrate how multiple risk factors combine to push an individual prediction towards higher or lower risk [24, 25]. Such individualised explanations have been shown to facilitate clinical adoption by clarifying model logic, thereby increasing physician trust and guiding targeted interventions [26, 27]. In practical terms, the thresholds surfaced by SHAP are actionable: patients with CD4 < 150 cells µL⁻¹ or a CD4/CD8 ratio < 0.3 could be prioritised for rapid ART optimisation and immune re-evaluation, whereas those with BMI < 18 kg m⁻² may benefit from early nutritional supplementation and weight-monitoring protocols. Similarly, individuals identified as socio-economically vulnerable (e.g., unemployed) can be channelled into adherence-support programmes or community health-worker follow-up. For example, a patient flagged as high risk owing to a severely low CD4 count and under-weight status would merit intensified nutritional support, closer monitoring and, when appropriate, adjustment of antiretroviral therapy.

Although direct comparisons are constrained by differences in study design and outcome definitions, our overall performance aligns with that of other ML-based TB-outcome studies. Earlier studies reported an XGBoost model with an AUROC of 0.89 for predicting anti-TB drug-induced liver injury [28], and another achieving near-perfect discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.97) for neonatal biliary atresia using a comparable SHAP-enabled framework [29]. These figures bracket the performance range observed in our cohort and confirm the competitive accuracy of gradient-boosted tree approaches. Importantly, by embedding patient-level SHAP explanations, our model helps close the transparency gap that has historically limited clinical uptake of predictive algorithms. By highlighting both immunological (CD4, CD8) and socio-economic (employment status) drivers of risk, the present work offers nuanced, actionable insights for clinicians and researchers seeking to optimise HIV/TB care.

Despite encouraging performance, several limitations merit consideration. First, as a single-centre retrospective study with a limited number of events, our findings’ generalisability is constrained. We strongly emphasize that rigorous external validation in diverse, multi-centre cohorts is not just a future step but an essential prerequisite to ascertain the model’s real-world performance and reliability. Second, we did not capture TB drug resistance or detailed ART adherence, potentially omitting key determinants of outcome. Third, SMOTE may create synthetic observations that do not fully reflect the true minority-class distribution, which could distort real-world class balance [19, 30]. Prospective evaluation is required to verify the clinical impact.Fourth, grouping heterogeneous comorbidities (detailed in Supplementary Table S2) into a single ‘Other’ variable was a necessary trade-off for model stability. However, this approach reduces clinical granularity by masking the distinct prognostic impact of diverse conditions like malignancy versus cardiovascular disease. This, in turn, limits the model’s utility for guiding targeted, condition-specific interventions, a limitation that larger datasets may address in future research.

In future work, we will incorporate time-updated variables to enable dynamic risk assessment and will pursue prospective validation. A further priority is to embed the model in electronic medical-record systems so that it can generate real-time alerts. These alerts would prompt interventions such as additional nutritional support or accelerated clinic visits for patients identified as high risk. Ultimately, closing the gap between ML insights and clinical decision-making remains paramount to ensure that at-risk individuals receive timely, personalised care.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility of using a SHAP-interpreted LightGBM model to predict tuberculosis treatment outcomes in patients co-infected with HIV. By highlighting age, immune status (CD4/CD8), BMI and socio-economic factors as key determinants of risk, the model emphasises the multifaceted nature of HIV/TB co-management. Integrating these insights into routine care could enable clinicians to identify high-risk cases earlier, thereby improving treatment adherence and survival. Future work must prioritize external validation to confirm the model’s generalizability and reliability before it can be considered for any clinical utility.
