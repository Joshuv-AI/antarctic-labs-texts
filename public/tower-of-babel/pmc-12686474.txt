COVID-19 severity analysis for clinical decision support based on machine learning approach
Authors: Jung Eun Kim, Tobhin Kim, Gerardo Chowell, Hee-Sung Kim, Sunmi Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic has placed immense pressure on global healthcare systems, underscoring the urgent need for early and accurate prediction of disease severity to improve patient care and optimize resource allocation. Failure in ward allocation can lead to wasted hospital resources and inadequate treatment. This study analyzes data from 806 COVID-19 patients admitted to the emergency room of Chungbuk National University Hospital, Korea, between January 2021 and December 2022, to develop machine learning models that predict which patients should be prioritized for intensive care unit (ICU) placement based on initial clinical information. Additionally, two different severity criteria were considered based on actual ICU level interventions (Criterion I) and based on national policy definitions (Criterion II). Single models of logistic regression, random forest, support vector machine, light gradient boosting, and extreme gradient boosting, as well as ensemble learning models using voting classifiers, were used. The ensemble model achieved the best performance, with recall rates of 96.2% and 88.2% for each criterion, respectively. Key features such as glucose level, neutrophil count, high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) level, and albumin level were identified, improving model interpretability. This study provides valuable insights for healthcare professionals to support effective early ward allocation and treatment strategies.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has placed extraordinary pressure on healthcare systems worldwide, emphasizing the urgent need for early and accurate severity prediction to improve patient outcomes and optimize the use of limited medical resources. As the virus spread rapidly, hospitals faced shortages in beds, personnel, ventilators, and respiratory support equipment. In this context, timely and appropriate patient triage, particularly for intensive care unit (ICU) placement, emerged as a critical factor in both clinical outcomes and healthcare system efficiency.

In South Korea, initial public health policy required hospitalization for all confirmed COVID-19 cases. However, as case numbers surged, this approach became unsustainable, prompting a shift toward severity based resource allocation. The government introduced strategic directives to prioritize high-risk patients for ICU care, expedite discharges and inter ward transfers, and support institutions in managing resources according to patient severity1,2.

Improper placement of critically ill patients in general or intermediate care units can lead to missed treatment windows, insufficient monitoring, and increased mortality. Intermediate units typically lack the equipment and expertise needed to manage severe cases. Moreover, delayed ICU transfers demand additional resources and reduce hospital efficiency. Identifying high risk patients early and allocating ICU beds proactively is essential to improving clinical outcomes and preserving system capacity.

This study focuses on patients with semi critical to critical symptoms who visited the emergency department at Chungbuk National University Hospital, a designated high level COVID-19 treatment center. Rather than modeling all COVID-19 cases, we target those for whom early ICU triage is most consequential. At these centers, patients receiving low flow oxygen are typically admitted to intermediate care units, while those requiring high flow oxygen, mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), or renal replacement therapy (RRT) are admitted to ICUs3. As a patient’s condition worsens, inter ward transfers may be needed, introducing logistical challenges and burdening clinical staff. Tools that can predict peak severity at an early stage are therefore crucial for guiding clinical decisions and resource planning.

In Korea, certain hospitals have been designated as high level COVID-19 treatment centers, where bed allocation is severity based. Patients receiving low flow oxygen are typically admitted to intermediate care units (IMUs), while those requiring high flow oxygen, mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), or renal replacement therapy (RRT) are admitted to ICUs3. As a patient’s condition escalates, inter ward transfers become necessary, increasing logistical challenges and burdens on staff. Tools capable of predicting peak severity at an early stage are therefore crucial for improving clinical outcomes and planning ahead.

Between 2021 and 2023, Korea’s national guidelines defined patients requiring high flow nasal cannula or more intensive care as ‘severe’4. However, ICU admission in practice is often reserved for those needing invasive interventions such as mechanical ventilation5. Therefore, defining severity based on actual treatment requirements, rather than administrative policy, may better reflect clinical reality. We analyzed data from COVID-19 patients to assess ward placement based on disease severity using five representative machine learning algorithms, namely logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), light gradient boosting (LGB), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB), along with an ensemble voting classifier. In contrast to prior studies that emphasize model accuracy alone, our work contributes to both methodological development and clinical decision making. By directly comparing predicted ICU needs with actual allocation, evaluating two severity definitions for their practical relevance, and investigating misclassified outcomes, this study bridges the gap between algorithmic prediction and real time hospital operations. Ultimately, we offer a clinically actionable machine learning tool for early severity triage and provide strategic insights for optimizing hospital protocols in future health crises.

Finally, we apply Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to enhance model interpretability and investigate prediction errors. This dual approach yields insights into both model behavior and clinical decision making, with the goal of supporting early triage and optimizing hospital response in future high demand scenarios.

Methods

Study design and features

This study aimed to develop machine learning models to predict which COVID-19 patients arriving at the emergency department (ED) should be admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). The analysis used retrospective data from 806 patients treated at Chungbuk National University Hospital, Korea. All patients were seen at the hospital’s Department of Infectious Diseases between January 2021 and December 2022. The dataset included each patient’s initial clinical information, diagnostic test results, chest X-ray evaluations, and treatment confirmation records.

Candidate variables were first selected based on clinical relevance, informed by previous studies and physician expertise. The main clinical features extracted included sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse rate (PR), respiratory rate (RR), and body temperature (BT). Laboratory diagnostics provided values for albumin, blood glucose, white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count, high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), protein, platelet count, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), and pH. For radiological evaluation, chest X-ray images were assessed by medical professionals and classified based on COVID-19 severity.

We then computed Pearson correlation coefficients to examine redundancy between features. To enhance model stability and interpretability, two main exclusion criteria were applied. First, variables with strong pairwise correlations, defined as Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.8 in absolute value, were removed to minimize multicollinearity. Second, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded to reduce the risk of bias from excessive imputation. These thresholds were determined in line with common practices in clinical predictive modeling. Following expert clinical consultation, implausible physiological or laboratory values were identified and excluded. Severity classification criteria

The severity classification targets used to train the machine learning model were established based on treatment data of hospitalized COVID-19 patients. These severity criteria include factors such as oxygen mask use, high flow oxygen, mechanical ventilation, ECMO, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Two severity criteria were considered to establish severity criteria, which are important criteria for patient bed assignment priority and medical staff assignment. Criterion I is based on utilization of treatment facilities according to patient status, while Criterion II is used by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to classify severe patients. Criterion I determines patients who require a higher level of treatment than Criterion II as severe. Since this data only includes data on patients who visited the emergency room of a university hospital, mild patients are not included, and patients are categorized into semi-severe and severe patients. Data preparation

Data preprocessing in this study included managing missing values, outliers, categorical labels, and data imbalance within the dataset. Missing data were handled through a two step process. First, features with more than 20% missing values were excluded from further analysis to avoid bias due to excessive incompleteness. Among the remaining variables, any patient records containing missing entries were removed to construct a complete case dataset for model training and evaluation. No imputation techniques were applied, as certain clinical features exhibited nonrandom missingness patterns that could compromise model integrity. Outliers were addressed by considering potential human errors, consulting with experts, and examining the data distribution through histograms, box plots, percentiles, and the minimum and maximum values of each categorical feature. Binary variables were encoded using label encoding to ensure consistency and were converted to 0 or 1. After encoding categorical variables into binary form, feature scaling was applied to numeric variables using the minimum and maximum values to align all features to a similar scale21.

This preprocessing step prevents a single feature from disproportionately influencing the modeling process. The preprocessing dataset was initially split into a training set (80%) and a hold-out test set (20%). All model training, including hyperparameter tuning and cross-validation, was performed solely on the training set. Specifically, ten-fold cross-validation was employed within the training data to identify optimal model parameters and prevent overfitting. To address target variable imbalance, synthetic samples were generated using the SMOTE library22. These preprocessing steps ensured that the data were well prepared for model training and increased the reliability and accuracy of the results. To justify the application of SMOTE, we provide the class distribution of severity labels under both Criterion I and Criterion II before synthetic oversampling. These distributions are presented in the Supplementary Materials (see Figs. S1, S2). To evaluate the impact of oversampling, we also trained models under Criterion II without using SMOTE. Despite the relatively balanced class distribution, SMOTE improved recall and F1 score across all single models. The complete results are shown in Supplementary Materials (see Table S1).

Model training

We analyzed the dataset using various machine learning models. These included logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), lightweight gradient boosting (LGB), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), and ensemble learning (EL) models. Logistic regression (LR) is a regression based algorithm that estimates the probability of data belonging to a particular category and classifies it accordingly23. Random forest (RF) is an ensemble method that aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees24. The SVM algorithm utilizes support vectors and hyperplanes for classification25. LGB is a gradient boosting framework that uses tree based learning techniques26. XGB builds weak predictive models sequentially and adjusts errors to form a strong predictive model27.

Voting classifiers, a type of ensemble learning, combine predictions from multiple classifiers using hard or soft voting options to improve overall model performance28. This approach aggregates the most frequently predicted classes to create a stronger classifier. To integrate the outputs of multiple models, we implemented a soft voting ensemble classifier using LR, RF, SVM, LGB and XGB as base learners. In soft voting, the final prediction is based on the average predicted class probabilities across all base models. The ensemble assigned equal importance to each base classifier, with no additional weighting applied during probability aggregation.

Each model was trained using the training set of the dataset. We applied a rigorous 10-fold cross-validation approach to evaluate model performance and perform hyperparameter tuning29. We systematically explored and identified the most effective hyperparameters using the GridSearch library30. Detailed results of hyperparameter tuning for each model are provided in the Supplementary Materials (see Table S2). To improve model interpretability, we performed feature importance and correlation analyses. These analyses helped us understand how various features affect model predictions and develop prevention and treatment strategies. By understanding the most influential features and their relationships, we can develop more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Evaluation of model performance

We rigorously evaluated the performance of six machine learning models (XGB, LGB, SVM, RF, LR, EL) for predicting disease severity in COVID-19 patients. These models were selected based on their established effectiveness in complex clinical data and their diverse strengths in classification tasks. We utilized confusion matrices to summarize the performance of each model by comparing predicted and actual outcomes, which is a valuable tool for assessing classification accuracy31 (see Supplementary Materials Figs. S8, S9). To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we applied a variety of metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score32. By integrating these machine learning models and comprehensive indices evaluation, we developed an effective method for predicting COVID-19 severity, and we can expect improved resource allocation and improved patient conditions based on early detection and treatment of severe patients.

Results

Cohort characteristics

We analyzed data from 806 semi-critical or critical COVID-19 patients who visited the emergency room of Chungbuk National University Hospital. Among these patients, 18.8% were classified as severe according to Criterion I, and 41.1% were classified as severe according to Criterion II. According to Criterion I, the mean age of patients classified as severe and critical was 65.1 ± 19.4 and 67.1 ± 15.7 years, respectively. Under Criterion II, the mean age of severe patients was 63.2 ± 20.1 years, and that of critical patients was 68.7 ± 16.2 years. In addition, 62.7% of critical patients were male according to Criterion I, and 56.8% were male according to Criterion II. Criterion II classified relatively younger and female patients as severe cases.

Model performance

We assessed the performance of five distinct models—Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), LightGBM (LGB), and XGBoost (XGB)—alongside a voting ensemble classifier model (EL) using various evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and ROC-AUC. Recall was particularly emphasized, as it measures the proportion of actual severe cases correctly identified, aligning with the goal of improving ward allocation for severe patients.

Table 2 presents the predictive performance of each model based on Severity Criteria I and II. Among the single models, SVM and LGB achieved the highest recall of 94.6% under Criterion I, highlighting their effectiveness in detecting severe cases. These models also performed well across other metrics. For Criterion II, LGB and XGB achieved the highest recall at 84.9%. The EL model consistently delivered exceptional results across all evaluation metrics for both severity criteria, achieving a recall of 96.2% for Criterion I and 88.2% for Criterion II. These findings suggest that ensemble learning effectively synthesizes the strengths of individual models to enhance predictive robustness.

We also employed Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values, a technique that attributes feature importance to individual input features, to gain deeper insights into how each feature contributes to the model’s predictions33. Given that the LGB model demonstrated the best prediction performance for both Criterion I and Criterion II, we specifically computed SHAP values for the LGB model. Additional details on the SHAP values for the LGB model are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Figs. S13, S14). A mean absolute SHAP values provides a global measure of feature importance by quantifying the average magnitude of each feature’s contribution to the model’s predictions, regardless of direction. Features with higher average SHAP values are considered more influential across the dataset. This representation does not reflect whether a feature increases or decreases the predicted probability for a specific class, but rather how strongly it affects the model output overall.

Additionally, we trained a simplified decision tree classifier using the same feature set to provide an interpretable, rule-based structure for clinical use. The trees constructed under Criterion I and Criterion II are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Figs. S15, S16).

Analysis of misclassification in machine learning and initial ward allocation

To examine misclassification in machine learning and initial ward allocation, we compared the distribution of features based on patient status, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Panels (A) and (B) depict the feature distributions of patients classified according to Criteria I and II, respectively, using the LGB model, which showed the best performance. Blue boxes represent cases where a severe condition was incorrectly classified as moderate (false negatives), while orange boxes represent cases where a severe condition was correctly identified as severe (true positives). In Fig. 4A, BMI was higher in true positive (orange) patients, while SBP and DBP showed similar distributions in both groups. Glucose, neutrophil, and hsCRP levels were also elevated in true positive patients, with other variables displaying comparable distributions. Figure 4B reveals similar trends to (A), with BMI higher in true positive patients and SBP and DBP exhibiting similar patterns. The differences in glucose, neutrophils, and hsCRP distributions were consistent with those in (A), and other variables followed similar trends.

Figure 4c illustrates the distribution of features based on actual patient ward allocation. Blue boxes represent patients who were transferred from an intermediate ward to the ICU, while orange boxes represent patients who were either transferred from the ICU to an intermediate ward or discharged from the ICU or intermediate ward. Differences in glucose, neutrophil, and hsCRP levels were observed between the two groups. Patients transferred to the ICU (blue) had higher BMI, while SBP and DBP exhibited similar distributions across both groups. Glucose, neutrophil, and hsCRP levels were also higher in patients moved to the ICU (blue), with other variables showing either similar distributions or no significant differences.

These misclassification patterns provide meaningful insights into the comparative utility of the two severity criteria. Under Criterion II, false negatives often involved patients who ultimately required advanced interventions (e.g., mechanical ventilation or ECMO), suggesting that the broader definition may obscure important clinical distinctions and reduce model specificity. By contrast, errors under Criterion I were mostly concentrated near the clinical decision boundary and associated with borderline values of physiological or inflammatory markers, such as hsCRP and neutrophil count. This indicates that Criterion I offers a more learnable and clinically coherent target for machine learning algorithms.

Analysis of confusion matrices revealed that, under Criterion II, many false negatives involved patients who eventually required intensive treatments such as mechanical ventilation or ECMO, suggesting that its broader definition may blur distinctions between moderate and severe cases. In contrast, under Criterion I, most misclassifications occurred near the severity threshold, typically involving borderline inflammatory markers like hsCRP and neutrophil counts. This indicates that Criterion I offers clearer clinical boundaries for model prediction.

In addition, we conducted a logistic regression analysis to statistically examine factors associated with false negative misclassifications. Respiratory rate (RR), serum albumin, neutrophil count, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) were identified as significant predictors (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that patients with less prominent respiratory or inflammatory responses are more likely to be incorrectly classified as moderate. Details of the regression results are provided in Supplementary Materials Table S5.

Altogether, these findings emphasize the clinical relevance of specific features in distinguishing between borderline and truly severe cases. Discussion

This study aimed to improve hospital bed allocation and early clinical triage by evaluating the utility of machine learning (ML) models for predicting COVID-19 severity at the time of emergency department (ED) presentation. Using real-world data from Chungbuk National University Hospital, we developed and assessed five individual ML algorithms—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), light gradient boosting machine (LGB), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB)—as well as an ensemble learning (EL) model. Each model was evaluated against two severity classification criteria: Criterion I, based on actual treatment resource requirements (e.g., mechanical ventilation, ECMO), and Criterion II, based on policy guidelines issued by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KDCA).

Our findings that severe and critical cases tended to involve older patients and a higher proportion of males are consistent with previous reports identifying older age and male sex as significant risk factors for severe COVID-1934,35. This demographic pattern further supports the clinical relevance of our severity criteria, particularly Criterion I, which aligns more closely with actual ICU-level interventions.

Across models, Criterion I consistently resulted in superior predictive performance. For example, the ensemble model achieved a recall of 96.2%, accuracy of 94.2%, F1 score of 94.3%, and ROC AUC of 0.98 under Criterion I, outperforming all individual models. SVM and LGB also showed strong performance under Criterion I, whereas models evaluated using Criterion II demonstrated reduced accuracy and recall. These findings suggest that treatment-based criteria such as Criterion I provide a more clinically actionable basis for ML predictions compared to broader policy-based definitions.

Misclassification analysis also played a central role in this study. We compared both machine-predicted severity and real hospital ward assignments to identify trends in false negatives, where severe patients were under-triaged. These analyses revealed that certain features—such as elevated glucose, neutrophil count, and hsCRP levels—were more pronounced in correctly classified severe cases, suggesting that the ML models may capture clinical signals missed by traditional triage. Notably, patients who were misclassified (n = 47) had significantly longer hospital stays than correctly classified patients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 751$$\end{document}), with mean durations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$27.64 \pm 26.96$$\end{document} days versus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$17.20 \pm 22.09$$\end{document} days, respectively (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.0122$$\end{document}, Welch’s t-test). This finding suggests that misclassification may be associated with more complex or prolonged clinical courses and reinforces the importance of accurate early triage.

We also examined key features that contributed to classification performance. Variables such as elevated glucose, neutrophil percentage, and hsCRP levels were more prominent in correctly classified severe cases. These findings indicate that ML models can detect subtle clinical signals that might be overlooked during routine triage, supporting their role as augmentative tools in real-time decision-making.

These findings are also consistent with and extend prior ML-based severity prediction studies. Earlier work has predicted COVID 19 severity using chest CT and clinical variables, as well as blood test based clinical features, and has compared algorithm performance across methods such as logistic regression, random forest, and support vector machines. Studies have also examined individual algorithms and diverse settings and populations, including home quarantined cohorts and initial hospitalization labs. Our findings are consistent with this literature but extend it by training against treatment based labels and linking predictions directly to bed allocation, which emphasizes clinical actionability for ICU triage.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the extended data collection period (2021–2022) coincided with the emergence of various SARS-CoV-2 variants such as Delta and Omicron. These evolving viral strains may have influenced the clinical presentation and progression of disease, thereby affecting model performance. Unfortunately, the dataset did not permit subgroup analyses by time period, age group, or variant, limiting our ability to assess the generalizability of results across pandemic phases. Future studies should incorporate variant-specific features or period-stratified datasets to improve model robustness.

Another notable limitation is that this was a single-center study, which may restrict the generalizability of findings to other hospital systems with different triage protocols, care capacities, and patient demographics. Multicenter studies across diverse regions would help validate the scalability of the proposed models and determine the degree to which performance is affected by institutional variations.

In summary, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of ML models, especially those aligned with treatment-based severity criteria, in predicting COVID-19 severity and supporting hospital bed allocation decisions. Models trained under Criterion I consistently outperformed those based on policy-driven standards, offering a more accurate and clinically relevant approach to triage. The integration of these interpretable ML tools into hospital systems holds strong potential for improving care accuracy, easing resource constraints, and enhancing outcomes in future pandemics or high-demand clinical scenarios.
