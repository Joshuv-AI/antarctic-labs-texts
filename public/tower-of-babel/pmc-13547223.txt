Quantitative RNA modification mapping by mass spectrometry with isobaric tags and nucleobase fragment analysis
Authors: Junzhou Wu, Jingjing Sun, Yifeng Yuan, Shiuan Nee Goh, Hazel Chay, Dwijapriya, Valérie de Crécy-Lagard, Peter C. Dedon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA modifications regulate RNA stability, translation, stress responses, and disease processes, yet their function remains poorly understood due to technical limitations in sequence analysis. Here, we present an RNA-specific isobaric tandem mass tagging (RMT) platform for omic-scale quantitative mapping of RNA modifications. The platform combines RNA-specific tags adapted from proteomics with an end-to-end workflow spanning sample preparation through data processing. Validation using synthetic oligonucleotides and total tRNA from Pseudomonas aeruginosa yielded reproducible quantification, with coefficients of variation below 5%. Together with nucleobase fragment analysis, we identified and quantified 24 RNA modifications in PA14 tRNAs, including previously undescribed m2A38 and Gm/Cm39, and assigned their corresponding writer enzymes. Further analyses of tRNAs from writer knockout strains and stressed cells revealed dynamic modification patterns, modification interdependencies, and their potential roles in stress adaptation. This method provides a robust, cost-effective platform for quantitative RNA modification mapping, enabling deeper biological insights.

Introduction

Research in the realm of the epitranscriptome—the set of >170 post-transcriptional modifications of all forms of RNA—has unveiled the significant impact of RNA modifications on a wide range of molecular and cellular functions1–8, including RNA stability, translation efficiency, stress responses, and cellular differentiation. The epitranscriptome regulates protein synthesis through multiple mechanisms. For instance, modifications in messenger RNA (mRNA) can affect transcript processing, translation, and degradation9,10; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) modifications influence ribosome biogenesis and function11; and transfer RNA (tRNA) modifications are integral to maintaining proper structural conformation, codon recognition, and translation fidelity, particularly during stress responses6,7. Aberrant patterns of RNA modification are increasingly recognized as key contributors to disease pathology12, including neurological disorders13, cancers14,15, and immune-related diseases4. Furthermore, RNA modifications have also been implicated in cellular responses to drug exposures and chemoresistance16. Beyond the naturally occurring RNA modifications, RNA-based drugs are modified to improve drug potency and reduce the cell immune response17,18. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which most RNA modifications function in cellular and disease processes remain poorly understood.

To fully comprehend the function of the epitranscriptome, a variety of technologies have been developed to detect, quantify, and localize RNA modifications. Among these, RNA modification mapping by next-generation short-read sequencing (NGS) is now well-developed in dozens of methods relying on antibody enrichment, chemical derivatization, or misincorporation/stop events during reverse transcription to localize individual modifications19. Nanopore or third-generation direct sequencing is emerging as the key technology for capturing modifications in long RNA sequences by exploiting changes in electrical current during RNA translocation through the nanopore20. NGS-based methods, which involve RNA fragmentation, can provide transcriptome-scale readouts and, when coupled with modification-specific chemistry or enzymes, enable sensitive detection and mapping of selected RNA modifications. However, NGS and nanopore sequencing are constrained to a limited number of RNA modifications and cannot account for all 50 or more modifications in human tRNA. Thus, the demand for a quantitative and chemically accurate tRNA modification mapping method remains unmet21.

In contrast, mass spectrometry (MS) provides highly chemically specific information about RNA modifications22. MS analysis of ribonucleosides released from hydrolyzed RNA offers highly sensitive quantification of modifications23,24 but sacrifices information about location. Recent advancements in high-resolution tandem MS of partially hydrolyzed RNA have significantly improved our ability to localize RNA modifications within RNA fragments and their assignment to reference RNA sequences, which we refer to here as RNA sequencing and modification mapping25–27. Like all RNA sequencing technologies, MS-based RNA sequencing relies on translating MS machine signals into predicted sequences and aligning those sequences with reference datasets to identify the RNA species. MS offers the additional advantage of chemically identifying RNA modifications in the sequenced fragment. However, extending these approaches from modification mapping to quantitative comparison of site-specific modification levels across samples, which we refer to here as quantitative RNA modification mapping, has posed challenges. Classical label-free RNA quantification is susceptible to run-to-run variability, which is worse for capillary and nano-liquid chromatography (LC) systems than for analytical columns, as well as effects arising from differences in charge state and salt adduct formation25. Stable-isotope labeling strategies have therefore been developed to improve RNA quantification28. However, the mass difference between labeled and unlabeled RNA fragments separates them in MS1, making it impossible to fragment and analyze them together in the same MS/MS spectrum, thus obviating accurate quantification. Additionally, small mass differences (1–2 Da) may not effectively distinguish labeled from unlabeled RNA fragments due to the overlap of isotopologue peaks, while isotope back-exchange can further undermine quantitative precision.

To overcome the limitations of these approaches, we considered the obvious parallels in MS-based bottom-up or shotgun proteomics that provides broad and quantitative coverage of any proteome. The first step of fragmenting proteins to MS-manageable peptides is a critical balance between protease sequence specificity and the need to maximize peptide coverage. The choice of label-free analysis or isotope labeling by SILAC spiking or isobaric tags (e.g., iTRAQ, TMT) is then based on both budget and the need for quantitative analysis, with the former costing less for sample preparation but requiring analysis of individual samples, while the latter provides greater quantitative precision as well as multiplexing for large numbers of samples or conditions29–31. In either case, resolution of the proteolyzed peptides by reversed-phase LC is important to decrease the complexity of the mixture entering the MS, which must be a high-resolution instrument, such as a quadrupole time-of-flight or Orbitrap. Finally, specialized software is required to (1) deconvolute the thousands of collision-induced dissociation (CID) spectra arising from LC run, (2) identify the amino acid sequence and secondary modifications, (3) match the peptides with database to identify the protein, and (4) perform quantitative analysis of the detected proteins. However, despite the widespread adoption of label-based quantitative strategies in proteomics, analogous approaches have not yet been implemented for bottom-up RNA modification analysis.

Here, we applied the principles of MS-based shotgun proteomics to RNA sequencing and modification mapping in the tRNA pool from Pseudomonas aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14. Based on iTRAQ and TMT isobaric tandem mass tags, we designed a set of RNA-specific mass tags (RMTs) for quantitative RNA modification mapping and used these tags in an optimized end-to-end pipeline that integrates sample preparation, LC-MS/MS data acquisition, and customized tandem MS/MS data processing, combining nucleobase fragment-based modification identification with tag-enabled quantification. Applying this platform to PA14 tRNA demonstrated highly precise site-specific quantification (<5% CoV) of 22 modifications in 65 out of 66 predicted tRNAs, including two additional target sites for known modifications and their corresponding modifying enzymes. Quantitative analyses of strains from PA14 gene knockout library and cells cultured in varying growth media revealed the dynamic nature of tRNA modifications, uncovered interdependencies among modification pathways, provided deeper insights into writer enzyme function, and highlighted specific regulatory responses associated with environmental stress adaptation.

Results

An end-to-end pipeline for quantitative RNA sequencing and modification mapping

The concept of isobaric mass tag-based quantitative RNA modification mapping is illustrated in Fig. 1. Each digested RNA sample is labelled with a unique RMT tag, analogous to the iTRAQ or TMT proteomics tags. These tags have identical total molecular weights but differ in the isotopic composition of their balancer and reporter groups, where the balancer group (shown in grey) compensates for mass differences introduced into the reporter group (colored) so that all labeled species remain isobaric at the MS1 level while generating distinct reporter ions upon fragmentation. During MS/MS analysis of the pooled sample, identical RNA fragments uniquely tagged for each sample co-elute and appear as a single precursor in the MS1 spectrum. The resulting MS2 spectrum contains both RNA backbone fragment ions, which are used for sequence and modification analysis, and reporter ions, which quantify the contribution of each original sample to that RNA species. To adapt isobaric tandem mass tags from proteomics to epitranscriptomics, we optimized each step of the end-to-end pipeline for quantitative RNA analysis. First, RNA digestion was performed quantitatively using RNase T1, a highly G-specific RNase, in combination with recombinant shrimp alkaline phosphatase (rSAP) to remove 3′ phosphates. Importantly, no G mis-cleavage events were detected in any analysis, except in the presence of G-related modifications, such as Q, Gm, and m7G. Secondly, RMTs were designed as bifunctional molecules comprising an RNA-labelling group and an isobaric tandem mass tag. The RNA-labelling component uses a 3′-end-labelling strategy employing cytidine 3′,5′-bisphosphate (pCp) and T4 RNA ligase, enabling highly efficient enzymatic tagging of RNA32–34. The mass tag portion of the RMT employs established proteomic TMT-6plex and iTRAQ-4plex isobaric tags. Following labelling, RNA fragments were separated using capillary ion-pair reversed-phase chromatography and subsequently ionized using a nanospray source to enable high-sensitivity mass spectrometric detection. The TMT- and iTRAQ-derived RMTs require the use of positive ion mode to allow quantification of the reporter ions. Due to the lower collision energy needed for the RNA backbone compared to the peptide backbone and the fact that iTRAQ and TMT tags are designed to fragment at higher peptide energies, stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) was used during data-dependent MS/MS acquisition to account for the different fragmentation energies, applying with low NCE values (15 and 17) to promote RNA fragmentation and a high NCE (50) to induce dissociation of the isobaric tags derived from proteomics. An isolation window of 2 Th was used to accommodate the isotopic envelope of multiply charged RNA oligonucleotide precursors (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). MS data were processed using a customized OpenMS pipeline that integrated the NucleicAcidSearchEngine (NASE)25 for RNA modification mapping with pCp-iTRAQ treated as a customized modification and the IsobaricAnalyzer module for tag quantification (Supplementary Fig. 2), along with custom R scripts for downstream data processing. In our data processing workflow, sodium adduct species, such as those observed in Fig. 1, were managed in the OpenMS sequence alignment framework by subtracting sodium from MS1 and checking if the new MS1 and related MS2 match to any RNA fragments. A successfully identified adduct peak will be counted as a matched spectrum.

To assess the performance of the labels in LC-MS analysis, equivalent amounts of synthetic 69-nt RNA oligonucleotides were digested with RNase T1 (Supplementary Fig. 5a) and labelled individually with each of the pCp-TMT-6plex or pCp-iTRAQ-4plex tags. The labelled tags were then combined with others from the same series and analyzed by LC-MS/MS (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Reporter ion intensities were extracted and plotted against retention times. A clear, fragment size-dependent retention time shift was observed between RNA fragments labelled with different tags, for example, a 16-nt digested fragment labelled with pCp-TMT 1/3/5 eluted approximately 10 s slower than the same fragment labelled with pCp-TMT 2/4/6 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). This retention time shift introduced substantial bias in reporter ion-based quantification, resulting in a coefficient of variation (CoV) of up to 33% within a single spectrum (Fig. 2c) and causing inaccurate quantification of RNA fragments (Supplementary Fig. 5b, d). This different LC behavior reflects an isotope effect, with the pCp-TMT 1/3/5 tags labelled with [15N] at the amide, while pCp-TMT 2/4/6 tags have [15N] at the tertiary amine. Our results agree with a previous study35 reporting that [15N]-labelled peptides eluted approximately 2 s earlier than their unlabelled counterparts and indicating that 15N isotope labelling can influence retention time. The more pronounced shift observed in our study with TMT-based tags may be attributed to the high polarity of the oligonucleotide backbone combined with the hydrophobicity of the isobaric mass tag, which enhances the tag's contribution to overall retention. This likely amplifies the effect of 15N incorporation, resulting in a greater retention time shift than typically observed with peptides. In contrast, pCp-iTRAQ-4plex labelling, without [15N] in the amide, yields identical retention times across all labelled RNAs, with a CoV of less than 8% (Fig. 2d). This consistency is particularly important in high-throughput omics studies, where quantification often depends on only a single spectrum or a small number of spectra. Therefore, the pCp-iTRAQ-4plex tag was selected for further analyses.

We then calculated fold-change values derived from iTRAQ reporter ion intensities for 7 RNA fragments ranging from 5 to 16 nt. The results revealed that all fragments were identified and quantified in all samples, achieving a CoV of less than 10% for spectra with iTRAQ signals exceeding 2 × 105 (Supplementary Fig. 5c) and a CoV of less than 1.3% for fragment quantification across 3 technical replicates (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, the iTRAQ2/3/4 signals demonstrated strong linear regression with the iTRAQ1 signals, yielding slopes of 0.998, 1.010, and 1.006, respectively, and R2 values exceeding 0.998 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). These results confirm the robustness of the pipeline for RNA quantification with minimal variability.

Application of RMTs to quantify tRNAs from Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14

To evaluate the performance of pCp-iTRAQ RMT tags with complex biological samples, the pipeline was applied to small RNAs (<200 nt; ~85% tRNA) isolated from Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14. The PA14 tRNA pool comprises 66 predicted mature tRNAs36 with 41 identified modifications24. Small RNA was aliquoted into four tubes and digested with RNase T1, with the RNA fragments in each tube labelled with a different pCp-iTRAQ tag. All four labelled RNA samples were pooled, and 1 µg of the combined sample was subjected to LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic performance across the run, including representative peak widths, is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. An RNase T1-digested reference library was generated using the OpenMS RNADigestor module, allowing for one missed cleavage, with a manually appended pCp-iTRAQ tag at the 3′-end of each fragment (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A total of 149 RNA fragments were identified using NucleicAcidSearchEngine (NASE) with mapping score cut-off of 50. These account for 73% of the RNase T1-digested products that are ≥5 nt and provide 41% sequence coverage across the total tRNA pools. Of these, 103 fragments could be mapped back to a specific tRNA or isodecoder. In all three replicates, 78 fragments were consistently detected, and 110 fragments were observed in at least two replicates (Fig. 3a). These mapped fragments originated from 65 out of 66 predicted tRNAs in PA14, except Arg-ACG-2, which was also not detected by tRNA sequencing37. All 4320 matched spectra exhibited iTRAQ signals with an average intensity of 2.2 × 105. No reporter-ion signals were detected in label-free RNA LC–MS/MS runs, confirming the absence of background ions in the iTRAQ mass range (Supplementary Fig. 7). Of these, 3777 spectra had a CoV of <10% among the iTRAQ-4plex signals, while the remaining 543 spectra with a CoV >10% showed a lower average iTRAQ intensity of 3.8 × 104. Similar to the results with synthetic oligonucleotides, the iTRAQ2/3/4 signals demonstrated strong linear regression with the iTRAQ1 signals, yielding slopes of 0.995, 0.996, and 0.995, respectively, and R2 values exceeding 0.995 (Fig. 3b). Among the 78 fragments detected in all three replicates, 98% of the calculated fold-change values fall within the range of 0.95–1.05, and 96% of the CoVs were below 5% (Fig. 3c). Modification landscape of PA14 tRNAs

With the RMT labelling and LC-MS parameters established, we proceeded to develop and refine the data processing and analysis features of the pipeline (Fig. 1a) to map RNA modifications. The modification profile was extracted from iTRAQ-labelled wild-type PA14 tRNA sequencing and modification mapping data noted above, revealing 24 modifications localized across 58 RNA fragments. Among these, 41 fragments contained a single modification, 15 had two modifications, and two exhibited three modifications (Supplementary Data 1). The majority (67%) of these modifications were located in the anticodon loop, followed by the D loop and T loop (Fig. 4a, b). Many PA14 tRNAs have anticodons that span positions 33–35 or 35–3736. For consistency, we refer to all anticodon positions using the standard 34–36 numbering, except in Fig. 4b. In this analysis, we identified two modification sites not previously characterized in bacteria: m2A at position 38 and Gm/Cm at position 39 (Fig. 4a, b). One notable observation was the presence of multiple members of the same modification family in a single tRNA isoacceptor population. This was particularly apparent for position 37. Here, we observed that tRNACysGCA, tRNAPheGAA, and tRNASerCGA all possessed varying proportions of i6A, io6A, ms2i6A, and ms2io6A at position 37 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 1). Less surprising was the presence of both t6A and ct6A in tRNAAsnGUU and tRNAMetCAU, with the caveat that ct6A is unstable to ring opening to t6A and to epimerization to a hydantoin form under alkaline conditions during RNA isolation and processing38. Modification stoichiometry was estimated from MS1 peak areas of RNA fragments carrying different modification states and should therefore be interpreted as semi-quantitative. These results elucidate the conserved and species-specific nature of tRNA modifications in PA14, as well as the potential to tune tRNA function by varying the position 37 modification.

One of the major challenges faced by MS-based modification analysis is distinguishing isobaric modifications, such as mono-methylations of the four canonical ribonucleotides. Although the pair of a and a-B ions have traditionally been used to differentiate nucleobase versus sugar methylation, their frequent absence from MS/MS spectra often hampers the unambiguous identification of specific modification types. In this study, we observed that the presence of nucleobase ions and their fragments at low mass range is highly useful for distinguishing isobaric modifications. Base methylation can be easily distinguished from sugar methylation based on the presence or absence of methylated base ions, such as m1G/m7G vs Gm (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, some nucleobase methylation isomers can be further distinguished by matching fragmentation patterns of nucleosides. For example, m2A, but not m6A, is prone to losing ammonia (NH3), yielding a 133 Da fragment that is absent in m6A-containing sequence (Fig. 4d, e). In addition, nucleobase ions provide great confidence in confirming sequence modifications, complementing conventional mapping algorithms (Supplementary Fig. 9). These findings highlight the use of nucleobase ions to accurately distinguish RNA modifications, reduce misassignments, and improve the reliability of MS-based identification approaches.

RNA modification sites introduced by uncharacterized enzymes

The preceding RNA modification mapping process revealed several notable observations. In addition to conserved tRNA modification sites, two additional modification patterns were identified in PA14: m2A at position 38 and Gm/Cm at position 39 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 1). While the m2A modification is typically found at position 37 in E. coli and other species, it also appears at position 38 in PA14 in three specific tRNAs adjacent to ms2i6A/ms2io6A at position 37: tRNAPheGAA, tRNASerCGA, and tRNATrpCCA. In our previous studies, we discovered two distinct tRNA m2A writers in PA14: PA14_14830, which are homologous to E. coli RlmN, and PA14_40730, a tRNA-specific enzyme that we designated as TrmV24. To link these enzymes to the observed modifications at positions 37 and 38, isodecoders were isolated from strains lacking RlmN and TrmV and subjected to modification quantification by mass spectrometry (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 10). The results confirmed that RlmN is the m2A methyltransferase for position 37, while TrmV is responsible for m2A at position 38. In Pseudomonas and representative bacteria, trmV co-occurs with genes encoding RlmN and ms2i6A/ms2io6A37 biosynthetic enzymes, MiaAB (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Functionally, knockout of miaB led to the loss of both m2A and ms2i6A, whereas knockout of miaE had no effect on m2A levels (Fig. 5b). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of the ‘U[ms2i6A]AC’ sequence in complex with the predicted TrmV structure revealed that residues YPIQ243–246 and FAV273–275 form a well-defined binding pocket that specifically accommodates the ms2i6A/ms2io6A base (Fig. 5c). Meanwhile, residues Met150 and Cys101 were positioned near the C2 atom of the adenine base, suggesting their potential role in catalyzing the m2A modification. In addition, the ms2i6A modification weakens the A37(N6) with U33 hydrogen bond, which leads to an open-loop conformation of the anticodon loop39, thereby allowing TrmV to access and modify A38 position (Supplementary Fig. 11c). The identification of Gm and Cm at position 39 in PA14 aligns with our previous findings on the non-orthologous displacement of TrmH by PA14_68100, which we designated as TrmS24. While TrmH typically catalyzes the addition of Gm at position 18, TrmS instead modifies position 39 with either Gm or Cm in tRNAGlnUUG and tRNAGluUUC (Fig. 5a, b). Knockout of TrmS resulted in a complete loss of Gm and a ~25% reduction in Cm in total tRNA quantification24, indicating that PA14 lacks a TrmH homolog and that all Gm modifications are confined to position 39. Docking and MD simulation of TrmS in complex with tRNAGluUUC and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) revealed a positively charged groove/concave surface that can accommodate tRNA anticodon loop. Notably, the 2′-hydroxyl group of G39 is positioned in close proximity to the S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine (SAH) ligand and an arginine residue R18 (Fig. 5d), consistent with a catalytically competent conformation for TrmH40. Both TrmS and TrmH contain a SAM-dependent methyltransferase domain, but they share less than 30% sequence similarity and less than 5% structural similarity. TrmS proteins are predominantly found in Pseudomonas with the absence of TrmH (Supplementary Fig. 12). It is evolutionarily distinct from the canonical 2′-O-methyltransferases TrmH and RlmB, yet they are functionally convergent in tRNA modification pathways24.

Quantitative mapping of tRNA modifications in PA14 strains lacking synthetic enzymes

The results to this point establish the utility of our complete pipeline of iTRAQ labelling, RNase T1 digestion, ion-pair LC, and MS data processing using nucleobase ions to quantitatively map RNA modifications in complex biological mixtures. We next applied the pipeline to quantify the effects on the tRNA pool in PA14 caused by knocking out six genes for RNA-modifying enzymes: CmoB, TrmA, TcdA, MnmCD, MiaA, and MiaB involved in the synthesis of cmo5U, m5U, ct6A, mnm5s2U, i6A, and ms2i6A, respectively. For this analysis, samples were organized into two 4-plex groups, with tRNAs from the wild-type PA14 serving as the internal control. Both groups were analysed in biological triplicate, and the quantification results were normalized against wild-type tRNA. A total of 171 RNA fragments were detected, of which 58 fragments contained tRNA modifications (Fig. 6a). Loss of writer enzymes resulted in the expected loss of the corresponding modifications and an increase in either unmodified sequences (ΔtrmA, ΔmiaA) (Supplementary Data 2) or modification intermediates (ΔcmoB, ΔtcdA, ΔmnmCD, and ΔmiaB) (Fig. 6a). In addition to modifications detected in wild-type PA14, two additional modifications were identified in the knockout strains: ho5U in ΔcmoB and nm5s2U in ΔmnmCD. The results also suggested a dependency among different modifications. Considering Cm34, loss of ΔmiaA caused Cm34 to drop to 5% of wild-type levels in the tRNASerCGA fragment “AUU[Cm]G”, while the unmodified fragment “AUUCG” increased 3.5-fold and other fragments from tRNASerCGA remained unchanged (Supplementary Data 2). In the ΔmiaB strain, there was a 30% reduction in tRNASerCGA Cm34 when i6A modifications were present (Fig. 6a). In contrast, Cm32 levels in tRNATrpCCA remained unaffected by knockout of miaA/B/E (Fig. 5b). Together, these results indicate that TrmL recognises i6A modifications and methylates C34 (Fig. 6b), in agreement with previous studies41,42. Another observation is the increase of fragments with i6A and io6A in the ΔcmoB and ΔmnmCD strains, especially for the fragment ‘AA[i6A/io6A]AUCCCCG’ from tRNAPheGAA in the ΔcmoB strain. Codon-usage analysis shows that PA14 miaB gene is enriched in GUC/GUG (Val) and AAG (Lys) codons relative to the genome average (Supplementary Fig. 13a). GUC and GUG are primarily decoded by tRNAValUAC, as evidenced by tRNA sequencing37, which carries cmo5U, while AAG is decoded solely by tRNALysUUU that contains mnm5s2U. Both modifications are critical for efficient decoding43,44. Therefore, in the ΔcmoB and ΔmnmCD strains, miaB translation is likely impaired, potentially explaining the increased i6A/io6A levels (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Quantitative mapping of nutrient limitation-induced changes in tRNA modifications

To further assess the performance of the MS-based modification mapping platform, we compared tRNAs extracted from P. aeruginosa PA14 cultured in rich LB medium compared to M9 minimal medium (M9). Here, we identified and quantified a total of 109 RNA fragments, with 90 fragments displaying a CoV <10%. Figure 6c shows a volcano plot illustrating fold-change versus false discovery rate, revealing distinct differences in the modification patterns for the LB and M9 growth conditions. Notably, fragments containing i6A/io6A at position 37 were increased in minimal medium, whereas those containing ms2i6A/ms2io6A showed a corresponding reduction. This oligonucleotide behavior was corroborated by LC-MS/MS analysis of individual ribonucleosides (Supplementary Fig. 14). Interestingly, the fragment ‘AA[i6A/io6A]AUCCCCG’ from tRNAPheGAA showed the largest increase in modification level among all tRNAs, similar to that observed in ΔcmoB strain (Fig. 6a). This pattern is consistent with reduced MiaB activity in minimal medium, possibly due to limited iron availability required for the MiaB Fe–S cluster, or less likely a cryptic desulfurase. It is interesting that there are different levels of fragments containing i6A family members, which is consistent with tRNA-dependent activity of MiaB, a cryptic desulfurase, or a ribonuclease. Also interesting is the 1.8-fold increase in the fragment ‘CCCU[mnm5s2U]UCAC[Gm]G’ from tRNAGluUUC under M9 conditions, while other fragments from tRNAGluUUC changed by less than 1.3-fold (Fig. 6c). Again, LC-MS/MS quantification of mnm5s2U corroborated this behavior (Supplementary Fig. 14). The synthesis of mnm5s2U involves MnmEG and MnmC using either glycine or ammonium pathways (Fig. 6d). The glycine-dependent pathway has been reported to predominate when cells are cultured in minimal medium45. This observation raises the possibility that M9 medium may enhance mnm5s2U formation by increasing flux through this pathway. These observations lead to a variety of mechanistic hypotheses and opportunities for experimental validation.

Discussion

Here, we have demonstrated the power and utility of a bottom-up LC-MS/MS-based RNA sequencing and modification mapping workflow using RNA-specific isobaric tandem mass tags for quantitative modification mapping. By adapting tandem mass tag technology originally developed for proteomics, this method effectively addresses critical limitations of previous isotopic and label-free RNA analysis approaches25,28, including retention-time bias and limited quantitative accuracy. Indeed, the precision of our label-based analysis of a complex mixture of RNAs digested with RNase T1 (R2 > 0.995; n = 1080) exceeded that for a label-free analysis of a low-complexity sample of in vitro-transcribed yeast lncRNA (340 nt; lacking RNA modifications) also digested with RNase T1 (R2 ~ 0.94; n = 25)25. However, because these datasets differ in sample composition, experimental design, and LC-MS conditions, this comparison should be interpreted as contextual evidence of within-workflow reproducibility rather than a direct benchmark against label-free quantification. In addition, limitations of isobaric mass tagging, including reporter-ion crosstalk and co-isolation interference, have been well characterized in proteomics and may constrain quantitative accuracy and dynamic range46,47. Thus, further systematic evaluation using known-ratio spike-in under matched LC-MS conditions will be required to define the dynamic range and relative performance of RMT-based and label-free workflows. A key advantage of this technique is the direct and accurate identification of RNA modifications by molecular weight—an ongoing challenge for methods dependent on indirect signals, such as reverse transcription misincorporation or termination in NGS and ionic current alterations during strand translocation in nanopore sequencing. Additionally, direct RNA analysis circumvents biases associated with adapter ligation and PCR amplification during sample preparation, enhancing quantitative rigor. Importantly, our pipeline uses commercially available reagents and open-source software (OpenMS) for data analysis, providing immediate and broad accessibility for laboratories and expanding opportunities for advanced research in RNA biology.

To bring the concept of isobaric mass tagging from proteomics into RNA analysis, we built and rigorously optimized a complete, end-to-end pipeline—from sample preparation through data analysis. To enable efficient RNA labelling, the mass tags were incorporated into a classic pCp scaffold, serving as an optimal substrate for T4 RNA ligase. This approach achieved high-yield, quantitative labelling using a 100-fold molar excess of pCp over RNA. Notably, compared to chemical labelling in proteomics—which typically requires around 2 µmol (0.8 mg) of isobaric tags per reaction—the enzymatic labelling approach required only 10 nmol of the pCp substrate, reducing reagent cost and enhancing scalability for RNA applications. One drawback of the isobaric tags developed for proteomics was found to be isotope-based retention time shift for RNA oligonucleotides labelled with the TMT tags. This isotope effect was observed to a small extent with peptides35 but caused up to 10 s retention time differences with the anionic homogeneous RNA polymer. This observation is a critical issue for the design of RNA-specific tags as well as for optimizing the use of existing proteomics tags—such as separating the C and N series of TMTpro reagents—for RNA applications. Moreover, the use of the proteomics tags required that RNA analyses be performed in positive mode. Previous studies48,49 have reported that positive mode provides higher sensitivity, a more concentrated charge distribution, and reduced contaminant interference compared to negative mode for RNA analysis. Furthermore, due to the higher collision energy required by proteomics tags compared to the RNA backbone, stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE) was required during RNA analysis. SNCE not only facilitated accurate quantification by mass tag fragmentation but also produced more informative modified nucleobase fragments, aiding mapping confirmation and even distinguishing isobaric modifications such as m6A from m2A (Fig. 4b) and m1G/m7G from Gm (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, while diagnostic MS/MS ions with mass accuracy of ≤5 ppm support a structural assignment for most modifications, the data do not definitively establish structural identity in all cases. Additional confirmation using authentic standards or orthogonal evidence may therefore be required for definitive structural assignments. Nonetheless, a substantial fraction of the fragmentation time is devoted to generating reporter-ion signals, highlighting the need to develop RNA-specific isobaric tags that are compatible with the lower energy of RNA fragmentation.

Owing to the high density of modifications, tRNA encodes information far beyond its primary nucleotide sequence. Such epigenetic codes are more broadly accessible by site-specific quantitative mapping of RNA modifications in a population of tRNAs without a priori knowledge of the identity of modifications at conserved locations in tRNA, as is required for Nanopore sequencing50. This was demonstrated in the analyses of PA14 WT and tRNA modifying enzyme knock-out strains, as well as cells cultured in LB and M9 minimal medium. A total of 24 modifications were quantitatively mapped across 58 RNA fragments, together with over 100 unmodified fragments. In addition to known established modification sites, MS-based analysis of WT tRNAs revealed m2A at position 38 and Gm/Cm at position 39 installed by TrmV and TrmS, respectively. These positions have not been previously reported to carry modifications in bacteria and may contribute to shaping the anticodon stem-loop structure39,51,52 to influence codon–anticodon recognition. Genomic analysis indicates that both genes are largely restricted to Pseudomonas, potentially reflecting phylum-specific traits such as broad environmental adaptability and diverse host associations. Further studies are required to elucidate their precise biological roles.

More subtle modification information not accessible by any other method involved the observation that i6A, io6A, ms2i6A, and ms2io6A all exist at position 37 in the same tRNA isoacceptor population. With i6A-based modifications apparently restricted to tRNAs reading UNN codons in bacteria (Cys, Leu, Phe, Ser, Trp, and Tyr)53, there is a rich information system here with variations in four modifications on six tRNAs along with variable levels of the tRNAs. Deciphering this epitranscriptomic code can be achieved with a combination of high-throughput phenotypic profiling of modifications and tRNA levels coupled with machine learning models that account for the other i6A family behaviors we observed.

The degree of regulatory control exerted by i6A-based modifications becomes even more complicated by the fact that they are modulated by modifications at other sites in tRNA. Application to six knockout strains confirmed the accuracy of the mapping results and provided additional insights into modification dependencies, for example, the loss of i6A/io6A37 led to loss of Cm34, while the loss of cmo5U34 resulted in an increase in i6A/io6A37. Further comparison of cells cultured in M9 minimal medium revealed an increase in i6A/io6A37 accompanied by a decrease in ms2i6A/ms2io6A37 in many tRNAs, likely due to reduced MiaB activity, which is sensitive to oxidative stress. In both ΔcmoB and M9 conditions, the i6A/io6A37 modification in tRNAPheGAA was most affected compare with other tRNAs. Structural analyses of MiaB indicate that it engages in extensive interactions with approximately 10 nucleotides within the anticodon stem-loop region of its tRNA substrates54. This suggests that tRNAPheGAA may be particularly sensitive to reduced MiaB activity due to suboptimal binding affinity for its stem-loop structure. In addition, the data revealed two modification dependencies: m2A38 formation depends on TrmV binding to the methylthio group of ms2i6A/ms2io6A37, while Cm34 formation depends on TrmL binding to the isopentenyl/hydroxyisopentenyl group of i6A/io6A37 (Fig. 6b). Together, these results highlight a central role of MiaABE in regulating modification dynamics and underscore the need for caution when interpreting position 37 modifications due to their interdependencies55.

Furthermore, under M9 conditions, an increase in mnm5s2U levels was observed, which may be attributed to enhanced activity of the MnmEG glycine-dependent pathway. mnm5s2U has been previously shown to influence the expression of genes involved in virulence, promoting a proteomic shift toward pathogenic traits and physiologically adaptive states56. This increase in mnm5s2U may represent a mechanism by which cells modulate tRNA modification patterns to reprogram gene expression in response to environmental stress, such as nutrient limitation, as we have observed previously7,57,58.

There are several limitations beyond the use of isobaric mass tags that hinder the broader application of MS-based RNA modification mapping. One major challenge is achieving high mapping coverage, which is inherently constrained by the limited sequence diversity of the four native RNA bases compared to that for 20 amino acids in proteomics-based peptide analysis. As a result, longer RNA fragments are required for high-confidence mapping to reference sequences. Recent efforts to address this have led to the development of alternative RNases, such as RNase MC159, RNase Cusativin59, and human RNase 427, as well as partial digestion protocols26,60. Nevertheless, for quantitative applications, RNase T1 remains the only enzyme capable of generating highly consistent and reproducible digestion patterns across samples prior to labelling. However, the short fragments generated by RNase T1 limit sequencing and mapping coverage and reduce the ability to distinguish closely related tRNA isodecoders. Another challenge arises from the reliance on ion-pairing reagents to achieve chromatographic resolution of oligonucleotide sizes. Ion-pairing agents are well known to contaminate LS-MS systems and introduce low molecular-weight noise into the MS readout. This contamination restricts the use of advanced proteomics platforms for RNA analysis, thereby limiting achievable sensitivity and data quality as well as restricting the use of the LC-MS system. Here, it will be critical to develop HILIC or other chromatographic solid phase systems that provide adequate oligonucleotide resolution without ion-pairing agents. Additionally, specialized software tools are urgently needed to facilitate the identification of RNA modifications through nucleobase fragment ions, accurately distinguish between isobaric modifications or sequence isomers, and effectively integrate quantitative data with sequence annotations. Overcoming these hurdles will require integrated analytical and enzymatic innovations, including the development of two-base-specific RNases, robust ion-pairing reagent-free LC methods, and advanced computational algorithms tailored specifically for RNA mass spectrometry data analysis, coupled with orthogonal RNA-seq tools (e.g., NGS and nanopore).

In light of the rapid expansion in understanding the biological functions and pharmaceutical applications of RNA modifications, we anticipate a growing demand for high-throughput, quantitative, and chemically accurate RNA modification analysis platforms. This omics-style quantification approach presented here, combined with ongoing technological advancements in sequencing platforms, such as Illumina and nanopore sequencing and in machine learning tools, will play a pivotal role in decoding the regulatory significance of RNA modifications in health and disease, and substantially accelerate the development of RNA-based therapeutics and diagnostics.

Methods

Synthesis of RMTs

The synthesis was started from a 3′-PT-Amino-Modifier C6 CPG (Glen Research, 20-2956), following oligonucleotide synthesis protocols. Manual solid phase synthesis was performed with 15 µmol twist column (Glen Research, 20-0040-00) and disposable syringes. Between each step, the CPG column was washed with 5 mL dry acetonitrile (ACN). The initial DMT group was removed from the CPG using 2 mL 3% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) in dichloromethane (DCM) as detritylation reagent. For the coupling reaction, 0.2 mL C-TOM-CE phosphoramidite solution (0.1 M in dry ACN, Glen Research, 10-3014) and 0.2 mL 5-ethylthio-1H-tetrazole (ETT) solution (0.25 M in dry ACN, Glen Research, 30-3140) were loaded into separate syringes and connected to both ends of the column to form a sealed system. Coupling reaction was started by pushing both syringes back and forth to mix phosphoramidite solution and ETT solution, and continued for 5 min. After coupling, 2 mL iodine in pyridine/H2O (0.05 M, 9:1) was applied for the oxidation step. The capping step was omitted due to the simplicity of the target structure. To introduce the 5′-phosphate, chemical phosphorylation reagent II (Glen Research, 10-1901) was used for the second cycle with the same detritylation, coupling and oxidation steps. The final DMT group was removed using detritylation reagent.

The product was cleaved from the solid phase and deprotected using AMA (ammonium hydroxide/40% aqueous methylamine, 1:1, v/v) at 65 °C for 10 min. The crude product was dried under vacuum, redissolved in water, and purified on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 reversed-phase column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 µm) using a gradient from 10 to 30% phase B over 5 min. Phase A consisted of 20 mM triethylammonium acetate (pH 7.0) in water, and phase B was 100% acetonitrile. The yield pCp-NH2 was characterized by Agilent 6495C Triple Quadrupole MS: Calculated: [M + H]+ 647.2, Found: 647.3. The labelling reaction was performed in separate tubes with 18 µL pCp-NH2 (50 mM), 2 µL NaHCO3 (1 M in H2O) and 20 µl one of TMTsixplex reagents (0.8 mg in 20 µL ACN) in each tube. The iTRAQ labelling reaction was performed similarly. After 2 h incubation at ambient temperature, the reaction was quenched with 2 μL of 5% hydroxylamine solution. The reaction was dried in a vacuum concentrator and redissolved in 30 µL of DMSO, with addition of 30 µL of TEA·HF to remove the 2′-hydroxyl protection group. After 2 h of incubation at 65 °C, the reaction was dried under vacuum again, redissolved in water, and purified on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 reversed-phase column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 µm) using a gradient from 0 to 10% phase B over 5 min. Phase A consisted of 0.02% acetic acid in water, and phase B was 100% acetonitrile. The yield pCp-TMT-6plex and pCp-iTRAQ-4plex were characterized by Q Exactive Orbitrap MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The results are presented in the following order: calculated [M + H]+, measured [M + H]+, measured reporter ion: pCp-TMT1, 732.2938, 732.2934, 126.1278; pCp-TMT2, 732.2938, 732.2937, 127.1247; pCp-TMT3, 732.2938, 732.2939, 128.1344; pCp-TMT4, 732.2938, 732.2938, 129.1316; pCp-TMT5, 732.2938, 732.2938, 130.1413; pCp-TMT6, 732.2938, 732.2938, 131.1381; pCp-iTRAQ1, 647.2367, 647.2376, 114.1107; pCp-iTRAQ2, 647.2367, 647.2330, 115.1078; pCp-iTRAQ3, 647.2329, 647.2328, 116.1113; pCp-iTRAQ4, 647.2329, 647.2329, 117.1148.

Cell culture and small RNA isolation

Knockout strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14 were obtained from the non-redundant PA14 transposon insertion mutant library61. Individual strains were isolated as single colonies and verified by sequencing the transposon insertion sites, following the protocol described in ‘The PA14 Non-Redundant Set of Pseudomonas aeruginosa Transposon Insertion Mutants User Manual (version 2.2)’ (https://pa14.mgh.harvard.edu/pa14/downloads/manual.pdf). Wild-type and knockout strains were initially cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm in 2 mL of LB medium without or with gentamycin (15 µg/mL) in a 5 mL round-bottom tube. Subsequently, 100 µL of the overnight culture was transferred to 5 mL of fresh LB medium without or with gentamycin (15 µg/mL) in a 14 mL round-bottom tube and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm until the culture reached an OD600 of ~0.8. Cell culture in M9 minimal medium was carried out using a similar protocol.

Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation of 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Medium was removed, and cells were washed with cold 1× PBS buffer. After re-pelleting the cells, the PBS was discarded. Cell lysis buffer (100 µL; 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 4 M guanidine isothiocyanate, pH 7.5) was added to each well, and the plate was shaken vigorously at ambient temperature (1500 rpm) for 10 min to ensure complete cell lysis.

Small RNA (~80–90% tRNA) was isolated using an in-house magnetic bead–based protocol24. Large RNA was removed during the first round of bead binding, followed by tRNA purification in a second round of bead binding. Briefly, 45 µL of cell lysate was mixed with 105 µL of RNA binding buffer I containing 3 M LiCl, 7% PEG8000, 2 mM EDTA, 40 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.5), and 8 µL Sera-Mag carboxylate-modified magnetic beads (Cytiva, 65152105050350). The mixture was agitated thoroughly and incubated at ambient temperature for 5 min. The magnetic beads were separated on a magnetic rack at ambient temperature for 5 min and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The tube was then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 1 min and placed on the magnetic rack to remove any remaining beads. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 1.8 × (v/v) RNA-binding buffer II (0.05% magnetic beads in isopropanol). The mixture was agitated thoroughly at ambient temperature for 5 min. The magnetic beads were separated on the magnetic rack for 5 min and the supernatant was removed. The pelleted beads were washed twice using washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 80% EtOH, pH 7.5). The beads were air dried at ambient temperature. tRNA was eluted in 50 µL nuclease-free water and stored at −20 °C until further processing.

RNA fragmentation and labelling

RNA fragmentation was performed using 2–5 µg of RNA in a 50 µL reaction containing 1000 U of RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0541), 1 U of rSAP (NEB, M0371S), and 1× CutSmart buffer. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To remove RNase, an equal volume of phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) was added to the reaction mixture, followed by vigorous vortexing for 30 s. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at ambient temperature to achieve phase separation. The upper aqueous phase, containing the RNA fragments, was carefully collected. RNA was then recovered using Oligo Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo, D4061), with the addition of 2× (v/v) oligo binding buffer and 16× (v/v) ethanol.

The RNA labelling reaction was carried out in a 50 µL reaction containing 0.5–2 µg of digested RNA, 0.2 mM of a selected RMT, 1 mM of ATP, 20 U of T4 RNA ligase 1 (NEB, M0204S), and 1 × T4 RNA ligase buffer. Reactions were incubated for 4 h at 16 °C and inactivated by heating at 65 °C for 15 min. Following labelling, samples with tags from the same series were pooled and purified using an Oligo Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo, D4061), with the addition of 2× (v/v) oligo binding buffer and 16× (v/v) ethanol. The labelled RNA fragments were further desalted using Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter 3 KDa cutoff (Millipore, UFC500396) to remove residual ATP and isobaric mass tags. The eluted RNA was transferred to an HPLC injection vial, dried using a SpeedVac, and reconstituted in 1 µL of butylamine:acetic acid buffer (20 mM BAAA, pH 7.0) prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis of RNA fragments

The MS analysis was performed on Q Exactive Orbitrap MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with EASY-Spray Nano source and connected with a capillary LC system (Agilent Technologies). LC separation was carried out using a custom-packed C18 capillary column (0.15 × 150 mm) containing 5 µm YMC-Pack ODS-AQ C18 resin (YMC America). The mobile phases consisted of 20 mM butylamine:acetic acid buffer (BAAA, pH 7.0) in H2O (phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B). Separation was performed at a flow rate of 1 µL/min with a linear gradient from 3 to 25% phase B over 50 min. The eluent was ionized with a spray voltage of 3 kV and in-source dissociation of 100 eV. Data acquisition was performed in positive ion mode using data-dependent MS/MS (dd-MS2) with Xcalibur Data Acquisition and Interpretation Software v3.1. MS1 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 600–2000 at a resolution of 70,000. MS2 scans were collected with the following parameters: fixed first mass of 100 m/z, resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 2 × 105, maximum injection time of 150 ms, minimum AGC target of 2 × 104, stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) of 15, 17, and 50, and an isolation window of 2.0 m/z.

Data analysis

The data processing pipeline is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Briefly, raw data acquired from the Orbitrap mass spectrometer were converted to mzML format using the MSConvert GUI tool (version: 3.0.25239-8e11d5a) from ProteoWizard62. The reference tRNA FASTA file was downloaded from GtRNAdb36 and digested in silico using the RNADigestor63 module in OpenMS (version 3.1.0)64, allowing one missed cleavage and requiring a minimum fragment length of five nucleotides. An R script (“Fasta_prepare.R”)65 was used to append the [pCp-iTRAQ] tag to the 3′ end of each fragment and re-index the sequences to generate a reference library. During OpenMS analysis, the mzML file was first processed using PeakPickerHiRes to centroid the data and clean up high-resolution spectral peaks. The centroided data was then input into IsobaricAnalyzer to extract isobaric reporter ion intensities. Because the current implementation reports reporter ion intensities without associated signal-to-noise ratios, no reporter-ion S/N cutoff was applied in the downstream analysis. Fragment identification was performed using the NucleicAcidSearchEngine (NASE)25 with both the mzML file and the reference library as inputs. Search parameters included a mass tolerance of 5.0 ppm for both MS1 and MS2 scans, a minimum charge state of +1, and a maximum of +20. A total of 30 RNA modifications were included in the search: Am, mA, I, m66A, t6A, ct6A, m6t6A, i6A, io6A, ms2i6A, ms2io6A, Cm, ac4C, s2C, ava2C, m5C, Gm, Q, oQ1, mG, m22G, Um, s4U, D, acp3D, acp3U, cmo5U, ho5U, mcmo5Um, and mnm5s2U. pCp-iTRAQ was defined as a custom modification in the NASE custom modification table. After NASE, Identifications were filtered using IDFilter with a score threshold of 50 and mapped to reporter ion intensities using IDMapper. The resulting data, along with an experimental design file, were processed with MSStatsConverter to generate a quantification table. Identified RNA sequences with modifications and annotated MS2 spectra were exported using TOPPView. Both the quantification and identification tables were further processed using custom R scripts65, and the final data were visualized using R and GraphPad Prism. Briefly, identification and quantification tables were matched and filtered, reporter intensities were normalized across channels to correct for loading and labeling differences and then to the designated control sample within each run. RNA fragment abundances were subsequently summarized to calculate relative fold-change values.

tRNA isodecoder isolation

To isolate specific tRNAs, 2 pmol of biotinylated synthetic DNA oligos complementary to the target tRNAs (Anti_PA14_Glu_TTC: 5′-Btn-TTTGGCGTCCCCTAGGGGACTCGAA; Anti_PA14_Trp_CCA: 5′-Btn-TTGAGACCGCCGCTCTGCCAATTGAGCTACTG; GenScript) were incubated with 500 ng of total small RNAs in annealing buffer (50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5) adjusted to 25 μL with Milli-Q water. Samples were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, then slowly cooled to 25 °C at 2 °C/min to promote hybridization. Hydrophilic streptavidin C1 beads (12.5 μL; NEB, S1421S) were washed three times with 200 μL washing buffer (1 M NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 5 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5), then resuspended in 25 μL of 2× binding buffer (2 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5). Hybridized products were mixed with beads (final volume 50 μL) and incubated at ambient temperature for 15 min with mixing at 500 rpm. Beads were collected on a magnetic rack (Alpaqua Engineering, A000400) and the supernatant removed and discarded. Beads were washed three times with 200 μL washing buffer, followed by two washes with 0.1× diluted binding buffer to reduce salt. tRNA was eluted by incubating beads with 20 μL Milli-Q water at 95 °C for 5 min. Eluates were collected for RNA hydrolysis and subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis, as described in the following section.

tRNA hydrolysis and LC-MS/MS analysis of ribonucleosides

tRNA hydrolysis was performed in a 40 µL enzyme cocktail containing 10 U benzonase (Sigma, 34998), 4 U calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (Sigma, 524572), 0.1 U phosphodiesterase I (US Biological, P4072), 0.1 mM deferoxamine (Sigma, D9533), 0.1 mM butylated hydroxytoluene (Sigma, W218405), 4 ng coformycin (NCI, 27781713), 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. The resulting ribonucleosides were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (50× 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm) connected to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system and an Agilent 6495 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer using Agilent MassHunter acquisition software v10.1. The HPLC was operated at 25 °C with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. Initial conditions held 100% solution A (water, 0.02% formic acid) for 2 min, followed by 2–4 min at 0–8% solution B (70% acetonitrile, 0.02% formic acid), and from 4 to 5.9 min at 8–100% solution B. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive mode with an electrospray ionization source with following parameters: gas temperature of 200 °C, gas flow of 11 L/min, and a capillary voltage of 3000 V. Detection leveraged a dynamic MRM mode, targeting product ions from precursor ions of each RNA modification.

Structural modelling

The structure of PA14_40730 (TrmV) was predicted by AlphaFold3 and aligned to the RlmN-tRNAGlu complex (Protein Data Bank ID: 5HR7)66. TrmV together with tRNA 35-38 (‘UCAC’) were then isolated, and C36 was replaced with ms2i6A in CHARMM-GUI67 before solvation. In the resulting complex, the modified four-base segment “U[ms2i6A]AC” adopts a starting pose in which the neighbouring adenine (A37) flips into the TrmV’s catalytic pocket. Molecular dynamics simulations were subsequently performed for 10 ns using NAMD68. Structural visualization was carried out using the open-source version of PyMOL (https://github.com/schrodinger/pymol-open-source).

PA14_68100 (TrmS) structure prediction was performed using Boltz-169, incorporating the TrmS protein sequence, the P. aeruginosa tRNA Glu sequence, and S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine (SAH) as the ligand. The predicted structure was solvated using CHARMM-GUI67, and molecular dynamics simulations were subsequently performed for 10 ns using NAMD68. Structural visualization was carried out using the open-source version of PyMOL.

Genomic analysis

Sequences of Pseudomonas were retrieved from the BV-BRC database (https://www.bv-brc.org/) using filters “status: complete” and “quality: good” and sequences of representative bacteria were retrieved using filters “status: complete,” “quality: good,” and “reference: representative” in November 2023. Proteins were searched using BLASTp v2.15 (PMID: 20003500) with a P-value of 1E-10 and an identity percentage of 20% as initial cutoffs. Candidates near the cutoff were manually verified using the genome browser of the BV-BRC database.
