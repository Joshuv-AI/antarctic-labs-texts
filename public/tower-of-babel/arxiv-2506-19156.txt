Looking for Signs: Reasoning About FOBNNs Using SAT
Hans-Jörg Schurr, Athénaïs Vaginay

Abstract
First-Order Boolean Networks with Non-deterministic updates (FOBNN) compute a boolean transition graph representing the absence and presence of species over time. The utility of FOBNNs has been justified by their theoretical soundness with respect to the Euler simulation of the differential equations. However, we lack practical means to work with FOBNNs and an empirical evaluation of their properties. We present a sound and efficient reduction of the first-order FOBNN transition relation to a propositional logic formula. This makes it possible to use modern SAT solvers to reason on the full transition graph, even for large models. We use this encoding to assess the feasibility and efficiency of practical reasoning with FOBNNs. To do so, we focus on the computation of fixed points. We also compare the transition graphs obtained via FOBNNs to those computed by the classic boolean semantics of reaction networks. Overall, our encoding opens new directions for the analysis of FOBNNs and deepens the understanding of their relationship with reaction networks.

In this paper, we explore

FOBNNs use first-order logic to encode a transition relation on

To reason about these first-order interpretation of these formulas

FOBNNs can be constructed from the ODEs corresponding to a reaction network.
The resulting transition graph is a sound over-approximation of the traces
produced by the Euler integration of the original ODEs.
Additional first-order constraints can be used to refine the abstraction.
These constraints are either entailed by the full interpretation
of the FOBNN, or encode domain knowledge.
While this is a strong theoretical justification of the utility of FOBNNs,
we lack practical means to work with FOBNNs,
and an empirical evaluation.

To address this we use a sound and complete reduction of
the first-order FOBNN transition relation to a propositional logic formula whose
models correspond to the models of the original FOBNN formula.
This allows us to harness the power of modern SAT solvers to study the dynamics of FOBNNs.
For example, we can enumerate the transitions of the FOBNN
by enumerating the models of the resulting propositional formula.
Propositional representations of transition systems are also the target domain of SAT-based
model checking

[
4
]
.
Hence, our work is the first step towards applying these methods to FOBNNs.
Using the encoding, we perform an experimental evaluation of FOBNNs on models from the Biomodels repository.
To do so, we first assess the feasibility and efficiency of our SAT-based approach
to analyze the dynamics of reaction networks.
We focus on the computation of fixed points
(self-looping states
from which the system cannot escape, e.g.,
the state where all the species are present,

[\mathsf{S}=\signUP,\mathsf{E}=\signUP,\mathsf{C}=\signUP,\mathsf{P}=\signUP]

as it is one of the most standard inspection of dynamical system
[

,
20

Indeed, fixed points in cognate formalisms correspond to
the dynamics of the underlying biological system
(observable biological phenotypes
[
16
]
,
observable phase in the cell cycle
[
11
]
,
or different cell types after differentiation, for example).
They are also often
crucial for applications such as the control of the system
[
19
]

We also compare the transition graphs obtained from FOBNNs

In particular, we measure the density of the their transition graphs, which
determine how feasible it is to derived meaningful conclusion from the
graph.
In the case of FOBNNs the density can be reduced by encoding additional domain

Our results demonstrate that FOBNNs offer a scaleable and meaningful over-approximation of ODE dynamics,

2.2
First-Order Boolean Networks with Non-deterministic Updates

From a set of variables
V

we build four sets of annotated variables

\mathcal{S}=V
,

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}=\{v_{\mathrm{next}}\mid v\in V\}
,

\dot{}\mathcal{S}=\{\dot{v}\mid v\in V\}
,

\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{next}}=\{\dot{v}_{\mathrm{next}}\mid v\in V\}
.
Furthermore,

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}\cup\dot{}\mathcal{S}\cup\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{next}}
.
The rationale here is that variables in
\mathcal{S}
represent the species,
their value will model their amount of the species,
variables in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}
represent the amount of the species after a transition,
dotted variables in
\dot{\mathcal{S}}
represent the derivative of the species,
and dotted next variables in
\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{next}}
represent the derivative after a transition.

Syntax of FOBNN

Formally, a FOBNN
\mathcal{F}
is a first-order formula
\phi
over the signature
\Sigma_{\mathrm{arith}}
and variables
in
\mathcal{V}
.
The formula must have the form
\exists v_{1},\dots,v_{n}.\,\Omega
where

\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}=\dot{}\mathcal{S}\cup\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{next}}
.
The variables in
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}
remain free.
The formula
\Omega
is a quantifier free conjunction of atoms
(see
Example

3
).
Hence, the formula is
\Sigma_{1}
form:
the only quantifiers are existential quantifiers in the prefix.

FOBNN State Transitions

If we fix an interpretation
\mathcal{I}
, then each model of
\mathcal{F}

is determined by an assignment
\alpha
to the free variables
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}
.
This represents a transition between two states
\sigma\to\sigma_{\mathrm{next}}

where
\sigma:=\alpha_{|\mathcal{S}}

is the
current state
,
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{next}}=\alpha_{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}}

is the
next state
.
By enumerating the assignments that satisfy
\mathcal{F}
under
\mathcal{I}
,
we build the
base state transition graph
of the FOBNN

.
If we remove the existential quantifiers from

, the assignment

This represents a transition between two
extended states

\hat{\sigma}\to\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{next}}
where

\hat{\sigma}:=\alpha_{|\mathcal{S}{\cup\dot{\mathcal{S}}}}

and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{next}}=\alpha_{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}{\cup\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{next}}}}
.
The set of such transitions is the
extended state transition graph
of

FOBNNs from Reaction Network ODEs

FOBNNs are typically used to reason about dynamical systems.
In this case, they are built from the ODEs of a reaction network.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of species.
The amount of chemicals in a solution is modeled as a

concrete base state

\sigma:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Base states are written in the standard notation adopted from chemistry:

\sum_{\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\mathsf{X})*\mathsf{X}
.
For example the state

\sigma(\mathsf{A})=1.2,\sigma(\mathsf{B})=3

is written
1.2*\mathsf{A}+3*\mathsf{B}
.
Note that this is an arithmetic term
from
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{arith}}
.
A reaction
(\rho,e,\pi)
describes the transformation of
a pool
\rho
of reactants
into a pool
\pi
of products,
that happens at a speed
e
.
The elements
\rho
and
\pi
are concrete base states over
\mathcal{S}
.
Formally, a reaction is thus an element of

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{S}}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{arith}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{S}}
.

A
reaction network

is a set of reactions.
Any reaction network
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
is
associated with an ordinary differential equation system
{\mathsf{ode}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}
.
ODEs can be represented as first-order formulas over the signature

\mathbb{R}\cup\{+,-,*,/\}\cup\{\dot{\phantom{c}}\}
.
Those formulas are interpreted in the universe of
real valued functions
\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Constants are interpreted with constant functions, and the interpretation
of operators combine functions point-wise.
The interpretation of the dot-operator is such that
\mathcal{I}(\dot{\phantom{c}})(f)
is the
derivative of
f
for any differentiable function, and undefined otherwise.
An ODE system consists of a conjunction of atomic formulas (equations) over this
signature with the dot-operator applied to variables
on the left-hand side,
and an arithmetic term on the right-hand side:

\mathsf{ode}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}:=\bigwedge_{\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}}\dot{\mathsf{X}}\eqcirc\sum_{(\rho,e,\pi)\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}(\rho(\mathsf{X})-\pi(\mathsf{X}))*e

where
\rho(\mathsf{X})
and
\pi(\mathsf{X})
are the constant symbols
representing the quantity of species
\mathsf{X}
as reactant and product respectively.

2.3
Additional Constraints

The transition graph generated by an FOBNN contains spurious transitions compared
to the sign abstraction of the real transition graph of the ODE.

[
14
]

[
14
]

proposed to eliminate some spurious transitions from the graph
by using additional knowledge.

One approach is to add formulas to the FOBNN that are entailed by its models
over the real numbers. Since such formulas are not necessarily entailed by the
models over the signs, they can eliminate some spurious solutions.
In some cases this can even make the abstraction precise
[
1
]
.

Another approach is to encode domain knowledge.
For example, consider
a reaction network
with one reaction
\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}\xrightarrow{{{k*\mathsf{A}*\mathsf{B}}}}{}\mathsf{C}
.
The speed of this reaction follows the mass action law:
the rate of the reaction is
directly proportional to the product of the value for the reactants.
A consequence of using this law
is that it is impossible
for a species that is present (
\mathsf{X}=\signUP
)
to become absent (
\mathsf{X}=\signNO
).
With the naive construction of FOBNN,
the computed transition graph would have the edge

\signUP\signUP 0\tto\signUP\signUP\signUP
,

\signUP\signUP 0\tto\signUP 0\signUP
,

\signUP\signUP 0\tto 0\signUP\signUP
,

\signUP\signUP 0\tto 000
.
However, the first edge alone is a tighter sound abstraction
of the actual dynamics of the ODEs over the reals.
By adding
\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}\geq\mathsf{X}

to the formula
\mathcal{F}
for all the species
\mathsf{X}

whose dynamics are governed by a mass action law kinetics,
one can make the FOBNN abstraction tighter, and still correct.
A similar constraint could be added for other kinetics that do not allow species to go back to 0,
such as the Michaelis-Menten kinetics.
Another example of this is on the reaction network
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{enz}}
, where the kinetics of the three reactions follows the mass action law.

without and with external knowledge about the mass action constraints.
One can see the later graph is smaller.

Encoding FOBNNs as Propositional Formulas

As we just saw, FOBNNs use first-order logic to describe state transitions.
The models of this formula define a relation between the state variables,
and the next-state variables.
However, the typical interpretation is the fixed finite domain of signs.
In this case,

satisfiability is decidable

and formulas can efficiently be
translated into
equisatisfiable propositional logic formulas
.

Propositional formulas are simpler than first-order formulas
in that they do not use terms nor quantifiers.
The propositional formulas
\psi
are constructed using the production rule

\psi::=v\mid\neg\psi\mid\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}\mid\psi_{1}\lor\psi_{2}\mid\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}\\
\text{where }v\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\text{ are propositional formulas.}

The truth value of a propositional formula is determined
by an assignment
\beta
that maps variables
directly to truth values (
\boldsymbol{0}
or
\boldsymbol{1}
).
The interpretation
[\![\psi]\!]_{\beta}
of
a propositional formula
\psi
is defined just as for first-order
logic.
However, the atoms are propositional variables whose truth value
is determined by
\beta
.
If there is a
\beta

such that
[\![\psi]\!]_{\beta}=\boldsymbol{1}
, then we call
\beta

a
model
and say that
\psi
is
satisfiable
.

This section describes the encoding of an FOBNN formula
\mathcal{F}
into a
propositional formula
\mathcal{P}
.
The translations conceptually consists of two steps:
(1) we flatten the input FOBNN formula; (2) we encode the flat formula in propositional logic.
Note that we present those two phases separately for clarity, but
our implementation interleaves both phases.

Our translation of a FOBNN
\mathcal{F}
to a propositional formula
\mathcal{P}
is sounds.
That means that each model of
\mathcal{P}
can be translated to a state transition of
\mathcal{F}
,
and that
\mathcal{P}
is unsatisfiable, if
\mathcal{F}
has unsatisfiable with respect to the
sign interpretation.

3.1
Flat-Term Translations

The terms used by an FOBNN formula
\mathcal{F}
may be not flat, meaning that
they contain nested function application.

Conversely, a term
t
is a
flat term
if it can be produced by the rule

t::=v\mid c\mid f(v_{1},v_{2})\text{ where }v,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }c\in\mathbb{R}.

Furthermore, a first-order formula is
flat
if all its atoms have the shape

v\eqcirc t
where
v
is a variable, and
t
is a flat term.

The first step of the propositional encoding procedure is to translate an FOBNN

\mathcal{F}
to an equisatisfiable and flat first-order formula
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

The flat form uses additional variables.
We assume we have a large enough of set
\mathcal{W}
of fresh variables
such that
\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{W}=\emptyset
.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}
has the shape
\exists_{\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}}\mathsf{X}.\,\Omega
where
\Omega

is a conjunction of atoms.
Since the fresh variables are not relevant for the state transitions represented by an
FOBNN, the flat formula
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
will have the shape

\exists_{\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}}\mathsf{X}.\exists_{w\in\mathcal{W}}w.\,\Omega^{\prime}
.

The flattening procedure transforms nested terms into flat terms by introducing
fresh variables and auxiliary equations at each level of the term tree.
This means, to build
\Omega^{\prime}
we

1.

iterate over the atoms
t_{1}\eqcirc t_{2}
of
\Omega
,

2.

traverse the subterms of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
bottom up, and

(a)

for each
c\in\mathbb{R}
encountered, add
w\eqcirc c
to
\Omega^{\prime}
where
w\in\mathcal{W}
is fresh,

(b)

for each
f(t_{1},t_{2})
encountered, add
w\eqcirc f(w_{1},w_{2})
to
\Omega^{\prime}
where
w_{1},w_{2},w\in\mathcal{W}
,
w
is
fresh, and
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
are the fresh variables assigned to
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.

Example 4

The flat version of

\exists\dot{}\mathsf{C}.\,\dot{}\mathsf{C}\eqcirc\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{on}}*\mathsf{S}*\mathsf{E}-\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{off}}*\mathsf{C}-\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{cat}}*\mathsf{C}

is

\exists\dot{}\mathsf{C}.\,\exists w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5},w_{6},w_{7},w_{8},w_{9}.\\
w_{1}\eqcirc\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{on}}\land w_{2}\eqcirc w_{1}*\mathsf{S}\land w_{3}\eqcirc w_{2}*\mathsf{E}\land w_{4}\eqcirc\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{off}}\land w_{5}\eqcirc w_{4}*\mathsf{C}\\
\land w_{6}\eqcirc w_{3}-w_{5}\land w_{7}\eqcirc\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{cat}}\land w_{8}\eqcirc w_{7}*\mathsf{C}\land w_{9}\eqcirc w_{6}-w_{8}\land\dot{}\mathsf{C}\eqcirc w_{9}.

3.2
Encoding in Propositional Logic

Once the FOBNN formula
\mathcal{F}
has been flattened to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, we translate it
into a propositional formula
\mathcal{P}
.
We assign two propositional variables
v^{0}
,
v^{1}
to each
variable in
v\in\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{W}
.
These two proportional variables encode a sign.
Given a propositional assignment
\beta
,
we define a translation function to signs:

\sign(\beta)(v^{0},v^{1})=\begin{cases}\signUP&if $\beta(v^{0})=\boldsymbol{1}$ and $\beta(v^{1})=\boldsymbol{0}$\\
\signDO&if $\beta(v^{0})=\boldsymbol{0}$ and $\beta(v^{1})=\boldsymbol{1}$\\
\signNO&if $\beta(v^{0})=\boldsymbol{0}$ and $\beta(v^{1})=\boldsymbol{0}$\\
\end{cases}

Note that
\sign(\beta)(v^{0},v^{1})
is undefined if

\beta(v^{0})=\beta(v^{1})=\boldsymbol{1}
.
Hence, the encoding has to ensure such a
\beta
is never a model of

\mathcal{P}
.

We can now state precisely when the encoding is sound.
Overall, the formulas
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{P}
must be
equisatisfiable
:

\mathcal{F}
has a first-order model
\alpha

if and only if

\mathcal{P}
has a model
\beta
. Furthermore, the models
must agree. If
\beta
is a model of
\mathcal{P}
, then there
is a model
\alpha
of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with
\sign(\beta)(v^{0},v^{1})=\alpha(v)

for all free variables
v
of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Now assume, for example, that a model
\alpha
is such that
\alpha(v)=\signUP
.
We can enforce that
\sign(\beta)(v^{0},v^{1})=\signUP
by using
the formula
v^{0}\land\neg v^{1}
.
We write
v=\signUP
to denote this
formula. Furthermore,
v=\signDO
is
\neg v^{0}\land v^{1}
, and

v=\signNO
is
\neg v^{0}\land\neg v^{1}

To encode a flat atom
v\eqcirc f(v_{1},v_{2})
we have to ensure that

(\sign(\beta)(v_{1}^{0},v_{1}^{1})
,
\sign(\beta)(v_{2}^{0},v_{2}^{1})
,
\sign(\beta)(v^{0},v^{1}))\in\mathcal{I}(f)

for all
models
\beta
.
A naïve way to enforce this is the following encoding schema that enumerates
all elements of the relation:

\bigvee_{(s_{1},s_{2},s)\in\mathcal{I}(f)}v_{1}=s_{1}\land v_{2}=s_{2}\land v=s.

Recall that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has the form

\exists_{\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}}\mathsf{X}.\exists_{w\in\mathcal{W}}w.\,\Omega^{\prime}

where
\Omega^{\prime}
is a
conjunction
of flat atoms.
We can encode
\Omega^{\prime}
by forming the conjunction of the schema for each
individual atom.

Unfortunately, propositional logic does not allow us to encode the existential quantifiers,
and we have to omit them.
Consequently, each model
\alpha
of
\mathcal{F}
corresponds to multiple
models
\beta
of
\mathcal{P}
. We have to take this into account when we enumerate
models to compute transition graphs, as we describe in
Section

4
.

Practical Encoding

While the encoding we described so far is sound and complete, it is not practical.
On the one hand, it introduces a conjunct for each triple
(s_{1},s_{2},s)\in\mathcal{I}(f)
.
Those often can be combined and simplified.
On the other hand, the encoded formula is not in conjunctive normal form (CNF).
Most SAT solvers expect input problems as such a conjunction of disjunctions.
Hence, we use CNF formulas that are
equivalent
to the schema that encodes atoms.

For example, when encoding an addition
v\eqcirc v_{1}+v_{2}
, we know that a necessary condition for

[\![v_{1}+v_{2}]\!]_{\alpha}={\signUP}

is that either

[\![v_{1}]\!]_{\alpha}={\signUP}
or
[\![v_{2}]\!]_{\alpha}={\signUP}
.
Hence,
v^{0}
cannot be
\boldsymbol{1}
if neither
v_{1}^{0}
nor
v_{2}^{0}
are
1
.
The dual holds for
\signDO
. This gives formula

A:=(v_{1}^{0}\lor v_{2}^{0}\lor\neg v^{0})\land(v_{1}^{1}\lor v_{2}^{1}\lor\neg v^{1}).

Furthermore, a sufficient condition for
[\![v_{1}+v_{2}]\!]_{\alpha}=\signUP
is that one
of
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
is
\signUP
, and the other one is
\signUP
or
\signNO
.
Propositionally:

v^{0}
is
\boldsymbol{1}
if
v_{1}^{0}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
v_{2}^{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
or if
v_{1}^{1}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
v_{2}^{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
This also holds for
\signDO
with
v^{1}
, if
we flip
(.)^{0}
and
(.)^{1}
.
Overall, we use the formula

(\neg{v_{1}}^{0}\lor{v_{2}}^{1}\lor v^{0})\land(\neg{v_{2}}^{0}\lor{v_{1}}^{1}\lor v^{0})\land(\neg{v_{1}}^{1}\lor{v_{2}}^{0}\lor v^{1})\land(\neg{v_{2}}^{1}\lor{v_{1}}^{0}\lor v^{1})\land A.

Note that this formula is now a CNF formula.
We use similar encodings for all operators.

Sharing Variables is Unsound

To optimize the encoding, one might
consider reusing propositional variables for syntactically equal subterms.
However, this is unsound in the semantics of FOBNNs. Consider the formula

(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y})*(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y})\eqcirc-1
. This formula is
satisfied by the assignment
\alpha(\mathsf{X})=\signUP
,
\alpha(\mathsf{Y})=\signDO
,
because
[\![\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}]\!]_{\alpha}=\mathbb{S}
and consequently

[\![(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y})*(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y})]\!]_{\alpha}=\mathbb{S}
.
However, if the encodings of both occurrences of
\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}
share
propositional variables, the sign of both sides of the product will be the same
for all models
\beta
, and no propositional model will interpret the
encoding of the product with
\signDO
.

4
Incremental Reasoning About FOBNN Dynamics

Modern SAT solvers support an
incremental
interface
[
2
]

that allows users to modify SAT problem
while maintaining the solving state.
We can use this interface to explore the
dynamics of the FOBNN.

Two operations are possible:
(1) the addition of additional clauses;
(2) the use of extra literals as
assumptions
.
Both operations modify the problem to solve,
but in the first case, the clauses are added permanently
while in the second case
the SAT solver will discard the assumptions after each query.

Enumerating Transitions

The most basic form of systematic network exploration is to list all
outgoing transitions of any state.
This can be implemented efficiently by using the incremental
interfaces by adding helper clauses.

After adding
\mathcal{P}
to the SAT solver as described in
Section

3
,
any
model
\beta
of
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to a transition of
\mathcal{F}
.
Then, after obtaining a model
\beta
, we add clauses to the SAT solver
such that the formula is satisfiable by all transitions except

\beta
.
The necessary clauses contain two literals that invert the assignment to
the variables
\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{next}}
:
add
\neg\mathsf{X}^{0}\lor\mathsf{X}^{1}
if
\sign(\beta)(\mathsf{X}^{0},\mathsf{X}^{1})=\signUP
,
add
{\mathsf{X}}^{0}\lor\neg\mathsf{X}^{1}
if
\sign(\beta)(\mathsf{X}^{0},\mathsf{X}^{1})=\signDO
,
and add
{\mathsf{X}}^{0}\lor{\mathsf{X}}^{1}
if
\sign(\beta)(\mathsf{X}^{0},\mathsf{X}^{1})=\signNO
.
This enforces that the next model assigns a different value to at least one variable.
We can repeat this process until the formula is unsatisfiable.
This approach to model enumeration is standard.

To enforce that a transition starts at a certain
state we can use assumptions.
A state
\sigma
maps the variables
\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}
to signs.
The assumptions that enforce that every SAT model is a transitions starting
at
\sigma
have to enforce that the model assigns the propositional values
corresponding to the signs.
The encoding is the same as for constants.
We assume exactly two literals for each

\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}
:

{\mathsf{X}}^{0}
and
\neg{\mathsf{X}}^{1}
if
\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\signUP
,

\neg{\mathsf{X}}^{0}
and
{\mathsf{X}}^{1}
if
\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\signDO
,

\neg{\mathsf{X}}^{0}
and
\neg{\mathsf{X}}^{1}
if
\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\signNO
.

It is also possible to combine both approaches to enumerate, for example,
all outgoing transitions of a state, or to find fixed points.

Fixed Points

The search for fixed points consists of two steps.
First, we find a loop, i.e., a transition where start and end state are the same.
Then, we check if the loop is a proper fixed point.

To find loops we add clauses that encode the constraints
\mathsf{X}\eqcirc\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}
for all
\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Now, each model is a loop.
To test whether a loop on state
\sigma
is a fixed point we check
if there is a transition
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
such that
there is
\mathsf{X}\in\mathcal{S}
with
\sigma(\mathsf{X})\neq\sigma^{\prime}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}})
.
To do this, we force the solver
to produce a transition starting in
\sigma
, as described above.
This query is
unsatisfiable
if
\sigma
is a fixed point.

However, the constraints
\mathsf{X}\eqcirc\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}
are added permanently
to the SAT solver, and we must be able to “turn them off” when
checking whether there is an outgoing transition.
To do so we create an additional propositional variable
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
and
encode
\mathsf{X}\eqcirc\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}
as

(\neg v_{\mathrm{flag}}\lor\neg\mathsf{X}^{0}\lor\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}^{0})\land(\neg v_{\mathrm{flag}}\lor\neg\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}^{0}\lor\mathsf{X}^{0})\land(\neg v_{\mathrm{flag}}\lor\neg\mathsf{X}^{1}\lor\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}^{1})\land(\neg v_{\mathrm{flag}}\lor\neg\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{next}}^{1}\lor\mathsf{X}^{1})
.

If
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
is not assumed, the constraints can be satisfied
trivially by assigning
\boldsymbol{0}
to
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
, and they have no further
effect on the model.
However, when
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
is temporarily assumed during an SAT solver call,
then
\neg v_{\mathrm{flag}}
is
\boldsymbol{0}
, and the loop constraints are active.

Overall, we search fixed points by first solving the SAT formula under the assumption

v_{\mathrm{flag}}
to obtain a candidate loop model
\beta
, and then checking whether
there is an outgoing edge from the state of
\beta
by omitting the assumption
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
.
If this second SAT call is satisfiable,
\beta

is not a fixed point. In this case we exclude
\beta
as described above,
and we look for another loop by assuming
v_{\mathrm{flag}}
again.

Reference Comparison

As part of the proposal for FOBNNs,

[
14
]

[

used constraint programming (CP) to compute transitions.
Like us, this implementation implements an FOBNN solver, and also
the translation from reaction networks to FOBNNs.
However, for various reasons both may
generate different FOBNNs from one reaction network.
Different additional constraints may be added, for instance, logical consequences over

This makes it difficult to compare the

Both FOBNNs and the classic boolean abstraction of reaction networks
[
8
]
as implemented by Biocham
[
6
]

produce boolean state transition graphs.

Given a reaction network
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}

over species
\mathcal{S}
,
and a state
\sigma
where some species are present (are
\boldsymbol{1}
),
the classic boolean semantics selects (non-deterministically) at most one reaction

\mathcal{R}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}

such that

\sigma(r)=\boldsymbol{1}
for all reactants
r
of
\mathcal{R}
.
The products of
\mathcal{R}
are guaranteed to be present in the next state, while its reactants may not persist.
Uninvolved species keep their value.

A key problem when doing an abstraction is to refine it by eliminating spurious transitions, to better approximate the behavior of the original system.
The classic boolean semantics of reaction networks provides a tight and correct abstraction of the stochastic semantics of reaction systems, but not their differential semantics.
However, the abstraction of the differential semantics of reaction networks provided by naive FOBNNs is correct but not tight.
Yet, a key advantage of FOBNNs is that refinement can be naturally achieved by adding constraints.

This subsection is dedicated to the empirical evaluation of
(1) the intuition that the transition graphs obtained by a naive FOBNN are similar in size (number of nodes and number of edges) than those computed by the classic boolean semantics,
and (2) the impact of adding one constraint from domain knowledge (the mass action constraint, see
Section

2.3
) on the graphs computed from FOBNNs.

We compute the full state transition graphs of all 58 models with up to 12 species.
The complete graphs is for models with 12 contain at most 16 777 216 edges.
Since Biocham does not support computing a full boolean transition graph,
we compute those using an ASP-program (
).

The transition graphs
of FOBNNs and the classic transition graphs (
classic
)
turn out to show substantial differences.
First, the
classic
-graphs necessarily contain all base states,
while impossible states are omitted from FOBNN graphs.
These omitted states would falsify some constraints of the SAT encoding.
For example, in
B275
, which has 4 species, 8 states do not appear in the FOBNN graph.
The reason is the species
\mathrm{RA}
that appears in the denominator of the definition of the parameter
\rho
.
Hence,
\mathrm{RA}
cannot be
0
, as enforced by our encoding.
However, the classic boolean semantics does not capture this.
Interestingly, COPASI does not report an error for
a numerical simulation starting from a state with
\mathrm{RA}=0
.

Figure

4

shows the number of edges in the graph relative to the number of species.
We observe than the number of edges in the graphs computed by
classic
and

our noMA
is similar, but those computed with
our MA
have fewer edges.
As we discussed, using the mass action constraint may not be justified.
Yet, adding such constraints
can be a useful first step to study an FOBNN, as they reduce the number of edges. Furthermore, in the future,
species that
admit the mass action constraint could be detected automatically.
Finally, none of the transition graph of the classic boolean semantics
had a fixed point.
However, the FOBNN transition graph of 24 models of the 58 models tested had at least one fixed point.

6
Conclusion and Perspective

Overall, the novel FOBNN model is compelling because it soundly
abstracts the numerical Euler simulation.
We now also know that they can be handled in practice using
standard methods.
The encoded FOBNNs are
finite state transition systems with a
propositional
representation of
the transition relation, and it is possible to use techniques
from SAT-based model checking
[
4
]
on them.

This work suggests several future directions.
The immediate next step is to study classical dynamical properties, beyond fixed points.
In particular, SAT-based methods for detecting limit cycles
of classic
boolean networks
[
18
]
could be adapted to FOBNNs.
Moreover, it would be valuable to study how dynamical features
of the continuous system (such as steady state stability) can be anticipated
from the FOBNN abstraction. On the technical side, it would be useful to have a well-specified interchange format for FOBBNs,
and a benchmark collection of FOBNNs, in particular those derived from CoreSBML models, with various additional constraints.
This would allow us to compare different FOBNN solvers on the same inputs systematically.

Another key perspective is to further position FOBNN within the hierarchy
of reaction network semantics.
Finally, we could also place
FOBNNs among the
influence-based models
of biological systems.
In particular, they could be compared to classic boolean networks,
whose most-permissive semantics is known to capture the ODE dynamics
[
15
]
.
