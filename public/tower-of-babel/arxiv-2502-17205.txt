A hyperbolic model for two-layer thin film flow with a perfectly soluble anti-surfactant
Rahul Barthwal, Christian Rohde

Abstract
We consider the motion of a two-layer thin film that consists of two immiscible viscous fluids and is endowed with an anti-surfactant solute. The presence of such solute particles induces variations of the surface tension and interfacial stress driving a Marangoni-type flow. We first analyze a lubrication limit and derive one-dimensional evolution equations for film heights and solute concentrations. Then, under the assumption that the capillarity and diffusion effects are negligible and the solute is perfectly soluble, we obtain a conservative first-order system in terms of film heights and concentration gradients. This reduced system is found to be strictly hyperbolic for a certain set of states and to admit an entire class of entropy/entropy-flux pairs. We also provide a strictly convex entropy for the hyperbolic system. Thus, the well-posedness for the Cauchy problem is given. Moreover, the system is almost a Temple-class system, which allows to compute explicit solutions of the Riemann problem. The paper concludes with numerical experiments using a Godunov-type finite volume method, which relies on the exact Riemann solver.



Key words.
Thin film flow, anti-surfactants, lubrication approximation, hyperbolic conservation laws, entropy/entropy flux pairs, Riemann problems

Introduction

The study of thin film models is a
rich and intricate research area. This holds in particular true if the dynamics is subject to the influence of soluble or insoluble substances, inducing variations of the surface tension. These flows find important applications in technical and environmental fields like coating technologies, thin film solar cell technologies, surfactant replacement therapies, etc. (see e.g.
[
17
,
26
,
28
,
31
]
). The surface-tension driven flow is usually referred to as a Marangoni flow, which has been extensively studied by several researchers in the last two decades (see e.g.
[
8
,
19
,
22
,
25
,
28
,
30
]
). The variations in surface tension of the fluid can occur due to temperature changes or by the introduction of surfactants or anti-surfactants. While surfactants decrease the surface tension of the fluid, there are other solutes which behave in an opposite manner. When the molecules of a dissolved solute are expelled from the free surface of a solvent, the surface tension of the solution increases. Such solutes are usually referred to as “anti-surfactants”. Many salts, such as sodium chloride or short-chain alcohols, when added to water can be considered to act as anti-surfactants.

The large-scale dynamics of a pure one-layer thin film dynamics is typically governed by a first-order equation for the film height. Accounting for gravity effects, one is led to a scalar conservation law with non-convex fluxes, which allow for so-called overshoot solutions containing undercompressive shock waves, see
[
16
]
. Such equations have been analyzed from the
theoretical point of view in e.g.
[
7
]
and with regard to numerics in e.g.
[
11
]
.

More recently, Conn et al.
[
14
,
15
]
proposed and analyzed a mathematical model for a two-dimensional anti-surfactant solution in a one-layer thin film flow. They obtained a reduced one-dimensional hyperbolic form out of it assuming that the solute is perfectly soluble while neglecting diffusion and capillarity effects. In one spatial dimension, it reads for unknown film thickness
\displaystyle h=h(x,t)
and bulk concentration gradient
\displaystyle b=b(x,t)
as

\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{h^{2}b}{2}\right)=0,\qquad\dfrac{\partial b}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{hb^{2}}{2}\right)=0.

(1.1)

Notably, the system (
1.1
) belongs to the Keyfitz-Kranzer class type hyperbolic systems
[
39
]
, for which the shock and rarefaction curves coincide and form a straight line in the state space. The system (
1.1
) was studied for classical wave interactions by Minhajul et al. in
[
29
]
and for nonclassical wave interactions by Sen and Raja Sekhar in
[
35
]
. Barthwal et al.
[
2
]
were able to extend the aforementioned model to its two-dimensional counterpart and obtained self-similar and non-self-similar solutions for the Riemann problems
[
3
,
2
]
, which were further explored to solve a three-quadrant Riemann problem by Pandey et al.
[
32
]
. Barthwal et al.
[
4
]
recently developed the global existence of weak entropy solutions for a class of
\displaystyle 2\times 2
hyperbolic systems, including the system (
1.1
), using a novel viscous approximation.

Most of the mathematical models related to thin film flows are restricted to either single-layer flows or to homogeneous fluids. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are not many mathematical treatises of models related to two-layer thin film flows under the influence of a surfactant or anti-surfactant. However, there is the work of Brüll
[
10
]
who established local well-posedness for two-layer thin film flow under the influence of an insoluble surfactant.
The evolution equations accounting for the presence of anti-surfactants are different from the evolution equations for the case of surfactants-induced surface tension variations. In the case of insoluble surfactants, one does not require separate concentration evolution equations in each layer, while in the case of anti-surfactants, it is necessary to obtain the evolution equations of concentration in each layer.

In this paper, we consider such a thin film flow model accounting for an anti-surfactant solute. In the first part of the article, we formulate a one-dimensional lubrication approximation for the evolution of the two-layer thin film flow with a perfectly soluble anti-surfactant solute. For the full-dimensional two-layer flow, we start with two immiscible, incompressible, and viscous fluids lying on top of each other on a solid substrate. One representative example of such two-layer flows is a layer of water lying over an oil layer. Based on the full Navier-Stokes equations and the surfactant transport equations, we derive as first main result the evolution equations for film thickness and bulk concentration in each layer using the thin film approximation for a selected flow regime. This leads to a fourth-order system of governing equations that generalizes previously found descriptions, e.g. for one-layer configurations as in
[
15
]
.
Moreover, by assuming that the capillarity and diffusion effects are negligible, we obtain as the second main result a novel reduced (
\displaystyle 4\times 4
)-system of first-order conservation laws in terms of the dimensionless film thickness in the lower and the upper layer and the corresponding concentration gradients, see (
3.3
) in Section
3
. We show that the reduced system is strictly hyperbolic in a subset of the state space with positive concentration gradients. We consider this property to be essential also for the well-posedness of the system with finite capillarity and diffusion numbers. In the case of only one thin film layer, the system (
3.3
) collapses to system (
1.1
).

The reduced system (
3.3
) has a remarkable structure. It almost belongs to the Temple class, for which exact Riemann solvers are known, and global existence of solutions has been achieved
[
9
,
37
]
.
Our novel system has properties quite similar to a Temple-class system, as it can be decoupled along its Riemann invariants and also has a semi-triangular structure. However, it still does not belong to the Temple class because the relations for shock and rarefaction waves in the second characteristic field differ from each other and are not represented by straight lines in the state space. This makes the solution theory more complicated than its one-layer counterpart. Also, the family of Lax curves is more intricate in the two-layer case, and requires the solution of nonlinear algebraic relations. In the one-layer system, the Lax curves can be connected with each other in an affine manner, which is not the case here.

In the second part of the article, we succeed to show local well-posedness for the Cauchy problem for the reduced system. Notably, we can identify a whole family of entropy/entropy-flux pairs for the reduced system. We also find a strictly convex entropy such that the reduced system belongs to the class of Friedrichs-symmetrizable systems. This becomes possible because we were able to identify a novel set of Riemann invariants, which forms a coordinate system for the hyperbolic system. A similar approach for a (
\displaystyle 2\times 2
)-triangular system of conservation laws was utilized by Andreianov et al.
[
1
]
. Of course, two-layer thin film models cover more physics than the one-layer versions, but the rich mathematical structure of the reduced system is what makes this paper more interesting. Such hyperbolic systems with a class of entropies and a coordinate system are referred as a rich class of hyperbolic systems as pointed out by Serre
[
38
]
. We refer the interested reader to the classical paper by Conlon and Liu
[
12
]
for more details on Riemann invariants as a coordinate system. Finding such a new instance of a non-linear hyperbolic system with three or more equations is interesting from the point of view of the theory of hyperbolic systems. We note that hyperbolic systems with Riemann invariants as a coordinate system can admit global well-posedness even for weak solutions and the construction of accurate and high-order numerical solvers, see e.g.
[
6
,
36
,
40
]
.

The findings on the Riemann invariants enable us to provide all rarefaction-wave curves and Rankine-Hugoniot loci. Thus, we can determine the exact solutions to the Riemann problem for the reduced hyperbolic system (
3.3
) by solving a set of nonlinear algebraic equations in the state space. In this way, we are automatically led to a finite volume scheme with Godunov flux. The scheme is validated, and some numerical simulations are shown. We compare our results with a Riemann solver free Lax-Friedrichs scheme, which has been established as a prototype stable and convergent scheme for many one-dimensional hyperbolic systems. Comparison with the Lax-Friedrichs scheme validates the accuracy and robustness of the constructed Riemann solver.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
is devoted to developing the mathematical model for thin film flow and the evolution equations of film thickness and bulk concentration in each layer. In Section
3
, we obtain the reduced first-order system, show that the reduced model is strictly hyperbolic, and discuss its basic properties. Section
4
is devoted to derive entropy/entropy-flux pairs with convex entropy and hence to verify the local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for the reduced hyperbolic system. In Section
5
, we discuss a Riemann problem for the reduced hyperbolic system and supplement it with some numerical experiments in Section
6
. Concluding remarks are provided in Section
7
.

2.1
Governing equations and boundary conditions

We consider the dynamics of two viscous, incompressible, Newtonian, and immiscible fluid films occupying the two-dimensional,
time-dependent open sets

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}
, see Figure
1
. The lower region
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
is bounded from below
by a horizontal impermeable and fixed bottom, whereas the upper region
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}
is bounded from above by a free surface. The regions are separated by an impermeable fluid-fluid interfacial curve. We assume that both films contain a perfectly soluble solute, which increases the surface tension (anti-surfactant solute). Moreover, it is assumed that the interfacial stress separating the fluids is independent of external influences and that the external flow dynamics does not affect the film evolution. All quantities in this section are dimensional.

Let
\displaystyle L^{*}
be the length of the film and denote the characteristic film height by
\displaystyle H^{*}
. For our study, we assume that the film heights are small as compared to the film lengths, i.e., for the ratio we have
\displaystyle H^{*}/L^{*}=\epsilon
with
\displaystyle\epsilon\ll 1
. Thus, it is justified to neglect the effects of gravity.
We denote by
\displaystyle x^{*}
and
\displaystyle z^{*}
the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively. Moreover, the two film heights are supposed to be given for time
\displaystyle t^{*}
by the graphs of functions
\displaystyle f^{*}=f(x^{*},t^{*})
and
\displaystyle g^{*}=g^{*}(x^{*},t^{*}
) such that the interface is located at
\displaystyle z^{*}=f^{*}(x^{*},t^{*})
, and the free surface is located at
\displaystyle z^{*}=(f^{*}+g^{*})(x^{*},t^{*})
. The bottom layer is located at
\displaystyle z^{*}=0
.
The outward unit normal
\displaystyle{\mathbf{\hat{n}_{1}}}^{*}
and unit tangent vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{t}_{1}}^{*}
of the interface are given for
\displaystyle n_{1}=\sqrt{1+(\partial_{x^{*}}f^{*})^{2}}
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbf{\hat{n}_{1}}}^{*}=n^{-1}_{1}\left(-\partial_{x^{*}}f^{*},1\right)^{\top}\text{ and }\,\mathbf{\hat{t}_{1}}^{*}=n^{-1}_{1}\left(1,\partial_{x^{*}}f^{*}\right)^{\top}.

Analogously, with
\displaystyle n_{2}=\sqrt{1+\left(\partial_{x^{*}}(f^{*}+g^{*})\right)^{2}}
we define for the free surface

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{n}_{2}^{*}}=n_{2}^{-1}\left(-\partial_{x^{*}}(f^{*}+g^{*}),1\right)^{\top}\text{ and }\,\mathbf{\hat{t}_{2}}^{*}=n_{2}^{-1}\left(1,\partial_{x^{*}}(f^{*}+g^{*})\right)^{\top}.

The dynamics of the two immiscible fluids is governed by the Navier-Stokes equations. The transport of bulk concentrations of the anti-surfactant adheres to advection-diffusion equations. As for the geometrical quantities, we mark the physical quantities in
\displaystyle\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle i\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}=(u_{i},w_{i})^{\top}

\displaystyle p_{i}^{*}
and the bulk concentration by
\displaystyle c_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 150.79968pt\nabla^{*}.\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{*}\left(\partial_{t^{*}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}+\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}.\nabla^{*}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}\right)+\nabla^{*}p_{i}^{*}-\mu_{i}^{*}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}

The solute boundary conditions (

Here,

Finally, since the solute is assumed to be perfectly soluble, the bulk concentration in
\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle\{z^{*}=f^{*}+g^{*}\}
given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle-D_{b_{2}}^{*}\nabla^{*}c_{2}^{*}.\mathbf{\hat{n}_{2}^{*}}=0,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{*}
and the surface tension
\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{*}

If we assume equilibrium (see Remark

\displaystyle c^{*}_{1}

\displaystyle C_{\rm{interface}}^{*}=-\eta_{11}^{*}c_{1}^{*}-\eta_{12}K_{\rm eq}^{-1}c_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{*}=\eta_{11}^{*}+\eta_{12}^{*}{K^{-1}_{eq}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{*}=\sigma_{0}^{*}+R^{*}T^{*}\eta_{1}^{*}c_{1}^{*}.

\displaystyle R^{*}
is the ideal gas constant,
\displaystyle T^{*}

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}_{0}
is the interfacial tension of the pure solvent, and
\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{*}c_{1}^{*}
is the effective interfacial concentration.

For the free surface, we also use a linear surface-tension law from
[

,
15

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{*}=\sigma_{s}^{*}+R^{*}T^{*}\eta_{2}^{*}c_{2}^{*},

(2.11)

where
\displaystyle\sigma_{s}^{*}
is the surface tension of pure solvent at the upper layer and
\displaystyle\eta_{2}^{*}
is the assigned thickness of the free surface.

Remark 2.2
.

(i) To derive the surface tension relations (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) we have implicitly used the assumption that the anti-surfactant is perfectly soluble in the interface and the surface. For more general laws we refer to the literature (cf. eq. (6) in
[
15
]
). These treat cases such that
the ratio of the rate of particle adsorption to, and desorption from the interface or the free surface deviate from one.

(ii) Note that we employ simplified interfacial conditions in this article. In particular, we assume that the interfacial trace concentrations coincide with their corresponding bulk values, i.e.,
\displaystyle c_{i,\rm{int}}^{*}=c_{i}^{*}
for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2\}
, which is true in the leading order, see (
2.17
) below. Under this approximation and with equilibrium boundary condition
\displaystyle c_{1}^{*}=K_{eq}c_{2}^{*}
, the interfacial stress law becomes a function of
\displaystyle c_{1}^{*}
only. In a more realistic setting, the interfacial stress generally depends on the coupled interfacial composition of both layers. In particular, the trace values of interfacial concentrations need not to be equal to the bulk concentrations, and must be determined from interfacial equilibrium and mass-transfer constraints. Retaining these effects would introduce additional interfacial unknowns and a two-way coupling between the transport problems in the two fluids, substantially enriching and complicating the resulting dynamics. However, for the purpose of this article, we restrict attention to the simplified boundary conditions described above, which capture the leading-order influence of solute variations on interfacial stresses while keeping the model analytically and computationally tractable.

3
A two-layer thin film model on the large scale

We identify in this section a first-order sub-system of the evolution equations
(
2.29

dynamics and refers to a specific flow regime, both of which we explain in detail
in Section

. We show in Section

[

3.1
The first-order system for the asymptotic dynamics of the thin film model

The equations (
2.29
) contain the
third-order terms
\displaystyle Q_{i}
that vanish if we set for the inverse of the capillarity numbers
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Ca_{i}})^{-1}=0
. Furthermore we neglect diffusion and choose
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Pb_{i}})^{-1}=0
.
The remaining equations are given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\partial_{t}f+\partial_{x}\left(\dfrac{f^{2}(\mathrm{Ma_{1}}\partial_{x}c_{1}+\mu\mathrm{Ma_{2}}\partial_{x}c_{2})}{2}\right)&=&{0,}\\[8.1805pt]
\partial_{t}g+\partial_{x}\left(fg\,\mathrm{Ma_{1}}\partial_{x}c_{1}+\mathrm{Ma_{2}}\partial_{x}c_{2}\left(g\left(\mu f+\dfrac{g}{2}\right)\right)\right)&=&{0,}\\[8.1805pt]
f\partial_{t}c_{1}+\left(\dfrac{f^{2}}{2}\left(\mathrm{Ma_{1}}\partial_{x}c_{1}+\mu\mathrm{Ma_{2}}\partial_{x}c_{2}\right)\right)\partial_{x}c_{1}&=&0,\\[8.1805pt]
g\partial_{t}c_{2}+\left(\mathrm{Ma_{1}}\partial_{x}c_{1}fg+\mathrm{Ma_{2}}\partial_{x}c_{2}\left(g\left(\mu f+\dfrac{g}{2}\right)\right)\right)\partial_{x}c_{2}&=&0.\end{array}

(3.1)

The assumptions about the capillarity
and Péclet numbers select a specific flow regime. But regardless of whether the assumptions are justified, the equations (
3.1
) describe the
basic dynamics of our two-layer system.
A particularly interesting sub-system is found when we set
\displaystyle\mu=0
and choose the notional thicknesses
\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{*}
and the concentration reference numbers
\displaystyle C_{i}^{*}
in such a way that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ma_{1}}=\mathrm{Ma_{2}}=1

holds. The reduced system of evolution equations for the large-scale dynamics is then given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(f^{2}\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial x}\right)&=&0,\\[3.99994pt]
\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial t}+\dfrac{1}{2}f\left(\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}&=&0,\\[8.1805pt]
\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}g^{2}\dfrac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial x}+fg\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial x}\right)&=&0,\\[3.99994pt]
\dfrac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial t}+\dfrac{1}{2}g\left(\dfrac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}+f\left(\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial x}\right)\left(\dfrac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial x}\right)&=&0.\end{array}

(3.2)

To uncover the conservation law structure of (
3.2
) we differentiate the second and the fourth equations in
(
3.2
)
with respect to
\displaystyle x
, respectively, and obtain
for the film thicknesses
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle g
and the concentration gradients

\displaystyle\displaystyle b=\dfrac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial x},\quad q=\dfrac{\partial c_{2}}{\partial x}

the following system of first-order conservation laws:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}f^{2}b\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,\vskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial b}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}fb^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,\vskip 5.69046pt

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{g^{2}q}{2}+fgb\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,\vskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial q}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{gq^{2}}{2}+fbq\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0

The remainder of the paper is devoted to a detailed study of the system (
3.3
) which lays the foundations to understand (
3.1
) and even the fourth-order system from Section
2.5
.

Remark 3.1
.

(i)

The system (
3.1
) could have been also directly derived by an appropriate scaling of the capillarity
and Péclet numbers in terms of the height-to-length ratio
\displaystyle\varepsilon
. But then we would have not found the fourth-order system (
2.29
) which holds for any choice of the numbers. In what follows we provide a rather complete theory for the first-order system (
3.3
).

(ii)

The assumption that the viscosity ratio
\displaystyle\mu
is very small holds for e.g. oil-water type flows. Clearly, this means that the bottom layer should be more viscous than the upper layer. From the mathematical point of view, we observe that the choice
\displaystyle\mu=0
decouples the first two equations in (
3.3
). Also, we recover the one-layer system (
1.1
) by setting
\displaystyle f=b=0
.

(iii)

Note that the equilibrium condition
\displaystyle c_{1}=K_{eq}c_{2}
at the interface can be recovered from the reduced first-order system. For
\displaystyle K_{eq}>0
, if we have
\displaystyle K_{eq}f-g=0
, then at the level sets
\displaystyle fb=gq
we obtain the interfacial boundary conditions (
2.8
) because
\displaystyle f,b,g,q
satisfy
\displaystyle g/f=b/q=K_{eq}
. In Section
4.1
and Section
5
, we observe that these ratios can be expressed as Riemann invariants of the system (
3.3
). Note, however, that we
do not consider the case
\displaystyle fb=gq
, which may give rise to a possible resonance phenomenon in a purely first-order theory.

The quasilinear system (
3.3
) can be written in the compact conservative form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{t}+(\mathbf{F}{(\mathbf{U}))}_{x}=0,

(3.4)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=(f,b,g,q)^{\top}\in\mathcal{U}
denotes the vector of conservative variables and

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})=\left(\frac{1}{2}f^{2}b,\frac{1}{2}fb^{2},\frac{1}{2}g^{2}q+fbg,\frac{1}{2}gq^{2}+fbq\right)^{\top}

is the flux vector. The state space
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
is introduced as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\Big\{(f,b,g,q)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:f,b,g,q>0,fb<gq\Big\}.

(3.5)

For the well-posedness of the full system (
2.23
)-(
2.28
) it is essential that the reduced first-order system (
3.3
) is hyperbolic and equipped with a proper entropy structure
in the state space. We study these issues in the
remainder of the article.

Remark 3.2
.

The state space
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
is restricted to the case
\displaystyle f>0,b>0,g>0,q>0
and
\displaystyle fb<gq
which ensures the strict hyperbolicity of the system (
3.3
) as we will see in Section
3.2
, see also Remark

3.3
.

3.2
Strict hyperbolicity of the reduced system (
3.3
)

For smooth solutions, we can convert the system
(
3.3
)
into its primitive form as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{t}+\mathbf{DF}(\mathbf{U})\mathbf{U}_{x}=0,

with the flux Jacobian

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{DF}(\mathbf{U})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}fb&\frac{1}{2}f^{2}&0&0\\
\frac{1}{2}b^{2}&fb&0&0\\
gb&fg&fb+gq&\frac{1}{2}g^{2}\\
bq&fq&\frac{1}{2}q^{2}&fb+qg\\
\end{array}\right).

Due to its block matrix structure, the eigenvalues
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{U})
of
\displaystyle\mathbf{DF}(\mathbf{U})
can be explicitly computed for

\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,4
. They are real and obey for
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=(f,b,g,q)^{\top}\in\mathcal{U}
the ordering

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{U})=\dfrac{fb}{2}<~\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{U})=\dfrac{3fb}{2}<~\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{U})=fb+\dfrac{gq}{2}<~\lambda_{4}(\mathbf{U})=fb+\dfrac{3gq}{2}.

(3.10)

Moreover, the (right) eigenvectors
\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{k}}=\mathbf{r_{k}}(\mathbf{U})
are

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{1}}(\mathbf{U})=\left(-\frac{f}{b},1,0,0\right)^{\top},&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{2}}(\mathbf{U})=\left(\frac{fb-3gq}{4qb},\frac{fb-3gq}{4qf},\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top},\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{3}}(\mathbf{U})=\left(0,0,-\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top},&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{4}}(\mathbf{U})=\left(0,0,\frac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top}.\end{array}

(3.11)

The eigenvectors are linearly independent in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
. This implies the strict hyperbolicity of the reduced system (
3.3
) in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
.

Next, we obtain with the eigenvector formulas for the four characteristic fields

\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}\lambda_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r_{1}}=\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}\lambda_{3}\cdot\mathbf{r_{3}}=0\text{ and }\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}\lambda_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r_{2}}=3fb\text{ and }\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}\lambda_{4}\cdot\mathbf{r_{4}}=3gq.

(3.12)

Thus, the first and the third characteristic fields are linearly degenerate, while the second and the fourth characteristic fields of (
3.3
) are genuinely nonlinear in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

[
18
]
. Note that
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}
denotes the gradient of any field with respect to the vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=(f,b,g,q)^{T}
. The properties in (
3.12
) will be essential for solving the Riemann problem for (
3.3
) in Section

5
.

Remark 3.3
.

(i)
We consider here only the case
\displaystyle f,b,g,q>0
for which strict hyperbolicty can be shown. The same holds for negative concentration gradients and for concentration gradients with
\displaystyle b<0
and
\displaystyle q>0
.
We conjecture that a similar analysis to the following one can be done in these cases. The case
\displaystyle q<0
is not clear since then a Riemann invariant gets complex, see (
4.2
) below. For a more complete study of different choices of state spaces, we refer to
[
6
]
.

(ii)
In order to maintain strict hyperbolicity, we assume that
\displaystyle gq>fb
holds for
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}
, see (
3.5
). For the opposite case
\displaystyle fb>gq
, we have also strict hyperbolicity, but the ordering of eigenvalues changes, and the second and third wave change their role as long as
\displaystyle fb<3gq
. In the critical case
\displaystyle fb=gq
, eigenvalues coincide. The system remains hyperbolic and possesses a full set of eigenvectors. Due to the presence of the full set of eigenvectors, one can still define the rarefaction curves as the integral curves of the second and fourth characteristic fields. However, additional resonant waves may occur due to the recurrence of eigenvalues; see Remark
5.1
.

4
Entropy/entropy-flux pairs and well-posedness

Hyperbolicity is a necessary condition to ensure the well-posedness of the initial-value problem for (
3.3
). In this section, we show that the reduced system has much more structure.
Precisely, we propose a class of entropy/entropy-flux pairs for (
3.3
) and identify a specific pair with convex entropy. By an entropy/entropy-flux pair, we mean a pair

\displaystyle(E,Q):\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle(E,Q)
satisfies

\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}E(\mathbf{U})^{\top}\mathbf{DF(U)}=\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}Q(\mathbf{U})^{\top}\text{ for all $\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}$}.

(4.1)

The compatibility condition (
4.1
) implies in particular that any smooth solution
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}(x,t)
of (
3.3
) is entropy conservative, i.e.,

{E(\mathbf{U}(x,t))}_{t}+{Q(\mathbf{U}(x,t))}_{x}=0\mbox{ in $\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)$}

holds. For more implications in case of weak solutions, we refer to
[
18
,
36
]
.

4.1
Riemann invariants

To explore the entropic structure of (
3.3
), we rely on the finding that the strictly hyperbolic system (
3.3
) is equipped with a full set of Riemann invariants. Indeed, three
\displaystyle k
-Riemann invariants
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{k},\Gamma_{2}^{k},\Gamma_{3}^{k}
corresponding to the
\displaystyle k\rm{th}
-characteristic field,
\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,4
, are given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Gamma_{1}^{1}=g,~\quad\Gamma_{2}^{1}=q,~\quad\Gamma_{3}^{1}=fb,\\
\Gamma_{1}^{2}={b}/{f},~\Gamma_{2}^{2}={q}/{g},~\Gamma_{3}^{2}={(fb+gq)}/{(gq)^{1/4}},\\

(4.2)

From (

\xi=w_{1}=b/f,\quad u=w_{2}=fb,\quad\tau=w_{3}=q/g,\quad\eta=w_{4}=(fb+gq)/(gq)^{1/4}.

(4.3)

These form a coordinate system for (

Lemma 4.1
.

The functions
\displaystyle w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}
from (
4.3

3.3

) in terms of
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4})^{\top}
is equivalent to the diagonal system

\displaystyle\displaystyle w_{k,t}+\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{W})){w_{k,x}}=0\qquad(k=1,\ldots,4).

(4.4)

Proof.

A necessary and sufficient condition for
\displaystyle w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}
to form a coordinate system for (

) is that the gradients

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}w_{k}

are left eigenvectors of the flux Jacobian
\displaystyle\mathbf{D}\mathbf{F}
(see Theorem 7.3.1 in
[
18

), i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}w_{k}(\mathbf{U}).\mathbf{r}_{j}(\mathbf{U})\begin{cases}\neq 0~{\rm{if}}~j=k,\\
=0~{\rm{if}}~j\neq k,\end{cases}~\forall~k,j=1,\ldots,4\qquad(\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}),

(4.5)

with
\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{j}
being the
\displaystyle j
th eigenvector of the reduced system (
3.3
).

Here, we only prove the statement (
4.5
) for the most complicated function
\displaystyle w_{4}
and leave the other similar computations to the reader. Now, we directly compute

\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}w_{4}(\mathbf{U})=\left(\dfrac{b}{(gq)^{1/4}},\dfrac{f}{(bq)^{1/4}},\dfrac{-q(fb-3gq)}{4(gq)^{5/4}},\dfrac{-g(fb-3gq)}{4(gq)^{5/4}}\right)^{\top}.

It is straightforward to compute
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}w_{4}(\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{r_{j}}(\mathbf{U})=0
for
\displaystyle j\in\{1,2,3\}
while
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}w_{4}(\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{r_{4}}(\mathbf{U})=\dfrac{-g(fb-3gq)}{2(gq)^{5/4}}\neq 0
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}
, which implies that
\displaystyle w_{4}
satisfies (
4.5
).

For the equivalence of (
3.3
) and (
4.4

[

.
∎

In terms of the variables
\displaystyle\xi,u,\tau,\eta

) writes out for

as

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\xi_{t}+\dfrac{u}{2}\xi_{x}&=&0,&u_{t}+\dfrac{3u}{2}u_{x}&=&0,\\[6.45831pt]
\tau_{t}+\left(u+\dfrac{v}{2}\right)\tau_{x}&=&0,&\quad\eta_{t}+\left(u+\dfrac{3v}{2}\right)\eta_{x}&=&0.\end{array}

(4.6)

Before we return to the entropy discussion, let us mention that
\displaystyle k

-rarefaction waves, which will be used in Section

[

Remark 4.2

The statements of Lemma

4.2
Entropy/entropy-flux pairs for the system (
3.3
)

To prove (
4.1
) for some given pair
\displaystyle(E,Q)
, it suffices to verify the corresponding relation for the transformed system (
4.6
).

Theorem 4.3

(Entropy/entropy-flux pairs)
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho,\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
be arbitrary functions in
\displaystyle C^{1}(0,\infty)
.
For the system (
3.3
), there exists a class of entropy/entropy-flux pairs
\displaystyle(E,Q)
which is
given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}E[f,b,g,q]&=&\rho(fb)+\sqrt{fb}\mu\left(\dfrac{b}{\sqrt{fb}}\right)+\sqrt{gq}\nu\left(\dfrac{q}{\sqrt{gq}}\right)+\dfrac{1}{fb+gq},\\[12.91663pt]
Q[f,b,g,q]&=&\psi(fb)+(fb)^{3/2}\mu\left(\dfrac{b}{\sqrt{fb}}\right)+(\sqrt{gq})\nu\left(\dfrac{q}{\sqrt{gq}}\right)(fb+gq)\\
&&\hskip 99.58464pt-\ln\left((fb+gq)^{3/2}\right)-\dfrac{fb}{2(fb+gq)}.\end{array}

(4.7)

The function
\displaystyle\psi
is determined from
\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(w)=\dfrac{3}{2}w\rho^{\prime}(w)
.

Proof.

We have to prove the compatibility relation (
4.1
) for
\displaystyle(E,Q)
from (
4.7
). In view of the bijectivity of the map
\displaystyle(\xi,u,\tau,\eta)\mapsto(f,b,g,q)
, it suffices to show that
the transformed compatibility relation holds for the functions
\displaystyle E[\xi,u,\tau,\eta]:=E[f,b,g,q]
and

\displaystyle Q[\xi,u,\tau,\eta]:=Q[f,b,g,q]
. From (
4.6
) we observe that the componentwise
relations are given by

Q_{\xi}=\dfrac{u}{2}E_{\xi},\,\,Q_{u}=\dfrac{3u}{2}E_{u},\,\,Q_{\tau}=\left(u+\dfrac{v}{2}\right)E_{\tau},\,\,Q_{\eta}=\left(u+\dfrac{3v}{2}\right)E_{\eta}.

(4.8)

The quantity
\displaystyle v=gq
is related to the functions
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle\eta
by the nonlinear relation
\displaystyle u+v=\eta v^{1/4}
.

The functions
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle Q
from (
4.7
) in terms of the Riemann invariants
\displaystyle\xi,u,\tau,\eta
are given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}E[\xi,u,\tau,\eta]&=&\rho(u)+\sqrt{u}\mu(\xi)+\sqrt{v}\nu(\tau)+\dfrac{1}{\eta v^{1/4}},\\[8.61108pt]
Q[\xi,u,\tau,\eta]&=&\psi(u)+\dfrac{u^{3/2}}{2}\mu(\xi)+\dfrac{\sqrt{v}\nu(\tau)}{2}\left(\eta v^{1/4}+u\right)-\dfrac{3}{2}\ln\left(\eta v^{1/4}\right)-\dfrac{u}{2\eta v^{1/4}}.\end{array}

We readily check that the pair
\displaystyle\left(E[\xi,u,\tau,\eta],Q[\xi,u,\tau,\eta]\right)
satisfies (
4.8
).
∎

We have provided a class of entropy/entropy flux pairs for system (
3.3
). We can actually choose a generic strictly convex entropy within this class. For systems equipped with a set of Riemann invariants as a coordinate system, the convexity condition of an entropy
\displaystyle E
gets simpler: it is sufficient to check that the Hessian
\displaystyle H_{E}
of
\displaystyle E

\mathbf{r}_{k}(\mathbf{U})^{\top}H_{E}(\mathbf{U})\,\mathbf{r_{k}(\mathbf{U})}>0\qquad\text{for all }~k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}\text{ and all $\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U}$.}

(4.9)

Theorem 4.4

(Convex entropy)
.

The system (
3.3
) is endowed with at least one entropy/entropy-flux pair
\displaystyle(\bar{E},\bar{Q})
with strictly convex entropy
\displaystyle\bar{E}
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
. It is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{E}[f,b,g,q]=\dfrac{1}{fb}+\dfrac{f^{3/2}}{\sqrt{b}}+\dfrac{1}{fb+gq}+\dfrac{g^{3/2}}{\sqrt{q}}.

(4.10)

The associated entropy flux computes as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{Q}[f,b,g,q]=-\dfrac{3}{2}\ln\left((fb(fb+gq))\right)+\dfrac{f^{5/2}\sqrt{b}}{2}-\dfrac{fb}{2(fb+gq)}+g^{5/2}\sqrt{q}\left(fb+\dfrac{gq}{2}\right).

Proof.

With the choice
\displaystyle\rho(u)=u^{-1},\,\mu(\xi)=\xi^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\nu(\tau)=\tau^{-1}
, the function
\displaystyle\bar{E}
defined in (
4.10
) belongs to the class of entropies for system (
3.3
) found in Theorem

4.3
. Moreover, by using the relation
\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(u)=\dfrac{3u\rho^{\prime}(u)}{2}
, and using the values of
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
in the expression of
\displaystyle Q
in Theorem
4.3
, it is straightforward to obtain the expression for the entropy flux
\displaystyle\bar{Q}
. Thus, the pair
\displaystyle(\bar{E},\bar{Q})
forms an entropy/entropy-flux pair for the system (
3.3
). Therefore, we just need to check the strict convexity of
\displaystyle\bar{E}
via (
4.9
).
We directly compute the Hessian
\displaystyle H_{\bar{E}}(\mathbf{U})
of
\displaystyle\bar{E}(\mathbf{U})
, which reads as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{2b^{2}}{(bf+gq)^{3}}+\frac{8+3f^{2}\sqrt{fb}}{4bf^{3}}&\frac{4-3\sqrt{fb}}{4b^{2}f^{2}}+\frac{bf-gq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2bq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2bg}{(bf+gq)^{3}}\\
\frac{4-3\sqrt{fb}}{4b^{2}f^{2}}+\frac{bf-gq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2f^{2}}{(bf+gq)^{3}}+\frac{8+3f^{2}\sqrt{fb}}{4b^{3}f}&\frac{2fq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2fg}{(bf+gq)^{3}}\\
\frac{2bq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2fq}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2q^{2}}{(bf+gq)^{3}}+\frac{3}{4\sqrt{gq}}&\frac{gq-bf}{(bf+gq)^{3}}-\frac{3\sqrt{g}}{4q^{3/2}}\\
\frac{2bg}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{2fg}{(bf+gq)^{3}}&\frac{gq-bf}{(bf+gq)^{3}}-\frac{3\sqrt{g}}{4q^{3/2}}&\frac{2g^{2}}{(bf+gq)^{3}}+\frac{3g^{3/2}}{4q^{5/2}}\\
\end{array}\right).

A tedious but straightforward calculation leads to (skipping the
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}
-argument) the following positive numbers
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{1}^{\top}}H_{\bar{E}}\mathbf{r_{1}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{3f^{3/2}}{\sqrt{b}}+\frac{2bf}{(bf+gq)^{2}}+\frac{2}{bf},\quad\mathbf{r_{2}^{\top}}H_{\bar{E}}\mathbf{r_{2}}=\frac{6\left(3g^{2}q^{2}+2bf(gq-fb)\right)}{b^{3}f^{3}(3gq-fb)(bf+gq)},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{r_{3}^{\top}}H_{\bar{E}}\mathbf{r_{3}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2gq}{(bf+gq)^{2}}+\frac{3g^{3/2}}{\sqrt{q}},~~\quad\qquad\mathbf{r_{4}^{\top}}H_{\bar{E}}\mathbf{r_{4}}=\frac{2gq(3gq-bf)}{(bf+gq)^{3}}.

\displaystyle\bar{E}
satisfies (

) and the chosen entropy
\displaystyle\bar{E}
is strictly convex.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The choice of the strictly convex entropy
\displaystyle\bar{E}
is not unique, and other strictly convex entropies can be constructed based on the diagonal structure of the system (
4.4
) and a proof similar to that of Theorem
4.4
.
In fluid mechanics, the entropy typically has a thermodynamical interpretation. We are not aware whether this is also true for
\displaystyle\bar{E}
or other possible convex entropies. However, the expression of
\displaystyle\bar{E}
indicates that for very small thin films,
\displaystyle\bar{E}
increases rapidly, so to keep the system stable, film thickness should not be too low or the concentration gradient should be of a larger scale. For other possible state spaces, the existence of convex entropy can still be ensured due to the work of Conlon and Liu
[
12
]
. Moreover, the entropy/entropy-flux pairs for the system (
3.3
) become the entropy/entropy flux pairs of the one-layer system (
1.1
) for
\displaystyle f=0
and all values of
\displaystyle b
.

4.3
Local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for the system (
3.3
)

For general quasilinear systems of conservation laws, the Cauchy problem may not be well-posed. However, for Friedrichs-symmetrizable systems, the (local) well-posedness of classical solutions is guaranteed (see
[
18
,
24
,
27
,
36
]
and references cited therein). If a quasilinear system of conservation laws is equipped with an entropy/entropy-flux pair with strictly convex entropy, it is a Friedrichs-symmetrizable system. In Section
4.2
we found such a pair for the hyperbolic system (
3.3
). Therefore, we obtain immediately the local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for system (
3.3
) with initial data in the Sobolev space
\displaystyle(H^{m}(\mathbb{R}))^{4}
for
\displaystyle m>3/2
such that for a finite time
\displaystyle T<\infty
, there exists a unique solution
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in C([0,T];(H^{m}(\mathbb{R}))^{4})\cap C^{1}([0,T];(H^{m-1}(\mathbb{R}))^{4})
.

In this section, we first compute the elementary wave curves for the system (
3.3

3.3
). To solve the Riemann problem of the (
\displaystyle 4\times 4
)-system (
3.3
), it is crucial that the
\displaystyle 4
th characteristic field
turns out to be a Temple field, i.e., the
\displaystyle 4
th shock-wave curve coincides with the integral curve that defines the rarefaction waves of the field

5.1
Elementary waves

We determine the relations that are valid across the different types of admissible elementary waves (corresponding to
\displaystyle k
-shock waves,
\displaystyle k
-contact discontinuities and
\displaystyle k
-rarefaction waves) as algebraic equations. Admissibility refers to the property of an elementary wave to be an entropy solution of (

5.1.1
Rarefaction waves

Before we construct rarefaction wave curves, we
recall that the Riemann invariants
\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{1},\Gamma^{k}_{2},\Gamma^{k}_{3}
from (

) remain constant across a corresponding
\displaystyle k

A
\displaystyle k
-rarefaction wave can only exist for a genuinely nonlinear field. Therefore we consider the cases
\displaystyle k\in\{2,4\}

We start with
\displaystyle k=2

\displaystyle 2
-rarefaction wave is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{dx}{dt}=\dfrac{x}{t}=\lambda_{2}=\dfrac{3fb}{2}.

The characteristic speed increases across the rarefaction wave, which means that
\displaystyle fb\geq f_{l}b_{l}
must hold for any left state
\displaystyle\mathbf{U_{l}}=(f_{l},b_{l},g_{l},q_{l})^{\top}\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}_{1},\Gamma^{2}_{2},\Gamma^{2}_{3}

\displaystyle 2
-rarefaction wave is given for the left state
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{l}
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{2}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{dx}{dt}=\dfrac{x}{t}=\lambda_{2}=\dfrac{3fb}{2},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\dfrac{f}{b}=\dfrac{f_{l}}{b_{l}},~\dfrac{fb+gq}{(gq)^{1/4}}=\dfrac{f_{l}b_{l}+g_{l}q_{l}}{(g_{l}q_{l})^{1/4}},~\dfrac{g}{q}=\dfrac{g_{l}}{q_{l}},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
fb\geq f_{l}b_{l}.\end{cases}

(5.1)

\displaystyle R_{2}
is a curve in the
\displaystyle(f,b,g,q)
-space emanating from
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{l}
.

Similarly, the
\displaystyle 4
-rarefaction wave curve from a left state
\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{l}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{4}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{dx}{dt}=\dfrac{x}{t}=\lambda_{4}=fb+\dfrac{3gq}{2},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\dfrac{g}{q}=\dfrac{g_{l}}{q_{l}},~f=f_{l},b=b_{l},\vskip 5.69046pt\\

(5.2)

Remark 5.1
.

Note that the existence of rarefaction waves can be ensured even for the possible resonant case
\displaystyle fb=gq
, which is excluded for states in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
. The system (

) possesses a full set of eigenvectors even for
\displaystyle fb=gq

). For the second characteristic field with a given left state

and

, the 2-rarefaction curve is obtained by solving the IVP

\dfrac{d\mathbf{U}}{d\nu}=\mathbf{r}_{2}(\mathbf{U}),\quad\mathbf{U}(\nu_{l})=\mathbf{U}_{l},

where

\mathbf{r}_{2}(\mathbf{U})=\left(\dfrac{-g}{2b},\dfrac{-g}{2f},\dfrac{g}{q},1\right)^{\top}.

In view of the smoothness of

Note that this curve is different from the 3-contact discontinuity curve; see (

) below. However, due to

\displaystyle 2

20

(ii).

5.1.2
Discontinuous waves: shock and contact waves

For speed
\displaystyle\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, a discontinuous wave

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}_{l}=(f_{l},b_{l},g_{l},q_{l})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{U}}&:~x-\sigma t<0,\\

(5.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma[\![\mathbf{U}]\!]=[\![\mathbf{F(U)}]\!]

\displaystyle[\![\mathbf{W}]\!]=\mathbf{W}_{r}-\mathbf{W}_{l}

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{l},\mathbf{W}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}

For the system (

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\sigma[\![f]\!]&=&\!\!\!\!\dfrac{1}{2}[\![f^{2}b]\!],&\sigma[\![b]\!]&=&\!\!\!\!\dfrac{1}{2}[\![fb^{2}]\!],\\

(5.5)

Along the first and third linearly degenerate characteristic fields, for a given left state
\displaystyle\mathbf{U_{l}}=(f_{l},b_{l},g_{l},q_{l})^{\top}\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U_{l}}
in the
\displaystyle(f,b,g,q)
-space, which are given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{1}:=\begin{cases}\sigma_{1}=\dfrac{fb}{2}=\dfrac{f_{l}b_{l}}{2},\\
fb=f_{l}b_{l},~g=g_{l},~q=q_{l}\end{cases}

(5.6)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{3}:=\begin{cases}\sigma_{3}=fb+\dfrac{gq}{2}=f_{l}b_{l}+\dfrac{g_{l}q_{l}}{2},\\
f=f_{l},~b=b_{l},~gq=g_{l}q_{l}.\end{cases}

(5.7)

The corresponding
\displaystyle 1
- and
\displaystyle 3
-contact waves (
5.3
) are entropy solutions of (
3.3
) by definition.

For
\displaystyle k\in\{2,4\}
, the
\displaystyle k

For
\displaystyle k=2

by eliminating

\displaystyle 2-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{U})<\sigma_{2}<\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{U_{l}}),\,\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{U_{l}})<\sigma_{2}.

From the first two equations of (

\sigma_{2}=\dfrac{b_{l}(f_{l}^{2}+f_{l}f+f^{2})}{2f_{l}}.

Therefore, by eliminating
\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\dfrac{f}{b}=\dfrac{f_{l}}{b_{l}},\quad\dfrac{g}{q}=\dfrac{g_{l}}{q_{l}},~g=g_{l}+\Phi(f_{l},b_{l},g_{l},q_{l},f,b,g,q),

5.2
Solution of the Riemann problem

Consider the Riemann problem for (
3.3
), that is, the Cauchy problem with initial data of the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(x,0)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}_{L}=(f_{L},b_{L},g_{L},q_{L})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{U}}&:~x<0,\\
\mathbf{U}_{R}=(f_{R},b_{R},g_{R},q_{R})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{U}}&:~x>0.\end{cases}

(5.11)

Note that Lax curves overlap with each other in the one-layer thin film hyperbolic model, which simplifies the construction of the Riemann solver, which is not the case here. The relations across
\displaystyle R_{2}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
render the Lax curves to be nonlinear and different from each other. Thus, it is not easy to connect the Lax curves of two different families for solving the Riemann problem. We provide a novel construction of the Riemann solver by proving that these nonlinearities can be converted into a set of nonlinear algebraic equations, for which we can find a solution.

Due to the fact that the first and the third characteristic fields allow for contact discontinuities only, there are four wave configurations possible for the solution to the Riemann problem based on different choices of initial data. In (

-

7
Conclusions

In this work, we derived a reduced system of hyperbolic conservation laws governing the evolution of a two-layer thin film with a perfectly soluble anti-surfactant solute. The obtained system not only models surface-tension driven flow of a two-layer film but also exhibits a rich mathematical structure relevant to the theory of hyperbolic conservation laws. A crucial outcome of this work is the identification of a novel set of entropy/entropy-flux pairs, a set of Riemann invariants that forms a coordinate system, and an exact Riemann solver as the foundation for finite volume solvers. We emphasize that the results presented here were derived entirely within the framework of classical waves for hyperbolic conservation laws (shock, rarefaction, contact). This naturally raises the question of how the solution structure may evolve in more complex settings, particularly in regimes on the boundary of the state space where strict hyperbolicity is lost. The analysis of such scenarios, which may involve intricate wave interactions and structural changes in the solution, is left for further research.

Moreover, our study opens several other new research directions. Extending the model to multi-layer thin film flows is possible. It would be interesting to see how the special mathematical structure we found for the two-layer case is recovered. The same applies if one accounts for gravity and flow on inclined surfaces
[
5
]
. Finally, we think that the found entropy/entropy-flux pairs are important to derive appropriate energy estimates for higher-order lubrication models, including
capillarity and diffusion effects.
The development of entropy-stable numerical schemes, particularly high-order finite volume or discontinuous Galerkin methods, is a natural next step. Moreover, leveraging exact or approximate Riemann solvers, including Generalized Riemann solvers
[
6
]
, would enhance the robustness of numerical approximations.
