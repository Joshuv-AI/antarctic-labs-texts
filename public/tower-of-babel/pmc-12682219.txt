Internet-based versus synchronous cognitive behavioral therapy for gambling disorder: Study protocol for a non-inferiority randomized controlled trial
Authors: Anna Westh Stenbro, Thomas Marcussen, David C. Hodgins, Thomas Tandrup Lamm, Lisbeth Frostholm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First RCT to compare asynchronous iCBT with synchronous CBT (in person or remote) for gambling disorder.•Uses a non-inferiority design to assess clinical equivalence of iCBT and sCBT.•Includes detailed process and moderator measures to explore treatment mechanisms.•Employs real-world clinical recruitment to enhance ecological validity.

Background

Gambling Disorder (GD) is defined by persistent or recurrent gambling behavior with impaired control, prioritization of gambling over other activities, and continuation despite negative consequences (ICD-11, WHO, 2024). Lifetime prevalence is estimated at 0.4–1.0 % in the general population (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). In 2024, the Lancet Public Health Commission on Gambling estimated that 46.2 % of adults worldwide had gambled in the past year, with GD affecting 15.8 % of online casino/slot players and 8.9 % of sports bettors (Wardle et al., 2024). Despite the substantial public health burden of GD, treatment uptake remains low, with only about 20 % of affected individuals seeking professional help (Bijker et al., 2022, Statens Folkhälsoinstitut, 2010). In addition, dropout rates from treatment are high, averaging ∼42 % across clinical studies (Dunn et al., 2011, Melville et al., 2007, Pfund et al., 2021). Practical barriers such as travel distance, financial and time costs, or inflexible treatment options, as well as psychological barriers including stigma, shame, preference for self-reliance, and motivational ambivalence, have been identified as central reasons for this treatment gap (Suurvali et al., 2009). Such obstacles can complicate engagement in structured therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which require sustained participation across multiple sessions.

Nevertheless, CBT remains the treatment with the strongest empirical support for GD (Eriksen et al., 2023, Pfund et al., 2023). A recent umbrella review synthesizing five meta-analyses (56 studies, 5389 participants) gave a “strong” recommendation for CBT (Pfund et al., 2023). Compared to minimal or inactive controls, CBT produces clinically meaningful reductions in gambling severity, frequency, and intensity. However, effects vary substantially across individuals, and early dropout is common, limiting potential benefits (Pfund et al., 2020, Pfund et al., 2021). This highlights the need to optimize delivery formats to reduce barriers and improve adherence.

During COVID-19, synchronous video-based CBT (sCBT) became increasingly common, reducing barriers such as travel and stigma while maintaining real-time therapist contact (Smith et al., 2022). However, sCBT still requires scheduling and therapist availability. Internet-based treatment (IBT), by contrast, allows patients to access educational materials, exercises, and therapist support asynchronously through online platforms, providing greater flexibility, discretion, and opportunities for autonomy and immersion. Internet-based CBT (iCBT) for GD has shown promise, reducing symptoms and increasing accessibility (Boumparis et al., 2022). Evidence from other behavioral and substance use disorders further supports iCBT’s effectiveness in improving outcomes while lowering practical barriers (Boumparis et al., 2022, Watkins and Sprang, 2018).

Recent reviews suggest iCBT may have smaller effects than sCBT in indirect comparisons (Eriksen et al., 2023). Interpretation is complicated because sCBT trials often use passive controls, while iCBT trials more frequently include active comparators. It remains unclear whether observed differences reflect true superiority of sCBT or study design artifacts. This uncertainty underscores the need for direct head-to-head comparisons.

This paper presents the protocol for a non-inferiority randomized controlled parallel-group trial comparing the iCBT program, “SpilleFri“ (“Free from Gambling” in Danish) with individual sCBT for GD. SpilleFri (SF) is delivered mainly asynchronously with written therapist support, while sCBT is delivered in real-time sessions in-person or via video connection. The feasibility of SF was previously tested in an uncontrolled pilot with 24 patients, showing acceptable dropout (29 %), high satisfaction, and promising effects, with nearly half of completers achieving remission (Stenbro et al., 2023). Limitations included small sample size, no control group, and short follow-up. The present RCT addresses these by including a larger sample, randomization, an active comparator, longer follow-up, and broader assessments. The head-to-head comparison of iCBT and sCBT addresses the evidence gap on relative treatment effects.

Purpose and aim

Based on the pilot study we formulated the following main hypothesis: SF will not be clinically inferior to sCBT in reducing GD severity at the primary endpoint three months after end-of-treatment.

Further, we aim to explore potential differences in secondary outcomes (change in gambling behaviour, relationship quality, and anxiety, depression, and emotional distress) three months after end-of-treatment.

Methods

Study design

The study is a parallel-group randomized controlled trial designed to assess non-inferiority of iCBT compared to sCBT for individuals diagnosed with GD. Participants will be recruited among patients who contact a specialized outpatient clinic seeking treatment for GD. The primary objective is to test whether iCBT is no less effective than sCBT. Eligible participants will be randomized to one of the two arms (1:1 ratio). The study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committees for the Central Denmark Region (1-10-72-145-24). The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06989125). This protocol paper follows the SPIRIT guidelines for reporting clinical trial protocols (Chan et al., 2013).

Self-reported participant data are collected at eight time points: baseline (T1), randomisation (T2), three time points during treatment (T3, T4, T5), end-of-treatment (T6), 3-month follow-up (T7, primary endpoint), and at 12-month follow-up (T8) (see Fig. 1). Due to differences in treatment duration between the two arms, the 3-month follow-up for the iCBT group is conducted at week 24 post-baseline (T7a), whereas the 3-month follow-up for the sCBT group takes place at week 30 post-baseline (T7b). An additional measurement point is included for the iCBT group at the time when participants in the sCBT group reach their primary endpoint (T7b), in order to control for the effect of time.Fig. 1Flowchart.

Setting

The study is a single-center trial conducted at the Research Clinic for Gambling Disorder (RCGD), Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark. The SF treatment program was developed and feasibility tested in this clinic.

Study population and recruitment

A total of 150 patients with GD will be included over an estimated 2.5-year period. Eligible patients will be identified during first contact at the clinic and recruited consecutively. The participant flow is shown in Fig. 1.

Inclusion criteria

Screening and inclusion criteria are: aged 18 or older, meet diagnostic criteria for GD (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013), score 4 points or more on the National Opinion Research Centre Screen for Gambling Problems (NODS; Wickwire et al., 2008), ability to read, write, and speak Danish, basic IT skills and access to the internet and a computer, smartphone, and/or tablet.

Exclusion criteria

Patients are excluded if they present with severe comorbid somatic illness, cognitive deficits, severe trauma, or psychiatric disorders (e.g. depression, bipolar disorder, substance abuse, ADHD) that may hinder treatment engagement or benefit. Importantly, comorbid conditions are not excluded by default; only those expected to significantly hinder treatment engagement or benefit are grounds for exclusion. This assessment is made through clinical consensus, based on whether (1) gambling is deemed secondary to a primary disorder that dominates the clinical picture and is likely to maintain the gambling behavior if left untreated, or (2) comorbid conditions are assessed to demand such substantial personal resources that participation in gambling disorder treatment would not be feasible at the present time. Exclusion also applies to those unwilling to participate in iCBT or sCBT, those who do not provide informed consent, or seek treatment due to an imminent legal case. Additional criteria include substantial suicide risk (i.e. clear intent or plans), and highly unstable social conditions (e.g. homelessness) that may obstruct treatment.

Individuals previously treated with SpilleFri are excluded, as treatment content remains largely the same despite updates. Those with GD limited to stock/cryptocurrency trading are also excluded, as current screening tools target casino and sports betting and may not capture trading-related problems (Johnson et al., 2023).

Diagnostic assessment and informed consent

Patients who meet the screening criteria receive brief verbal information about the research project during their first contact with the clinic. After this, they receive a brochure with study information and a digital letter with an invitation for a diagnostic interview and a link to a clinical questionnaire.

During the diagnostic interview, the psychologist introduces the research project and answers any questions. Patients are given the opportunity to consider their participation for two days before giving their consent. The diagnostic interview is based on the DSM-IV Structured Clinical Interview for Pathological Gambling (SCI-PG), modified for DSM-5 criteria (Grant et al., 2004, Association, 2013). Experienced clinical psychologists conduct these interviews. The GD diagnosis and eligibility are confirmed at a weekly inclusion conference with the entire team of psychologists before the participants are included in the trial.

Randomization

Included patients will be randomized to SF or sCBT using block randomization with concealed computer-generated block sizes varying randomly from 8 to 12. Randomization occurs immediately after the inclusion conference via the randomization module in REDCap (Harris et al., 2009). After randomization, participants receive a link to the baseline questionnaire (T2) via text message. Upon completing the questionnaire, REDCap informs them of the randomization results. Those randomized to SF receive a link and login instructions immediately. Participants assigned to sCBT are encouraged to contact the clinic to schedule their first session.

Treatment

SpilleFri

SF consists of ten internet-delivered modules (including an introduction module) over 10–12 weeks, covering topics such as cognitive restructuring, coping with cravings, exposure, and response prevention (see Fig. 2). All treatment modules include interactive exercises and multiple-choice lists where participants work with thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Assignments are combined with psychoeducation via text, illustrations, videos with GD experts and former patients, and audio clips with mindfulness and visualization exercises. One module (“My relatives”) is optional. Participants can access the platform anytime via smartphone, tablet, or computer via a GDPR-compliant website, using the Danish digital identification system MitID.Fig. 2Overview of the SpilleFri treatment.

Overview of the SpilleFri treatment.

The content of SF was developed de novo based on a CBT program for severe GD, used for many years and further developed at the RCGD (Ladouceur & Lachance, 2007). The program is therapist-assisted to motivate participants, answer questions, and support engagement. Contact is primarily written via a secure embedded message system. Participants can write anytime; therapists respond on designated days.

The treatment is structured into five blocks: four standard and one optional. Blocks 1–4 are delivered via online modules and written therapist support, supplemented by a synchronous support session with a therapist (face-to-face or video-based) in Block 1 (S1, see Fig. 2). Block 5 includes up to two optional synchronous sessions (S2, S3).

Block 1 (Before you start, M1, M2, and S1) focuses on initiating treatment, setting goals, and addressing motivation and ambivalence. Participants receive psychoeducation GD and CBT principles, define gambling-specific and broader life goals, and implement gambling control measures. Block 2 (M3–M6) targets urges and thoughts, helping participants identify high-risk situations, develop coping strategies, challenge maladaptive thoughts, and strengthen emotional regulation. Block 3 (M7) introduces graded exposure, where participants gradually face gambling triggers. Control measures are removed as participants progress through an exposure hierarchy. Block 4 (M8) focuses on relapse prevention and treatment closure, including relapse risk education, prevention planning, and review of progress.

Block 5 (S2, S3, M9) addresses underlying issues or involves relatives. These sessions are assigned based on participant requests or therapist recommendations. Additional sessions may be offered if deemed necessary by the team of psychologists during the weekly clinic conference.

Treatment is considered complete if participants actively engage (logs in and scrolls through all pages) with the first six modules of the program (excluding the introduction). Because the final modules focus mainly on relapse prevention and closure, completing the first six ensures exposure to the core therapeutic content (psychoeducation, motivation, cognitive restructuring, and response prevention).

The program is hosted on 'internetbehandling.dk', developed at Aarhus University Hospital and used in other completed or ongoing RCTs (e.g., Christensen et al., 2023, Hoffmann et al., 2019, Pedersen et al., 2023). The platform uses Drupal as a CMS system for user creation, MitID log-in, and database management.

All RCGD psychologists are experienced with SF and receive ongoing internal supervision and external supervision monthly during data collection. The same therapists deliver both SF and sCBT. Before recruitment, all therapists complete a brief questionnaire on treatment modality preferences.

Synchronous CBT

In the sCBT arm, participants receive 9–12 individual sessions over 14–20 weeks, in a 1-to-1 format with a therapist, delivered either face-to-face or via video (see Fig. 3). Sessions are weekly at first, then at least biweekly from week 4 onward. The treatment follows the same five-block structure as SF: four standard blocks and one optional. Content is identical but delivered synchronously through therapist-led sessions, with homework provided via handouts.Fig. 3Overview of the sCBT treatment.

Overview of the sCBT treatment.

As such, Block 1 (sessions 1–2) focuses on treatment initiation, goal setting, and motivation. Block 2 (sessions 3–6) addresses gambling urges and thoughts, including identifying high-risk situations, developing coping strategies, and challenging maladaptive thinking. Block 3 (sessions 7–8) introduces graded exposure to gambling triggers. Block 4 (session 9) focuses on relapse prevention and treatment closure. The three optional sessions in Block 5 can address underlying issues or involve relatives and are scheduled at the therapist’s discretion after Block 1.

The treatment manual used in this trial is an updated version of the aforementioned CBT program, which has long been central to the RCGD’s work and also served as the foundation for developing the SF treatment. In preparation for the RCT, psychologists at the clinic collaboratively identified the core therapeutic components and outlined a standard treatment progression. All psychologists at the RCGD are experienced with the sCBT treatment and have been involved in developing the treatment manual. Manual fidelity and deviations are self-rated by therapists after each treatment session. 10 % of therapy sessions (randomly chosen) will be video recorded to allow for an independent rater to assess adherence to the treatment manual.

Registration and outcome measures.

Primary outcomes

The outcome for non-inferiority analysis is the severity of gambling problems, measured using the National Opinion Research Center DSM Screen for Gambling Problems (NODS) (Wickwire et al., 2008). The NODS is a 17-item self-report questionnaire scored “Yes” (1) or “No” (0), based on DSM-IV (Hodgins, 2004), and validated for DSM-5 and ICD-10 GD (Brazeau & Hodgins, 2022). The maximum score is 10; scores above 5 indicate probable GD. To detect changes in symptom severity post-treatment, the NODS was adapted to assess the past month instead of the past year. Shortening the NODS reference period to capture treatment-related changes follows established practice. For example, Wulfert et al. (2005) found that a 3-month adaptation of the NODS retained good psychometric properties, supporting the use of shorter timeframes for post-treatment assessment. The modified timeframe is used at all measurement points (T1, T6, T7, T8). At T1, a version with a 1-year timeframe is also administered to compare gambling severity with normative data.

Secondary measures

Socio-demographic and anamnestic data are collected at T1. Following diagnostic assessment, therapists document endorsement of GD diagnostic criteria, inclusion/exclusion criteria, readiness to change, treatment barriers, and presence of comorbid mental health disorders.

Secondary measures include gambling symptom severity (Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale; G-SAS) (Kim et al., 2009), estimated financial losses (self-reported net loss past week) (Walker et al., 2006), overall health improvement (Clinical Global Impression – Improvement; CGI-I) (Guy, 1976), symptoms of anxiety, depression, and emotional distress (three subscales from the Symptom Checklist; SCL-90) (Derogatis & Cleary, 1977), quality of life and health satisfaction (two WHO Quality of Life Assessment; WHOQoL, subscales) (Skevington, Lotfy & O’Connell, 2004), and quality of romantic relationships (Dyadic Adjustment Scale; DAS-4) (Sabourin, Valois & Lussier, 2005).

Process measures

To provide insight into change processes during treatment, variables targeted in the treatment are assessed. These include gambling preoccupation, urges and behaviour (G-SAS) (Kim et al., 2009), maladaptive gambling cognition (Gambling-Related Cognitions Scale; GRCS) (Raylu & Oei, 2004), and difficulties in emotion regulation (Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale; DERS-16) (Hallion, Steinman, Tolin & Diefenbach, 2018). To reduce questionnaire burden, a shortened GRCS version is used, selecting the two items with the highest factor loadings from each subscale, totalling 8 items. The subscale 'Inability to stop gambling' is removed, replaced by a confidence slider item measuring the participant’s belief in managing an unexpected urge to gamble ('on a scale of 1 to 10, how confident are you that you can handle an unexpected urge to gamble?') (Rodda et al., 2015).

Therapists register the length and themes of video sessions in SpilleFri and the length and overall themes of sessions in the sCBT arm. In both arms, therapists register the involvement of concerned significant others in treatment sessions.

SF use

Data on participant activity will be logged and retrieved from the SF web platform. Logged variables include: total time spent in the program and each module, time per page, login frequency, completed modules frequency, interactive activities engaged with in total and per module (including text-boxes written in, special-features interacted with), total word count in text boxes, and the textual content of all text boxes.

Other measures

To investigate moderating effects, the following covariates are collected: preferred treatment modality (SF, sCBT or indifferent), readiness to change (three items measuring importance, readiness, and self-efficacy), gambling motives (Gambling Motives Questionnaire-Financial; GMQ-F) (Schellenberg, McGrath & Dechant, 2015), treatment motives (two items on internal motivation/external control) (Rodda et al., 2015), and gambling pathway subtype (Gambling Pathways Questionnaire; GPQ) (Nower, Blaszczynski & Anthony, 2022). The GMQ-F is condensed by selecting the two items with the highest factor loadings from each subscale, totalling 8 items.

At end-of-treatment (T6), participants' expectations and experiences of the treatment and the working alliance with the therapist are measured (using the following scales: Experienced Treatment Credibility and Expectancy; CEQ (Devilly & Borkovec, 2000), Patient Treatment Satisfaction; ESQ (Brown et al., 2014), Evaluation of the internet program; IEUQ (Thorndike et al., 2008, Ritterband et al., 2008), Working Alliance Inventory; WAI-SR (Munder et al., 2010). Therapists also evaluate participant engagement and treatment effect (CGI-I) (Guy, 1976).

Analysis

The hypothesized non-inferiority of SF compared to sCBT, as measured by NODS, will be analysed using a restricted 2-level mixed linear model, with average condition-specific starting point restricted to be equal across conditions (due to randomization), and with random intercept – i.e., repeated measures nested within patients. REML estimation will be employed. If the assumptions underlying the mixed linear model are violated, generalized estimating equations (GEEs) will be used as an alternative. Model-predicted mean change in NODS from baseline to FU is estimated in both CBT and SF condition, and a 95 % confidence interval for the difference in change, i.e., ΔCBT-ΔSF, is calculated. The null-hypothesis of inferiority is rejected if the lower bound of this confidence interval is greater than the inferiority bound defined to be −2 NODS-scale points, i.e., SF leads to a change in NODS not more than 2 points less than CBT. The inferiority bound was based on RCGD clinical experience and the way NODS maps onto DSM-5 criteria for GD. Each NODS point corresponds to one DSM-5 criterion, with severity defined in two-point intervals (mild: 4–5, moderate: 6–7, severe: 8–9). Thus, a difference of fewer than two points rarely changes severity classification and is unlikely to be clinically noticeable, whereas a two-point difference shifts patients across categories, representing a meaningful change. The –2 margin therefore reflects the largest difference that can reasonably be considered clinically non-significant.

Secondary outcomes will be analysed for group differences at the primary endpoint. Potential moderators (e.g., baseline characteristics, motivation, GPQ subgroup, treatment preference) and process variables (e.g., cognitive distortions, emotion regulation difficulties, gambling craving) will be explored. Graphical trajectories will be compared across conditions to provide preliminary insights into mechanisms of change.

Results will be analysed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement (Piaggio et al., 2012). A statistician is continuously involved.

Power analysis for the primary outcome

Sample size estimation was done using simulation of mixed linear models in Stata version 17. The relative effects of SF and sCBT will be analysed using an a priori determined non-inferiority margin, designating the margin within which SF can be less effective than sCBT without being declared clinically inferior to the standard treatment (Angeli et al., 2020) (see section 5.6).

Simulation parameters were informed by 1) previous clinical data from the RCGD on reduction in NODS for patients receiving sCBT and 2) reduction in NODS for patients in the SF feasibility study (Stenbro et al., 2023). Assuming baseline-EOT-FU trajectory in NODS-score of 7-1-1 in sCBT condition and 7-2-2 in SF condition, power of 0.80 was reached for 100 patients (50 in each condition). To account for dropout and attrition, a sample size of 150 is ideal. Thus, we aim to include 75 participants in each arm.

Discussion

The significant consequences of untreated GD and low treatment-seeking rates highlight the need for accessible interventions. SpilleFri, an asynchronous internet-based CBT program (iCBT), addresses these challenges by offering a flexible, self-paced alternative to traditional approaches. Unlike synchronous CBT (sCBT), which relies on real-time therapist-patient interaction, iCBT reduces logistical constraints while maintaining essential therapeutic components. Evidence from previous studies in gambling disorder and related behavioral addictions suggests that iCBT can achieve clinically meaningful outcomes while reducing common barriers to treatment, such as travel requirements, scheduling constraints, and stigma (Boumparis et al., 2022, Watkins and Sprang, 2018). Understanding whether iCBT is as effective as sCBT is therefore crucial: if non-inferiority is demonstrated, iCBT could enable broader implementation in routine clinical practice, increasing treatment uptake and supporting more flexible, patient-centered care without compromising effectiveness.

A key strength of this trial is its comprehensive baseline assessment, incorporating tools like the Gambling Pathways Questionnaire (GPQ) and process measures to evaluate variables and treatment processes that may influence outcomes. This approach allows for the exploration of treatment effects across clinical subgroups, potentially identifying which treatment modality works better for specific subgroups. The inclusion of a 12-month follow-up will provide valuable insights into the long-term durability of treatment effects, a critical consideration given the risk of gambling symptom relapse (Smith et al., 2015). Ultimately, these findings could inform the development of tailored, stepped-care approaches in GD treatment, ensuring resources are allocated effectively.

Certain limitations should be noted. One key challenge is the difference in treatment durations: sCBT spans 14–20 weeks, whereas SF lasts 10–12 weeks. This discrepancy means that the primary outcome assessment point, three months after treatment, represents different time periods from baseline. sCBT participants have a longer interval for potential natural recovery or deterioration compared to SF participants, which could influence results. To mitigate this, an additional assessment point has been included for the SF group, timed to align with the 30-week follow-up in the sCBT group, allowing for statistical control of time effects. While this approach reduces bias from differing treatment durations, timing-related differences in assessments may remain. If time strongly affects outcomes, the primary endpoint could reflect both treatment effects and natural recovery or deterioration. To account for this, data will be modeled week by week, enabling predictions of outcomes at specific time points relative to baseline and allowing finer group comparisons than observed assessments alone. However, precision is limited by the number and spacing of measurements, meaning estimated trajectories are approximate. Thus, timing may still introduce uncertainty or residual bias, which will be considered in interpreting results.

Participant preferences for one treatment modality over the other could affect engagement and outcomes. To account for this, we measure both participant and therapist preferences and will include these data in our analyses. As the same group of therapists deliver both modalities, variability in treatment quality related to therapist attitudes remains a potential source of bias.

Finally, as a single-center trial recruiting nationally, the sample may not represent all GD treatment seekers, although recruitment takes place from regular referrals to the clinic. Some participants may have specifically chosen the clinic due to the availability of internet-based treatment. Moreover, findings may not generalize to other countries or healthcare systems, limiting external validity.

Conclusion

Findings from this trial have the potential to determine whether asynchronous internet-based CBT can offer an accessible and flexible alternative to traditional synchronous CBT without compromising effectiveness. The results may guide future implementation of scalable, patient-centered approaches and inform stepped-care models for gambling disorder treatment.
