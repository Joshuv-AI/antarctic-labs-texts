Congenital encephalomeningocele presenting as a giant orbital mass in a neonate: a case report

Introduction

Congenital orbital masses in neonates are rare and encompass a broad spectrum of etiologies, including teratomas, vascular malformations, and frontoethmoidal encephalomeningoceles [1, 2]. Among these, frontoethmoidal encephalomeningocele, which is defined as herniation of meninges and brain parenchyma through a congenital skull base defect is a rare entity, with an estimated incidence of 1:35,000 live births in Western populations, though significantly higher rates are reported in Southeast Asia [3, 4]. When encephaloceles involve the anterior skull base, they typically present as midline or paramedian masses in the frontoethmoidal region and are classified by Suwanwela and Suwanwela into nasofrontal, nasoethmoidal, and nasoorbital subtypes based on the location of the external bony defect [5].

Herein, we report a rare case of encephalomeningocele presenting as a giant orbital mass with atypical radiological features. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by imaging limitations and emphasizes the critical roles of histopathology and multidisciplinary teamwork in reaching the correct diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 2-hour-old female neonate was transferred to our institution due to a left orbital mass noted immediately after birth (Fig. 1). She was born at term via uncomplicated vaginal delivery. The pregnancy was unremarkable, and her 29-year-old mother had no history of pharmacologic therapy or known exposure to chemicals or radiation. Physical examination revealed a giant mass in the left orbit causing marked proptosis and preventing eyelid closure. The mass surface appeared gelatinous. The left cornea was clear with a preserved pupillary reflex, and fundus examination showed no retinal abnormalities. The right eye was normal. No abnormalities of the nasal cavity or palate were noted, and systemic examination was otherwise unremarkable.

Clinical photographs at birth demonstrating a giant left orbital mass with marked proptosis

Orbital ultrasonography revealed a well-defined, predominantly cystic mass measuring 4.7 cm × 3.7 cm × 3.2 cm, containing coarse internal septations and solid components with soft tissue echogenicity and scattered calcifications. The mass encased the left optic nerve, which appeared stretched and thinned, measuring 0.15 cm in diameter. Concomitant compression of the left globe was noted, with axial lengths measuring 1.4 cm (left) and 1.7 cm (right). The anterior chambers, lenses, and vitreous bodies were clear bilaterally, and the retinae demonstrated no abnormal echogenicity. No bony destruction was observed.

Computed tomography (CT) of the brain and orbits revealed significant proptosis of the left eyeball and a large mixed-density mass in the axial and coronal planes (Fig. 2A, B). The lesion exhibited predominantly cystic components with internal spotty calcifications and septations. Adjacent bony structures showed displacement and resorptive changes without an obvious bone defect.

Radiography appearance of the left orbital mass. (A, B) Axial and coronal CT images showing a giant mixed-density mass in the left orbit with internal calcifications, with no bony defect identified. (C, D) Axial T1-weighted and T2-weighted images demonstrating significant left-sided proptosis with posterior globe compression, along with a large heterogeneous retrobulbar mass containing internal septations. (E) Sagittal T2-weighted image demonstrating a heterogeneous mass. (F) Sagittal post-contrast MRI demonstrating peripheral rim and internal septal enhancement, with no evidence of intracranial extension

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed significant left-sided proptosis with posterior globe compression, along with a large heterogeneous retrobulbar mass. The lesion demonstrated heterogeneous hypointense signal on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images, containing cord-like internal septations. (Fig. 2C, D, E). Post-contrast images showed intense rim and internal septal enhancement (Fig. 2F). The left optic nerve appeared to be displaced superomedially, and the extraocular muscles were poorly defined. No intracranial extension was identified.

Multidisciplinary discussion involving ophthalmology, neurosurgery, oncology, radiology and neonatology favored teratoma as the leading diagnosis, given the imaging findings of mixed cystic-solid components with calcifications and septations. Congenital microphthalmos with orbital cyst and vascular malformations were also considered in the differential diagnosis. A transcranial surgical approach was chosen, given the tumor’s massive size, deep retrobulbar location, and the possibility of intracranial extension that could not be confidently excluded by imaging, as well as the advantage of better vascular control and direct visualization of the optic nerve during dissection. The female neonate underwent surgery on the fourth day after birth. Intraoperative findings revealed a small bony defect (approximately 0.7 cm × 0.5 cm) in the orbital roof with surrounding thin bone, through which the mass herniated into the anterior cranial fossa and communicated with intracranial meninges. The lesion was excised piecemeal and the globe was successfully repositioned. The orbital roof defect was repaired with artificial dura, and a temporary tarsorrhaphy was placed to protect the cornea.

Histopathological examination revealed the lesion was composed of neuroglia, neuroepithelium, choroid plexus, and meningeal tissue, and immunohistochemical study identified the immunoreactivity for S-100 and glial fibrillary acidic protein (Fig. 3), consistent with encephalomeningocele.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical images of the left orbital mass. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the mass showing neuroglia (white arrow), choroid plexus (black arrow), and meningeal tissue (asterisk). (B) Immunoreactivity of the mass for glial fibrillary acidic protein

The baby recovered well postoperatively, with preserved globe and intact optic nerve in the left eye. However, at the 2-month follow-up, mild strabismus, proptosis, and exposure keratopathy secondary to incomplete eyelid closure (with a 2-mm palpebral fissure gap) were noted (Fig. 4A). Follow-up ultrasonography revealed a residual cystic mass measuring 2.1 × 1.6 × 1.1 cm, with axial lengths of 1.6 cm for the left globe and 1.7 cm for the right globe. MRI at the 6-month follow-up demonstrated an irregularly shaped residual lesion in the left orbit, measuring approximately 1.8 × 1.3 × 1.2 cm, with heterogeneous signal intensity, as well as decreased compression of the left globe, with eye size approximating that of the right eye (Fig. 4B and C). Subsequent imaging at the 9-month follow-up showed a stable residual mass with no significant interval change.

(B, C) Axial and sagittal MRI at 6-month follow-up showing an irregularly shaped residual lesion with heterogeneous signal intensity in the left orbit, with decreased compression of the left globe and overall globe size approximating that of the right eye

Discussion

Congenital orbital encephalomeningoceles are extremely rare and defined by herniation of meninges and brain parenchyma through a congenital skull base defect. Our case exhibited several features consistent with this diagnosis: intraoperative identification of an orbital roof defect, communication with intracranial meninges, and histopathological confirmation of neuroglial tissue, choroid plexus, and meninges. However, there are some atypical features in this case. The orbital cyst was extremely large, whereas the associated orbital roof defect was very small, and the patient did not exhibit significant downward displacement of the eyeball despite the cyst protruding through the orbital roof defect. We hypothesize that a hybrid mechanism underlies this unusual encephalomeningocele, bridging classic encephaloceles and glial heterotopia. In our case, a small amount of meningeal and glial tissue likely herniated through a tiny orbital roof defect early in embryogenesis. The connecting stalk probably underwent spontaneous atrophy or fibrous obliteration—a phenomenon well recognized in nasal glial heterotopia [6, 7]—leaving the tissue isolated from the intracranial space. Once separated, the neuroglial and meningeal elements continued to proliferate and secrete fluid, leading to progressive cystic expansion. This accounts for the striking discrepancy between the giant cyst and the small bony defect. Notably, about 15–20% of nasal glial heterotopia cases retain a fibrous stalk without an open intracranial connection [6, 7], supporting this “detachment–proliferation” sequence. Given the massive size of the lesion and its extensive involvement of the retrobulbar and infraorbital spaces, the patient presented with pronounced proptosis without appreciable ocular displacement.

Classical imaging of encephalomeningocele shows a cystic mass communicating with the intracranial space through a bony defect, with cerebrospinal fluid signal [8, 9]. CT delineates bony anatomy and MRI delineates soft tissue relationships and intracranial communication [10, 11]. However, as in the few previously reported cases [12, 13], preoperative imaging in our case failed to identify the defect or the encephalomeningocele. Several factors explain this failure: the defect was small and located in the orbital roof, a region of thin and incompletely ossified bone in neonates [14]. CT may miss small defects due to partial volume averaging or obscuration by overlying soft tissue, and MRI may not reveal the connection when the mass is large, the defect is small, and the encephalocele contains little cerebrospinal fluid. This case underscores that imaging has limitations, and that the absence of a radiologically identifiable bony defect does not exclude encephalomeningocele.

Several differential diagnoses were considered. Teratoma was the leading preoperative suspicion given the mixed cystic-solid appearance with calcifications and septations on imaging. However, teratomas typically contain fatty tissue, manifesting as increased echogenicity on ultrasound, fat density on CT, and high T1 signal with fat suppression on MRI [15, 16], which was absent on all imaging modalities in our case, and histopathology ultimately revealed exclusively neural tissue rather than elements of all three germ layers, definitively excluding this diagnosis. Congenital microphthalmos with orbital cyst was another consideration, as the left globe appeared smaller on initial imaging. This entity results from failed embryonic fissure closure, with undifferentiated neuroectodermal tissue herniating into the orbit, usually inferiorly. Histopathology typically shows cyst walls composed of outer collagenous connective tissue and inner neuroectodermal layers with variably differentiated retinal elements [17]. Its exclusion was based on intraoperative confirmation of an orbital roof defect with intracranial communication and histopathological demonstration of choroid plexus and meningeal tissue—findings pathognomonic of an intracranial origin. And the postoperative increase in globe size and normal fundoscopy further supported this exclusion. Vascular lesions were also considered, as the imaging features overlap with those of congenital hemangiomas and venolymphatic malformations [18]. However, Doppler ultrasound, which typically demonstrates characteristic flow patterns in vascular malformations [19], was absent in our case. And postoperative histopathology further excluded a vascular etiology.

The diagnosis was ultimately established by the combination of intraoperative findings, which identified an orbital roof defect with intracranial communication, and histopathology, which revealed neuroglia, neuroepithelium, choroid plexus, and meningeal tissue with immunoreactivity for S-100 and GFAP. Multidisciplinary collaboration was critical in guiding both the diagnostic workup and surgical planning. Although the lesion was initially suspected to be a teratoma, vascular malformation, or congenital microphthalmos with orbital cyst, the collaborative discussion ultimately directed the surgical strategy toward a transcranial approach with preparation for possible intracranial exploration. This approach enabled adequate exposure, piecemeal excision of the mass, and successful globe repositioning, with repair of the orbital roof defect. Postoperatively, the patient demonstrated preserved globe and optic nerve integrity. At the 2-month follow-up, however, mild strabismus, proptosis and exposure keratopathy secondary to incomplete eyelid closure were observed, indicating the need for ongoing corneal protection and potential further intervention.

This is one of the few reported cases of congenital orbital encephalomeningocele in a neonate with such a giant lesion, and it highlights several clinically relevant points. It demonstrates that a small orbital roof defect can give rise to a giant orbital mass, a phenomenon seldom documented in the literature. It also illustrates that the absence of a radiologically identifiable bone defect or intracranial communication on CT and MRI does not exclude the diagnosis of encephalomeningocele, particularly in neonates. In addition, it underscores the pivotal role of histopathological examination and the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration in establishing the correct diagnosis.

Several limitations of this report should be acknowledged. As a single case report, the findings and proposed mechanism cannot be generalized, and the embryological hypothesis, though plausible, remains speculative. High-resolution CT with thin-section reconstruction, which might have improved detection of the minute bone defect, was not performed preoperatively due to concerns about radiation exposure in the neonate. Finally, although short-term follow-up (up to 9 months) showed a stable residual mass and improvement in globe size, long-term data on globe development, visual acuity, and recurrence risk are not yet available.

Conclusions

Congenital orbital encephalomeningocele can present with atypical features, including a giant lesion with a disproportionately small bone defect that was radiologically occult on preoperative imaging. The absence of a radiologically detectable bone defect does not exclude this diagnosis, particularly in neonates, in whom histopathological confirmation remains the diagnostic cornerstone. Encephalomeningocele should be considered in the differential diagnosis of congenital orbital masses, even when classic imaging findings are lacking.
