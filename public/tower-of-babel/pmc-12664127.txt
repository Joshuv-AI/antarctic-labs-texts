Immune cell infiltration and inflammatory response in nucleus pulposus following annular disruption in degenerative disc disease
Authors: Jinmin Lv, Jinbing He, Yichao Ni, Linwei He, Zhuoming Li, Bo Tian, Hao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a common cause of lower back pain. However, the role of annulus fibrosus rupture in altering the immune microenvironment of nucleus pulposus remains unclear. This study elucidated the impact of annulus fibrosus rupture on the immune response within the nucleus pulposus in IDD.MethodsThe study included patients diagnosed with intervertebral disc herniation who underwent surgical treatment at the Orthopedics Department of the Second Hospital of Soochow University from September 2020 to July 2021. Based on the integrity of the annulus fibrosus, patients were categorized into two groups: Annulus Intact (AI) group and Annulus Rupture (AR) group. Nucleus pulposus samples were collected during surgery for analysis. Flow cytometry was used to assess immune cell populations in these samples. Levels of inflammatory factors were measured using RT-PCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsSamples from patients with annulus rupture showed significantly higher levels of macrophages and T cells compared to those with intact annulus. Gene expression of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, and IL-6, were markedly elevated in the AR group than those in AI group (P < 0.05). The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β detected by ELISA were also significantly increased in AR group (P < 0.05).ConclusionAnnulus fibrosus rupture significantly alters the immune microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus, characterized by increased inflammatory cell infiltration and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This enhanced local inflammatory response may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of IDD, offering potential targets for immune-modulatory therapies to delay IDD progression.Keywords: Intervertebral disc degeneration, Annulus rupture, Immune microenvironment, Inflammatory response

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a leading cause of neck, shoulder, and low back pain affecting hundreds of millions globally and imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens [1]. The intervertebral disc, comprising the nucleus pulposus (NP), annulus fibrosus, and cartilaginous endplates, functions as a biomechanical shock absorber. However, its avascular nature limits nutrient supply, rendering the NP particularly vulnerable to degenerative changes [2]. IDD is characterized by progressive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, driven by an imbalance between matrix synthesis and catabolic enzyme activity [3]. Key pathological features include loss of proteoglycans and type II collagen, increased apoptosis of NP cells, and chronic inflammation mediated by cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [4, 5]. The overexpression of inflammatory factors activates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), leading to ECM breakdown and further propagation of inflammation [6].

Notably, as the annulus fibrosus ruptures, a hallmark of advanced IDD, the nucleus pulposus is exposed to the systemic circulation, potentially triggering immune cell infiltration and local immune responses [7]. The nucleus pulposus is an immune-privileged tissue, and its exposure to the immune system may induce autoimmune reactions, further exacerbating the pathological progression of IDD [8]. Targeted modulation of this rupture-induced inflammatory microenvironment represents a promising yet challenging therapeutic strategy, particularly given the avascular nature of the disc. Recent advances in green nanotechnology, such as the development of biocompatible, targeted drug-delivery systems [9, 10], offer novel potential to overcome this delivery barrier for anti-inflammatory agents.​.

However, there is a lack of in-depth research on the changes in the immune microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus before and after annular rupture, and the role of inflammation in IDD remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of annular rupture on the local immune microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus, with a focus on the infiltration of inflammatory cells and the expression of inflammatory mediators. This research intends to provide new theoretical insights for clinical treatments, particularly in immune modulation interventions for IDD, with the goal of delaying disease progression and improving patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

This study included patients diagnosed with lumbar intervertebral disc herniation who underwent disc excision surgery at the Department of Orthopedics, The Second Hospital of Soochow University, between September 2020 and July 2021. All patient data were approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Soochow University, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients and their families prior to surgery. The ethical approval number was JD-LK-2020-096-01. The informed consent form provided detailed information regarding the study’s purpose, methods, potential risks, and the protection of patient rights, ensuring that all participants fully understood and voluntarily consented to participate. All participants retained the right to withdraw from the study and study complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

Imaging and intraoperative confirmation of annular rupture

Preoperatively, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, 3.0 Tesla) was used to identify imaging markers suggestive of annular disruption, such as high-intensity zones (HIZ) on T2-weighted sequences and discontinuity of annular fibers. During surgery, the operating surgeon visually confirmed annular rupture by observing nucleus pulposus extrusion or fissures in the annulus. While microscopic tears or partial ruptures may escape detection by this method, we minimized misclassification by excluding ambiguous cases of which patients with conflicting imaging and intraoperative findings (e.g., intact annulus on MRI but suspected microtears during surgery) were excluded from the AR group and consensus evaluation that all MRI scans and intraoperative records were reviewed by both the operating surgeon and an independent radiologist to ensure concordance.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Clinical diagnosis of lumbar intervertebral disc herniation confirmed by MRI and computed tomography (CT) with persistent low back pain (LBP) and/or radicular pain (e.g., sciatica) correlating to the affected segment. (2) Failure of non-surgical treatment for at least six months. (3) Single-segment involvement confirmed by symptom-imaging concordance. (4) Consent: Participants must comprehend the study’s objectives and voluntarily sign the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Imaging evidence of disc calcification. (2) Concurrent lumbar infectious diseases. (3) Disc degeneration at asymptomatic adjacent levels (incidental findings unrelated to clinical symptoms).

Based on the integrity of the annulus fibrosus during surgery, the excised nucleus pulposus tissue samples were classified into two groups: AI (Annulus Intact) and AR (Annulus Rupture). The excised nucleus pulposus tissue was thoroughly cleaned and stored in specimen preservation tubes according to standard protocols. All patient information and research data were kept confidential and managed in compliance with relevant privacy protection laws and regulations.

Flow cytometry analysis

The nucleus pulposus tissue was minced and processed using a tissue homogenizer, then filtered and centrifuged to obtain a single-cell suspension. For the identification of M1 macrophages, the single-cell suspension was transferred to the tubes, ensuring a cell concentration greater than 1 × 10⁶ cells per tube. The cells were centrifuged at 1000 r/min (r = 7.2 cm) for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of diluted FITC-CD11b, APC-CD80 and PE-CD206 antibodies and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 h. The reaction was terminated by adding 2 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuging at 1000 r/min for 5 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS. In parallel, to identify T cells, 50 µL of diluted FITC-CD45 and PE-CD3 antibodies were added to single-cell suspensions, each containing 1 × 10⁶ cells. These samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 h. After the incubation period, the cells were analyzed using a BD C6 flow cytometer and FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence

Fresh nucleus pulposus tissues were embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 s, and subsequently stored at −80 °C. Cryosections (10 μm thickness) were prepared using a cryostat (Leica CM1950) for immunofluorescence analysis. The sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBST (Phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20), each lasting 5 min. To block non-specific binding, the sections were incubated with 5% BSA for 10 min. Antibody dilution buffer was prepared containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and 5% BSA. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in the dark with primary antibodies: APC-conjugated anti-CD80 (1:200) and PE-conjugated anti-CD206 (1:200). On the following day, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min. After mounting, the slides were observed under a confocal microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The nucleus pulposus tissue was pulverized in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA following the instructions provided in the Plus All-in-one 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (gDNA Purge) kit. Using cDNA as a template, PCR amplification was performed with the NovoStar SYBR qPCR SuperMix plus kit. The expression levels of target genes were quantified by the 2–ΔΔCt relative quantification method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in nucleus pulposus tissue were measured using ELISA. Initially, the nucleus pulposus tissue was weighed, minced, and homogenized. Protein lysis buffer was then added for digestion. The ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China), and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, German).

Immunohistochemistry

After paraffin embedding of the nucleus pulposus specimens, deparaffinization was performed at room temperature, followed by hydration through a graded alcohol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inactivated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and antigen retrieval was carried out at high temperature using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The primary antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by the secondary antibody the next day. DAB chromogenic staining was performed, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The specimens were then dehydrated and mounted. Observation and imaging were carried out under a high-power inverted phase-contrast microscope, with random field selection for documentation.

Statistical analysis

All results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was analyzed by two-tailed Student’s test for comparing differences between two groups with a normal distribution. All the statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Participants

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 28 cases were collected in the AI group, comprising 18 males and 10 females, with an average age of 51.78 ± 10.52 years (range 36 to 68 years). In the AR group, there were 32 cases, including 20 males and 12 females, with an average age of 52.17 ± 10.78 years (range 34 to 70 years). A comparison of age and gender distribution between the two groups showed no statistically significant differences (Age: t = 0.14, P > 0.05; Gender: χ²=0.02, P > 0.05), indicating that the baseline characteristics were balanced and comparable between the groups.

Local inflammatory microenvironment of nucleus pulposus post-annulus fibrosus rupture

Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of M1 macrophages [(44.70 ± 0.49) % vs. (15.00 ± 0.55) %, t = 69.84, P < 0.01] and T cells [(20.40 ± 0.32) % vs. (4.78 ± 0.12) %, t = 79.16, P < 0.01] in the AR group compared to the AI group (Fig. 1A and B). In the immunofluorescence staining of nucleus pulposus tissue, a significant increase in CD80+ (M1) cells was observed in the AR group compared to the control group [(23.93 ± 10.66) % vs. (17.48 ± 2.82) %, t = 4.01, P < 0.01] (Fig. 2A and B). Additionally, the AR group showed an increase in CD68, F4/80 and IL-1β positive area in the nucleus pulposus specimens compared to the AI group (Fig. 3A).

Discussion

The nucleus pulposus in a healthy intervertebral disc is encased by the annulus fibrosus, forming the largest avascular and enclosed structure, isolated from the immune system [11]. This study found that rupture of the annulus fibrosus leads to the formation of a localized inflammatory microenvironment in the nucleus pulposus, characterized by increased immune cell infiltration and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines. This finding is consistent with previous research. Wang et al., through bioinformatics analysis, identified key genes associated with immune cell infiltration in the nucleus pulposus of patients with disc herniation, showing a significant infiltration of regulatory T cells and macrophages in herniated disc tissues compared to controls [12]. Our study further confirmed a significant increase in M1 macrophages in the AR group through flow cytometry analysis.

Ling et al. demonstrated through single-cell sequencing analysis that macrophage polarization plays a crucial role in regulating the metabolism of nucleus pulposus precursor cells and that macrophages are essential in the progression of disc degeneration [13]. M1 macrophages and their secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines enhance the inflammatory response, causing further tissue damage. Activated immune cells release large amounts of inflammatory cytokines, which not only upregulate the expression and activity of MMPs but also stimulate cells to produce other inflammatory mediators, leading to a cascade amplification response in tissue and organ degradation.

Interleukins and tumor necrosis factors are critical inflammatory cytokines. Aberrant immune responses lead to the release of inflammatory cytokines in the nucleus pulposus tissue, causing back pain and discomfort [14]. Our findings align with the seminal work by Burke et al., who reported elevated IL-6 and IL-8 in discogenic low back pain cohorts [15]. However, our study extends these observations by demonstrating that annular rupture (AR) not only amplifies IL-6 production but also triggers significant expression of TNF-α and IL-1β, which are undetected in Burke’s analysis. This divergence may reflect technological advancements in cytokine detection. Critically, we identified M1 macrophage and T-cell infiltration as a hallmark of AR-induced inflammation. It demonstrates that annular rupture disrupts the immune-privileged status of the nucleus pulposus, enabling immune cell infiltration and amplifying cytokine release. The dual mechanism of structural failure and immune activation may synergistically accelerate ECM degradation and nociceptive signaling.

Breaking this vicious cycle and regulating the immune microenvironment within the degenerative nucleus pulposus is crucial. It is undeniable that a moderate inflammatory response serves as a fundamental physiological process in addressing disc herniation, essential for debris clearance, recruitment of reparative cells, and initiation of healing. Evidence indicates that intervertebral disc herniations can undergo spontaneous resorption through such self-regulated inflammatory processes [16, 17]. However, our findings demonstrate that annulus rupture generates a distinct inflammatory microenvironment characterized by M1 macrophage polarization, substantial T-cell infiltration, and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6). In Zhao et al.’s study, M1 macrophage-conditioned medium was used to treat NP cells, resulting in decreased NP cell viability, increased apoptosis, upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, apoptosis-related proteins, and catabolic enzymes, and decreased ECM components [18]. Targeting TNF-α and IL-1β signaling attenuates inflammatory cascades in IDD [19], yet systemic inhibitors (e.g., TNF-α receptor antagonists) exhibit limited efficacy in ameliorating clinical symptoms of IDD. Future research should also explore new anti-inflammatory treatment strategies, verifying their clinical efficacy and safety, with the goal of developing more effective treatments to improve patient quality of life.

While our study primarily focuses on the immune microenvironment in annulus rupture-associated disc degeneration, the clinical relevance of these findings to discogenic pain warrants further elaboration. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which were markedly elevated in the AR group (Fig. 4A-F), are known to sensitize sensory nerve endings and promote nociceptive signaling within degenerated discs. García-Cosamalón et al. demonstrated that sensory nerve ingrowth into degenerative discs which was driven by neurotrophins like NGF and BDNF is a hallmark of discogenic pain [20]. Our observation of M1 macrophage infiltration and IL-1β overexpression in ruptured discs aligns with this mechanism, as activated macrophages secrete cytokines that stimulate nerve sprouting and hyperalgesia. Furthermore, recent work by Matta et al. showed that suppressing inflammatory mediators via NTG-101 reduces pain-related neurotrophins in degenerative discs, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting cytokine-driven pathways [21]. Although our study did not directly quantify neurotrophic levels or neuronal markers, the pronounced inflammatory milieu in AR discs suggests a plausible synergy between immune activation and pain generation. Future studies should integrate neuronal profiling (e.g., substance P, TRPV1) to dissect how annular rupture-induced inflammation modulates disc innervation and pain perception.

Despite revealing significant changes in the immune microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus following annulus fibrosus rupture, our study has some limitations. Firstly, we mainly focused on specific inflammatory cytokines and immune cells, failing to cover all potential inflammatory mediators and cell types. Additionally, due to the study design, we cannot establish a definitive causal relationship, showing only correlations. Furthermore, this study focused on chronic disc degeneration with confirmed annular rupture (symptoms ≥ 6 months), which limits its generalizability to acute disc herniation or asymptomatic degeneration. To further validate and expand our findings, future studies should employ multi-omics approaches (such as spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics), combined with single-cell sequencing techniques, to comprehensively analyze the mechanisms and strategies in disc degeneration before and after annulus fibrosus rupture.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that annulus fibrosus rupture disrupts the immune-privileged status of the nucleus pulposus, driving a pro-inflammatory microenvironment characterized by ​dominant M1 macrophage polarization, T cell infiltration, and TNF-α/IL-1β overexpression. Building on these findings, we propose that ​targeted modulation of macrophage phenotypes, representing a potential strategy to interrupt TNF-α/IL-1β signaling cascades. Future work should: (1) validate this approach in humanized disc organoid models incorporating patient-derived NP cells and immune components; (2) integrate spatial transcriptomics to map crosstalk between infiltrating T cells and polarized macrophages in ruptured discs; (3) assess functional outcomes through concurrent monitoring of ECM preservation and nociceptive neuropeptide expression.
