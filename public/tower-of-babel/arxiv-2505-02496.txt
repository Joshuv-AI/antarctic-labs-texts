About diffusion equations in bounded systems
F. Sattin, D. F. Escande

Abstract
Differential equations need boundary conditions (BC’s) for their solution. It is commonly acknowledged that differential equations and BC’s are representative of independent physical processes, and no correlations between them is required. Two recent papers [D. Hilhorst, et al, Nonlinear Analysis, 245, 113561 (2024); J-W.Chung, et al, Jour. Math. Phys. 65, 071501 (2024)] focus on diffusion equations (DE’s) in a case with continuity of the physics at the boundary, where transport coefficients go smoothly to zero in a very small layer about it. They argue that, once the analytical expression of the DE is chosen, only one kind of BC’s may emerge (e.g., Neumann rather than Dirichlet). In this paper, we show that this case is very peculiar. Indeed, DE’s generally arise as long-wavelength limit out of a stochastic picture of microscopic dynamics, in the form of an integro-differential Master Equation (ME). Accordingly, they are justified only on a statistical basis, provide accurate pictures of the system’s evolution only over large enough length and time scales. In realistic cases, the width of the interface between the interior and exterior of the system is much smaller than transport scales, providing effectively a discontinuity and therefore a decorrelation between DE and BC.



I
Introduction

Diffusion equations (DE’s) are ubiquitous in physics, employed for modeling the behaviour of stochastic or chaotic systems
kotelenez08
. In this work we will refer specifically to the two expressions:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(D\,\partial_{x}u-V\,u)

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}(D\,u)-V^{\prime}\,u)

(2)

The two expressions above are obviously equivalent, related to each other through
V^{\prime}=V+\partial_{x}D
. Historically, they were reached through different paths, though. Expression (1), with
V=0
, was first written by Fourier in 1822 to model phenomenologically the propagation of heat, and then by Fick, in 1855, to include also the transport of matter. Expression (2), with arbitrary
V^{\prime}
, is the Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE), arising in the context of generic stochastic systems out of a microscopic picture of dynamics.
Generically, the quantity
u
is any scalar quantity that evolves stochastically,
D
quantifies the level of disorder, and
V
accounts for any spatial asymmetry.

Just like any differential equation, Eqns. (1,2) need to be supplemented by initial and boundary conditions (BC’s) in order to be solved. In physics and engineering, one attributes different roles and meanings to the transport coefficients
D,V
, from the one side, and to the BC’s to the other side: transport coefficients express the system’s internal dynamics, while BC’s represent the effect of external constraints. They are thus expressions of two independent physical mechanisms, hence no correlation is required to exist between the form of the DE and the kind of BC’s that are imposed upon it. Arbitrary combinations of BC’s and DE’s are routinely employed in practical applications.

This view has been questioned recently by the two works
bc1
;
bc2
. These papers examined a “smooth” version of bounded diffusive systems: The boundaries are not infinitely sharp; rather, a transition occurs between the inner region, characterized by finite transport, and the outer region, where transport occurs with arbitrarily small coefficients
D,V
.
The authors of
bc1
;
bc2
argue that,
once that the analytical expression for
D
and
V
throughout all space is given
, only one “natural” kind of BC emerges, consistent with the required boundedness of the motion. In particular, these are Neumann BC’s with null derivatives for the Fick’s equation (Eq. 1 with
V=0
), and Dirichlet BC’s with null density for the Fokker-Planck one without convection (Eq. 2 with
V^{\prime}=0
).

Hilhorst
et al
assume that the boundary is a region of continuous and smooth transition. In this note we will make two observations to this regard: the first, key observation is that DEs are fluid models of physical processes that, ultimately, possesses some sort of granularity, both spatial and temporal. Below some scale, DEs are no longer accurate pictures of the physical reality. This is the situation commonly encountered at the boundaries of most physical systems, where the width of the interface region is usually much shorter than the typical scales of transport.
We recall in the following that a basic microscopic picture of transport can be formulated in terms of a Master Equation (ME), an integro-differential equation where dynamics is expressed in terms of jump probabilities
p
and waiting times
\tau
for an ensemble of random walkers. DEs arise as long-wavelength limits of the ME, justified when only macroscopic scales with respect to transport are involved. This is a common occurrence in systems not too far from equilibrium, since the intrinsic dynamics itself of the system acts to wash out all but the largest-scale features. Accordingly, DE’s are good proxies for the true dynamics in the core of these systems, and for pdf’s broader than the typical jump. On the other hand, the physics near the edges can be dealt with correctly only within the ME formalism.
For practical purposes, one matches directly DEs with BC’s, bypassing the ME formulation, and introducing therefore a discontinuity region. Hence, Hilhorst
et al
’s argument does not apply, and no correlation is required to exist between the two terms.

The second observation, which can be regarded as a byproduct of the previous analysis, is that, even in those cases where the boundaries can be considered smooth according to the criteria of Hilhorst
et al
, the concept of a DE defined a priori
abstracting from the BC’s
must be discarded; rather, the BCs and the DE are both produced by the same ME, and therefore must be mutually consistent: the functional form of the latter adjusts itself in order to match the former.

II
Probabilistic modeling of DE’s

We write the local rate of change of the scalar quantity
u
,
\partial_{t}u(x,t)
, in terms of processes that move
u
across different points according to some probability
risken
:

\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\int{p(x,x^{\prime})\over\tau(x^{\prime})}u(x^{\prime},t)dx^{\prime}-{u(x,t)\over\tau(x)}

(3)

In this expression, a version of a Master Equation (ME),
p(x,x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}
is the probability for the scalar
u(x^{\prime},t)
to hop to
x
. Consistently with most of the literature, we will suppose that
p
depends from
x,x^{\prime}
through the combination:
p=p(\Delta=x-x^{\prime};x^{\prime})
, i.e, it depends on the starting point and from the relative distance to the arrival point. The integral in Eq. (
3
)– implicitly understood as spanning all space–becomes thus a convolution.

Within this simplified picture of the dynamics, the time needed for
u
to move from
x^{\prime}
to
x
is regarded as negligible but, between any two consecutive jumps, there is a waiting time
\tau(x^{\prime})
–which we suppose to depend upon the starting location
x^{\prime}
alone, still adhering to most literature. Additional effects may be added to Eq. (
3
). Among them: temporal memory effects, finite traveling times, sources/sinks, etc …, but here we will stick to this barebone picture for stochastic motion. Throughout this work, we will consider just one-dimensional dynamics, but it is clear that Eq. (
3
) may generalized to higher dimensions, and also be regarded as an effective one-dimensional equation, where the dynamics along the other directions has been averaged out. See
langevin
;
tabar
for further details. It should be mentioned, furthermore, that it is not truly mandatory that dynamics be stochastic. It may be replaced by deterministic but chaotic enough dynamics
prl07
;
ppcf08
.

The integro-differential equation (
3
) can be turned into a fully differential one under some conditions. Here, we will adhere to the popular Kramers-Moyal (K-M) method
risken
;
tabar
, expand Eq. (
3
) in series of the small parameter
\Delta
, and truncate the expansion after the first two terms. Note that neither the series expansion nor the truncation to a finite number of terms are trivially justifiable, and a lot of attention has been devoted to them. We will return in the next section upon this issue.

We arrive, eventually, to the FPE (2), where

D=\int d\Delta{\Delta^{2}\over 2}{p(\Delta,x)\over\tau(x)},\quad V^{\prime}=\int d\Delta\,\Delta\,{p(\Delta,x)\over\tau(x)}

(4)

Important particular cases correspond to
V^{\prime}=0
and
V^{\prime}=dD/dx
.
In terms of stochastic processes, the condition
V^{\prime}=0
is realized by jumps equally probable in both directions:
p(-\Delta,x)=p(\Delta,x)
. The condition
V^{\prime}=dD/dx
, which–as we have remarked earlier–leads to Fick’s form of diffusion, is associated with a more complicated relation:

{p(\Delta,x)\over\tau(x)}={p(-\Delta,x+\Delta)\over\tau(x+\Delta)}

(5)

This expression is odd-looking at first sight, but actually it ensures that the rate of jumps from
x
to
x^{\prime}
and
vice versa
are identical
pla08
. We recall that the constraint
V^{\prime}=dD/dx
holds for general 1 degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian dynamics, as originally derived by Landau, whereas it vanishes for higher dimensional dynamics
prl07
;
ppcf08
.

By tailoring appropriately the functional form of
p
and
\tau
, any sought form of the DE can be obtained.
In principle, this allows one to go beyond the results of Hilhorst
et al
even remaining fully within the fluid diffusive formulation.
For example, let us start with a system whose intrinsic dynamics is left-right symmetrical:
p(-\Delta,x)=p(\Delta,x)
:
V^{\prime}=0
, and the pure FPE (2) holds.
When one approaches the boundaries, i.e., when the distance from the boundary is of the order of the average jump length, different scenarios may be envisaged, depending upon the boundary conditions holding. Let us start by considering absorbing boundaries: The walker may cross the boundary but, once there, it does not return into the system any longer. This scenario can be modeled with
\tau(x)
going to infinity when
x
is outside the boundary, while being finite inside the boundaries. The left-right symmetry properties of
p
need not be affected, hence
V^{\prime}=0
still holds everywhere.
Let’s consider now the case of reflecting boundaries. Each walker hitting the wall must return back inside the system. This is accomplished provided that the rates of outbound and inbound hops equilbrate: it corresponds to the condition (
5
) which leads to the Fick’s form for the diffusion equation.

In conclusion, even within the framework of Hilhorst
et al
, i.e., allowing for a smooth transition of transport properties between core and boundaries, it is possible to match the DE with arbitrary BC’s. The critical difference lies in the fact that one cannot treat the DE as fixed
a priori
and independently of the BC’s. However, this result is just academical: it is based upon the hypothesis that the DE formalism is valid throughout the core-boundary interface region. In realistic cases, the interface width is much smaller than transport scales; in the case of solid walls, as an instance, the former is on the atomic scale. The K-M expansion and truncation, therefore, becomes doubtful in these instances. In the next section, we will argue that it is the whole DE formulation that cannot be retained over these small scales.

III
From the Master Equation to the Fokker-Planck Equation, and back

In the previous section, we have repeatedly adopted the procedure of guessing the functional form for
p,\tau
on the basis of details about the system physics, which are given as known in advance–for example, on the basis of some measurements: see the discussion around Eq. (
5
). This is quite a standard recipe: one has hardly ever direct access to the knowledge about the microphysics needed to write down
p,\tau
. It is frequent, instead, the case where only large-scale details are known, that can be modelled through effective diffusion and convection coefficients. A corollary is that the ME cannot be guessed uniquely, rather all ME’s that share the same first two moments are physically equivalent. The ME has a more fundamental aspect, but the FPE is more fit to cope with the empirical evidence.
The mutual relation between the ME and the FPE is actually a nontrivial topic, that has been–and to some degree still is–debated in the literature.

In the previous section, we have justified the passage from the ME to the FPE employing the popular Kramers-Moyal approach. It amounts to expanding the ME in powers of the jump length
\Delta
, and truncate it after the second-order term. The K-M expansion is implicitly based upon the ansatz that a separation exists between
\Delta
and the slower variation scale of the density
u
. The lack of a rigorous control parameter which may provide a justification for the neglect of the higher-order terms has always been a source of concern. The Pawula theorem
tabar
;
pawula
states that if the number of nonvanishing terms of the series is finite, it must be either one or two, in order to avoid unphysical features in the solution, such as negative density. This is, however, just a justification
a posteriori
of the K-M result.

In order to overcome this lack of rigour, van Kampen
vankampen1
;
vankampen2
suggested his system-size expansion in terms of a small parameter
\Omega^{-1}
, where
\Omega
, often (but not necessarily) identified with the size of the system, is a manifestly large parameter, exceeding all other scales of the system. van Kampen distinguishes a macroscopic deterministic contribution to the system’s trajectory, scaling like
\Omega
, and a fluctuating part, scaling like
\Omega^{1/2}
.
van Kampen’s approach is more rigorous than K-M’s, but still contains a dose of arbitrariness, since the relative weight between macroscopic deterministic and fluctuating terms is set
ad-hoc
. Basically, his ansatz is based upon the law of larger numbers. Attempts of improving upon van Kampen’s approach appear still nowadays (see, e.g.,
cianci17
;
peralta18
). However, a convincing analysis was carried out by Ryskin
ryskin97
,where he recalls how a Markovian process, over times longer than the decorrelation time, fulfills the conditions for the validity of the Central Limit Theorem, and entails therefore the emergence of Gaussian processes. Ryskin’s work explains why, even if the higher-order terms in the K-M expansion are not vanishing, they nonetheless may be disregarded: the reason is that they do not enter the long-time evolution equation
notariskin
.
The FPE is correct only over times longer than the decorrelation one. This entails correspondingly the accumulation of several displacements over these time scales. Therefore, the FPE is accurate only over distances larger than the jump length as well. This brings an important consequence for this work: transport in the presence of sharp structures is not faithfully described by the FPE. In the case of neutral fluids bounded by material walls, the interface region is provided by the outermost atomic layer of the solid surface, and is therefore on the atomic scale, much smaller that any turbulence scale
notaplasmi
.
The ME, on the other hand, does not suffer from the same problem: one is allowed to pick up arbitrary
p,\tau
, provided that they fulfill the coarse-scale constraints dictated by the experiment
notabayes
, hence issues of scale are of no concern here.

IV
Conclusions

The overall conclusion is that the BCs exert no effective constraint upon the DE. The latter is a valid picture of the physics of transport only over sufficiently large length scales. When the boundary-system interface width is smaller than these scales, it acts as an effective discontinuity region, making independent the physics determining the DE, valid in the interior, and that determining the BCs. When the transition region is large enough to make the diffusive approximation valid throughout the whole system, on the other hand, the DE and the BCs both arise consistently out of the same model of transport quantified by the ME. This entails that, in the transition region, the DE cannot be given independently from, rather adapt precisely to the form consistent with, the BCs.
