Economic Outcomes of Dalbavancin versus Standard Therapy in Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia in the DOTS Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Shelby D Reed, Yanhong Li, Nicholas A Turner, Dana K Pasquale, Richard Drew, Paul Cook, Smitha Zaharoff, Vance G Fowler, Thomas L Holland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:The DOTS trial demonstrated similar clinical outcomes among 200 adults with complicated Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) randomized to two doses of dalbavancin or standard-therapy antibiotics after initial clearance. We aimed to determine whether the higher cost of dalbavancin is offset through reductions in medical resource use.Methods:We used dates for hospitalizations and antibiotics from the trial starting from randomization. Antibiotic costs were based on generic pricing for dalbavancin and most other antibiotics. Medicare payment rates were used to estimate costs for scenarios representing home-based or clinic-based outpatient antibiotic therapy (OPAT).Results:On average, patients randomized to dalbavancin spent 2.2 fewer SAB-related days in the hospital (95%CI: −5.3 to 1.3) and received antibiotics on 24.4 fewer days (95%CI: −29.0 to −19.9). Mean inpatient costs for SAB-related admissions were lower by $5805 (95%CI: −14,311 to 3390) in the dalbavancin group. Antibiotic drug costs were $2343 higher (95%CI: 1728 to 3027), but OPAT costs were lower with dalbavancin by $2469 (95% CI: −2913 to −1970) in the home-based scenario and $1112 (95% CI: −1289 to −914) in the clinic-based scenario. Net costs for SAB-related care were $5931 lower (95%CI: −14,660 to 3004) and $4574 lower (95%CI: −13,312 to 4335) with dalbavancin versus standard-therapy with home-based and clinic-based OPAT, respectively. Among patients with injection drug use (IDU), savings with dalbavancin were approximately $22,000.Conclusions:On average, savings from reduced inpatient care and less OPAT fully offset the higher cost of dalbavancin in patients with SAB. Savings were greater in patients with IDU.ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04775953 Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, bacteremia, dalbavancin, clinical trial, cost-effectiveness, medical costs

INTRODUCTION

Staphylococcus aureus is the leading cause of death due to bloodstream infection worldwide1, with 90-day mortality rates of 25-30%.2,3 Standard therapy for complicated SAB traditionally requires four to six weeks of intravenous antibiotics, and patients often experience complications. The need for prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy requires careful transition from hospital to home-based care or other healthcare facilities to ensure adequate monitoring and continued treatment.

Individuals with injection drug use (IDU) are at especially high risk for SAB and poor treatment outcomes.4 Due to concerns about poor access to or adherence to long-term intravenous therapy in this population, extended institutionalized care, either in a hospital or skilled nursing facility, is often provided, resulting in high medical care costs. Understanding economic consequences of alternative antibiotic regimens is important to ensure that constrained healthcare dollars are spent wisely.

Previous observational studies have estimated the cost impact of dalbavancin in acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections5, severe gram-positive infections in hospitalized persons with IDU6, and sternal wound infections.7 However, economic evaluations of dalbavancin for SAB from randomized trials are lacking.

The Dalbavancin as an Option for the Treatment of S. aureus bacteremia (DOTS) trial was an open-label, assessor-masked, randomized superiority trial conducted at 23 sites across the US and Canada.8,9 In the current evaluation, we compare inpatient medical resource use and costs plus the cost of inpatient and outpatient antibiotic therapy between patients in the DOTS trial who were randomized to dalbavancin versus standard therapy from randomization to day 70. We also make these comparisons among persons with IDU.

METHODS

Data collection

Medical resource data pertaining to inpatient care collected in the DOTS trial case report form included hospital admission and discharge dates, reason for admission, and primary and secondary discharge diagnoses. For antibiotics, data collection included start and stop dates, drug name, dose, and frequency of administration. Information on outpatient care such as discharge destination, number of outpatient visits, or location of intravenous treatments was not collected.

Readmissions attributable to SAB or other causes were based on blinded assessments of free-text information for primary and secondary discharge diagnoses recorded in the trial by three expert physicians (NAT, PC, TLH). Attribution was assigned when at least two physicians agreed.

Counts of antibiotic days for drugs dosed once per day or more frequently were calculated based on start and stop dates. However, for drugs dosed less frequently, only days the drugs were administered were counted. For example, dalbavancin dosed on days 1 and 8 counted as two antibiotic days. Overlapping days with combination therapy were not counted as additional days

Cost assignment

Costs were valued in 2025 US dollars (USD) from the perspective of the US healthcare system. Table 1 summarizes unit costs used in the economic evaluation. Inpatient costs were estimated using the 2022 Nationwide Inpatient Sample available through HCUPnet.gov10 and updated to 2025 values using the Consumer Price Index for Medical Care.11 The cost per inpatient day was computed by dividing the mean cost for MS Diagnosis-Related Group (MS-DRG) 872 representing hospitalizations for “septicemia/sepsis without mechanical ventilation and without major complications and comorbidities” ($10,570) and dividing by the corresponding mean length of stay (4.2 days). This MS-DRG code was chosen because it better reflects the inpatient mortality rate observed in the trial (4%) compared to other candidate MS-DRG codes.9

While dalbavancin was not available in a generic formulation during the DOTS trial, multiple manufacturers have subsequently received FDA approval for generic versions. The cost of dalbavancin was thus based on the updated 2026 price for a generic formulation, and all other antibiotic medication costs were estimated using prices from the Federal Supply Schedule for Pharmaceuticals and Drugs (FSS)12 in the base-case analysis because these prices are publicly available and likely to approximate discounted prices paid by many larger healthcare institutions. Antibiotic costs were assigned using the closest minimum package size needed to fulfill the dose. For example, if a 2000 mg dose of an antibiotic was needed, the cost of a single 2000 mg vial (if available) was assigned rather than two 1000 mg vials. If the drug was available only in 1000 mg vials, the cost of two vials would be assigned. In the base-case analysis, we assumed no wastage of antibiotics where any leftover drug in a vial or bag could be used to fill other doses for the same or other patient.

Across both study groups for patients who were discharged during the follow-up period and continuing to receive antibiotic therapy, we assigned costs for two outpatient physician visits for clinical assessments, to manage antibiotic therapy and assess clinical outcomes. The cost of each visit was based on the 2025 national average payment for fee-for-service Medicare for CPT 99214.13 For patients receiving two or more weeks of outpatient antibiotic infusions, we assigned costs associated with placing a PICC line in the inpatient setting. Frequencies and types of outpatient laboratory tests for toxicity monitoring for specific antibiotics were based on 2018 IDSA practice guidelines (Supplemental Table S1).14 Blood draws and laboratory monitoring costs were based on 2025 Medicare payment rates.15

We designed two scenarios to estimate medical costs associated with delivering antibiotic therapy after hospital discharge. One scenario represents home healthcare-based outpatient antibiotic therapy (home-based OPAT) and the other represents clinic-based OPAT. For home-based OPAT, the cost for an initial visit by a home health nurse was assumed to take 60 minutes including administration of the first dose plus patient education and training to administer subsequent doses.16 For subsequent weekly visits, costs for 45-minute visits were assigned for home health services, including a draw blood for laboratory monitoring17, to change dressings, and to assess the patient. For clinic-based OPAT, costs were assigned for intravenous infusions along with weekly blood draws for laboratory monitoring.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted three sets of sensitivity analyses. First, instead of assigning a cost for generic dalbavancin, we assigned the FSS price for branded dalbavancin. Second, we applied average wholesale prices (AWP) from the Micromedex RED BOOK®18 instead of FSS-based prices for antibiotics. In both cases, we applied different assumptions to account for how packages of antibiotics are prepared by the pharmacy. Instead of assuming no wastage of injectable antibiotic products when doses are prepared (base-case), we assumed that any remaining drug in the smallest package size available was discarded and the cost of the full vial/package was assigned. Third, to evaluate the impact of cost outliers resulting from extended hospital stays, we excluded two patients from each group with the highest cumulative inpatient costs (SAB-related and total) and recomputed descriptive statistics.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, medical resource use, and costs. Inpatient days and costs were computed from the date of randomization. Applying the intent-to-treat principle, all patients randomized in the trial were included in the analysis. We used generalized linear models to compare inpatient days and days of antibiotic therapy between study groups using negative binomial error distributions and log links. Given limited statistical power for cost comparisons and to provide descriptive information on uncertainty associated with between-group comparisons, we used nonparametric bootstrapping to report bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for differences in mean estimates. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina) was used for all statistical analyses.

All participating patients provided written informed consent for trial participation upon enrollment. The economic evaluation was determined exempt by the Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

From April 2021 to December 2023, 100 patients were randomized to each treatment group. Mean age at baseline was 54 years (SD 16), 69% were male, and 69% were white. (Supplemental Table 2). Fifteen percent of patients had history of IDU, and twenty-one percent had osteomyelitis at baseline. The median number of inpatient days between admission for the index hospitalization and study randomization was three days. When excluding patients with osteomyelitis at baseline who were followed for 180 days, mean duration of follow up was 66.3 days in the dalbavancin group and 69.1 days in the standard therapy group. Among the 129 readmissions across both study groups for the full duration of follow-up, 73 (56.6%) were deemed to be SAB-related.

Medical Resource Use and Costs for Full Sample

The mean number of inpatient days from randomization to discharge from the index hospitalization was 5.8 days in the dalbavancin group versus 7.6 days in the standard therapy group, a mean reduction of 1.8 days (p=0.06) [Table 2]. Twenty percent of patients in the dalbavancin group were readmitted for SAB-related causes within 70 days compared to 26% in the standard therapy group (p=0.32). All-cause readmission rates within 70 days were also similar between treatment groups (37% in dalbavancin group; 35% in standard therapy group, p=0.77). Total inpatient days associated with SAB over the 70-day follow-up period trended lower in the dalbavancin group versus standard therapy group (8.3 vs. 10.4 days p=0.11). However, there were significant differences in the number of days on which antibiotics were administered. On average, compared to patients in the standard therapy group, patients in the dalbavancin group had 5.0 fewer days when they received antibiotics in the hospital and 19.4 fewer days of OPAT for a total reduction of 24.4 fewer days over the follow-up period (p<0.0001 for each).

Estimated costs for inpatient care, antibiotic costs, and costs associated with OPAT are reported in Table 2. From randomization to discharge for the index admission, mean costs were $4681 (95% CI: −11,499 to 2452) lower among patients randomized to dalbavancin compared to standard therapy. When accounting for readmissions associated with SAB, mean total inpatient costs from randomization to day 70 were $22,122 in the dalbavancin group and $27,927 in the standard therapy group, a difference of $-5805 (95% CI: −14,311 to 3390).

In the base-case analysis, estimated mean costs for inpatient and outpatient antibiotic medications were $3079 in the dalbavancin group versus $736 in the standard therapy group, an incremental cost of $2343 (95%CI: 1728 to 3027). However, mean estimated costs associated with administering OPAT were lower in the dalbavancin group by an estimated $2469 (95% CI: −2913 to −1970) when therapy is provided in patients’ homes and a mean savings of $1112 (95% CI: −1289 to −914) when therapy is delivered in an outpatient clinic setting.

When accounting for lower mean costs for SAB-related hospitalizations, higher antibiotic costs, and lower OPAT costs in the dalbavancin, total mean costs were $5931 lower (95%CI: −14,660 to 3004) with dalbavancin versus standard therapy in the scenario representing home-based OPAT and $4574 lower in the scenario representing clinic-based OPAT (95%CI: −13,312 to 4335). When accounting for all-cause readmissions, mean total costs remained lower in the dalbavancin versus standard therapy group.

Sensitivity analysis

Findings from three sets of sensitivity analyses are reported in Table 3 to convey the extent to which our results changed when varying our costing and analytic approaches. Applying branded prices for dalbavancin using either the FSS or AWP almost doubled the cost for antibiotics in the dalbavancin arm. Instead of a net increase in antibiotic costs of approximately $2300, the net increase was about $5400 with branded dalbavancin. Applying AWPs instead of prices based on the FSS for antibiotics and different assumptions about drug wastage increased costs in the standard therapy group, but the impact on differential antibiotic costs between study groups was relatively minimal. When excluding the top two cost outliers, mean costs decreased in both groups, but differential costs remained about the same as in the base-case analysis.

Subgroup with Intravenous Drug Use

Mean estimates of medical resource use for subgroups defined by IDU are reported in Table 4. Sixteen patients in the dalbavancin group and 13 patients in the standard therapy group had history of IDU at baseline. Figure 1 shows the mean numbers of inpatient days from randomization to initial discharge and with readmissions for SAB-related causes to day 70 stratified by IDU and treatment group. Among patients with IDU, the mean number of inpatient days associated with SAB was lower in the dalbavancin group versus the standard therapy group (9.1 days vs. 18.6 days, 95%CI for difference: −18.6 to −1.5; p=0.03). Inpatient days were similar between treatment groups among people who do not inject drugs. Reductions in inpatient antibiotic days in the dalbavancin vs the standard therapy group were greater in the IDU group (−10.7 days; p=0.04) compared to the non-IDU group (−4.2 days; p<0.01), but total reductions in all antibiotic days were similar (−26.4 vs. −24.1 days; p<0.01 for each).

DISCUSSION

This secondary analysis of the DOTS trial reveals two key findings. First, at a cost of $450 per 500 mg vial of dalbavancin, or $1350 per 1500mg dose, mean total costs of SAB-related care were lower by about $5900 in the two-dose dalbavancin group compared to standard therapy in the home health-based OPAT scenario and about $4600 lower in clinic-based OPAT scenario. Second, mean total SAB-related costs were lower among patients without IDU and dramatically lower among patients with IDU. These findings have significant implications for the populations and clinical settings in which dalbavancin has a unique clinical role for treatment of complicated SAB.

The main driver of cost differences between treatment groups was the two-day reduction in mean length of stay in the dalbavancin arm versus the standard therapy arm. The associated savings of nearly $4700 is more than sufficient to offset the cost of an initial one or two doses of dalbavancin. Most of the length of stay reduction was among patients with IDU. The mean duration of initial hospitalization among the patients with IDU in the standard therapy group was over three times that in the dalbavancin group (17 days vs. 5 days), leading to a difference of almost $32,000 per patient. That savings was somewhat attenuated by higher costs for more readmissions among patients with IDU in the dalbavancin group resulting in savings of about $25,000 for SAB-related inpatient care. This savings more than offset higher costs associated with antibiotic therapy (drug costs and OPAT cost), resulting in total net costs for SAB-related care that were about $22,000 per patient lower with dalbavancin. When individuals with IDU were excluded from the cost comparisons, total mean SAB-related costs over 70 days were lower by about $3500 and $2100 in the dalbavancin versus standard therapy groups in scenarios representing home health-based OPAT and clinic-based OPAT, respectively.

As reported by Turner et al., clinical efficacy was lower among trial participants with IDU (52%) than those without (76%), but there were not differences between treatment groups.9 The trial did not collect information to determine whether prolonged hospital stays were attributable to poor clinical status or because of concerns or hospital-level policies about OPAT and central venous access among people who inject drugs. Our results likely reflect variable practice patterns across participating institutions. For hospitals requiring antimicrobial completion therapy in the inpatient setting or in a skilled nursing or other facility for patients with IDU, our estimated cost-savings associated with dalbavancin are likely to be an underestimate. Given the estimated duration of OPAT in the standard therapy arm, if applying the mean Medicare payment for skilled nursing facility care of about $575 per day ($540 per day17 updated to $2025), estimated costs for this care would approximate $14,550 per patient, further increasing cost-savings with dalbavancin.

Our study has several important strengths. First, the randomized design allows us to avoid selection bias that often confounds results from observational studies. Second, we applied several conservative assumptions when estimating costs of standard-therapy antibiotics, including use of the FSS which represents prices that are lower than those attainable by non-federal institutions, omitting pharmacist time monitoring and adjusting inpatient vancomycin levels, and exclusion of additional nursing time, supplies and waste associated with intravenous therapy. Third, study participants were enrolled across 23 hospital systems from across the United States and Canada, and the study protocol allowed sites to apply their local practice norms with regard to hospital discharge and location of care for OPAT. However, our findings may not be readily generalizable beyond the U.S. Fourth, a substantial proportion of enrolled patients had IDU, which allowed us to report on financially meaningful subgroups. Lastly, our findings were robust across several sensitivity analyses.

There are also some limitations that should be considered. First, limited data on medical resource use and costs were collected in DOTS. We did not have information on whether patients were discharged from the hospital to home or to other facilities, and we relied on externally derived unit costs and assumptions about receipt of OPAT. While these assumptions may reflect typical courses of outpatient care, they undoubtedly do not reflect real-world complexity. However, the information was adequate to capture the expected two differential cost drivers: inpatient costs and days on various antibiotics. Our approach to assigning two sets of assumptions to estimate OPAT-related costs for administration at home or outpatient clinics provides transparency and allows hospitals to calculate weighted average costs based on proportion of patients receiving home-based or clinic-based OPAT. Our disaggregated results also can be used to estimate costs from different perspectives, including a scenario in which patients are discharged prior to receiving their first dose of dalbavancin and for institutions with different pricing for dalbavancin. We also did not assign differential antibiotic costs for patients who completed therapy using oral antibiotics because this applied to just six patients in the standard therapy group, and they would still have incurred follow-up visits and laboratory tests as necessary. Another concern may be that the duration of follow up in DOTS was relatively short. However, we have included the majority of costs associated with SAB; only one patient without osteomyelitis remained hospitalized for an SAB-related cause at day 70. Lastly, the sample sizes were relatively small for cost comparisons, especially for the subgroup defined by IDU, but we have reported confidence intervals for differences in means to transparently report levels of uncertainty.

CONCLUSION

Across all patients in the DOTS trial, higher antibiotic costs in the dalbavancin group were fully recovered from lower costs for SAB-associated inpatient care and additional savings accrued from lower costs associated with OPAT. Among patients with IDU, those treated with dalbavancin experienced significantly fewer SAB-related inpatient days and needed fewer days of OPAT, resulting in expected cost-savings for SAB-related care.
