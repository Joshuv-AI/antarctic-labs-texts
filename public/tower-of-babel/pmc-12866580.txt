Overexpression of cancer testis antigens in gastric cancer and their correlations with the patients’ clinicopathological characteristics
Authors: Maliheh Alimardani, Hossein Rahimi, Ali Ghasemi, Meysam Moghbeli, Mehran Gholamin, Mohammad Reza Abbaszadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-testis antigens are typically expressed in malignant cells, germ-line cells, and the placenta. Given their potential as targets for cancer immunotherapy, it is essential to profile their expression. This study examines the clinical significance of MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO expression levels in gastric cancer compared to normal tissues.MethodsRNA was isolated from fresh gastric specimens of 76 patients diagnosed with GC before any therapeutic intervention. The Real-Time PCR evaluated the MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO relative mRNA levels. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH using the ΔΔCt method. Fold changes > 2 with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsThe results indicated that the elevated mRNA expression levels of MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO were observed in 21.05%, 22.38%, and 26.32% of the cases, respectively. Furthermore, a substantial upregulation of NY-ESO was noted in non-cardia tumors with T1/2 depth of invasion (p = 0.021). This marked increase in NY-ESO expression was also observed in primary-stage tumors situated in non-cardia regions (p = 0.026). MAGE-A4 expression tended to be higher in lymph node–negative tumors, while NY-ESO expression was associated with overall survival, and LAGE-1 showed variable expression.ConclusionOur research demonstrates that MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO expression are often upregulated in patients with GC. This overexpression in GC tumors is significantly associated with poor prognosis. Therefore, these may serve as a significant prognostic indicator for gastric cancer, and the cancer-testis antigens could represent a potential therapeutic target for a gastric cancer vaccine.Keywords: Gastric cancer, MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, NY-ESO, Expression, Immunotherapy

Background

One of the most frequent and lethal malignancies globally is Gastric cancer (GC), accounting for the fourth greatest cause of cancer-related death [1]. It is regarded as a serious threat to human health, and some variables, such as epigenetic, genetic, and multi-step processes, influence its occurrence and development [2]. Helicobacter pylori infection is commonly observed in GC patients, and the majority of GC patients are often identified at an advanced stage due to the absence of distinctive symptoms that can be used to diagnose the disease [3, 4]. Since the symptoms of early-stage GC are not very noticeable, a large proportion of GC patients are diagnosed with advanced-stage illness [5]. Thus, it is imperative to understand the molecular process causing gastric cancer to find novel treatment approaches and limit its incidence [6].

Cancer testis antigens (CTAs) are increasingly recognized as optimal targets for cancer immunotherapy because they are expressed only in the testis and placenta [7]. To date, over 200 CTA genes have been identified and classified into 44 families [8]. Two of the most well-studied cancer-testis antigens are NY-ESO-1 (New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma-1) and MAGE-A (melanoma-antigen family A), both encoded by genes on chromosome X. These antigens are considered promising targets for immunotherapy because they exhibit low expression levels in nonmalignant tissue [9, 10].

The human MAGE family is classified into two major subgroups based on variations in tissue-specific gene expression and gene structure: MAGE-I and MAGE-II [11]. The MAGE-I family includes the MAGE-A, MAGE-B, and MAGE-C subfamilies. Abnormal expression of these antigen proteins is frequent in a wide range of cancers. Currently, more than 60 members of the MAGE family have been recognized. These members possess a highly conserved MAGE homology domain (MHD) consisting of around 170 amino acids. The MAGE-A family’s MHD makes up the majority of the protein and is 60–80% conserved among family members [12]. The MAGE-A family consists of 12 members known as MAGE-A1-MAGE-A12 [13]. Research indicates that the overexpression of MAGE-A4 enhanced the cancer cells’ survival through its interaction with transcriptional partners of c-myc, Gankyrin, and Miz1. In addition, recruitment of the MAGE-A4-Miz1 transcriptional complex to the Cip1/p21 leads to downregulation of Cip1/p21, thereby enhancing cancer cell survival [14]. Moreover, MAGE-A4 can repress transcription of the STAT family by binding to PIAS2. This c-terminal fragment leads to the activation of several proteins, like HDAC1(histone deacetylase 1) and SNW1, at promoters, resulting in local transcriptional repression [15]. Also, this fragment inhibits p21-mediated cell cycle arrest and blocks the upregulation of p21 [14]. Consequently, MAGE-A4 mostly plays an oncogenic role in some malignancies. Novel approaches aimed at targeting MAGE-A antigens hold significant potential for immunotherapy in individuals diagnosed with cancer.

The gene responsible for NY-ESO-1, which is referred to as cancer testis antigen 1B (CTAG1), serves as a representative member of the CT gene family and was originally identified in esophageal carcinoma [16]. The expression of this factor has been reported in various malignancies, including renal cancer, esophageal carcinoma, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and penile squamous cell carcinoma [16–18].

Another gene belonging to the CTA family is LAGE-1, which spans 3245 base pairs and consists of three exons. The variants LAGE-1a, 1b, 1 S, and 1 L are designated as LAGE-1 family genes. These genes are situated at the Xq28 locus, adjacent to the MAGE-A1 gene within the MAGE gene family [19]. Research indicates a strong association between the expression of LAGE-1 and those of MAGE and NY-ESO-1 genes. LAGE-1 is classified as a cancer-testis antigen, making it a promising candidate for tumor immunotherapy, as its expression is significantly elevated in various tumor types, including prostate, melanomas, head and neck, ovarian, bladder, and lung cancers, while being largely restricted to normal tissues [20, 21]. Additionally, LAGE-1 exhibits tumor-specific expression patterns akin to those of MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO, suggesting its potential as a specific antigen aimed at facilitating anti-tumor immunotherapy. We focused on MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO in this study because these cancer-testis antigens are commonly expressed in gastric and other solid tumors, show limited expression in normal tissues, and have been linked to tumor immunogenicity and patient prognosis. Their established clinical relevance and potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers make them ideal candidates for investigation in gastric cancer.

Methods

Human gastric tissue samples

A total of 76 untreated GC patients involving surgically resected tumoral and adjacent normal tissue specimens were gathered at Omid and Emam Reza Hospitals, which are part of MUMS in Mashhad, Iran. The specimens were promptly preserved in RNA later solution and stored at −20 °C until the extraction process. Informed consent was duly obtained from all patients before surgery, and the research protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Mashhad Medical University [Approval number: IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1400.077]. Clinical and pathological information, such as the patient’s age, tumor dimensions, histological grade, involvement of lymph nodes, and TNM classification, was extracted from the patient’s medical records.

Quantitative Real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and statistical analysis

Total RNA was isolated from both GC and adjacent normal tissues utilizing the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, showing sharp 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands. RNA purity was determined by A260/280 ratios, and only samples with ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for downstream analysis. For the reverse transcription process, a cDNA synthesis kit (Parstous, Iran) was employed, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. qRT-PCR reactions were carried out in 20 µL volumes containing 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix (Parstous, Iran), 0.5 µM forward and reverse primers, and 50 ng cDNA. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed with a 2X SYBR Green Master Mix (Parstous, Iran) on the Light Cycler 96 instrument (Roche, Germany). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 25 s, annealing at 60 °C for 25 s, and extension at 72 °C for 25 s. The primer sequences are detailed in Table 1. No-template controls (NTC) were included in each run to monitor contamination.

Primer specificity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, showing a single amplicon of the expected size, and melt-curve analysis revealed a single peak. Normalization of the data was performed using GAPDH (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase) as a reference. qRT-PCR data were processed using the comparative ΔΔCt method. For each sample, Ct values were normalized to the housekeeping gene [GAPDH] to obtain ΔCt (ΔCt = Ct_target − Ct_housekeeping). For paired comparisons, ΔΔCt was calculated as ΔCt_tumor − ΔCt_adjacent for each patient, and relative log2fold change was computed as − ΔΔCt. Log 2 fold changes exceeding + 2 were classified as overexpression, while those below − 2 were categorized as underexpression. All experiments were conducted independently in triplicate.

All statistical evaluations were conducted utilizing the SPSS 19.0 software package (SPSS, Inc., USA). Normality of the paired ΔΔCt values for each gene was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For genes whose paired ΔΔCt differences were approximately normally distributed, a two-sided paired t-test was used to compare tumor versus matched adjacent non-tumor tissues. For genes that violated normality, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The relationships between patients’ clinical characteristics and gene expression levels were also analyzed using the Chi-square test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Survival rates were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed with the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves are presented in Fig. 1.

Results

General data of gastric cancer patients

Seventy-six GC patients were included in this research, and general data of these patients are detailed in Table 2. There were 57 males and 19 females, 48 cases with age ≥ 60 years, and 28 cases with age < 60 years, and the mean age was 62.97 ± 10.9(mean ± SD). The number of tumors in the non-cardia was higher than those in the cardia (45/76 vs. 31/76). The distribution of stages was as follows: stage I accounted for 5 cases (6.58%), stage II comprised 15 cases (19.7%), stage III included 41 cases (53.9%), and stage IV represented 15 cases (19.7%). Metastatic lymph nodes were identified in 64 out of 76 cases, representing 84.2%. Among the 80 tumors examined, 48 (63.16%) exhibited moderate differentiation. Additionally, Helicobacter Pylori (HP) was detected in 36 of the 76 cases (47.4%).

MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO mRNA expression in GC tissue

To assess the mRNA expression levels of MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO in gastric cancer (GC) tissues, Real-time polymerase chain reactions were employed. The qRT-PCR analysis findings indicated that these genes’ expression pattern was changed (Fig. 2). For example, the mRNA expression level of MAGE-A4 in 21.05% (16/76) of gastric cancer tissue was markedly elevated compared to that in adjacent non-cancerous tissue. Consistently, a high expression of LAGE-1 and NY-ESO was noted in 22.38% and 26.32% of the cases, respectively (Table 3). Moreover, Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that ΔΔCt values for MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO were normally distributed (p = 0.061, 0.25, respectively), whereas LAGE-1 deviated from normality (p = 0.005). Accordingly, we used paired t-tests for MAGE-A4, NY-ESO, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for LAGE-1. The relative mRNA expression level of MAGE-A4 was higher in tumor tissues than in the corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues (mean ΔΔCt = 0.1816 ± 3.0712; paired t-test, p = 0.608); however, the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, NY-ESO expression showed no significant change between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues (mean ΔΔCt = 0.0692 ± 3.7004; paired t-test, p = 0.781). The distribution of LAGE-1 expression data was not normal (Shapiro–Wilk test, p = 0.005); therefore, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, which also revealed no significant difference between tumor and adjacent tissues (Z = − 1.547, p = 0.122). Furthermore, a significant direct correlation was identified between the expression levels of LAGE-1 and MAGE-A4 (r = 0.558, p < 0.001). Likewise, there was a direct significant correlation between NY-ESO and MAGE-A4 (r = 0.0.633, p < 0.001). Additionally, a significant direct correlation between LAGE-1 and NY-ESO (r = 0.447, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

The correlation between MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO mRNA expression in GC tissue and clinicopathological characteristics of patients

To enhance our comprehension of the association between the expression levels of MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO and disease progression, we investigated the correlation between the mRNA expression of these markers and a range of clinical characteristics within a cohort of 76 patients (Table 4). The findings indicated a notable upregulation of LAGE-1 in male subjects (p = 0.024) and tumors at the primary stage (p = 0.029). Conversely, a significant downregulation of LAGE-1 was identified in male patients with positive lymph nodes (p = 0.036). Furthermore, MAGE-A4 expression was marginally higher in lymph node-negative tumors than in node-positive tumors (p = 0.05). In male patients with cardia tumors, MAGE-A4 levels were relatively increased (p = 0.032), though this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of cases and lack of multiple comparison correction. Specifically, the expression levels of NY-ESO were markedly reduced in the differentiation grades from 1 to 3 in non-cardia tumors (p = 0.033). Additionally, a significant upregulation of NY-ESO was observed in non-cardia tumors characterized by T1/2 depth of invasion (p = 0.021). Also, this pronounced upregulation of NY-ESO was evident in primary-stage tumors located in non-cardia (p = 0.026). A significant inverse relationship was identified between NY-ESO expression levels and tumor size in non-cardia tumors (p = 0.021). Finally, there was a correlation between the levels of NY-ESO expression and prognosis, in which NY-ESO downregulation was significantly associated with poor prognosis (p = 0.047) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Gastric cancer continues to be a prominent contributor to cancer-related deaths globally. Investigating tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) has become increasingly important in elucidating gastric cancer’s biology and advancing targeted treatment strategies. Notably, antigens such as MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO have emerged as critical markers, necessitating an in-depth examination of their expression patterns and potential roles in gastric cancer. Our study extends previous reports by demonstrating a distinct expression pattern of selected CTAs in gastric cancer. While earlier studies have primarily described NY-ESO-1 and MAGE-A4 expression in melanoma and sarcoma, our results highlight their association with tumor size and grade, specifically in gastric cancer, suggesting possible tumor-type-specific regulation.

MAGE-A4 belongs to the MAGE family, primarily found in malignant tissues and germ cells. In the context of gastric cancer, the expression of MAGE-A4 has been correlated with poor prognosis and more aggressive tumor characteristics. In the present study, we found overexpression of MAGE-A4 in 21% (16/76) of GC patients. Although higher MAGE-A4 expression appeared in lymph node-negative and cardia tumors, particularly among male patients, these observations are preliminary due to their marginal significance and limited sample size. Further investigations with larger cohorts are needed to substantiate these trends. whereas Ishihara et al. demonstrated that MAGE-A4 was expressed in 35.0% (7 out of 20) of gastric cancer cases [22]. MAGE-A4 has been detected in various other malignancies, including melanoma, esophageal, lung, breast, and bladder cancer, with expression levels generally falling between 10% and 50%. The MAGE-A4 mRNA expression rates in melanoma have been documented to range from 10% to 30%, indicating a significant level that warrants its exploration in immunotherapeutic interventions, including cancer vaccines [23]. Moreover, in breast cancer, the expression of MAGE-A4 is more variable, with reported levels between 4% and 40%, which may be influenced by tumor subtype and other factors [24, 25]. Although the influence of CT antigens on survival remains controversial, likely due to variations in tumor type, stage, and/or burden, Bandic et al. show that MAGE-A4 antigen expression was found in significantly fewer women experiencing locoregional relapse (p = 0.014). Also, they revealed a significant difference in MAGE-A4 expression specifically between women with locoregional relapse and those in the 5-year relapse-free group (p = 0.005) [24]. Additionally, MAGE-A4 has been identified in NSCLC (non-small cell lung cancer), with expression levels reported between 18% and 60%, suggesting its involvement in the pathogenesis of lung cancer [26]. In this research, MAGE-A4 expression was found to be linked to squamous cell carcinomas, present in 47% of cases, while only 16% of adenocarcinomas exhibited MAGE-A4 (p < 0.0001). Also, the positive expression rate was notably higher in tissues from patients with advanced pathological stages, detected in 21%, 32%, and 34% of patients with stage I, II, and III/IV disease, respectively (p = 0.0309) [26]. Other investigations noted a 33–38% expression of MAGE-A4 in bladder carcinoma [27, 28].

Additionally, in the current investigation, we analyzed the expression levels of the LAGE-1 and NY-ESO genes in gastric tumor tissues alongside their matched nontumor counterparts. The findings revealed that among 76 pairs of gastric tumors and nontumor tissues, LAGE-1 and NY-ESO mRNA exhibited overexpression in 22.37% (17 out of 76) and 26.32% (20 out of 76) of the tumor samples, respectively. Additionally, these results indicate a significant increase in NY-ESO expression in non-cardia tumors with T1/2 depth of invasion (p = 0.021). This marked upregulation of NY-ESO was also apparent in primary-stage tumors situated in non-cardia regions (p = 0.026). An important inverse relationship was found between NY-ESO expression levels and tumor size in non-cardia tumors (p = 0.021). Our data suggests that tumors with higher NY-ESO-1 levels may exhibit a smaller size at presentation. One plausible biological explanation is that elevated expression of the cancer-testis antigen NY-ESO-1 might increase tumor immunogenicity, thereby promoting immune-mediated tumor control and restraining tumor growth. Previous studies have demonstrated that NY-ESO-1 expression is significantly associated with tumor size and grade. Hashimoto et al. reported a positive correlation between NY-ESO-1 expression and larger tumor size in soft-tissue sarcomas [29], while Ries et al. found a similar relationship in oral squamous cell carcinoma [30]. These observations highlight that NY-ESO-1 may be linked to tumor progression and aggressiveness. However, in our study, we observed an inverse correlation, suggesting that NY-ESO-1 might also act as an immunogenic marker associated with enhanced immune recognition and smaller tumor burden. Lastly, there was a connection between NY-ESO expression levels and prognosis, with downregulation of NY-ESO significantly linked to poorer outcomes (p = 0.047). These CT genes have been associated with various malignancies, such as gastric and esophageal cancers. Similarly, Mashino et al. conducted a study involving 46 esophageal carcinoma and 102 gastric carcinoma samples analyzed through RT-PCR. They discovered that NY-ESO was expressed in 11 (23.9%) cases of ESCS and 8 (7.8%) of GC samples. Furthermore, it was established that LAGE-1 was expressed in 18 cases (39.1%) of ESCS and 12 cases (11.8%) of GC samples [31]. Out of 25 primary gastrointestinal lesions, 3 (12%) tested positive for MAGE, while 4 out of 24 metastatic gastrointestinal lesions (17%) also showed positive results [32]. Kerkar observed a markedly elevated expression of MAGE-A, exceeding 50% in tumor cells, in comparison to NY-ESO-1 across various cancer types. This was particularly evident in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (50% versus 0%), bladder urothelial carcinoma (40.4% versus 8.3%), ovarian carcinoma (26.4% versus 3.6%), endometrial carcinoma (26.3% versus 1.3%), gastric adenocarcinomas (20% versus 4%), hepatocellular carcinoma (18.8% versus 1.2%), and colorectal adenocarcinomas (10.7% versus less than 1%) [9]. Akcakanat et al. investigated the immunohistochemical expression of cancer/testis antigen (CTA) proteins in a cohort of 213 patients diagnosed with esophageal carcinoma. Their findings revealed that NY-ESO-1 and MAGE-A exhibited heterogeneous expression in 44 (21%) and 111 (52%) of the tumors, respectively [33]. Moreover, the 3-year survival rates for patients who were antigen-positive and antigen-negative were 52.1% and 51.6% for NY-ESO-1, and 46.1% and 57.6% for MAGE-A, respectively [33]. Additionally, another investigation revealed NY-ESO mRNA expression in 41 specimens, accounting for 33% of the carcinoma samples, with notably higher expression levels observed in well-differentiated and moderately differentiated types of carcinoma. The survival rate was greater in cases that tested positive for the NY-ESO-1 protein compared to those that were negative. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.0959) [34]. Recent evidence underscores the clinical and immunological significance of tumor-associated and immune-related biomarkers across malignancies. For instance, IL27RA and TMEM71 have been identified as immune-regulatory indicators in breast cancer, linking their expression to immune infiltration and therapeutic responsiveness [35, 36]. Within this context, the aberrant expression of cancer-testis antigens (CTAs) such as MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO-1 in gastric cancer may not only represent markers of immune recognition but also contribute functionally to tumor progression and patient stratification for immunotherapy. These observations collectively reinforce the emerging concept that CTA expression, when considered alongside immune-regulatory and inflammatory biomarkers, could enhance prognostic modelling and guide the design of combination immunotherapeutic strategies in gastric cancer. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the mRNA expression levels of MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO-1 in gastric cancer tissues from Iranian patients. This analysis provides essential baseline data for our ongoing dendritic cell-based clinical trial, in which dendritic cells are loaded with recombinant chimeric antigens containing these three genes. We hypothesize that assessing CTA expression in this cohort will aid in patient stratification, guide immune monitoring, and inform the design of CTA-targeted immunotherapeutic strategies in gastric cancer. Ishihara et al. demonstrate that the NY-ESO-1 expression rates were notably elevated in lung cancer (28.6%), ovarian cancer (22.6%), and esophageal cancer (25.3%) [22]. LAGE-1 was detected in 3 out of 11 (27%) esophageal cancer cell lines and in 3 out of 7 (43%) esophageal cancer samples, while it is not present in normal esophageal epithelial tissue [37]. In our previous work, Forghanifard et al. investigated the expression profile of cancer-testis antigens in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) within the Iranian population using Real-time PCR-based approaches. In that study, we observed a significant positive correlation between MAGE-A4 and both NY-ESO-1 and LAGE-1. Notably, overexpression of LAGE-1, MAGE-A4, and NY-ESO-1 mRNA was detected in 39%, 90.2%, and 41.4% of tumor samples, respectively. Moreover, a strong co-expression pattern between LAGE-1 and MAGE-A4 was identified exclusively in well-differentiated tumors (p = 0.014; correlation coefficient = 0.776), while MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO-1 showed significant co-expression in moderately differentiated tumors (p < 0.001). These findings from our own earlier studies provided a strong rationale for prioritizing MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO-1 in the current investigation [38]. Beyond their potential as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers, cancer-testis antigens (CTAs) may play functional roles in tumorigenesis. Emerging evidence suggests that CTAs such as NY-ESO-1 and MAGE-A family members can modulate cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune evasion by interacting with transcriptional regulators and signaling pathways [39]. In gastric cancer, such mechanisms remain incompletely understood, but our results raise the possibility that aberrant CTA expression may contribute not only to tumor immunogenicity but also to carcinogenic progression. A limitation of this study is the absence of protein-level validation for MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO expression. Although mRNA expression generally parallels protein abundance, discrepancies may exist. Further investigations incorporating immunohistochemical or western blot analyses are therefore warranted to confirm and further substantiate these findings.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that MAGE-A4, LAGE-1, and NY-ESO mRNA are frequently upregulated in gastric cancer (GC) patients. The overexpression of these mRNA in GC tumors correlates significantly with adverse prognosis and reduced overall survival among affected individuals. Additionally, to determine which cancer/testis (CTA) antigens should be prioritized for a GC vaccine, it is essential to elucidate further the expression levels of each member of this antigen family in GC patients. Given their roles in tumor progression and immune evasion, this highlights the necessity for continued investigation into targeted therapies and immunotherapeutic strategies. Future research should aim to develop targeted therapies that leverage these antigens, potentially facilitating personalized treatment modalities that improve patient survival and enhance quality of life.
