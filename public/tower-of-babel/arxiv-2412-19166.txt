Fluid-particle interactions and fluctuation-dissipation relations I - General linear theory and basic fluctuational patterns
Massimiliano Giona, Giuseppe Procopio, Chiara Pezzotti

Abstract
The article provides a unitary and complete solution to the fluctuation-dissipation relations for particle hydromechanics in a generic fluid, accounting for the hydrodynamic fluid-particle interactions (including arbitrary memory kernels in the description of dissipative and fluid inertial effects) in linear hydrodynamic regimes, via the concepts of fluctuational patterns. This is achieved by expressing the memory kernels as a linear superposition of exponentially decaying modes. Given the structure of the interaction with the internal degrees of freedom, and assuming the representation of the thermal force as a superposition of modal contributions, the fluctuation-dissipation relation follows simply from the moment analysis of the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation, imposing the condition that at equilibrium all the internal degrees of freedom are uncorrelated with particle velocity. Moreover, the functional structure of the resulting equation of motion corresponds to the principle of complete decoupling amongst the internal degrees of freedom. The theory is extended to the case of confined geometries, by generalizing previous results including the effect of fluid inertia.



I
Introduction

Fluctuation-dissipation theory represents a major field in statistical physics
connecting the deterministic (particle or field) interactions of
a physical system (e.g. a micrometric particle in
a fluid) with the characterization
of stochastic thermal fluctuations that should be taken into account in
order to describe its statistical properties at thermal equilibrium
[
1
,
2
]
.
Pioneered by the research of Einstein, Langevin, Smoluchoswki
[
3
,
4
,
5
]

on the physics of Brownian
motion - expressed in its simplest form, i.e. for a spherical particle
subjected to the Stokesian drag and to a random thermal force -
the concept of fluctuation-dissipation relations evolved in a manifolds of different
branches and meanings: the extension to electric fluctuations
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
,
the connection to macroscopic properties of materials (e.g. the susceptivities)
[
9
,
10
]
,
the application to quantum fluctuations
[
11
,
12
]
,
the statistical physics of fields (e.g. statistical hydrodynamics)

[
13
,
14
,
15
]
and generalized field equations
[
16
]
, the extension
to non-equilibrium conditions (mostly considering steady states)

[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, the analysis of molecular simulation experiments in a statistical
physical perspective
[
20
,
21
]
.

In this work, we focus on the classical problem of the thermal motion of a
micrometric particle in a generic fluid of arbitrary complex linear rheological
behaviour, that corresponds to the generalization
of the seminal work by Kubo
[
22
,
23
]
, accounting also for the
detailed representation of the fluid-particle interactions
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
This problem is not only crucial in the theory of Brownian motion, as recent
accurate experiments at short time scales have revealed
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
,
but it plays also a fundamental role in the design of microfluidic systems

[
33
,
34
]
, of chemical separation processes
at microscale and of analytical chemical microassays
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. It is therefore of central importance both in theory and in practice.

The Kubo theory of fluctuation-dissipation originated as the
generalization of the
Einstein-Langevin picture, where the Stokesian drag was extended to encompass dissipative memory
effects
[
22
,
23
]
. This has represented a major
advance in statistical
physics, as it has permitted to include in a simple description the class of models
introduced by Zwanzig
[
38
,
39
]
, and referred to as Generalized
Langevin Equations (GLE).
Regarded in the light of a hydrodynamic theory of fluid-particle interactions, the Kubo
extension solve just one half of the problem and probably the more peculiar one.
The experiments on Brownian motion even for Newtonian fluids (such as water or acetone at room
temperature)
[
28
,
29
,
30
]

have clearly shown that fluid inertial effects (referred to,
in fluid dynamics, as
the Basset force and the added mass contribution
[
13
]
) are the main responsible
for the long-term tails in the particle velocity autocorrelation function.
The inclusion of these effects in a generalized fluctuation-dissipation theory
for particle motion in a fluid is the basic prerequisite in order to
predict the outcome of highly resolved Brownian experiments both in space and time,
and to improve the description of fluid-particle
interactions in Stokesian dynamics and suspension theory
[
40
,
41
]

beyond the
the range of applicability of the Stokes regime.

In linear hydrodynamic regimes (Stokes flows, time-dependent Stokes flows
[
24
,
25
]
), the motion of particle of mass
m
is described by the
evolution equation for the particle velocity
v(t)
of the form

m\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt}=F_{f\rightarrow p}[v(t)]+R(t)

(1)

where
{F}_{f\rightarrow p}[v(t)]={\mathcal{L}}[v(t)]
represents
the mean-field hydrodynamic
force exerted by the fluid onto the particle
and is a linear functional of
v(t)
(and of its time derivative),
and
R(t)
the random force. Throughout this work we adopt the notationally simpler
scalar representation for particle dynamics (corresponding to
the description of a spherical Brownian particle in the free space).
The vectorial extension and the
inclusion of the tensorial character of the hydromechanic kernels are addressed in
Section
IX
(in connection with problems in confined
geometries) and in Appendix
A
(as a generalization of the theory).
Moreover, we consider exclusively the translatory motion of a rigid particle,
leaving rotational and roto-translatory effects to a specific and further analysis.

Following Kubo
[
22
,
23
]
, the
fundamental problems in the fluctuation-dissipation theory
of particle motion in a fluid are essentially two:

•

the determination of the velocity autocorrelation
function
C_{vv}(t)=\langle v(t)\,v(0)\rangle_{\rm eq}
;

•

the determination
of the correlation properties of thermal force
R(t)
.

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm eq}
indicates the expected
value with respect to the equilibrium probability measure.

The first problem can be solved in general, invoking the principle
of uncorrelation of the thermal force
R(t)
from
v(0)

for
t\geq 0
,

\langle R(t)\,v(0)\rangle_{\rm eq}=0

(2)

that extends the original intuition by Paul Langevin
[
4
]
.
Eq. (
2
) is referred to by some authors as the
causality
principle

[
42
,
43
]
.
Essentially, it means that the future evolution of
R(t)
, i.e., for
t\geq 0

is completely independent of the state of the velocity variable
at any previous time (
t=0
).
This corresponds to the situation in which
R(t)
is not influenced
by the previous history of the particle velocity, and consequently

v(t)
behaves passively with respect to
R(t)
without altering
its future evolution.
For this reason, eq. (
2
) can be properly indicated as

the principle of fluctuational independence for passive
particles
, or simply as the Langevin condition. Conversely, if eq. (
2
) is not fulfilled,
particle dynamics may have an active influence on the outcome
of the fluctuational force. This issue is addressed in
[
44
]
.

Enforcing eq. (
2
), the equation for the velocity
autocorrelation function
C_{vv}(t)
follows straightforwardly

m\,\frac{dC_{vv}(t)}{dt}={\mathcal{L}}[C_{vv}(t)]

(3)

equipped with the initial condition
C_{v}(0)=\langle v^{2}\rangle_{\rm eq}
,
that represents the basic result of the Linear Response Theory
(LRT for short)
[
23
]
.
Conversely,
the determination of the thermal force
R(t)
and its statistical properties
has been fully addressed
solely in the some particular cases
as discussed below.

In the terminology introduced by Kubo
[
23
]
eq. (
3
) corresponds
to the
Fluctuation-Dissipation relation of the first kind
(FD1k, for short),
while the problem of deriving the correlation
properties of the thermal force
R(t)
is named by
Kubo as the
Fluctuation-Dissipation relation of the second kind
(FD2k, for short).
Moreover, Kubo observed that FD2k is not a simple problem as the separation
of the force into a hydrodynamic,
{\mathcal{L}}[v(t)]
in eq. (
2
),
and a random force
R(t)
is a complex problem in statistical physics (see
[
23
]
p. 37).
This is the reason why other alternatives to FD2k are possible, namely the
description of particle thermal motion via
a comprehensive stochastic hydrodynamic velocity field, encompassing both the effects (hydrodynamic and fluctuational) in a single
entity
[
45
]
.

In the light of the accurate description of particle motion at microscales,
be it associated with Brownian motion theory or with particle dynamics
in a microfluidic device, it is convenient to introduce
a further relation, referred to as the
Fluctuation-Dissipation
relation of the third kind
(FD3k), corresponding to the explicit
representation of the thermal force, consistent with FD1k and FD2k,
in terms of elementary stochastic processes, which represents the
fundamental theoretical tool to infer prediction about Brownian
and microparticle dynamic properties beyond the
analysis of its correlation properties of the second-order, addressing
also microlocal and regularity properties of trajectories
and velocity realizations (see
[
44
]
).

Many developments have been carried out to extend the
classical framework pioneered by Kubo, in order to account for a wide range of dynamic phenomena, such as those observed in non-Markovian, semi-Markovian or
anomalous systems.

The fundamental role of the dissipative memory kernel has been pointed out in the analysis of many biological environments, in which the form of kernel itself is a key feature in the description of the physics. For instance, the integration of semi-Markov models has been effective to describe molecular motors in viscoelastic environments, such as kinesin motion
[
46
,
47
]
, while coarse-grained modeling of non-Markovian dynamics is particularly effective in capturing the dynamics of colloidal suspensions and active matter
[
48
,
49
]
.

Advances in theoretical formulations of GLE have been achieved through the exploration of hydrodynamic memory effects and non-Gaussian diffusive behaviors. For instance, the memory kernel formulation has been revisited to account for geometrical inhomogeneities and non-classical shapes of the particles
[
50
]
. In parallel, the analysis of non-Gaussian diffusion in heterogeneous media has bring the necessity for a unifying framework to combine distinct mechanisms of heterogeneity
[
51
]
.

From a methodological perspective, novel solutions to the GLE have been proposed to compute thermodynamic quantities such as entropy production and work in systems with memory
[
52
]
. These works are complemented by explicit extensions of fluctuation-dissipation theorems for systems experiencing hydrodynamic backflow and confined geometries, which incorporate memory kernels with non-trivial decay behavior
[
47
,
53
,
54
]
.

Since experimental results on Brownian motion further underscored the necessity of dissipative and viscoelastic effects into predictive models
[
55
]
, different studies have investigated both their impact on the fluctuation-dissipation relation
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
, and their interactions with external perturbations
[
59
,
60
]
or anomalous leading effects
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
.

All these references show how in the purely dissipative case the analysis developed by Kubo is completely self-consistent
[
22
,
23
]
and, out of it, it is possible
to provide an explicit representation of
R(t)

[
64
]
(see also
[
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
]
).

Moreover, the
general case of a complex viscoelastic fluid for which
both dissipative and fluid inertial contributions are simultaneously accounted
for has not been considered.
More precisely, Bedeaux and Mazur
[
69
]

proposed a formal solution to this problem in a Newtonian fluid, in terms of the
Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function of
R(t)
, but this solution
does not correspond to any feasible stochastic process. This has been discussed in

[
45
]
, and we arrive to the same conclusion in section
VII
from
a totally different perspective.

The scope of this work, that represents the first part of a more comprehensive
analysis and extension of fluctuation-dissipation relations
(see
[
70
,
44
]
),
is to provide a general and unitary solution of FD3k for particle motion
in a fluid in the presence
of arbitrary linear hydrodynamic regimes and fluid-particle interactions,
both in the free space and in confined systems.
This unitary picture is achieved via the concept of
fluctuational patterns

and their additive algebra that follows directly from the Langevin
condition. This permits further and natural generalizations
of fluctuation-dissipation theory, removing the Langevin condition eq.
(
2
), and addressing the regularity properties of velocity dynamics
in a comprehensive perspective, see
[
44
]
.

In developing the theory we make use of two main tools: (i) the
representation of hydromechanic memory kernels in terms of
exponentially decaying modes, and (ii) moment analysis
of the associated extended particle dynamics.

The representation of the
memory kernel in terms of
exponentially decaying modes
is the standard rheological description of complex fluids in the linear
hydrodynamic regime
[
26
,
27
]
, usually
referred to as the Prony series
expansion
[
71
,
72
]
.
In the statistical mechanical literature it represents the key assumption
for deriving a Markovian embedding out of a memory dynamics.

The extension of this approach to
the fluid inertial contribution has been developed in
[
73
]
.
Moreover, following
[
73
]
, we enforce the physical property
that the fluid inertial kernel is a bounded function of time.
This property stems from the
finite propagation velocity of the shear stresses. In this framework, the singularity of the
Basset kernel at time
t=0
should be regarded as the rather unphysical byproduct
of the purely Newtonian
constitutive equations (instantaneous Newtonian approximation)
[
44
]
, as
the boundedness of this kernel is a mathematical property of
any viscoelastic fluids
[
73
]
. In point of fact,
any fluid, that
for practical hydrodynamic purposes is regarded as Newtonian,
possesses a small but non-vanishing relaxation
time (which is order of 1 ps for water at room temperature
[
74
]
).
Recently, Giona et al.
[
75
]

argued that this approximation might represent the
consequence of the local stationary and linear nature
of fluid-particle interactions (principle of local realizability)
associated with the modes of the hydrodynamic field interacting with the
particle.

Moment analysis is a classical tool in the study of stochastic
processes
[
76
]
. The application of moment analysis
to particle hydromechanics in generic fluid and flow conditions
is particularly cogent as it permits to unveil many subtle
and fundamental issues of fluctuation-dissipation theory: (i) the
complete independence of the internal degrees of freedom at equilibrium,
associated with the detailed Langevin conditions stemming from eq. (
2
),
leading to the concept of fluctuational patterns (Section
VIII
),
(ii) the extension of the theory to the confined case with its peculiarities
as regards the formulation of a thermodynamically consistent mean-field
hydromechanic force. For this reason, moment analysis is
introduced starting from the
simplest problem, i.e. the purely dissipative case of Section
IV

(for which the representation of
R(t)
is well known
[
64
]
),
as a fil-rouge connecting all the cases of increasing
hydrodynamic complexity in a single methodological perspective.

The article is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the
hydromechanic problem, and Section
III

addresses the modal representation of the hydrodynamic kernels.
Section
IV
develops
the FD3k representation in the simplest case of purely dissipative memory without
any fluid inertial effect.
Albeit the results obtained coincide with those derived in
[
64
]
,
the analysis of this simple case permits us to introduce and
describe the approach to FD3k
that is extended to the general case in the subsequent Sections.
Section
V
briefly reviews a simple but important representation issue
associated
with fluid inertial effects, and Section
VI

develops the analysis of the FD3k relation
in the most general hydrodynamic conditions
where both dissipative and fluid inertial
memory effects are considered.
Section
VII
develops an explicit representation
of the stochastic force
R(t)
related to fluid inertial
effects deriving FD2k in this case.
From the expression of the correlation function of the inertial stochastic
perturbation, the regularity issue of the inertial kernel at time

t=0
becomes
analytically evident.
Section
VIII
reframes the results obtained in the previous Sections
in a unitary
respective via the concepts of fluctuational patterns.
Up to
VIII
the analysis is focused on a particle in a free space,
neglecting the constraints deriving from
the presence of solid walls, such as in microfluidic channels

[
33
,
25
]
, and their hydromechanic implications
(position dependent memory kernels).
This extension is provided in Section
IX
that generalizes the
results recently obtained in
[
77
]
to include fluid inertial
effects. The analysis of this
problem puts fluctuation-dissipation theory in a completely
new light, from the sheer description of thermal fluctuations
to the necessary theoretical
tool to infer a thermodynamically consistent
mean-field hydromechanics via the analysis of its response in a thermal
fluctuational field at equilibrium.
The
extension of the theory to vectorial models and to the case of nonisotropic
hydrodynamic interactions, and some numerical examples are
left to the Appendices.

II
Particle hydromechanics

The motion of a spherical Brownian particle of mass
m

at constant temperature
T
in a quiescent fluid,
considered by Einstein and Langevin, can
be described by the simple stochastic equation for the particle velocity
v(t)

m\,dv(t)=-\eta\,v(t)\,dt+\sqrt{2\,k_{B}\,T\,\eta}\,dw(t)

(4)

where
\eta
is the friction factor,
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant and

dw(t)
the increment of a Wiener process in the time interval
(t,t+dt)
.
Introducing the dimensionless quantities

\widetilde{v}=\frac{v}{\sqrt{\langle v^{2}\rangle_{\rm eq}}}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_{B}\,T}}\,v\,,\quad\widetilde{t}=\frac{t}{t_{\rm diss}}=\frac{\eta}{m}\,t

(5)

where
t_{\rm diss}=m/\eta
is the characteristic
dissipation time,
eq. (
4
) attains the nondimensional form

d\widetilde{v}=-\widetilde{v}\,d\widetilde{t}+\sqrt{2}\,dw(\widetilde{t})

(6)

where
dw(\widetilde{t})
, as above, corresponds to the increment
of a Wiener process in the interval
(\widetilde{t},\widetilde{t}+d\widetilde{t})
.
Consequently,
\langle\widetilde{v}^{2}\rangle_{\rm eq}=1
.
Henceforth, we will use this nondimensional formulation unless otherwise stated (as
in Sections
VIII
and
IX
), by replacing, for notational simplicity,
\widetilde{v}
and
\widetilde{t}
with
v
and
t
, thus

\langle v^{2}\rangle_{\rm eq}=1
.

The Einstein-Langevin equation (
4
) stems from the assumption
that the fluid model is Newtonian and it can be described by means of the instantaneous Stokes
equation. The timeless and instantaneous
response of the fluid corresponds
to the occurrence of a
\delta
-correlated white-noise thermal force
R(t)=\sqrt{2\,k_{B}\,T\,\eta}\,\xi(t)
in eq. (
4
),
where
\xi(t)=dw(t)/dt
is the distributional
derivative of a Wiener process,
\langle\xi(t)\,\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. Hydrodynamic interactions modify the structure of eq. (
4
) (or of
eq. (
6
)), determining the occurrence of memory effects in the
formulation of the equation of motion of a Brownian particle. In the latter
case, the
thermal force
R(t)
is no longer
\delta
-correlated, and its stochastic structure
should be determined (representing the essence of the FD2k and FD3k relations).
Three major classes of problems should be considered:

1.

Memory effects in the dissipative term,

\frac{dv}{dt}=-\int_{0}^{t}\,h(t-\tau)\,v(\tau)\,d\tau+R(t)

(7)

with
h(t)\geq 0
,
\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\,dt=1
, such that
the dynamics associated with
h(t)
is dissipatively stable

[
75
]
.
The boundedness of the integral of
h(t)
implies the existence of
a finite non-zero value of the particle diffusivity, i.e. an asymptotic
linear scaling of the particle mean square displacement with time.
This model physically corresponds
to the motion in a non-Newtonian linear viscoelastic fluid, in which the Stokes approximation
for
the force exerted by the fluid on the Brownian particle is assumed.
Eq. (
7
) represents the so called generalized Langevin equation with memory
treated by Zwanzig
[
38
]
and Kubo
[
22
]
.

2.

Memory effects in the fluid inertial contribution,

\frac{dv}{dt}=-v-\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\,\frac{dv(\tau)}{d\tau}\,d\tau+R(t)

(8)

with
k(t)\geq 0
.
Eq. (
8
) corresponds to the classical model of a Brownian particle in
a Newtonian fluid in which the fluid inertia is taken into account, and the
mass considered is the extended mass
[
13
]
.
The force exerted by the fluid on the particle
stems from solution of the time-dependent Stokes equation.

3.

Memory effects both in the dissipative and in the fluid inertial contributions:

\frac{dv}{dt}=-\int_{0}^{t}\,h(t-\tau)\,v(\tau)\,d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\,\frac{dv(\tau)}{d\tau}\,d\tau+R(t)

(9)

with
h(t)>0
, summable and dissipatively stable and
k(t)>0
.

Equation (
9
) can be viewed as the generalization of
eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) for a linear viscoelastic fluid,
and more generally for any isotropic complex fluid in the linear
time-dependent hydrodynamic regime.
This represents the most complete description of Brownian dynamics in
a unconfined fluid phase.

As addressed in
[
45
]
, eqs. (
8
)-(
9
) apply
for
v(t=0)=v(0)=0
. In the general case, they should contain
an impulsive initial condition, so that
eqs. (
8
)-(
9
) are substituted by
the corresponding equations

\frac{dv}{dt}=-v-\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\,\left(\frac{dv(\tau)}{d\tau}+v(0)\,\delta(\tau)\right)\,d\tau+R(t)

(10)

\frac{dv}{dt}=-\int_{0}^{t}\,h(t-\tau)\,v(\tau)\,d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\,\left(\frac{dv(\tau)}{d\tau}+v(0)\,\delta(\tau)\right)\,d\tau+R(t)

(11)

III
Modal representation

Let us consider the case where the memory kernels admit a modal representation
in terms of sums of exponentially decreasing functions of time.
As discussed in the introduction this represents either
the classical rheological formulation
[
26
,
27
]

or the stochastic assumption amenable to a Markovian embedding
of the particle dynamics or the consequence of the principle
of local realizability formulated in
[
75
]
.

In the discrete case, this means that there exist integers
N_{d}
,
and
N_{i}
, and constant
a_{i}>0
,
\lambda_{i}>0
,
\gamma_{\alpha}>0
,
and
\mu_{\alpha}>0
, such that,

h(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}\,,\qquad k(t)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}

(12)

where in principle, either
N_{d}
or
N_{i}
, or both, could be infinite.
Eq. (
12
) represents a model for the memory response (both
dissipative and inertial) characterized by linear relaxation
decays (with timescales
\lambda_{i}^{-1}
and
\mu_{\alpha}^{-1}
, respectively).
There is no direct elementary relation between the
spectrum of rates associated with the fluid inertial contribution
\{\mu_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}

and the spectrum of relaxation rates
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{d}}

describing viscoelastic dissipation.
In order to maintain the distinction between these two mechanisms, we use latin
indexes for the dissipative terms, and greek ones for those
pertaining to the fluid-inertial contribution.

Eq. (
12
) can be extended to the case of a continuous
spectrum of relaxation rates as

h(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}a(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda\,t}\,d\lambda\,,\qquad k(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma(\mu)\,e^{-\mu\,t}\,d\mu

(13)

and therefore
h(t)
and
k(t)
can be viewed as the Laplace transforms
of the relaxational dissipative and inertial spectra
a(\lambda)
,
and
\gamma(\mu)
, respectively.

As regards
h(t)
, the existence of a long-term friction factor
\eta
,
and the normalization considered eq. (
5
), extended to
the memory case, imply that

\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\,dt=1
, and consequently we have in the discrete/continuous case

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{a_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}=1\,,\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a(\lambda)}{\lambda}\,d\lambda=1

(14)

In the continuous case, this implies the asymptotics

a(\lambda)=O(\lambda^{\varepsilon})
,
\varepsilon>0
for
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, and
a(\lambda)=O(\lambda^{-\delta})
,
\delta>0

for
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Observe that eq. (
14
) does not
represent a physical principle, but solely a normalization condition.
The physical constraint underlying eq. (
14
) is that
the quantities
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{a_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}
,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a(\lambda)}{\lambda}\,d\lambda
should be bounded, corresponding to the finiteness of the long-term friction factor.
While
a_{i}>0
,
i=1,\dots,N_{d}
, ensures the dissipative stability of the model,
the condition
\mu_{\alpha}>0
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}
,
are consistent with the structure of the
generalized Basset kernel in a Maxwell fluids
[
73
]
and
are further discussed in Section
V
.

As regards the fluid inertial kernel
k(t)
, we have in the
Newtonian case
k(t)\sim 1/\sqrt{t}
, and therefore
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}k(t)=\infty
. However, as shown in
[
73
]
, it is
a generic property of viscoelastic fluids that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}k(t)=k_{0}<\infty

(15)

and this is physically associated with the bounded velocity
in the propagation of the shear stresses. As a consequence, we have
the general conditions

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}=k_{0}<\infty\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma(\mu)\,d\mu=k_{0}<\infty

(16)

As discussed in
[
70
]
, if
N_{d}
or
N_{i}
go to infinity,
the long-term scaling of
h(t)
or
k(t)
, respectively,
may be different from an exponential behaviour, and e.g. may
attain a power-law scaling, which can describe, within
the hydromechanic formalism, the occurrence of anomalous
diffusive properties.

IV
The dissipative case

In the presence of pure dissipation, the
FD2k relation is subsumed in the Kubo theorem
[
22
,
23
]
,
assessing that at thermal equilibrium, the correlation function
of the stochastic force is proportional to the dissipative
memory kernel, i.e.,
\langle R(t)\,R(0)\rangle\sim h(t)
.
An explicit representation of

in the modal case has been
developed by Goychuk
[

(see also

66

67

In this Section we develop another equivalent approach to this problem,
in order to introduce
the moment formalism will be used in the

Consider eqs. (

,

\frac{dv}{dt}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*v(t)+R(t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*\left(v(t)+r_{i}(t)\right)

indicates convolution.
In eq. (

amongst the dissipative modes, introducing the modal fluctuation

Setting

\theta_{i}(t)=e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*\left((v(t)+r_{i}(t)\right)

(18)

eq. (

differential equations

\displaystyle\frac{dv(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,\theta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{i}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{i}\,\theta_{i}(t)+v(t)+r_{i}(t)

(19)

For

, assume a Wiener representation, i.e.

r_{i}(t)=\sqrt{2}b_{i}\,\xi_{i}(t)\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,N_{d}

(20)

where
\xi_{i}(t)
are the
distributional derivatives of mutually
independent Wiever processes, thus
\langle\xi_{i}(t)\,\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{i,j}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
and the coefficients
b_{i}
should be determined from the Langevin condition
eq. (
2
) that, expressed in terms of the mutually independent
modal fluctuations

, takes the form

\langle r_{i}(t)\,v(0)\rangle_{\rm eq}=0\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,N_{d}

(21)

The normalized velocity autocorrelation function
C_{vv}(t)=\langle v(t)\,v(0)\rangle_{\rm eq}
is
the solution of the equation

\frac{dC_{vv}(t)}{dt}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*C_{vv}(t)\,,\qquad C_{vv}(0)=1

(22)

Indicating with

C_{\theta_{i}v}(t)=\langle\theta_{i}(t)\,v(0)\rangle_{\rm eq}
the correlation
function of
\theta_{i}(t)
with
v(0)
at equilibrium,
it follows from eqs. (
19
) and (
21
) that

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{vv}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,C_{\theta_{i}v}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{\theta_{i}v}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{i}\,C_{\theta_{i}v}(t)+C_{vv}(t)

(23)

The latter system of equations
admits the solution
C_{\theta_{i}v}(t)=C_{\theta_{i}v}(0)\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}+e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*C_{vv}(t)
, and thus

\frac{dC_{vv}(t)}{dt}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}*C_{vv}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{i}\,C_{\theta_{i}v}(0)\,e^{-\lambda_{i}\,t}

(24)

Eq. (
24
) coincides with eq. (
22
) provided that

C_{\theta_{i}v}(0)=\langle\theta_{i}\,v\rangle_{\rm eq}=0\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,N_{d}

(25)

and
C_{vv}(0)=1
, where
\langle\theta_{i}\,v\rangle_{\rm eq}
represents the
second-order mixed moment of
\theta_{i}
and
v
at equilibrium.
The expansion coefficients
b_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N_{d}
can be
thus determined imposing the
N_{d}
-relations eq. (
25
).

To this purpose, consider the Fokker-Planck equation
for the density
p(v,\bm{\theta},t)
,
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1}\,\dots,\theta_{N_{d}})
associated with eqs. (
19
)-(
20
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,\theta_{h}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial v}+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{h}}\left(\lambda_{h}\,\theta_{h}\,p\right)-v\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{h}}+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}b_{h}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\theta_{h}^{2}}

(26)

from which, the dynamics of the second-order moments,

m_{vv}(t)=\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle\,,\quad m_{\theta_{i}v}=\langle\theta_{i}(t)v(t)\rangle\,,\quad m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}=\langle\theta_{i}(t)\theta_{j}(t)\rangle

follows

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{vv}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,m_{\theta_{h}v}

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{\theta_{i}v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{h}}-\lambda_{i}\,m_{\theta_{i}v}+m_{vv}

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})\,m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}+m_{\theta_{i}v}+m_{\theta_{j}v}+2\,b_{i}^{2}\,\delta_{ij}

where
\delta_{ij}
are the Kronecker symbols.
Indicating with
m_{vv}^{*}=\langle v^{2}\rangle_{\rm eq}
the
equilibrium (steady-state) value of
m_{vv}
, and similarly for the other moments,
and imposing the conditions
eq. (
25
), from the third equation (
27
)
one obtains

m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}^{*}=\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}\,\delta_{ij}

(28)

that substituted into the second equation (
27
), for

,
provides

, i.e.,

b_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{i}}{a_{i}}}\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,N_{d}

(29)

defining the thermal fluctuations, thus solving FD3k.

In the continuous case, associated with the spectrum
a(\lambda)

of dissipation rates, a continuous spectrum
of internal degrees of freedom
\theta_{\lambda}(t)
,
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
,
can be defined,
and each
\theta_{\lambda}(t)
satisfies the equation

\frac{d\theta_{\lambda}(t)}{dt}=-\lambda\,\theta_{\lambda}(t)+v(t)+\sqrt{2}\,b(\lambda)\,\xi_{\lambda}(t)

with
\xi_{\lambda}(t)\,\xi_{\lambda^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
This leads to the continuous analogue of eq.
(

), namely

b(\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{a(\lambda)}}

(31)

The overall stochastic thermal force is thus given by

R(t)=\sqrt{2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}a(\lambda)\,b(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda\,t}*\xi_{\lambda}(t)\,d\lambda

(32)

and this result is consistent with Kubo theory since

\langle R(t)\,R(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{a(\lambda)\,\lambda}\,d\lambda\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda\,(t-\tau)}\,d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{a(\lambda^{\prime})\,\lambda^{\prime}}\,d\lambda^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}e^{-\lambda^{\prime}\,(t^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}\langle\xi_{\lambda}(\tau)\,\xi_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle\,d\tau^{\prime}

(33)

Setting

without loss of generality, it follows
from eq. (

) after elementary manipulations

\langle R(t)\,R(t^{\prime})\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}a(\lambda)\,e^{-\lambda(t-t^{\prime})}\,d\lambda+\rho(t,t^{\prime})

(34)

where
\rho(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow 0
for

,
consistently with the Kubo result
\langle R(t)\,R(t^{\prime})\rangle=h(|t-t^{\prime}|)
. Numerical examples are discussed in the Appendix
B
.

V
Inertial effects: initial conditions and representations

The equations of motion for a particle immersed in a fluid in which fluid inertial
effects are considered in the limit of negligible Reynolds number
can be derived using integral transforms (Laplace transform)
to express the force
F(t)=F_{f\rightarrow p}[v(t)]

exerted by the fluid on the particle
[
24
]
.
In the case of a rigid
spherical particle of mass
m
and radius
R_{p}
immersed in a Newtonian fluid in
the time-dependent
Stokes regime, one obtains for the Laplace transform
\widehat{F}(s)

of
F(t)
the expression

-\widehat{F}(s)=\eta\,\widehat{v}(s)+\widehat{k}(s)\,s\,\widehat{v}(s)+m_{a}s\,\widehat{v}(s)

(35)

where

\eta=6\,\pi\,\mu\,R_{p}
,
\mu
being the fluid viscosity,
\widehat{k}(s)=6\,\pi\,\sqrt{\rho\,\mu}\,R_{p}^{2}/\sqrt{s}
, where
\rho
is the fluid density, and
m_{a}=2\pi R_{p}^{3}\rho/3
the added mass.
Transforming eq. (
35
) in time domain, and inserting it into
the equation of motion of the particle,
one obtains

(m+m_{a})\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt}=-\eta\,v(t)-k(t)*\left(\frac{dv(t)}{dt}+v(0)\,\delta(t)\right)

(36)

equipped with the initial condition
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}v(t)=m\,v(0)/(m+m_{a})
,
where
k(t)=6\,\sqrt{\pi\,\rho\,\mu}\,R_{p}^{2}/\sqrt{t}
.
The presence of the term
v(0)\,\delta(t)
in the fluid inertial contribution is fundamental in order to
provide a correct representation of the LRT.

Next, consider the more
general nondimensional equation (
11
). It is convenient
to rewrite this equation in a slightly different way, making use
of the result derived in
[
73
]
, and expressed by
eq. (
15
), indicating that, whenever viscoelastic effects
are considered, the value
k(0)
exists and is finite.
Integrating by parts, the fluid inertial contribution entering
eq. (
11
) can be
expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\left(\frac{dv(\tau)}{d\tau}+v(0)\,\delta(\tau)\right)\,d\tau\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\left(k(t-\tau)\,v(\tau)\right)\,d\tau

(37)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial k(t-\tau)}{\partial\tau}\,v(\tau)\,d\tau+k(t)\,v(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(0)\,v(t)-k(t)\,v(0)-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial k(t-\tau)}{\partial\tau}\,v(\tau)\,d\tau+k(t)\,v(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(0)\,v(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial k(t-\tau)}{\partial\tau}\,v(\tau)\,d\tau

This expression will be used in the next section.

VI
Dissipative/inertial memory effects

Consider particle dynamics in which both the dissipative term and the
fluid inertial contribution
account for memory effects. Enforcing the representation developed in the
previous section eq. (
37
), and the modal
decomposition of the kernels
h(t)
,
k(t)
eq. (
12
), we have in
nondimensional form

\displaystyle\frac{dv(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*v(t)-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*\left(\frac{dv(t)}{dt}+v(0)\,\delta(t)\right)+R(t)

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,v(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*v(t)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*v(t)+R(t)

As stated in section
III
, for the sake of notational clarity, we
keep latin indexes for referring to variables
associated with the dissipative modes, and greek ones for the
inertial modes.
Consider the following internal variables

\theta_{h}(t)=e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*v(t)\,\quad h=1,\dots,N_{d}\,,\qquad z_{\alpha}(t)=e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*v(t)\,\quad\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}

(39)

and assume that the stochastic forcing
R(t)
can be decomposed
into independent contributions
\xi_{i}(t)
,
\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)
acting on
the internal degrees of freedom
\theta_{h}(t)
,
\zeta_{\alpha}(t)
, respectively,
and
expressed as distributional derivatives of independent Wiener processes,

\langle\xi_{h}(t)\,\xi_{k}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{hk}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,,\quad\langle\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\,\xi_{\beta}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,,\quad\langle\xi_{h}(t)\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})\rangle=0

(40)

Due to the representation of the inertial effects
involving the presence of two terms, the first one,

-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,v(t)
directly proportional
to the velocity, and the other one,
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*v(t)

of convolutional nature,
a stochastic contribution

\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)

has been added into the evolution equation for the velocity, where
the expansion coefficients
d_{\alpha}
need to be determined.
Therefore, eq. (
38
) is recast into a a system of
N_{d}+N_{i}+1

differential equations,

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\,v-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,\theta_{h}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,z_{\alpha}+\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{h}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{h}\,\theta_{h}+v+\sqrt{2}\,b_{h}\,\xi_{h}(t)

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{dz_{\alpha}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu_{\alpha}\,z_{\alpha}+v+\sqrt{2}\,c_{\alpha}\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)

where the
(2N_{i}+N_{d})
coefficients,
b_{h}

h=1,\dots,N_{d}
,
c_{\alpha}
,
d_{\alpha}

\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}
should
be determined by imposing the conditions

\langle\theta_{h}\,v\rangle_{\rm eq}=0\,,\;\;h=1,\dots,N_{d}\,,\quad\langle z_{\alpha}\,v\rangle_{\rm eq}=0\,,\;\;\alpha=1,\dots,N_{\alpha}

(42)

The Fokker-Planck equation associated with eq. (
41
) is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(v\,p\right)+\left(\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\theta_{h}\right)\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial v}-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,z_{\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial p}{\partial v}+\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}^{2}\right)\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial v^{2}}

(43)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\lambda_{h}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{h}}\left(\theta_{h}\,p\right)-v\,\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{h}}+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}b_{h}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\theta_{h}^{2}}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\mu_{\alpha}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{\alpha}}\left(z_{\alpha}\,p\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle v\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial z_{\alpha}}+2\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial v\partial z_{\alpha}}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}c_{\alpha}^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial z_{\alpha}^{2}}

from which the evolution equations for the second-order moments follow

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{vv}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\,\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\beta}\right)\,m_{vv}-2\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}a_{h}\,m_{\theta_{h}v}+2\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,m_{z_{\alpha}v}+2\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\beta}\right)\,m_{\theta_{h}v}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}a_{k}m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,m_{\theta_{h}z_{\alpha}}-\lambda_{h}m_{\theta_{h}v}+m_{vv}

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{z_{\alpha}v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\beta}\right)\,m_{z_{\alpha}v}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{i}}a_{k}\,m_{\theta_{k}z_{\alpha}}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\beta}\,\mu_{\beta}\,m_{z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}}-\mu_{\alpha}\,m_{z_{\alpha}v}+m_{vv}+2\,d_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\lambda_{h}+\lambda_{k})\,m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}+m_{\theta_{h}v}+m_{\theta_{k}v}+2\,b_{h}^{2}\,\delta_{h,k}

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}z_{\alpha}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{h}\,m_{\theta_{h}z_{\alpha}}+m_{z_{\alpha}v}-\mu_{\alpha}\,m_{\theta_{h}z_{\alpha}}+m_{\theta_{h}v}

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\mu_{\alpha}+\mu_{\beta})\,m_{z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}}+m_{z_{\alpha}v}+m_{z_{\beta}v}+2\,c_{\alpha}^{2}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}

The fluctuation-dissipation representation of the stochastic
contribution can be derived imposing eqs. (
42
),
which imply for the steady-state values of the mixed
moments the conditions

m_{\theta_{h}v}^{*}=0\,,\quad h=1,\dots,N_{d}\,,\qquad m_{z_{\alpha}v}^{*}=0\,,\quad\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}

(45)

and
m_{vv}^{*}=1
.
Inserting these conditions into the systems of evolution equation for the second-order moments,
one obtains at steady state the following relations: (i)
from the first equation (
44
)

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d^{2}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}

(46)

(ii) from the fourth equation (
44
)

m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}^{*}=\frac{b_{h}^{2}}{\lambda_{h}}\,\delta_{hk}

(47)

(iii) from the sixth equation (
44
)

m_{z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}}^{*}=\frac{c^{2}_{\alpha}}{\mu_{\alpha}}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(48)

Moreover, (iv) from the fifth equation (
44
) we have

m_{\theta_{h}z_{\alpha}}^{*}=0

(49)

Enforcing the relations (
46
)-(
49
) into the second equation (
44
) the coefficients
b_{h}
are determined

b_{h}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{h}}{a_{h}}

(50)

The third equation (
44
) provides

\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}m_{z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}}^{*}+1+2\,d_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}=0

(51)

which, due to eq. (
48
), simplifies as

\gamma_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}^{2}+1+2\,d_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}=0

(52)

Eq. (
46
) is certainly satisfied if

are related
to
\gamma_{\alpha}
in the form

d_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}

(53)

which, inserted into eq. (

), provides

\gamma_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}^{2}+1+2\,\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\,c_{\alpha}=0

(54)

admitting the solution

. Therefore,
the whole system of coefficients accounting
for the stochastic fluctuations is given by

b_{h}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{h}}{a_{h}}}\,,\quad h=1,\dots,N_{d}\,,\qquad d_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\,,\quad c_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}}\,,\quad\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}

and this represents a general solution for the representation of stochastic fluctuations, i.e. for the FD3k problem,
in the presence of both dissipative and inertial memory effects.
Observe that the value of the coefficients

associated
with the dissipative degrees of freedom concides with eq. (
29
), i.e.
it is completely independent of the presence of the fluid inertial term.
Numerical cases studies are addressed in the Appendix B.

A final remark concerns the uniqueness of eq. (
53
) starting
from eq. (
46
)
and the positivity of the
\gamma_{\alpha}
’s.
Consider eq. (
52
) for

, with

still to be determined.
It admits the solutions

and

is real-valued provided that

.
If for some
\alpha
,
\gamma_{\alpha}<0
, there is no solution of the FD3k problem
associated with the dynamic scheme eq. (
38
).
If all the
\gamma_{\alpha}>0
, any choice
d_{\alpha}^{2}>\gamma_{\alpha}

violates eq. (
46
). Therefore the condition eq. (
53
)
is not only sufficient but also necessary, modulo
the fact that
d_{\alpha}
and
c_{\alpha}
can flip sign,
i.e.
d_{\alpha}=-\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}
,

c_{\alpha}=1/\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}
, defining
a stochastic dynamics fully equivalent to eq. (
55
).

VII
Representation of the stochastic force
R(t)

Consider the general setting eq. (
11
) with the kernels given by eq. (
12
),
and the representation of the dynamics via the internal degrees of freedom

\theta_{h}(t)
and
z_{\alpha}(t)
as developed in section
VI

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\theta_{h}(t)=e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*v(t)+\sqrt{2}\,b_{h}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*\xi_{h}(t)\\

(57)

From eq. (
41
) the random force
R(t)
assumes the following representation

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\,a_{h}\,b_{h}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*\xi_{h}(t)}_{\small dissipative\;fluctuations}

(58)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}d_{\alpha}\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)}_{\small fluid\;inertial\;fluctuations}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{d}(t)+R_{i}(t)

Substituting the values derived for
the expansion coefficients eq. (
55
), these two contributions
take the form

\displaystyle R_{d}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\sqrt{a_{h}\,\lambda_{h}}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*\xi_{h}(t)

\displaystyle R_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}}\left(-\mu_{\alpha}e^{-\mu_{\alpha}t}*\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)+\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\right)

(59)

where
\langle R_{d}(t)\rangle=\langle R_{i}(t)\rangle=0
.
By definition of the stochastic forcings

\{\xi_{h}(t)\}_{h=1}^{N_{d}}
and
\{\xi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}
,
R_{d}(t)
and
R_{i}(t)
are independent of each other, and therefore

\langle R_{d}(t)\,R_{i}(0)\rangle=\langle R_{i}(t)\,R_{d}(0)\rangle=0

(60)

for any

, and
\langle R_{d}(t)R_{d}(0)\rangle=h(t)
, as previously derived, consistently with the
Kubo theory.
In order to determine the autocorrelation function of
R_{i}(t)
, consider the
process
\xi(t)
(i.e., the distributional derivative of a Wiener
process), its first-order mollification
y(t)=e^{-\mu\,t}*\xi(t)
,
\mu>0

and the elementary properties

\displaystyle\langle\xi(t)\,\xi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle\langle y(t)\,y(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\mu\,t}}{2\,\mu}

\displaystyle\langle\xi(t)\,y(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(61)

\displaystyle\langle y(t)\,\xi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\mu\,t}

Since
R_{i}(t)
is the superposition of such processes for different values of the
exponent
\mu=\mu_{\alpha}
, and these processes,
i.e.
y_{\alpha}^{\prime}=e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)
,
are independent of each other, it follows that

\displaystyle\langle R_{i}(t)\,R_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle 2\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{i}}\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha}\,\gamma_{\beta}}\,\left[-\mu_{\alpha}\,y_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)+\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\right]\,\left[-\mu_{\beta}\,y_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)+\xi_{\beta}^{\prime}(t)\right]\right\rangle

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\left[\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\,\langle y_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\,y_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)\rangle-\mu_{\alpha}\,\langle y_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)\rangle-\mu_{\alpha}\,\langle\xi_{\alpha}(t)\,y_{\alpha}(0)\rangle+\langle\xi_{\alpha}(t)\,\xi_{\alpha}(0)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\left[\frac{\mu_{\alpha}}{2}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}-\mu_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}+\delta(t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\mu_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}+2\,\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\gamma_{\alpha}\,\delta(t)

The latter result can be expressed in a meaningful way with respect to the fluid inertial
kernel
k(t)
as

\langle R_{i}(t)\,R_{i}(0)\rangle=\frac{dk(t)}{dt}+2\,k(0)\,\delta(t)

(63)

that provides a compact representation of this autocorrelation function and
the solution of FD2k as regards fluid inertial effects.
Including also the dissipative effects,
we arrive to the general formulation of FD2k in particle hydromechanics

\langle R(t)\,R(0)\rangle=h(t)+2\,k(0)\,\delta(t)+\frac{dk(t)}{dt}

(64)

Observe the importance of the condition eq. (
15
), i.e.
k(0)<\infty
,
otherwise the second term at the r.h.s of eqs. (
63
) and (
64
)
would be meaningless,
consistently with the analysis
developed in
[
73
]
.

As
k(0)>0
, fluid inertial effects induce necessarily the
presence of a white-noise contribution, the term
2\,k(0)\,\delta(t)

at the r.h.s. of eq. (
64
) in the autocorrelation
function of
R(t)
. This term plays an important role
as regards the regularity properties (and specifically
the Hölder continuity) of the velocity fluctuations
as extensively addressed in
[
44
]
.

It follows from the structure of
R_{d}(t)
and
R_{i}(t)
, that the
fluctuations acting on a particle due to the fluid hydrodynamics involve two classes of
elementary stochastic processes:

y^{(d)}(t)=\sqrt{\lambda}\,e^{-\lambda\,t}*\xi(t)

(65)

associated with the dissipative memory contributions, and

y^{(i)}(t)=\xi^{\prime}(t)-\mu\,e^{-\mu\,t}*\xi^{\prime}(t)

(66)

associated with fluid inertial effects. Let
v^{(d)}(t)
, and
v^{(i)}(t)
be the
resultant velocities determined by the sole action of these processes, respectively,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{dv^{(d)}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e^{-\lambda\,t}*v^{(d)}(t)+y^{(d)}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{dv^{(i)}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v^{(i)}(t)+\mu\,e^{-\mu\,t}*v^{(i)}(t)+y^{(i)}(t)

(67)

assuming
v^{(d)}(0)=v^{(i)}(0)=0
. Figure

depicts a realization of these
processes:
panel (a) for the dissipative
and panel (b) for the fluid inertial case.
Due to the singularity of
y^{(i)}(t)
, containing a contribution equal to
the distributional derivative of a Wiener process, the integral process
Y^{(i)}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}y^{(i)}(\tau)\,d\tau
is depicted instead of
y^{(i)}(t)
.

VIII
Fluctuational patterns

The modal representations of FD3k obtained in the previous paragraphs requires further
analysis. To begin with, it clearly indicates the conceptual and thermodynamic
difference of dissipative and fluid inertial contributions that
induce structurally different
representations of the action of the thermal fluctuations. Moreover,
the modal representation of the memory kernels eq. (
12

the elementary stochastic forcings acting on each mode.
This suggest the definition of a hierachy of basic fluctuational patterns of increasing
hydrodynamic complexity.
Owing to the linearity of the interactions, and to the
independence of the stochastic forcings acting on each internal
variable, these patterns are susceptible of composition rules, i.e. of their
own pattern algebra.

VIII.1
Comment on the uniqueness of the representation

A final comment involves the uniqueness of the FD3k modal representation.
To address this issue, consider the case of purely dissipative memory,
adopting the nondimensional formulation used in Sections
IV
-
VI

in the case
the kernel
h(t)
is expressed in the form

h(t)=\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\alpha_{h}\left(e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}\,t}\right)_{hk}\beta_{k}

(78)

where
{\bm{\Lambda}}
is a
N_{d}\times N_{d}
positive definite matrix,
and
\alpha_{h}
,
\beta_{k}
are such that
h(t)>0
, for any
t>0
.
Of course, the kernel
h(t)
expressed by eq. (
78
) can be also
expressed in the form eq. (
12
), where
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N_{d}
are the
eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Lambda}}
, but the aim of the present analysis is to
enforce the representation eq. (
78
) in the derivation of the FD3k modal
representation.
Eq. (
78
) can be compactly rewritten as

h(t)=\left({\bm{\alpha}},e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}\,t}\,{\bm{\beta}}\right)

(79)

where
({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}})=\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\alpha_{h}\,\beta_{h}
.
Within this setting, it is natural
to express the internal dissipative degrees of freedom
{\bm{\theta}}(t)

in the form

{\bm{\theta}}(t)=e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}\,t}*{\bm{\beta}}\,v(t)

(80)

or, componentwise

\theta_{h}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\left(e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}\,t}\right)_{h,k}\,\beta_{k}\,v(t)

(81)

leading to the dynamic representation of the FD3k relation

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\alpha_{h}\,\theta_{h}

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{h}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\Lambda_{hk}\,\theta_{k}+\beta_{h}\,v+\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\sigma_{hk}\,\xi_{k}(t)

where
\sigma_{hk}
are the entries of a
N_{d}\times N_{d}
matrix

{\bm{\sigma}}
to be determined.
The associated Fokker-Planck equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial v}+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{h}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\Lambda_{hk}\,\theta_{k}\,p\right)

(83)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\beta_{h}\,v\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{h}}+\sum_{h,k=1}^{N_{d}}D_{hk}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\theta_{h}\partial\theta_{k}}

where
D_{hk}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}\sigma_{hj}\,\sigma_{kj}
.
From moment analysis at steady steate, enforcing the condition
m_{vv}^{*}=1
,
m_{\theta_{h}v}^{*}=0
,
one obtains

2D_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{d}}\left(\Lambda_{ik}\,m_{\theta_{j}\theta_{k}}^{*}+\Lambda_{jk}m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{k}}^{*}\right)

(84)

and

\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}\alpha_{h}\,m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{h}}^{*}=\beta_{i}

(85)

The latter equation is satisfied by

m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{h}}^{*}=\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\,\delta_{ih}

(86)

Inserting this expression into eq. (
84
) the expression for

D_{ij}
follows

D_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Lambda_{ij}\,\frac{\beta_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}+\Lambda_{ji}\,\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)

(87)

which is a symmetric and positive definite matrix
{\bf D}
. Consequently, there exists
a unique symmetric and positive definite matrix
{\bm{\sigma}}
, such that

{\bm{\sigma}}={\bf D}^{1/2}
.

The principal conclusions that can be drawn from this example are the following:
(i) the explicit representation of the modal FD3k relation is not unique,
as in the present case
we could have used the simpler form derived in section
IV
, in which each mode
is characterized by an independent fluctuational forcing,
nevertheless (ii) the set of all these equivalent FD3k representations possesses analogous
structural properties in the meaning of the block-diagrammatic structure depicted in figure

2
panel (b), and corresponding to
a well defined fluctuational pattern, in this case the
{\mathbb{D}}
-pattern,
where
\{\xi_{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{N_{d}}

interact with each dissipative degree of freedom
\theta_{h}(t)
,

h=1,\dots,N_{d}

Moreover, independently of
the fine modal representation of the fluctuational forcing, the dissipative internal degrees of
freedom are at steady state (equilibrium) uncorrelated from each other, as it follows
from the diagonal structure of the moments
m_{\theta_{i}\theta_{h}}^{*}
at equilibrium, eq. (
86
).

To conclude, the fluctuational patterns considered above refer to
a diagonal representation of the internal degrees of freedom.
Non-diagonal internal dynamics can be introduced without
problem, still representing a fully equivalent model,
without modifying the structural properties of the fluctuational patterns.

IX
Fluctuation-dissipation theory in confined systems

The extension of fluctuation-dissipation theory from the free-space to
confined systems, such as microchannels and, more generally, any fluidic
device for which its characteristic size is comparable with that
of the diffusing particles, requires particular care and attention,
as it poses new challenging problems that are not encountered in the
free-space case.
The main hydromechanic effect induced by confinement is that the
fluid-particle interactions depend on the particle location
in the proximity of the device walls, and consequently they become
function of the particle position
[
33
,
25
]
.

This poses a conceptual challenge in expressing the particle equation
of motion, even in the case the thermal fluctuations are overlooked,
whenever memory effects, be them associated with fluid-inertial interactions
or with the non-Newtonian rheology of the medium, are accounted for.
This stems from the intrinsic ambiguity in the functional
form of the force exerted by the fluid on the
particle that can be derived from the hydrodynamic analysis
of the problem.

For the sake of physical clearness, in this section we use
dimensional units and vector-valued quantities for the particle
velocity.

Indeed, in the case of the Stokes regime, the fluctuation-dissipation
patterns derived in the free-space can be straingthforwardly
extended to the confined case. If
\bm{\eta}({\bf x})
is
the friction tensor function of particle position
{\bf x}
,
the fluctuational pattern is

{\mathbb{S}}_{m,E}^{(3,1,0)}(\bm{\xi}(t))
with

{\bf a}({\bf x})=\sqrt{k_{B}\,T}\bm{\eta}^{1/2}({\bf x})
,
where the symmetric square-root matrix
\bm{\eta}^{1/2}({\bf x})

is uniquely defined since
\bm{\eta}({\bf x})
is symmetric and
positive definite
[
33
,
25
]
.
This corresponds to the equation of motion

m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\bm{\eta}({\bf x}(t))\,{\bf v}(t)+\sqrt{2\,k_{B}\,T}\,\bm{\eta}^{1/2}({\bf x}(t))\,\bm{\xi}(t)

(88)

where
\bm{\xi}(t)
is the distributional
derivative of a three-dimensional Wiener process, and eq. (
88
)
is coupled with the kinematic equation

\frac{d{\bf x}(t)}{dt}={\bf v}(t)

(89)

In order to understand the hydromechanic complexity of the extension of
this results in the presence of memory interactions,
consider the case of a rigid particle of mass
m
moving with
velocity
{\bf v}(t)
and position vector
{\bf x}(t)

in a fluid device defined by the spatial domain
\Omega
, with a non-moving
solid boundary
S_{w}
. Let
S_{p}({\bf x}(t))
be the external surface
of the particle, the spatial location of which depends on the
particle position
{\bf x}(t)
, and assume, without loss
of generality, no slip boundary conditions both on
S_{w}
and
S_{p}
.
As regards fluid hydrodynamics and rheological properties,
consider the incompressible time-dependent Stokes equation, and
Newtonian constitutive equations (viscosity
\mu
, fluid density
\rho
).
In order to avoid confusion, since
{\bf x}(t)
refers to the particle
position, the running spatial variables for the
hydrodynamic fields in
\Omega
will be indicated with
{\bf y}
,
and correspondingly the differential operators will refer to this variable.
Thus, the fluid velocity field
{\bf u}({\bf y},t)
is
the solution of the moving-boundary problem

\displaystyle\rho\,\frac{\partial{\bf u}({\bf y},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\,\nabla^{2}{\bf u}({\bf y},t)-\nabla p({\bf y},t)=-\nabla\cdot{\bf T}({\bf y},t)\,,\quad\nabla\cdot{\bf u}({\bf y},t)=0\,,\quad{\bf y}\in\Omega

\displaystyle{\bf u}({\bf y},0)|_{{\bf y}\in\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,\quad{\bf u}({\bf y},t)|_{{\bf y}\in S_{w}}=0\,,\quad{\bf u}({\bf y},t)|_{{\bf y}\in S_{p}({\bf x}(t))}={\bf v}(t)

(90)

where
{\bf T}({\bf y},t)
is the overall stress tensor accounting both for
pressure ans shear stress contributions.
The hydromechanics of the problem is defined by the force exerted by the
fluid onto the particle

{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}(t)]=-\int_{S_{p}({\bf x}(t))}{\bf T}({\bf y},t)\cdot{\bf n}_{e}({\bf y},t)\,dS({\bf y})

(91)

where
{\bf n}_{e}({\bf y},t)
is the normal, outwardly oriented, unit vector
to
S_{p}({\bf x}(t))
at the point
{\bf y}\in S_{p}({\bf x}(t))
.
Observe that eq. (
90
) defines a moving-boundary problem due to
explicit dependence of the boundary conditions on the particle position
within
\Omega
, and because of it, also the force defined by eq. (
91
)
depends on
{\bf x}(t)
.

While this moving boundary problem can be suitably approached via
numerical simulations, the analytic determination of the
mean-field hydromechanic force eq. (
91
) shows intrinsic
criticalities. Owing to the linearity of eqs. (
90
)-(
91
),
the hydrodynamic problem can be solved in the Laplace domain at any fixed
value of
{\bf x}
, i.e. taking the particle
position as a parameter. Letting
\widehat{\bf v}(s)=L[{\bf v}(t)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s\,t}\,{\bf v}(t)\,dt
be the Laplace transform
of the particle velocity, and

\widehat{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\widehat{\bf v}}(s);{\bf x}]=-\int_{S_{p}({\bf x})}\widehat{\bf T}({\bf y},s)\cdot{\bf n}_{e}({\bf y})\,dS({\bf y})

(92)

the Laplace transform of the force on the particle at the fixed
location
{\bf x}
, the latter quantity is amenable to
analytical calculation, obtaining by linearity

\widehat{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[\widehat{\bf v}(s);{\bf x}]=-\widehat{\bf K}(s;{\bf x})\,\widehat{\bf v}(s)

(93)

But, even if a closed and exact expression for
\widehat{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[\widehat{\bf v}(s);{\bf x}]
, or for its
inverse Laplace transform in the time domain would be available,
and a variety of semianalytical models are available in the literature
[
78
,
79
,
80
]
, this
analytical result
would not solve the hydromechanic problem as regards the
determination of
{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}(t)]
in
eq. (
91
), just because the latter is a functional of the particle positional
history
\{{\bf x}(\tau)\}_{\tau=0}^{t}
from
\tau=0
to
\tau=t
,
evolving according to eq.
(
89
) while in eq.
(
93
)
{\bf x}
is a fixed parameter.

This problem has been analyzed in
[
77
]
in the case of purely
dissipative viscoelastic kernels, i.e. neglecting fluid-inertial
contributions. In this case, for fixed
{\bf x}
, we have

\widehat{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\widehat{\bf v}}(s);{\bf x}]=-\widehat{\bf h}(s;{\bf x})\,\widehat{\bf v}(s)\quad\Rightarrow{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}]=-\int_{0}^{t}{\bf h}(t-\tau;{\bf x})\,{\bf v}(\tau)\,d\tau

(94)

It has been shown in
[
77
]
that fluctuation-dissipation
theory, i.e. the analysis of the hydromechanic problem
in the presence of thermal fluctuations, provides a way
for obtaining thermodynamically
consistent expression for the functional form of the
mean-field force acting onto the particle

{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}(t)]
, and the
explicit representation of the stochastic thermal force
{\bf R}(t)
.

The fluctuation-dissipation theory developed in
[
77
]

enforces two basic representation principles: (i)
the principle of local realizability, corresponding to
the series expansion in exponentially decaying modes,
introduced in
[
75
]
and discussed in the introduction and in
Section
III
, and (ii) the principle
of local consistency.
Local realizability implies that the kernel
{\bf h}(t;{\bf x})

for fixed
{\bf x}
, can be represented as

{\bf h}(t;{\bf x})=\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}{\bf A}_{h}({\bf x})\,\lambda_{h}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}

(95)

where
{\bf A}_{h}({\bf x})
are symmetric, positive definite matrices
function of
{\bf x}
, while the relaxation rates are assumed to be position independent.
The principle of local consistency implies that the
correct functional form for the
force functional
{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}(t)]
if
restricted to the case where
{\bf x}(t)={\bf x}^{*}=\mbox{const.}
,
should coincide with
{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}^{*}]

(in the present case defined by eq. (
94
)), corresponding to
a fluctuation-dissipation problem analogous to those defined
in the free-space, for which the expression for the associated
effective thermal force
{\bf R}(t,{\bf x}^{*})
can be derived
using the fluctuational patterns addressed above (see also Appendix
A
).
Essentially, the local consistency principle, restricts the
class of admissible expressions for
{\bf F}_{f\rightarrow p}[{\bf v}(t);{\bf x}(t)]
but does not solve the problem.

The solution of the problem can be obtained by enforcing thermodynamic
principles, namely the property that the marginal density function
for particle position in a closed and bounded domain
\Omega
, and in the
absence of
external forces and perturbations, should be uniform at equilibrium.
For the sake of exactness, this marginal density should be uniform in the
subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
defined by accounting for the
possible sterical constraints associated with the finite size of the particles.
A stronger form of the
equilibrium principle states that the uniformity condition
at equilibrium holds for all the auxiliary variables
introduced in the local representation of the
memory dynamics.

The following result has been obtained in
[
77
]
.

Theorem I
- For the confined hydromechanic problem at
constant temperature
T
defined
by the position dependent memory kernel
{\bf h}(t;{\bf x})
at constant

{\bf x}
eq. (
95
),
the only thermodynamically consistent representation of the
particle dynamics, providing uniform equilibrium marginal position
density in a closed and bounded domain, and uniform statistical
properties for the auxiliary memory varables is given
by

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf x}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf v}(t)

\displaystyle m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}{\bf A}_{h}^{1/2}({\bf x}(t))\,\lambda_{h}\,{\bf z}_{h}

(96)

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf z}_{h}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{h}\,{\bf z}_{h}(t)+{\bf A}_{h}^{1/2}({\bf x}(t))\,{\bf v}(t)+\sqrt{2\,k_{B}\,T}\,\bm{\xi}_{h}(t)\,,\qquad h=1,\dots,N_{d}

where
\bm{\xi}_{h}(t)=(\xi_{h,1}(t),\xi_{h,2}(t),\xi_{h,3}(t))
are
the distributional
derivatives of three-dimensional independent Wiener processes,

\langle\xi_{h,i}(t)\,\xi_{k,j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{hk}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
h,k=1,\dots,N_{d}
,
i,j=1,2,3
.

For the proof see
[
77
]
.
\diamond

In the remainder we extend this result to fluid inertial effects first
in a Newtonian fluid, and subsequently in the
case of generic complex fluids characterized by a viscoelastic memory
response.

For a confined Newtonian fluid in the time-dependent incompressible Stokes
regime, the particle hydromechanics at constant particle position
{\bf x}
is
defined by the equation

m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\bm{\eta}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)-{\bf k}(t;{\bf x})*\left(\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}+{\bf v}(0)\,\delta(t)\right)+{\bf R}(t;{\bf x})

(97)

where
\bm{\eta}({\bf x})
and
{\bf k}(t;{\bf x})
are respectively the friction tensor and the fluid-inertial tensorial
kernels at constant
{\bf x}
.
In eq. (
97
) we have neglected the added mass. This
contribution
can be easily imbedded in the theory as developed in Appendix
C
.
As in section
II
, we assume

{\bf k}(0,{\bf x})
to be bounded, as it follows from the finite propagation
velocity of the shear stresses.
As for eqs. (
10
) and (
37
), eq. (
97
) can be
transformed into

m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\bm{\eta}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)-{\bf k}(0;{\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d{\bf k}(t-\tau;{\bf x})}{d\tau}\,{\bf v}(\tau)\,d\tau+{\bf R}(t;{\bf x})

(98)

From the principle of local realizability (i.e. using
the expansions outlined in section
III
), we can represent
the kernel
{\bf k}(t;{\bf x})
in the form

{\bf k}(t;{\bf x})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}{\bf g}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}

(99)

where
{\bf g}_{\alpha}({\bf x})
are
3\times 3
matrices parametrically
dependent on
{\bf x}
, and the relaxation rates
\mu_{\alpha}
are constant.
Substituting eq. (

98
) we obtain

m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\bm{\eta}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)-{\bf G}({\bf x}){\bf v}(t)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}{\bf g}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,\mu_{\alpha}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}t}*{\bf v}(t)+{\bf R}(t;{\bf x})

.
Eq. (

,
\alpha=1,2,3

\displaystyle m\,\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bm{\eta}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)-{\bf G}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\mu_{\alpha}\,\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,{\bf z}_{\alpha}(t)+\sqrt{2}\,{\bf a}({\bf x})\,\bm{\xi}(t)+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}{\bf d}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,\bm{\xi}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle-\mu_{\alpha}\,{\bf z}_{\alpha}(t)+\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,{\bf v}(t)+\sqrt{2}\,{\bf c}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\,\bm{\xi}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\,,\quad\alpha=1,\dots,N_{i}

We have he following results:

The results formalized by Theorems II and III in the presence of fluid-inertial
effects
are qualitatively and functionally analogous to those obtained in the dissipative case. The contribution of each
{\bf g}_{\alpha}({\bf x})

is splitted symmetrically onto the dynamic equation
for particle velocity and for the auxiliary memory variables

Moreover, the resulting stochastic force eq. (

)

at time

and not on its previous history.

Also in the confined case the concept of fluctuational patterns and
their algebra applies. Different fluctuational patterns can be combined together
for producing consistent models of particle hydrodynamics of increasing
hydrodynamic complexity owing to the independence of the stochastic
forcings acting on each internal degree of freedom.
Therefore, joining together the results of Theorems I, II and III, the
general case of FD3k for confined particle hydromechanics in the presence of
dissipative and fluid inertial memory effects can be derived.

X
Concluding remarks

A complete formulation of stochastic hydromechanics for generic
linear fluid and flow conditions has been developed, including
the case of constrained geometries.
The approach followed starts from the solution of the FD3k relation,
i.e. the representation of the thermal force
R(t)
in terms
of elementary stochastic processes, and derives from it
FD2k, i.e. its autocorrelation properties. Eqs. (
63
)
and (
64
) solve completely this problem
in the presence of fluid inertial effects.
It is remarkable the occurrence of the impulsive contribution

k(0)\,\delta(t)
in these relations,
that mathematically implies the condition of bounded

k(0)
.
This is exactly the result derived in
[
73
]
for generic fluids
enforcing the boundedness of the velocity of propagation of the internal shear stresses.

It is also worth of attention, the new, active role of fluctuation-dissipation
theory and thermodynamic concepts in deriving in the presence of
constraints and memory hydromechanics, a thermodynamically
consistent expression for the mean-field force alongside with
the expression for the thermal one. It suggests the importance of considering
hydromechanics in a fluctuational environment as the most conclusive
setting for deriving pure hydromechanic consequences.

The decomposition in internal degrees of freedom, possessing the property of being uncorrelated
with each other at equilibrium, provides an elegant
way of framing FD3k in a compact way via the concept of
fluctuational patterns and their algebra. This concept is
of practical application to any problem of Brownian
motion theory and can be extended to microfluidic investigation including
the effects of external potentials and external velocity fields.
The properties of the fluctuational patterns associated
with different hydrodynamic regimes is extensively used in part III

[
70
]
to analyze the local regularity properties
of velocity fluctuations in a hydrodynamic perspective.
Morover, the structure of the fluctuational patterns provides
a proper setting for generalizing the Kubo fluctuation-dissipation theory.

This point deserves some clarifications. Extensive hydrodynamic analysis, coupled
with recent experimental results on the
possible violation of equipartition theorem in Brownian motion in liquids,

[
81
]
suggests a generalization of the current
fluctuation-dissipation approach removing the Langevin
condition eq. (
2
). Enforcing the structure
of the fluctuational patterns this can be naturally achieved
preserving the global fluctuation-dissipation relations.
This theoretical extension is thoroughly addressed in part III
[
44
]
.

Throughout this work we have considered the representation
of the modal fluctuational
forces proportional to the distributional derivatives of independent
Wiener processes,

\{\xi_{h}\}_{h=1}^{N_{d}}
,
\{\xi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{d}}
. Of course, this
corresponds to the most traditional choice which, as discussed in
[
70
]
, leads necessarily to
Gaussian equilibrium density function. Other choices
are possible, still preserving the validity of FD1k, i.e. of eq. (
3
).
This issue and its statistical physical implications are addressed in part II

[
70
]
.

Appendix A
Vectorial models

In this section we consider the representation of fluctuation-dissipation
relations for vectorial models in which the hydrodynamic parameters are
expressed
by non-isotropic matrices. A non-dimensional formulation is
adopted, where
{\bf v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
is the nondimensional
particle velocity fulfilling the
normalized equilibrium relation
\langle v_{i}v_{j}\rangle_{\rm eq}=\delta_{ij}
.

A.1
Dissipative memory

To begin with, consider a single dissipative
mode with rate constant
\lambda

\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-{\bf a}\,e^{-\lambda\,t}*{\bf v}(t)+{\bf R}(t)

(108)

where
{\bf v}(t)=(v_{1}(t),v_{2}(t),v_{3}(t))
, and similarly for
{\bf R}(t)
, while

{\bf a}=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{3}
is the nondimensional friction matrix,
which is symmetric and positive definite. Introducing
\bm{\theta}(t)=e^{-\lambda\,t}*{\bf v}(t)
, and assuming, as in the main text,
that the stochastic
fluctuations act solely on the internal degrees of freedom, we have
componentwise,

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{i,j}\,\theta_{j}

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda\,\theta_{i}+v_{i}+\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{i,j}\,\xi_{j}(t)

(109)

i=1,2,3
, where
\sigma_{i,j}
are the entries of the
symmetric matrix
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}^{T}
to be determined, and
\langle\xi_{i}(t)\,\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. The Fokker-Planck equation for the
density
p({\bf v},\bm{\theta},t)
associated with eq. (
109
)
reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}\,\theta_{j}\,p\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\lambda\,\theta_{i}\,p\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_{i}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}D_{ij}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}

(110)

where

D_{ij}=\sum_{h=1}^{3}\sigma_{ih}\,\sigma_{jh}

(111)

The conditions to be imposed to the moments at steady-state are

m_{v_{i}v_{j}}^{*}=\delta_{ij}\,,\qquad m_{\theta_{i}v_{j}}^{*}=0\,,\qquad i,j=1,2,3

(112)

The dynamic equations for the moments read

\frac{dm_{v_{h}v_{k}}}{dt}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{hj}\,m_{\theta_{j}v_{k}}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{j}v_{h}}

(113)

that is identically verified at equilibrium, owing to eq. (
112
).
Similarly,

\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}}{dt}=-2\,\lambda\,m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}-m_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}-m_{\theta_{k}v_{h}}+2\,D_{hk}

(114)

At equilibrium, it reduces to

m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}^{*}=\frac{D_{hk}}{\lambda}

(115)

Finally, for the mixed moments
m_{\theta_{k}v_{h}}
we have

\frac{dm_{\theta_{k}v_{h}}}{dt}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{hj}m_{\theta_{j}\theta_{k}}-\lambda\,m_{\theta_{k}v_{h}}+m_{v_{h}v_{k}}

(116)

that at steady-state becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{hj}\,m_{\theta_{j}\theta_{k}}^{*}=m_{v_{h}v_{k}}^{*}

(117)

Using eqs. (
112
) and (
115
), eq. (
117
)
can be compactly expressed in matrix form

{\bf a}\,{\bf D}=\lambda

(118)

Since
{\bf D}=\bm{\sigma}^{2}
, and owing to the symmetric
and positive definite nature of
{\bf a}
, there exists a unique
symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix

\bm{\sigma}
such that
[
82
]

\bm{\sigma}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,{\bf a}^{-1/2}

(119)

This result can be generalized to a system of dissipative modes

\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}{\bf a}^{(h)}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*{\bf v}(t)+{\bf R}(t)

(120)

Letting
\bm{\theta}^{(h)}(t)=e^{-\lambda_{h}\,t}*{\bf v}(t)
,

h=1,\dots,N_{d}
, we
have the representation

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}{\bf a}^{(h)}\,\bm{\theta}^{(h)}

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\theta}^{(h)}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{h}\,\bm{\theta}^{(h)}+{\bf v}+\sqrt{2}\bm{\sigma}^{(h)}\,\bm{\xi}^{(h)}

(121)

where
\bm{\sigma}^{(h)}
are symmetric
3\times 3
matrices,

\bm{\xi}^{(h)}(t)=(\xi_{i}^{(h)}(t))_{i=1,3}
, and

\langle\xi^{(h)}_{i}(t)\,\xi^{(k)}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta^{hk}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(122)

As the dissipative internal degrees of freedom are uncorrelated, one
finally obtains for the matrices
\bm{\sigma}^{(h)}
the
expression

\bm{\sigma}^{(h)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{h}}\,\left({\bf a}^{(h)}\right)^{-1/2}\,,\quad h=1,\dots,N_{d}

(123)

generalizing eq. (
119
).

A.2
Inertial and dissipative memory

Next, consider the simultaneous occurrence of dissipative and inertial
memory effects, in the presence of a single mode for the two phenomena,

\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-{\bf a}\,e^{-\lambda\,t}*{\bf v}(t)-\bm{\gamma}\,{\bf v}(t)+\mu\,\bm{\gamma}\,e^{-\mu\,t}*{\bf v}(t)+{\bf R}(t)

(124)

Introducing the internal degrees of freedom
\bm{\theta}(t)=e^{-\lambda\,t}*{\bf v}(t)
,
{\bf z}(t)=e^{-\mu\,t}*{\bf v}(t)
, eq.
(
124
) can be represented as

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}\,\theta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{ij}\,v_{j}+\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{ij}\,z_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}d_{ij}\,\xi^{\prime}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda\,\theta_{i}+v_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}b_{ij}\,\xi_{j}(t)

(125)

\displaystyle\frac{dz_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\,z_{i}+v_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{ij}\,\xi_{j}^{\prime}(t)

i=1,2,3
, where
b_{ij}
,
d_{ij}
and
c_{ij}
are the entries of the
the
3\times 3
symmetric matrices
{\bf b}
,
{\bf d}
, and
{\bf c}
,
respectively.
The Fokker-Planck equation for the density
p({\bf v},\bm{\theta},{\bf z},t)
associated with eq. (
125
) is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}\,\theta_{j}\,p\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{ij}\,v_{j}\,p\right)-\mu\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{ij}\,z_{j}\,p\right)

(126)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}D_{ij}^{(1)}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial v_{i}\partial v_{j}}+2\,D_{ij}^{(c)}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial v_{i}\partial z_{j}}+\lambda\,\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\theta_{i}\,p\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}D_{ij}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu\,\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\left(z_{i}\,p\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_{i}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial z_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}D_{ij}^{(2)}\,\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}}

where

{\bf D}={\bf b}^{2}\,,\quad{\bf D}^{(1)}={\bf d}^{2}\,,\quad{\bf D}^{(2)}={\bf c}^{2}\,,\quad{\bf D}^{(c)}=\frac{{\bf c}\,{\bf d}+{\bf d}\,{\bf c}}{2}

(127)

and
D_{i,j}
are the entries of the
3\times 3
matrix
{\bf D}
,
and analogously for
{\bf D}^{(u)}
,
u=1,2,c
.
As in the scalar case, the conditions to be imposed at steady state are

m_{v_{i}v_{j}}^{*}=\delta_{ij}\,,\quad m_{\theta_{i}v_{j}}^{*}=m_{z_{i}v_{j}}^{*}=0\,,\qquad i,j=1,2,3

(128)

Consider the second-order moment equations:

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{v_{h}v_{k}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{hj}\,m_{\theta_{j}v_{k}}-\sum_{j?1}^{3}a_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{j}v_{h}}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{hj}\,m_{v_{k}v_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{kj}\,m_{v_{h}v_{j}}

(129)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{hj}m_{z_{j}v_{k}}+\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{kj}m_{z_{j}v_{h}}+2\,D_{hk}^{(1)}

At equilibrium,

\gamma_{hk}+\gamma_{kh}=2\,D_{hk}^{(1)}

(130)

Since
\gamma_{hk}
is symmetric, we have

D_{hk}^{(1)}=\gamma_{hk}\,\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\bf d}=\bm{\gamma}^{1/2}

(131)

Next,

\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}}{dt}=-2\,\lambda\,m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}-m_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}-m_{\theta_{k}v_{h}}+2\,D_{hk}

(132)

At steady-state, enforcing the conditions eq. (
128
),
we have

m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{k}}^{*}=\frac{D_{hk}}{\lambda}

(133)

As regards
m_{z_{h}z_{k}}
,

\frac{dm_{z_{h}z_{k}}}{dt}=-2\,\mu\,m_{z_{h}z_{k}}-m_{x_{h}v_{k}}-m_{z_{k}v_{h}}+2\,D_{hk}^{(c)}

(134)

that, at equilibrium, provides

m_{z_{h}z_{k}}^{*}=\frac{D_{hk}^{(2)}}{\mu}

(135)

The evolution equation of the mixed moments associated with
dissipative/inertial degrees of freedom is given by

\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}z_{k}}}{dt}=-\lambda m_{\theta_{h}z_{k}}+m_{z_{k}v_{h}}-\mu\,m_{\theta_{h}z_{k}}+m_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}

(136)

implying at equilibrium

m_{\theta_{h}z_{k}}^{*}=0

(137)

indicating that the dissipative degrees of fredom are uncorrelated from
the inertial ones. As regards
m_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}
we have

\frac{dm_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}}{dt}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{h}v_{j}}+\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{h}z_{j}}-\lambda\,m_{\theta_{h}v_{k}}+m_{v_{h}v_{k}}

(138)

that at equilibrium reduces to

-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{kj}\,m_{\theta_{h}\theta_{j}}^{*}+m_{v_{h}v_{k}}^{*}=0

(139)

Enforcing the relations previously derived and the conditions eq. (
128
), the latter equation becomes in matrix form

{\bf a}\,{\bf D}=\lambda\,{\bf I}

(140)

where
{\bf I}
is the identity matrix. Thus,

{\bf D}=\lambda\,{\bf a}^{-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\bf b}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,{\bf a}^{-1/2}

(141)

Finally,

\frac{dm_{v_{h}z_{k}}}{dt}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{hj}\,m_{\theta_{j}z_{k}}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{hj}m_{v_{j}z_{k}}+\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{hj}\,m_{z_{j}z_{k}}-\mu\,m_{v_{h}z_{k}}+m_{v_{h}v_{k}}+2\,D_{hk}^{(c)}

(142)

At steady-state it becomes

\mu\,\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{hj}\,m_{z_{j}z_{k}}^{*}+m_{v_{h}v_{k}}^{*}+2\,D_{hk}^{(c)}=0

(143)

that, enforcing the relations previously obtained for
m_{z_{j}z_{k}}^{*}

and the conditions eq. (
128
), attains the form

\bm{\gamma}\,{\bf D}^{(2)}+{\bf I}+2\,{\bf D}^{(c)}=0

(144)

Expressing
{\bf D}^{(2)}
and
{\bf D}^{(c)}
in terms of the matrices

{\bf d}
and
{\bf c}
, eq. (
144
) becomes

\bm{\gamma}\,{\bf c}^{2}+{\bf I}+{\bf d}{\bf c}+{\bf c}{\bf d}=0
, and since
{\bf d}=\bm{\gamma}^{1/2}
,
{\bf c}
is the solution
of the matrix equation

\bm{\gamma}\,{\bf c}^{2}+{\bf I}+\bm{\gamma}^{1/2}{\bf c}+{\bf c}\bm{\gamma}^{1/2}=0

(145)

and thus

{\bf c}=-\bm{\gamma}^{-1/2}

(146)

that completes the determination of the representation of the
stochastic contribution.
This result can be generalized to an arbitrary number of modes, i.e.,
to

\frac{d{\bf v}(t)}{dt}=-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{i}}{\bf a}^{(h)}\,e^{-\lambda_{h}t}*{\bf v}(t)-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\,{\bf v}(t)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\mu_{\alpha}\,\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\,e^{-\mu_{\alpha}\,t}*{\bf v}(t)+{\bf R}(t)

(147)

The corresponding representation in terms of dissipative/inertial degrees
of freedom takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{h=1}^{N_{d}}{\bf a}^{(h)}\,\bm{\theta}^{(h)}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\,{\bf v}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\mu_{\alpha}\,\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\,{\bf z}^{(\alpha)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}{\bf d}^{(\alpha)}\,\bm{\xi}^{\prime,(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\theta}^{(h)}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{h}\,\bm{\theta}^{(h)}+{\bf v}+{\bf b}^{(h)}\,\bm{\xi}^{(h)}

(148)

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf z}^{(\alpha)}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu_{\alpha}\,{\bf z}^{(\alpha)}+{\bf v}+{\bf c}^{(\alpha)}\,\bm{\xi}^{\prime,(\alpha)}

with
\langle\xi^{(h)}_{i}(t)\xi^{(k)}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta^{hk}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
\langle\xi^{\prime,(\alpha)}_{i}(t)\xi^{\prime,(\beta)}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta^{\alpha\beta}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
\langle\xi^{(h)}_{i}(t)\xi^{\prime,(\alpha)}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=0
. The fluctuational
intensity matrices in this general case are given by

{\bf b}^{(h)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{h}}\,\left({\bf a}^{(h)}\right)^{-1/2}\,,\quad{\bf d}^{(\alpha)}=\left(\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\right)^{1/2}\,,\quad{\bf c}^{(\alpha)}=-\left(\bm{\gamma}^{(\alpha)}\right)^{-1/2}

(149)

generalizing the previous expression
eq. (
55
)
obtained in the scalar case.

Appendix C
Fluctuation-dissipation in confined geometries

First of all, consider eqs. (
101

an elementary corollary of the principle of local consistency.
Integrating the second system of equations (
101

100

Next, analyze the dynamics eq. (
102

position
{\bf x}
as a parameter.
Componentwise, it reads as

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\eta_{hk}({\bf x})\,v_{k}(t)}{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{G_{hk}({\bf x})\,v_{k}(t)}{m}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,hk}({\bf x})\,\mu_{\alpha}\,z_{\alpha,k}(t)}{m}

(165)

i.e.,

{\bf c}_{\alpha}^{2}-\sqrt{k_{B}\,T}\,\left(\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,{\bf c}_{\alpha}+\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1}\,{\bf c}_{\alpha}\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\right)+k_{B}\,T\,\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}=0

(166)

Assuming that
{\bf c}_{\alpha}
commute with
\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}

(this property will be demonstrated a posteriori), we have

{\bf c}_{\alpha}^{2}-\sqrt{k_{B}\,T}\,\left(\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,{\bf c}_{\alpha}+{\bf c}_{\alpha}\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}\right)+k_{B}\,T\,\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}=0

(167)

that reduces to
({\bf c}_{\alpha}-\sqrt{k_{B}T}\,\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2})^{2}={\bf 0}
, and thus

{\bf c}_{\alpha}({\bf x})=\sqrt{k_{B}\,T}\,\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1/2}({\bf x})\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}({\bf x})

(168)

Eqs. (
162
)-(
163
), (
168
) solve
the fluctuation-dissipation problem, when
{\bf x}
is treated as a parameter.
But there is a particular choice for which all the statistical
properties of the steady-state solution do not depend on
{\bf x}
.
From eq. (
163
) and from the definition eq. (
155
)
of the matrices
\bm{\sigma}({\bf x})
, the second-order
moments of the auxiliary
{\bf z}_{\alpha}
variables are position
independent if and only if the matrices
{\bf c}_{\alpha}({\bf x})

do not depend on
{\bf x}
. Resorting to eq. (
168
),
this occurs if and only if the matrix product

\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{-1/2}({\bf x})\,\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{1/2}({\bf x})
is a constant matrix, that without loss of generality, can be
taken equal to the identity matrix. In this way, the commutation
property of
{\bf c}_{\alpha}
used above is surely satisfied.
Thus, we arrive at the important result that, assuming

\bm{\gamma}_{\alpha}({\bf x})
and
\bm{\zeta}_{\alpha}({\bf x})

equal to each other, i.e. eq. (
104
), we have

{\bf c}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{k_{B}\,T}\,{\bf I}\,,\qquad m_{z_{\alpha,p}z_{\beta,q}}=\frac{k_{B}\,T}{m}\delta_{pq}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(169)

It follows that within this choice, i.e. enforcing eq. (
104
), the
steady-state density
p^{*}({\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}})

solution of eq. (
154
does not depend on
{\bf x}

and is given by

p^{*}({\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}})=C\,\exp\left(-\frac{m|{\bf v}|^{2}}{2\,k_{B}\,T}\right)\,\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu_{\alpha}|{\bf z}_{\alpha}|^{2}}{2\,k_{B}\,T}\right)

(170)

where
C
is the normalization constant.

Next, consider the real dynamic problem where
{\bf x}(t)
evolves
as a function of time according to the kinematic equation in a closed
and bounded domain
\Omega
.
The probability density function
p({\bf x},{\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}},t)
is the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial p({\bf x},{\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}},t)}{\partial t}=-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}p({\bf x},{\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}},t)+{\mathcal{L}}_{\bf x}[p({\bf x},{\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}},t)]

(171)

where the differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\bf x}
is defined
by eq. (
154
).
It is straightforward to see that eq. (
170
) is the steady-state
solution of eq. (
171
) as
p^{*}({\bf v},\{{\bf z}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N_{i}})
does not depend on
{\bf x}
and, by linearity, it is indeed the
unique equilibrium solution. This proves Theorem II, while
Theorem III is just a consequence of Theorem II, i.e. of eq. (
103
)
enforcing the conditions eq. (
104
).
