Effect of Occlusal Reduction on Pain in Posterior Teeth with Necrosis and Apical Periodontitis: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Pankaj Sangwan, Hans Raj Saini, Aditi Sangwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis randomized controlled trial evaluated the impact of occlusal reduction on postoperative pain after chemomechanical preparation.Materials and methods102 asymptomatic teeth with pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis were randomly allocated to two equal groups: the control group (NOR) and the occlusal reduction (OR) group. The root canal treatment was performed according to a predefined standardized protocol. Patients were prescribed Ibuprofen 400 mg to be consumed 8 hourly, if required. Postoperative pain and analgesic consumption were documented for 7 days following chemomechanical preparation. The data analysis was performed using Mann–Whitney U and Chi-square tests.ResultsA significant postoperative pain difference was found between the two groups on the first 3 days, with higher pain observed in NOR as compared to OR (p < 0.05). The prevalence of pain was higher in NOR (47.06%) as compared to OR group (29.41%), but the difference was not significant (p > 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the mean number of analgesic doses consumed and the prevalence of analgesic intake between the two groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionPostoperative pain incidence was comparable in the two groups. The difference in postoperative pain intensity was statistically significant on the first 3 days. The reduction observed, however, lacked clinical relevance.How to cite this articleSangwan P, Saini HR, Sangwan A. Introduction

Postoperative pain is commonly experienced by patients following root canal treatment, and is often disturbing for both the patient and the treating endodontist. While most cases report mild pain, it may be moderate or severe in severity. Development of pain after the endodontist's intervention in an otherwise asymptomatic nonvital tooth may especially undermine the confidence of the patient in the operator's skill. Minimization of postoperative pain is, thus, a key objective in the mind of an endodontist. Several strategies have been suggested to avoid this unpleasant situation, including, among others, intracanal medications, use of steroidal and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents, surgical trephination, and occlusal relief.1–4

Occlusal reduction relieves the tooth of mechanical forces, leading to reduced stimulation of nociceptors and hence less pain.5 However, the question as to whether occlusal reduction must be carried out in all cases of endodontically involved teeth is still a matter of debate. The passage of time has seen a shift in trend in the indications of occlusal reduction.6,7 Evolution of newer strategies, sometimes contradicting previous ones, is a consequence of continuous research and a better understanding of biological mechanisms in the field of dentistry. Creech et al.8 and Jostes et al.9 concluded that prophylactic occlusal reduction may be of limited utility in the prevention of pain. On the contrary, around a decade later, Rosenberg et al.4 and Babick et al.10 found that occlusal reduction effectively reduced the postoperative pain in specific case types. Arias et al.11 reported a lower postendodontic pain incidence after the reduction than in the presence of occlusion. Thus, studies evaluating occlusal reduction in the prevention of postoperative pain have produced conflicting results, creating uncertainty regarding its value in the control of postoperative pain. Knowledge of the proper indications for the procedure will certainly help improve the quality of care in endodontic practice.

Pain after root canal treatment may vary depending on several factors, including, among others, the vitality status of the involved tooth and the presence of apical periodontitis.12 The results of the studies evaluating its role in teeth with similar diagnoses have been found to be conflicting. Studies have been conducted on posterior teeth with sensitivity to percussion, of which some concluded that occlusal reduction failed to provide any further relief in postoperative pain compared to no occlusal reduction,13–15 while the other two found that it was beneficial during the initial study period.16,17 On these lines, a systematic review18 has highlighted the need for further studies to evaluate the role of OR in teeth with different pulpal and periapical diagnoses. Based on the literature review conducted by the authors, no randomized controlled trial has evaluated the value of this procedure in nonvital teeth. This study, therefore, sought to evaluate the incidence and severity of postoperative pain following occlusal reduction in nonvital mandibular molars with chronic apical periodontitis and compare it with no occlusal reduction. It was hypothesized that occlusal reduction would not provide any additional benefit in postoperative pain compared to control. The primary outcome measure was the incidence of postoperative pain. The analgesic consumption was analyzed as a secondary outcome.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in the Department of Conservative Dentistry at Postgraduate Institute of Dental Sciences (PGIDS), Rohtak, Haryana, India, from September 2014 to March 2016. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of PGIDS, Rohtak, Haryana, India (PGIDS/2013/IEC/81). Only asymptomatic patients with nonvital permanent mandibular first molars with radiographically evident apical periodontitis (minimum size of 2 × 2 mm) were included. Vitality status was ascertained by a negative response to the electric pulp test and cold test. It was further ascertained by the absence of bleeding upon entry into the pulp chamber. All the patients selected for the study had one mandibular molar with pulpal necrosis. Subjects reporting preoperative pain, a history of antibiotic intake in the past 3 months, or analgesics during the past 24 hours, or those with an indication for antibiotic premedication prior to dental treatment were excluded from the study. Diabetics, immunocompromised patients, and pregnant subjects were also not included.

Once eligibility for the study was confirmed, the investigator explained the study design, treatment protocol, and possible discomforts and risks to the patients and obtained informed consent. All the included patients were informed that their enrollment in the study was voluntary and that the required treatment would be provided regardless of their decision regarding participation.

Based on an alpha error of 0.05 and a power of 0.8, a sample size of 50 patients in each group was determined to be sufficient to adequately detect the difference between the two groups. Calculation of the standardized difference was based on experience from a previous study, using the data limited to teeth exhibiting radiographic evidence of apical periodontitis.4

Treatment Protocol

All the patients were treated by a single endodontist (PS) using a standardized protocol. The tooth was anesthetized with an inferior alveolar nerve block using a local anesthetic solution containing 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine. After rubber dam isolation, an access cavity preparation was completed. The coronal portion of the canals was prepared with low-speed Gates-Glidden drills (Mani Inc, Utsunomiya, Tochigi, Japan). Working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (EAL) (Root ZX; J Morita, Irvine, CA, USA), and confirmed with a diagnostic file radiograph. Canals were then prepared with 0.02 taper ISO stainless steel files (Mani, Inc.) to a size reaching three times larger than the first apical binding file. The step back technique was used for chemomechanical preparation, with each successively larger file placed 0.5 mm shorter than the earlier one. During the entire procedure, canals were irrigated with 5 mL 3% sodium hypochlorite (Hyposol; PrevestDenpro Limited, Jammu, India) between each sequential file size using a 27-G endodontic needle (Monoject; Sherwood Davis and Geck, St. Louis, MO). Canal patency was maintained and verified with a #10 stainless steel file. Once the canal had been enlarged, it was irrigated with 5 mL of 17% EDTA for 1 minute, followed by 5 mL 3% NaOCl. After drying with sterile paper points, the canals were dressed with calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)2] for 7 days using lentulospiral filler. The teeth were then restored with a temporary restorative material (IRM, Dentsply Ltd, Weybridge, United Kingdom).

The patients were then randomized to either of the two groups: (1) Occlusal reduction group (OR) and (2) Control group (NOR). Using an equal-proportion randomization allocation ratio, an investigator not involved in the endodontic treatment (HRS) sequentially assigned patients to the two treatment groups using envelopes containing concealed group codes. It was ensured that neither the operator nor the patient was aware of the treatment protocol assigned till the temporization of the involved tooth had been completed. In the OR group, all occlusal contacts were reduced by 1 mm using a diamond bur in an air-rotor handpiece. The absence of contact was confirmed with articulating paper. In NOR, the air-rotor handpiece was activated inside the mouth without contacting the occlusal surface to ensure that the patient stayed blinded to the allotted group.

Assessment of Treatment Outcome

The patients recorded their pain levels on the visual analog pain scale (VAS), where 0 value on the scale referred to no pain at all and 10 referred to extremely severe pain. The scale was explained to the patients, who were then asked to rate their pain every day for 7 days after chemomechanical preparation. The pain recorded was further categorized into four groups as no pain (0), mild (1–3), moderate (4–6), and severe (7–10). The patients were instructed to use analgesics (Ibuprofen 400 mg) to relieve pain in case they experienced postoperative pain and record the details of the same.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to analyze data distribution. The findings were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare the groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for ordinal data, and the Chi-square test was used for dichotomous data. A partial correlation was run to determine the relationship between pain and interventions while controlling for the effects of age, gender, and the tooth quadrant. The level of significance was kept at 0.05 for the study, using a 2-tailed analysis.

Results

A total of 102 patients were recruited in the study (51 patients in each group). Being a short-term follow-up, all the patients returned with the pain chart at the next appointment (Fig. 1). The mean age of patients in OR was 34.57 ± 10.53 years, while it was 31.22 ± 10.23 years in NOR (Table 1). Both groups did not show statistically significant differences regarding the patients' age, gender, and quadrant involved (p > 0.05).

The overall incidence of pain was 38.24%. There was a higher incidence of pain in the NOR group (47.06%) as compared to the OR group (29.41%). The difference, however, was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Maximum pain scores of 6 and 5 were recorded in NOR and OR, respectively. Mean VAS scores were statistically significantly higher in the NOR group than in the OR group during the first 3 days following chemomechanical preparation, after which they became nonsignificant for the rest of the study period (Table 2). No patient reported severe pain during the study. At 24 hours, the incidence of moderate pain was 11.76% (5.88% in OR group and 17.65% in NOR group), while 26.47% had mild pain (23.53% in OR group and 29.41% in NOR group), and 61.76% of the patients had no pain (70.58% in OR group and 52.94% in NOR group) (Fig. 2). Pain decreased in the days following the treatment. Controlling for age, gender, and quadrant involved, the intensity of pain at 24 hours showed a statistically nonsignificant correlation with the interventions (p = 0.058).

A total of 13.73% of the patients (7 out of 51) in the OR group and 23.53% (12 out of 51) in the NOR group required analgesics for pain control (p > 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the mean number of analgesic doses required by both groups (p = 0.138), with less analgesic intake in the OR group (mean = 0.33 ± 1.03) as compared to the NOR group (mean = 0.96 ± 1.97).

Discussion

Occlusal reduction has been suggested to reduce pain following endodontic treatment. However, various research exploring the issue has produced conflicting results.4,8–10,13–17 This observed variation could be due to differences in study designs, selection criteria, instrumentation techniques, experience of the treating endodontist, and methods used to record pain.19,20 Most of these studies have been conducted on teeth with positive or mixed vitality status. Since the status of the pulp and periapex may modulate the response12 to pain-preventive strategy, the main objective of the present randomized controlled trial was to evaluate the effect of occlusal reduction on postoperative pain in asymptomatic mandibular molars with pulpal necrosis and radiographic evidence of apical periodontitis. Since no significant difference in incidence of postoperative pain (primary outcome measure) was observed between the two groups, the null hypothesis was accepted.

Nonvital teeth are more likely to be microbially contaminated than vital teeth.21 In such teeth with chronic apical periodontitis, there is a delicate interplay between the root canal irritants and the host immune system.22 Consequently, the chronic periapical inflammation exists without any clinical signs or symptoms. However, when the endodontic treatment is initiated, this balance may tilt in favor of irritants when these are pushed into the periapical area during chemomechanical preparation, leading to localized acute inflammatory reaction and pain.22 It is generally accepted that there is a neurogenic component involved in the pathophysiology of inflammation.23 The nerve sprouting observed in the periapical region of necrotic teeth24 and their consequent sensitization due to certain mediators of inflammation lead to spontaneous depolarization and spontaneous pain. Occlusal trauma further deteriorates the condition by increasing the secretion of neuropeptides such as Substance-P in the periodontal ligament,25 which are capable of producing neurovascular changes and hyperalgesia.23,26 It is believed that relief from occlusal stresses may thus reduce the mechanical activation of the sensitized nerve endings in the periapical region and lower postoperative pain.

Strict inclusion criteria were adopted, and the same endodontist treated all the cases using a standardized, predefined treatment protocol in order to minimize the effect of confounding preoperative and intraoperative variables. It has been shown that the type of teeth included and the jaws involved may have an influence on the postoperative pain.11 Keeping this in consideration, it was decided to include asymptomatic mandibular first molars with apical periodontitis in this study. Pain was analyzed after chemomechanical preparation since the procedure of obturation would have added another set of confounding variables affecting the pain levels.27

A total of 38.24% of the recruited subjects experienced postoperative pain at 24 hours. In both groups, the incidence of postoperative pain decreased steadily over time, with the highest proportion observed at 24 hours following chemomechanical preparation. By the sixth postoperative day, 100% patients in both groups reported no pain. The mean postoperative pain, however, in contrast to the incidence, showed a statistically significant difference on the first 3 postoperative days, with lower values observed in OR as compared to NOR throughout the study period. The significantly higher intensity of pain during the initial study period was interestingly not reflected in analgesic intake, where the statistical difference was found not to be significant. This may be suggestive of the fact that the significant difference in pain intensities between the groups observed statistically was not meaningful from the patient's perspective. The magnitude of difference between the values of mean pain intensities in those 3 days was not substantial. It must be remembered that the p-value by itself only indicates if the given difference between the groups has a probability of occurring simply by random chance.28 Drawing conclusions on its basis alone can be misleading sometimes in clinical studies. It is equally important to examine the magnitude of difference between the groups to see if it is clinically beneficial, often referred to as minimal clinically significant difference (MCSD). While its magnitude remains elusive in dentistry, it has been reported to be in the range of 9–13 mm on a 100 mm VAS scale in medical studies.29,30 Since MCSD has been found to vary depending on pathoanatomic elements,31 applying these findings to our field could be considered inappropriate. Further studies are warranted to effectively address this issue.

The findings of the present study are in consonance with the observations of various previous studies that evaluated the impact of occlusal reduction on the experience of postoperative pain.4,8,10 Creech et al.8 reported that the prophylactic removal of occlusal contacts does not significantly reduce the postoperative pain. Rosenberg et al.4 and Babick et al.10 also suggested that relieving occlusion in teeth with nonvital pulp or apical periodontitis may not be useful for the prevention of pain. They stated that occlusal reduction is indicated only in teeth with prior pain, vital pulp, tenderness, and/or absence of periapical radiolucency. The explanation for these discrepant results may be offered, at least partially, on account of certain methodological differences between these studies and the present study. The previous studies had included posterior teeth (presumably including molars as well as premolars) from both jaws, with vital or nonvital pulps. There were also no restrictions applied to the teeth included with respect to preoperative pain and apical periodontitis. The tooth type, arch, preoperative pain, and apical periodontitis have all been shown to significantly influence postoperative pain.11 The numbers and the distribution of factors may or may not have been uniform and, thus, may have confounded the results. Also, the scoring methods utilized for recording postoperative pain varied. While Creech et al.8 used a numeric rating scale, Rosenberg et al.4 merged patients with mild pain into the group with no pain, thus underestimating the prevalence of postoperative pain. Lastly, the placement of intracanal medicaments has been proposed to influence the postoperative pain. Both Creech et al.8 and Rosenberg et al.4 did not use any intracanal medicament, and sealed the access cavity following root canal preparation with temporary restorative material after simply placing a sterile cotton pellet. Sufficient methodological details were not available in one publication10 to make a valid comparison.

It is pertinent to mention here that while occlusal reduction is a pain preventive measure, it poses a peculiar limitation in clinical practice. Once the occlusion is reduced, the tooth becomes nonfunctional. Although full coverage of posterior teeth is suggested following root canal treatment, not all patients may opt for it due to certain personal or financial reasons. Hence, it becomes the responsibility of the treating dentist to inform and discuss the consequences of relieving occlusion before proceeding. In this study, the reduction was performed after the root canals had been prepared and the cavity was sealed to ensure blinding. However, in practice, the occlusal reduction may be advisable at the beginning of root canal treatment for two reasons. One, it avoids any change in occlusal landmarks used as reference points during working length determination, and second, it reduces the chances of coronal fracture. This entails that it is important for the endodontist to be aware of specific indications for occlusion reduction. The present study indicated that occlusal reduction in asymptomatic nonvital teeth with chronic apical periodontitis may not be beneficial for pain relief. It is the authors' opinion, therefore, that occlusal reduction may not be practiced routinely in such teeth.

Strengths of this trial include restricting recruitment to teeth with a specific pulpal and periapical diagnosis, thereby avoiding its influence on the outcome. It helped demonstrate a true indication of OR in such cases. Only mandibular molars were included to eliminate the confounding effect of tooth type on pain. These teeth have restricted access during treatment, possess a complex root canal morphology, and an increased number of possible portals of exit for causing periapical inflammation and pain. The findings of this study could be limited by a relatively limited sample size. Intracanal placement of calcium hydroxide may have potentially influenced the periapical inflammatory process and hence postoperative pain. It was, however, included in the protocol to mimic the clinical practice scenario more closely, where it is an established practice. Further, a recent systematic review has concluded that no clear evidence exists that it may influence the postoperative pain.32 It must be kept in mind that quantifying the pain experienced by the patient is complicated by its subjective nature. The same amount of pain may be perceived differently by different patients. Given the multifactorial nature of pain, isolating the effect of one variable on pain is extremely difficult. It may be worthwhile to further explore the issue using larger sample sizes to confirm or refute these findings. It is also desirable that the efficacy of occlusal reduction in preventing postoperative pain may be assessed in relation to various preoperative factors, such as tooth vitality, preoperative pain, and acute versus chronic apical periodontitis.

Conclusion

Occlusal reduction failed to reduce the postoperative pain to clinically significant levels in asymptomatic mandibular molars with necrotic pulps and apical periodontitis.
