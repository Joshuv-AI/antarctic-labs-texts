Intelligent web archiving and ranking of fake news using metadata-driven credibility assessment and machine learning
Authors: Muhammad Faisal Abrar, Muhammad Saqib, Ali Alferaidi, Tariq S Almuraziq, Raza Uddin, Wilayat Khan, Zawar Hussain Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe explosive growth of online news has intensified concerns over preserving authentic content and combating misinformation. While established preservation frameworks such as the Open Archival Information System (OAIS) and the Web Archiving Life Cycle Model (WALCM) ensure structural durability and access, they lack mechanisms for evaluating the credibility of news before archiving. This critical gap risks embedding misinformation into permanent repositories, thereby distorting historical records and misleading future users. To address this limitation, we introduce the Enhanced Web Preservation Framework (EWPF), which embeds credibility assessment directly into the digital archiving process. EWPF integrates literature-based identification of fourteen key Credibility Factors (CFs), their weighting through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), and the development of a custom dataset of 800 annotated articles. Multiple machine learning models were trained for classification, with Random Forest and Gradient Boosting achieving the highest accuracy (0.99). An ablation analysis further revealed the relative impact of each credibility factor, offering deeper insight into the framework’s robustness. To promote transparency and user engagement, EWPF also includes an interactive interface that visualizes preservation outcomes and dynamic trust scores. Experimental evaluations confirm that EWPF enhances archiving accuracy while remaining computationally efficient and scalable. By linking preservation with credibility analytics, EWPF provides an empirically validated solution for strengthening trustworthy digital journalism in the era of misinformation.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

There is not much that separates one digital news platform from another in terms of access and use. Anyone who wants to send a news article can easily and quickly do so between portable smart devices. In case of emergencies and urgent updates, most digital news platforms result to bundled news with very little time spent in detailed and thoughtful analysis. Most of these news articles also run the risk of extremal issues like link rot, content drift, and loss of a digital scared infrastructure. Estimates suggest that nearly 80% of web pages die within a year, and broken link rot affects more than 13% of scholarly references within 27 months1–3. Archiving tools do exist, and while some describe long-term access challenges as technical, most access and long-term challenges are is of a semantic and informational nature4–6.

The advent of swiftly changing digital platforms of news made much of traditional print formats obsolete very quickly. Print formats, as a news source, are static and do not easily lend themselves to change in access and dissemination speed7. So long as news content is of a single medium curation, validation and the maintenance of a content loom large in the challenge of spaced access. Rapid circulation of automated embedded curation with user contribution and algorithms makes validation and curation difficult8–11.

Existing preservation frameworks such as the Open Archival Information System (OAIS) Reference Model have provided essential guidance for archival storage, ingest, and access protocols6. Similarly, the Web Archiving Life Cycle Model (WALCM) offers a practical operational workflow for selection, capture, storage, and replay12. However, these established models lack formal, embedded mechanisms for evaluating the credibility or trustworthiness of content before archiving. Even systematic approaches to web preservation emphasize methodological consistency in capture and storage but generally assume that editorial judgment or external validation has already been applied13. As a result, current frameworks fall short of integrating automated credibility filtering and ranking—an increasingly urgent requirement given the sophistication and scale of contemporary misinformation.

Credibility in digital news is a multidimensional construct involving factors such as source authority, journalistic integrity, topical relevance, social endorsement, and alignment with verified information. The sophistication of modern misinformation makes it challenging to distinguish reliable news from manipulative or misleading content. If such content is preserved without credibility checks, it risks distorting historical records, biasing AI models trained on archived data, and misleading future audiences. Although prior research has identified numerous credibility signals (e.g., publisher reputation, author profile, content-based cues, social engagement), most efforts operate separately from archiving workflows, leaving a critical gap between preservation infrastructure and credibility assessment.

To address this gap, we propose the Enhanced Web Preservation Framework (EWPF)—a comprehensive system that integrates credibility analytics directly into the digital news archiving process. EWPF is built on four pillars: (i) identification of credibility signals via a Multivocal Literature Review, (ii) principled weighting of these signals using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), (iii) automated classification using machine learning, and (iv) transparent visualization of credibility scores through an interactive interface.

Accordingly, this study aims to develop and evaluate a credibility-aware framework for digital news preservation that integrates automated trust assessment into the web archiving process. Specifically, the objectives are to (1) define and operationalize digital news credibility through a synthesis of academic and grey literature; (2) identify and weight key credibility factors using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP); (3) construct and annotate an 800-article dataset aligned with these factors; (4) develop and evaluate machine-learning models for credibility classification and ablation analysis; (5) implement the Enhanced Web Preservation Framework (EWPF) embedding credibility scoring within the archiving workflow; and (6) design an interactive interface for transparent visualization of trust scores.

Identifying credibility factors through MLR

In a previous research paper, Abrar et al.14, we conducted a Multivocal Literature Review (MLR) in the process of identifying and isolating influential factors that lead to the credibility of online news stories. The MLR process involved reviewing a combination of peer-reviewed scholarly materials and grey literature, including technical reports, blogs, and trade publications, to develop a more in-depth view on the data regarding the measurement and definition of credibility in online news contexts.

The goal of the MLR was to identify thorough, practically applicable, and empirically tested credibility indicators that could serve as the basis for constructing intelligent news filtering and archiving systems. Through stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by thematic analysis, we identified 14 key credibility factors that are broad in their application and recognized in both academic and practice environments, as shown in Table 1.

Independent ground-truth validation

In order to ensure that the appraisal of the Enhanced Web Preservation Framework was not tainted by any form of possible label effects, circularity was prevented by constructing an independent validation corpus because of the usage of the 14 identified Credibility Factors (CFs). This auxiliary data was 200 digital news samples selected in the different topical areas: politics, health, technology, and social issues). Two independent domain experts, who are formally trained in journalism and annotated each article information verification. The disagreements were decided by consensus and the final credibility labels were cross-verified with. These also contain IFCN-approved repositories like PolitiFact and Snopes. This process ensured that that is, all ground-truth labels were not dependent on the CF-derived framework.

Equally, validation corpus underwent the same processing pipeline with the primary dataset which includes data normalization. Aggregated credibility scoring, and AHP-based weighting–care should be taken that the labels of the experts were used exclusively to validate and not for model fitting. This process provided opportunity to evaluate the EWPF without bias in relation to its predictive consistency verified fact-check labels.

Ablation study on the EWPF

To evaluate the individual impact of each CF defined in the canonical mapping (Section Canonical mapping of credibility factors) on the performance of the EWPF, a comprehensive ablation study was conducted. The objective of this analysis is to determine the extent to which each factor contributes to the final credibility score and ranking of digital news stories.

For each of the fourteen CFs discussed above in the MLR and ranked through the AHP, I systematically removed each CF one at a time and held the remaining CFs constant. We then evaluated the modified framework again with the F1-score, a well-acknowledged and authoritative statistic of precision, accurate and recall of classification and ranking frameworks.

Per the definitions of CFs set out in Section Canonical mapping of credibility factors and the transformations and AHP-derived weights, I ensured uniformity across all figures and tables in terms CF-IDs, names, and weights, thus resolving the ambiguities and mismatches I rectified in previous iterations of the experiment.

As illustrated in Table 5, the baseline performance of the EWPF, with all 14 credibility factors included, yielded an F1-score of 0.89. The most significant drop in performance (F1 = 0.09) occurred with the removal of CF10 (Reader’s Feedback) as shown in Fig. 3. This was followed by CF14 (Latest Updates) (F1 = 0.07) and CF12 (Content Creator’s Association) (F1 = 0.07). This implies that crowd-based validation, recentness of information, and author provenance are the most significant components of credibility in the framework proposed.

Development of credibility ranking algorithm

In order to facilitate smart digital preservation involving credibility, we came up with a powerful and understandable algorithm that we refer to as Digital News Credibility and Ranking Algorithm (DNCRA) (see Algorithm 1). The algorithm scores the credibility of each incoming story of news based on 14 proven credibility predictors discovered through our Multivocal Literature Review (MLR) and subject poll. These cues are properties of source credibility, author credibility, linguistic clues and cross-source congruence.

Our Credibility Ranking Algorithm occurs as a problem of multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) where different indicators are required to be normatively combined into a single score, which is policy consistent. We use the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) since it produces expert value trade-offs through a pairwise comparison, produces ratio scale weights that can be inputted to an additive value model, and is governed by inherent governance due to documented judgment matrices and consistency indices. This transparency allows weight perturbation auditability, version control, and principled sensitivity analysis, which are crucial in preservation pipelines that use explicit risk preferences. Conversely, data-based weighting systems like learning-to-rank assume large, high-quality ground-truth rankings; in systems of credibility, labels are limited, noisy or proxy engagement cues which might be miscalibrated with trustworthiness and subject to gameplay. AHP thus provides a theoretically based, explainable and policy controllable, basis of weighting with data-driven approaches as further refinements when vetted labels can be used.

Runtime integration, scalability, performance, and security

Runtime integration

The website operates as a thin client over the EWPF/DNCRA pipeline. Upon ingestion, EWPF extracts Step-9 factors, normalizes them, applies versioned AHP weights, and computes the DNCRA score; both factor values and final scores (with optional explanations) are persisted. The UI issues read-only API calls to these persisted artifacts. The 5-second refresh on designated pages is a lightweight poll of the scored index and is not a server-side full re-estimation, thus maintaining auditability and consistent semantics. All scoring functions are bound to the versioned weight files that allow for rollbacks to ensure stability, meaning when a newly deployed set of weights does not exhibit stable behavior, archivists can seamlessly revert to the last known stable weight set without affecting user access.

Scalability, latency, and concurrent access

The system is deployed as stateless services behind an API gateway, with an ingestion service writing to a durable queue and a scoring service consuming messages. Because online computation is linear in the number of indicators, scaling is primarily I/O-bound; horizontal scaling of scoring workers and read replicas increases throughput while keeping tail latency low. Hot endpoints (Index, Credible/Less-Credible, Ranking) are cached (CDN + in-memory cache) and invalidated on new scores or weight-version changes via pub/sub. Writes use idempotent keys (content hash/news ID) and optimistic upserts; reads are lock-free. Scores are immutable within a weight version, and weight updates trigger background re-scoring with an atomic version flip, providing eventual consistency for the UI and a consistent snapshot for analysis.

From execution traces collected under simulated load (1,000 concurrent users), p95 latency was 280 ms for online credibility scoring and 200 ms for read-API calls, with a sustained throughput of 1,500 requests per second. These results confirm that the system maintains responsiveness at scale while ensuring consistent scoring semantics.

Usability and performance evaluation

We carried out task-based usability walk-throughs focusing on key user experiences (finding credible items, reviewing author rank, and comparing “similar news” clusters), collecting results and qualitative feedback from users that were used to refine navigation and labeling. Performance was evaluated primarily using instrumented API tracing and concurrent load generation to derive page render time, the distribution of API latency, and cache hit ratio on the API over time. When accessed concurrently, median latencies for the read endpoints all stayed under a second, with throughput remaining consistent, and the application introduced no functional regressions. Explainability computations run asynchronously or on user demand to try to keep the online path performant. Incorporating trace-based measurements (p95 latency/throughput) adds confidence to our expectation of system performance in real-world operational conditions under normal operational stress.

Security and integrity

To reduce the risk of adversarial submissions, the ingest path has enforced server-side validation, content type checks, sanitation of user-supplied fields, and rate limiting on records and aggregates. Each public endpoint also supports optional CAPTCHA mechanisms and IP throttling. Each item is content hashed so that duplicate submissions and replay attempts can be detected. Weight files are signed and versioned; administrative actions (e.g., weight updates) use role based access control and are recorded in immutable audit logs. Rollback procedures exist and allow weight files to be restored to a prior version if an anomaly is detected. Integrity of displayed results is derived from serving only persisted, versioned scores; similarity assignments and explanations are computed in server managed background jobs and verified before being exposed to users.

Score and positioning

The website is an example reference implementation made to illustrate the research contribution of credible, explainable ranking for preservation while implementing architectural practices (stateless services, queuing, caching, versioning) that parallel production. This design cleanly connects Section 6 to the paper’s primary research question by simplifying the illustration of how the EWPF is scored, served, and validated in practice without conflating UI issues with core algorithm evaluation.

Conclusion

In today’s digital age, sadly characterized by the rapid spread of misinformation, most current archival systems do not offer explicit, auditable trust appraisal prior to the act of archiving. This allows for low-quality or otherwise misleading content to persist in the archival record. We propose the Enhanced Web Preservation Framework (EWPF), which includes an explainable scoring component (DNCRA) that implements a multivocal literature review into fourteen indicators (i.e. source of content, user feedback, social reputation, and geo-alignment, to name a few) and then aggregates them by Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) weights; in particular, we see that Belonging to the News Place scored highest, while Number of Views scored lowest. Using a pre-compiled corpus of 800 news articles, we calculated credibility scores for each article showing that the commonly used classifiers, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting in particular replicate the AHP thresholding remarkably well on a holdout test set ( 0.99 accuracy). We have also developed a web interface that allows for live visualization and interaction with the ranked articles and scores, demonstrating full end-to-end integration.

We acknowledge limitations. The selected data is fairly limited in scope and geography. Engagement signals (e.g., likes, followers) may hold some measure of bias, whether platform or demographic. Although our results from hold-out sets are promising, labels are also based on the same engagement signals used during inference, which may inflate expectations about generalizability; we still believe it is useful to focus on independent ground truth and testing across different domains. The AHP weights used in our results were elicited from experts and set during the evaluation; while doing so allows for a means of auditability of sorts, the weights will lag if misinformation tactics evolve.

Looking at deployment operations, DNCRA scoring is done on the server side at ingestion with versioned factors and scores persisted to storage, and is read from the storage using APIs optimized for reading, which satifies the OAIS-style architecture by clearly delineating appraisal from storage and access. From a design perspective, the application can horizontally scale, use caching, and handle background re-scoring on a factor weight update, providing near real-time visibility, without reducing the auditability of the scores.

Making generalizations beyond this context will require some extension of the indicators and models. Signals unique to language - for example provenance, geo-alignment, or update history, function fairly easily in most contexts as language-agnostic signals. Signals unique to language typically require multi-lingual NLP processing and assessment by induction per language. In addition, if multimedia (e.g., visuals and audio) is included in DNCRA, visual and audio forensics (e.g., detection of manipulation, provenance, and speech and text alignment files) could be considered additional normalized signals. To capture evidence of evolving misinformations, assembly will involve periodic re-eelication or online calibration of weights under governance, detection of drift, and continuous time-sliced evaluation.

Automated credibility filtering has a variety of ethical and societal ramifications. False negatives can lead to the retention of harmful and misleading information, while false positives can deny the retention of legitimate information. Therefore, we recommend tiered retention (for example, put that content in quarantine/flag it, rather than simply delete it), transparency in public weight versions and criteria for scoring, precise audit logs, audit bias and fairness over time, and providing humans in the loop review in borderline cases, balancing the establishment of credible archives with transparency and accountability.

In conclusion, evidence of the value of EWPF shows that credibility-informed, explainable appraisal can be efficiently embedded into preservation workflows, with manageable costs for operations, and an authentic pragmatic approach to saving credible content for future access and combating misinformation, while also establishing credible technical and moral pathways to establish across languages, types of media and contexts and changing information ecosystems.

Future work

An interesting direction for future research is to further explore the variables generated in this study by exploring their interactions and causal impact on story trustworthiness. Specifically, we will re-elicit AHP weights on a quarterly basis under documented governance and proprietary procedures to measure whether updating weights improves agreement with expert judgments and ranking stability (goal of Kendall’s ; where <10% of the items shift relative to the original ranking by > 5 positions after updating). We will also explore constrained learning-to-rank calibrated by the AHP priors to enhance the additive baseline, again with no loss of monotonicity or fairness (goal of ; expected calibration error ; equal-opportunity difference ) and also explore practical tactics and tools for verification - particularly adding fact-checking signals and provenance checks as additional, normalized, evidence into the scoring pipeline.

To ground these advances, we will build a stratified, multi-region, time-sliced corpus (50k articles from 100 publishers over 12 months) and obtain independent labels via expert annotation and fact-check alignment with double-blind adjudication (target ). Evaluation will move beyond accuracy to macro-F1, ROC-AUC, (), and calibration (Brier/ECE), with variance estimated via repeated stratified splits and bootstrap CIs. To further reduce bias and improve robustness, future evaluations will employ leakage-proof designs, including source- and time-based splits with train-on-past test-on-future setups, and will additionally report confidence intervals and per-group metrics. We will assess transfer across at least three languages (per-language macro-F1 ; vs. monolingual) and extend to multimedia with image/video forensics and speech-text alignment (10k items; macro-F1 ).

In addition, future iterations of this work will leverage fact-check datasets curated by IFCN partners and/or employ expert double-blind annotation of article-level credibility and claim-level veracity. This will enable evaluation that does not depend on engagement or follower counts, thereby mitigating popularity-driven biases and strengthening the robustness of EWPF.

Finally, we will harden robustness, operations, and usability. Adversarial simulations (bot-inflated engagement, coordinated sharing) will test resistance ( under perturbation; 90% detection of synthetic bots). Deployment targets include online scoring ms, read-API ms at 1k concurrent users, and availability of 99.9%. With archivists, we will measure usability (SUS score ) and decision support (20% reduction in time-to-triage). To facilitate replication, we will release transformation functions, weight versions, evaluation scripts, and a de-identified schema subject to licensing constraints.
