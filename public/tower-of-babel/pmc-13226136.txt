Successful use of a percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy system after failed aspiration thrombectomy for acute limb ischemia
Authors: Langford Green, Georges Jreij, Julia Rosenberg, Justin Robbins, Richa Kalsi, Khanjan Nagarsheth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute limb ischemia (ALI) carries significant morbidity, and procedural adaptability is critical when standard endovascular therapies fail. We describe four cases of ALI in which the Artix Thrombectomy System resulted in successful revascularization after aspiration thrombectomy failed. Target limb salvage at 30 days was achieved in all patients. Thromboaspiration in Peripheral Ischemia 2/3 flow was achieved in all patients and 75% maintained primary patency at 30 days. There were no device related complications. These findings support Artix as a safe and effective rescue therapy for ALI and encourage future studies to investigate its utility as a first-line treatment.Keywords: Acute limb ischemia, Peripheral arterial disease, Vascular surgical procedures, Mechanical thrombectomy, Endovascular surgery

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) has been estimated to occur in 1.5 out of 10,000 individuals per year with 30-day death and amputation rates of approximately 15%.1 Disease etiology is typically divided into two main categories: thrombosis and embolism. Thrombosis often occurs at the site of atherosclerotic plaque, although it can occur in previously healthy arteries in patients with thrombophilic conditions or in autogenous or prosthetic bypass grafts. Embolic-induced ALI is typically cardiogenic, as a result of thrombogenic conditions such as atrial fibrillation or left ventricular dysfunction in patients who are not receiving or taking anticoagulation, but can also come from other diseased arteries.2

Although open surgical options have often been considered the gold standard, endovascular approaches have become increasingly favored in recent decades. The type of endovascular intervention selected to treat ALI is based on numerous factors: location of the occlusion, extent of thrombus burden, duration of ischemia, presence of underlying atherosclerotic disease, and patient comorbidities.

The Artix Thrombectomy System (Stryker-Inari Medical) is a novel device that can be used in treating this heterogenous patient population. Since its recent approval by the US Food and Drug Administration, the device has been increasingly adopted as a potential first-line option for endovascular treatment of ALI. At our institution, it has demonstrated particular utility as a rescue option when standard therapies fail.

As such, we report four cases where unsuccessful aspiration thrombectomy was salvaged through successful revascularization with the Artix Thrombectomy System. Informed consent was obtained before publication.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We included any patients in whom the Artix Thrombectomy System was used after aspiration thrombectomy was unsuccessful in completely removing lower extremity arterial occlusions. Data from four patients at our institution were available for inclusion in this report. These procedures occurred between July and September 2025. General procedure

The procedures were performed under local or general anesthesia. Percutaneous access of the contralateral common femoral artery was obtained under ultrasound guidance. A diagnostic aortogram from the aortic bifurcation was performed to assess aortoiliac occlusive disease. Selective angiograms were then obtained to assess disease of the external iliac, femoral, popliteal, and tibial vessels. The aspiration thrombectomy system was advanced into the arterial vessels as indicated and aspiration thrombectomy was attempted. Once the decision to pivot was made, an 0.014” guide wire was advanced into the occluded vessel, the aspiration thrombectomy system and sheath were removed, and the Artix sheath was inserted over the 0.014” guide wire. A covered funnel catheter was passed along through the sheath to provide proximal flow restriction. The mechanical thrombectomy device was then deployed distal to the occlusion and pulled retrograde to mechanically activate and capture and extract the thrombus. The pullback of the mechanical thrombectomy device was repeated several times until arterial flow was restored. Adjunctive procedures, including nitroglycerin administration for vasospasm or percutaneous transluminal angioplasty/stenting for residual atherosclerosis, were performed if needed post Artix, and this decision was made by the attending surgeon. Study outcomes

The primary end point assessed was target limb salvage at 30 days. Secondary end points included technical success, which was defined as Thromboaspiration in Peripheral Ischemia 2/3 flow immediately after the procedure, primary patency at 30 days, all-cause mortality at 30 days, and device-related complications (bleeding, serious adverse events, and distal embolization).

Results

Target limb salvage at 30 days was achieved in all patients. Furthermore, Thromboaspiration in Peripheral Ischemia 2/3 flow was achieved in all patients. Primary patency at 30 days was achieved in three of the four patients. Patient 2 had reocclusion of her ilio-superficial femoral artery (SFA) bypass within 1 week, owing to poor outflow and questionable compliance with anticoagulation, for which she underwent another bypass. There were no device-related complications in any of the patients (Table III).

Patient 1 presented after 1 week of pain and no sensation below her ankle. She underwent successful aspiration thrombectomy of her popliteal and proximal tibioperoneal trunk arteries, but attempts to effectively clear the distal tibioperoneal trunk with the aspiration thrombectomy catheter were unsuccessful. Given the flow-limiting occlusions of the distal tibioperoneal trunk and peroneal arteries, the decision was made to pivot to Artix.

Patient 2 presented with 5 days of ischemic rest pain, a cold right foot, and difficulty bending her toes. Aspiration thrombectomy was attempted in her ilio-SFA bypass, but did not result in meaningful thrombus removal (Figure A). As such, the decision to pivot to Artix was made, which was extremely successful in debulking most of the bypass graft (Figure B, C). There was some degree of pseudointima noted in the bypass, which the Artix system could not capture. Nonetheless, laminar flow was restored and the patient was treated with anticoagulation accordingly, postoperatively.

Patient 3 presented with 3 days of severe right lower extremity pain. Aspiration thrombectomy with a 7F catheter resulted in moderate thrombus removal from the SFA. This device was then downsized to a 6F catheter, which allowed for slightly more thrombus removal from the distal SFA. Ultimately, the catheter was unable to pass beyond a certain point, and the decision to pivot to Artix was made. Mechanical thrombectomy with Artix of the entire popliteal artery and distal SFA was performed and successful in removing the rest of the thrombus.

In patient 4, who presented with a cold left lower extremity 1 day after sudden onset of cramping in her calf and thigh, aspiration thrombectomy of the P3 segment of the popliteal artery, the tibioperoneal trunk, and peroneal artery resulted in meaningful thrombus removal. However, there was still occlusive thrombus in the distal peroneal artery that could not be cleared with the aspiration thrombectomy device. Thus, the Artix device was introduced and was successful in clearing up this occlusion.

Discussion

This case series highlights the utility of Artix as a rescue therapy for thrombus that is more organized as well as thrombus that is in smaller, more distal vessels. The system allows for both mechanical and aspiration thrombectomy and is composed of an 8F thin-walled sheath with a 7F access profile, a mechanical thrombectomy device with an over-the-wire nitinol element and integrated delivery catheter, a covered funnel catheter for proximal flow restriction, and an 8F beveled tip aspiration catheter. The mechanical thrombectomy device can treat vessels ranging from 3 to 8 mm in diameter, allowing for comprehensive treatment of infrainguinal arteries. In cases where there is acute thrombus as well as unrecognized subacute thrombus, the Artix system can capture the acute thrombus with its aspiration component, as well as subacute thrombus with the mechanical component, as has been shown in two of the patients discussed. The wide applicability of this two-in-one thrombectomy system supports its growing role in the endovascular treatment of ALI.

The only reports of Artix in the literature to date show very promising results, with a median thrombus removal of 95% and flow restoration achieved in 96% of the 193 cases included in a multicenter retrospective study3 and a great outcome in a single-patient case report.4 As it becomes more popular in the treatment of ALI, larger studies will be crucial in verifying its safety and efficacy.

This multimodal approach to treating ALI brings up another idea as well. Each endovascular approach has been shown to have its strengths and weaknesses. Given the breadth of endovascular approaches currently available to treat ALI and the heterogeneity of presentation, it is crucial that physicians have access to and proficiency in all these unique approaches. It may be the case that, if one endovascular approach does not successfully restore arterial perfusion, another approach may be required to do so. This, combined with the urgent nature to restore perfusion to prevent irreversible limb loss, demands that physicians should be able to pivot and adapt intraoperatively.

Conclusions

The Artix Thrombectomy System proved effective in achieving revascularization in patients with ALI after aspiration thrombectomy failed, with excellent procedural and technical success. These findings demonstrate its utility as a rescue option when standard endovascular treatments fail and highlight its potential for broader use in the treatment of ALI.
