Despite this progress, developing deep research agents remains difficult in practice due to (1) inefficient open-source data synthesis frameworks, (2) limited availability of high-quality training trajectories, and (3) the high cost of online reinforcement learning, which together hinder scalability, accessibility, and reproducibility in academic research.

In contrast, offline training is constrained by inadequate data synthesis frameworks and datasets. A key bottleneck lies in obtaining diverse seed entities, as most methods depend on well-cleaned and well-structured Wikipedia resources

To solve this, our work systematically addresses the three key challenges described above by providing a comprehensive, fully open-source deep research agent training solution.

First, we introduce
DeepForge
, an end-to-end pipeline for synthesizing deep research tasks through real-world web interaction.
It uses LLM-based prompting and iterative expansion to construct large collections of initial entities and URLs, together with entity graph.
This lightweight design eliminates the need for heavy preprocessing and enables the synthesis of dynamic and diverse tasks beyond static resources such as Wikipedia.

Second, to address the scarcity of academic resources, we release the first comprehensive large-scale deep research dataset to the public, including 66k challenging QA pairs generated by
DeepForge
, 33k SFT trajectories, and 21k DPO preference pairs. This dataset substantially lowers the entry barrier for research in this area.

Third, we demonstrate that
online reinforcement learning is not all you need
to develop enterprise-level offline-trained deep research agents. We present
OffSeeker
, an 8B-parameter deep research agent based on Qwen3-8B
[
30
]
, trained entirely using offline supervised fine-tuning and DPO. Experimental results show that our offline approach matches or even exceeds the performance of prominent online RL systems, for example, 26.6 on BrowseComp-zh, compared with 25.5 for WebSailor-32B, while significantly reducing training costs and providing better reproducibility.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We open-source
DeepForge
, an end-to-end framework for synthesizing large-scale and diverse deep research tasks that require extensive multi-step reasoning and exploration.
The framework is ready to use out of the box and does not require heavy preprocessing.

•

We release the first fully open deep-research resource suite, including the full codebase and dataset: 66k challenging deep research tasks, 33k high-quality SFT trajectories, and 21k DPO preference data, substantially lowering barriers to academic research.

•

We release our offline-trained 8B model,
OffSeeker
, via a fully offline training recipe. It achieves strong deep-research capabilities without online RL, reducing API costs to near zero. Experiments show
OffSeeker
ranks top-tier among comparable-scale agents and rivals 30B systems trained with online RL.

DeepForge
: Large Scale Deep-Research Data Synthesis

Figure 1
:
Overview of our
DeepForge
data synthesis pipeline.
DeepForge
comprises two main stages: (a) Scalable Entity Expansion and (b) Complex Question Generation. After synthesizing complex deep search tasks, we further deploy an agent framework to collect high-quality trajectories.

Existing open-source data synthesis frameworks require extensive preprocessing, such as downloading full Wikipedia dumps or querying the Wikipedia SPARQL API
[
13
,
6
,
14
,
23
,
11
]
. These heavy requirements increase implementation complexity and constrain dataset diversity. Moreover, frameworks from ASearcher and DeepDive rely primarily on fully offline Wikipedia datasets, which cover only a small fraction of real-world web sources. Consequently, these approaches cannot generate dynamic, up-to-date high-quality data.

To address these limitations, we introduce
DeepForge
, a simple yet highly effective pipeline that synthesizes large-scale deep research tasks and is ready to use immediately. We then construct
DeepForge-QA
, a 66k challenging deep search dataset for model training.

2.1
Scalable Entity Expansion

In contrast to existing approaches that rely on extensive preprocessing infrastructures,
DeepForge
initiates the process with a lightweight and scalable entity expansion stage. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we first prompt an LLM to generate a broad pool of initial entities, typically simple nouns that collectively span a wide range of domains. We then iteratively refine this pool into a comprehensive seed entity set through a two-step process: (1) For each initial entity, we query a web search API to collect a set of relevant URLs, thereby constructing a large-scale seed URL corpus; (2) For each seed URL, we extract clean textual content using
html2text
, and prompt an LLM to identify additional entities mentioned within the retrieved text. To ensure that the generated questions encourage genuine research, the LLM is instructed to prioritize the extraction of long-tail and low-frequency entities. Following deduplication and noise filtering, this procedure results in a large, diverse, and high-quality entity pool suitable for constructing multi-hop knowledge graphs.

2.2
Complex Question Generation

Given the expanded entity set,
DeepForge
constructs an entity-centric knowledge graph through multi-step traversal. Starting from each seed entity, a lightweight search agent retrieves both intrinsic attributes and relational links to other entities. Recursively expanding along low-frequency or sparsely documented relations yields an entity graph with rich cross-entity dependencies.

Using this graph as context, we prompt DeepSeek-v3.1
[
12
]
to synthesize questions that require multiple rounds of web search, cross-entity reasoning, and ambiguity resolution. Each question is paired with a gold answer grounded in the retrieved evidence. To further enhance difficulty, we introduce a pruning stage that removes overly specific clues while preserving solvability, intentionally reducing surface-level cues and increasing the depth of reasoning required. Using this pipeline, we construct the query of
DeepForge-QA
, a dataset containing 66k high-difficulty, multi-hop, and high-uncertainty deep search questions. More details can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

3
Methodology

3.1
Overall Agent Framework

We adopt a ReAct-style agent framework
[
32
]
, which explicitly interleaves reasoning and action. The agent’s intermediate reasoning, tool invocations, and tool outputs are represented using
<think>
,
<tool_call>
, and
<tool_response>
tags, respectively. Tool responses are appended to the user context and fed back to the agent as part of the context, enabling iterative interaction with the environment.

Formally, given an initial task query
q
, we denote the agent’s internal reasoning at step
t
as
\tau_{t}
, the action (tool invocation) as
\alpha_{t}
, and the environment feedback as
o_{t}
. The agent iteratively reasons and interacts until producing a final answer enclosed by
<answer>
. A complete interaction trajectory is:

T=\left(q,\tau_{0},\alpha_{0},o_{0},\tau_{1},\alpha_{1},o_{1},\ldots,\tau_{t},\langle\texttt{ans}\rangle\right).

To support efficient deep-research behavior, we equip the agent with four tools:
search
,
visit_urls
,
search_wiki
, and
code_execution
.
More details about the agent framework can be found in Appendix
A.3
.

3.2
Supervised Fine-tuning

SFT Trajectory Construction.

To obtain high-quality step-by-step reasoning trajectories, we built an agent framework with corresponding tools and used proprietary models to sample and obtain trajectories. For each question, the agent interacts with the real Internet environment, performs multi-step exploration, retrieves evidence, and produces a final answer.
Since our constructed data primarily focuses on web search tasks, we include a small portion of open-source datasets containing other task types to mitigate catastrophic forgetting and ensure the model maintains the ability to utilize tools such as code execution.

To ensure the quality, the sampled trajectories are then filtered through a multi-stage quality pipeline that checks for: (1) invalid tool results and API errors; (2) format consistency and structural validity; (3) answer correctness through semantic equivalence evaluation; and (4) overall trajectory quality using LLM-based assessment. After filtering, we obtain
33k high-quality trajectories
for SFT training. Detailed filtering procedures can be found in Appendix
A.4
.

Training Objective.

We perform standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the curated trajectories. Formally, let
\left(x,T\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}
denote a training example, where
x
is the initial task query and

T=\left(q,\tau_{0},\alpha_{0},o_{0},\ldots,\tau_{t},\langle\texttt{ans}\rangle\right)

is the full interaction trajectory containing intermediate reasoning, tool invocations, environment observations, and the final answer. We adopt the standard autoregressive SFT objective, minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth trajectory:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,T\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}}\left[-\sum_{t=1}^{|T|}\log p_{\theta}(T_{t}\mid x,T_{<t})\right]

3.3
Direct Preference Optimization

To address high-cost challenges in online RL, we explore an offline alternative that avoids online interaction altogether. Specifically, we adopt Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
20
]
, a widely used offline RL method that operates purely on preference data.

Preference Data Construction.

We first deploy the SFT-trained model to generate multiple candidate trajectories for each task. Subsequently, a powerful LLM-based evaluator is utilized to assess and assign scores to each trajectory across several dimensions, such as logical consistency, factual correctness, and overall solution quality.

For each task, we select the top two highest-scoring trajectories and the bottom two lowest-scoring trajectories, and pair them exhaustively to form four preference pairs. To ensure high-quality supervision, we further filter the data by retaining only pairs in which the preferred trajectory receives a strictly higher evaluation score than the rejected one. Following this procedure, we construct approximately 21k high-quality preference pairs for DPO training.

Training Objective.

DPO encourages the model to assign higher likelihood to preferred trajectories while remaining close to the reference SFT policy. Let
(T^{+},T^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
denote a preference pair. We define the log-ratio

\Delta_{\theta}(T)=\log\pi_{\theta}(T)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(T),

(2)

where
\pi_{\theta}
is the current policy and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
denotes the frozen SFT reference model. The DPO objective is then given by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(T^{+},T^{-})}\left[\log\sigma\big(\beta(\Delta_{\theta}(T^{+})-\Delta_{\theta}(T^{-}))\big)\right],

(3)

where
\beta
is the hyperparameter that controls the strength of preference optimization.

To assess API overhead and time costs in online RL for training deep research models, we experimented with a SFT-warmup 8B model further refined via online RL.

Experiment Setup.

We used the GRPO

]
algorithm, implemented in the rllm
[

]
framework, on a subset of
DeepForge-QA
.
We tracked (1) model performance on a sampled BrowseComp-ZH subset, (2) cumulative search API costs during training, and (3) per-step training time.

.

Online RL is highly API-costly.

As shown in Figure
3
(middle), GRPO training for 50 steps incurred
\approx\$350
in API costs.

Offline training, by contrast, incurs no API costs, enabling strong deep-research models without search API expenses.

5.2
Main Results

Table
3
summarizes the main evaluation results of
OffSeeker
across six challenging deep-research benchmarks.

Online RL is Not All You Need.

OffSeeker
attains top-tier performance among agents of comparable scale and matches models with up to 30B parameters that are trained using online RL. Across all benchmarks, it delivers consistently strong results, indicating that advanced deep-research capabilities can be realized solely through offline training, and that online RL is not all you need for effective deep-research modeling.
On BrowseComp-zh,
OffSeeker
achieves a score of 26.6, surpassing medium-scale systems such as WebSailor-32B (25.5) and approaching the performance of WebSailor-72B (30.1). On WebWalkerQA, it reaches 61.7, comparable to large foundation models including Claude-4-Sonnet (61.7) and DeepSeek-V3.1 (61.2). Importantly, these results are obtained without any online RL, whereas many competitors depend on extensive and costly online fine-tuning.
Among agents below 10B parameters,
OffSeeker
consistently ranks at the top, significantly outperforming open-source baselines such as ASearcher and DeepDive on BrowseComp-zh, BrowseComp-en, and XBench-DS. All improvements over baseline models are statistically significant (
p<0.05
).

OffSeeker
Can Generalize Effectively.

Although the training data primarily targets deep search tasks,
OffSeeker
exhibits strong generalization across diverse task categories. For example, on the HLE benchmark,
OffSeeker
(DPO) attains a score of 13.8, matching or surpassing models trained with online reinforcement learning, including WebExplorer-8B and ASearcher-Web-QwQ. This result is particularly significant because HLE involves reasoning patterns distinct from standard search tasks. We attribute this success to the model’s ability to internalize high-level complicated problem-solving strategies, rather than merely memorizing search patterns. The consistent performance beyond the training domain underscores the effectiveness of our offline preference-based approach, showing that high-quality preference data can yield robust and transferable reasoning capabilities.

Offline RL Training Achieves Competitive Results.

The comparison between the SFT and DPO variants highlights the benefits of our offline DPO methodology. DPO consistently enhances performance across all benchmarks: +3.9 on GAIA, +2.4 on BrowseComp-zh, +2.2 on BrowseComp-en, +2.1 on HLE, +1.0 on XBench-DeepSearch, and +1.7 on WebWalkerQA.
These consistent gains confirm that advanced deep-research competency can be attained through offline preference optimization.
Moreover, our offline framework incurs no API costs during training, offers greater stability, and provides a cost-efficient paradigm for academic researchers, advancing our goal of enabling accessible development of deep-research agents.

6.2
Effect of Different Context Window Sizes

To assess how context length influences the performance of deep research models, we systematically evaluate
OffSeeker
across GAIA and BrowseComp-ZH, under context windows from 16k to 128k tokens.
As illustrated in Figure

The effect is even more pronounced on the more demanding BrowseComp-ZH benchmark, where accuracy improves from 3.48% to 29.97%. Notably, a 32k context window yields only about 35% of the performance achieved with the 128k setting.
These findings underscore the critical role of extended context capacity in enabling complex, multi-hop reasoning and the integration of dispersed evidence, capabilities essential for advanced research-oriented tasks.

Related Work

7.1
Deep Research Agents

Deep research agents have rapidly emerged as a new frontier in LLMs
[
16
,
24
,
9
,
4
,
18
,
25
]
, delivering strong performance on complex reasoning benchmarks such as GAIA
[
15
]
. A growing body of work focuses on large-scale web-based exploration, exemplified by the Tongyi Deep Research series
[
28
,
11
,
10
]
, WebExplorer
[
13
]
, and ASearcher
[
6
]
, which exhibit advanced browsing and information synthesis capabilities on benchmarks like BrowseComp
[
27
]
.

Most advanced systems heavily rely on online RL to achieve robust performance in deep research tasks. However, these methods are computationally expensive and resource-intensive. In contrast, offline learning remains underexplored, largely due to the limited availability of high-quality data. To address this gap, we introduce the first large-scale deep research SFT and DPO datasets designed to support efficient offline training for deep research agents.

7.2
Deep Search Task Synthesis

Deep search tasks differ fundamentally from standard web-based QA benchmarks such as HotpotQA
[
31
]
and 2WikiMultihopQA
[
7
]
. Unlike conventional benchmarks, these tasks require reasoning under high uncertainty and sustained interaction with real-world search environments. However, high-quality data for deep search is currently scarce. Therefore, it is essential to automatically synthesize large-scale, diverse, and high-fidelity tasks to develop models with advanced web-based reasoning capabilities.
Several frameworks have been proposed to support such automatic synthesis. WebSailor
[
11
]
and WebShaper
[
23
]
focus on enhancing question diversity and uncertainty via fuzzing or set-theoretic composition.
ASearcher
[
6
]
and DeepDive
[
14
]
both use offline Wikipedia data to construct complex research tasks. They employ techniques like LLM agents and knowledge graph walks for complex query generation, and have publicly released their code and synthesized tasks.

Despite these advances, most existing frameworks rely on intricate preprocessing workflows and rarely release complete pipelines or datasets. Here, we present a simple and efficient framework that produces challenging deep search questions directly, without extensive preprocessing. All code and data are fully released to enable transparent and reproducible research.

8
Conclusion

This work addresses three critical challenges in deep research agent development: (1) the lack of efficient open-source data synthesis frameworks, (2) the shortage of high-quality trajectory data, and (3) the prohibitive cost of online reinforcement learning.
We show that online reinforcement learning is not essential for building strong deep research agents by establishing a fully offline and cost-effective training paradigm.
To alleviate data scarcity and training costs, we introduce a fully open-source resource suite:
DeepForge
, a simple yet effective framework for synthesizing large-scale and diverse deep research tasks without heavy preprocessing;
DeepForge-Dataset
, which includes 66k QA pairs, 33k supervised fine-tuning trajectories, and 21k DPO preference pairs; and
OffSeeker
, an 8B-parameter agent trained entirely offline.
Experiments across six benchmarks demonstrate that our offline approach matches or outperforms online RL-based systems, significantly lowering the barrier to developing high-quality deep research agents.

9
Limitation

Our offline approach achieves strong performance, but some limitations remain. Even with a 128k context window, certain complex queries (e.g., in BrowseComp-en) may exceed token limits, hindering full evidence integration; external memory or summarization may help. Additionally, our data primarily targets web search scenarios and does not yet cover domains like scientific literature or multimodal tasks, which we plan to explore in future work.

A.4
SFT Data Filtering Pipeline

To ensure high-quality training data, we adopt a robust filtering pipeline that systematically evaluates each trajectory across five quality criteria. These checks are applied sequentially, and a trajectory is discarded immediately upon failing any step:

Invalid Tool Result Check:
This check identifies trajectories where the result of the current tool call is empty or contains an error message. Such issues are typically caused by external factors (e.g., network or server environment problems) rather than the agent’s use of the tool itself.

Format Error Check:
We verify that every assistant message conforms to the required output format (e.g., all reasoning steps are wrapped in
<think>
tags, tool calls are contained within
<tool_call>
tags with proper JSON formatting).

Token Length Check:
For each trajectory generated by
DeepForge
, we ensure that the tokenized length is within the range of 8,192 to 131,072 tokens. Trajectories shorter than 8k tokens or longer than 128k tokens are filtered out. Short trajectories usually correspond to overly simple tasks that do not require deep research.

Answer Correctness Check:
We utilize an LLM-based evaluator to determine whether the model’s final answer is semantically equivalent to the ground truth. The evaluator is provided with the question, ground truth, and model response, and instructed to assess semantic equivalence while allowing flexibility in terms of formatting, capitalization, word order, and minor linguistic variations. Only trajectories with semantically correct answers are retained.

Overall Quality Check:
Finally, we perform a comprehensive LLM-based quality assessment that examines each trajectory for: (1) tool call hallucinations (falsely claiming successful tool use); (2) mismatches between search keywords and returned results; (3) fabricated search results (claiming retrieval of information that was not actually obtained); (4) process fabrication (presenting plausible reasoning with incorrect underlying logic); (5) cognitive inconsistencies (persisting in incorrect assumptions in the face of contradictory evidence); and (6) logical errors within reasoning chains. The evaluator produces a structured judgment on whether the trajectory meets quality standards, and any trajectory failing this broad review is removed.

A.5
Model Training Details

For supervised fine-tuning, we set the maximum sequence length to 128k tokens to accommodate long-horizon reasoning and multi-step tool interactions. To enable efficient long-context training, we adopt
360-LLaMA-Factory

[
34
]
with sequence parallelism. We use the
cosine_with_min_lr
learning rate scheduler, with an initial learning rate of 3e-5, a minimum learning rate of 1e-7, and a warmup ratio of 0.1. The model is trained for 3 epochs, with global training batch size of 512.

For DPO training, we set the maximum sequence length to 48k tokens. We employ the same
cosine_with_min_lr
scheduler, with a learning rate of 1e-6, a minimum learning rate of 1e-7, and a warmup ratio of 0.1. DPO training is performed for 1 epoch, with global training batch size of 512. We select the results after training 200 steps as the final DPO model.

We determine all the hyperparameters by grid search. All training experiments are conducted on 64 NVIDIA H20 GPUs, each with 80 GB of memory. Detailed training dynamics are shown in Figure
