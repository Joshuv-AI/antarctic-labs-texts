“Making it so more people do research”: leveraging engagement to develop the Equipped to Engage Toolkit for research partners with intellectual disability
Authors: Ariel Schwartz, Katherine McDonald, Madison Brodeur, Karla Ausderau, Karla Ausderau, Kayte S. Barton, Jena Crafton, Shawna Dedonado, Brendan Durkin, Micah Fialka-Feldman, Kami L. Gallus, Karen Heath, Liberty Hicks, Garrett Jones, Jessica M. Kramer, Courtney Anne Krueger, Eric J. ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with intellectual disability have been under-engaged in the conduct of research. When engaged in research, community research partners with intellectual disability can enhance relevance, accessibility, translatability, and validity. While some tools exist to support community research partners with intellectual disability to engage in research, few comprehensively address the full lifecycle of a research study and are customizable to teams’ unique needs, contexts, and research questions.MethodsWe collaborated with a Workgroup of 20 researchers with and without intellectual disability to develop the Equipped to Engage Toolkit. First, we cross walked existing engagement tools to stages of research (planning, doing, and sharing) and evaluated them for accessibility. Second, we developed new tools to fill gaps in accessible tools and comprehensive coverage of research tasks. Third, five pairs of researchers with and without intellectual disability evaluated the toolkit for utility, likelihood of future use, and accessibility.ResultsThe Equipped to Engage Toolkit is an online set of 28 ready-to-use, customizable tools usable across planning (17 tools), doing (8 tools), and sharing (3 tools) stages of research. The tools include plain language, images, breaking tasks into small steps and facilitating community research partner input and study leaders’ responsivity to input. Participants perceived the toolkit to be usable and accessible and endorsed future use.ConclusionsTeams can select and customize tools from the Equipped to Engage Toolkit enhance engagement of community research partners with intellectual disability across the full life cycle of a research study. Further, its accessibility features may be relevant to a wide range of community research partners, including those with low literacy or who are unfamiliar with research.

Background

People with intellectual disability experience disparities in health [1, 6], community participation [7], employment [8], social connections [9, 10] and education [11, 13]. Research has the potential to reduce these disparities. There is recognition that community-engagement in research is an effective approach to addressing disparities [14, 15]. While there has been an increase in the engagement of individuals with intellectual disability in research [16, 19], engagement of this group is sometimes critiqued for lacking robust engagement, due to their more limited decision-making authority and scope of involvement across a full study lifecycle. Limited engagement is often because individuals with intellectual disability are sometimes constrained to roles in which they only advise or consult with researchers, rather than collaborating with researchers or directing research [18, 20]. These critiques are particularly present in quantitative and intervention research [20]—designs particularly common in health and education research and needed for scientifically-driven advances in improved outcomes. For example, research teams may fail to engage stakeholders with intellectual disability in all phases of intervention research or only involve them as advisory board members or data collectors. Or, they may consult people with intellectual disability about accessibility of methods after the methods have been determined, but not engage them in conversations about design decisions and approaches to answering a research question [18, 20]. When meaningful engagement is absent, the promise of community-engaged research to address disparities and improve the lives of people with intellectual disability is compromised.

Community research partners with intellectual disability approach engagement in research from their lived experiences. Some common experiences that may influence how they perceive, evaluate, and engage as research partners include lower educational attainment, lifelong experiences of surveillance, perceived incompetence, less or little autonomy [21, 22]. In addition, people with intellectual disability may have accessibility needs related to literacy, communication, understanding abstract concepts, and learning new information. Finally, each community research partner may have a different set of experiences with research and background knowledge and skills, necessitating their own unique combination of supports and capacity-building to meaningfully contribute to a research team. [23, 26].

Research suggests that community researcher partners’ individual characteristics and shared experiences necessitate customizable tools and processes to ensure they have cognitive and social access to engagement across all stages of research. Engagement is enhanced and sustained when research leaders are responsive to this input [27]; and such responsivity can be scaffolded by effective engagement tools. Research and frameworks, such as the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute’s (PCORI) Foundational Expectations for Research Partnerships [28, 30] further emphasize the importance of tools that support reflective processes that allow for ongoing cycles of engagement, reflection, and evaluation so that engagement can continue to grow.

In recognition of the need for tailored engagement tools for research partners with intellectual disability, many research teams are creating study-specific engagement resources. While these resources support engagement of partners with intellectual disability [31, 32], tools developed for a specific team or research study may not be usable for different research questions, contexts, and team members with different access needs. Further, these study-specific tools are often not readily available for broader adoption. Typically, teams will describe engagement methods but rarely disseminate the materials that supported engagement. Consequently, new research teams invest significant resources to continuously develop resources de novo and/or identify and request permission to use and, as needed, adapt engagement resources from multiple sources for each study they conduct. Furthermore, given barriers to robust engagement across the full span of research stages and activities, tools may be focused on a specific stage of research (e.g., data collection), where community research partners were most involved, leaving gaps in other areas of research partnerships (e.g., dissemination) that threaten actualization of the full promise of community engagement. Finally, tools may not facilitate ongoing reflection and evaluation of engagement, necessary components of continuous improvement.

Here, we describe how we addressed gaps in tools to support the meaningful engagement of community research partners with intellectual disability across all stages of research by developing the Equipped to Engage Toolkit. Our goal was to develop a toolkit that could be used for the full range of research methods utilized across human participants social-behavioral-educational and biomedical research.

Methods

We—two study Co-Leads, a project coordinator, and a 20-member Workgroup of community-engaged researchers with and without intellectual disability—developed the Equipped to Engage Toolkit. The study Co-Leads have a combined > 30 years conducting inclusive research in the United States, the project coordinator has previous experience developing inclusive research tools, and the Workgroup members with and without intellectual disability had experience with at least 1 inclusive research project; many Workgroup members are established leaders in inclusive research. We developed the toolkit by: (1) Reviewing existing engagement resources; (2) Developing and adapting resources; and (3) Evaluating the toolkit.

Workgroup

The Workgroup included 11 researchers with intellectual disability and 9 researchers without intellectual disability who had experience in community-engaged research with research partners with intellectual disability from around the United States. We used a paired approach to identify Workgroup members, in which researchers without intellectual disability identified 1–2 community research partners with intellectual disability to participate. This allowed Workgroup members to receive individualized, local support, as needed, via established research collaborators. We worked together for two years, with only two Workgroup members discontinuing collaboration early; both due to personal reasons. We met remotely, approximately monthly using Zoom. One member with intellectual disability requested to participate asynchronously by providing feedback via email due to availability. Three Workgroup members with intellectual disability attended meetings with a supporter of their choice. As evidence of their varied and equally meaningful contributions, Workgroup members are co-authors.

To enhance access, we offered Workgroup members the opportunity to preview meeting materials with the project coordinator 1-on-1 before group meetings and we sent meeting materials one week in advance of meetings. We used simple language and images to support comprehension in all meeting materials, read all materials aloud, and gave everyone time to think and communicate in their preferred modality. We allocated time for community building by having icebreakers at every meeting and often kept the Zoom room open for informal conversation after the meeting. The team felt that icebreakers, responsiveness to feedback, teamwork, and respect helped everyone feel comfortable expressing their opinions and speaking up when they were confused or something was unclear.

Reviewing existing resources

To identify existing resources, the project coordinator reviewed the descriptions of all resources in the PCORI Engagement Resource Repository (Spring-Summer 2024). She extracted resources made specifically for partners with intellectual disability and those with similar functional and social characteristics, such as developmental disabilities (e.g., autism, cerebral palsy), neurological disorders (e.g., traumatic brain injury), and those with low formal education, literacy, and social power/capital. The project leaders also identified additional resources based on their knowledge of the field and searching databases using terms such as “intellectual disability” OR “developmental disability” AND “inclusive research” OR “community engaged research” OR “patient-engaged research” etc.; these included resources developed in the United States and internationally. The leadership team then mapped the phases of research (planning, doing, and sharing) and which of PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Research Partnerships [28] the tool supported. We selected PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research as a guiding framework, as the expectations address engagement across a study life cycle and ongoing reflection on and evaluation of engagement.

We also evaluated tools for adherence to our guiding design principles—comprehensive, cognitively accessible, and ready-to-use (Table 1). We assessed the mapping of tools to identify activities within each phase of research that engagement tools may support and gaps that existed in resources available to address different research activities (e.g., picking a research question, designing data collection, establishing eligibility criteria, selecting an intervention) or one or more of PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research.

Developing and adapting resources

The Workgroup met to learn about the project goals and reach a shared understanding of the stages of research and PCORI’s Foundational Expectations. We also trialed approaches for giving feedback to ensure our methods were accessible. After three meetings, team members with intellectual disability selected the stage of research they were most interested in working on, and their local research collaborator without intellectual disability joined them in this subgroup. The subgroups then worked on resources for each phase of research (planning, doing, and sharing); each subgroup included 3–4 members with intellectual disability and three members without intellectual disability.

The leadership team drafted initial versions of adapted or new tools for each research task identified based on review of the existing resources. We adhered to accessibility principles, such as simple language, use of images, inclusion of white space, breaking down complex tasks into steps, and defining terms and complex ideas [33, 36, 38]. During Workgroup subgroup meetings, we reviewed the research task a tool was meant to support (e.g., data collection, developing a research question). Then, Workgroup members gave feedback on tools. When possible, we role played using the tool to gather observational information about its accessibility and provide members with experiential insight to inform their reflections on its accessibility and utility.

We used a modified Delphi process [39] to evaluate the tools. After review of each tool and consensus-based agreement of revisions to it, subgroup members evaluated the tool by voting on two questions: (1) Does the tool help research teams work well together (options: Yes, Some changes are needed; No, I’m not sure); (2) Is the tool easy to understand (options: Yes, No, I’m not sure). We discussed any votes other than “yes” to gather information about how the tool could be improved. We then made revisions and voted again until at least 80% of members provided “yes” votes for both questions.

Next, we brought tools to a different subgroup for additional feedback and replicated the voting process. In cases where multiple tools had a similar approach (e.g., similar templates for meeting agendas and meeting notes), we only reviewed one representative tool with the second subgroup. For some tools (e.g., a template for developing a PCORI biosketch, a document required for grant submission; a process for selecting outcome measures), we identified a need for more in-depth experiential review and feedback. We asked pairs of members with and without intellectual disability to use/complete these resources for themselves or a research study on which they work and to provide feedback outside of a meeting.

Evaluating the Equipped to Engage Toolkit

To evaluate the Equipped to Engage Toolkit, we received IRB approval from the Co-Leads’ universities and recruited researchers with and without intellectual disability through professional networks. All researchers must have completed at least one research study as part of a team of people with and without intellectual disability and be able to complete the study activities in English. Together, members of the same team with and without intellectual disability reviewed the Equipped to Engage Toolkit website and 3–4 engagement tools by simulating their use. To ensure a wide range of tools were reviewed by multiple teams, we grouped tools by their main function (e.g., reaching agreement, making complex decisions, supporting meeting engagement, etc.) and assigned tools with similar functions to different pairs of participants based on relevance to their ongoing and past work. Assigning tools in this way ensured participants had relevant background knowledge and experience to draw upon during their evaluation.

After reviewing the Equipped to Engage Toolkit website and tools, each team member completed a survey reporting the extent to which they were easy to use, perceived utility, likelihood of future use, and any accessibility concerns. Participants also had the option to participate in follow-up interviews.

Findings

Reviewing existing resources

We identified 58 resources that support engagement of diverse research partners. The review also helped us identify key engagement processes for which tools may support partner engagement (e.g., communication, planning for data collection, conflict resolution, dissemination decisions, etc.). Many of the resources were embedded within large toolkits or handbooks created by teams while they were conducting a specific study. Nearly all tools were developed prior to the publication of PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research, however, we were able to identify how each tool supported at least one of these expectations. After review of existing resources, we identified that few were intended to be accessible to research partners with intellectual disability1 and usable across diverse research studies and contexts. As we were driven by the goal of developing a toolkit usable across research studies and contexts, we identified that we would need to primarily undertake major adaptations and develop new, accessible tools. We also identified a gap in resources that addressed “doing” research. This included tools to support engagement in data collection and analysis. While there were many tools that addressed the planning stage of research, few addressed proposal development. We also developed tools to address these gaps. We decided to link to research trainings made specifically for research partners with intellectual/developmental disabilities, such as READI [40] and Research Ethics for All [41, 45] into a resources section of the toolkit, in unaltered forms, as they can build capacity and facilitate readiness for engagement.

Developing new resources and adapting resources

The resource review helped us identify specific processes that would require a new resource (e.g., setting up initial group agreements, meeting agendas, etc.) and content that should be included in the resources. To build the Equipped to Engage Toolkit, the Co-leads and project coordinator drafted or adapted 28 tools: 17 planning resources developed over 6 meetings, 8 doing resources developed over 7 meetings, and 3 sharing resources developed over 4 meetings (Table 2). While most tools were developed de novo, three tools represent adaptations of existing resources (Table 2). Subgroups reviewed and voted on drafts of 21 of the 28 tools in the first round of modified Delphi review. Pairs of Workgroup members with and without intellectual disability completed four lengthy and complex tools together and gave feedback based on their experiences applying these tools to their own work. We iteratively refined the tools with the subgroups until all received at least 80% “yes” votes for both evaluation questions. In general, revisions centered on adding more images, defining terms, and including more explicit instructions. Fifteen out of the twenty-one tools that underwent a first round of modified Delphi review also underwent a second round with a different subgroup (tools not reviewed by a different subgroup were conceptually or methodologically similar to those that were). Three additional tools were suggested toward the end of our modified Delphi process, and therefore not reviewed due to lack of time (Travel Accommodations Tip Sheet: Things to Think About for Research Travel, Organizing Research Projects, and Adding the Project to a Resume).

The final tools and website adhere to the accessibility features described above. The Workgroup emphasized three key accessibility features. First, they shared the importance of simple language and grammar supported by images. The Workgroup collaborated to suggest images for abstract concepts and sometimes agreed to not use images when ideas in text were too abstract to comprehensively and accurately represent visually. Second, they suggested that numbering sequential steps can increase accessibility. We therefore numbered steps whenever possible, including in table headers to support users to know the order in which they should complete a table. Third, we learned through working together that examples can support both study leaders and community research partners to understand how to complete the tools. Accordingly, we included examples in the tools.

Equipped to Engage Toolkit website

The full Workgroup met eight times to provide feedback on the overall design and layout of the Equipped to Engage Toolkit, including the website hosting the toolkit. The toolkit is freely available at https://iod.unh.edu/equipped-engage. As tools on the website are organized by both stage of research (planning, doing, and sharing) and PCORI’s six Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research, the Workgroup helped develop plain language descriptions of PCORI’s Foundational Expectations. They also gave feedback on the website organization and how to most easily identify and use tools. Some of the tools include additional instructions and examples. The Workgroup decided that it would be easiest for users to find the information and version they wanted if two versions were made available—one with additional instructions and examples for study leaders to review as they prepare to use the tools (akin to an annotated version of the tool) and another version with minimal additional instructions and no examples that can be immediately printed/downloaded and used with teams. The tools are downloadable as Microsoft files (e.g., Word, PowerPoint), so teams can adapt them as needed for their partners and study.

Equipped to Engage Toolkit evaluation participants

All participants reported the Equipped to Engage Toolkit was easy to use, would support engagement of research partners with intellectual disability, and would recommend the toolkit to other researchers (Table 4). Researchers felt the toolkit would support engagement and overall, commented that it was easy to use. Researchers without intellectual disability shared that the toolkit provided them ideas for engaging their team members, noting, “Tools also provide suggestions for increased inclusion on projects. There are things I hadn’t considered - authorship agreement, adding project to resume,” and “Some topic areas (e.g., emailing) were things that our research team has not thought about yet, but think would be very relevant to our team.” Some researchers also appreciated that the tools were linked to both stages of research and PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research because it gave them the option to find tools based on their needs.

In addition, community research partners with intellectual disability shared that the tools in the toolkit were easy to use. They thought, “it was formatted with easy-to-understand directions” and liked, “how [the tools] support people with intellectual and developmental disabilities.” Overall, they felt that having someone support them to navigate the website was necessary, given the large number of tools. They suggested adding videos to the website explaining each stage of research, and “more pictures of what you are trying to get across for people that uses visuals instead of just reading everything,” and additional instructions for some tools. Community research partners with intellectual disability were interested in having more information about the team that developed the toolkit on the website. They also reported that some tools would benefit from defining some research words (4/15 tools evaluated). We reviewed this feedback with the Workgroup to decide how to incorporate it into the final toolkit. Based on feedback, we added a glossary and added additional directions and examples to some tools.

Discussion

Authentically and comprehensively engaging people with intellectual disability in research holds promise for scientific discoveries that can improve the lives of people with intellectual disability. We engaged research partners with and without intellectual disability and reviewed available engagement resources to create The Equipped to Engage Toolkit. The Equipped to Engage Toolkit is a freely available online collection of tools that support engagement of community research partners with intellectual disability across all stages of research and supports research teams to enact all of PCORI’s Foundational Expectations. Customizability is central to the toolkit, and all tools can be used as-is or customized for teams’ members and studies. While tools are linked to planning, doing, and sharing stages of research, teams may choose to use the tools as relevant for them. Further, to support research teams to meaningfully engage partners, each tool is indexed by PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research.

The Equipped to Engage toolkit can be used by teams just getting started with a research study or to address specific needs within an ongoing research study. While the tools are designed to be accessible to community research partners with intellectual disability, the website is primarily designed for study leaders managing engagement processes. As teams identify a need for support within a research task or to enact a Foundational Expectation, study leaders can select relevant tools. Within Table 5, we also show how adaptations can be made to ensure relevance of tools for a specific research team. We primarily suggest making adaptations to include adding study-specific information and eliminate irrelevant options/information. Study leaders may also adapt materials to increase access by using larger font, printing materials, and/or using study-specific language, and adding relevant background information and examples.

We note that just as planning is critical to ensure rigor of any research study, careful planning also facilitates authentic and sustained engagement. This is reflected in the high number of tools available for the planning stage of research. During planning, team members can establish relationships, team processes, and develop ownership over the research study by influencing key research design decisions. Importantly, because studies and team members’ goals, needs, and capacities may change over time 30, it is critical to re-evaluate plans—perhaps returning to some of the planning tools on a regular basis (e.g., Accommodation checklist, Agreements for working together). This re-evaluation may include attention to use of tools that comprehensively address PCORI’s Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research. As a collective, these foundational expectations (see Table 2) are strong indicators of engagement; when a study does not yet have an identified means of implementing all of these expectations, study leaders may specifically utilize the Equipped to Engage Toolkit to address gaps.

While the Equipped to Engage Toolkit was developed with and for community research partners with intellectual disability, its accessibility features are relevant to a wide range of users. The tools’ step-by-step processes, literacy supports, and design to reduce visual processing and working memory demands [38, 46, 47] enhance access to partners who are new to research, learning English, and/or have other types of disabilities and cognitive support needs. Furthermore, the toolkit can scaffold study leaders’ responsivity to community research partners’ input. Herein, when used, many tools clearly document the ideas and preferences of community research partners and final decisions made for a study. Review of and reflection on how final decisions reflect the input of community research partners can hold study leaders accountable. If study leaders notice that final decisions consistently do not reflect community research partners’ input, they may return to planning tools to reflect on the factors influencing these discrepancies.

Limitations and future research

Our robust engagement process ensured that the Equipped to Engage Toolkit is accessible to a wide range of community research partners with intellectual disability. However, this Workgroup did represent individuals with intellectual disability with previous research experience and individuals who can communicate verbally or with augmentative communication. Therefore, this group and the participants who evaluated the toolkit who had similar experiences and characteristics, do not represent those who are new to being a part of a research team or who have more significant communication support needs. Yet, several Workgroup members and participants did not have experience with some of the tasks we developed the tools for; this was especially true for tools that addressed tasks associated with comparative effectiveness research. Therefore, positive feedback on these tools may suggest they are usable by and accessible to less experienced community research partners. Furthermore, participants evaluating the toolkit did so in a 1-on-1 context, whereas the toolkit is likely to be used in a group setting.

Future research is needed to evaluate if additional supports are needed to complete tools in a larger group, including when there are varied accommodation needs. Additional future work may include evaluating the efficacy of the toolkit not only with community research partners with intellectual disability, but also those with other types of cognitive impairments (e.g., memory loss due to aging, traumatic brain injury, people with serious mental illness, individuals with specific learning disabilities, etc.) and other types of support needs (e.g., low literacy) and exposure to research. Finally, we hope to develop a section of the website that is easily accessible to and meaningful for community research partners so they can more easily direct selection of resources as a team leader.

Conclusions

Availability of engagement tools to foster engagement of research partners with intellectual disability across a study’s full lifecycle may foster deeper, more meaningful engagement, yielding strong impacts on desired research outcomes and scientific discoveries positioned to improve the lives of people with intellectual disability. The Equipped to Engage Toolkit offers research teams composed of researchers with and without intellectual disability customizable, ready-to-use accessible tools that can foster such engagement. Furthermore, the toolkit’s accessible design may enhance engagement for community research partners without intellectual disability who benefit from decreased cognitive demands and have less experience with research. Research teams can select tools to use across a full study lifecycle and to enhance engagement indicators, with intention.
