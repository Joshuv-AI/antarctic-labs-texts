SHARe-KAN: Holographic Vector Quantization for Memory-Bound Inference
Jeff Smith

Abstract
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) face a fundamental memory wall: their learned basis functions create parameter counts that impose extreme bandwidth demands, hindering deployment in memory-constrained environments. We show that Vision KANs exhibit a holographic topology, where information is distributed across the interference of splines rather than localized to specific edges. Consequently, traditional pruning fails (10% sparsity degrades mAP from 85.23% to 45%, a ∼ \sim 40-point drop). To address this, we present SHARe-KAN , a framework utilizing Gain-Shape-Bias Vector Quantization to exploit functional redundancy while preserving the dense topology. Coupled with LUTHAM , a hardware-aware compiler with static memory planning, we achieve 88 × \times runtime memory reduction (1.13 GB → \to 12.91 MB) and match uncompressed baseline accuracy on PASCAL VOC. Profiling on NVIDIA Ampere architecture confirms > > 90% L2 cache residency, demonstrating that the workload is decoupled from DRAM bandwidth constraints inherent to spline-based architectures.



Keywords:
Neural Network Compression, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks, Vector Quantization, Memory-Bound Inference, Cache Optimization, Spline Networks

3.2
The Holographic Hypothesis

We conjecture that information in Vision KANs is stored in the
constructive interference
of overlapping spline bases, analogous to holographic storage systems
[
21
]
.
Consider a layer with
N_{\text{in}}\times N_{\text{out}}
edges, each with a spline
\phi_{ij}(x;\mathbf{c}_{ij})
parameterized by grid coefficients
\mathbf{c}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{G}
.
The output neuron
j
computes:

y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{in}}}\phi_{ij}(x_{i};\mathbf{c}_{ij}).

(1)

Removing any single edge
\phi_{ij}
disrupts the phase relationships across all
N_{\text{in}}
contributions, similar to occluding part of a hologram.

The Synthesizer Metaphor.

The network behaves analogously to a signal synthesizer: the shape codebook represents a shared library of oscillators (basis functions), while the index/gain tables represent the dense wiring diagram (how oscillators combine).

Pruning
cuts the wires, collapsing the output signal (catastrophic);
vector quantization
standardizes the oscillators while preserving the wiring, enabling efficient reconstruction (lossless).

Spectral Evidence.

We perform Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on the spline coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{\text{in}}\times N_{\text{out}})\times G}
, treating each edge’s grid as a row vector.
The spectrum decays rapidly: the top 512 singular values capture 94% of variance, indicating that while the
topology
is dense, the
functional signal
is low-rank.
This aligns with recent large-scale spectral analyses of Transformer weights
[
12
]
, suggesting that the low-rank functional redundancy we exploit via VQ is a fundamental geometric property of deep representations, persisting across architectures.
Rather than removing edges (which breaks interference), we compress the coefficient space by discretizing the functional subspace through vector quantization.

4
SHARe-KAN: Shape-Sharing via Vector Quantization

Motivated by the low-rank structure of spline coefficients (§
3
), we introduce SHARe-KAN (Spectral Hardware-Aware Representation), a post-training compression method that exploits functional redundancy through vector quantization.

4.1
Iso-Latent Scaling via Lookups

A fundamental distinction between MLPs and lookup-based architectures lies in their scaling behavior.
For traditional networks, latency is coupled to parameter count:
\text{Latency}\propto\text{Parameters}
(matrix-vector multiplications scale linearly with weight count).
LUTHAM decouples
capacity
(grid resolution
G
) from
latency
(memory fetch): increasing
G
from 5 to 128 does not increase compute; evaluation is a single index lookup
[x/\Delta x]
and linear interpolation, both
O(1)
operations.

We term this property iso-latent scaling: the model’s representational capacity can be increased without computational penalty, limited only by the
bandwidth wall
(DRAM transfer speed) rather than the
compute wall
(FLOP throughput).
For cache-resident codebooks (as achieved by SHARe-KAN), this means
G
can be selected based purely on accuracy requirements, with negligible latency impact.
Weight-based architectures lack this degree of freedom.

4.2
Gain-Shape-Bias Decomposition

Consider a trained KAN layer with spline grids
\{\mathbf{c}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{G}\}
for
i\in[N_{\text{in}}]
,
j\in[N_{\text{out}}]
.
We decompose each spline into:

\phi_{ij}(x)=g_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{C}[k_{ij}](x)+b_{ij},

(2)

where:

•

\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times G}
is a
layer-wise shared codebook
with
K
entries (learned independently per layer to capture varying frequency characteristics across network depth),

•

k_{ij}\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
is the per-edge codebook index,

•

g_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
is the per-edge gain (amplitude scaling), quantized via logarithmic Int8 to capture high dynamic range,

•

b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
is the per-edge bias (vertical offset).

The original grid
\mathbf{c}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{G}
(e.g., 10 float32 values = 40 bytes for
G=10
) is replaced by:

Per-edge storage

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil\text{ bits}}_{\text{index}}+\underbrace{2\times 8\text{ bits}}_{\text{gain/bias}}

\displaystyle=32\text{ bits}\quad(K=2^{16}).

(3)

The codebook
\mathbf{C}
is shared across all edges within a layer, amortizing its cost.

Training Procedure.

We apply this decomposition post-training via
k
-means clustering:

1.

Normalize all spline grids to zero mean and unit variance:
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}=(\mathbf{c}_{ij}-b_{ij})/g_{ij}
.

2.

Run mini-batch
k
-means on
\{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}\}
to learn codebook
\mathbf{C}
with
K
entries.

3.

Assign each edge to nearest centroid:
k_{ij}=\arg\min_{k}\|\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}-\mathbf{C}[k]\|_{2}
.

4.

Store original statistics
(g_{ij},b_{ij})
as float32 scalars.

Reconstruction quality is measured via coefficient of determination:

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{ij}\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}-\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{ij}\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}\|_{2}^{2}},

(4)

where
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}=g_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{C}[k_{ij}]+b_{ij}
and
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
is the global mean.

4.3
LUTHAM (LookUp Table Hardware-Aware Mapping) Runtime and ExecuTorch Integration

A key property of spline-based KANs is that increasing grid resolution
G
does
not
increase computational cost during inference: evaluation requires a single index lookup
[x/\Delta x]
and linear interpolation, both
O(1)
operations.
The bottleneck shifts to
memory bandwidth
; each forward pass must fetch
G\times E
coefficients from DRAM, where
E
is the number of edges.

Kernel Implementation and Memory Mapping.

Our CUDA kernel implementation maps the quantized codebook
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times G}
to GPU global memory in row-major layout:

•

Codebook:
K\times G
matrix (e.g., 65,536
\times
10) stored contiguously per layer.

•

Format: Linear Int8 per coefficient (codebook), logarithmic Int8 for gains (high dynamic range).

•

Access Pattern: Warp-level coalesced reads with high L2 cache reuse.

At inference, each edge evaluation performs:

y=g\cdot\text{LinearInterp}(\mathbf{C}[k],x)+b,

(5)

where manual linear interpolation (
\sim
5 cycles) replaces texture hardware.
While texture memory units (TMUs) would provide 1-cycle interpolation, large KAN layers exceed CUDA 2D texture limits (65K height); global memory with manual interpolation provides unlimited scalability.
1
1

1

Future work explores 3D texture layouts (
K\times\text{in}\times G
) that fit within 16K
3
limits, reclaiming TMU benefits.

Cache Residency Analysis.

For a detection head with 3.2M edges (compressed from 55M parameters), the per-layer codebook size is:

Codebook Size

\displaystyle=K\times G\times 1\text{ byte}

\displaystyle=65{,}536\times 10\times 1=655\text{ KB}.

(6)

At 655 KB per layer, the codebook fits within the NVIDIA Ampere L2 cache hierarchy (e.g., 40 MB on A100, 4 MB on Jetson Orin).
During inference, all warps reuse the same codebook within a layer, achieving
>
90% L2 hit rate (measured via
nvprof
; see §
5
).

Static Memory Planning and ExecuTorch Integration.

We implement LUTHAM as a custom ATen operator (
torch.ops.share_kan.pli_lookup
) compatible with PyTorch’s ExecuTorch
[
22
]
edge runtime.
The per-layer codebook size (
K\times G\times 1
byte for Int8) is determined at
compile time
during model export, enabling ExecuTorch’s ahead-of-time (AOT) memory planner to allocate fixed buffers (655 KB per layer).
Zero runtime
malloc
calls are guaranteed, which is critical for safety-certified robotics where dynamic allocation is prohibited (e.g., ISO 26262 automotive standards).

Zero-Copy Execution.

The codebook is loaded once at model initialization via
mmap
or CUDA pinned memory, then persists in cache for the lifetime of the inference session.
During forward passes, the kernel directly indexes into this static buffer:

\texttt{output}=\texttt{pli\_lookup}(\texttt{codebook\_ptr},\texttt{idx},\texttt{g},\texttt{b}),

(7)

requiring no data movement.
Dynamic quantization schemes require per-layer dequantization buffers, increasing peak memory by 2–4
\times

[
11
]
.

5.5
Runtime Efficiency & Bandwidth Analysis

We utilize the high-bandwidth NVIDIA A100 as a precision measurement instrument to validate the memory mechanics of LUTHAM.
The goal is not to benchmark inference speed, but to mathematically demonstrate that the kernel is compute-bound (texture units) rather than memory-bound (DRAM).

Breaking the DRAM Speed Limit.

The LUTHAM kernel processes a batch of 1000 images in 3.44 ms (290,000 inferences/second,
3.4\mu
s/sample).
A naive implementation fetching the full uncompressed spline grids (9.4 GB per batch) from HBM would be theoretically bounded by memory bandwidth (1.5 TB/s) to a minimum of
\sim
6.0 ms.
Our measured result of 3.44 ms
violates the DRAM speed limit
, demonstrating that the VQ codebook (12.9 MB) resides in the 40 MB L2 cache throughout inference.
L2 residency decouples compute throughput from off-chip memory bandwidth, confirming Vector Quantization successfully moved the workload from Memory-Bound (DRAM) to Cache-Bound (L2).

Cache Residency as Structural Property.

Since the VQ codebook (12.9 MB per model) fits within the L2 cache hierarchy of the Ampere architecture (40 MB on A100, 4–6 MB on modern mobile SoCs with multi-layer cache), this cache residency property is architectural, not device-specific.
The 88
\times
reduction in required memory bandwidth validates the approach’s suitability for bandwidth-constrained hardware, where the primary bottleneck is memory transfer rather than compute throughput.

While we validate the
memory mechanics
(cache residency) and
throughput scaling
(bandwidth decoupling) on server-grade silicon, we leave specific
power profiling
(Joules/inference) on embedded SoCs to future work.
However, the elimination of DRAM traffic, the primary energy consumer in inference (100–1000
\times
higher cost than on-chip access
[
10
]

We identify the Int8 VQ model as a specialist configuration; it achieves in-domain parity with Float32 VQ on PASCAL VOC (84.74% vs 82.66%, +2.08pp), validating that Int8 quantization acts as beneficial regularization when the target distribution is known.

Error Decomposition.

The ablation decomposes the total 18.6-point COCO generalization gap into:
(1) VQ architecture loss: 3.5pp (59.54% → 56.00%), acceptable for 13
\times

(2) Int8 quantization loss: 15.1pp (56.00% → 40.88%), the dominant bottleneck.

The steeper drop on COCO (40.88% Int8 vs 56.00% FP32) highlights the outlier sensitivity of the logarithmic quantization scheme.
Out-of-distribution features likely generate activation magnitudes that fall into the coarse regions of the Log-Int8 bins, amplifying quantization noise.

Engineering Roadmap.

Future work should pursue adaptive precision schemes (Float16, block-floating point, or per-layer bit allocation) to interpolate between the generalist (56% COCO) and specialist (12.91 MB) configurations.
The codebook architecture itself is robust; improving quantization could recover Float32 VQ’s generalization while maintaining compact memory footprints.
Universal codebooks hold promise for MESH-KAN (Mixture of Experts with Shared-basis KAN) architectures, where thousands of lightweight expert topologies share a single codebook for zero-latency task switching.

Discussion and Conclusion

Vision KANs encode information through the constructive interference of distributed splines, a property we define as holographic topology.
Because this distribution resists localized pruning, we utilize vector quantization to exploit the functional redundancy of the basis set.
Shifting the compression strategy from structural removal to parameter sharing enables an 88
\times
reduction in memory footprint (1.13 GB
\to
12.91 MB) without the accuracy collapse observed in sparse baselines.

6.1
Limitations

Our zero-shot COCO results reveal a trade-off between quantization aggression and out-of-distribution robustness.
While the Float32 VQ model retains 94% of the baseline’s generalization capacity (confirming the robustness of the codebook), the Int8 VQ model suffers a performance drop on domain-shifted data.
This performance drop indicates that while logarithmic Int8 quantization acts as a beneficial regularizer for in-domain tasks, it clips the dynamic range required for novel feature statistics.
Future work on adaptive precision schemes (e.g., block-floating point) could recover this range while maintaining the compact footprint.

The sensitivity of Int8 VQ to out-of-distribution data suggests that the learned splines contain high-frequency components that are hard to quantize logarithmically.
Future work could integrate smoothness regularization methods like LipKAN
[
13
]
during the pre-training phase to produce basis functions that are more amenable to low-bit quantization without accuracy loss.

6.2
Future Directions

The extreme compactness of the SHARe-KAN architecture opens several specific avenues for edge-native deep learning:

Universal Basis Sets.

The current codebook was trained on PASCAL VOC.
Pre-training the codebook on diverse, large-scale corpora (e.g., ImageNet, OpenImages) could resolve domain specialization issues, creating a robust visual dictionary transferable across tasks without re-learning the basis functions.
This approach is strongly supported by the universal weight subspace hypothesis
[
12
]
, which empirically demonstrates that neural networks trained on disjoint tasks naturally converge to shared parameter manifolds, implying that a single, frozen visual dictionary is achievable.

Scalable Mixtures of Experts.

Traditional MoEs are difficult to deploy on the edge due to the bandwidth cost of loading expert weights.
By reducing an expert to a lightweight set of integer indices pointing to a shared, cache-resident codebook, SHARe-KAN theoretically enables systems with thousands of hot-swappable experts that incur near-zero switching latency.

Integration with Hybrid Architectures.

Recent efficient architectures often combine sub-quadratic token mixers (e.g., state-space models, gated convolutions) with dense MLP channel mixers.
SHARe-KAN offers a complementary primitive for these designs: by offloading the channel mixing steps to texture units via VQ lookups, hybrid architectures could maximize utilization of heterogeneous edge silicon (using both NPUs and texture units) to further reduce memory pressure.

Broader Impact.

Reducing neural network memory footprints directly impacts energy efficiency: DRAM access consumes 100–1000
\times
more energy per bit than on-chip SRAM
[
10
]
.
By fitting models in cache memory, SHARe-KAN enables energy-efficient inference on bandwidth-constrained platforms, democratizing deployment of expressive architectures beyond data centers.

Impact Statement

This work targets energy-efficient deployment of neural networks on resource-constrained devices.
Potential societal impacts include:

•

Positive:
Reduced energy consumption for edge AI applications (autonomous vehicles, medical devices, environmental monitoring), lowering carbon footprint and enabling off-grid deployment.

•

Risks:
More efficient models may enable privacy-invasive surveillance systems; we advocate for regulatory frameworks governing edge AI deployment.

We note that our compression technique applies to any B-spline network, including those trained for sensitive applications; practitioners bear the responsibility for ethical deployment.
