Elucidating the potential of Annona muricata L. grown in Sri Lanka to be used in developing an anticancer drug against colorectal and breast cancers
Authors: Onela Canith Pathirana, Madhavi Priyanka Paranagama, Kavindra Kumara Wijesundera, Thilini Anupama Nanayakkarawasam Mahakapuge, Abeykoon Mudiyanselage Anuththara Upamali Abeykoon, Jayantha Rajapakse
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince ancient times many traditional medicine systems around the world have been using different parts of Annona muricata L. (AM), to treat cancer. Indeed, numerous in vitro and in vivo studies also have shown anticancer properties of different solvent extracts of different parts of AM. Even the same part of the plant has shown different levels of anticancer properties based on geographical variations. Therefore, in the present study, the anticancer potential of the leaves, fruit pulp and the fruit peel of the AM that is grown in Sri Lanka was comparatively analyzed with the intention of identifying the most suitable part to be developed into a nutraceutical with anticancer effects.MethodsFreeze-dried aqueous extracts of immature leaves (ILAM), mature leaves (MLAM), pulp (PAM) and peel (PLAM) of AM were analyzed for their antioxidant activity using 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging and 2,2-azinobis-3-ethylbenzothiozoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) cation decolorization assays. Their cytotoxicity on breast cancer (MCF-7) cells, colorectal adenocarcinoma (DLD-1) cells and normal human gingival fibroblasts (HGF-1) were determined by the 3-(4,5- dimethylthiazole-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazoliumbromide (MTT) assay. Their effect on mRNA expression of proapoptotic (Bax and caspase-7) and cell cycle arresting (p21) genes was analyzed by RT- qPCR in the same cell lines.ResultsILAM demonstrated the highest antioxidant activity in both DPPH and ABTS assays followed by MLAM, PLAM and PAM. In the MTT assay, both ILAM and MLAM demonstrated strong cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 and DLD-1 cell lines while there were no cytotoxic effects on the normal human cell line HGF-1. Both ILAM and MLAM demonstrated concentration-dependent upregulation of mRNA expression of cell cycle arresting gene p21 and apoptosis inducing genes Bax and caspase-7 in MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells.ConclusionThe AEAM leaves grown in Sri Lanka has significantly higher antioxidant activity as well as selective cytotoxic effects on MCF-7 and DLD-1 cancer cells compared to its PL and P counterparts. Further, the AEAM leaves induced mRNA expression of the anticancer genes p21, Bax and caspase-7, indicating its potential to be developed into an anticancer drug against breast and colorectal cancer.Keywords: Annona muricata L., Anticancer, Antioxidant, Cytotoxic, Gene expression

Background

Over the past few decades, cancer has become a major global health issue, and its incidence has gradually increased due to many factors, such as unhealthy dietary habits, environmental pollution, and sedentary lifestyles [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022 alone, there were 2.3 million (11.6%) new cases of breast cancer which was ranked the second most common cancer followed by colorectal cancer which was ranked third with 1.9 million (9.6%) new cases worldwide. Also colorectal cancer has caused 900,000 (9.3%) deaths which is the second most common cause of cancer related deaths and breast cancer amount up to 670,000 (6.9%) deaths, which was the fourth commonest cause of cancer related deaths [3].

In Sri Lanka, the landscape of cancer presents several unique challenges, with oral, tracheal, bronchial, lung, colorectal and prostate cancers being prevalent among males, while females commonly experience breast, thyroid, colorectal, uterine and ovarian cancers [4, 5]. This highlights the need for innovative, novel, and region-specific approaches to combat this pervasive condition.

Due to the limitations and side effects of conventional cancer therapies, there is an increasing interest in exploring complementary and alternative treatments to substitute them [6–8]. Herbal remedies, long used in traditional practices, are gaining significant attention because they are time-tested and cost-effective cancer therapies. Secondary metabolites in plants, including alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and phenolics, have been pivotal in the execution of their anticancer effects. Some of the most notable plant-derived anticancer drugs include Paclitaxel (Taxol) from the Pacific yew tree, vinca alkaloids, camptothecin, and podophyllotoxins, all of which have shown significant activity against a variety of cancers [9–11]. The discovery of these compounds underscores the potential of plants as a source of novel anticancer agents that are selective for cancer cells while sparing healthy cells, reducing the side effects typically associated with chemotherapy. Annona muricata (AM), commonly known as Soursop is one of the key herbal remedies against cancer in Sri Lankan traditional medicine [12–14]. Among the numerous phytochemicals in AM, acetogenins and flavonoids have demonstrated outstanding cytotoxic properties on several in vivo and in vitro cancer models. Furthermore, some of the phytochemicals have demonstrated potent antioxidant capabilities [10, 11, 15]. As phytoconstituent profiles can vary based on geographical factors such as climate and soil conditions [16, 17], AM growing in Sri Lanka is worth exploring for their therapeutic efficacy against cancer since this information is underrepresented in existing scientific literature [18].

Generally, pharmacological inhibitors control cancer through inhibiting the progression of cell cycle or induction of apoptosis [19]. The p21 gene, for instance, inhibits cell cycle progression by inhibiting the activity of cyclin dependent kinases (CDK2, CDK1, and CDK4/6 complexes) [20, 21] and arrest the cell cycle at G1 and S phase [20]. Generally, cancer cells evolve numerous mechanisms to evade apoptosis. Expression of proapoptotic genes such as Bax and caspases have been shown to control cancer through induction of cancer cell apoptosis [22]. Bax gene induces apoptosis through intrinsic apoptosis pathway. Caspase-7 gene is involved in cellular apoptosis mediated through either extrinsic or intrinsic pathways since it is an effector caspase [23–25]. Most studies on antioxidant and anticancer activities of AM have used organic solvents, but Sri Lankan indigenous medicinal system relies on water extract of AM. Therefore, this study used water extract of AM to assess antioxidant and anticancer activities of the Sri Lankan cultivar of AM, while focusing on its ability to modulate the expression of p21, Bax and caspase-7 at the mRNA level.

According to current data, breast and colorectal cancers rank among the top five cancer types globally [3]. Therefore, we have focused on the effect of AEAM on MCF-7 and DLD-1 cell lines, which are the corresponding in-vitro cell lines for breast and colorectal cancer, respectively.

Materials and methods

Preparation of plant materials

Preparation of plant materials was carried out in the laboratory of the Department of Veterinary Pathobiology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science, University of Peradeniya. Annona muricata L. (AM) fruits and leaves were collected from Pilimathalawa area in Kandy District, Central province 7˚16′12.00" N, 8˚34′12.00"E) [26], with a tropical climate where the temperature is in the range of 18˚C to 32˚C throughout the year. The plant specimens were authenticated by Mrs. Thilini Muthukuda Arachchi, Assistant Director, National Herbarium, Department of National Botanic Gardens, Peradeniya. A voucher specimen (OCPSP01) of this plant material has been deposited in the National Herbarium, Royal Botanical Gardens, Peradeniya, Sri Lanka, where it is publicly available for reference.

Preparation of plant extracts

Healthy leaves and mature fruits of AM were collected and washed thoroughly with distilled water. Mature (ML) and immature leaves (IL) were carefully separated, and peels (PL) and pulp (P) were manually separated from the fruits [27]. The samples were then air-dried at (25 ± 2 °C) with 65 ± 5% humidity in shade for one week until they reached a constant weight. Subsequently, the dried plant samples were ground in a stainless-steel electric grinder (Panasonic, Japan) into a fine powder and passed through a 0.5 mm mesh sieve. To extract the bioactive compounds, 5 g of the powdered sample were suspended in 30 ml of distilled water and sonicated for 30 min at 40 °C using an ultrasound sonicator (J.P SELECTA, Ng series, Spain). The sonicated solution was then filtered using a Whatman No. 1 filter paper [28, 29] and the resulting filtrates were subjected to centrifugation (Beckman Avanti, UK) at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and freeze-dried in a freeze dryer (Christ-Alpha 1-4LD plus, Germany). The freeze-dried samples were stored in aliquots at -20 °C until analysis [30, 31].

Antioxidant assays

DPPH free radical scavenging assay

1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity of AM extracts (AEAM) were measured as described by Paranagama et al. 2020 [32]. Aqueous extracts of IL (ILAM), ML (MLAM), PL (PLAM) and P (PAM) were dissolved in distilled water to prepare stock solutions. For each sample, 8 different concentrations (0, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 175 and 200 μg/ml) were tested. Briefly, 200 μl of the sample (2000 µg/ml) diluted in 70% methanol was added to 1.8 ml of 1 mM DPPH-methanol solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min at 25 °C. Absorbance was measured at λ = 517 nm in a spectrophotometer (UV-1800 UV–Vis spectrophotometer, Shimadzu, Japan). Three replicates were carried out for each concentration of the sample. The free radical scavenging activity (antioxidant activity) is expressed as the fifty percent inhibition capacity (IC50) which is defined as the quantity of antioxidant needed to neutralize the initial DPPH radical concentration by 50%. For calculation of IC50, the percentage inhibition of absorbance, which were calculated using the following equation were plotted against the concentration of samples to generate graphs.

Regression equations were used to calculate the IC50 of each extract. Even though the DPPH assay results are most frequently expressed as IC50 values, latest findings recommend to express the results as equivalents of a well-known antioxidant standard such as vitamin C since the IC50 is influenced by the initial DPPH concentration used in the assay [33]. Therefore, DPPH assay results were expressed as vitamin C equivalents/ml of extract also. For this purpose, a calibration curve was prepared by plotting percentage inhibition of absorbance against a series of vitamin C concentrations in the range of 0—10 µg/ml. The regression line equation for the calibration curve was y = -0.1528x2 + 14.258x with R2 = 0.9997. This regression line equation was used to calculate the vitamin C equivalent antioxidant capacity of the tested extracts.

ABTS radical scavenging assay

AEAM were analysed by a 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) cation decolourization assay as described by Paranagama et al. 2020 [32]. ABTS radical cation was produced by reacting a 7.4 mM ABTS stock solution with 2.6 mM potassium persulfate (final concentration) solution. The working solution was prepared by mixing the two stock solutions in equal quantities and allowing them to react for 16 h at room temperature in the dark. The solution was diluted by mixing 1 ml ABTS+ solution with 40 ml methanol to obtain an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.02 units at 734 nm. A fresh ABTS+ solution was prepared for each assay. The assay was carried out by allowing AEAM (20 µl) to react with ABTS solution (1980 µl) for 6 min in the dark. Water soluble analogue of vitamin E (Trolox) was used as the standard [34]. The antioxidant capacity was expressed in terms of Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) which was expressed as Trolox equivalents (µmol TE) per gram. For this purpose, percentage inhibitions of absorbance, which were calculated using the following equation were plotted against concentrations of a serial dilution of the Trolox standard (0.4–4.0 µmol/ml) to generate the calibration curve. The regression line equation for the calibration curve was y = 14.528x2 + 177.33x, R2 0.9996. This regression line equation was used to calculate the Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity of the tested extracts.

Culture of human cell lines

Human gingival fibroblasts HGF-1 (ATCC-CRL 2014; passage 3), human breast cancer cells MCF-7 (ATCC HTB-22; passage 6), colorectal adenocarcinoma cells DLD-1 (ATCC CCL-221; passage 15) obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were used in the study. HGF-1 cells were cultured in DMEM–F12 medium (Gibco BRL (USA)) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco BRL (USA)), penicillin/ streptomycin (100 μg/ml) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) [34] and MCF-7 and DLD-1 were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco BRL (USA)) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, penicillin /streptomycin (100 μg/ml) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 96-well cell culture plates and incubated in a humidified, 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

In-vitro cytotoxicity assay using MTT

Cell viability was assessed by 3[4,5dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) dye reduction, as previously described by Riss et al. 2004 [35]. The cytotoxic potential was derived from this assay based on the generated IC50 values. A total of 2 × 105 cells in 200 µl were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained for 48 h before treatment. Afterwards, the cells were treated with ILAM, PLAM, PAM and PLAM extracts for 48 h. Concentration of the extracts used for treatment varied from 3.1–1600 µg/ml. As the negative control, cell culture medium was used. As the positive control, (4000 µg/ml) Bleomycin (Takasaki, Nippon kayaku, Japan) was used [32] since it is a well-known anticancer drug with potent cytotoxic effects against breast [36, 37] and colorectal cancer cells [38].

After 48 h treatment period, cell culture plates were washed with PBS and 10 µL of MTT solution [5000 µg/ml in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] and 90 µL of respective cell culture medium were added to each well and incubated in the dark for 4 h at 37 °C. Afterwards, the purple formazan product formed was centrifuged to obtain a sediment, and the supernatant was discarded cautiously. Finally, 100 µL of DMSO was added to the sediment to solubilize the formazan crystals. The absorbance values were read using an ELISA plate reader (Thermo, Multiskan, USA) at 570 nm. IC50 values (extract concentration resulting in 50% reduction in cancer cell viability) were determined by plotting, percentage cell viabilities against extract concentrations and generating regression equations [39].

Cytotoxic effect on HGF-1, MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells

A concentration gradient of each ILAM, MLAM, PLAM, and PAM were used to identify the cytotoxic effects on cells using the MTT assay [22, 40, 41]. Cells were seeded and grown until they reached 70% confluence in a 96-well plate. After reaching the needed number of cells during the incubation period, which took approximately 48 h, extracts were separately added to the 96-well plate in a twofold dilution concentration series. The plate was incubated for 48 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Afterwards, the media and extracts were removed. Then, MTT assay was carried out as described earlier. As the negative control, cell culture medium was used, and as the positive control, Bleomycin was used.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

AM leaf extract-treated MCF-7, DLD-1 and HGF-1 cells were used to investigate the differences in expression of selected genes. Total RNA was extracted using the Bio &Sell RNA mini kit (Lohweg, Germany) using manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity and quality of extracted RNA were measured using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher, USA). Complimentary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 100 ng of total RNA using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (5X All-In-One RT MasterMix, Canada) following manufacturer’s instructions. Prepared cDNA was stored at -20 °C until further use.

Real-time quantitative RT‒PCR

Gene transcription analysis was performed on a Quant Studio™ 6 Real Time PCR system using Power SYBR™ Green PCR master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For one reaction, 5 µl of Power SYBR™ Green master mix, 0.3 µl each forward and reverse primers (9 µM), 2 µl of template cDNA and 2.4 µl of nuclease-free water in 10 µl of total reaction volume were used.

The mRNA expression of selected target genes, p21, Bax and caspase-7, was determined using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene as the internal control as described elsewhere [42–45]. All samples were run in triplicate. Gene expression was expressed as the relative fold change using the 2 − ΔΔCT method [46].The primer sequences used are listed in Table 1.

Data analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), n = 3 or n = 8. Graph Pad Prism (version 9, San Diego, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel 2021 (version 2108, Roselle, IL, USA) were used for statistical analysis and graphical illustrations. Mean values obtained for each extract were compared using one-way ANOVA, followed by post-hoc Tukey’s test. P values ≤ 0.05 are considered statistically significant.

However, there was no statistically significant difference among the DPPH radical scavenging activities of ILAM, MLAM and PLAM while, the DPPH radical scavenging activity of PAM was significantly lower than those of other extracts. Cytotoxicity assay

AEAM have no significant effect on HGF-1 cell viability

HGF-1 cells treated with a serial dilution of ILAM, MLAM, PLAM and PAM were evaluated for their viability using the MTT assay. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, the percentage cell viability of ILAM treated cells was in the range of 21.15 to 88.75% (highest concentration to the lowest), with the highest cell viability shown at the lowest concentration, which was 3.1 µg/ml. The ILAM demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects only at concentrations ≥ 4000 µg/ml (P ≤ 0.01, 95% CI, 0.04–0.30). In MLAM, PLAM and PAM treated cells, the cell viabilities were in the range of 65.65 to 97.56%, 80.38 to 99.99%, and 71.45 to 99.59%, respectively. The MLAM treated cells also demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects only at concentrations ≥ 4000 µg/ml (P ≤ 0.05, 95% CI, 0.005–0.20) (Fig. 4B), while the PAM treated cells demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects at the concentration of 8000 µg/ml (P ≤ 0.05, 95% CI,0.006–0.34) (Fig. 4D), However, the cytotoxicity of PLAM treated cells did not reach a significant level even at the highest tested concentration of 8000 µg /ml (P ≤ 0.05, 95% CI, -0.006–0.53) (Fig. 4C). Since none of the tested extracts were cytotoxic at a concentration of 2000 µg/ml, we selected a serial dilution starting from 1600 µg/ml for our subsequent experiments on HGF-1 cells to analyse their effect on gene expression.

IC50 values of ILAM and MLAM on MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells

When the IC50 of MLAM and ILAM on MCF-7 cells were compared, the IC50 for ILAM was significantly lower compared to the MLAM (P ≤ 0.01: 95% CI, 0.8–7.0). Similarly, the IC50 for ILAM was significantly lower compared to the MLAM (P ≤ 0.0001: 95% CI, 151.5–157.7) for DLD-1 cells also (Table 2). These findings indicate that ILAM has better cytotoxic effects on both MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells compared to MLAM. However, the IC50 of both ILAM and MLAM on MCF-7 cells were significantly lower compared to those on DLD-1 cells (P ≤ 0.01:95% CI,0.0.7–6.9 and P ≤ 0.0001:95% CI,151.4–157.6, respectively. (Table 2). These findings indicate that both ILAM and MLAM are more cytotoxic on MCF-7 cells than on DLD-1 cells. (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Since ancient times, many civilizations have been using medicinal plants to treat cancer. Indeed, several anticancer agents available for cancer treatment in modern medicine also were initially invented based on the active ingredients extracted from medicinal plants. AM is one of the medicinal plants used in Sri Lankan traditional medicine to treat cancer [47–49]. Our initial experiments on AEAM demonstrated strong antioxidant activity in ILAM and MLAM extracts which were positively correlated with their cytotoxic properties on MCF-7 and DLD-1 cancer cells. Indeed, previous research also have indicated positive correlations between strong antioxidant activity and cytotoxicity in numerous herbal extracts [50]. In this regard, it is worth mentioning that secondary metabolites of AM with strong antioxidant and cytotoxic properties such as acetogenins, flavonoids, and alkaloids, are also found in abundance in leaves when compared to peel and pulp [10]. Therefore, we selected the MLAM and ILAM for subsequent analysis of the potential of AM to modulate the expression of pro-apoptic and cell cycle arresting genes.

Based on the results of the antioxidant assays on AM grown in Sri Lanka, it was found to be a rich source of antioxidants. Generally, if the IC50 value of DPPH assay is less than 50 µg/ml, it is considered to have exceptionally strong antioxidant activity. If the IC50 is between 50 and 100 µg/ml, it is considered to have strong antioxidant activity. If the IC50 value is between 100–150 µg/ml, the antioxidant activity is considered to be moderate, and if the IC50 value is > 150 µg/ml, the compound is considered to have weak antioxidant activity [51]. Accordingly, based on the IC50 values obtained from the DPPH assay for the AEAM grown in Sri Lanka, (Fig. 2), both ILAM and MLAM have a strong antioxidant activity while PLAM has a moderate and PAM has a weak antioxidant activity. The TEAC estimated in the ABTS assay also were comparable to the results of the DPPH assay (Fig. 3). The antioxidant activity of ILAM and MLAM were significantly higher than that of PLAM and PAM. However, there was no statistically significant difference between the antioxidant activity of MLAM and ILAM (P ≤ 0.05). This finding supports the use of AM leaves to manage cancer in Sri Lankan indigenous medicine. Even in contemporary medicine, antioxidants are known to have a tremendous role in preventing the progression of cancer and alleviating the side effects of radiotherapy and chemotherapy in cancer patients [52–54].

Generally, geographical and climatic variations can affect the medicinal properties of plant. When we compared the antioxidant activity of the aqueous extracts of the Sri Lankan variety of AM leaves with that of other countries, the Sri Lankan variety was found to be a better source of natural antioxidants than that from Uganda (IC50 = 907 µg/ml) [55], South Korea (EC50 = 810 µg/ml) [56] and India (> 400 µg/ml) [57].

Similarly, the antioxidant activity of the ethanol extract of AM leaves from Indonesia ((IC50 = 141.127 µg/ml) [51] and Uganda (IC50 = 2045.6 µg/ml) [55] also were lower than that of AM leaves from Sri Lanka. Moreover, the IC50 of the DPPH assay for hexane (312 ± 40 µg/ml), dichloromethane (143 ± 20 µg/ml) and ethyl acetate (136 ± 60 µg/ml) extracts of AM from Turkey also were higher than that of the AM from Sri Lanka. However, the antioxidant activities of the ethanol extract of AM from India (DPPH assay IC50 = 70 µg/ml) and methanol extract of AM from Turkey (DPPH assay IC50 = 63 ± 40 µg/ml) were comparable to the results obtained for the AEAM tested in our study. When we compared the TEAC of the AEAM from Sri Lanka with those reported from other counties revealed that TEAC of hexane extract (222 ± 29 µmol TE/g) and dichloromethane (242 ± 80 µmol TE/g) extracts of AM from Turkey were lower than that of the AM from Sri Lanka while that of the ethyl acetate extract (474 ± 9 µmol TE/g) was comparable and methanol extract (848 ± 11 µmol TE/g) was higher than that of the AM from Sri Lanka. However, the TEAC of the AEAM from Sri Lanka was lower than that of the methanol extract of AM from Turkey (848 ± 11 µmol TE/g) and AEAM from Mexico (17,930 ± 310 µmol TE/g) [58].

Selective cytotoxicity to cancer cells is another important characteristic of anticancer drugs. When we analysed the cytotoxicity of the ILAM, MLAM, PLAM, and PAM of AM from Sri Lanka on a breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) a colon cancer cell line (DLD-1) and a normal human cell line (gingival fibroblasts, HGF-1), ILAM and MLAM were found to be selectively toxic to cancer cells. According to the classification of National Cancer Institute (NCI) of US the cytotoxicity of a compound is high if the IC50 < 20 μg/ml, moderate if IC50 ranged between 21–200 μg/ml, weak if IC50 ranged between 201–500 μg/ml, and no cytotoxic activity if IC50 > 500 μg/ml [59]. According to this classification, both ILAM and MLAM with IC50 values of 2.4 ± 0.44 and 6.3 ± 0.35 μg/ml, respectively have strong cytotoxic activity on MCF-7 breast cancer cell line. In contrast, only the ILAM demonstrated strong cytotoxic activity (IC50 = 6.24 ± 0.38 μg/ml) on DLD-1 cells while the MLAM demonstrated a moderate cytotoxic activity (IC50 = 160.84 ± 0.22 μg/ml). This finding provides further evidence for the potential of leaves of AM growing in Sri Lanka to be developed as an anticancer drug with selective cytotoxic effects on cancer cells at very low concentrations.

Comparison of the cytotoxicity of the MLAM from Sri Lanka with those reported by others revealed that the cytotoxicity of MLAM from Sri Lanka on MCF-7 cells (6.3 µg/ml) is higher than that of a Malaysian variety (IC50 = 221.67 ± 1.67 µg/ml [41]), an Indonesian variety (IC50 = 48.31 µg/ml [40]), and a Mexican variety (IC50 = 2280 µg/ml [60]) on the same cell line. Similarly, a study carried out using MLAM from Malaysia on two other breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 has shown relatively low cytotoxic effects with IC50 values of 350 ± 5.77and 251.67 ± 6.01 µg/ml, respectively, [41] Another study carried out by González-Pedroza has shown that the MLAM from Mexico also has comparatively lower cytotoxic effects on the breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-468 [60] (IC50 = 776.4 µg/ml). According to Gavamukulya et al. 2014, AEAM leaves from Uganda has no cytotoxic effect against breast cancer cell lines EACC and MDA/SKBR3. Even the ethanolic extract of AM has comparatively low cytotoxic activity against the breast cancer cell lines EACC, MDA and SKBR3 with IC50 values of 335.85 µg/ml, 248.77 µg/ml, and 202.33 µg/ml, respectively [55]. In the context of the DLD-1 colon cancer cell line, the cytotoxicity of the AEAM reported in this study (160.84 ± 0.22 µg/ml) could not be compared with those from other countries since they have not tested the cytotoxicity on DLD-1 cells. However, the cytotoxicity of AM from Mexico on another colon cancer cell line HCT-116 (IC50 = 404.8 µg/ml [41]) was lower than that of AM from Sri Lanka. In contrast, the cytotoxicity of the ethyl acetate extract of AM reported from Malaysia on colon cancer cell lines HCT-116 (IC50= 11.43 ± 1.87 µg/ml) and HT-29 (IC50= 8.98 ± 1.24 µg/ml) were found to be higher than that from Sri Lanka.

Even though cancer development is a complex process, its ultimate effect is increasing the rate of cell proliferation and/or reducing the rate of cell death (apoptosis) [21]. Generally, the rate of cell proliferation is tightly regulated by modulating the activity of cell cycle regulatory proteins such as cyclins, cyclin dependent kinases and tumour suppressor proteins such as p53, p21 and pRb. Increased cell proliferation is a consequence of deregulation of the cell cycle. Therefore, induction of genes responsible for cell cycle arrest such as p53, p21 and pRb can exert anticancer effects by reducing the rate of cell proliferation [61]. It is well known that cellular apoptosis is tightly regulated by modulating the expression of Bcl-2 family of proteins. The Bcl-2 family of proteins comprises pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic subfamilies. The proapoptotic subfamily comprises of Bax, Bak, Bid, Bim, Puma, Noxa, Bad, and Blk while the anti-apoptotic subfamily comprises of Bcl-2, Bcl-XL, Bcl-X, and BAG and Bcl-B proteins [62]. Activation of proapoptotic proteins can activate either the extrinsic or the intrinsic signalling pathways of apoptosis. These two pathways converge to a common pathway which is responsible for execution of cell death. Caspases are a family of proteases which play the key role in cellular apoptosis. They are classified into two groups: the initiator caspases, which include caspase- 2, -8, -9 and -10 and the effector caspases, which include caspases-3, -6 and -7 [62]. Reduction in the rate of cell death is a consequence of repression of apoptosis inducing genes. Hence, induction of pro apoptotic genes such as Bax and caspase-7 can mediate anticancer effects through increasing the rate of cancer cell death [61].

Since AEAM (ILAM and MLAM) from Sri Lanka demonstrated the highest antioxidant activity and the highest cytotoxicity on MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells, next we analysed their effect on the expression of the p21 gene which can arrest the cell cycle and the apoptosis inducing genes Bax-1 and a caspase-7 in the same cell lines. According to our results, both ILAM and MLAM demonstrated a dose dependent upregulation in the expression of p21, Bax and caspase-7 in both MCF-7 and DLD-1 cells after 48 h of treatment. Interestingly, a statistically significant increase in the expression of p21 gene was shown at higher concentrations of the ILAM and MLAM (> 400 µg/ml) indicating the potential of ILAM and MLAM to arrest the cell cycle only at higher concentrations.

In MCF-7 cells, the proapoptotic gene Bax demonstrated a significantly increased expression at all tested concentrations only with ILAM. However, MLAM demonstrated a statistically significant increase in expression only at the concentration of 1600 µg/ml. These findings indicate a more potent activation of proapoptotic pathway by ILAM compared to MLAM in MCF-7 cells. However, increase in Bax gene expression was statistically significant at all tested concentrations of ILAM and MLAM in DLD-1 cells indicating a strong activation of the propapotic pathway in the colorectal adenocarcinoma cells. In MCF-7 cells, increase in caspase-7 gene expression was not significant at lower concentrations of ILAM and MLAM. In contrast, in DLD-1 cells, a significant increase in caspase -7 gene expression was observed even at lower concentrations of ILAM and MLAM. This observation indicates that both ILAM and MLAM have more potent effect on caspase-7 mediated cancer cell apoptosis in DLD-1 colorectal adenocarcinoma cells compared to MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Thus, qRT-PCR on p21, Bax and caspase -7 gene expression demonstrated that both ILAM and MLAM has anticancer effects in both DLD-1 and MCF-7 cells. This effect was more evident in DLD-1 cells.

In summary, we have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of AEAM leaves grown in Sri Lanka on breast and colorectal cancers in an in vitro setup. However, this study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential. Specially, in vivo testing is essential to confirm its efficacy, bioavailability, and safety in a physiological context. Further, bioactivity guided fractionation of the AEAM is warranted to identify the specific bioactive compounds responsible for the observed anticancer effects. Moreover, the results of our gene expression studies can be further confirmed by proteomic analysis to facilitate comprehensive understanding of the effect of AEAM on gene expression.

Conclusion

This study reveals remarkable antioxidant activity and selective cytotoxic, proapoptic and cell cycle inhibitory effects of aqueous extract of leaves from A. muricata grown in Sri Lanka on breast and colorectal cell lines. This suggests that AEAM leaves can be a potential source of chemotherapeutic agents for treating breast and colorectal cancers.
