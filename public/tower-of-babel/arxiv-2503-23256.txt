Abstract
. For a fixed, compactly supported probability measure μ \mu on the d d -dimensional space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , we consider the problem of minimizing the p th p^{\mathrm{th}} -power average distance functional over all compact, connected Σ ⊆ ℝ d \Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} with Hausdorff 1-measure ℋ 1 ​ ( Σ ) ≤ l \mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)\leq l . This problem, known as the average distance problem, was first studied by Buttazzo, Oudet, and Stepanov in 2002, and has undergone a considerable amount of research since. We will provide a novel approach to studying this problem by analyzing it using the so-called barycentre field considered previously by Hayase and two of the authors. This allows us to provide a complete topological description of minimizers in arbitrary dimensions when p = 2 p=2 and p > 1 2 ​ ( 3 + 5 ) ≈ 2.618 p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})\approx 2.618 , the first such result that includes the case when d > 2 d>2 .



1.
Introduction

Suppose one has been tasked with constructing a network
\Sigma
of
water pipes for a city in which demand is distributed according to a
given probability measure
\mu
and the construction costs are
modelled by a given functional
{C}(\Sigma)
. Given a fixed
budget
l
, how can one determine the best possible network shape

\Sigma
?

To be more specific, let
\mu
be a probability measure on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where

d\geq 1
. Suppose that for fixed
p\geq 1
, each “client”
x\in\supp\mu
has to pay a price
\mathrm{dist}^{p}(x,\Sigma)
to connect to the
network
\Sigma
. Then, the best network
\Sigma
should be the one
minimizing the
\mu
-average cost

J_{p}(\Sigma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{dist}^{p}(x,\Sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

The choice of different values for
p\geq 1
affects the proportional
weighting of outliers in
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)
, and for us the main case
is
p=2
. The problem may now be stated succinctly as:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\text{Minimize:}\quad&J_{p}(\Sigma)\\
\text{Subject to:}\quad&C(\Sigma)\leq l.\end{cases}

The choice of constraint functional
C
greatly impacts the
qualitative traits of solutions to (
1.1
)
as well as the analysis required to characterize them. Broadly, the
two categories one typically considers are “intrinsic” constraints
such as
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
and “extrinsic” constraints such as

\mathrm{arclength}(\gamma)
, where
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

parametrizes
\Sigma
. We briefly discuss some references for
different formulations in
Section

1.2
.

In this paper we consider the
average distance problem
(ADP),
also called the
irrigation problem
, which was first introduced
by Buttazzo, Oudet, and Stepanov in 2002
[
5
]
:

(ADP)

\begin{cases}\text{Minimize:}\quad&J_{p}(\Sigma)\\
\text{Subject to:}\quad&\Sigma\text{ is compact and connected, and }\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)\leq l.\end{cases}

For the (
ADP
), a longstanding problem of interest—which we
address in this paper—is characterizing the topology of optimizers.
A chronology of progress on the topological characterization problem
can be found in the survey
[
21
]
; let us briefly summarize
the main points.

The first topological characterization result for the (
ADP
)
was obtained by Buttazzo and Stepanov in 2003
[
6
]
. In
that work they proved for
d=2
that if
\mu\ll\mathrm{Lebesgue}

and has a density that is
L^{q}
for some
q>4/3
, then optimizers of
the (
ADP
) are
(topological) finite, binary trees
. That
is, optimizers contain no loops, are comprised of finitely many
“branches” (homeomorphic copies of intervals), and the branches only
meet in triple junctions. In 2004, Paolini and Stepanov proved the
absence of loops in general dimensions
d\geq 2

[
26
, Theorem
5.6]
under only a mild regularity assumption on
\mu
;
namely, that if
\Sigma
is optimal then
\mu(\Sigma)=0
. However,
the finitely-many branches and triple junction results remained
unknown.

In 2006, Stepanov
[
29
]
established the following
“conditional” topological characterization for
d\geq 2
. For fixed

\Sigma
, let
\pi_{\Sigma}
denote any measurable selection of the
closest point projection onto
\Sigma
. Let
\Sigma
be an optimizer,
and suppose (a) that
\mu(\Sigma)=0
, and (b) that

(1.2)

\text{ the pushforward measure }\nu\coloneqq(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu\text{ contains an atom.}

Then, by
[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
,
\Sigma
is a finite, binary
tree.

As we do not know the optimizer a priori, Stepanov’s hypothesis (a) is
effectively equivalent to the condition that

(1.3)

\mu(A)=0
for each set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\mathcal{H}^{1}(A)<\infty.

The hypothesis (
1.3
) is essentially sharp: If

\supp\mu
is a 1-d set and
l\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\supp\mu)
, then
for all optimizers
\Sigma
one has
\supp\mu\subseteq\Sigma
; thus
if
\supp\mu
contains at least one of
\{\text{a loop},\ \text{infinitely-many branches},\ \text{more-than-triple junctions}\}

then
\Sigma
will too.

In this way, Stepanov’s result
[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
reduces
the topological characterization question to proving the existence of
an atom of
\nu
. Such existence seems, intuitively, quite plausible,
since “
\Sigma
has no loops”
[
26
, Theorem 5.6]
implies
“
\Sigma
has endpoints,” and (as noted in the discussion following

[
21
, Lemma 7]
) we would expect those “endpoints” to
receive positive mass in
\nu
.

Despite this intuitive picture, in dimensions
d>2
, the existence
of an atom result has remained elusive in the ensuing years. Hence,
the full topological characterization has remained open—though,
seemingly, tantalizingly close—in dimensions
d>2
.

1.1.
Key Contributions of Our Paper

In this work, we resolve the topological characterization problem in
general dimensions
d\geq 2
for most values of
p\geq 2
, including

p=2
. This is our first main theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(see
Theorem

3.9
for a more
precise statement)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and let
p=2
or
p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})
. Assume
(
1.3
) and let
\Sigma_{\rm opt}
be a solution to
the (
ADP
). Then, (a)
\Sigma_{\rm opt}
does not contain
any loops and is thus a topological tree, (b)
\Sigma_{\rm opt}
has
only finitely many endpoints and branching points, and (c) each
branching point is a triple junction.

Note that part (a) was already proven in
[
26
]
*Theorem 5.6.
Our approach centers on establishing the missing link that turns
Stepanov’s
conditional
result
[
29
]
*Theorem 5.5
into a
general
one: Namely, that for each optimal
\Sigma
, the
measure
\nu
contains an atom (
1.2
).

To obtain this result, the key insight is to view the problem from the
perspective of the
barycentre field

(
Definition

2.9
), which was considered previously by
Hayase and two of the authors in
[
18
]
in studying a
version of (
1.1
) where
C
is
defined via a Sobolev norm. Roughly speaking, the barycentre field is
a vector field on
\Sigma
encoding the “gradient” of the objective

J_{p}(\Sigma)
under continuous perturbations of
\Sigma

(
Proposition

2.11
). Similar ideas to the
barycentre field had appeared in the literature on
(
1.1
) prior to
[
18
]
; see the
discussion in
[
18
, §4.1]
for a brief overview. For the
(
ADP
) in particular, we simply highlight that the first term
of the first variation formula in
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]

coincides for
p=1
with the barycentre field expression in

Proposition

2.11
. However, it seems that
previously, objects like the barycentre field have not been studied
carefully in their own rights, and hence their potential utilities in
analyzing (
1.1
) have not been fully
appreciated.

In our case we show that the barycentre field provides a non-obvious
link between two tractable problems; chaining them together ultimately
yields the existence of an atom result, and hence the topological
characterization. We now explain these two problems, which give the
second and third main theorems.

1.1.1.
Nontrivial barycentre field implies existence of an
atom

Our first step in proving the existence of atoms is to bound the

\nu
-mass of
noncut points
of an optimizer
\Sigma
(i.e. points
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
such that
\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}

is connected) in terms of the
L^{2}(\nu)
norm of the barycentre field.
The intuition is that
[
26
, Theorem 5.6]
implies that
noncut points of optimizers must be endpoints, and we expect endpoints
of
\Sigma
should be atoms for
\nu

[
21
, Discussion following Lemma
7]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(See

Theorem

3.2
for a more precise
statement). Let
p\geq 2
and suppose that
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
is a solution to the (
ADP
) and that
\Sigma

contains at least two points. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
be the
barycentre field of
\Sigma
. Then there exists some constant
C>0

depending only on
l
and
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
such that for all
noncut points
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
we have

\nu\{\sigma^{*}\}|\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}(\sigma^{*})|\geq C\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma).

Remark 1.3
.

Note that every
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
containing at least two
points has at least two noncut points
[
19
, §47 Theorem
IV.5]
, whence

Theorem

1.2
shows that

L^{2}(\nu)
-nontriviality of
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
implies existence of
atoms, and further, that the barycentre field
at
atoms must
be nontrivial.

Notice that
Theorem

1.2
is very
similar to a result first obtained by Buttazzo and Stepanov for
d=2

[
6
]
*Proposition 7.1 and later generalized by Stepanov
to
d\geq 2

[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
. Essentially, they showed
that “if
\nu
has an atom
\sigma_{0}
, then there exists
C>0
such
that for all noncut points
\sigma^{*}
,
\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})>C\nu(\{\sigma_{0}\})
.” The difference is that in

Theorem

1.2
the lower bound is
given in terms of the barycentre field, which is a subtle—but
powerful—conceptual refinement, as we now explain.

Essentially,
Theorem

1.2
allows us
to substitute “
L^{2}(\nu)
-nontriviality of
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
” in
many places where “existence of an atom” is used in the
(
ADP
) literature to characterize optimizers. A typical
argument in this vein proceeds as follows: One supposes that an
optimizer contains some feature, then uses that feature to show there
exists a perturbation recovering
\varepsilon
budget with

o(\varepsilon)
impact on
J_{p}
, and finally reallocates the
recovered budget to the atom in a way that strictly improves
J_{p}
, thus contradicting optimality.

For arguments like this, using
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
is more appropriate
than using atoms of
\nu
, since
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
directly encodes
the first-order variational structure of
J_{p}

(
Corollary

2.16
). And, in the cases where atoms of

\nu
truly
are
the natural objects to study (e.g. when
proving optimizers contain only
finitely
-many branches), as
noted in
Remark

1.3
,
L^{2}(\nu)
-nontriviality of

\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
recovers the existence of an atom anyways. In this
sense,
Theorem

1.2
reveals a
fundamental structure in the problem.

To further illustrate the power of

Theorem

1.2
, let us note how it
almost trivially solves the topological characterization problem in
the
soft-penalty
version of (
ADP
).

1.1.2.
Digression: the soft-penalty problem

Let

\mathcal{S}=\{\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\Sigma\text{ is
compact and connected}\}.

Then for all
\lambda>0
, the
soft-penalty
problem is to find
solutions to

(1.4)

\displaystyle\inf_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}}J_{p}^{\lambda}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\inf_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}}(J_{p}(\Sigma)+\lambda\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)).

It is straightforward to see that every solution of a soft-penalty
problem (
1.4
) is a solution to a hard-constraint
problem (
ADP
): Namely, for fixed
\lambda>0
, a solution

\Sigma_{\lambda}
to (
1.4
) is a solution to the
hard-constraint problem (
ADP
) with

l=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda})
.

Surprisingly, it is unknown (see
[
21
, Remark 22]
and

[
18
, §1.7.4]
) whether the converse holds. We suspect
there are ways that one could prove such equivalence under certain
restrictions on
\mu
; however, discussion of these topics goes beyond
the scope of this paper, and hence we leave it for a future work.

For now, the fact that soft-penalty optimizers are also
hard-constraint optimizers is interesting, because one of our
barycentre field results (
Proposition

2.11
) makes
it almost immediate that soft-penalty optimizers have nontrivial
barycentre fields (
Proposition

1.4
). Chaining

Proposition

1.4
with

Theorem

1.2
then directly implies
the topological characterization (
Corollary

1.5
)
for the soft-penalty problem. The simplicity of this approach stands
in stark contrast with the difficulty of proving the analogy of

Proposition

1.4
in the hard-constraint problem
(
ADP
); see
Section

1.1.3
.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
p\geq 1
, taking the extra hypothesis (
1.3
) in the
case
p=1
. Fix
\lambda>0
and let
\Sigma_{\lambda}
be a
minimizer of the soft-penalty problem (
1.4
).
Suppose that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda})>0
. Then

\Sigma_{\lambda}
has nontrivial barycentre field.

Proof.

Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that
\Sigma_{\lambda}
has
trivial barycentre field. Then by

Proposition

2.11
, the perturbation

\Sigma_{\lambda}\mapsto(1-\varepsilon)\Sigma_{\lambda}
increases

J_{p}
by at most
o(\varepsilon)
, while on the other hand
decreasing
\lambda\mathcal{H}^{1}
by
\varepsilon\lambda\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda})
. So, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small one
obtains
J_{p}^{\lambda}((1-\varepsilon)\Sigma_{\lambda})<J_{p}^{\lambda}(\Sigma_{\lambda})
, contradicting optimality of

\Sigma_{\lambda}
.
∎

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
p\geq 2

and let
\lambda>0
. Let
\Sigma_{\lambda}
be a minimizer of the
soft-penalty problem (
1.4
). Then
\Sigma_{\lambda}

is a (topological) finite, binary tree, as in

Theorem

1.1
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

1.4
,
\Sigma_{\lambda}
has
nontrivial barycentre field. By virtue of being a soft-penalty
solution,
\Sigma_{\lambda}
also solves the hard-constraint problem
(
ADP
) with
l=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda})
. Then, since

p\geq 2
,
Theorem

1.2
implies

\nu\coloneqq(\pi_{\Sigma_{\lambda}})_{\#}\mu
contains an atom.
Applying
[
29
]
*Theorem 5.5 thus yields the topological
characterization.
∎

The above discussion demonstrates that

Theorem

1.2
is an important step
for understanding the structure of optimizers, especially for the
soft-penalty problem. We conjecture that

Theorem

1.2
extends to general
p\geq 1
, whence
Corollary

1.5
would similarly
extend to general
p\geq 1
; this is left for a future work.

Having seen the simplicity of proving the nontriviality result

Proposition

1.4
in the soft-penalty
formulation, let us now return to considering the hard-constraint
problem (
ADP
), for which the result appears much harder.

1.1.3.
The barycentre field is
nontrivial for (
ADP
)

As mentioned in
Remark

1.3
, in order to show existence
of atoms using
Theorem

1.2
one must
prove that the barycentre field is nontrivial. Doing so is easy for
optimizers of the soft-penalty problem (
1.4
), as we
saw in
Proposition

1.4
. However, it seems much
more difficult for the hard-constraint problem (
ADP
). Our
next main theorem addresses this.

Theorem 1.6
.

(See also

Theorem

3.5
). Assume (
1.3
) and
suppose
l>0
, and let
\Sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}
be a solution to
the (
ADP
). Assume
p=2
or
p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})
. Then,

\Sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}
has nontrivial barycentre field.

The idea of the proof is as follows: We suppose, to obtain a
contradiction, that
\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}\rVert_{L^{2}(\nu)}=0
.
Then all
continuous
perturbations of
\Sigma
will impact

J_{p}
by at most
o(\varepsilon)

(
Proposition

2.11
). We exploit this to recover
order
\varepsilon
budget with only
o(\varepsilon)
impact to

J_{p}
and then reallocate the recovered budget via a

non-continuous
perturbation to get a competitor
\Sigma^{*}
.
Careful estimates involving
\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
then show that

J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0
, which contradicts
optimality of
\Sigma
.

Remark 1.7
.

We highlight that in the approach just sketched, the supposition

\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}\rVert_{L^{2}(\nu)}=0
aids not only in
estimating the continuous perturbation, but also in tightening the
estimates for the
non-continuous
perturbation. Note,
a
priori

\mathcal{B}_{\Sigma}
has no compatibility with
non-continuous perturbations, so these tightened estimates are a bit
unexpected. But in any case, they play an important role in making

Theorem

1.6
tractable.

The core ideas of the strategy above were pioneered (though with very
different terminology) by Delattre and Fischer in their work on the
special
p=2
case of the
length-constrained principal curves

problem, which is essentially a parametrization-dependent version of
the (
ADP
). Using these methods, Delattre and Fischer were
able to establish a result
[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
analogous to

Theorem

1.6
for
p=2
; however, as they
specifically noted
[
9
, §1.3]
, they were not able to
show the result for other
p
values.

We found that by (very roughly speaking) “factoring”
J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})
into

(1)

a term that looks like the
p=2
difference
J_{2}(\Sigma)-J_{2}(\Sigma^{*})
, and

(2)

a piece whose precise form (see

Lemma

4.3
) differs for certain
p
regimes,

we were able to analyze part (1) using Delattre and Fischer’s methods,
while still being able to control part (2) via technical analysis
involving the barycentre field (see
Section

4.1
for a
detailed overview). Ultimately, this is what allows us to prove

Theorem

1.6
, whence

Theorem

1.2
gives existence of an
atom, and finally, the topological characterization

Theorem

1.1
follows via
[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
.

Remark 1.8
.

The strange gap in
Theorem

1.6
between

p=2
and
p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})\approx 2.618
, arises
because controlling the “part (2)” factor in the regime
2<p<3
requires controlling a term of the form
\lvert u-v\rvert^{p-2}
, which becomes difficult in the range
p\in(2,3)
.
In the end, we found that even just treating the
p\in(\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5}),3)
regime required quite nontrivial
technical analysis.

Remark 1.9
.

We do not believe that restriction to
p=2
or
p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})\approx 2.618
in

Theorem

1.6
is optimal, and in fact
conjecture that the result should at least hold for
p\geq 2
, and
likely also
p\geq 1
. An approach, which looks quite nontrivial,
is to establish the equivalence of the soft-penalty problem
(
1.4
) and the hard-constraint problem
(
ADP
), and carry the results in
Section

1.1.2

over to the hard-constraint case. We leave this for a future work.

1.2.
Related works and optimal transport interpretation of the
average distance problem

Other variants of the problem (
1.1
) (i.e. using different choices of
C
) have applications in many
different contexts, hence the literature contains a range of both
terminology and conceptual frameworks. We may group them into three
loose families:
principal curves/manifolds

[
14
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
25
,
12
,
3
,
17
,
22
,
9
,
23
,
31
]

(these works typically view (
1.1
) through
a parametrized lens);
the average distance problem

(
ADP
)

[
5
,
6
,
26
,
29
,
4
,
21
]

(which takes the parametrization-independent constraint
\mathcal{H}^{1}
); and an unnamed body of recent works more explicitly informed by
optimal transport (OT)

[
8
,
20
,
7
,
18
]
.
More thorough discussion of most of these frameworks can be found in

[
18
]
; here, we just highlight the connection to OT by
briefly recalling the main ideas. (The interested reader is referred
to
[
30
,
28
,
27
]
for
comprehensive accounts of OT theory).

The goal of typical OT problems is to “deform” a given measure

\mu_{0}
into another given measure
\mu_{1}
in a maximally-efficient
manner. Perhaps the most famous example is the problem of evaluating
the
Monge-Kantorovich
p
-cost
(also called the

Wasserstein
p
-cost
), which is defined by

(1.5)

\mathrm{MK}_{p}^{p}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{dist}^{p}(x_{0},x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x_{0},x_{1}),

where
\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
denotes the set of
transport
plans

\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
first marginal
\mu_{0}
and second marginal
\mu_{1}
. We may interpret

\gamma
as telling us how to “redistribute” the mass at each
x_{0}\in\supp\mu_{0}
so as to yield
\mu_{1}
, or, symmetrically, how to
“redistribute” the mass at each
x_{1}\in\supp\mu_{1}
to yield

\mu_{0}
.

As it turns out, the seminal work on the (
ADP
)

[
5
]
grew out of consideration of a particular OT problem
in which one distinguishes a subset
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

along which transportation of mass is “free.” The question of how to
choose the “best” compact, connected
\Sigma
with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)\leq l
then turned out to be equivalent to the
(
ADP
). A similar perspective was given in
[
18
, Proposition
2.7]
, where an elementary argument showed

J_{p}(\Sigma)=\inf_{\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)}\mathrm{MK}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu^{\prime}).

That is,
J_{p}(\Sigma)
evaluates
\Sigma
by computing the
best possible transport cost between
\mu
and a measure supported on

\Sigma
.

Given these perspectives, the
ADP
’s relationship to

[
7
]
, in particular, becomes clearer. There, those
authors investigated a soft-penalty version of
(
1.1
) with
C(\Sigma)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
, but replacing
J_{p}(\Sigma)
with

\mathrm{MK}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu_{\Sigma})
where
\nu_{\Sigma}
is the uniform
measure on
\Sigma
. We note that in their §8 they list a
“topological characterization” problem analogous to the one we
consider as open in their framework; extending our methods to their
context is an interesting problem which we leave for a future work.

Proposition 2.1

Next, we establish a basic inequality which we will use throughout the

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
p\geq q>0
. Then, for all
a,b\geq 0
, we have

\frac{p}{q}(a^{q}-b^{q})b^{p-q}\leq a^{p}-b^{p}\leq\frac{p}{q}(a^{q}-b^{q})a^{p-q}.

Here we take the convention that
0^{0}=1
when
a=0
or
b=0
.

Proof.

We begin by eliminating some trivial cases. Note that if
a=0
,

b=0
, or
a=b
, then the statement holds trivially. So, suppose
that
a,b>0
and
a\neq b
. If
p=q
, the statement again holds
trivially. Thus, from now on, assume
p>q
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(x)=x^{p/q}
.
Notice that
f
is convex and differentiable, and so for all
x_{0},x_{1}
,

f^{\prime}(x_{0})(x_{1}-x_{0})\leq f(x_{1})-f(x_{0}).

Writing
f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{p}{q}x^{(p-q)/q}
and taking
x_{0}=b^{q},x_{1}=a^{q}
yields

\frac{p}{q}b^{p-q}(a^{q}-b^{q})\leq a^{p}-b^{p}.

Similarly, taking
x_{0}=a^{q}
and
x_{1}=b^{q}
yields

\frac{p}{q}a^{p-q}(b^{q}-a^{q})\leq b^{p}-a^{p},

whence combining these two inequalities yields the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Measurable selection)
.

Let

be a metrizable space,

a compact metrizable space,

a

, and let

where

Then,

is closed in

is lower semicontinuous,
and there exists a Borel-measurable function

Lemma 2.4

(Existence of closest-point

For any compact

Proof.

Define the set

, we claim that

is
closed. Indeed, the map

is continuous,
and thus

is closed. Define

by

Lemma 2.7
.

Let

Lemma 2.8

(Minimizers lie in the convex hull of

\mu
)
.

Assume
(
2.2
). Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
be a

\Sigma\subseteq S\coloneqq\mathrm{ConvexHull}(\supp\mu)
.

Proof.

Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that

.
Since

such

\Sigma\setminus P\neq\emptyset
. By

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{P}(\Sigma\setminus P))<\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma\setminus P),

so
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{P}(\Sigma))<l
. For concision denote

\Sigma_{P}\coloneqq\pi_{P}(\Sigma)
and
\delta\coloneqq l-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{P}(\Sigma))

Since

, we have

(2.3)

\mathrm{dist}(x,\pi_{\Sigma_{P}}(x))\leq\mathrm{dist}(x,\pi_{P}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x)))\leq\mathrm{dist}(x,\pi_{\Sigma}(x));

Next, by (

2.3.
The barycentre field

Now, we are ready to define the barycentre field. The following
definition appeared in
[
18
]
.

Definition 2.9

(Barycentre field)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and nonempty, and let

\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
be a closest-point projection onto

\Sigma
. Let

\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}=(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu

be the
pushforward measure on
\Sigma
. Let
(\{\rho_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\Sigma},\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}})
be the disintegration of
\mu
by

\pi_{\Sigma}
. Then, we define the
barycentre field of

\pi_{\Sigma}
, a vector field along
\Sigma
, by

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)\coloneqq p\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}\{\sigma\}}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))d\rho_{\sigma}(x),\quad\hbox{for $\sigma\in\Sigma.$}

Remark 2.10
.

One may view
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
as a function

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, though it is
often helpful to instead picture a vector with tail
\sigma
and
head
\sigma+\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)
. In any case, as
seen in
[
18
, CE. 4.8]
,
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
can
badly lack regularity, sometimes being discontinuous even when

\Sigma
is a
C^{1}
manifold and
\mu
is uniform. Thus, an
approximation result (
Proposition

2.15
) is necessary to
ensure we can “follow” it and remain in
\mathcal{S}_{l}
.

As mentioned previously, the barycentre field essentially encodes the
“gradient” of the objective functional
{J}_{p}
. This feature
is made precise by the following result, which is an adaptation of

[
18
]
*Theorem 4.8 and Corollary 4.10 to the
non-parameterized context. In fact,
[
18
]
*Theorem 4.8
already implies a stronger result than

Proposition

2.11
, namely that equality holds in

Eq.

2.5
(this justifies us calling the barycentre
field a “gradient”). However, the inequality
(
2.5
) is all that is needed for the purposes of
this paper, hence we give a slightly-simpler, self-contained proof of
this fact.

Proposition 2.11

(The “gradient” of
{J}_{p}
)
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
and let
\xi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be continuous. For
\varepsilon>0
, define

(2.4)

\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi}\coloneqq\{\sigma+\varepsilon\xi(\sigma)\ |\ \sigma\in\Sigma\}=(\mathbf{1}+\varepsilon\xi)(\Sigma).

Suppose
p>1
or that
p=1
and
\mu(\Sigma)=0
.
Then

(2.5)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)}{\varepsilon}\leq\max_{\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}}-\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)\,d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma).

Proof.

Fix
\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
. For concision, for all
x
, let

\sigma_{x}\coloneqq\pi_{\Sigma}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\pi_{\Sigma}(x)+\varepsilon\xi(\pi_{\Sigma}(x)).

Notice that for all
x
we have
\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi}
, so
\lvert x-\pi_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi}}(x)\rvert\leq\lvert x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}\rvert
.
Consequently,

{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|^{p}-|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p}d\mu(x).

Now, taking
a=|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|
,
b=|x-\sigma_{x}|
, and

q=1
in
Lemma

2.2
, we have

|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|^{p}-|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p}\leq p\big(|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|-|x-\sigma_{x}|\big)|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-1}.

We claim that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{p\big(|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|-|x-\sigma_{x}|\big)|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-1}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-p\xi(\sigma_{x})\cdot(x-\sigma_{x})|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-2},

taking the convention that the right hand side is zero when

(x-\sigma_{x})=0
and
p>1
. To that end we proceed by casework.

Case 1:
Suppose
x\notin\Sigma
. Then
\lvert x-\sigma_{x}\rvert\neq 0
, whence
\frac{1}{\lvert x-\sigma_{x}\rvert}
is
well-defined. So,

\displaystyle|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=\Big[(x-\sigma_{x}-\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x}))\cdot(x-\sigma_{x}-\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x}))\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Big[\lvert x-\sigma_{x}\rvert^{2}-2\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x})\cdot(x-\sigma_{x})+\varepsilon^{2}\lvert\xi(\sigma_{x})\rvert^{2}\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\lvert x-\sigma_{x}\rvert\bigg[1-2\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x})\cdot\frac{(x-\sigma_{x})}{|x-\sigma_{x}|}+\varepsilon^{2}\frac{|\xi(\sigma_{x})|^{2}}{|x-\sigma_{x}|^{2}}\bigg]^{1/2}.

Applying the Binomial approximation then yields

\lvert x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}\rvert=|x-\sigma_{x}|-\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x})\cdot(x-\sigma_{x})+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Thus, (
2.6
) holds when

x\notin\Sigma
. It remains to show the result when
x\in\Sigma
.

Case 2:
Suppose
x\in\Sigma
. Then we have two trivial
subcases.

Subcase 2.i:
First, suppose
p>1
. Then
|x-\sigma_{x}|=0
, and so the left side of
(
2.6
) is
0
, whence by
our choice of convention for the right hand side we get the equality
in (
2.6
) as desired.

Subcase 2.ii:
Next, suppose
p=1
. Recall that in this case
we assume the additional hypothesis
\mu(\Sigma)=0
. Thus

\mu
-a.e.
x\not\in\Sigma
, and so the reasoning of Case 1
shows that (
2.6
) holds

\mu
-a.e. and there is nothing to prove.

Since Cases 1 and 2 are exhaustive,
(
2.6
) holds
\mu
-a.e. as claimed.

Now we will conclude by applying dominated convergence with
(
2.6
) to obtain
(
2.5
). For this we must first establish a
uniform bound on the terms inside the limit on the left hand side of
(
2.6
). To that end,
observe that for any
\varepsilon>0
the the reverse triangle
inequality gives
\big\lvert\lvert x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}\rvert-\lvert x-\sigma_{x}\rvert\big\rvert\leq\lvert\varepsilon\xi(\sigma_{x})\rvert
, so

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert\frac{p\big(|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|-|x-\sigma_{x}|\big)}{\varepsilon}|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-1}\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leq p|\xi(\sigma_{x})||x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leq p(\sup\lvert\xi\rvert)\diam(\supp\mu)^{p-1}.

Recalling that
\xi
is continuous,
\Sigma
is compact, and
\mu

is compactly-supported, we see the right hand side is finite, thus
giving the desired uniform bound. Applying the dominated convergence
theorem and the
\mu
-a.e. inequality
(
2.6
) thus gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\xi})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{p\big(|x-\sigma_{x}^{\varepsilon}|-|x-\sigma_{x}|\big)|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-1}}{\varepsilon}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p\xi(\sigma_{x})\cdot(x-\sigma_{x})|x-\sigma_{x}|^{p-2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma),

as was to be shown.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Similarly to the above, the
idea of the proof of the equality case in

[

]
*Theorem 4.8 is to get lower/upper bounds for
the first variation (left side of (

)) by
expanding certain expressions of the form

to

\varepsilon
. In the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
these
first-order terms converge to the right side of
(

The takeaway is that, at least when it comes to regularity,

inherits the flavour of

. Indeed,
the special

is
essentially used to handle the nondifferentiability of

at

. When
p>2
(or when

with an extra
hypothesis), similar ideas may be used to derive a formula for the
second variation, whence for small

we obtain the
expansion

\eqref{eq:fbary-grad-J-min}})+O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Note, the fact
that the error term in this expansion is

(instead of
the coarser

) will inform our intuition for the proof
of the topological description result in
Section

4.1
.

.

Let

, and let
\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}=(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu

We expect that any minimizer

in

.

One of the uses of the barycentre field in studying the (

)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{J(l+\varepsilon)-J(l)}{\varepsilon}\quad\hbox{for $J(l)$ given as in \eqref{eq:jdefinition}.}

The idea behind producing these bounds is as follows. Suppose we are
given an optimizer
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
with nontrivial

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}

\frac{1}{l}
-Lipschitz map

such

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{J(l+\varepsilon)-J(l)}{\varepsilon}

-\alpha||\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu)}
for some
constant
\alpha>0
depending on
\xi
. We will make this idea
precise in

2.15
, but first, let us start with a
density result.

Lemma 2.14
.

Fix any finite measure
\nu
over
\Sigma

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Then the set

\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\{\Xi|_{\Sigma}\mid\Xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ is Lipschitz}\}
is dense in

L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

By analyzing the component functions individually, it suffices to
just prove the
d=1
case, whence we suppress writing the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
’s. To that end, one may first show
C(\Sigma)
is dense in

(L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu),\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu)})
by
fixing
f\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu)
, applying a textbook result like

[
11
, Proposition 6.7]
, and then mollifying to yield a

C(\Sigma)
approximant. Thus it suffices to show

\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
is dense in
(C(\Sigma),\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu)})
. Observe that

||f-g||_{L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu)}\leq\nu(\Sigma)\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}|f(\sigma)-g(\sigma)|.

By the Stone-Weierstrass theorem
\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
is dense in

C(\Sigma)
with respect to the uniform topology, and applying this
to the above inequality yields the claim.
∎

We use this lemma to prove:

Proposition 2.15

(Approximation of

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
)
.

Let
p\geq 1
. Suppose that
\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
has
nontrivial barycentre field. Then, there exists a Lipschitz map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)>\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)>0.

Proof.

First, observe that the right inequality follows immediately from
nontriviality of the barycentre field. For the remainder: Denote

\nu=\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\coloneqq(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu
, the

L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
inner product by
\langle f,g\rangle\coloneqq\int_{\Sigma}f\cdot g\ d\nu
, and the associated

L^{2}(\Sigma,\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. Then
it suffices to find a Lipschitz
\xi
with
\langle\xi,\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rangle>\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert^{2}
.

Observe that we have the uniform bound

|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|\leq\diam(\supp\mu)^{p-1}
. Since
|\nu|<\infty
this gives
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\in L^{2}(\nu)
; in particular
\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert<\infty
. So, pick some
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert
. By

Lemma

2.14
, there exists a Lipschitz
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lVert\xi-\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert<\delta
, and thus

\displaystyle\langle\xi,\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert^{2}-\langle\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}-\xi,\ \mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert^{2}-\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}-\xi\rVert\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert

\displaystyle>\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert^{2}-\delta\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\rVert^{2},

as required.
∎

Corollary 2.16

(Nontrivial barycentre field implies strictly
decreasing optimal value
J
)
.

Suppose
p>1
, or
p=1
and
\mu(\Sigma)=0
for all
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
. Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
, and suppose that

\pi_{\Sigma}
has nontrivial barycentre field. Then, for all
sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, there exists some
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{l+\varepsilon}
such that

{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)<-C\varepsilon,

for some constant
C>0
. In particular, we have that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{J(l+\varepsilon)-J(l)}{\varepsilon}\leq-C<0.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{\Sigma}
has nontrivial barycentre field, by

Proposition

2.15
there exists a Lipschitz map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)>\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)>0.

Let
\eta=(l\,\mathrm{Lip}(\xi))^{-1}
, then the map
\mathbf{1}+\varepsilon\eta\xi
, that is,
\sigma\mapsto\sigma+\varepsilon\eta\xi(\sigma)
, has Lipschitz constant
\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbf{1}+\varepsilon\eta\xi)\leq(1+\varepsilon/l)
, so
\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\eta\xi}=\{\sigma+\varepsilon\eta\xi(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}
satisfies

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\eta\xi})\leq(1+\varepsilon/l)\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)\leq l+\varepsilon.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\eta\xi}\in\mathcal{S}_{l+\varepsilon}
. So, letting

C=\frac{\eta}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)>0,

and applying
Proposition

2.11
, for all
sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
we have

{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\eta\xi})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)<-C\varepsilon.

Taking
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma_{\varepsilon,\eta\xi}
yields the
desired result.
∎

2.4.
Negligibility of the ambiguous
locus

To conclude this section, we will use the barycentre field to
generalize
[

]
*Proposition 3.1, which proves for

that the ambiguous locus of any minimizer of the related

has
\mu
-measure
zero.

We first prove in the next lemma that if the net barycentre field, a
single vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is nonzero, then shifting the whole

J_{p}
. Since the set
\mathcal{S}_{l}
is closed under
translation, this shows that the net barycentre field of any minimizer

Lemma 2.17

(The objective decreases along the net
barycentre field direction)
.

Assume
p>1
, or
p=1
with the extra hypothesis
\mu(\Sigma)=0
. Suppose
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact and
nonempty, and let
\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
. Let

\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma),

and define
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\Sigma+\varepsilon\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
. Then,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)}{\varepsilon}\leq-|\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}|^{2}.

In particular, for any minimizer
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
of the
(
ADP
), we have
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}=0
.

Proof.

Applying
Proposition

2.11
with
\xi(\sigma)=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
immediately yields

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)}{\varepsilon}\leq-\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)=-|\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{net}}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}|^{2},

as desired.
∎

Remark 2.18
.

Observe from the definition of the net barycentre field that

, so rearranging

Consider the special case

. Examination of the integrand in
(

) shows when

, taking the convention

causes no

) reduces to

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[x]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\pi_{\Sigma}(x)]
. This
invariant appeared in
[
9
]
*Remark 2, and plays a key
role in their result
[
9
]
*Proposition 3.1.

Accordingly, by replacing the invariant
[
9
]
*Remark 2
with the conclusion of

2.17
, we are able to generalize

[
9
]
*Proposition 3.1 to the case of
p>1
(or
p=1

with an extra hypothesis).

Proposition 2.19

(Negligibility of the ambiguous
locus)
.

Suppose
p>1
, or
p=1
with the extra hypothesis
\mu(\Sigma)=0
, and let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
be a minimizer of the
(
ADP
). Then
\mu(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})=0
.

Proof.

We first show that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, there exist
well-defined, Borel-measurable functions
P^{(j)}_{0},P^{(j)}_{1}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
(see
Definition

2.5
) such that defining

\pi_{j}
via
(x^{1},\ldots,x^{d})\xmapsto{\pi_{j}}x^{j}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x)
as in
Definition

2.6
we have

\pi_{j}\circ P^{(j)}_{0}(x)=\min\pi_{j}(\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{j}\circ P^{(j)}_{1}(x)=\max\pi_{j}(\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x)).

Fix a
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
; as in

Lemma

2.4
let
D=\{(x,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Sigma\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x)\}
, and note
D
is closed. Define
f:D\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(x,\sigma)=-\pi_{j}(\sigma)
; then
f
is
continuous, and so
Lemma

2.3
yields a
Borel-measurable
P^{(j)}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Sigma
such that for
each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
(x,P^{(j)}_{1}(x))\in D

(whence
P^{(j)}_{1}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
), and

f(x,P^{(j)}_{1}(x))=-\pi_{j}(P^{(j)}_{1}(x))=\min\{-\pi_{j}(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x)\}=-\max\pi_{j}(\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(x)),

as desired. An analogous argument yields the desired
P^{(j)}_{0}

by taking
f(x,\sigma)=\pi_{j}(\sigma)
rather than
f(x,\sigma)=-\pi_{j}(\sigma)
.

Now, by
Lemma

2.17
, for any
\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
(and
in particular, for
P^{(j)}_{0},P^{(j)}_{1}
) the net barycentre field
vanishes. So, using (
2.7
) we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-P^{(j)}_{0}(x)|^{p-2}(x-P^{(j)}_{0}(x))d\mu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-P^{(j)}_{1}(x)|^{p-2}(x-P^{(j)}_{1}(x))d\mu(x)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p-2}(P^{(j)}_{1}(x)-P^{(j)}_{0}(x))d\mu(x),

where the last equality comes from
|x-P^{(j)}_{1}(x)|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)=|x-P^{(j)}_{0}(x)|
.

Finally, suppose for the sake of contradiction that

\mu(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})>0
. Observe that

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\pi_{j}\circ P_{0}^{(j)}(x)<\pi_{j}\circ P_{1}^{(j)}(x)\}.

In particular, since
\mu(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})>0
, then for at
least one

the set

has

. Fix such a

and examine the

everywhere, with the

, since otherwise

and so

. Thus the

component of (

) is

as desired.
∎

3.
Topological properties of average distance
minimizers

In this section, we discuss the topological properties of minimizers
of the average distance problem (
ADP
). Such properties have
been one of the main areas of study related to the (
ADP
)
since its introduction in
[
5
]
. A complete topological
description of minimizers of (
ADP
) was given in

[
6
]
in two dimensions (
d=2
) with
p=1
, where it was
shown that optimal networks contain no loops (i.e. homeomorphic
images of
S^{1}
), have finitely many noncut points, and meet only in
triple junctions. This characterization relies on

[
6
]
*Lemma 7.1, which says that

(3.1)

for
d=2
and any optimizer
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
,
the measure
\nu=(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu
has an atom.

(Here, “atom” means a point
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
with

\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})>0
).

The fact that minimizers contain no loops was later shown to hold in
any dimension
d\geq 2

[
26
, Theorem 5.6]
, and it was
shown in
[
29
]
*Theorem 5.5 that

(3.2)

\hbox{\small for $d\geq 2$ and $p\geq 1$, if
\eqref{eqn:atom-exists} holds, then every noncut point of $\Sigma$
is an atom of $\nu$}.

Property (
3.2
) plays a crucial role in
providing the desired topological description of the minimizers.
However, establishing (
3.1
) (and so
(
3.2
)) for
d>2
proved to be difficult,
being described over a decade ago as an open problem “of great
interest”
[
21
]
.

It turns out that the barycentre field is a very useful tool for
studying when (
3.2
) holds for
d\geq 2
; as
we show in
Section

3.1
, the
property (
3.2
) holds if the barycentre field
of the optimizer is nontrivial. This nontriviality will be shown in

Section

3.2
for all
d\geq 2
when
p=2
or
p>\frac{1}{2}(3+\sqrt{5})
, thus implying
(
3.2
) and consequently topological
description of optimal
\Sigma
for these cases; see

Section

3.3
.

3.1.
Barycentre nontriviality and atomic noncut
points

We show in this section that nontrivial barycentre field implies
(
3.2
): namely, that all noncut points are
atoms.

As a motivation for our argument, we first discuss an idea from the
proofs of
[
6
]
*Proposition 7.1 and

[
29
]
*Theorem 5.5, which show that property
(
3.2
) (namely, that all noncut points are
atoms of
\nu
) follows from property (
3.1
) (the
existence of an atom for
\nu
). Their idea is as follows. Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
be an optimal solution of the (
ADP
), and
suppose we are given an atom
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
of
\nu
, so

\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})>0
. Let
\sigma\in\Sigma
be a noncut point
with
\sigma^{*}\neq\sigma
. By constructing a competitor
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
, we will use the optimality of
\Sigma
to bound

\nu(\{\sigma\})
below by
\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})
. First, we remove a
neighbourhood of radius
\varepsilon
centred at
\sigma
from
\Sigma

to produce a set
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
. This set
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}

recovers
\varepsilon
budget, while only increasing the objective value

{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})
by something proportional to

\varepsilon\nu(\{\sigma\})
. Then, using the fact that
\sigma^{*}
is an
atom, by adding a line segment to
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
at
\sigma^{*}
we
may construct a competitor
\Sigma^{\prime}
to
\Sigma
which is better than

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
by something proportional to
\varepsilon\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})
. So, in order to avoid contradicting the optimality
of
\Sigma
, we must have

\nu(\{\sigma\})\geq C\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})

for some constant
C
depending only on
\sigma^{*}
.

Our idea is to use the barycentre field instead of the atom
\sigma^{*}

to construct a competitor using
Corollary

2.16
. By the
same principle, this will allow us to bound
\nu(\{\sigma\})
in terms
of the barycentre field, thus proving that all noncut points are atoms
if the barycentre is nontrivial. We begin by recalling the following
technical lemma from
[
6
]
*Lemma 6.1.

Lemma 3.1

(Noncut-neighbourhood lemma)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a locally connected metric continuum (i.e. a
compact, connected, and locally-connected metric space) containing
more than one point, and let
\sigma\in\Sigma
be a noncut point
of
\Sigma
. Then, there exists a sequence
\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of open subsets of
\Sigma
satisfying the following
conditions:

(i)

\sigma\in B_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
,

(ii)

\Sigma\setminus B_{n}
is connected for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(iii)

\diam(B_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and

(iv)

B_{n}
is connected for every
n
.

Now, we provide a result that formalizes our discussion about
replacing the atom in the arguments of
[
6
]
*Proposition
7.2 and
[
29
, Theorem 5.5]
with the barycentre field.

Theorem 3.2

(Bounding the mass of noncut
points)
.

Suppose
l>0
.
Let
p\geq 2
and suppose that
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
is an
optimizer and that
\Sigma
contains at least two points. Let

\pi_{\Sigma}\in\Pi_{\Sigma}
and
\nu=(\pi_{\Sigma})_{\#}\mu
.
Then, there exists some constant
\lambda>0
depending only on

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
such that for all noncut points
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
we have

\nu\{\sigma^{*}\}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})|\geq\frac{\lambda}{4l}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma).

Proof.

Observe that if
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)
is trivial
(
Definition

2.13
) then the claim is trivial as
well; hence suppose
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)
is
nontrivial.

Let
\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in

Lemma

3.1
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\diam(B_{n})
. Define
\Sigma_{n}=\Sigma\setminus B_{n}
and let
P_{n}(x)
denote the closest-point projection
onto
\Sigma\cap\partial B_{n}
. We now bound
J_{p}(\Sigma_{n})
by decomposing the domain of integration into two
pieces and bounding them separately. The first will be

\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus\overline{B_{n}})
, on which by
construction for all
x\in\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus\overline{B_{n}})
we have
d(x,\Sigma_{n})=d(x,\Sigma)
. The second
piece will be
\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})
, on which by
construction we have
d(x,\Sigma_{n})=d(x,P_{n}(x))
. In general
|x-P_{n}(x)|\leq|x-P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))|
, so

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus\overline{B_{n}})}\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p}d\mu(x)+\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})}|x-P_{n}(x)|^{p}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus\overline{B_{n}})}\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-(P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))|^{p}d\mu(x).

For the last term, since
p\geq 2
, applying

Lemma

2.2
gives

\displaystyle|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-(P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))|^{p}-|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{2}(|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-(P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))|^{2}-|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2})|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\Big(|P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}-2(P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))\cdot(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))\Big)|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2},

and since for all
x\in\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})
we have

|P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|\leq\varepsilon_{n}
,
defining
M=\diam(\supp(\mu))
and recalling the definition of the
barycentre field
\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
yields

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n})\leq

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-p\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})}(P_{n}(\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))\cdot(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle+\frac{p}{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}M^{p-2}\nu(\overline{B_{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-\int_{\overline{B_{n}}}(P_{n}(\sigma)-\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)+\frac{p}{2}\varepsilon^{2}M^{p-2}\nu(\overline{B_{n}})

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma)+\varepsilon_{n}\int_{\overline{B_{n}}}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|d\nu(\sigma)+\frac{p}{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}M^{p-2}\nu(\overline{B_{n}}).

On the other hand, by
Proposition

2.15
there exists a
Lipschitz map
\xi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(3.4)

\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)>\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma)>0.

Let
L>0
be a Lipschitz constant for
\xi
, and let
\lambda=\max\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{1}{\max|\xi|}\}
; note
\lambda
does not
depend on
\sigma^{*}
. Then
\lambda\xi
is
1
-Lipschitz, and thus

\sigma\mapsto\sigma+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{(l-\varepsilon_{n})}\lambda\xi(\sigma)
is
1+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{l-\varepsilon_{n}}
-Lipschitz.
So,

\Sigma_{n}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\sigma+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}\lambda\xi(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}\}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}.

Now, we want to estimate
{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n}^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n})
in terms of
\int_{\Sigma}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)
. To do this, we
first will estimate the difference

\bigg|\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)-\int_{\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}d\nu(\sigma)\bigg|,

where we recall that
\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}\coloneqq(\pi_{\Sigma_{n}})_{\#}\mu
.

By
Proposition

2.19
we have
\mu(\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma})=0
, and so for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})
we get
\pi_{\Sigma}(x)=\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}(x)
. For the
x\in\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\overline{B_{n}})
,
note
\Sigma_{n}\subseteq\Sigma
gives the uniform bound

|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}(x)|\leq\mathrm{diam}(\Sigma)\leq l.

Next, note that regardless of the choices of
\Sigma
and
x
we get

|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(x)|\leq pM^{p-1}
. So the Lipschitz
condition on
\xi
gives

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)-\int_{\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}d\nu(\sigma)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq pLM^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}(x)|d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq pLM^{p-1}l\nu(\overline{B_{n}}\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}),

which is
o(1)
. Thus, in particular,

(3.5)

\varepsilon_{n}\bigg|\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)-\int_{\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}d\nu(\sigma)\bigg|=o(\varepsilon_{n}).

Now, by
Proposition

2.11
we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n}^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{l-\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{\Sigma_{n}}\lambda\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)d\nu_{\pi_{\Sigma_{n}}}(\sigma)+o(\varepsilon_{n}),

whence (
3.5
) gives

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n}^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{l-\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}\lambda\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)+o(\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{l-\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{\Sigma}\lambda\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon_{n}\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})\lambda\xi(\sigma^{*})\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})+o(\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{l-\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{\Sigma}\lambda\xi(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)d\nu(\sigma)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon_{n}\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})|+o(\varepsilon_{n}).

Note that the error term in (
3.3
) is

o(\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
. So, adding (
3.3
),
(
3.7
) and then applying
(
3.4
) we have

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n}^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle<\varepsilon_{n}\bigg(\int_{\overline{B_{n}}}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|d\nu(\sigma)-\frac{1}{2(l-\varepsilon_{n})}\lambda\int_{\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})|\bigg)+o(\varepsilon_{n}).

Since
\Sigma
was assumed to be optimal,
{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{n}^{\prime})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma)\geq 0
, and so for all sufficiently large
n

we have

\int_{\overline{B_{n}}}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|d\nu(\sigma)-\frac{\lambda}{2(l-\varepsilon_{n})}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma)+\nu(\{\sigma^{*}\})|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})|\geq 0.

Decomposing the left integral via
\overline{B_{n}}=\{\sigma^{*}\}\cup(\overline{B_{n}}\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\})
and taking
n\to\infty
thus yields that

\nu\{\sigma^{*}\}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma^{*})|\geq\frac{\lambda}{4l}\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}d\nu(\sigma),

as desired.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove that
\Sigma
having nontrivial
barycentre field implies noncut points are atoms of
\nu
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose

. Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
be a minimizer of
the (
ADP
), and let

Since

\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}(\sigma)|^{2}\,d\nu(\sigma)>0

3.2
immediately yields the

∎

Remark 3.4
.

In fact, the converse of

3.3
holds:
namely, under (

), if there is an atom
\sigma^{*}

for

Remark 3.8

Intuitively, we expect that the barycentre field should

4.
Proof of
Theorem

3.5

Finally, we give a proof of
Theorem

3.5
.

4.1.
Proof roadmap.

We first outline the intuition for the argument; as mentioned
previously, our proof is inspired by the approach of

[
9
]
*Lemma 3.2.

4.1.1.
Intuition.

From a high-level perspective, the idea is the following. Let

(4.1)

\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
be an optimizer of
(
1.1
),

and for the sake of contradiction suppose that

(4.2)

\pi_{\Sigma}
has trivial barycentre field

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}\equiv 0
.

First, we shrink
\Sigma
to obtain a
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
that recovers

O(\varepsilon)
budget; by triviality of the barycentre field and the
fact that
p\geq 2
, we will get (
Remark

2.12
)

{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-J_{p}(\Sigma)=O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Second, we use the
O(\varepsilon)
recovered budget to modify

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
to obtain a
\Sigma^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
with

{J}_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})=O(\varepsilon^{\alpha}),

where
\alpha<2
. For a particular choice of
\varepsilon
sufficiently
small, this yields
J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})<J_{p}(\Sigma)
, a
contradiction to optimality of
\Sigma
.

4.1.2.
Organization of the proof

Our actual proof differs
slightly from the intuition above, in that we estimate
J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})
directly rather than separately
estimating
J_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-J_{p}(\Sigma)
and

J_{p}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})
. We found
that this approach yielded a proof which encodes the same ideas, but
cuts down on the number of technical estimates required. However, even
with this simplification, the proof is nontrivial, and requires
delicate analysis. There are three main steps.

First (
Section

4.2
), via

Lemmas

4.2
to
4.5
we obtain a technical
lower bound (
Corollary

4.6
) for
J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})
that holds independently of whether certain
parameters were chosen favourably. The main parameters in question are
a point
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
(used in the construction of
\Sigma^{*}

in
Section

4.1.3
) and a set
A\subseteq\Sigma
.

Second (
Section

4.3
), in
Lemmas

4.7
and
4.9
,
we show that there exist favourable ways to choose
\sigma^{*}
and
A

that allow us to make quantitative refinements to the bounds from the
first step. In particular, given scalars
0\leq s<1
and
K\geq 0
, we define a set
B_{K}^{s}
that loosely encodes points of
\Sigma

where
\nu
has “local dimension” less than or equal to
s
.
Roughly, from the disintegration theorem we may expect that points

\sigma\in B_{K}^{s}
have fibres under the closest-point projection
containing greater-than-average mass from
\mu
. Then, in

Lemma

4.7
we show that when
\nu(B_{K}^{s})>0
one may pick

\sigma^{*}\in B_{K}^{s}
so that the fibres of points in a neighbourhood
of
\sigma^{*}
give an average-or-better contribution to
J_{p}

than what is typical on
B_{K}^{s}
. The case
\nu(B_{k}^{s})=0

(
Lemma

4.9
) is more subtle, but the general idea is still to
try to find a
\sigma
where local contributions to
J_{p}

from fibres are average-or-better. In either case, we obtain
quantitative bounds with decay rates depending on
s
, plus an
additional parameter
q
in
Lemma

4.9
.

Third (
Section

4.4
), we show that provided
p=2
or
p>\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, there exist choices of
s
,
K
, and (when
applicable)
q
such for all
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, the decay
rates of the previous step yield
J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0
, contradicting optimality of
\Sigma

(
4.1
), thus proving our claim.

In any case, the key arguments in the first two steps rely on the
specific construction of the competitor
\Sigma^{*}
, hence we detail it
now.

4.1.3.
Construction of the competitor

\Sigma^{*}

The general
construction for
\Sigma^{*}
is as follows. First, fix some
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
. Since
J_{p}
and
C
depend only on the
metric structure of
\supp(\mu)\cup\Sigma
, observe that

(4.3)

without loss of generality, we may use coordinates
having
\sigma^{*}
at the origin.

Next, fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and consider

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)\Sigma
; observe that

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
recovers
l\varepsilon
budget (in particular, we have

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{(1-\varepsilon)l}
) while keeping

\mathbf{0}=\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
. For
\tau>0
, define
the
d
-dimensional “cross shape”

by

S_{\tau}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\{e_{i}t\mid t\in[-\tau,\tau]\}.

This perturbation was previously considered in the context of the
(

) by Paolini and Stepanov in
[
26
]
*Lemma
3.3, and in the context of length-constrained principal curves by
Delattre and Fischer in
[

, Lemma 3.1]
. Anyways, observe
that taking

(4.4)

\displaystyle\tau=\alpha\varepsilon\qquad\text{ where}\qquad\alpha=\frac{l}{2d}

guarantees
C(S_{\tau})=\varepsilon
.

With this, we define the competitor

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}\coloneqq(\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\cup S_{\tau})\in\mathcal{S}_{l}.

Observe that we have suppressed the dependence of
\Sigma^{*}
on

\sigma^{*}
and
\varepsilon
in our notation.

Remark 4.1
.

With the simple tweak
\alpha=\frac{l}{4d}
in the construction of
the competitor, the same proof that we present below for

3.5
works in the case of

length-constrained principal curves
(see e.g.
[
9
]
). The extra factor of

comes from the fact that
we need to parameterize the

of the cross adds twice as much length as in the case of the

constraint.

4.2.
First step: lower bounding

Now, we proceed with the proof. We use the notation
|x|_{\infty}=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,d\}}|x_{i}|
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As in the
roadmap section above, fix
\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and recall
\Sigma^{*}
from (
4.5
).
Lastly, let

be an arbitrary neighbourhood of
\sigma^{*}
; we
will choose
A
in a favourable way later.

4.2.1.
Two general lemmata

We begin with
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.3
, which hold
in general, regardless of the optimality of
\Sigma
(see
(
4.1
)) or whether the barycentre field is trivial
or not (see (
4.2
)).

Lemma 4.2
.

Using the coordinate system with
\sigma^{*}=0

(see (
4.3
)), construct
\Sigma^{*}
as in
(
4.5
). Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2}\geq\psi(x),

where
\psi(x)=\min[\psi_{1}(x),0]
with

(4.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)=\max[

\displaystyle-\!2\varepsilon\pi_{\Sigma}(x)\cdot(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\varepsilon^{2}|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2},

\displaystyle-\!2\phantom{\varepsilon}\pi_{\Sigma}(x)\cdot(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\phantom{\varepsilon^{2}}|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}+2\tau|x|_{\infty}-\tau^{2}].

Before presenting the proof, we note here that the terms
(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))
in (
4.6
) will be important in

Lemma

4.4
to obtain a relation to the barycentre field

\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\Sigma}}
.

Proof.

We derive the lower bound involving
\psi
. First, since
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2}\leq|x-\pi_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}(x)|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}-|x-(1-\varepsilon)\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon x\cdot\pi_{\Sigma}(x)+(2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2})|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon\pi_{\Sigma}(x)\cdot(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))-\varepsilon^{2}|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}.

Next, we want to estimate
|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}
. We have two
subcases.

First suppose
|x|_{\infty}\leq\tau
. Since each
v\in S_{\tau}
has
at most one nonzero component, we see
\min_{v\in S_{\tau}}|x-v|

is achieved by taking
v
to be the largest component of
x
:

|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}=\min_{v\in S_{\tau}}\sum_{i}|x_{i}-v_{i}|^{2}=\Big(\sum_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}\Big)-|x|_{\infty}^{2}=|x|^{2}-|x|_{\infty}^{2}.

Second, suppose
|x|_{\infty}>\tau
. The same reasoning shows the
optimal
v
is the endpoint of the cross arm that points in the
direction of the largest component of
x
, whence

|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}=|x|^{2}-|x|_{\infty}^{2}+(|x|_{\infty}-\tau)^{2}.

We may combine the two subcases by writing

|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}=|x|^{2}+((|x|_{\infty}-\tau)_{+})^{2}-|x|_{\infty}^{2}.

Since
((|x|_{\infty}-\tau)_{+})^{2}\leq(|x|_{\infty}-\tau)^{2}

we obtain

|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}\leq|x|^{2}-2\tau|x|_{\infty}+\tau^{2}.

So using
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2}\leq|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}-|x-\pi_{S_{\tau}}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}-|x|^{2}+2\tau|x|_{\infty}-\tau^{2}

whence expanding
|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}
yields

\displaystyle=-2x\cdot\pi_{\Sigma}(x)+|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}+2\tau|x|_{\infty}-\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=-2(x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x))\cdot\pi_{\Sigma}(x)-|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}+2\tau|x|_{\infty}-\tau^{2}.

Combining this with (
4.7
) we get the desired lower
bound.
∎

Now, we use
Lemma

4.2
to find a lower bound on

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume (
2.2
). Then for
p\geq 2
and
\psi
defined
as in
Lemma

4.2
, we have

(4.8)

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})\geq\frac{p}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p-2}d\mu(x)+\frac{p}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\zeta(x)d\mu(x),

where

\displaystyle\zeta(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&p=2,\\
-|\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})|^{p-2},&2<p<3,\\
(p-2)(\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma))\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p-3},&p\geq 3.\end{cases}

Proof.

Below we take the convention that
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{0}\equiv 1
, even
when
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)=0
; by (
2.2
), this will cause
no problems. For
p\geq 2
, using the inequality
(
2.2
), namely,
a^{p}-b^{p}\geq\frac{p}{2}(a^{2}-b^{2})b^{p-2}
(recall the convention
0^{0}=1

there), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p}-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p}{2}(\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{2})\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{p-2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p}{2}\psi(x)\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{p-2}

for
\psi
from
Lemma

4.2
.
From this the
p=2
case of (
4.8
) follows.
It remains to show that for the remaining case of
p
,

(4.9)

\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{p-2}\geq\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p-2}+\zeta(x)

First, suppose
2<p<3
, and define
f(t)=|t|^{p-2}
.
Note that
f
satisfies
f(a+b)\leq f(a)+f(b)
. Since
f
is also
increasing on
[0,\infty)
, for all

we further
obtain

. We apply this fact with

,

,
and
c=\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)
. To verify, we have

a+b=\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})+|\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})|\geq\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)=c,

whence from
f(a)\geq f(c)-f(b)
we get

\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})^{p-2}\geq\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)^{p-2}-|\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma^{*})-\mathrm{dist}(x,\Sigma)|^{p-2}.

This gives (
4.9
) with the desired form
of
\zeta(x)
for the

case.

Now, suppose
p\geq 3
. To obtain
(
4.9
) we apply the lower bound of
(
2.2
), this time with “
p
”
=p-2
, and

(note that

Step 1.

\displaystyle S=\Sigma\setminus B_{K}^{s}=\{\sigma\in\Sigma\mid\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\nu(B_{r}(\sigma))}{r^{s}}\leq K\}.

\displaystyle S=\bigcap_{\lambda>0}\bigcup_{t>0}S^{t}_{\lambda}.

Recall that for a general function

\displaystyle\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}\Big(\bigcap_{j}X_{j}\Big)=\bigcap_{j}\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(X_{j})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}\Big(\bigcup_{j}X_{j}\Big)=\bigcup_{j}\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(X_{j}).

Thus
\displaystyle\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(S)=\Big(\bigcap_{\lambda>0}\bigcup_{t>0}\pi^{-1}(S^{t}_{\lambda})\Big)

\displaystyle\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(S)\setminus N_{\delta}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigcap_{\lambda>0}\bigcup_{t>0}\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(S^{t}_{\lambda})\Big)\setminus N_{\delta}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\lambda>0}\bigcup_{t>0}\left(\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(S^{t}_{\lambda})\setminus N_{\delta}(\Sigma)\right).

Recalling that preimages respect

\lambda

So, for any

and

To that end, since

\frac{r}{4l}N\leq 1

As in the proof of

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(B_{r}(\sigma_{i})\cap\Sigma)\setminus N_{\delta}(\Sigma)}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|_{\infty}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta^{p-1}}{\sqrt{d}}\mu(\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(S_{\lambda}^{t})\setminus N_{\delta}(\Sigma))

So, there is some

For all

\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(B_{r}(\sigma_{k})\cap\Sigma)}|x-\sigma_{k}|_{\infty}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}d\mu(x)\geq\frac{C_{1}}{8l}r-rM^{p-2}\nu(B_{r}(\sigma_{k})).

Furthermore, since

Now, for some

.

By our hypothesis (

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(A)}(-|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}+2\tau|x|_{\infty}-\tau^{2})|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\qquad-\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus A)}|\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{2}|x-\pi_{\Sigma}(x)|^{p-2}d\mu(x).

For all the

4.4.
Final steps of the proof: deriving a
contradiction

By combining
Lemmas

4.7
and
4.9
, we can now finally establish
that under the assumption (
2.2
), there is a
contradiction between (
4.1
) (“
\Sigma
is
optimal”) and (
4.2
) (“
\pi_{\Sigma}
has trivial
barycentre field”).

We proceed by casework on

, separately treating the regimes
p=2
,

p\geq 3
, and
\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}<p<3
. Of course, we
conjecture the result should hold in the intermediate range
2<p\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
as well, but it seems a different argument
would be necessary for this. In any case, for each of these
p

regimes we prove the result by separately considering (for some
K\geq 0
and
s\geq 0
) the subcases
\nu(B_{K}^{s})>0
and
\nu(B_{K}^{s})=0
.

Case 1 (
p=2
).
First, suppose
p=2
. Then, from
(
4.13
),
\kappa_{p}=0
. Fix
s=\frac{2}{3}
, and let
K>0
be arbitrary. Consider
B_{K}^{s}
.

Subcase 1.1:
If
\nu(B_{K}^{s})>0
, then by
Lemma

4.7

we may find a sufficiently small choice of
\varepsilon
and a
corresponding choice of
\sigma^{*}
so that

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})\geq K\varepsilon^{5/3}\eta_{0}(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{4}M^{p-2}>0,

as desired.

Subcase 1.2:
If
\nu(B_{K}^{s})=0
, then taking
q=\frac{7}{8}
and
\lambda>0
in
Lemma

4.9
, for all
sufficiently small
\varepsilon
there is a choice of
\sigma^{*}
so that

\displaystyle{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p}{2}\Big(\frac{C_{1}}{8d}\varepsilon^{15/8}-2\alpha K_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{1+\frac{35}{24}}M^{p-2}\Big)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the
\varepsilon^{15/8}
term dominates, and thus
there is a choice of
\varepsilon
for which
{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0
, as desired.

Case 2 (
p\geq 3
).
Next, suppose
p\geq 3
. Then, from
(
4.13
)
\kappa_{p}=a\varepsilon
for some constant
a

depending only on
\mu
,
p
, and
\alpha
. As before, fix
s=\frac{2}{3}
and let
K>0
be arbitrary.

Subcase 2.1:
If
\nu(B_{K}^{s})>0
, then by
Lemma

4.7

we may find a sufficiently small choice of
\varepsilon
and a
corresponding choice of
\sigma^{*}
so that

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})\geq\frac{p}{2}\Big(K\varepsilon^{1+2/3}\eta_{0}(\varepsilon)-a\varepsilon^{2}\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)\Big)>0,

as desired.

Subcase 2.2:
If
\nu(B_{K}^{s})=0
, then as in Subcase 1.2 take

q=\frac{7}{8}
and
\lambda>0
in
Lemma

4.9
. The only
difference in this case is that we have
\kappa_{p}=a\varepsilon
rather
than
\kappa_{p}\equiv 0
; however, since
(1+s)q=\frac{35}{24}>1
,
the term
-\kappa_{p}(\varepsilon)(K_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{(1+s)q}\eta_{3}(\varepsilon)+2M^{2}\varepsilon)
has order

, and
so can be ignored safely. So we get
J_{p}(\Sigma)-J_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0

Case 3
({\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}}<p<3)
.
This case is more
difficult, so for organizational reasons we shall state our two
subcases as lemmata. We initially proceed with the more general case

and then show the further restriction

arises naturally from our bounds.

Lemma 4.10

(Subcase 3.1)
.

Suppose
2<p<3
. Then for any

0\leq s<p-2,

if
\nu(B_{K}^{s})>0
for some

, then there exists some

\Sigma^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
so that
{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0

Proof.

Since
2<p<3
, from (
4.13
) we have
\kappa_{p}=a\varepsilon^{p-2}
for some
a
depending only on
\mu
,
p
, and

\alpha
. By

4.7
, we may find arbitrarily small choices
of
\varepsilon
and a corresponding choice of
\sigma^{*}
so that

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})\geq\frac{p}{2}\Big(K\varepsilon^{1+s}\eta_{0}(\varepsilon)-a\varepsilon^{1+(p-2)}\eta_{1}(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{4}M^{p-2}\Big).

Since
s<p-2<1
, taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small yields

{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 4.11

(Subcase 3.2)
.

Suppose that
2<p<3
. Then for any
s>0
with

\frac{1}{s+1}>p-2,

if
\nu(B_{K}^{s})=0
for some

, then there exists some

\Sigma^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
so that
{J}_{p}(\Sigma)-{J}_{p}(\Sigma^{*})>0

Proof.

We will be using (
4.29
) in

Lemma

4.9
. Since
2<p<3
, from (
4.13
) we have

\kappa_{p}=a\varepsilon^{p-2}
for some
a
depending only on
\mu
,

p
, and
\alpha
. Fix
0<q<1
,
s>0,
and take
\lambda>0
in

Lemma

4.9
and
K_{\lambda}\coloneqq K+\lambda
. Observe
that since the
\varepsilon^{1+q}
term is positive and since

, for small
\varepsilon
we may ignore the higher-order

), leaving

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1+q}\eta_{2}(\varepsilon)-{2\alpha}K_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{q(2+s)}M^{p-2}-a\varepsilon^{p-2}(K_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{(1+s)q}\eta_{3}(\varepsilon)+2M^{2}\varepsilon).

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\varepsilon^{1+q}-c_{2}(\varepsilon^{2q+qs}+\varepsilon^{p-2+q+sq}+\varepsilon^{p-2+1}).

\displaystyle 1+q<\min[2q+qs,\ p-2+q+qs,\ p-2+1].

That is,

, and

(ii)

, and

Condition (i) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+s}<p-2\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{3-p}{s}<p-2.

Observe that the second inequality is equivalent to

, picking any

In summary, for any

∎

Finally, combining

and

, we see that if there exists

, then we can find

. Existence of such

\displaystyle\frac{3-p}{p-2}<p-2,\quad\text{ or equivalently,}\quad 0<p^{2}-3p+1.

Together with

, this gives
\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}<p<3
.

Combining cases 1-3, we see that in each of these cases

, a contradiction to

\Sigma
(

). So
(

) cannot hold. This completes the proof of

Theorem

3.5
. ∎
