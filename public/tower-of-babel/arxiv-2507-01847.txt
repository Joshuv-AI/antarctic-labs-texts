On C C -Symmetric and C C -Self-adjoint Unbounded Operators on Hilbert Space
Yury Arlinskii, Konrad Schmüdgen

Abstract
. Let C C be a conjugation on a Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H}\, . A densely defined linear operator A A on ℋ \mathcal{H}\, is called C C -symmetric if C ​ A ​ C ⊆ A ∗ CAC\subseteq A^{*} and C C -self-adjoint if C ​ A ​ C = A ∗ CAC=A^{*} . Our main results describe all C C -self-adjoint extensions of A A on ℋ \mathcal{H}\, . Further, we prove a C C -self-adjointness criterion based on quasi-analytic vectors and we characterize C C -self-adjoint operators in terms of their polar decompositions.

]

2.
Basic Notions and Some General Results on
C
-Self-adjoint Operators

For a linear operator
A
on a Hilbert space, we write
\mathcal{D}\,(A)
for its domain,
\mathcal{N}(A)
for its kernel and
A^{*}
for its adjoint. The projection on a closed subspace
\mathcal{G}
is denoted
P_{\mathcal{G}}
.

The most important notions for this paper are introduced in the following definitions.

Definition 1
.

A
conjugation
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\,
is a conjugate-linear mapping
C
of
\mathcal{H}\,
on
\mathcal{H}\,
such that
C^{2}x=x
and

(1)

\displaystyle\langle Cx,Cy\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle\quad\textrm{for}~~~x,y\in\mathcal{H}\,.

Clearly, condition (
1
) in Definition
1
can be replaced by

\displaystyle\langle Cx,y\rangle=\langle Cy,x\rangle\quad\textrm{for}~~~x,y\in\mathcal{H}\,.

Throughout this paper,
C
denotes a conjugation on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\,
.

Definition 2
.

A densely defined linear operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}\,
is called

\bullet

C
-symmetric
if
CAC\subseteq A^{*}
,

\bullet

C
-self-adjoint
if
CAC=A^{*}
,

\bullet

C
-real
if
CAC\subseteq A
.

It is easily verified that
A
is
C
-symmetric if and only if
A\subseteq CA^{*}C
, or equivalently, if

\displaystyle\langle Ax,Cy\rangle=\langle x,CAy\rangle\quad\textrm{for}~~~x,y\in\mathcal{D}\,(A).

This equation can be taken as a definition of
C
-symmetry for non-densely defined linear operators.

The following proposition characterizes the
C
-self-adjointness of a closed extension in terms of domains only.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
A
is a densely defined linear operator on
\mathcal{H}\,
. Let
\widetilde{A}
be a closed extension of
A
on
\mathcal{H}\,
. Then
\widetilde{A}
is
C
-self-adjoint if and only if

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\,(\tilde{A}^{*})=C\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}).

Proof.

If
\widetilde{A}
is
C
-self-adjoint, then
C\widetilde{A}C=\widetilde{A}^{*}
and hence
\mathcal{D}\,(\tilde{A}^{*})=C\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}).

Conversely, suppose that (
2
) holds. Then, by (
2
) , the operator

B:=\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{A}+C\widetilde{A}^{*}C)

has the domain
\mathcal{D}\,(B)=\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})
. Since
A\subseteq\widetilde{A}
, we have
\widetilde{A}^{*}\subseteq A^{*}
. Hence
C\widetilde{A}^{*}C\lceil\mathcal{D}\,(A)=CA^{*}C\lceil\mathcal{D}\,(A)=A
, so
B
is an extension of
A
.

Define
T:=\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{A}^{*}+C\widetilde{A}C)
with domain
\mathcal{D}\,(T)=\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})
. Clearly,
B^{*}\supseteq T
. Since
B\supseteq A
, we have
A^{*}\supseteq B^{*}\supseteq T
. Thus,
T
is a restriction of
A^{*}
. Since
\mathcal{D}\,(T)=\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})
and
\widetilde{A}^{*}
is a restriction of
A^{*}
, we conclude that
T=\widetilde{A}^{*}
. Hence

C\widetilde{A}C=\widetilde{A}^{*}
which means that
\widetilde{A}
is
C
-self-adjoint.
∎

Suppose that
A
is a densely defined closed
C
-symmetric operator. We define
B:=CAC
with
\mathcal{D}\,(B)=C\mathcal{D}\,(A)
.
Then we have

B^{*}=CA^{*}C,\;\mathcal{D}\,(B^{*})=C\mathcal{D}\,(A^{*}),

and

B\subseteq A^{*},\;A\subseteq B^{*}.

This means that the operators
A
and
B=CAC
form an adjoint pair (or equivalently, a dual pair).

Further, for any
C
-symmetric extension
\widetilde{A}
of
A
one has

A\subseteq\widetilde{A}\subseteq B^{*},\>B\subseteq\widetilde{A}^{*}\subseteq A^{*}.

Recall that if
T
is a closed densely defined operator on
\mathcal{H}\,
, its domain becomes a Hilbert space, denoted
\mathcal{H}\,_{T}
, with respect to the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\,_{T}}=\langle f,g\rangle+\langle Tf,Tg\rangle,\quad f,g\in\mathcal{D}\,(T).

Set
\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}:=(\mathcal{D}\,(A))^{\perp}_{\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}}

and
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}:=(\mathcal{D}\,(B))^{\perp}_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}.
We easily verify that

(3)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}=\mathcal{N}(I+A^{*}B^{*})=\mathcal{N}(I+A^{*}CA^{*}C),\\[5.69054pt]
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}=\mathcal{N}(I+B^{*}A^{*})=\mathcal{N}(I+CA^{*}CA^{*})\end{array}

Using (
3
) it is not difficult to derive

(4)

B^{*}\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}},\;A^{*}\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}},\;C\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}

and

(5)

\begin{array}[]{l}||B^{*}f||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}=||f||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}},\;~~~f\in\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}},\\[5.69054pt]
||A^{*}g||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}}=||g||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}},\;~~~g\in\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}},\end{array}

(6)

\begin{array}[]{l}||Cf||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}^{2}=||Cf||^{2}+||A^{*}Cf||^{2}=||f||^{2}+||CA^{*}Cf||^{2}\\
\qquad\qquad=||f||^{2}+||B^{*}f||^{2}=||f||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}},\;f\in\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}.\end{array}

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{A}
is a closed operator satisfying
A\subset\widetilde{A}\subset B^{*}
. Then there exist closed linear subspaces
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
and
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
of the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}
, respectively, such that

(7)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(A)\dot{+}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}},\;\;\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}},\\[5.69054pt]
\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})=\mathcal{D}\,(B)\dot{+}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}},\;\;\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}.\end{array}

Moreover,

\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
is the orthogonal complement of
B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
in
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}
.

Proof.

For the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}
there are the orthogonal decompositions

\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}=\mathcal{D}\,(A)\oplus\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}},\;~~\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}=\mathcal{D}\,(B)\oplus\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}.

It follows that for a closed extension
\widetilde{A}
such that
A\subset\widetilde{A}\subset B^{*}
and its adjoint
\widetilde{A}^{*}
we have the orthogonal decompositions in
\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}
:

\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(A)\oplus\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}},\;~~\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})=\mathcal{D}\,(B)\oplus\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}

where
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
and
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
are closed subspaces of
\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}
, respectively.

If
h\in\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
and
g\in\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
, then

\langle\widetilde{A}h,g\rangle=\langle h,A^{*}g\rangle
and hence

\langle h,A^{*}g\rangle=\langle B^{*}h,g\rangle=\langle B^{*}h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}-\langle A^{*}B^{*}h,A^{*}g\rangle=\langle B^{*}h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}+\langle h,A^{*}g\rangle.

Thus,
\langle B^{*}h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}=0
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
is contained in the orthogonal complement of
B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
in
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}.

On the other side, if
\mathcal{L}\,
is the orthogonal in
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}
to
B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
, then for each
h\in B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
and for each
y\in\mathcal{L}\,
we have

\begin{array}[]{l}0=\langle y,B^{*}h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}=\langle y,B^{*}h\rangle+\langle A^{*}y,A^{*}B^{*}h\rangle\\
=\langle y,B^{*}h\rangle+\langle A^{*}y,A^{*}CA^{*}Ch\rangle=\langle y,B^{*}h\rangle-\langle A^{*}y,h\rangle.\end{array}

Hence for all
f_{A}\in\mathcal{D}\,(A)
and all
h\in\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}

\langle A^{*}y,f_{A}+h\rangle=\langle y,Af_{A}\rangle+\langle y,B^{*}h\rangle=\langle y,B^{*}(f_{A}+h)\rangle=\langle y,\widetilde{A}(f_{A}+h)\rangle.

It follows that
y\in\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})
. Thus,
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
is equal to the orthogonal complement of
B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
in
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}

∎

In
[
Kn81
, Lemma 3.3]
it is shown that each closed
C
-symmetric extension
\widetilde{A}
of
A
is a restriction of
B^{*}=CA^{*}C
to
a closed subspace
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\,
of
\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}},

\mathcal{D}\,(A)\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\,\subset\mathcal{D}\,(CA^{*}C)
, such that

\langle A^{*}Cy,x\rangle=\langle A^{*}Cx,y\rangle
for all
x,y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\,
.
The next result characterizes
C
-self-adjoint extensions of
A
more precisely.

Corollary 5
.

Suppose that
A
is a densely defined closed
C
-symmetric operator on
\mathcal{H}\,
and retain the preceding notation. For a closed operator
\widetilde{A}
on
\mathcal{H}\,
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\widetilde{A}
is a
C
-self-adjoint extension of
A
.

(ii)

\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
is the orthogonal complement to
(A^{*}C)\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
in
\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}
, i.e.

(8)

\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\oplus(A^{*}C)\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3
, (i) is equivalent to
C\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})
.
From Lemma
4
,
(
4
) and
\mathcal{D}\,(B)=C\mathcal{D}\,(A)
it follows that
the equality
C\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}^{*})
holds if and only if

C\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}=\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}\ominus B^{*}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\Longleftrightarrow C\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\oplus(CA^{*}C)\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}.

The latter equality is equivalent to (
8
), since

B^{*}=CA^{*}C
,
C\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}
,
||Cf||_{\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}}^{2}=||f||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}}
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}
, and
C^{2}=I
.
∎

A consequence is the following formula obtained in
[
Ra85
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Corollary 6
.

For each
C
-self-adjoint extension
\widetilde{A}
of the densely defined closed
C
-symmetric operator
A
we have

\dim\left(\mathcal{D}\,(CA^{*}C)/\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A}\right)=\dim\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})/\mathcal{D}\,(A)\right).

Remark 7
.

Define the following operators:

\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}:=A^{*}C{\upharpoonright\,}\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}},\;\mathcal{S}_{A^{*}}:=CA^{*}{\upharpoonright\,}\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}.

Due to (
4
), (
5
), (
6
) the operators
\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A^{*}}
are anti-unitary in
\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}
w.r.t. the inner products of
\mathcal{H}\,_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{H}\,_{A^{*}}
, respectively. Moreover, from (
3
) it follows that

\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}^{2}=-I_{\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A^{*}}^{2}=-I_{\mathcal{M}\,_{A^{*}}}.

This means that the operators
\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A^{*}}
are anti-involutions.

Now the equality (
8
) can be rewritten as

\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}=\mathcal{M}\,_{B^{*}}.

Using Zorn’s lemma it is proved in
[
Ga62
]
that for any anti-involution
\mathcal{S}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\,
there exists an orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}\,=\mathcal{H}\,_{0}\oplus\mathcal{S}\mathcal{H}\,_{0}
. Another proof of this fact can be derived from
[
Luc69
, Lemma 7]
.
Applying this to the anti-involution
\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}
it follows that there exists a decomposition

(9)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,\oplus_{\mathcal{H}\,_{CA^{*}C}}(A^{*}C)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,=\mathcal{M}\,_{CA^{*}C}.

As shown in
[
Ga62
]
and
[
Kn80
]
,
(
9
) yields the existence of a
C
-self-adjoint extension of
A
. From Lemma
4
and Corollary
5
follows that the operator

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{D}\,(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(A)\dot{+}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
\widetilde{A}(f_{A}+h)=CA^{*}C(f_{A}+h)=Af_{A}+CA^{*}Ch,\;\;f_{A}\in\mathcal{D}\,(A),\;h\in\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,.\end{array}

is a
C
-self-adjoint extensions of
A.

Another
C
-self-adjoint extension
\widehat{A}
of
A
can be obtained from (
9
) if we replace
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,
by
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\,:=(A^{*}C)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,
(because
(A^{*}C)\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\,=\mathcal{S}_{B^{*}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\,=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,
),

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{A})=\mathcal{D}\,(A)\dot{+}(A^{*}C)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
\widehat{A}(f_{A}+A^{*}Ch)=CA^{*}C(f_{A}+A^{*}Ch)=Af_{A}-Ch,\;\;f_{A}\in\mathcal{D}\,(A),\;h\in\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\,.\end{array}

Some descriptions of the linear manifolds
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}}
and
\mathcal{L}\,_{\widetilde{A}^{*}}
corresponding to
C
-selfadjoint extensions will be given in Section

4
(see (
26
)).

3.
The Matrix Trick for
C
-Symmetric operators

Let
A
be densely defined operator on
\mathcal{H}\,
. To
A
we asscociate
an operator
\mathfrak{A}\,
on
\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
with dense domain
\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{A}\,)=\mathcal{D}\,(CAC)\oplus\mathcal{D}\,(A)
defined by the operator matrix

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\,=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~0&A\\
CA\,C&0\end{array}\right).

Clearly, the adjoint operator
\mathfrak{A}\,^{*}
of
\mathfrak{A}\,
is given by the operator matrix

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\,^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&CA^{*}C\\
A^{*}&~~~0\end{array}\right),\;\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{A}\,^{*})=\mathcal{D}\,(A^{*})\oplus\mathcal{D}\,(CA^{*}C).

Further, we define a conjugation
\mathfrak{C}
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
by

(14)

\mathfrak{C}(x,y)=(Cy,Cx),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{H}\,.

Lemma 8
.

For
(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A}\,\mathfrak{C})=\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{A}\,)
we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A}\,\mathfrak{C}(x,y)=\mathfrak{A}\,(x,y).

Proof.

First we note that the domain of
\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A}\,\mathfrak{C}
consists of all vectors
(x,y)\in\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
for which
\mathfrak{C}(x,y)=(Cy,Cx)
is in the domain of the operator matrix
\mathfrak{A}\,
, that is,
Cx\in\mathcal{D}\,(A)
and
Cy\in\mathcal{D}\,(CAC)
, or equivalently,
x\in\mathcal{D}\,(CAC)
and
y\in\mathcal{D}\,(A)
. But this is precisely the domain of
\mathfrak{A}\,
. Thus
\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A}\,\mathfrak{C})=\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{A}\,).

For
(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{A}\,\mathfrak{C})=\mathcal{D}\,(\mathfrak{A}\,)
we compute

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&C\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~0&A\\
CAC&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&C\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}x\\
y\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&C\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~ACx\\
CAC^{2}y\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}C^{2}AC^{2}y\\
~CACx\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~Ay\\
CACx\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~0&A\\
CAC&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}x\\
y\end{array}\right).

∎

Proposition 9
.

The operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}\,
is
C
-symmetric if and only if
\mathfrak{A}\,
is a symmetric operator on
\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
. In this case, the symmetric operator
\mathfrak{A}\,
on
\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
is also
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric. Further,
A
is
C
-self-adjoint if and only if
\mathfrak{A}\,
is self-adjoint on
\mathcal{H}\,\oplus\mathcal{H}\,
.

Proof.

Recall that
A
is
C
-symmetric if and only if
CAC\subseteq A^{*}
and
A\subseteq CA^{*}C.
Therefore, it follows from (
3
) and (
3
) that
A
is
C
-symmetric if and only if
\mathfrak{A}\,\subseteq(\mathfrak{A}\,)^{*}
, that is, the operator
\mathfrak{A}\,
is symmetric. By the same reason,
A
is
C
-self-adjoint if and only if
CAC=A^{*}
and
A=CA^{*}C
, equivalently,
\mathfrak{A}\,=(\mathfrak{A}\,)^{*}
, that is
\mathfrak{A}\,
is self-adjoint.
∎

Since
\mathfrak{A}\,\subseteq(\mathfrak{A}\,)^{*}
, Lemma
8
implies in particular that the symmetric operator
\mathfrak{A}\,
is
\mathcal{C}\,
-symmetric.

Because of Proposition
9
the operator matrix
\mathfrak{A}\,
is an important technical tool, since it allows one to reduce problems of a
C
-symmetric operator to a
symmetric
and
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric operator.

From now on we assume that
the operator
A
is
C
-symmetric and closed.

Next we study the operator
A
by means of the operator matrix
\mathfrak{A}\,
and derive a number of useful formulas and facts.

We begin with the deficiency spaces of the symmetric operator

. Recall that they are defined by

and

\mathcal{N}(T^{*}+\overline{\lambda}I)

. In particular, the latter result yields that

Let us recall another well-known fact from the von Neumann theory of symmetric operators. If

A
is
C
-self-adjoint.

Proof.

Let the operator
\mathfrak{A}\,
be given by (
3
) and let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}(AC)^{2n}&~~~~0\\
~~~~0&(CA)^{2n}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}(AC)^{2n}&~~~~~0\\
~~~~0&C(AC)^{2n}C\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~~~~0&(AC)^{2n}A\\
(CA)^{2n}CAC&~~~~0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~~~0&(AC)^{2n+1}C\\
C(AC)^{2n+1}&~~~~0\end{array}\right).

Fix

\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n}(x,y)=((AC)^{2n}x,C(AC)^{2n}Cy),\\
\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n+1}(x,y)=((AC)^{2n+1}Cy,C(AC)^{2n+1}x).

Using that

(34)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n}(x,y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(AC)^{2n}x\|^{2}+\|(AC)^{2n}Cy\|^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{A}\,^{2n+1}(x,y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(AC)^{2n+1}Cy\|^{2}+\|(AC)^{2n+1}x\|^{2}.

Now we set

is quasi-analytic for

. Similarly, setting

is quasi-analytic for
\mathfrak{A}\,
. Therefore, since the linear span of
\mathcal{D}\,^{qa}(AC)

by assumption, it follows that the linear span of

6.
Polar decomposition

Recall that each densely defined closed operator
A
has a the polar decomposition
A=U_{A}|A|
. Here
|A|:=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
is the
modulus
of
A
and
U_{A}
is a partial isometry, called the
phase operator
of
A
, with initial space
\overline{\mathcal{R}(|A|)}
and final space
\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
. We begin with some elementary facts.

Proposition 18
.

Suppose
A
is a densely defined closed linear operator and
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}\,
. Then:

(i)

|CAC|=C|A|C
and
U_{CAC}=CU_{A}C
, so
CAC=(CU_{A}C)\,(C|A|C)
is the polar decomposition of the densel defined closed m operator
CAC
.

(ii)

If
A
is
C
-real (that is,
CAC\subseteq A
), then
|CAC|=C|A|C=|A|.

Proof.

(i): Clearly,
C|A|C
is a positive self-adjoint operator. Since
(CAC)^{*}=CA^{*}C
, we have

\displaystyle(

\displaystyle C|A|C)(C|A|C)=C|A|^{2}C=CA^{*}AC

(36)

\displaystyle=(CA^{*}C)(CAC)=(CAC)^{*}(CAC)=|CAC|^{2}.

Thus,
C|A|C
is a positive square root of the positive self-adjoint operator
|CAC|^{2}
. From the uniqueness of the positive square root we conclude that
C|A|C=|CAC|.

Set
V:=CU_{A}C
. Using that
U_{A}(U_{A})^{*}U_{A}=U_{A}
, because
U_{A}
is a partial isometry, we derive

\displaystyle VV^{*}V

\displaystyle=CU_{A}C(CU_{A}C)^{*}CU_{A}C=CU_{A}CC(U_{A})^{*}CCU_{A}C

\displaystyle=CU_{A}(U_{A})^{*}U_{A}C=CU_{A}C=V.

Therefore,
V
is a partial isometry such that

(37)

\displaystyle V^{*}V=(CU_{A}C)^{*}CU_{A}C=C(U_{A})^{*}U_{A}C.

By the properties of the polar decomposition the partial isometry
U_{A}
has the intial space
\overline{\mathcal{R}(|A|)}
. Hence it follows from (
37
) that the intial space of
V
is the closure of

C\,\mathcal{R}(|A|)=\mathcal{R}(C|A|C)=\mathcal{R}(|CAC|).
We have

CAC=CU_{A}|A|C=(CU_{A}C)\,(C|A|C)=V\,|CAC|.

Therefore, by the preceding, the uniqueness assertion for the polar decomposition (
[
Sch12
, Theorem 7.2]
) yields
V=U_{CAC}
.

(ii): From
CAC\subseteq A
we obtain
A^{*}\subseteq(CAC)^{*}=CA^{*}C
. Using (
6
) we get

|CAC|^{2}=CA^{*}AC\subseteq C(CA^{*}C)AC=A^{*}CAC\subseteq A^{*}A=|A|^{2}.

Therefore, since
|CAC|^{2}
and
|A|^{2}
are self-adjoint operators, it follows that
|CAC|^{2}=|A|^{2}
and hence
C|A|C=|A|
by the uniqueness of the positive square root. Combined with (i) this gives the assertion.
∎

The following notion was invented in
[
GP06
]
. It is the conjugate linear counter-part of a partial isometry.

Definition 19
.

A
partial conjugation
is a bounded conjugate linear mapping
J:\mathcal{H}\,\to\mathcal{H}\,
such that
J\lceil\mathcal{N}(J)^{\bot}
is a conjugation of
\mathcal{N}(J)^{\bot}
into
\mathcal{H}\,
.

In this case the closed linear subspace
\mathcal{R}(J)=\mathcal{N}(J)^{\bot}
is called the
intial space
of
J
and
J^{2}
is the orthogonal projection on this space.

The following theorem gives some characterizations of
C
-self-adjoint operators in terms of the polar decomposition.
The notion of partial conjugation and the characterization (iii) appeared in
[
GP06
]
for bounded operators and in
[
CK23
]
in the general case.

Theorem 20
.

For a densely defined closed linear operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}\,
the following are equivalent:

(i)

A
is
C
-self-adjoint.

(ii)

(U_{A})^{*}=CU_{A}C
and
|A^{*}|=C|A|C.

(iii)

There exist a positive self-adjoint operator
T
and a partial conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}\,
with initial space
\overline{\mathcal{R}(T)}
such that
JT\subseteq TJ
and

(38)

\displaystyle A=CJT.

If (iii) is satisfied, then
T=|A|
and
CJ=U_{A}
.

Proof.

(i)
\to
(ii): That
A
is
C
-self-adjoint means that
A^{*}=CAC
. Therefore,
(U_{A})^{*}=U_{A^{*}}=U_{CAC}=CU_{A}C
and
|A^{*}|=|ACA|=C|A|C
by Proposition
18
(i).

(ii)
\to
(i): From (ii) and Proposition
18
(i),
U_{A^{*}}=(U_{A})^{*}=CU_{A}C=U_{CAC}
and
A^{*}=C|A|C=|CAC
. Hence the operators
A^{*}
and
CAC
have the same moduli and the same phase operators. From the polar decomposition it follows that both operators coincide, that is,
A
is
C
-self-adjoint.

(ii)
\to
(iii): Set
T:=|A|
and
J:=CU_{A}
. Since
U_{A}
is a partial isometry, it is easily verified that
J
is a partial conjugation. Since

by (ii), we have and

, so

as

. Then

.

It remains to show that

. We have

by the equivalence of (i) and (ii). Using this equality we derive

\displaystyle JTJ=J|A|J=CU_{A}|A|CU_{A}=CACU_{A}=A^{*}U_{A}=|A|=T,

(iii)
\to
(ii):
Put

. Then

we compute

\displaystyle\langle Ux,y\rangle=\langle CJx,y\rangle=\langle Cy,Jx\rangle=\langle x,JCy\rangle.

Hence

and we have

and

\displaystyle C|A|C=CTC=C(JTJ)C=UTU^{*}=U_{A}|A|(U_{A})^{*}=|A^{*}|.
