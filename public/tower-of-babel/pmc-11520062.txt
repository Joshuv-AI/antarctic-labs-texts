The real-world efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in patients with HER2-negative advanced gastric cancer
Authors: Yu-Yin Liu, Ming-Yen Tsai, Ting-Ting Liu, Yueh-Wei Liu, Yu-Hung Lin, Cheng-Hsi Yeh, Yu-Cheng Lin, Yen-Hao Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study is to investigate the real-world efficacy and safety of nivolumab in combination with chemotherapy for patients with advanced human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative gastric cancer (GC).MethodsWe enrolled patients diagnosed with unresectable advanced or metastatic GC who received nivolumab plus chemotherapy as first-line systemic treatment. The combined positive score (CPS), indicating the number of programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-stained cells, was utilized. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Adverse events (AEs) were graded, and treatment was ceased upon disease progression or intolerance.ResultsA total of 27 patients were included in the study, comprising 15 patients with CPS ≥ 5 and 12 patients with CPS < 5. The objective response rate (ORR) was 55.6%, with a disease control rate (DCR) of 74.1%. Patients in the CPS ≥ 5 group exhibited higher ORR and DCR compared to those in the CPS < 5 group. Median PFS and OS were 6.1 months and 14.6 months, respectively; patients with CPS ≥ 5 showed a trend towards better PFS and OS than those with CPS < 5. Most AEs were grade 1–2, with a few instances of grade 3–4 toxicities reported, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, diarrhea, and anemia. There were no grade 5 AEs reported in our cohort. Furthermore, 64.7% of patients received subsequent anticancer treatment following disease progression on nivolumab plus chemotherapy.ConclusionsThe results of our study demonstrate the efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in real-world practice support its adoption as a new standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced HER2-negative GC, particularly those with CPS ≥ 5.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), including gastro-esophageal junction cancer, stands as one of the most aggressive gastrointestinal malignancies and ranks eighth in cancer-related mortality in Taiwan [1]. Adenocarcinoma represents the predominant histological subtype, accounting for nearly 80% of GC cases in Taiwan [1]. The standard first-line treatment for advanced unresectable or metastatic human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative GC involves a fluoropyrimidine plus platinum-based chemotherapy regimen, yet median overall survival (OS) remains poor at under one year [2, 3]. Despite advancements in targeted therapies, no substantial survival benefits have been achieved [4–10]. Programmed cell death (PD)-1 inhibitors, also known as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), present a novel therapeutic approach in cancer management, offering distinct anti-tumor mechanisms compared to traditional chemotherapy. Tumor cells often evade immune surveillance and establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment to promote survival, which ICIs can counteract. Preclinical studies indicate that chemotherapy not only exerts direct cytotoxic effects but also enhances the anti-tumor response mediated by ICIs through induction of immune-mediated cell death [11–14].

The phase 3 ATTRACTION-2 trial demonstrated improved OS with nivolumab, an ICI, compared to placebo in heavily pre-treated patients with advanced or metastatic GC [15–17]. The combined positive score (CPS), evaluating programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on both tumor and immune cells, has shown to enhance ICI efficacy more significantly than the tumor proportion score (TPS, expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells) in advanced GC [18, 19]. Therefore, combining ICIs with chemotherapy appears promising. Recent studies investigating ICIs plus standard chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in advanced HER-2-negative GC patients have shown varying outcomes [20–24]. In the ATTRACTION-4 study, nivolumab combined with chemotherapy improved progression-free survival (PFS) but not OS in untreated Asian patients with unresectable advanced or metastatic GC [22]. The CheckMate 649 study revealed improved objective response rate (ORR), PFS, and OS with nivolumab plus chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone, particularly benefiting patients with CPS ≥ 5 as a first-line treatment [20, 21]. Another phase 3 study, ORIENT-16, included 650 Chinese patients with advanced unresectable or metastatic GC. It found that adding sintilimab to chemotherapy significantly extended OS compared to chemotherapy alone in patients with a CPS of 5 or higher [24]. Similarly, the phase 3 KEYNOTE-859 trial demonstrated that pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy led to significant improvements in OS, PFS, and ORR compared to chemotherapy alone [23]. These benefits were observed across the entire study population, including participants with CPS scores of 1, 10, or higher.

In conclusion, combining an ICI with chemotherapy may offer a clinically significant long-term survival advantage over chemotherapy alone, with an acceptable safety profile, thereby supporting its role as a standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced GC. However, there is limited real-world data on the effectiveness and safety of ICIs combined with chemotherapy in this context, underscoring the need for further investigation. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the real-world efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in patients with advanced HER2-negative GC.

Methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective review of patients with advanced HER2-negative GC who underwent systemic therapy at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. Only patients with unresectable advanced or metastatic GC were included, excluding those with operable early-stage disease. Eligibility was restricted to adenocarcinoma histology, while other types such as squamous cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, or lymphoma were excluded. Additional therapeutic interventions such as radiotherapy, surgical resection, or other targeted therapies were not permitted. Patients with esophageal adenocarcinoma or central nervous system metastases were also excluded. Moreover, individuals with a history of secondary primary malignancies, known autoimmune diseases, positive human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), or solid organ transplantation were ineligible. All enrolled patients had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) scores of 0 or 1, with comprehensive medical records documenting clinical information. Ultimately, we identified 27 patients with advanced HER2-negative GC who received first-line systemic therapy consisting of nivolumab plus chemotherapy.

Tumor assessment

All enrolled patients were required to have at least one measurable lesion for evaluation of tumor response. Tumor assessments were conducted using dynamic multiphase computed tomography (CT) at baseline and subsequently every 8–12 weeks following the initiation of nivolumab plus chemotherapy. The response was evaluated independently by two radiologists who were blinded to clinical and medical information, adhering to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria version 1.1 [25].

Procedure and safety

The expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells was assessed by evaluating the percentage of viable tumor cells showing partial or complete membrane staining among at least 100 viable tumor cells. The CPS was calculated as the number of PD-L1-stained cells (including tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of viable tumor cells, multiplied by 100.

Nivolumab was administered intravenously at a dose of 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks, in combination with chemotherapy regimens such as FOLFOX (oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 on day 1, leucovorin 400 mg/m2 on day 1, fluorouracil 400 mg/m2 on day 1 and 1200 mg/m2 on days 1 and 2), modified XELOX (oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 on day 1 and capecitabine 1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1–10), or FLOT (docetaxel 50 mg/m2 on day 1, oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 on day 1, leucovorin 200 mg/m2 on day 1, fluorouracil 2600 mg/m2 on day 1), administered intravenously every 2 weeks [26].

Treatment was discontinued in cases of disease progression or intolerable adverse events (AEs). AEs were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 [27].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). PFS was defined as the interval from the initiation of nivolumab plus chemotherapy to the occurrence of disease progression or death from any cause. OS was calculated from the start of nivolumab plus chemotherapy to death from any cause or the last visit date. Cumulative survival rates were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Medical Foundation (approval number: 202400922B0) and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Medical Foundation (approval number: 202400922B0) due to the retrospective design of the study. Patient confidentiality was rigorously upheld throughout the study, in full compliance with the applicable ethical standards and guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized or de-identified to safeguard privacy.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 27 patients with advanced HER2-negative GC who received nivolumab plus chemotherapy as first-line systemic treatment at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023 were identified. The cohort consisted of 13 men and 14 women, with a median age of 64 years (range: 39–84 years). All patients had an ECOG PS score of 0 or 1. Primary tumor locations included gastric cancer in 21 patients (77.8%) and gastro-esophageal junction cancer in 6 patients (22.2%). Of these, 5 patients (18.5%) had locally advanced unresectable disease, while 22 patients (81.5%) presented with metastatic disease. Metastatic sites included liver metastasis in 4 patients (14.8%), lung metastasis in 4 patients (14.8%), peritoneal carcinomatosis in 8 patients (29.6%), bone metastasis in 3 patients (11.1%), and distant lymph node metastasis in 8 patients (29.6%). One patient (3.7%) had a history of surgical resection for GC prior to receiving nivolumab plus chemotherapy, and 5 patients (18.5%) had signet-ring cell carcinoma. Fifteen patients (55.6%) had a CPS of ≥ 5, while 12 patients (44.4%) had a CPS of < 5. Microsatellite instability (MSI) testing revealed that 15 patients (55.6%) were microsatellite stable (MSS), one patient (3.7%) had microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), and the remaining 11 patients (40.7%) did not have their MSI status assessed. Chemotherapy regimens included FOLFOX in 17 patients (63.0%), modified XELOX in 6 patients (22.2%), and FLOT in 4 patients (14.8%). Clinical outcome

A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with CPS ≥ 5 and CPS < 5 revealed no significant differences in age, sex, ECOG PS, or the primary tumor location. However, a notable difference was found in tumor stage; patients with CPS < 5 had a higher incidence of metastatic disease compared to those with CPS ≥ 5 (100.0% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.027). The treatment response to nivolumab plus chemotherapy was assessed according to the RECIST criteria version 1.1. This included a complete response (CR) in one patient (3.7%), partial response (PR) in 14 patients (51.9%), stable disease (SD) in 5 patients (18.5%), and progressive disease (PD) in 7 patients (25.9%), resulting in an ORR of 55.6% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 74.1%. We also compared the distribution of ORR between patients with CPS ≥ 5 and CPS < 5. The CPS ≥ 5 group demonstrated a higher ORR (66.7% versus 41.7%) and DCR (80.0% versus 66.7%) compared to the CPS < 5 group, although the difference was not statistically significant. In our cohort, the median PFS and OS were 6.1 months and 14.6 months, respectively (Fig. 1). Patients were stratified based on the status of CPS into two groups: CPS ≥ 5 and CPS < 5. Patients with CPS ≥ 5 showed a trend towards better PFS compared to those with CPS < 5 (not reached versus 6.1 months, P = 0.08, HR: 0.42 [0.15–1.13], Fig. 2A). Similarly, OS was prolonged in patients with CPS ≥ 5 compared to CPS < 5, though this trend was not statistically significant (not reached versus 14.6 months, P = 0.15, HR: 0.45 [0.15–1.36], Fig. 2B).

In terms of PFS and OS, factors such as age, sex, ECOG PS, primary tumor site, PD-L1 expression (CPS), and the occurrence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were not statistically significant in the univariate analysis. Patients with locally advanced disease exhibited superior PFS (not reached versus 4.4 months, P = 0.028) and OS (not reached versus 13.0 months, P = 0.041) compared to those with metastatic disease. However, none of the variables were found to be significant in the multivariate analysis for PFS and OS. The survival outcomes from both univariate and multivariate analyses are detailed in Table 3.

In our cohort, 8 patients (29.6%) presented with peritoneal metastasis. The ORR for patients with and without peritoneal metastasis was 63.2% and 37.5%, respectively (P = 0.22). A detailed breakdown of the ORR in patients with peritoneal metastasis revealed one patient with CR, 12 with PR, three with SD, and four with PD. In contrast, the group without peritoneal metastasis showed three patients with PR, two with SD, and three with PD. Furthermore, patients without peritoneal metastasis demonstrated better PFS (6.1 months versus 5.0 months) and OS (not reached versus 14.6 months) compared to those with peritoneal metastasis, though these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Safety

The most frequently observed treatment-related AEs included neutropenia (63.0%) and thrombocytopenia (63.0%), followed by vomiting (33.3%), diarrhea (33.3%), peripheral neuropathy (29.6%), nausea (25.9%), decreased appetite (22.2%), elevated liver function tests (22.2%), anemia (22.2%), and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (14.8%). The majority of AEs were graded as 1–2, with few instances of grade 3–4 toxicities reported, including neutropenia (22.2%), thrombocytopenia (7.4%), diarrhea (7.4%), and anemia (7.4%).

The incidence of irAEs included grade 1–2 hypothyroidism (11.1%), hepatitis (7.4%), dermatitis (7.4%), pneumonitis (7.4%), colitis (3.7%), and hyperthyroidism (3.7%). Grade 3–4 immune-mediated AEs comprised hepatitis (3.7%), dermatitis (3.7%), and colitis (3.7%). No grade 5 AEs were reported in our cohort. Detailed safety profiles are presented in Tables 4 and 5.

Patient disposition

In our cohort, 17 patients experienced disease progression following treatment with nivolumab plus chemotherapy. Subsequently, 11 patients (64.7%) received anticancer therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and ICIs. The most frequently administered chemotherapy regimens were docetaxel plus ramucirumab (54.5%), docetaxel alone (45.5%), irinotecan (45.5%), and paclitaxel (9.1%). One patient (9.1%) underwent treatment with alpelisib due to a PIK3CA mutation identified through next-generation sequencing. Additionally, two patients (18.2%) underwent nivolumab rechallenge following disease progression. Discussion

Based on previous phase 3 trials, the combination of ICI and chemotherapy has shown superior ORR, PFS and OS compared to chemotherapy alone. This combination therapy has been established as standard first-line systemic treatment for patients with advanced HER2-negative GC [2, 3, 20–24]. In our real-world cohort, we investigated the efficacy and safety profiles of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in patients with GC. Our findings revealed an ORR of 55.6% with a DCR of 74.1%. The median PFS and OS were 6.1 months and 14.6 months, respectively. Furthermore, patients with a CPS ≥ 5 showed trends towards improved ORR, PFS, and OS compared to those with CPS < 5, with results consistent with findings from the CheckMate 649 trial [20, 21]. Common treatment-related AEs included bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal symptoms/signs, and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome. No patients experienced grade 5 toxicity in our cohort. More than half of the patients received subsequent therapy following progression on nivolumab plus chemotherapy, including some who underwent nivolumab rechallenge. Our cohort demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety profiles of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in real-world clinical practice, consistent with results observed in clinical trials.

In contrast to the CheckMate 649 trial, our cohort exclusively enrolled patients with advanced GC who received nivolumab plus chemotherapy, without including a chemotherapy alone group. This decision was based on the well-established clinical efficacy of chemotherapy alone, focusing our study on exploring the efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in this patient population. Our results demonstrated an ORR of 55.6%, which aligns closely with the 60% ORR reported in the nivolumab plus chemotherapy group of the CheckMate 649 trial [20, 21]. However, the CheckMate 649 trial reported a higher CR rate of 13% in the nivolumab plus chemotherapy group with CPS ≥ 5, whereas our cohort showed a CR rate of only 6.7%. This discrepancy may be attributed to bias due to our smaller sample size, where even a single patient’s outcome can significantly impact the percentage. Despite the lower CR rate in our cohort, the overall ORR remains comparable to that reported in the CheckMate 649 trial. Furthermore, in our cohort, patients with CPS ≥ 5 showed higher ORR (66.7% versus 41.7%) and DCR (80.0% versus 66.7%) compared to those with CPS < 5, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, the ORR of 41.7% in patients with CPS < 5 in our cohort was similar to the ORR of 44% reported in the chemotherapy alone group of the CheckMate 649 trial. This observation may be influenced by the higher incidence of peritoneal carcinomatosis in patients with CPS < 5 compared to those with CPS ≥ 5 (33.3% versus 26.7%). Peritoneal metastasis is generally associated with poorer response to chemotherapy.

Furthermore, the CheckMate 649 trial reported a median PFS of 7.7 months and OS of 13.1 months in the overall population. In our cohort, we observed a median PFS of 6.1 months and OS of 14.6 months, showing no significant difference compared to the trial results. In the subgroup analysis of patients with CPS ≥ 5 in the CheckMate 649 trial, the median PFS and OS were 8.3 months and 14.4 months, respectively, indicating improved outcomes compared to the entire trial population. Similarly, our cohort demonstrated not reached median PFS and OS in patients with CPS ≥ 5, suggesting borderline significant improvements compared to CPS < 5 patients. In the CheckMate 649 trial, no significant OS benefit was observed in the nivolumab plus chemotherapy group with CPS < 5 (hazard ratio: 0.95), indicating no difference compared to the chemotherapy alone group. The chemotherapy alone group in the trial showed a median PFS of 6.0 months and OS of 11.1 months. These findings closely resemble the outcomes observed in patients with CPS < 5 in our cohort, where the median PFS was 6.1 months and OS was 14.6 months.

Although trends in PFS and OS between the CPS ≥ 5 and CPS < 5 groups were observed in our cohort, these differences did not reach statistical significance. This lack of significance may be attributed to several factors, including the relatively small sample size, which reduces the statistical power to detect meaningful differences. As a result, the observed trends may not accurately reflect true differences between the groups. In addition to the small sample size, other factors such as variability in patient characteristics and treatment heterogeneity could have contributed to the lack of statistically significant differences. These confounding factors might have diluted the potential impact of CPS on survival outcomes, and further studies with more homogeneous populations are warranted. Further investigations with larger patient populations are necessary to explore these potential differences in survival outcomes and to determine whether the observed non-significant trends in PFS and OS can be validated. This would help clarify the role of CPS in influencing treatment response and survival.

The incidence of treatment-related AEs in our cohort generally aligned with the results from the CheckMate 649 trial, except for hematologic toxicities (neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia), which were relatively higher in our study. Specifically, we reported any-grade neutropenia in 63.0% of patients, anemia in 22.2%, and thrombocytopenia in 63.0%, with grade 3–4 neutropenia observed in 22.2% of cases. Several factors may contribute to these findings. First, four patients in our cohort received FLOT chemotherapy, which has been associated with a higher incidence of neutropenia [28]. A study of 206 patients undergoing perioperative FLOT in Italy reported a neutropenia rate of up to 19.9% [29]. Second, tolerance to chemotherapy may be relatively poorer in the Asian population. In the Chinese subgroup analysis of the CheckMate 649 trial, the incidence of treatment-related AEs was higher compared to the overall trial population [20, 21, 30]. Furthermore, hematologic toxicities, including neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, were more frequent in the Chinese subgroup compared to the entire trial population [20, 21, 30]. Therefore, bone marrow suppression is a significant consideration for these patients, particularly in the Asian population.

The incidence of irAEs in our cohort was relatively lower than that reported in the CheckMate 649 trial, but higher than in the KEYNOTE 859 study [20, 21, 23]. Compared to the CheckMate 649 trial, our cohort experienced a lower occurrence of hepatitis, dermatitis, colitis, and hypo- or hyperthyroidism. This discrepancy may be attributed to the shorter exposure duration to nivolumab in our cohort, as the median PFS was 6.1 months, compared to 7.7 months among all randomized patients in the CheckMate 649 trial. Another potential explanation could be the dosage difference; in our cohort, nivolumab was administered at 3 mg/kg, whereas in the CheckMate 649 trial, a fixed dose of 240 mg was used. Additionally, we examined the relationship between the occurrence of irAEs and clinical outcomes. In our cohort, nine patients who developed irAEs had shorter PFS (5.0 months versus 8.2 months) and OS (13.5 months versus 15.6 months) compared to the remaining 18 patients who did not experience irAEs, though the differences were not statistically significant.

In our cohort, three chemotherapy regimens were combined with nivolumab as first-line treatments: FOLFOX, modified XELOX, and FLOT. Typically, FOLFOX and XELOX are regarded as standard first-line treatments for advanced GC and are widely used in clinical practice. Additionally, the combination of FLOT and nivolumab is of particular interest. Similar to the use of durvalumab plus FLOT in the peri-operative setting of the MATTERHORN study, this combination may offer enhanced efficacy [31]. On the other hand, 17 patients experienced disease progression while on first-line treatment with nivolumab plus chemotherapy. Among these patients, more than half (64.7%) subsequently received additional anti-ancer therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. The most common regimen chosen was docetaxel plus ramucirumab, which is frequently used as a second-line treatment for GC in Taiwan due to the reimbursement of docetaxel under the health insurance system, whereas paclitaxel is not covered. This combination regimen has been established in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer based on findings from the REVEL trial and has been under investigation for GC treatment in clinical trials conducted in Japan [32, 33]. In one case within our cohort, a patient received alpelisib due to the presence of a PIK3CA mutation identified through next-generation sequencing, which resulted in a rapid response to this targeted therapy. Additionally, two patients underwent nivolumab rechallenge after disease progression on second-line chemotherapy regimens, based on their initial favorable response to first-line nivolumab plus chemotherapy.

Several limitations were identified in our cohort study. Firstly, due to its retrospective nature and relatively small sample size, there may have been inherent selection biases. The small sample size of 27 patients is a limitation of this study, particularly when making subgroup comparisons. A limited sample size reduces the statistical power to detect significant differences, which may result in a higher risk of Type II errors. This means that some potential differences between subgroups might not have been identified, such as survival analysis based on CPS status. Secondly, certain common chemotherapy regimens, such as paclitaxel or irinotecan, are not covered by Taiwan’s health insurance system. This limitation could have restricted the choice of subsequent treatments following progression on first-line therapy, which may differ from practices in other countries. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the few investigations into the real-world efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy for the treatment of advanced HER2-negative GC.

Conclusions

The results of our study demonstrate the efficacy and safety of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in real-world practice support its adoption as a new standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced HER2-negative GC, particularly those with CPS ≥ 5.
