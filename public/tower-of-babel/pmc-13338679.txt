Infective Endocarditis in a Previously Healthy Adolescent Caused by Streptococcus sanguinis: Diagnostic Challenges and Clinical Implications
Authors: Boram Jang, Young Min Cho, Joowon Lee, Young Hwan Song, Hyoung Woo Chang, Hyunju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infective endocarditis (IE) is uncommon in children, particularly in those without congenital heart disease or indwelling catheters. We describe an adolescent patient without prior history of cardiac disease or previously diagnosed predisposing conditions who developed Streptococcus sanguinis endocarditis of the native mitral valve. Despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, the clinical course was complicated by acute mitral regurgitation and heart failure requiring valve replacement. This report highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of pediatric IE in the absence of traditional risk factors and emphasizes the importance of early clinical suspicion, pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical intervention.

The 2015 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines on childhood infective endocarditis (IE) [1] report that 8–10% of cases occur in children with no prior health issues or identifiable risk factors. Similarly, a Korean study conducted from 2004 to 2019 found that 4.9% of IE cases occurred in previously healthy children without structural heart disease or other clear risk factors [2].

We report a previously healthy 17-year-old male treated for IE following a month-long fever. This emphasizes considering IE and atypical infection sources in children with prolonged fever lacking cardiac history.

A 17-year-old male presented to the emergency room (ER) following 26 days of fever, headache, and generalized weakness. The patient was an ordinary high school student with a previously unremarkable medical history. He was taking oral methylphenidate for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and had no significant underlying disease, including congenital heart disease (CHD). He did not have a central venous catheter. He underwent bilateral meniscal repairs 4 and 2 years ago, with a left knee implant. He reported a recurrent buttock boil over the past 3 years, with the most recent episode occurring 1 week before presentation. Three weeks prior, a 1-cm right lower eyelid mass was drained without purulence; biopsy confirmed a cyst. He had been taking oral doxycycline for acne for 2 weeks, which was completed one week prior to his presentation at the ER. No other antimicrobial agents were administered before the ER visit, including during the drainage of the eyelid cyst. Height was 183.3 cm (90–95th percentile), weight 54.25 kg (5–10th percentile), and body mass index 16.12 kg/m2. The patient was tall and lean and appeared in no acute distress. Initial vital signs: blood pressure 101/68 mmHg, heart rate 97/min, respiratory rate 22/min, and body temperature 38.3°C. His heartbeat was regular, without murmur. Initial labs showed white blood cell 10.35 × 103/μL (86.3% neutrophils) and C-reactive protein (CRP) 4.78 mg/dL.

Other findings including chest plain posteroanterior radiograph were unremarkable. He was discharged after conservative treatment but recalled the next day as Gram-positive cocci were identified in two sets of initial blood cultures (CRP, 5.04 mg/dL). Empiric ampicillin and cefotaxime were initiated upon admission.

At admission, he complained of persistent fever and chills, headache, rhinorrhea, generalized weakness, and a 50% reduction in oral intake. His temperature exceeded >38ºC 2–3 times a day and he was administered non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs 2–3 times a day.

On the day of admission, the initial echocardiography (EchoCG) results showed vegetation at the anterior mitral leaflet (AML) but there was no definitive evidence of mitral valve prolapse.

During the first ER visit and the following day, two separate blood cultures grew penicillin-intermediate Streptococcus sanguinis (maximum inhibitory concentration, 0.25). On hospital day (HD) 2, antibiotics were switched to intravenous ampicillin (2 g q4hrs) and gentamicin (3 mg/kg qd) [3]. Follow-up cultures were negative.

The patient’s fever continued to peak >38ºC 1–2 times a day. On HD 3, the patient developed sudden dyspnea and chest discomfort. Chest anteroposterior radiograph revealed pulmonary edema. Hypotension (80/43 mmHg) and a new systolic murmur prompted transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) with high-flow nasal cannula support. Serum N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (pro-BNP) was 517.7 pg/mL (normal range <120 pg/mL). EchoCG revealed severe mitral regurgitation (MR), a suspected vegetation, and ruptured chordae tendineae of the AML (Fig. 1A), along with a flail anterior mitral leaflet during systole (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with IE, fulfilling two major criteria [34]: two separate blood cultures confirming the growth of viridans streptococci and EchoCG showing vegetation.

Intravenous dobutamine and nitroprusside were administered to improve cardiac output and decrease afterload. Acute mitral insufficiency with ventricular failure prompted a consultation with thoracic surgery [5]. In the ICU, the patient's fever resolved on day 3 of targeted antibiotic treatment (HD 4). His vital signs remained stable, and after confirming the final negative blood culture, mitral valve replacement (MVR) surgery was performed on HD 7. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed severe MR, A2/A3 chordae ruptures, 1 cm vegetation, and a normal left ventricular ejection fraction. Surgical findings revealed no left atrial thrombus; however, the P3 portion of the mitral valve was severely damaged, and old vegetation was attached to it. A2/A3 of the mitral valve showed multiple ruptured chordae, subvalvular vegetation, and destruction. As a result, repair was not possible, and a mechanical MVR was performed.

The excised mitral valve anterior leaflet showed fibromyxoid valvulopathy with focal active inflammation and necrosis on histopathological examination (Fig. 2). No aerobic or anaerobic bacteria were isolated from the tissue cultures. 16s rRNA PCR was not performed on the surgical specimen as the causative pathogen, S. sanguinis, was definitively identified through multiple blood cultures. Postoperatively, the patient was maintained on intravenous antibiotics and started on oral warfarin. Given the tall and lean feature (dolichostenomelia), arm span/height >1.05, thumb sign (Steinberg sign) positive, joint hypermobility in the setting of MR, evaluation for Marfan syndrome was initiated. Three days after surgery (HD 10), EchoCG revealed a well-functioning prosthetic valve and normal left ventricular (LV) function. As the patient's overall condition improved, he was transferred to the general ward on day 13.

On HD 20, dyspnea and fever developed. Echocardiography revealed a large fibrinous effusion, indicating post-pericardiotomy syndrome. Prednisolone, colchicine, furosemide, spironolactone, and enalapril were initiated. On HD 27, the effusion decreased to small-to-moderate with good LV function. The patient received intravenous antibiotics until HD 49 (6 weeks and 5 days after blood culture negative conversion) and was discharged in good overall condition after discontinuing the antibiotics. Comprehensive evaluations for immunological, and rheumatological etiologies revealed no abnormalities. Genetic testing for Marfan syndrome was also negative. The initial dental consultation and panoramic radiograph conducted on the admission day showed no evidence of acute or chronic odontogenic lesions. Cultures from the left buttock lesion were also negative. Notably, a follow-up panoramic radiograph taken several months after discharge subsequently identified chronic periodontitis and generalized alveolar bone loss that were not clinically or radiographically apparent during the initial hospitalization.

According to the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria, our case is classified as Definite IE. S. sanguinis is now recognized as a typical IE pathogen (Major Microbiologic Criterion). The intraoperative findings of vegetation and valvular destruction fulfill the newly established Surgical Major Criterion, which, combined with the echocardiographic evidence of vegetation and fever, confirms the diagnosis under the most recent guidelines, fulfilling 3 major and 1 minor criterion [4].

Up to 56% of pediatric IE cases now occur without pre-existing heart disease. This group is typically older (median 14 vs. 7 years in CHD), peaking during late adolescence (17–20 years) [6].

In a Korean study of 96 pediatric IE cases (2004–2019), 84.4% involved structural cardiac abnormalities. Among 15 children with structurally normal hearts, most had underlying conditions with central venous catheters; only five were previously healthy [2]. Staphylococcus aureus (32.9%) and viridans streptococci (30.2%) were the most common pathogens. The surgery and mortality rates were 66.7% and 7.9%, respectively [2].

Viridans-type streptococci (α-hemolytic streptococci groups such as S. mitis, S. anginosus, S. mutans, S. salivarius, S. bovis, S. sanguinis, and S. mitis) and S. aureus remain the leading causative agents for endocarditis in pediatric patients. S. aureus endocarditis predominates in patients without heart disease; viridans streptococcal infections are more common after dental procedures [57].

Although the infection route was unclear, the patient had several potential risk factors such as prior knee surgery, eyelid biopsy, and acne treated with doxycycline. Despite initial negative findings, chronic buttock lesion and delayed periodontitis diagnosis suggest these as probable portals of entry. According to the 2015 AHA guidelines on IE in childhood, as children age, their oral flora becomes more similar to that of adults, with an increase in the proportion of viridans streptococci [1]. Taken together, given the persistent isolation of S. sanguinis—a common oral commensal—from blood cultures, periodontitis is the most plausible portal of entry, outweighing the other potential risk factors.

This case highlights key clinical implications for pediatric IE. First, viridans streptococcal endocarditis, typically more indolent than S. aureus infection, can nonetheless progress rapidly to severe valvular destruction and acute heart failure in adolescents without known structural heart disease [8]. Second, underlying connective tissue vulnerability—suggested by characteristic phenotypic features including dolichostenomelia and a positive Steinberg sign and confirmed fibromyxoid valvulopathy—may predispose patients to severe mechanical complications despite the absence of genetically confirmed Marfan syndrome [9]. In addition, this case underscores the diagnostic challenges in identifying portals of entry when initial screenings are negative. Specifically, in cases involving oral flora such as S. sanguinis, clinicians should be aware that periodontal disease may initially remain clinically silent or radiographically undetectable, requiring careful follow-up even after the acute phase of infection.

Taken together, this case emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion for IE in previously healthy adolescents with persistent fever or bacteremia and supports a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that considers host susceptibility, subtle connective tissue abnormalities, and atypical infection sources.
