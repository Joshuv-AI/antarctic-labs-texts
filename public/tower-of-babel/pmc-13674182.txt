The last‐step integrator thesis: Heterarchical control of the mammalian gastrointestinal tract
Authors: William Bechtel, Leonardo Bich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo perform the work necessary to maintain themselves in diverse situations, organisms orchestrate the operations of a host of production mechanisms that individually perform specific physiological and behavioural activities. Effective orchestration depends upon control mechanisms that acquire and integrate information about conditions in the organism and its environment and deploy the results to adjust the operation of production mechanisms. How are control mechanisms organized? A common assumption is that they need to be organized in a pyramid, with controllers at a higher level overseeing the activities of numerous subordinate mechanisms, procuring information from them and sending directives to them. In this article, we challenge this view and argue that physiological control is organized heterarchically. To illustrate the distinctive features of heterarchical control, we examine the current understanding of the regulation of the mammalian gastrointestinal tract. We show that individual production mechanisms can be acted on by multiple control mechanisms. However, the activity of a production mechanism is not dictated by these control inputs, which can conflict: one may recommend one action, another a different action. It is up to the local production mechanism to integrate these inputs with its own current state in determining its operation. The integration of control signals that determines what action is performed is therefore not carried out at a highest level by a central decider issuing directives to a host of executors, but it is ultimately executed at the last step, within the controlled mechanism itself, and this is what we call the ‘last‐step integrator.’ Keywords: central nervous system, constraints, control, enteric nervous system, functional integration, gastrointestinal tract, heterarchy, hierarchy, mechanisms

Introduction

Living organisms employ energy and matter from the environment to produce, replace and repair their parts at different levels of organization (molecules, organs, etc.) and so maintain themselves alive. They do so through a host of activities such as procuring and taking in food, processing it, synthesizing proteins, assembling tissues, repairing wounds and moving around in the environment. These activities are mutually dependent and not carried out independently from one another. Accordingly, these activities, and the operation of proteins, organelles, tissues or organs that contribute to carrying them out, need to be integrated for the organism to maintain itself and not degrade and collapse into a mess of independent processes. To this end, organisms employ a host of control mechanisms that modify the operation of production mechanisms responsible for different physiological and behavioural activities. Control mechanisms measure various conditions in the organism and its environment and integrate these measurements so as to assess which actions will most probably contribute to the maintenance of the organism. Integration has two main dimensions. The first, information integration, concerns how information, procured from different sources, is combined, processed and distributed by control mechanisms to make it accessible for the modulation of the activity of different mechanisms in the organism. The second, activity integration, concerns how control inputs, potentially received from multiple control mechanisms, are integrated to generate activity. We refer to the combination of these two dimensions as functional integration.

How is each mode of integration achieved? A common assumption is that control is organized hierarchically: vertebrates employ a central nervous system in which afferent signals are integrated with each other at successive stages and decisions made centrally generate integrated commands that are distributed to different parts of the organism that carry out the requisite actions. In this article, we argue that organisms often employ a different, heterarchical, mode of integration and control. We illustrate heterarchical control by focusing on the control of the mammalian gastrointestinal tract. On the heterarchical view, information integration and activity integration take place in both the tissues themselves and throughout the nervous system in a highly distributed manner. Functional integration, comprising the co‐ordination of multiple controllers, is not performed by a central controller at the top but through the operation of control mechanisms operating throughout the organism. This has the potential to give rise to diverse commands being delivered to the most basic controlled mechanisms (which are positioned at the very bottom by those who envision a hierarchical scheme). Organisms possess an extensive and elaborate network of control mechanisms that feed input to the basic production mechanisms and generate the basic activities of the organism (secretions, movement, etc.). These local mechanisms accommodate the effects of different control mechanisms acting upon them and are the ultimate determiners of how they will act. We call this the ‘last‐step integrator thesis.’

We view the last‐step integrator thesis to be a consequence of heterarchy, which allows for multiple controllers to act on the basic production mechanisms. To support our thesis that last‐step control is characteristic of biology, we begin in Section “What is Heterarchy” by introducing heterarchy and contrasting it with hierarchy. Although hierarchical organization is often represented in a pyramid, heterarchical organization corresponds more closely to an inverted pyramid. The analysis in this section is abstract. To make the case that control in biological organisms better fits with the heterarchical picture, in Section “Heterarchical control of the gastrointestinal tract” we discuss a specific example: control in the mammalian gastrointestinal tract, especially in the stomach and intestines. Starting with the production mechanisms that require control, absorptive and secretory epithelial cells and enteric muscles, we trace some of the diverse pathways through which control is exercised on them. We begin with the endocrine and neural control that is exercised within the gastrointestinal tract itself. These control mechanisms are themselves numerous and operated on by many others located in the central nervous system. We need to be selective and will focus first on brainstem control involving the dorsal vagal complex. We then turn to one example of yet further order of control that draws upon the dorsal vagal complex and acts on it, a network involving the arcuate nucleus, the lateral hypothalamus and the periventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus. Regions in both the brainstem and hypothalamus integrate multiple sources of information and provide inputs to control and production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract that must integrate them into actions.

With this grounding for our discussion, in Section “Production and control: integration of fixed and flexible constraints”, we return to a more abstract analysis, developing a theoretical framework for understanding how control mechanisms integrate information and production (and control) mechanisms integrate inputs from control mechanisms into actions. On the account we offer, biological mechanisms consist of constraints that direct the flow of free energy (often provided by energy molecules such as adenosine triphosphate, ATP) into the performance of work. For what we term production mechanisms, this work involves activities such as synthesizing and secreting molecules or contracting muscles. One mechanism can control another when its work involves changing the constraints in the mechanism it controls, thereby altering the work it performs. The control mechanism integrates information from multiple sources, providing outputs to production mechanisms. The production mechanisms, in turn, integrate inputs from potentially multiple control mechanisms with their current configuration of constraints in order to produce a specific activity.

Section “Sorting out all the control messages: the last‐step integrator” pulls together the understanding of production and control mechanisms with the discussion of the heterarchical organization of control of the gastrointestinal tract. The production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract receive inputs from both local controllers and ones in the central nervous systems, with each setting some constraints in the production mechanisms. It is up to the integrative processes in the production mechanism to configure its constraints so that it performs in a specific manner. Throughout the organism, we are dealing with cells that integrate control inputs and, at the production level, we confront the last‐step integrators that generate activity. In the Conclusion, we summarize this core idea and discuss some of its implications.

What is heterarchy?

The importance of the last step in integrating control signals and determining how a system will behave is often obscured by the fact that we commonly analyse control systems from the top‐down. Scientists often begin their analysis of control in biological systems from what is considered to be the highest level and then investigate where it gets information from and where it sends directives to. In the case of mammals, the neocortex is taken to be the highest‐level controller, and, in the case of higher mammals, frontal areas of the cortex. Subcortical regions of the brain are viewed as supplying information to the neocortex, sometimes processing it along the way, and executing the commands issued by the neocortex, sometimes filling in information needed to carry out the directive. These directives are then carried out by muscles or glands that simply implement them.

Looked at from the top, control appears to be organized into a pyramid in which information from local controllers is funnelled to ones at higher levels and commands are directed from the top down (Fig. 1). At each level, control is distributed over several subordinates which report to just one superior. Control involves ‘superseding levels of evaluation, ranging from simple reflex arcs to complex central mechanisms that represent the highest level of integration’ (Berntson et al., 2016, p. 404). In this type of organization integration takes place at the highest level, which receives many inputs whose integration requires high processing capacity and is subject to bottlenecks. Lower levels, instead, exhibit limited integrative capacity (Berntson & Cacioppo, 2008).

It is often assumed that such hierarchical organization is the only scheme that could produce coherent behaviour as it allows conflict, when it arises, to be settled by the high‐level controller.As a result, often no conceptual alternative is presented to hierarchical organization. McCulloch (1945), however, advanced an alternative when, in the course of his pioneering research on neural networks, he investigated what types of networks could produce transitive and non‐transitive preferences. Inspired by the work of early cyberneticists (Rosenblueth et al., 1943) on negative feedback, McCulloch represented preferences in terms of negative feedback loops and investigated how negative feedback loops could be linked to represent preference orders. If the order was transitive (A preferred to B, B to C and A to C), he envisaged multiple loops organized hierarchically one inside the other, with each loop further out able to inhibit that within it (Fig. 2A ). If the order was non‐transitive (i.e. when C was preferred to A), this order was broken: an inner loop had to send inhibition to a loop outside the one surrounding it (Fig. 2B ). For him, what was significant was that such an arrangement was more complex than could be represented in a plane. What is important for our purposes is that McCulloch coined the term heterarchy to designate the deviation from hierarchy represented by non‐transitive inferences.

Although McCulloch did not elaborate the concept of heterarchy further and did not return to it in subsequent research, the notion has been adopted sporadically by others to express a rejection of hierarchy. Accordingly, it has made cameo appearances in the work of other theorists in cybernetics (Von Foerster, 1984), artificial intelligence (Minsky & Papert, 1971), cognitive science (Hofstadter, 1980), theoretical biology (Pattee, 1991) and the social sciences (Crumley, 2015; Cumming, 2016; Hejl, 1990).

With respect to physiology, to our knowledge only a few scientists have explicitly employed the term or discussed, without using the term, divergences from pure hierarchical organizations. For example, Berntson & Cacioppo (2008; see also Norman et al., 2014) employ the term heterarchy to describe how control can skip levels: instead of traversing all the intermediate levels, sometimes information from lower levels is transmitted directly to the neocortex and sometimes commands from the neocortex can operate directly upon the controlled lower‐level processes. The result is a view that resembles McCullogh's representation of non‐transitive preferences (Fig. 2B ). Neuroscientist Cohen (2000) invokes the term heterarchy in her account of motor control to signal her rejection of hierarchy and emphasis on coupling between subsystems and bidirectional interactions between motor controllers and the central nervous systems.

Other neuroscientists such as Swanson do not employ the term but advance perspectives that depart from traditional hierarchical ones. Swanson (2000), when characterizing the behavioural control column as a classic hierarchical architecture proceeding downward from the hypothalamic controller to motorpool neurons, points out that all nuclei at each level generate a dual projection, with ascending and descending branches, a feature that departs from a strict hierarchical view. Thompson & Swanson (2003) proposed that the hypothalamic visceromotor pattern generator, in a manner analogous to somatic pattern generators, co‐ordinates neuroendocrine and autonomic outputs into organized functional patterns. Although the authors situate the network within a hierarchical organization beneath the behaviour control column and above the neuroendocrine/preautonomic motor zone, they propose that the six nuclei are extensively interconnected, enabling distributed processing. In his ongoing research on the hypothalamus and higher brain centres, Swanson has proposed replacing a hierarchical perspective with a network perspective: ‘Because “higher” and “lower” are difficult to define objectively in this organization, a network model of organization (e.g. the Internet) is clearly favoured over a hierarchical model (e.g. the US Army) (Thompson & Swanson, 2010, p. 15239). In subsequent connectomics research on the hypothalamus (Hahn et al., 2019) and the whole mammalian brain (Swanson et al., 2024), Swanson advances a nested hierarchy account of topological brain organization but identifies hubs and rich‐club organization that supports an integrated network perspective on function.

Finally, closer to the example we focus on here, in his research on the gut–brain axis, Mayer emphasizes the importance of considering bidirectional interactions between lower and higher centres, but does not use the term heterarchy (Mayer, 2011; Mayer, Nance & Chen 2022). Our proposal differs from the works we just mentioned (which recognize features of control architectures that depart a strict hierarchical view and attempt to amend this view) in that we aim to flesh out the notion of heterarchy itself by providing a conceptual account of heterarchical control. After providing a theoretical framework to understand biological control in terms of heterarchy, we focus specifically on how heterarchical control inputs are integrated to generate activities in the gastrointestinal tract.

To provide a more precise account of heterarchical organization in biology, in this article, we focus on one feature of heterarchy that gives rise to what we are terming last‐step integrators: when looking up from one controlled agent rather than down from the top, one may find multiple controllers exercising influence over it. In biology, we argue, multiple controllers operating on component systems is what we should expect. The control systems of even the simplest organisms were not designed but are the product of variation and selective retention. If a mutation introduces a receptor into a component mechanism that makes it responsive to a chemical released elsewhere in the organism and it doesn't incapacitate the ability of the organism to reproduce, it may be retained. In some cases, such actions of one part of an organism on another will be beneficial in that they allow for local components to be informed by those elsewhere. Retention is indeed more probable in cases in which the new control connection improves the organisms ability to reproduce, but the account we are offering does not depend on evolution being an optimizer, it only has to produce what Partridge (1982) referred to as good‐enough calculi.

The introduction of variation in which one component of an organism ends up regulating the behaviour of another is common. A control component can itself become regulated by another control component, perhaps one that draws information from yet a larger domain, including outside the organism. These orders of control allow for a sense of levels in which higher‐level controllers have a larger scope. But these control components can be distributed and not subject to any one higher‐level controller. Moreover, even this minimal sense of levels can be violated if a component viewed as at a higher level is regulated in turn by a control mechanism at a lower level. Even to the extent one can identify levels of controllers, there may be many more controllers added to the system at any level, giving rise, at best, to an inverted pyramid (Fig. 1B ).

What we have said so far is intended to make it plausible that the organization of control in living organisms does not have to conform to a hierarchical pyramid. To ground our case that control in biology is in fact heterarchical, we turn in the next section to control in the mammalian gastrointestinal tract.

Heterarchical control of the gastrointestinal tract

The gastrointestinal tract presents many challenges to an organism. It is organized around a passageway through which elements from the external world, including numerous friendly and unfriendly organisms, pass. The intestinal tract contains the largest surface through which humans and other animals engage the external world. Just as it must do with respect to the world totally outside the organism, the organism must procure information about conditions in the gastrointestinal tract and respond appropriately. To do this, it relies on both endocrine signalling and the gastrointestinal tract's own nervous system, the enteric nervous system. Furness & Stebbing (2018) propose the enteric nervous system is a phylogenetic descendant of the first brain that emerged in hydra, an animal with a tubular body open to water from outside. To prepare for discussing these control activities, we begin by discussing the activities of the production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract, muscles and epithelial cells.

One activity is moving foodstuffs through the tract or, in some cases, pushing them back out of the entrance. This is carried out by longitudinal and circular smooth muscles that generate compressions. These muscles reside in two tissue layers behind the epithelial cells, a single layer of cells that constitutes the interface with the lumen. Appropriate timing results in a wave of sequential contractions of longitudinal muscles in successive segments of the gastrointestinal tract (Grider, 2003). As they contract, longitudinal muscles shorten the current segment, expanding its circumference. When circular muscles immediately behind the food contract, they exert propulsive force. Simultaneously, those ahead relax, opening a space for the food to move forward. The sequence of contractions usually favours peristalsis, pushing forward towards the anus (Sharkey & Mawe, 2022). However, the sequence can be reversed through retroperistalsis, resulting in vomiting that expels detected toxins or irritants. In addition to moving food forwards or backwards, simultaneous contractions of circular muscles serve to mix the contexts in the lumen.

The physiological engagement with food is via the epithelial cells that line the surface of the gastrointestinal tract. There are many different types of epithelial cells, some of which release chemicals into the tract that break down the food to extract their nutrients, whereas others absorb nutrients and transport them to the blood or interstitial space. For example, in the stomach, parietal cells release hydrochloric acid that acidifies the lumen, whereas chief cells secrete pepsinogen (the precursor of the protease pepsin), gastric lipase and leptin, and surface mucous cells and mucous neck cells secrete mucus and bicarbonate that protect the gastric mucosa from damage resulting from acids or enzymes (Simpson & Jain‐Spangler, 2026). The intestines contain a large variety of secretory cells including, for example, Brunner's glands in the small intestine that secrete alkaline bicarbonate that neutralizes gastric acid and goblet cells in the colon that secrete MUC2 mucin (Krause, 2000). Many gastrointestinal enzymes secreted into the small intestine are synthesized in other organs, such as the pancreas (responsible for synthesis of trypsin, lipase, amylase, DNase and RNase, among others) and then transported to the small intestine for release and activation (Karpińska & Czauderna, 2022). Yet other enzymes are synthesized and released by bacteria in both intestines.

The intestines contain many absorptive cells (enterocytes in the small intestine and colon) through which the products of the catabolic activities of gastrointestinal enzymes are transported into the organism. To expand the surface area for absorption, these cells manifest extensive apical microvilli (constituting the brush border). Employing specialized transporters, these cells take up nutrients (e.g. glucose, peptides, amino acids, vitamins, minerals), as well as various ions (e.g. sodium, calcium, magnesium) (Kong et al., 2018). As a result of being connected by tight junctions, these cells realize a barrier that prevents bacteria and other material from crossing into the organism. A multitude of enzymes synthesized in these cells complete the digestion of numerous nutrients. The products are then transported into the bloodstream (Miron & Cristea, 2012). In addition to their processing of nutrients, these cells synthesize and secrete hormones, as we discuss shortly.

The production mechanisms involved in these activities must be carefully regulated. For example, many gastrointestinal enzymes are just as capable of catabolizing the epithelial cells and other gastrointestinal tissues as they are of digesting foods and so must be released only when foodstuffs are available and when mucus is also released to protect the epithelium. Likewise, the contractions of muscles must be co‐ordinated so that they occur in an order that moves food through the gastrointestinal tract at the same time as allowing adequate time for the enzymes released in each tissue to carry out their activities. The primary control processes that procure information and act on production mechanisms are situated in the gastrointestinal tract itself. They involve enteroendocrine cells that release hormones and enteric neurons that activate muscles as well as release hormones. A second iteration of control is accomplished in the brainstem were both neural and chemical afferent signals from the gastrointestinal tract and elsewhere give rise to efferent signals that act on enteric neurons, modulating their signalling to muscles and release of hormones. By drawing on signals from throughout the gastrointestinal tract (as well as the cardiovascular system and respiratory system), the brainstem centres are able to provide efferent signals that represent a broader perspective on the state of the gastrointestinal tract and the organism. Yet more inclusive information about the state of the organism is available at mid‐brain and forebrain structures that receive inputs from the control systems in the brainstem, integrate them with other sources of information, including information about the external environment, and send efferents back through the brainstem to the production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract. At each iteration, cells draw upon information available to them, integrate them, and generate outputs, that when received (directly or indirectly) must be integrated by the production mechanisms, epithelial cells and enteric muscles, in determining their activity.

Control within the gastrointestinal tract

Even when the gastrointestinal tract is disconnected from the central nervous system, it co‐ordinates the operation of its production mechanisms so as to propel food through it and extract nutrients from the food. This is because the control that is necessary to maintain the basic operation of these mechanisms is local to the gastrointestinal tract. It takes two forms: endocrine and neural. In both cases, this involves the control components registering and processing conditions in the local environment and sending efferent signal to the local production mechanisms.

The control of epithelial cells relies on enteroendocrine cells that are located among the epithelial cells but make up less that 1% of their population. Each of these employs receptors that are responsive either to mechanical forces or to particular molecules, some released by epithelial cells, and releases endocrines in response that bind to and modify the operation of epithelial cells (Bany Bakar et al., 2023). For example, among the enteroendocrine cells in the stomach are G cells that release gastrin in response to mechanosensation. When gastrin binds to receptors on chief cells, they upregulate secretion of pepsinogen. Several other enteroendocrine cells interact with this process. Gastrin also binds receptors on enterochromaffin‐like cells, resulting in the release of histamine, which in turn upregulates pepsinogen release from chief cells. In addition to increasing pepsinogen release, histamine together with gastrin increases the release of acid by parietal cells (Simpson & Jain‐Spangler, 2026). Countering these actions, D cells are activated by carbohydrates, fats and products of protein digestion (as well as a variety of hormones generated further along the gastrointestinal tract such as glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1), cholecystokinin (CCK), and acetylcholine and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) released from neurons. D cells secrete somatostatin, which binds G‐protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) on parietal cells, inhibiting their activity (Simpson & Jain‐Spangler, 2026). It should already be clear that in the stomach multiple hormones are acting on epithelial cells, which must integrate these different inputs to determine their activities.

The same is true in the small intestine (Gribble & Reimann, 2016, 2019). Individual enteroendocrine cells detect specific nutrients and release hormones: I cells secrete CCK, K cells secrete GIP and L cells secrete GLP‐1 and GLP‐2 and peptide YY (PYY). Receptors for each of these endocrines are distributed throughout the gastrointestinal tract so that individual endocrines often have multiple effects. For example, there are CCK receptors on (i) smooth muscles in the gallbladder that regulate its contraction, (ii) on acinar cells in the pancreas that stimulate enzyme secretion, (ioo) on the pyloric sphincter that regulate gastric emptying and (iv) on interstitial Cajal‐like cells that regulate motility in bile ductions as well as regions in the central nervous system discussed below. Not only are there multiple targets of individual endocrines, but also individual cells often have receptors for multiple endocrines. Of particular note are enteroendocrine cells that act to regulate muscle contraction. Motilin is released in a rhythmic manner from M cells in the intestinal mucosa in response to acid from the stomach entering the intestine and acts on GPCRs on neurons in the myenteric plexus, enhancing activity of both cholinergic and serotoninergic neurons, resulting in rapid contraction of smooth muscles (Deloose et al., 2019).

The co‐ordinated contraction of muscle cells is in part facilitated by gap junctions connecting the cytoplasm of muscle cells directly to that of interstitial cells of Cajal (ICCs) and to that of PDGFRα+ cells in what is known as the SIP syncytium (Browning et al., 2026; Koh et al., 2024). The ICCs are pacemaker cells that generate rhythmic contractions of the connected cells. For co‐ordination over greater differences, the gastrointestinal tract employs its own neural network, the enteric nervous system that extends from the oesophagus to the anus. In humans, it consists of ~500 million neurons classified into 15 types (Spencer & Hu, 2020; Wood, 2018). These neurons are organized into many ganglia that are situated in two layers or plexuses: the myenteric plexus between the layers of longitudinal and circular muscles and the submucosal plexus between the circular muscles and the mucosa (Fig. 3). Most ganglia also contain numerous glial cells that interact not only with enteric neurons but also with epithelial and immune cells (Browning et al., 2026). Neurons in the submucosal plexus are either intrinsic primary afferent neurons or motor neurons, with afferent neurons synapsing directly onto motor neurons. The motor neurons project into the epithelium, where they release acetylcholine near epithelial cells, modulating their secretory activity as well as blood flow (Walsh & Zemper, 2019). Motor neurons in the myenteric plexus typically synapse onto circular and longitudinal muscle cells, with the connections between the afferent and motor neurons frequently mediated by Interneurons. Motor neurons release either acetylcholine to elicit contraction or inhibitory transmitters such as nitric oxide to relax circular muscles. The intrinsic primary afferent neurons are frequently polymodal, detecting and integrating both mechanical and chemical stimuli, including many of the hormones released by enteroendocrine cells.

Historically, connections within the enteric nervous system have been viewed as implementing simple reflex arcs, each of which produces a specific response to a specific condition that is detected (Furness & Costa, 1987). The complex interconnections within the enteric nervous system suggest a different perspective, with multiple sources of information being integrated within the network and with the network able to initiate its own rhythmic behaviours (Sharkey & Mawe, 2022).

The activity of enteric muscles is also modulated by enterochromaffin cells located among epithelial cells in the crypts of both intestines (Bellono et al., 2017). Among other transmitters, these cells release serotonin in response to both detection of various chemicals in the lumen as well as to mechanosensors that detect movements in the gastrointestinal tract. In addition to activating smooth muscles engaged in peristaltic contractions, serotonin promotes fluid secretion from various secretory cells and activates neurons signalling to the central nervous system. Serotonin is just one of several neurotransmitters that are released into the gastrointestinal tract by enterochromaffin cells and act on muscles as well as epithelial cells (Song et al., 2025). These neurotransmitters modulate production mechanisms in ways that go far beyond simple reflexes.

A particularly sophisticated mechanism through which the gastrointestinal tract controls its own operation involves the ileal brake. In response to detecting unabsorbed nutrients, especially lipids (free fatty acids, triglycerides, phospholipids), proteins and amino acids, and carbohydrates, L‐cells in the small intestine and colon release PYY, which acts on excitatory enteric neurons to block release of acetylcholine, and GLP‐1, which acts on inhibitory enteric neurons to release nitric oxide. The effect is to delay emptying of contents of the stomach into the intestine by contracting the pyloric sphincter (Grunddal et al., 2016). Both PPY and GLP‐1 are also able to act directly on enteric muscles, which integrate both endocrine and neural inputs. When these actions of the brake force food to remain in the stomach, food is not only further digested, but also the stomach itself is distended, activating mechanosensitive signalling of satiation through the vagus nerve as we discuss below. The various hormones released as part of the brake mechanism also act to inhibit secretion in both the stomach (gastric acid) and the intestine (pancreatic enzymes). Again, control even in the gastrointestinal tract is highly distributed and fails to conform to a simple hierarchy.

Before turning to control activities that are clearly part of the central nervous system, we briefly note how, in the course of preparing to send outputs to the central nervous system, four prevertebral ganglia (celiac, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric and aorticorenal ganglia) extend the local control of the enteric nervous system (Frantzides et al., 1987). These ganglia receive information about distension and motility via intestinofugal afferent neurons and send inhibitory postgangionic output to the myenteric and submucosal plexa, where they both directly stimulate sphincter muscles to contract and, via excitatory enteric neurons, indirectly inhibit acetylcholine release, reducing peristalsis and secretion. These ganglia allow for these output neurons to integrate a broader range of information than the local ganglia within the enteric nervous system itself. These ganglia also allow neural projections from the enteric neurons to pass through to the central nervous system via splanchnic nerves (these target the dorsal horn, the nucleus of the solitary tract, the hypothalamus, thalamus, and insular cortex) and receive inputs via preganglionic axons from the CNS. Accordingly, the outputs to enteric ganglia integrate information provided by CNS projections with the information provided by the intestinofugal neurons (Fig. 4).

This sketch of control within the gastrointestinal tract has only identified some of the modes of endocrine and neural control. The actual system is much more complex. For example, we have only identified one endocrine released by a given type of enteroendocrine cell, but most release multiple endocrines. The epithelial cells they target commonly have receptors for several endocrines. Although this makes it difficult to present a simple account of how control is achieved, it reveals that the control system even within the gastrointestinal tract is highly distributed so that multiple sources of information are available for integration in both the control network and the production mechanisms they target.

Gastrointestinal control in the brainstem

The enteroendocrine and enteric nervous systems, complemented by the prevertebral nuclei, provide sufficient control mechanisms to enable the production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract to process food, extract nutrients and expel waste (Li & Owyang, 2003). However, there are limitations to their ability to co‐ordinate mechanisms across the whole gastrointestinal tract and to respond to conditions elsewhere in the organism. In various conditions, the maintenance of the whole organism requires modulation of the activities of the gastrointestinal system. Such control is implemented in the central nervous system. In this subsection and the next, we describe two networks of control of the gastrointestinal tract, one involving the brainstem and one involving the hypothalamus. These illustrate the distributed heterarchical control of activities in the gastrointestinal tract. As in the previous section, we will focus on the regulation of the stomach and small intestine. Control of earlier areas (oesophagus) and later areas (colon and anus) involve many of the same central control mechanisms but also invoke, to a greater extent, cortical activity that is important for controlling voluntary swallowing and expulsion of waste. These are clearly important but, because appeals to the neocortex too easily distract focus from the diversity of central nervous system processes that regulate the gastrointestinal tract, we will not focus on them.

There are two neural pathways through which the central nervous system receives neural inputs from the gastrointestinal tract and sends efferent signals to the gastrointestinal tract: the vagus nerve and the splanchnic nerves (it also, at different points, receives endocrine inputs). Although there is significant differentiation of vagal and splanchnic processing, the two are also interconnected at various locations in the brainstem (e.g. rostral and caudal ventrolateral medulla and the parabrachial nucleus) and via higher brain centres such as the lateral hypothalamus, and both send projections back to the gastrointestinal tract, whether through the vagus or via splanchnic neurons (Browning et al., 2026). The vagal pathway primarily serves parasympathetic functions in which efferent projections promote activities such as secretion and motility and mediate activities such as vomiting, the efferents in the splanchnic pathways are largely sympathetic, inhibiting digestive activities at the same time as promoting alternative energetic activities such as gluconeogenesis. Both pathways, however, include efferent neurons that are excitatory and inhibitory. For simplicity, we will focus on the pathways involving the vagus nerve.

The vagus nerve carries afferent signals from the gastrointestinal tract and other visceral organs, such as the cardiovascular and respiratory systems, and efferent signals to them (Prescott & Liberles, 2022). The afferent inputs arise from some of the local control mechanisms we discussed above (in the case of neuroendocrine cells, they originate from extensions that have been designated neuropods; see Kaelberer et al., 2020), and the efferent outputs project back to them or directly to the production mechanisms. The vagus nerve terminates in the dorsal vagal complex, which historically was viewed as simply a gateway to anatomically higher‐level systems in the brain (Rogers et al., 1995), but is increasingly recognized as itself integrating neural and endocrine inputs from visceral organs and generating its own efferent signals that help regulate these systems (Powley, 2021).

The dorsal vagal complex consists of four nuclei (Chrobok et al., 2022). Afferent neurons in the vagus nerve have their cell bodies in the nodose ganglia, whose axons then project into the nucleus of the solitary tract. The nucleus of the solitary tract also receives inputs from neurons in the area postrema, which, lying outside the blood–brain barrier, respond to chemical signals in the blood (Price et al., 2008). The dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus is the source of efferent projection to the gastrointestinal tract where they synapse on enteric neurons, modifying their activity. Neurons in the dorsal motor nucleus integrate inputs from the nucleus of the solitary tract as well as those with cell bodies in the nodose ganglion and the area postrema. To interpret the processing in the dorsal vagal complex, researchers have attempted to identify specific reflex circuits, but neurons in the nucleus of the solitary tract and the dorsal motor nucleus each integrate multiple inputs, undermining simple reflex accounts of their operation. For example, some dorsal motor nucleus neurons receive inputs not just from the nucleus of the solitary tract and the area postrema, but also extend dendrites that project into the fourth ventricle, enabling them to respond to circulating endocrines (Travagli & Anselmi, 2016). In addition, their activity is subject to modulation by a host of neurotransmitters and modulators including dopamine, serotonin, opioid peptides, orexin, oxytocin, thyrotropin‐releasing hormone and GLP‐1. Rather than manifesting distinct circuits, as was initially assumed, Browning & Travagli (2014) report that ‘that individual vagal fibres contact many enteric neurons suggesting that the role of the vagus is a more generalized modulation of existing activity levels within enteric neural circuits’. The efferents back to local regions of the gastrointestinal tract thus reflect integration of information from multiple loci within the gastrointestinal tract (Browning et al., 2026).

Anatomically higher levels of control of the gastrointestinal tract

The dorsal vagal complex enables integration of information from throughout the gastrointestinal tract and other visceral organs. However, it is often important to integrate that information with additional information either about the state of the whole organism or conditions in the organism's environment. This is particularly true when the question is whether to eat (or continue eating) but also true when the question is how to use different energy sources. Accordingly, information integrated in the dorsal vagal complex is not only used directly to co‐ordinate activities in the gastrointestinal tract, but is directed to diverse regions in the midbrain and forebrain, including nuclei in the thalamus and hypothalamus, limbic structures such as the amygdala and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, and regions of the neocortex such as the insular cortex. Most of these projections involve the parabrachial nucleus, a highly interconnected collection of neuronal cell bodies situated at the junction of the hindbrain and midbrain (Pauli et al., 2022). The anatomically higher brain regions to which the parabrachial nucleus sends afferents also send projections back to it, enabling the outputs of the information integration in which they engage to be available to other anatomically higher brain regions.

To illustrate the control functions performed by these anatomically higher brain regions, we focus on one hypothalamic region, the arcuate nucleus, which directly receives input from the parabrachial nucleus, and sends outputs back to it as well as two other nuclei in the hypothalamus (Fig. 5): the lateral hypothalamus and the periventricular nucleus (Jais & Brüning, 2022). In addition to the connections we discuss, the arcuate is linked to many other brain regions, as discussed by Pessoa et al. (2019) and Pessoa (2022) ().

Together, these hypothalamic structures play fundamental roles in determining whether the animal terminates eating or continues to eat as well as how it utilizes available energy. The arcuate nucleus enables a competition between signals indicating that the animal needs food and others indicating that it is satiated and has sufficient energy (Brüning & Fenselau, 2023). It is well situated to carry out this competition because it not only receives neural inputs from the brainstem but also, as a result of being located adjacent to the median eminence outside the blood–brain barrier, responds to chemical signals carried by the blood. The competition is carried out through two populations of neurons, agouti‐related peptide/neuropeptide Y‐coexpressing (AgRP/NPY) neurons that promote eating and pro‐opiomelanocortin/cocaine‐amphetamine related transcript (POMC/CART)‐coexpressing neurons that suppress it (Jais & Brüning, 2022; Song & Choi, 2023) (Fig. 5). Among other inputs, AgRP/NPY neurons are responsive to ghrelin, a hormone released in the stomach when no food is being processed. Activation of POMC neurons, in contrast, is increased in response to leptin, a hormone released by adipose tissue in proportion to fat mass and so signals energy reserves, as well as insulin and serotonin (Yeo et al., 2021). All of these, as well as hormones such as CCK, act to inhibit AgRP/NPY neurons. AgRP/NPY neurons in turn inhibit POMC neurons (Al Massadi et al., 2017). This is in part reminiscent of the representation of heterarchy provided by McCullogh (Fig. 2B ), according to which control is realized by multiple control loops, one inside the other. Not only does each loop further out modulate those within it, as in the hierarchical view, but also the inner loops modulate those loops surrounding it.

Both populations of arcuate neurons send projections to neurons in the lateral hypothalamus and paraventricular hypothalamus. The lateral hypothalamus contains neurons called orexin neurons because, when they were discovered, they were taken to primarily promote eating (subsequently they have been shown to help maintain alertness and other activities). Orexin neurons play a central role in promoting eating both through direct projections to the parabrachial nucleus and other brainstem regions and through projections to areas thought to regulate hedonic eating (ventral tegmental area), motivational eating (amygdala) and cognitively controlled eating (paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus). Orexin neurons also send projections back to the arcuate, establishing a positive feedback with AgRP neurons at the same time as inhibiting POMC neurons (Arrigoni et al., 2019). POMC neurons, in contrast, project to melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R)‐expressing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus. The paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus contains even more MC4R neurons that are activated by POMC neurons and inhibited by AgRP neurons (Yeo et al., 2021). Both of these populations suppress eating through projections to the central lateral parabrachial nucleus, with the effect of decreasing the total time spent eating as well as the number of bouts of eating, and to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, with the effect of reducing meal size but not frequency (Brüning & Fenselau, 2023).

In addition to the control of eating itself, different regions of the hypothalamus regulate muscular and gastrointestinal activities in the gastrointestinal tract. In doing so, they send signals to some of the same control and production mechanisms as we discussed above (see Fig. 5). We offer selected examples. First, the orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus send projections to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, from which projections to the stomach act to stimulate gastric acid secretion by parietal cells and motility from muscles, as well as to the pancreas to promote secretion (Okumura & Takakusaki, 2008). In addition, through receptors in the small intestine and colon, orexin neurons act on enteric neurons and directly on smooth muscle to regulate motility (Baccari, 2010). NPY neurons in the arcuate also send projections that increase motility and transit (Zhang et al., 2019).

By contrast, cells in the periventricular hypothalamus respond to satiation signals from POMC neurons and act to reduce gastric motility via arginine vasopressin projections to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (Zhu et al., 2016). Oxytocin, a major peptide produced by periventricular hypothalamic neurons, has been shown to promote contraction of both longitudinal and circular muscles in the gastrointestinal tract (Alqudah et al., 2022). Other cells in the paraventricular nucleus initiate a cascade of reactions that result in cortisol release, for which there are receptors on various epithelial and neuroendocrine cells. In addition, cortisol also inhibits tight junction protein expression and, as a result, increases epithelial permeability. Finally, although details of how the signal is conducted are not clear, Toledo et al. (2025) have shown that stimulation of AgRP and POMC neurons in the arcuate nucleus result in changes to the composition of the microbiome.

With this example of a multilevel heterarchical control, we turn now to provide a theoretical framework for understanding how control is achieved through integration of fixed and flexible constraints and then articulate how a heterarchical network of control can evolve and how, within it, the last‐step integration of constraints determines the activity of mechanisms in organisms.

Production and control: integration of fixed and flexible constraints

We have characterized the mechanisms that carry out the activities of the digestive tract such as secreting enzymes, absorbing metabolites and generating motility, as production mechanisms. These mechanisms rely on multiple components, each performing its own task and being organized to produce what is desired. Philosophers of science have examined how scientists seek to understand these mechanisms by decomposing them into their components (Bechtel & Richardson, 1993/2010; Machamer, Darden, & Craver, 2000; Bechtel & Abrahamsen, 2005; Glennan & Illari, 2018). Research on these production mechanisms exemplifies this approach: accounts of cell secretion, for example, decompose epithelial cells into the activities of protein synthesis and folding in the ribosome, post‐translational modifications and packaging in the Golgi apparatus, and release from the membrane, with products at each step transported to the next locus.

Although Machamer et al. (2000) emphasized the active nature of the components of mechanisms, these philosophical accounts of mechanistic explanation failed to note that, in most cases, the activities depend on an energy source such as ATP. Drawing attention to this requirement, Winning & Bechtel (2018) recharacterized production mechanisms in terms more familiar to physicists: they constrain flows of free energy into the performance of work. Their account emphasizes two elements that need to be specified in accounts of production mechanisms: the source of free energy and the constraints that direct its release into changes to the material that is operated on. This approach belongs to a long tradition of theoretical biology and philosophy of biology focused on the notion of constraints (see, among others, Ceruti, 1986/1996; Deacon, 2012; Hooker, 2013; Juarrero, 1999; Kauffman, 2000; Kupiec, 2009; Montevil & Mossio, 2015; Moreno & Mossio, 2015; Mossio, Bich, & Moreno, 2013; Pattee, 1972). On this account, the activity of muscle contraction is understood as resulting from the hydrolysis of ATP imposing movement on parts of the myosin protein that enables it to exercise force on actin (Bechtel & Bollhagen, 2021).

Both the initial characterization of mechanisms and this revised one emphasize that mechanisms must be organized so that the various activities feed appropriately into each other. However, once we focus on why a production mechanism operates in the way it does in the context of an organism, the picture is much more complex.

To identify the composition of biological mechanisms and determine how the components move as they are performing their activities, researchers commonly create conditions in the laboratory in which the mechanisms continually operate except when the investigators perturb them. In organisms, however, such continuous activity would be counterproductive not to mention wasteful of energy. For the organism to take advantage of these mechanisms, they must be regulated so that they operate only when, and in the manner, needed (Bich et al., 2016). The framework developed by Winning & Bechtel (2018) provides a means not just for understanding the operation of mechanisms, but also their control. What makes control possible is that not all constraints are understood as fixed in the way they operate (as they are, for instance, for Montevil & Mossio, 2015). Many, as in Pattee's initial account (1972), are considered to be subject to change (see Bich, 2024a; Bich & Bechtel, 2022, for a discussion of the different interpretations of constraints and their implications for an understanding of biological organisms; for an alternative view not grounded in mechanisms but dynamical systems, see the discusson of ‘governing constraints’ by Juarrero, 2023). It is common that as it is operating, some of a mechanism's activities change its constraints, enabling the mechanism to carry out different activities in succession. In addition to such endogenous change, change can also be imposed on the constraints by an external mechanism. This is what makes it possible for a control mechanism to act on a production mechanism; by altering the constraints in the production mechanism, the control mechanism changes how the production mechanism operates. Yet other mechanisms external to a control mechanism can also operate on the control mechanism itself, a feature of control that will become important below. Importantly, as will be discussed in next section to flesh out the last‐step integrator thesis, multiple control mechanisms, with possibly conflicting demands, can operate on the same mechanism with resolution occurring at the effector. For now, we focus just on one mechanism acting to control another.

In one sense, one might speak of any mechanism that acts to alter the activity of another mechanism as controlling it. In this sense, a mechanism operating earlier in a sequence would control the next one by providing inputs such as reactants for chemical reactions to be carried out by the second mechanism. Typically, the notions of control and regulation require more than providing input in a broad sense (Bich et al., 2016). Control is generally understood in biology as the ability to actively modify the dynamics of a system in the service of goal states (Fell, 1997; Rosen, 1970). The use of such terms as goal, end or purpose might seem to be problematic in modern science: the scientific revolution is often viewed as a repudiation of such teleological perspectives. But theorists who were inspired by the ability of negative feedback to provide a form of control over mechanical systems argued that negative feedback in fact grounds a notion of end or purpose: the conditions maintained or achieved via negative feedback can be viewed as goals or ends of the system (Rosenblueth et al., 1943). This strategy for grounding notions like goal or end can be generalized. Although cyberneticists tended to focus just on negative feedback, organisms actually employ a host of strategies that serve to maintain conditions in them and their own viability, as illustrated in the broad range of procedures by which Bernard (1878) showed that birds and mammals maintained free and independent life. Cannon (1929) supplied the name homeostasis and envisaged multiple means by which organisms maintained themselves (Bechtel & Bich, 2024).

Rosenblueth and colleagues, as well as Bernard and Cannon, emphasized organizational schemes through which organisms obtain ends, but do not themselves identify and establish the ends. A complementary perspective was advanced by Maturana & Varela (1980), among others, when they characterized the activities of organisms as making the organisms themselves (which they termed autopoiesis). The entity they make is distinguished as an organized structure distinct from its environment. It is far from equilibrium. As articulated in the second law of thermodynamics, closed systems tend towards equilibrium. For an organism to avoid moving towards equilibrium with its environment, it must not be closed but open to energy outside itself. Moreover, its autopoietic activities must use this energy together with matter from the wider environment to continually build and repair itself (Bich, 2024b). The ‘must’ in the above sentences should be understood as part of a conditional sentence; if the organism is to continue to exist, it must perform these activities. Plenty of organisms die, a process in which they dissipate to equilibrium. Those that are alive today are the current individuals in lineages of organisms that have, from the origins of life to now, maintained a non‐equilibrium relation to their environment. Because they enshrine the production and control mechanisms required to do this, they can be understood as having self‐maintenance as their goal.

From that perspective, we can identify the goals of control mechanisms with their functions within the system: they act to maintain the organism through their actions on production mechanisms (Bich, 2024b). Since the organism needs different activities from production mechanisms under different conditions, production mechanisms must be controlled. For the actions of control mechanisms on production mechanisms to fulfill the goal of self‐maintenance, control mechanisms need to acquire and act on information about the conditions within the organism or the conditions the organism is confronting in its environment. Pattee (1991) characterizes this as making measurements. By carrying out measurements the control mechanism takes on particular configurations of constraints through its encounter with the condition about which it acquires information. The configuration of constraints acquired from the measurement process contributes to determining the action of the control mechanism on the constraints of the mechanism that is controlled.

In some cases, as with on–off switches, the changed constraints in the control mechanism may so act on the constraints in the controlled mechanism to fully determine its behaviour. But, in many cases, the control mechanism will only modify the constraints in the controlled mechanism and thereby modify the behaviour of the controlled mechanism without fully determining it. Both of these modes of control are illustrated in the control exercised within the gastrointestinal tract. We start with switch‐like control performed by enteric neurons on enteric muscles. It appears switch‐like in that these neurons respond to inputs in a threshold manner: if the input is sufficient, it configures constraints in the neuron such that it generates an action potential. When the action potential reaches the synapse to a muscle, it alters the constraints in the muscle in such a manner that it contracts. But this account is over simplified. Both neurons and muscles often require multiple inputs within a limited timeframe to respond. And whether they respond depends on the state of their internal constraints when the stimulus is provided. Moreover, in addition to the commonly described inotropic receptors, which respond to input by altering the ion concentration in the recipient neuron or muscle, neurons and muscles contain many metabotropic or GPCRs which respond by modifying the constraints related to metabolism in the recipient cell.

If neural control is less like a binary switch than often portrayed, this is even more true of endocrine control. Typically, individual enteroendocrine cells make multiple measurements and produce multiple endocrines. Epithelial cells have receptors for multiple endocrines and how they respond in terms either of secretion or absorption depends not only on the current constraints within them, but also on how they integrate these with the effects of endocrine receptors.

Before turning to illustrating how heterarchical control processes act to regulate epithelial and muscle cells in the gastrointestinal tract, there are some lessons we should draw about the organization of control. The plausibility of the perspective that individual control mechanisms operate as switches or rheostats stems in large part from designing experiments in which one maintains the production mechanism in a constant state and manipulates one control component at a time. From this perspective, it is natural to conceive of the control component as acting at a higher level and determining the behaviour of the production mechanism it controls: contraction in the case of muscles or secretion or absorption in the case of epithelial cells. This simple hierarchical framing, however, is an artifact of experimental designs. This framing loses its attractiveness when one looks beyond the experimental context and recognizes that under normal physiological conditions the responsiveness of production mechanisms varies depending on their internal states. Moreover, combining the results of multiple experimental designs, one recognizes that control in the gastrointestinal tract is highly distributed, with multiple control components each contributing to determining the activities of individual production mechanisms. Moreover, as will be argued in the next section, because they receive multiple, possibly conflicting, inputs from various control components, production mechanisms are last‐step integrators. Their behaviour depends on how they integrate inputs from diverse control elements with their own constraints in determining action.

Sorting out all the control messages: the last‐step integrator

A feature of hierarchical pyramids is that control at each level is segregated. Different control streams are separated from others until they are integrated at a higher level, with some streams only being integrated at the very highest level. The picture emerging from the earlier discussion of the control of the gastrointestinal tract, instead, is that of a host of control mechanisms that may possess several sensitive constraints that enable them to measure different variables. These in turn may act on the constraints of several other mechanisms both upstream and downstream (Fig. 1B ). Moreover, control mechanisms often exhibit cross‐talk, that is, they participate in signalling networks that transmit and integrate the result of multiple measurements and enable multiple control mechanisms to affect different production mechanisms. Cross‐talk is often conceptualized as disruptive. From a hierarchical perspective, it is. The command message may be transformed so that the controlled mechanism doesn't behave as it was instructed to. But, from a heterarchical perspective, it allows other sources of information, which may dictate a change in the command, to influence the signal the recipient receives. In this way, it allows the parts of a biological organism to co‐ordinate their operation; individual production mechanisms are deployed and their operation fine‐tuned in light of the circumstances the organism confronts.

Even just looking at the organization of the central nervous system, usually depicted as a centralized hierarchical system, we find this very heterarchical organization (Pessoa, 2022; Swanson, 2012). Traditional depictions of the action of the central nervous system on the gastrointestinal view it as an extension of the hierarchical relation between control mechanisms and production mechanisms within the gastrointestinal tract. The central nervous system receives information from the gastrointestinal tract and, after integrating it with information from other sources, sends efferent commands back to it (Mayer, 2011). Historically, the components of the dorsal vagal complex, especially the nucleus of the solitary tract and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, have been viewed as junction boxes: the locus of synapses that connected the vagus nerve to higher brain centres such as the hypothalamus (Rogers et al., 1995). Synapses in the nucleus of the solitary tract served to direct information to those higher brain centres, and synapses onto neurons in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus directed efferent commands to the production mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract. As we discussed in Section “Heterarchical control of the gastrointestinal tract”, however, current descriptions of the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus include not just synapses to neurons projecting to higher brain centres, but synapses between afferent neurons coming from the gastrointestinal tract and efferent neurons projecting to it.

Even those who portray the dorsal vagal complex as more than a way station still often view it as simply the locus of reflexes implementing relatively simple input‐output circuits in which the same input results in the same output. However, as discussed in Section “Heterarchical control of the gastrointestinal tract”, these circuits are not so simple (Browning et al., 2026). First, synapses in the dorsal vagal complex integrate multiple afferents and the affected neurons produce multiple efferents. By holding everything else constant, researchers can try to identify how the efferents from one neuron are affected by a given afferent, and create a portrayal of individual efferent neurons in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus as always producing the same response to a given input. But, once experimental control is relaxed, the picture becomes much more complex, with neurons from the dorsal motor nucleus receiving a variety of inputs that each modulate their activity. Likewise, they often send projections to multiple enteric neurons. This pattern of connectivity constitutes a tangled heterarchical network, not what one would expect in a hierarchically organized system. Second, individual neurons do not always respond the same even given what are taken to be the same input as a result of varying conditions (constraints) operative in the neurons themselves. Their response on a given occasion depends on how they integrate internal constraints and the inputs they receive.

When we look beyond the dorsal vagal complex, the organization gets even more complicated. We have focused on the hypothalamus and the outputs from the dorsal vagal complex to the hypothalamus and the projections back to the dorsal vagal complex. Similar to the connections in the dorsal vagal complex, these processing routes have been viewed as reflexes, but we have emphasized that neurons in them often integrate multiple inputs with varying internal conditions. Both populations of neurons we discussed in the arcuate nucleus receive numerous inputs, both from the dorsal vagal complex and via the circulation. Neurons in the hypothalamic regions to which they project receive inputs not just from the arcuate nucleus, but also from other regions of the hypothalamus and diverse neural systems ostensibly involved in other processing (Hahn et al., 2019).

Although heterarchical connections between control systems may seem to be chaotic, we can appreciate what they accomplish when we view them as situated within an organism. In that context, these interconnections between control mechanisms contribute to maintaining the organism by integrating multiple sources of information about conditions in the organism and the environment that are relevant to how production mechanisms should operate to maintain the organism. Even if we are unable to understand, at least with current tools, how this integration facilitates successful self‐maintenance, the fact that it has been maintained by evolution assures us that it has been effective (Partridge, 1982).

Our focus is on an important yet apparently counterintuitive feature of this heterarchical form of organization. Although each controller integrates different sources of information and is modulated by other controllers in the system, how the information is implemented depends ultimately on the very last mechanism involved: the controlled production mechanism. This feature may pass unnoticed if we adopt a hierarchical view characterized by a straightforward control architecture with independent control streams in which one final controller modulates the operation of a production mechanism. What makes the last step important is not only that it (the controlled production mechanism) integrates control inputs (like controllers do) but that it, rather than the highest level, determines the output. This is particularly significant when multiple different control signals arrive at a product mechanism. It then must integrate the different inputs it receives and its own internal constraints to arrive at a behaviour.

In the framework we developed above, what the outputs of control mechanisms do is alter the constraints in the controlled mechanism. If every other constraint in the production mechanism remains constant, then the change the control mechanism imposes on constraints in the production mechanism seems to determine its behaviour. Generally, this is not the case. Even when just one control mechanism is acting on the production mechanism, how the production mechanism responds depends on the constraints already instantiated in the production mechanism. The production mechanism accommodates within its existing constraints those modifications imposed on it to determine the work it performs. When these existing constraints are different, the control input will have different effects. In light of this, one might view imposed modifications of constraints as being integrated with the existing configuration of constraints in the production mechanism and thereby modulating its behaviour.

So already in the simple case when a single control mechanism modifies a constraint in a production mechanism, the way the latter carries out a certain task depends on how it accommodates the modification of its flexible constraints. However, as the examples discussed in earlier sections make clear, it is highly unusual for the constraints in a production or a control mechanism to only be modified by one control input. Diverse inputs, the result of measuring different variables, may act on either the same constraint in the controlled mechanism, or on different constraints. In either case, how the controlled mechanism responds will depend on how it integrates these inputs with its current constraints. When there are multiple inputs, they may act in a consistent fashion, enhancing the likelihood that the production mechanism will act in a particular manner. However, they may also act in an inconsistent way, some changing constraints to favour a given action, others in a way that favours inhibiting it. In this scenario, it is up to the controlled mechanism, the last‐step integrator, to determine how it responds to different combinations of actions performed on it by multiple control mechanisms.

The last‐step integrator thesis, therefore, combines two elements. The first is the fact that control inputs are integrated with the internal constraints of the controlled mechanisms, so that control inputs only modulate the activity of effectors rather than determining it. The second is that effectors receive multiple, potentially conflicting control inputs whose conflict is not resolved upstream but in the effector itself.

On this view, the last‐step integrator is whatever effector carries out a given activity by integrating control inputs. The last step is indexed to such activity. The same component, however, may do other things as well. Some cells can carry out two distinct activities, by acting as production mechanisms with regards to some activity and controllers of other production mechanisms on the basis of information. This does not mean they are not the last step with regards to that activity.

Let us consider the case of the control of the production mechanisms of the gastrointestinal tract. Epithelial cells or muscle cells are the production mechanisms. They can also operate as control mechanisms. Muscle cells, for example, may regulate each other via gap junctions and by virtue of sharing cytoplasm ICCs and PDGFR α+ so that the differentiation of production and control may not be sharp. However, only epithelial and muscle cells carry out digestive activities rather than processing information. For this reason, they can be identified as the ‘last steps’, as the last‐step integrator is indexed to a given digestive activity. Each of the diverse control inputs, involving hormones or neural stimulation, acts as a modulator, altering in some way the constraints in the muscle cells or epithelial cells. These muscle and epithelial cells in turn receive hormonal or neural inputs from multiple other control mechanisms, so that they are modulated not only on the basis of the presence, location and stage of digestion of food in the gastrointestinal tract itself but also according to the states of the organism and the environment. In order to illustrate this, we briefly return to the control processes we identified in Section “Heterarchical control of the gastrointestinal tract”.

Muscle cells along the gastrointestinal tract receive inputs not only from each other via gap junctions constituting the SIP syncytium, which enables basic co‐ordination of contractions, but also from motor neurons that promote contractions by releasing transmitters such as acetylcholine or inhibit it by releasing inhibitory transmitters such as nitric oxide. These motor neurons themselves respond to primary afferent neurons that are responsive to and integrate mechanical and chemical stimuli and regulate the motor neurons. The constraints in motor neurons are also affected by neurotransmitters such as serotonin. After release by enterochromaffin cells, serotonin diffuses through the connective tissues and modulates motor neurons. Serotonin, however, can also act directly on muscle cells to change constraints in a manner conducive of contraction. When the ileal brake is activated, it inhibits muscles both directly and via enteric neurons. Likewise, inputs from prevertebral ganglia inhibit activity by acting directly on some muscles and indirectly, via enteric neurons, on others. Enteric neurons also receive input from neurons in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus. Finally, various peptides synthesized by hypothalamic neurons act, either directly or indirectly via the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, on enteric neurons and muscles, providing other changes to constraints. Muscle contractions throughout the gastrointestinal tract are clearly modulated in multiple ways. The diverse muscle cells are the final integrator.

Turning now to epithelial cells, they too are the recipients of multiple control inputs that they integrate in determining their activity. Some of these inputs originate from enteroendocrine cells within the gastrointestinal tract itself. In the stomach, parietal cells receive inputs from gastrin and histamine that promote acid release and from somatostatin that inhibits this release. The same is true in the small intestine where epithelial cells are modulated by multiple endocrines released by different enteroendocrine cells. Epithelial cells also receive inputs from motor neurons that project into the epithelium, releasing acetylcholine or vasoactive intestinal polypeptide. These are modulated by inputs from sensors in afferent enteric neurons and from inputs from the central nervous system, especially via projections from the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus. Epithelial cells are also responsive to volume transmission of a number of neurotransmitters such as epinephrine, which travels via the blood from the adrenal medulla and promote glucose absorption, norepinephrine, released by some postganglionic sympathetic axons that project near to the epithelial lining and inhibit chloride secretion, and calcitonin gene‐related peptide and substance P, released by spinal afferents and modulate immune responses (Duan et al., 2021).

The behaviour of muscle and epithelial cells in promoting or inhibiting digestion, ultimately depends on how they integrate the effects of these various inputs with their own current constraints. In integrating multiple inputs, they are not distinctive. What makes them distinctive is that they have ‘the final say’ in determining the productive activities they execute: they are the last‐step integrators (even if in some cases these cells can also act as controllers).

Conclusions

Far from exhibiting a neat hierarchy of control in which higher‐level control mechanisms direct the activities of the gastrointestinal tract, control mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract exhibit a highly distributed, convoluted heterarchy in which various control mechanisms produce efferent outputs to diverse production mechanisms, modulating each of their behaviours. The field's practice already describes features of control that depart from strict hierarchy. In this article, we have provided a conceptual framing to account for some of them. Specifically, we have identified a crucial, yet at first sight counterintuitive, aspect of biological heterarchical control. Rather than ultimate decision making occurring at the top of the control architecture by subsystems receiving and processing information about the state of the system and the environment and releasing instructions downstream, control is much more distributed. If every controller is an integrator of the control inputs it receives, then the last step is not just one among many integrators, but it ultimately determines what happens. The integration of control inputs, necessary for the cohesive functioning of the organisms, takes place at the very bottom, in the controlled production mechanism, which determines the ultimate effects of the operations of controllers. We termed this view, which turns the perspective on control integration upside down, the last‐step integrator thesis.

Addressing how controllers work and are organized is crucial to explaining how an organism is functionally integrated such that its production mechanisms operate in the way they need to so as to contribute to the maintenance of the organism. Acknowledging that control is organized heterarchically and identifying what are the features of this type of organization, beyond mere failure of hierarchy, comprises an important step in this direction. An important feature of heterarchy is that frequently the constraints in a given mechanism are acted on by multiple control mechanisms. This is what results in the production mechanisms needing to function as integrators and ultimately determining the activity of the organism. If production mechanisms were not the final integrators, and the system instead realized a hierarchical control architecture, observing the highest levels would suffice to explain what occurs in the gastrointestinal tract. Adopting the last‐step integrator thesis, however, supports the opposite view. Rather than focusing on the highest levels to understand the activity of the gastrointestinal tract, this framework supports a bottom‐up approach. In cases where the complexity of the system is especially difficult to manage and study, this thesis provides the theoretical grounds for a heuristic that may help understand how control is exerted in the gastrointestinal tract: namely, examining production mechanisms, the flexible constraints they exhibit, how these constraints are related among themselves and respond to multiple inputs, and what are the relevant controllers operating on each of them.

It is a widespread assumption that hierarchy is necessary for any complex system to behave coherently. In biological systems, one might expect that heterarchy would generate chaos, with different production mechanisms interfering with the operation of others. We conclude by offering an intuitive way to understand why control in living organisms is actually most probably organized heterarchically and how heterarchically organized control mechanisms could be successful in maintaining living organisms. This requires adopting an evolutionary perspective on how the control systems in today's organisms have come about. Evolution has generated complex organisms incrementally through a long process of variation and selective retention. Variation has sometimes led to the introduction of new production mechanisms; for example, muscles only evolved in animals. New production mechanisms have often derived from composing existing ones in new ways. This involves changes in the control processes that regulate development. More generally, we suggest that most of the changes in evolutionary history have involved changes to existing control mechanisms or the development of new ones (see, for example, Kirschner et al., 2005). We have little understanding of how the control mechanisms in the earliest organisms developed. What we know is that by the time of the last universal common ancestor, an organism had a sufficient assemblage of control mechanisms to maintain itself. Those modes of organization that failed to allow organisms to maintain themselves had died off. From then on, control mechanisms have undergone change. When an organism in which a modification of a control mechanism reproduces, the variant gets passed on. There is no evolutionary advantage per se to changes that conform to hierarchical organization. Changes, even if they deviate from hierarchy, will be maintained as long as the organism is able to act so as to maintain itself and reproduce. There are many more modifications that deviate from hierarchy than those that conform to it. Thus, we should expect that the heterarchical organization found in today's organisms is sufficient to maintain them, at least when conditions do not change too much.

The conditions organisms confront, however, do change. That requires organisms to adjust their behaviour. In such situations, heterarchical organization may actually be advantageous to organisms. In a hierarchically organized organism, even if adjustments are made lower in the hierarchy, they are subject to regulation at the highest level. Finding the right adjustments that conform to the hierarchical organization and address the changed circumstances would be difficult. Moreover, a failure of one controller might undermine the functioning of the others down the hierarchy and ultimately the viability of the system. In a heterarchically organized system, there are multiple controllers that interact in any situation. In a novel situation, one can expect that the control mechanisms in different organisms would respond differently, with the production mechanisms in each organism ultimately integrating their outputs. Some of these responses might suffice to maintain the organism. Those whose responses did not maintain the organism would disappear. The same is then true as control mechanisms themselves change. In a heterarchical system, there are many possibilities for variation and any that would enable the organism to confront the challenge and maintain itself will be preserved if they arise. Moreover, given the partial overlapping of activities of different controllers, the failure of one controller does not risk having catastrophic effects (Joyner, 2013; Williamson, 2015). Those controllers whose activities overlap with the failing one may be upregulated so that the deficiency is mitigated.

To explain how heterarchical organizations can enable organisms to maintain themselves, we can invoke the long process of variation and selective retention. The particular heterarchical organization found in an organism today is one that has both maintained the recent ancestors of the organism and allowed for both adaptive behaviour and adaptive evolutionary change. Recognizing that control is heterarchically organized, however, is not a modest change. It inverts the expected hierarchy of control. As we have argued, on a heterarchical scheme, the basic production mechanisms are the ultimate integrators of control activities and the determiners of actions.
