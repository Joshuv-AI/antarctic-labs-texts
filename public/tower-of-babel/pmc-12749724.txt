Navigating the new normal: understanding the impact of COVID-19 on e-learning readiness among nursing students in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatchima Laouali Moussa, Mahaman Laouali Moussa, Ali Kerari, Tagwa Omer, Thamer Abdullah Alsufayan, Musaad Bedah Alsahly, Aboubacar Laouali Moussa, Hussain Ahmed Sufyani, Najla Burayk Alharbi, Huda Saeed Binzager, Bander Hammad Alblowi, Fayez Bakhat Alawfi, Sally Hammad Alblowi, Zaheen Ahmed Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated a sudden transition to e-learning, creating challenges for nursing education, which relies heavily on clinical and practical training. This study examined male nursing students’ fear of COVID-19, their e-learning readiness, and the relationship between these variables in Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 110 male nursing students from multiple Saudi universities. Data were collected using the Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCoV-19 S) and an e-learning readiness questionnaire covering five domains. Descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha reliability testing, Rasch logit transformation, Pearson’s correlation, and multiple linear regression were performed. The mean Fear of COVID-19 score was 21.7 ± 6.3 (α = 0.86). The mean overall e-learning readiness score was 112.5 ± 18.7 (α = 0.962), with the highest subscale scores in computer self-efficacy and motivation, and the lowest in online communication. Fear of COVID-19 demonstrated a significant negative correlation with e-learning readiness (r = − 0.34, p < 0.01). Regression analysis confirmed that fear of COVID-19 was a significant negative predictor of readiness (β = − 0.31, p = 0.002), explaining 12% of the variance. Fear of COVID-19 had a measurable negative impact on e-learning readiness among Saudi nursing students, particularly affecting motivation and self-directed learning. Although internal reliability of the instruments was excellent, formal cultural validation procedures were not conducted. As the sample included only male participants, generalizability is limited. Institutions should integrate psychological support, skill-building strategies, and enhanced digital infrastructure to strengthen readiness for future academic disruptions.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic profoundly disrupted societies worldwide, affecting physical health, mental well-being, and educational systems1. Beyond the risk of infection, individuals experienced heightened fear and anxiety, with university students identified as one of the most vulnerable groups2–4. As part of emergency, most educational institutions suspended in-person classes and rapidly transitioned to online learning5–7. Although digital platforms ensured educational continuity, the abrupt shift posed significant challenges especially in regions with uneven technological infrastructure and digital literacy8–10.

For nursing students, these challenges were particularly critical because their education depends heavily on clinical exposure and experiential learning11,12. During the pandemic, theoretical courses moved online, but clinical training was suspended, and assessments were rescheduled or modified13. Such disruptions limited opportunities to develop essential competencies and heightened students’ uncertainty about academic progress and career readiness, contributing to greater psychological distress14–16.

In Saudi Arabia, higher education faced similar disruptions, with considerable variation in universities’ digital readiness, internet accessibility, and institutional support17–19. These disparities, especially in rural regions, hindered smooth adaptation to online learning. Moreover, Saudi students reported increased psychological strain associated with fear of infection and abrupt educational changes, factors known to reduce motivation and hinder learning outcomes4. Regional evidence has also highlighted gaps in health literacy and pandemic preparedness among healthcare students, emphasizing the need to assess their readiness for digital learning in crisis contexts20.

E-learning readiness encompasses more than access to technology—it includes motivation, self-directed learning, digital confidence, and effective online communication21. Understanding these dimensions is particularly important for nursing education, where students must maintain engagement with theoretical learning while clinical practice is constrained. While prior international studies have linked fear of COVID-19 to reduced academic focus and digital learning adaptation22–24, this relationship has not been thoroughly explored among Saudi nursing students.

The Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCoV-19 S) has been validated internationally25, but its use among Saudi nursing students has not been systematically examined. Similarly, while e-learning readiness instruments demonstrate strong internal consistency, their cultural applicability in Saudi Arabia remains underexplored. Understanding these measures in the Saudi context is critical, given the rapid expansion of e-learning in the region and the government’s emphasis on digital transformation in education.

The Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCoV-19 S) has demonstrated reliability across diverse settings25, yet its cultural applicability in Saudi Arabia remains insufficiently tested. Similarly, although e-learning readiness instruments show strong internal consistency, their contextual validation among Saudi nursing students is limited. Addressing this gap, the present study examines male nursing students’ fear of COVID-19, their readiness for e-learning, and the relationship between these variables across Saudi universities. The findings aim to inform educators and policymakers seeking to enhance digital preparedness and resilience in nursing education during future crises.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among male undergraduate nursing students enrolled in government universities in Saudi Arabia between September and December 2021. A convenience sampling strategy was employed due to logistical constraints and accessibility during the COVID-19 pandemic. Students were invited to participate through institutional email lists and online learning platforms. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. All study procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Saud University, Saudi Arabia (Ref. No. 21/0208/IRB).

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.3) with 80% power and a significance level of 0.05, requiring a minimum of 84 participants. To account for non-response, a target of 120 students was set, of which 110 complete responses were included in the analysis. This sample size is comparable to similar studies on nursing education during the COVID-19 pandemic11,12.

Measures

Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCoV-19 S): The seven-item FCoV-19 S developed by Ahorsu et al.25 was used to assess students’ fear of COVID-19. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater fear. The scale has been validated internationally and has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80). For this study, items were translated into Arabic and back-translated to ensure linguistic equivalence. Although formal cultural validation was not undertaken, Cronbach’s α for this sample was 0.86, indicating acceptable reliability.

E-learning Readiness Questionnaire: Adapted from Rasouli et al.21, this instrument assessed students’ readiness across five domains: self-directed learning, motivation for learning, learner control, computer/internet self-efficacy, and online communication. Items were scored on a five-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting greater readiness (Cronbach’s α = 0.91). Internal consistency for the present sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.962).

Data collection

Data were collected using a secure online survey distributed via Google Forms. The questionnaire consisted of demographic variables (age, year of study, place of residence), FCoV-19 S items, and e-learning readiness items. To ensure methodological transparency, detailed instructions were provided to participants, and responses were anonymized.

Data transformation

For regression analysis, both the FCoV-19 S and e-learning readiness scores were transformed to a logit scale. This transformation rescales ordinal Likert-type responses into a continuous metric suitable for logistic modelling, thereby reducing ceiling effects and improving interpretability. A positive logit value indicates higher readiness (above average), while a negative value indicates lower readiness (below average). This approach is commonly applied in educational and health research when analysing ordinal questionnaire data, ensuring that the resulting scores are suitable for correlation and regression analyses21.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Reliability of the instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships between fear of COVID-19 and e-learning readiness. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to determine whether fear of COVID-19 significantly predicted readiness for e-learning after adjusting for demographic covariates. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 110 male nursing students participated in the study, with a mean age of 21.4 years (SD = 2.1). Most were in their third or fourth year of study (68%), and approximately 60% reported residing in urban areas. Response completeness was 91.6%, and all included participants provided valid data.

Fear of COVID-19

The mean score on the Fear of COVID-19 Scale (FCoV-19 S) was 21.7 (SD = 6.3; range 7–35). The reliability of the scale in this sample was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). Item-level analysis indicated that the majority of students agreed with statements reflecting fear of infection and anxiety when thinking about COVID-19 (Table 1).

E-learning readiness

The overall mean readiness score was 112.5 (SD = 18.7; range 70–150). Subscale scores were highest for computer/internet self-efficacy (M = 24.3, SD = 4.2) and motivation for learning (M = 23.7, SD = 4.8), while the lowest scores were observed for online communication (M = 20.1, SD = 4.9). Internal consistency for the readiness questionnaire was strong (Cronbach’s α = 0.962) (Table 1).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis showed a significant negative association between fear of COVID-19 and e-learning readiness (r = − 0.34, p < 0.01), indicating that higher fear was related to lower readiness. Among readiness subscales, motivation (r = − 0.29, p < 0.01) and self-directed learning (r = − 0.26, p < 0.05) were most affected by fear levels.

Regression analysis

Multiple linear regression was conducted with e-learning readiness as the dependent variable and fear of COVID-19 as the primary predictor, controlling for age, year of study, and residence. Fear of COVID-19 remained a significant negative predictor of readiness (β = − 0.31, p = 0.002), accounting for 12% of the variance in readiness scores. Discussion

This study examined the relationship between Saudi nursing students’ fear of COVID-19 and their readiness for e-learning. The findings revealed a negative association between fear of COVID-19 and e-learning readiness, particularly in the domains of motivation and self-directed learning. These results highlight how psychological responses to the pandemic can influence students’ capacity to adapt to online education.

The present findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating that elevated fear and anxiety during the pandemic hinder students’ focus, motivation, and engagement in remote learning. Cao et al.4 and Wang and Zhao22 reported that heightened anxiety among university students was associated with reduced concentration and learning efficiency. Similarly, Salman et al.23 observed that Pakistani students experiencing psychological distress struggled more with online education. Collectively, these findings suggest that fear of COVID-19 exerts a broadly negative impact on learning adaptation across different educational and cultural contexts.

Within the Saudi context, this study extends existing evidence regarding healthcare students’ preparedness during crises. Moussa et al.20 reported significant pandemic-related concerns among Saudi healthcare students, which may have reduced their confidence and adaptability. Our findings complement this by showing that such fear directly undermines digital learning readiness, particularly in motivational and self-regulatory aspects essential for successful e-learning.

Despite the negative influence of fear, participants demonstrated relatively high levels of computer self-efficacy and motivation, consistent with results from Singh et al.12 and Rana et al.11 in India and Nepal. This indicates that Saudi nursing students generally possess the technical competence and willingness required for e-learning, although their psychological state remains a critical factor influencing overall readiness.

These results carry several implications for educators and policymakers. Universities should strengthen psychological support systems and incorporate resilience training within nursing curricula to help students manage fear and anxiety during health emergencies. Targeted interventions, such as mentoring programs, workshops, and learner-cantered pedagogies, could enhance motivation and self-directed learning. Institutional efforts to improve internet accessibility, digital infrastructure, and instructor preparedness are also vital. Encouraging active participation through collaborative learning, timely feedback, and peer interaction may further enhance engagement and confidence in online environments. Overall, a comprehensive approach addressing both psychological and technological aspects is essential for effective e-learning implementation.

The study’s limitations should be noted. First, only male nursing students were included, which restricts generalizability to the wider student population. Second, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias. Third, although both instruments demonstrated excellent internal reliability, formal cultural validation procedures—such as confirmatory factor analysis and linguistic adaptation—were not conducted. Future studies should address these methodological limitations, include both genders, and adopt longitudinal designs to explore changes in fear and readiness over time. Strengthening cultural validation of assessment tools will further enhance the reliability and contextual applicability of findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fear of COVID-19 negatively influenced e-learning readiness among Saudi nursing students, particularly in the areas of motivation and self-directed learning. Although participants showed strong technical competence, psychological barriers limited their effective adaptation to online education. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating psychological support, targeted skill-building, and instructor development within e-learning strategies to foster resilience and engagement. Policymakers should also focus on improving technological accessibility and digital equity to enhance preparedness for future educational disruptions. As the sample included only male nursing students, the findings should be interpreted with caution and may not fully represent female or mixed-gender populations. Future research should therefore include both genders and employ culturally validated instruments to strengthen generalizability and contextual relevance.
