Comparison of radiographic parameters between sciatic scoliosis and Lenke 5/6 type idiopathic scoliosis in young patients: a LASSO and nomogram analysis
Authors: Zhongning Xu, Jintao Ao, Yanbin Zhang, Bin Xiao, Jile Jiang, Yuqing Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo compare radiographic parameters between sciatic scoliosis (SS) and Lenke 5/6 idiopathic scoliosis (IS) in young patients and to develop a predictive model.MethodsA total of 148 patients (SS: IS = 75:73) were retrospectively enrolled. Demographic data and preoperative radiographic parameters were recorded. Univariate analysis was conducted between the two groups. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and logistic regression were used to screen for risk factors, and a nomogram designed to differentiate the two types of scoliosis was further constructed. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA), with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations).ResultsCompared with the IS group, the SS group was found to have a smaller lumbar lordosis (LL) and vertebral rotation, but larger pelvic tilt (PT) and coronal trunk shift (all p < 0.05). LASSO regression retained five variables, while logistic regression identified body mass index (BMI), LL, and mean vertebrae rotation (MVR) as independent predictors. The nomogram based on these predictors achieved an AUC of 0.957 (95% CI: 0.930–0.983) and showed good calibration (mean absolute error = 0.019).ConclusionsBMI, LL, and MVR are accurate, reliable predictors for distinguishing SS from Lenke 5/6 IS in young patients, providing a diagnostic basis for clinical practice.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06893-2.Keywords: Sciatic scoliosis, Idiopathic scoliosis, EOS images, LASSO analysis, Nomogram

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common disorders in spinal surgery, often presenting with low back pain and radiculopathy affecting the lower extremities. [1, 2] Sciatic scoliosis (SS), a common symptom of LDH, is considered a compensatory response to ipsilateral nerve root compression, and its incidence has been reported to range from 16.9 to 17.9% of LDH cases. [3, 4] Moreover, significant resolution of SS is typically observed within 2 years after endoscopic discectomy [5].

In contrast, idiopathic scoliosis (IS) is a structural scoliosis caused by multifactorial etiologies, [6, 7] and some patients with IS may also present with back pain. [8] For mild SS, conservative management and further evaluation of the underlying cause (e.g., MRI) are typically recommended, whereas management strategies for IS focus on observation, bracing, or corrective surgery depending on curve progression [9]. Although antalgic scoliosis and idiopathic scoliosis differ in etiology, they may present with similar radiographic features in the early stage, particularly in adolescents and young adults. Antalgic scoliosis may present with mild or transient pain, or even without obvious symptoms, making clinical differentiation challenging at the initial visit, thus increasing the risk of misdiagnosis [10]. Although numerous studies have investigated the radiographic characteristics of each of these two conditions individually, [5, 11, 12] direct comparative studies remain limited. Therefore, identifying reliable imaging features to distinguish between these two conditions is of considerable clinical importance. This study aimed to compare the radiographic and clinical variables between SS and IS in young patients, and to develop a predictive model using key variables.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients who attended the outpatient clinic between January 2021 and December 2023 were retrospectively included. Patients aged 10–25 years who were diagnosed with adolescent or adult idiopathic scoliosis and were classified as Lenke type 5 or 6 were included in the IS group. Patients aged 10–25 years (the same age range as the IS group) who were diagnosed with lumbar disc herniation (LDH), had a preoperative Cobb angle > 10°, and showed improvement in scoliosis after non-fusion surgery were included in the SS group. Patients with a prior history of spinal surgery or spinal deformities caused by other factors were excluded. Demographic data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. This study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the committee’s guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Radiological measurements

All patients underwent preoperative EOS imaging, a low-dose bi-planar stereoradiography technique [13]. Radiological parameters were measured using SterEOS software (Version: 1.6.5.8188, EOS Imaging, Paris, France), where the vertebral models were semi-automatically generated and manually adjusted by experienced observers, allowing measurement of coronal, sagittal, and axial rotation parameters [14]. Each observer performed all measurements twice independently. Coronal measurements included the distance between C7 Plumb Line and Central Sacral Vertical Line(C7PL-CSVL), Cobb angle, curve orientation, and the number of vertebrae involved in the main curve. Sagittal parameters included thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), pelvic incidence (PI), sacral slope (SS), pelvic tilt (PT), sagittal vertical axis (SVA), and T1 pelvic angle (TPA). For rotational measurements, the axial angle of each vertebra was calculated relative to the sagittal reference plane, which is perpendicular to the line connecting the centers of the femoral heads. To simplify the statistical analysis, only the upper and lower end vertebrae, apical vertebra, and the mean value of vertebrae within main curve were included. Moreover, additional clinical characteristics of LDH, including herniation level and protrusion side, were collected.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.5.1; www.r-project.org). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (Q25,Q75). To evaluate measurement reliability, intra- and inter-observer agreement were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The relationship between protrusion side and curve convexity was further analyzed using the chi-square test. Univariate analysis (independent t-tests for normally distributed variables or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables) was conducted to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between the two groups. Categorical variables were presented as proportions and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variables showing significant differences in univariate analysis were included in LASSO regression analysis to identify potential predictors. Thus, a multivariable logistic regression model was established using the predictors selected by LASSO regression, and this model was presented as a nomogram for clinical application.

Model validation included assessment of discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was evaluated by Harrell’s C-index and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (an AUC > 0.80 indicated good discrimination ability), while calibration was assessed by calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify clinical utility of the model. Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was used to validate.

Results

A total of 148 patients were included in the study. The intra-observer reliability ranged from 0.83 to 0.91, while the inter-observer reliability ranged from 0.79 to 0.86, indicating good to excellent agreement. As shown in Table 1, the SS and IS groups demonstrated significant differences in baseline clinical characteristics. Patients in the SS group exhibited a higher BMI and were more likely to be male than those in the IS group (both p < 0.001). The duration of scoliosis was also significantly shorter in the SS group (5.5 months vs. 15.5 months, p < 0.001). However, the direction of the major curve did not differ significantly between the groups. Additional characteristics of lumbar disc herniation in the SS group are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The distribution of herniation levels was predominantly at L4/5 (68.0%) and L5/S1 (29.3%). A significant association was observed between herniation side and curve convexity (p = 0.003).

LASSO regression

A correlation plot demonstrated significant intercorrelations among the majority of radiographic variables (Fig. 1). To address this multicollinearity and select the most informative predictors, we subsequently performed LASSO regression. All variables with significant differences in univariate analysis, along with BMI, were included in the LASSO regression model, which performed variable selection and reduced multicollinearity through L1-norm regularization. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ), which was found to be 0.00740. At this level, the model retained six variables (BMI, MVR, LL, PT, AVR, TPA), although TPA was excluded from further analysis due to its negligible coefficient value (< 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Logistic regression and nomogram

Thus, the five key variables identified above were incorporated into a logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for gender, the logistic regression showed that BMI, LL, and MVR were independent predictors (Table 3). A nomogram was then developed based on these three independent predictors (Fig. 3), with an AUC of 0.957 (95% CI: 0.930–0.983), indicating excellent discrimination ability (Fig. 4a). Calibration analysis, validated with bootstrap resampling, showed a strong agreement between the predicted and observed outcomes, with a mean absolute error of 0.019 (Fig. 4b). Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the nomogram provided substantial clinical net benefits for distinguishing IS from SS across a wide range of risk thresholds (Fig. 4c). Bootstrap validation confirmed the robustness of the nomogram model, yielding a corrected C-index of 0.951.

Representative case

This was a 17-year-old male patient who presented with low back pain and left lower extremity radiculopathy for 6 months. At the initial consultation, radiographic examination revealed significant scoliosis (Fig. 5a). The patient underwent percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy. One month after surgery, the patient’s pain was completely relieved, and at the 3-month follow-up, the scoliosis was significantly reduced (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

Sciatic scoliosis (SS) is not uncommon in patients with LDH, particularly in young individuals. [3] This type of scoliosis has typical characteristics and has been reported to diminish significantly after effective treatment. [5, 11] However, in clinical practice, especially in adolescents and young adults, early-stage SS may present primarily with spinal curvature while pain symptoms are mild, transient, or even absent, which may lead to confusion with idiopathic scoliosis at the initial evaluation. [10] Such diagnostic ambiguity may result in delayed identification of the underlying pathology in SS or inappropriate management in IS. Although MRI is regarded as the gold standard for identifying underlying pathologies in SS, its routine use is often constrained by cost, accessibility, and waiting time, particularly in resource-limited settings [15, 16]. Therefore, developing a reliable radiographic-based differentiation approach is of particular clinical relevance.

Our results showed significant differences in most imaging parameters between SS and IS. Despite comparable Cobb angles between the two groups, the SS group was associated with greater coronal imbalance and involvement of more vertebrae. The positions of the apical and end vertebrae may also be potential predictive variables; however, due to the restrictions of the Lenke classification in the IS cohort, direct comparison would introduce unavoidable selection bias, so these parameters were excluded from the analysis.

In the sagittal plane, the SS group had larger SVA, TPA, and PT, as well as reduced TK, SS (sacral slope), and LL, indicating greater sagittal malalignment. This pattern is likely attributable to pain-driven compensatory adaptation in response to radiculopathy, characterized by forward trunk inclination and reduced lumbar lordosis, as reported in previous studies [17–19]. Nerve root irritation may induce asymmetric muscle activation and protective muscle spasm, prompting patients to adopt a forward-leaning posture to reduce neural tension [20]. At the same time, pelvic retroversion serves as a compensatory mechanism to maintain sagittal balance [21]. These biomechanical adaptations are typically reversible following surgical intervention [22, 23].

Given the numerous significant variables, the inclusion of all parameters in logistic regression was inappropriate. In both groups, sagittal alignment parameters have been consistently reported to have significant clinical correlations, [24, 25] highlighting the need to reduce multicollinearity when constructing predictive models. LASSO regression was a feasible method in this study, as it applies an L1 penalty to regression coefficients, shrinking the coefficients of less informative predictors to zero. In this context, TPA was initially retained but was subsequently excluded. Given that TPA is a global parameter reflecting sagittal alignment [26], its effect may have been absorbed by these related variables, resulting in a near-zero coefficient. Interestingly, all coronal variables were excluded by the LASSO regression, possibly due to two reasons: (1) Coronal parameters are highly correlated with parameters in the other two planes; (2) In LDH patients, sciatic scoliosis occurs less frequently than sagittal imbalance, and reduced LL may help directly enlarge the intervertebral foramina, thereby alleviating radicular pain. This finding suggested sagittal and axial plane imbalances may play a more critical role in distinguishing SS from IS than the coronal plane.

Subsequent logistic regression further narrowed down the predictive variables to three. We observed significant differences in both gender distribution and BMI between the SS and IS groups, with a male predominance and higher BMI in the SS group. This is consistent with previous evidence, as overweight is a well-recognized risk factor for LDH, [27] whereas a low BMI has been repeatedly linked to IS. [28–30] Logistic regression confirmed BMI as an independent predictor after adjusting for gender. In the nomogram, BMI was included as a modifiable and clinically relevant factor, whereas gender, though significant, was excluded due to its fixed nature and limited applicability.

To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing SS and IS. Distinguishing between these two types of scoliosis in clinical practice poses challenges for surgeons. By screening for significant variables and constructing a predictive model, this study provided a quantitative and clinically applicable approach for diagnosing these two types of scoliosis. It further demonstrates that these common forms can be distinguished using sagittal and axial parameters—an insight derived from variable screening. Although external validation is warranted, the current model demonstrated good discriminative performance based on internal validation. Furthermore, beyond the differentiation between SS and IS, these findings may have broader implications. Since these two etiologically distinct scoliosis types have corresponding imaging features, it may be possible to quantitatively assess the proportions of pain-related, idiopathic, and degenerative components in adult spinal deformities with multiple potential etiologies, thereby guiding the selection of appropriate treatment strategies. This also explains why this study only included young patients to exclude the influence of degenerative factors. The limitations of this study include: (1) A single-center retrospective design; (2) Lack of external validation, though bootstrap internal validation showed excellent performance; (3) Absence of symptom-related data; (4) Inapplicability to older patients or those with severe scoliosis. Future studies will involve constructing a prospective cohort to validate the nomogram model and incorporating biomechanical analysis to explore the role of these variables in distinguishing the etiologies of scoliosis.

Conclusion

This study compared radiographic characteristics between SS secondary to LDH and Lenke 5/6 type IS. BMI, LL, and MVR were identified as predictive variables with high accuracy and reliability for differentiating between these two types of scoliosis.
