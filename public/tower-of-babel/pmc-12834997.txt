Information theory and thermodynamic study of a 2D harmonic oscillator modified by inverse-square potential
Authors: Chou-Yi Hsu, Pradeep Kumar Singh, C. A. Onate, Yusufbay Yusupov, Doniyor Jumanazarov, Ibrahim Mahariq, Ali A. Rajhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This work provides analytical study of a two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator (HO) coupled with a perpendicular magnetic field (B) in the x-direction and an inverse-square potential in the y-direction. The system’s Hamiltonian is exactly solved via variable separation, to obtain the energy levels and wavefunctions composed of Hermite and associated Laguerre polynomials. Fisher information (FI) and Shannon entropy (SE) for both position space (PS) and momentum space (MS) are fully examined under the magnetic field strength and anisotropic potential parameters. The canonical partition function is computed and thermodynamic quantities such as internal energy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy are evaluated. The result shows that the presence of magnetic field enhances spatial localization and reduces uncertainty in PS but has a revised effect in the MS. In the thermodynamic properties, there is asymmetric thermodynamic responses in the x- and y-directions due to the presence of the magnetic field and the inverse-square potential. This study highlights the deep relationship between quantum information theory and thermodynamics in low-dimensional systems with structured interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33259-1.

Introduction

The quantification of information in information theory is a fundamental concept described in the theoretic quantity by FI and SE. The concept guides the understanding of uncertainty in every system, complexity, and predictability of any phenomenon. These FI and SE have wide applications in different areas of science, such as statistics, physics, information systems, and complexity science1,2. The concept of SE was introduced as a quantitative measure of uncertainty in a probability distribution3. This concept bridges statistical mechanics and information theory4. Studies have shown its application in thermodynamics, quantum information, signal processing, and data compression. On the other hand, FI was introduced to measure the quantity of information that an observable random variable carries about an unknown parameter upon which the probability depends. This FI is a local measure, significantly sensitive to small-scale fluctuations in the signal distribution. It characterizes the precision with which one can evaluate parameters and underpins the Cramer-Rao inequality (CRI), which sets a lower bound on the variance of unbiased estimators. In quantum systems. The localization of wavefunctions, analyzation of quantum phase transitions, and uncertainty relations are significant applications of FI. These complement those of SE5,6. Although both FI and SE measure aspects of uncertainty as well as information, they approach it from different angles. SE emphasizes the overall spread or unpredictability within a system, while FI captures how sensitive the probability distribution is to changes in its underlying parameters. These two measures often behave in a complementary manner. For instance, highly localized states tend to have low entropy and high FI and vice versa. This interplay is captured in composite complexity measures like the Lopez-Ruiz, Mancini, and Calber (LMC) complexity and Fisher-Shannon product. These have been used to characterize certain concepts such as atomic and molecular systems, quantum wells and biological networks7,8.

In quantum systems, thermodynamic properties serve as a vital link between the microscopic features of quantum mechanics and the macroscopic properties of classical thermodynamics. The quantum systems are described by probabilistic principles, characterized by wave functions, discrete energy spectra, and quantum statistics. These uniquely quantum features have a profound impact on thermodynamic behaviour, especially at low temperatures as well as in systems exhibiting confinement, energy quantization, or interactions with external fields. At the heart of thermodynamic analysis in quantum systems lies quantum statistical mechanics, where the partition function plays a significant and unifying role. The thermodynamic behaviour of a quantum system relates to the structure of its energy levels of the same system. In a quantum harmonic oscillator, the energy levels are evenly spaced, hence, a well-characterized temperature-dependent property. However, some potential models like quantum wells, anharmonic oscillators and others yield nonlinear energy level spacings leading to a nontrivial thermodynamic phenomenon and critical behaviour9. In low-dimensional quantum systems, the spatial confinement and discrete energy spectra intensify quantum effects in thermodynamic quantities, which become more significant under external magnetic fields induced by Landau quantization. This greatly alters the partition function, leading to measurable changes in quantities like specific heat and magnetization10. Several authors have reported the theoretic quantity as well as thermodynamic properties for different potential terms11–31. However, to the best of our understanding, there is no report on the 2D HO (considering x and y directions) interacting with another potential term. Motivated by the significant influence of both theoretic measure and thermodynamic properties, this study aims to investigate the sensitivity of FI, SE, and some thermodynamic properties within a 2D HO interacting with an inverse-square potential. Discussion

Figure 1a,b present the effect of B on the FI. An increase in B enhanced the localization of the wave function in the PS as a result of the confining effect of the presence of the magnetic field. Conversely, an increase in B results to delocalization of the wave function in the MS. The behaviour in both the PS and MS reflects the uncertainty trade off due to inverse variation. Figure 2a,b show the variation of SE in PS and in MS versus B. An increase in B reduces the uncertainty in the PS as the SE decreases. This result aligns with the result of the FI for the PS in Fig. 1a. On the other hand, an increase in B shows that the SE for the MS becomes more delocalized, complementing the findings of decreasing FI for the MS. Figure 3a,b present FI for both PS and MS as a function of the y-component frequency. An increase in the frequency results to an increase in FI for the PS. This shows that the oscillator becomes stiffer in the component of y-direction leading to tighter spatial confinement. However, in the MS, an increase in the frequency results to delocalization of the wave function. Figure 4a,b show the variation of the SE both for the PS and the MS as a function of the y-component of the frequency. An increase in the frequency increases the SE for the MS but decreases the SE for the PS. Figure 5a,b show the effect of the x-component of the frequency on the FI for both the PS and MS. The result obtained in Fig. 3 is also obtained here. This shows the both the x-component of the frequency and the y-component of the frequency have the same effect on the FI. Figure 6a,b present the effect of the x-component of the frequency on the SE for both the PS and the MS. An increase in the frequency confirms the position localization of the particle as the SE for the PS decreases. On the other hand, there is a momentum delocalization of the particle in the MS as the x-component of the frequency increases. In Figs. 3 and 5 where the effect of frequency on the FI is examined, the PS FI is a straight line while the MS FI is curve even though they vary inversely. The characteristic contrasts the behaviour due to inverse relation between the position and momentum representations governed by the Fourier transform. In the PS, FI increases linearly because higher frequencies leading to tighter localization of the wave function that grows linearly. On the other hand, the FI for the MS shows a non-linear dependency on the frequency. The wave function in the momentum distribution becomes broader which does not scale linearly. Mathematically, a Gaussian wave function which is of the type of the interacting potential, increases linearly at one space and decreases non-linearly in the other space giving rise to curved behaviour. Figure 7 a,b,c presents the effect of cyclotron frequency (mostly dominated by the magnetic field) on the thermodynamic properties such as the internal energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy at constant temperature (500 K). In Fig. 7a, the internal energy decreases monotonically as the cyclotron frequency rises. This effect is also seen in Fig. 7b, where entropy versus cyclotron frequency is plotted. However, at every value of the cyclotron frequency, the internal energy is far greater than the entropy. It is observed that at all the values of the cyclotron frequency, the entropy is less than half, while the minimum value of the internal energy is 16.5 Jmol–1 K-1. It is also observed that a decrease in either internal energy or entropy is very small for all the values of the cyclotron frequency studied. Figure 7c shows a direct variation of the Gibbs free energy and the cyclotron frequency. Except that the Gibbs free energy increases as the cyclotron frequency increases, other exhibited features in (a) and (b) are also displayed in (c). Figure 8 a,b,c shows the effect of the frequencies (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}$$\end{document} respectively) on the internal energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy. Figure 8a, an increase in the frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document}decreases internal energy while an increase in the frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}$$\end{document} increases the internal energy. This same effect is exhibited in Fig. 8b for entropy. This suggests that the system is not fully symmetric due to the effects of B or inverse-square potential. In the presence of the inverse-square potential, an increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x},$$\end{document} tightens the confinement and thus, reduce the probability of the particle entering the high-energy repulsive region. Therefore, the system’s effective energy will be lowered due to the suppress on the high energy excitations. This effect could also be due to the wider spread of the energy levels in the x-direction as a result of B that suppresses the thermal population at any given temperature. Thus, an increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document} makes excitations less probable leading to thermal contribution and thus lower the internal energy. This situation may not occur at the y direction since the magnetic field is attached to the x-component. However, in Fig. 8c, the Gibbs free energy increases for increase in either \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}.$$\end{document} In a 2D harmonic oscillator, as w increases, both zero-point energy as well as the thermal excitation energy increase indicating a normal situation. Thus, an increase in either \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}$$\end{document} will increase the internal energy. Figure 9 shows the variation of complexity against the magnetic field. An increase in B leads to an inverse variation between the PS and the MS. For all values of B, the complexity for the PS has small value (less than one) but the complexity for the MS has high magnitude.

Figure 10 presents the effect of B on the sum of complexity and the product of complexity. An increase in B increases the sum of complexity but decreases the product of complexity. The sum of complexity behaves like the MS complexity while the product of complexity behaves like the PS complexity. Figure 11 shows the effect of the frequency of the x-direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document}) and y-direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}$$\end{document}) on the complexity in the PS. The frequencies from the different directions have the same variation on the complexity for the PS. Figure 12 shows the effect of the frequency of the x-direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_x}$$\end{document}) and y-direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_y}$$\end{document}) on the complexity in the MS. The frequencies from the different directions have the same variation on the complexity for the MS. However, the variation in the PS is inverse of the variation in the MS. Table 1 presents the effect of frequency on Fisher information for both the x-component and y-component. On both components, the Fisher information for the position space grow with frequency due to stronger localization of the wave functions leading to sharper probability distribution. In the momentum space, the revise situation occurred for both components which is consistent with the uncertainty principle. However, at every frequency, the FI for both the PS and the MS in the y-component consistently grow higher than their counter part in the x-component. The potential and its subsets have a unique behaviour in terms of the Fisher product. The product of the FI remains the same for all frequencies for both the x-component and y-component. Table 2 shows the effect of B on the FI for the x-component and effect of the strength of inverse-square potential on the FI for the y-component. The FI for the position space increases with increase in the B indicating a stronger localization due to the magnetic confinement. In the momentum space, FI decreases monotonically as the B increases. The product of the FI remains 4.000 at all values of the B. This Fisher product also known as the Cramer-Rao inequality justifies the symmetric nature or unchanged kinetic behaviour in the x-component. However, in the y-component, the FI for the PS and the MS respectively, increases as the strength of the inverse-square potential increases. The product of the FI in this component does not show symmetric behaviour. Its products are different at every value of the potential strength. The FI for the y-component obeyed the Cramer-Rao inequality but failed to obey uncertainty. Table 3 shows the effect of frequency on the SE for both PS and MS. Conclusion

In this study, the analytical Schrödinger equation is solved for a 2D HO system subjected to a perpendicular B and modified by an inverse-square potential along the y-axis. FI and SE are calculated from the wave functions while the thermodynamic properties are calculated from the energy levels. The computations show that the system of the theoretic measures adheres to the information theoretic duality indicating enhancement by the presence of B or oscillator frequency. The system also shows and confirms the intrinsic trade off govern by uncertainty principle. The system maintains the constancy of the Fisher product in the x-direction validating the symmetry preserved by the magnetic confinement. The deviations in the y-direction highlight the breakdown of uncertainty-product invariance. Thermodynamic analysis, grounded on the exact partition function, shows that the internal energy and entropy as thermodynamic properties decrease with increasing cyclotron frequency in the x-direction due to enhanced localization and reduced thermal population of higher energy states. However, the y-component, frequency induces an increase in internal energy and entropy, reflecting the asymmetric influence of the inverse-square potential. This study offers a theoretical basis for applications in confined quantum gases, quantum wells, and quantum information thermodynamics. The study also have some other useful applications. The magnetic field term corresponds to a cyclotron motion of electrons in the quantum dot or 2D electron gas, just like in semiconductor heterostructures. The inverse square potential is like a repulsive defect center inside the dot that modifies confinement symmetry similar to how nonparabolic quantum dots behave. The FI and SE relationship provides insight into how quantum localization and delocalization respond to external magnetic turning that is directly impacts the optical and transport properties of quantum dots.
