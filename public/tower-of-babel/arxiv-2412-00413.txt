Global existence and large-time behavior of solutions to cubic nonlinear Schrödinger systems without coercive conserved quantity
Satoshi MASAKI

Abstract
. In this article, we investigate the large-time behavior of small solutions to a system of one-dimensional cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equations with two components. In previous studies, a structural condition on the nonlinearity has been employed to guarantee the existence of a coercive, mass-type conserved quantity. We identify a new class of systems that do not satisfy such a condition and thus lack a coercive conserved quantity. Nonetheless, we establish the global existence and describe the large-time behavior for small solutions in this class. In this setting, the asymptotic profile is described in terms of solutions to the corresponding system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). A key element of our analysis is the use of a quartic conserved quantity associated with the ODE system. Moreover, for a specific example within this class, we solve the ODE system explicitly, showing that the asymptotic behavior is expressed using Jacobi elliptic functions.



1.
Introduction

In this article, we consider the initial value problem of a system of cubic NLS equations composed of two components

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})u_{j}=F_{j}(u_{1},u_{2}),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},\quad j=1,2,\\
&(u_{1}(0),u_{2}(0))=(u_{0,1},u_{0,2})\in H^{0,1}\times H^{0,1}\end{aligned}\right.

with

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})={}&\lambda_{1}|u_{1}|^{2}u_{1}+\lambda_{2}|u_{1}|^{2}u_{2}+\lambda_{3}u_{1}^{2}\overline{u_{2}}+\lambda_{4}|u_{2}|^{2}u_{1}+\lambda_{5}u_{2}^{2}\overline{u_{1}}+\lambda_{6}|u_{2}|^{2}u_{2},\\
F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})={}&\lambda_{7}|u_{1}|^{2}u_{1}+\lambda_{8}|u_{1}|^{2}u_{2}+\lambda_{9}u_{1}^{2}\overline{u_{2}}+\lambda_{10}|u_{2}|^{2}u_{1}+\lambda_{11}u_{2}^{2}\overline{u_{1}}+\lambda_{12}|u_{2}|^{2}u_{2},\end{aligned}\right.

where
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{12})\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
.
We aim to establish the global existence of solutions for small initial data and study their asymptotic behavior under a suitable condition on the parameters.

It is well known from previous studies that the cubic nonlinear term is critical in one dimension. Firstly, let us briefly review for a single equation.
In the single-equation case,
the gauge-invariant cubic NLS has the form:

(1.3)

(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})u=\lambda|u|^{2}u,

with a number
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

The number

If it is a real number
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

[
Oz

(1.4)

u(t,x)=(2it)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\widehat{u_{+}}(\tfrac{x}{2t})\exp\left(-i\lambda|\widehat{u_{+}}(\tfrac{x}{2t})|^{2}\tfrac{\log t}{2}\right)+O(t^{-\frac{3}{4}+})

When

[

We refer the readers to

Even for the cubic NLS systems

The system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})u_{1}=-2(|u_{1}|^{2}-|u_{2}|^{2})u_{1}+\overline{u_{1}}u_{2}^{2},\\

In these ansatzes,

The example also highlights that the global existence of solutions is not always guaranteed

A simple yet powerful tool for ensuring global existence is the use of conservation laws, which are intrinsic properties of the system.
Specifically, if a system possesses a conserved quantity of the form

(1.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(a|u_{1}|^{2}+2b\operatorname{Re}(\overline{u_{1}}u_{2})+c|u_{2}|^{2})dx,\quad ac>b^{2},

then solutions exist globally in time for arbitrary initial data in
L^{2}\times L^{2}
.
The condition
ac>b^{2}
ensures the coercivity of this conserved quantity, i.e., its equivalence to the square of the
L^{2}\times L^{2}
-norm.
The existence of such a conserved quantity, as well as its coercivity, is determined by the structure of the system’s nonlinearity.

In this article, we refer to this condition as the
weak null gauge condition
, as it represents a weaker form of the null gauge condition introduced in
[

,

]
, which was originally motivated by studies of nonlinear wave equations (see
[

]

The results concerning the asymptotic behavior of system (

In this article, we treat systems that do not possess coercive conserved quantities and are not dissipative either. We will specify the conditions for the coefficients later. The simplest model case that satisfies our condition is given by:

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})u_{1}=|u_{2}|^{2}u_{2},\\
&(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})u_{2}=|u_{1}|^{2}u_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

This system possesses

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}\overline{u_{1}}u_{2}\,dx

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}\overline{\partial_{x}u_{1}}\partial_{x}u_{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|u_{1}|^{4}+|u_{2}|^{4})\,dx

as the conserved quantities.
However, since the quadratic part of these quantities are not coercive, these do not imply the global existence of solutions.
The corresponding ODE system for this system is given by:

(1.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\tfrac{d}{dt}A_{1}=|A_{2}|^{2}A_{2},\\
&i\tfrac{d}{dt}A_{2}=|A_{1}|^{2}A_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

The crucial point is that the solution of this ODE system preserves a
quartic quantity
:

|A_{1}|^{4}+|A_{2}|^{4}.

By utilizing this quantity, we can establish the global existence of small solutions to (
1.9
) and prove the asymptotics as in (
1.7
). The systems under consideration are those whose corresponding ODE systems possess an effective quartic conserved quantity.

In the above example, the conserved quartic quantity for (
1.10
) coincides with the integrand of the second term of the conserved “energy-like” quantity for (
1.9
). However, this is merely a coincidence (see Remark
1.10
, below). We also note that this quartic quantity is conserved only for the corresponding ODE system, and its spatial integral is not a conserved quantity for the NLS system. This can be seen, for instance, from the fact that the
L^{4}\times L^{4}
-norm of the solutions studied in this article decays in time.

1.2.
Matrix-Vector representation and weak null condition

A significant advancement in this article is the exclusion of the weak null gauge condition, which is commonly employed in the analysis of asymptotic behavior (see
[
NST
,
KS
,
M3
,
U
]
).
The precise formulation of the condition in the current context is as follows:

(H0)

There exists a
positive Hermitian matrix
\mathscr{H}
such that

\operatorname{Im}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\overline{u_{1}}&\overline{u_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})\\
F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\end{bmatrix}\right)=0

holds for all
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

One major benefit of this condition is that it guarantees the global existence of
L^{2}
solutions to the NLS system (
1.1
).
More specifically, if the nonlinearity satisfies this condition, the quantity

(1.15)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{u_{1}}&\overline{u_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\ u_{2}\end{bmatrix}dx

becomes a conserved quantity of the corresponding NLS system (
1.1
).
Since
\mathscr{H}
is a positive Hermitian matrix, this quantity is equivalent to
\left\lVert u_{1}\right\rVert{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert u_{2}\right\rVert{L^{2}}^{2}
, providing a sufficient condition for the global existence of
L^{2}
solutions. (See, e.g.,
[
CazBook
]
.)

We prove in Corollary
6.3
below that, as long as we consider the system (
1.1
), the condition (H0) is equivalent to the following condition.

(S0)

There exists a positive real-symmetric matrix
\mathscr{S}
such that

\operatorname{Im}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\overline{u_{1}}&\overline{u_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\mathscr{S}\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})\\
F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\end{bmatrix}\right)=0

holds for all
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

The validity of (S0)
is easily checked for (
1.1
)
by looking at the matrix-vector representation of the system as the following equivalence is valid:

\operatorname{Im}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\overline{u_{1}}&\overline{u_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})\\
F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\end{bmatrix}\right)=0\Leftrightarrow{\vphantom{\!(a,b,c)}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!(a,b,c)\in\Ker\mathscr{A}.

From this property, one easily sees that (S0) can be stated in the language of the matrix
\mathscr{A}
as follows:

(S1)

W(0,\mathscr{A})\cap\mathcal{P}_{+}\neq\emptyset
.

The systems in the scope of this article, i.e., the systems satisfy the Assumption
1.1
, do not necessarily satisfy this condition.
Indeed, the model system (

This can be seen by (

W(0;\mathscr{A})=\Span\{{\vphantom{\!(0,1,0)}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!(0,1,0)\}
and hence
W(0;\mathscr{A})\cap\mathcal{P}_{+}=\emptyset
.
Further, a necessary condition for (S1) is
\rank\mathscr{A}\leqslant 2
. Our model contains the system for which

.

\mathscr{H}

\operatorname{Im}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\overline{u_{1}}&\overline{u_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})\\
F_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\end{bmatrix}\right)\leqslant 0

holds for all
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

It is obvious that this condition is weaker than (H0).
This condition does not assure the existence of a conserved quantity.
However, it claims that the non-increase of the quantity (
1.15
) as time increase.
Hence, the condition yields the global existence of solutions to (
1.1
)
for positive time direction.
The asymptotic behavior of solutions to NLS systems is studied
under this condition.
Assumption

does not implies (D0). Again,

) is a concrete example.

1.3.
Conservation of a quartic quantity for the ODE system

In our model, we utilize the analysis of the ODE system (
1.8
) to derive the global existence of solutions to (
1.1
). This approach is similar to those used in the previous studies where coercive conservation laws (or non-increasing property of
L^{2}
-norm)
are applicable.
However, the structure in the ODE system that realizes this is different.
In our case, there exists a conserved quantity which is
quartic
with respect to the unknown.
We describe how the quartic conserved quantity for the solutions of the ODE system is obtained under the assumptions of the main theorem.

To this end, we recall one result from
[
MSU2
]
.
Let
(A_{1},A_{2})
be a solution to the system of (
1.8
).
Let
\mathscr{A}
be the matrix given by (
1.11
).
Let

(1.16)

\rho_{1}=|A_{1}|^{2},\quad\rho_{2}=|A_{2}|^{2},\quad\mathcal{R}=2\operatorname{Re}(\overline{A_{1}}A_{2}),\quad\mathcal{I}=2\operatorname{Im}(\overline{A_{1}}A_{2}).

We have the following identity, which is one benefit of the matrix-vector representation.

Proposition 1.7

(
[
MSU2
]
)
.

For any
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, one has

(1.17)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}&\mathcal{R}&\rho_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
b\\
c\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{I}\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}&\mathcal{R}&\rho_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mathscr{A}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
b\\
c\end{bmatrix}.

Let us derive the conservation of a quartic quantity.
Suppose that
\mathscr{A}^{2}
has a negative eigenvalue
-k^{2}
as in Assumption
1.1
.
Pick a non-zero vector
\Gamma:={\vphantom{\!(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})\in W(-k^{2},\mathscr{A}^{2})

and define another eigenvector

\widetilde{\Gamma}={\vphantom{\!(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{2},\widetilde{\gamma}_{3})}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{2},\widetilde{\gamma}_{3})

(1.18)

\widetilde{\Gamma}:=k^{-1}\mathscr{A}\Gamma\in W(-k^{2},\mathscr{A}^{2}).

Then, one sees from (

(\gamma_{1}\rho_{1}+\gamma_{2}\mathcal{R}+\gamma_{3}\rho_{2})^{\prime}=k\mathcal{I}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\rho_{1}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}\mathcal{R}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{3}\rho_{2})

(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\rho_{1}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}\mathcal{R}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{3}\rho_{2})^{\prime}=-k\mathcal{I}(\gamma_{1}\rho_{1}+\gamma_{2}\mathcal{R}+\gamma_{3}\rho_{2})

These two identities immediately give us the conservation of

(1.19)

\mathcal{Q}:=(\gamma_{1}\rho_{1}+\gamma_{2}\mathcal{R}+\gamma_{3}\rho_{2})^{2}+(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\rho_{1}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}\mathcal{R}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{3}\rho_{2})^{2}.

Further,

Hence, the conservation of

1.8
).
We use this new kind of conserved quantity to prove our main theorem.
We discuss this conserved quantity in more detail in Section
5.2
.
It will turn out that this quantity is determined independently on the choice of

up to a positive constant. Further,
\mathcal{Q}
is an invariant polynomial
(see Proposition

).

Here, we would like to underline the center role played by the characteristic properties, such as eigenvalues and eigenspaces, of the linear map represented by matrix
\mathscr{A}
. This emphasizes that the matrix representation is more than just a square arrangement of coefficients of the system; it adeptly reflects the inherent nature of the system.
In our previous results, we focused on the fact that the rank of the matrix
\mathscr{A}
indicates the number of (quadratic) conserved quantities for (

From the perspective of eigenvalues, this involves examining whether the matrix

2.
Global existence

Now we turn to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
In this section, we establish the global-existence part of the theorem.
We also establish the global bound of the solution.

2.1.
Notations

We introduce operators.
Let

U(t)=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}

be the Schrödinger group.
The following factorization is known:

U(t)=M(t)D(t)\mathcal{F}M(t),

where
\mathcal{F}
stands for the standard Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}
,
M(t)=e^{i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\times
is a multiplication operator,
and
D(t)
is a dilation operator given by

(D(t)f)(x)=(2it)^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(\tfrac{x}{2t}).

Note that
U(-\frac{1}{4t})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}M(t)\mathcal{F}
.
Let us define an operator
J(t)
as

J(t):=x+2it\partial_{x}.

We have the identity

J(t)=U(t)xU(-t)=M(t)D(t)i\partial_{x}D(t)^{-1}M(t)^{-1}=M(t)2it\partial_{x}M(-t),

where the last two representation makes sense for
t\neq 0
.

2.2.
Local well-posedness of (
1.1
)

Proposition 2.1
.

There exists
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}>0
such that if
\varepsilon:=\left\lVert u_{0,1}\right\rVert_{H^{0,1}}+\left\lVert u_{0,2}\right\rVert_{H^{0,1}}
satisfies
\varepsilon<\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}
then there exists a unique solution
(u_{1},u_{2})\in C([-1,1];L^{2}\times L^{2})
to (
1.1
) such that

(U(-t)u_{1},U(-t)u_{2})\in C([-1,1];H^{0,1}\times H^{0,1})

and

\max_{t\in[-1,1]}(\left\lVert u_{1}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert J(t)u_{1}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert u_{2}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert J(t)u_{2}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}})\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

The proof is standard (see
[
CazBook
]
, for instance).
We merely note that, since the nonlinearities have the gauge invariant property, the operator
J(t)
acts like a derivative on them. More precisely, for
t\neq 0
,

\displaystyle J(t)(v_{1}\overline{v_{2}}v_{3})={}

\displaystyle M(t)2it\partial_{x}(M(-t)v_{1})(\overline{M(-t)v_{2}})(M(-t)v_{3}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(J(t)v_{1})\overline{v_{2}}v_{3}+v_{1}(\overline{J(t)v_{2}})v_{3}+v_{1}\overline{v_{2}}(J(t)v_{3}).

2.3.
Introduction of new variables

Introduce a new variable

(2.1)

w_{j}(t):=\mathcal{F}U(-t)u_{j}(t).

Note that
U(-t)u_{j}(t)\in H^{0,1}
if and only if
w_{j}(t)\in H^{1}
.
One sees that

(2.2)

i\partial_{t}w_{j}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2t}F_{j}(w_{1},w_{2})+r_{j}

for
j=1,2
and
t\neq 0
,
where

(2.3)

r_{j}:=\mathrm{I}_{j}+\mathrm{II}_{j}

with

\mathrm{I}_{j}=(U(\tfrac{1}{4t})-1)D(t)^{-1}M(t)^{-1}F_{j}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))

and

\mathrm{II}_{j}=\tfrac{1}{2t}(F_{j}(U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})w_{1}(t),U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})w_{2}(t))-F_{j}(w_{1}(t),w_{2}(t))).

The following relations will be useful:

(2.4)

\left\lVert w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\quad\left\lVert\partial_{x}w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert J(t)u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

and

(2.5)

\left\lVert U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

The last identity is valid for
t\neq 0
.

2.4.
Bootstrap argument

We shall obtain a global bound
of the small solutions.
For
\delta>0
and
T>0
, we define

X_{T}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(1+t)^{-\delta\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{j=1,2}(\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert J(t)u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}})

and

Y_{T}:=\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

By the local theory (Proposition
2.1
) and the inequality

\|u_{j}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|M(t)u_{j}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|M(t)u_{j}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}M(t)u_{j}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert J(t)u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}})

for
t>0
,
for any choice of
\delta>0
, one has

X_{1}+Y_{1}\lesssim\varepsilon

for small
\varepsilon>0
.
The goal of this section is to establish the following:

Proposition 2.2
.

There exist
\delta>0
,
C>0
, and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{0}]
such that if
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
then
the unique solution
(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))
given in Proposition

(2.6)

where

is as in Theorem
1.4
.

Remark 2.3
.

We only consider the bounds for positive time, however similar bounds hold for negative time and hence the solution is global also for negative time direction.

Proof.

It suffices to establish the existence of
\delta
,
C_{0}>0
,
\widetilde{C}_{0}>0
, and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

(2.8)

Let
\delta
,
C>0
, and
\varepsilon_{0}
to be chosen later. Suppose that (
2.7
) is true for some

.
Let us evaluate
X_{T}+Y_{T}
.
The estimate for

is standard.
Since the equation is cubic, one sees from the Duhamel formula that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle C_{1}\varepsilon+C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert u_{j}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{2}\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert u_{j}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle C_{1}\varepsilon+C_{1}Y_{T}^{2}X_{T}\int_{0}^{t}(1+s)^{\delta\varepsilon^{2}-1}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle C_{1}\varepsilon+\tfrac{C_{1}\widetilde{C}_{0}^{2}}{\delta}C_{0}\varepsilon(1+t)^{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}.

We have similar estimate for

\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert J(t)u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant C_{2}\varepsilon+\tfrac{C_{2}\widetilde{C}_{0}^{2}}{\delta}C_{0}\varepsilon(1+t)^{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}.

Hence,

Now, if one has

(2.9)

and

(2.10)

then one obtains

which is the first half of (
2.8

Let us turn to the

-decay estimate. For
t\in[0,1]
, we use

t^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant Y_{1}.

Hence, we consider
t\in[1,T]
.
It follows from Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality that

\displaystyle\left\lVert(U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})-1)w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert(U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})-1)w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\partial_{x}(U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})-1)w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

Hence, we see from (

)
and (

)
that

\displaystyle t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=\left\lVert U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\left\lVert w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+Ct^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\lVert\partial_{x}w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\left\lVert w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+C(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{4}+\delta\varepsilon^{2}}X_{T}

for
t\in[1,T]
.

Hence, we shall estimate

\left\lVert w_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
for

t\in[1,T]
.
To this end, we introduce the quartic quantity.
Let
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})\in W(-k^{2},A^{2})\cap\mathcal{P}_{+}

and

Q_{1}:=\gamma_{1}|w_{1}|^{2}+2\gamma_{2}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{w_{1}}w_{2})+\gamma_{3}|w_{2}|^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{w_{1}}&\overline{w_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\gamma}_{1}&{\gamma}_{2}\\

Hence, for

(2.11)

and
r_{2}
for

.

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{I}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\lVert\partial_{x}D(t)^{-1}M(t)^{-1}F_{j}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\lVert J(t)F_{j}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{5}{4}}\left(\sum_{j=1,2}t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{2}\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert J(t)u_{j}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{5}{4}+\delta\varepsilon^{2}}Y_{T}^{2}X_{T}

and

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{II}_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert(U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})-1)w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert U(-\tfrac{1}{4t})w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{5}{4}}\left(\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert\partial_{x}w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1,2}(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})^{2}+\sum_{j=1,2}\left\lVert w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\partial_{x}w_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle t^{-\frac{5}{4}+3\delta\varepsilon^{2}}(Y_{T}^{2}X_{T}+X_{T}^{3}).

), (

.

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{3}{2}}\alpha\left(\dn\left(\alpha t+t_{0},m\right)-\sqrt{m}\sn\left(\alpha t+t_{0},m\right)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t)

•

Note that a computation shows that

\displaystyle=2^{-1}\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{i\theta_{0}}\sn(\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha t+t_{0}),\tfrac{1}{2})\sqrt{1+\nd(\alpha t+t_{0},\tfrac{1}{2})},

\displaystyle A_{2}(t)

\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\mathscr{M}\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\end{bmatrix}
