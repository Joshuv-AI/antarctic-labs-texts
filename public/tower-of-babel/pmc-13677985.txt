Lumbar Facet Joint Morphology and Degenerative Lumbar Spondylolisthesis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Alberto Suarez, Justin Gumbs, Michelle Schmidt, Zeena Shather, Hanna Neustadter, Dominic Lafalce, Marissa Middleton, Jaden Wise, Isabella Merem, Maohua Lin, Frank D Vrionis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.ObjectivesTo evaluate the associations between lumbar facet orientation, facet tropism, and degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS), while assessing sex-specific differences in facet morphology.MethodsA PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261417450). PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Web of Science were searched through June 2026. Studies evaluating lumbar facet morphology in adults with DLS were included. Random-effects meta-analyses using restricted maximum likelihood estimation and Hartung–Knapp adjustment were performed. Risk of bias and evidence certainty were assessed.ResultsThirty-nine studies met the inclusion criteria. Sixteen studies (3,321 participants) were included in the facet orientation meta-analysis. Patients with DLS demonstrated more sagittally oriented facet joints than controls (SMD 1.30, 95% CI 0.78–1.82; p < 0.0001; I2 = 97.2%). Nine studies (1,913 participants) showed greater continuous facet tropism in DLS patients (MD 2.72°, 95% CI 2.07°–3.38°; p < 0.0001; I2 = 47.2%). Binary facet tropism was not statistically significant (OR 2.20, 95% CI 0.85–5.66; p = 0.0817). Sex-specific, severity, and instability data were insufficient for quantitative synthesis.ConclusionDLS is associated with more sagittally oriented facet joints and greater continuous facet tropism than controls. Limited sex-specific data and heterogeneity highlight the need for standardized measurement techniques and prospective longitudinal studies to clarify causality and sex-related effects.Keywords: Spondylolisthesis, degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis, lumbar facet orientation, lumbar facet tropism, lumbar facet sex differences, meta-analysis

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) is a common degenerative spinal disorder characterized by anterior translation of a vertebral body relative to the adjacent caudal vertebra in the absence of a pars interarticularis defect. 1 The condition is associated with progressive degeneration of the intervertebral discs, facet joints, and surrounding stabilizing structures, and frequently presents with low back pain, neurogenic claudication, radiculopathy, and functional impairment.1,2 DLS most commonly occurs at the L4–L5 level and demonstrates a well-recognized predominance among females, 3 suggesting that both anatomical and biomechanical factors contribute to disease development.

Lumbar facet joints play a critical role in maintaining spinal stability by resisting rotational, translational, and shear forces. 4 Variations in facet joint morphology have long been proposed as potential contributors to degenerative instability.5,6 Two morphologic characteristics have received particular attention: facet orientation and facet tropism. 6 Facet orientation describes the angular alignment of the facet joints relative to the sagittal plane, whereas facet tropism refers to asymmetry between the left and right facet joints. 7 More sagittally oriented facets may provide less resistance to anterior vertebral translation, while facet tropism may produce asymmetric loading patterns that accelerate degenerative changes and instability.5,7 These morphologies are visualized schematically in Figure 1.

(C) Proposed mechanisms linking sagittal facet orientation and facet tropism with susceptibility to degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS). Numerous observational studies have investigated the relationship between facet morphology and DLS; however, the reported findings remain inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated associations between sagittal facet orientation, increased facet tropism, and the presence of DLS,5,6 whereas others have reported weaker or nonsignificant relationships. Differences in imaging modality, measurement techniques, vertebral levels evaluated, and definitions of facet tropism have contributed to substantial heterogeneity across the literature. Consequently, the magnitude and consistency of these associations remain uncertain.

Interest has also emerged regarding potential sex-specific differences in lumbar facet morphology. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including postmenopausal hormonal changes, differences in ligamentous and soft-tissue support, pelvic morphology and sagittal alignment, bone quality, and sex-specific patterns of spinal degeneration.8-10 Declining estrogen levels after menopause may influence intervertebral disc, facet joint, bone, and ligamentous homeostasis, potentially altering the mechanical environment of the lumbar motion segment.11-13 In addition, differences in spinopelvic morphology and alignment may modify the shear forces transmitted through the lower lumbar spine and interact with facet orientation to influence resistance to anterior vertebral translation.14-17

Determining whether facet morphology differs between females and males is therefore important not only for explaining the epidemiologic predominance of DLS among women, but also for distinguishing the contribution of fixed osseous anatomy from other sex-associated degenerative and biomechanical factors. If facet orientation or tropism contributes differently to DLS according to sex, these characteristics could eventually improve understanding of disease susceptibility and inform sex-specific risk stratification.

Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the association between lumbar facet joint morphology and DLS. Specifically, we examined the relationships between facet orientation, facet tropism, and DLS, while also exploring available evidence regarding sex-specific differences, vertebral level–specific associations, and measures of disease severity and instability.

Methods

Study Design and Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420261417450) prior to study selection and data extraction. The review aimed to evaluate the association between lumbar facet joint morphology and DLS, with a particular focus on facet orientation, facet tropism, and potential sex-specific differences.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A comprehensive literature search was performed from database inception through June 2026 using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science. Searches combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to DLS, facet joint morphology, facet orientation, facet tropism, facet angle, lumbar instability, and sex differences. No restrictions were placed on the publication date. Only studies published in English were considered for inclusion. The full search strategies are provided in Supplementary Appendix.

Following the removal of duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (M.M. and M.S.). Full-text review was subsequently performed by two reviewers for potentially eligible studies (H.N. and Z.S.). Disagreements during study selection were resolved through screening by a 3rd reviewer (D.L). The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they evaluated adult patients undergoing lumbar spine imaging and reported quantitative measurements of lumbar facet joint morphology in relation to DLS. Eligible morphologic variables included facet orientation, facet tropism, facet asymmetry, facet angulation, facet inclination, or other quantitative measures of facet morphology. Imaging modalities included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), three-dimensional CT (3D-CT), and radiographic assessments.

Included study designs consisted of prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies. Studies were required to include a comparator group, such as patients without DLS, male versus female patients, or patients with and without facet tropism.

Studies were excluded if they involved pediatric populations, animal models, cadaveric-only investigations without patient imaging data, or non-degenerative forms of spondylolisthesis, including isthmic, congenital, dysplastic, traumatic, infectious, neoplastic, or postsurgical etiologies. Case reports, case series without comparator groups, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, conference abstracts lacking full-text data, and protocol papers were also excluded.

Data Extraction and Outcomes

Data extraction was performed using a standardized data collection form. Extracted variables included study characteristics (author, publication year, study design, country), patient demographics (sample size, age, sex distribution), imaging modality, vertebral level evaluated, facet morphology measurements, and reported DLS outcomes.

The primary outcome was the association between lumbar facet morphology and DLS. Facet orientation was analyzed as a continuous measure of facet angulation. Because studies used different measurement conventions, facet orientation values were harmonized so that positive effect estimates consistently represented greater sagittal orientation among patients with DLS.

Facet tropism was evaluated using both continuous and binary outcome measures. Continuous analyses examined differences in mean facet tropism angles between DLS and control groups, whereas binary analyses evaluated the prevalence of facet tropism according to study-specific threshold definitions. Facet tropism as a continuous measurement was evaluated separately due to possible heterogeneity from study-specific cutoffs.

Secondary outcomes included sex-specific differences in facet morphology, vertebral level-specific analyses, prevalence and severity of facet tropism, slip percentage, Meyerding grade, and radiographic or dynamic measures of lumbar instability. When outcome definitions or reporting methods were insufficiently comparable across studies, findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively.

Statistical Analysis and Risk of Bias

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.6.0 and the meta package (version 8.3-0). Random-effects meta-analyses were conducted using the restricted maximum likelihood estimator. Quantitative synthesis was performed when at least two studies reported sufficiently comparable outcome measures. Continuous facet orientation outcomes were pooled using standardized mean differences (SMD) with Hedges correction because measurement scales differed between studies. Continuous facet tropism outcomes were pooled using mean differences (MD) expressed in degrees. Binary facet tropism outcomes were pooled using odds ratios (OR).

Between-study variance was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, and confidence intervals were calculated using the Hartung–Knapp adjustment. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic. Prespecified sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analyses and restriction to the L4–L5 vertebral level, the most common level affected by DLS. Additional subgroup analyses were performed according to imaging modality and measurement methodology when sufficient data were available.

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers (A.S. and M.S.). Cohort and case-control studies were evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, whereas cross-sectional studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The certainty of evidence for major outcomes was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework.

Results

Study Selection

The literature search identified 373 records across five databases, including Scopus (n = 184), Web of Science (n = 81), PubMed (n = 54), CENTRAL (n = 29), and Embase (n = 25). After removal of 111 duplicate records, 262 studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 187 records were excluded, leaving 75 studies for full-text review. Following eligibility assessment, 33 studies were excluded, due to inappropriate outcomes (n = 24), non-English publication (n = 3), wrong intervention (n = 1), cadaver-only design (n = 1), or unavailable full text (n = 7). Ultimately, 39 studies met inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review. Study Characteristics

Thirty-nine studies evaluating lumbar facet morphology in relation to DLS were included in the systematic review. Included studies consisted primarily of retrospective cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs and were conducted across diverse geographic regions. Imaging modalities included MRI, CT, three-dimensional CT (3D-CT), myelogram-CT, and mixed imaging approaches. Most studies evaluated the L4–L5 level, the vertebral level most frequently affected by DLS. Outcomes included facet orientation, facet tropism, sex-specific morphologic differences, vertebral level-specific analyses, slip severity, and measures of radiographic instability. Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias assessments are included in Supplemental Tables 1a and 1b. Risk of bias was generally low to moderate across the included studies. Among studies assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, scores ranged from 3 to 7, with most studies demonstrating adequate exposure ascertainment, outcome assessment, and absence of the outcome at baseline. The most common limitations were reduced representativeness of the exposed cohort and limited adjustment for key confounders, specifically age and sex.

Studies assessed using the JBI checklist were overall of good quality, with most reporting clear inclusion criteria, standardized measurement, valid diagnostic methods, consecutive and complete inclusion, demographic characteristics, clinical information, and outcomes. Two studies were rated as moderate quality because of incomplete reporting of clinical information, while the remaining studies were rated as good quality.

For both tools, follow-up-related domains were marked as “N/A” when the study design or outcome was cross-sectional or imaging-based and did not require longitudinal follow-up to assess the primary outcome. Therefore, these items were not considered deficiencies in study quality. Overall, the evidence base was acceptable for synthesis, although residual confounding and variability in cohort representativeness should be considered when interpreting the results.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of evidence was rated as very low. Although most included studies demonstrated acceptable methodological quality on the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale or Joanna Briggs Institute checklist, all evidence came from observational study designs and was therefore initially rated as low certainty. The certainty was further downgraded because pooled outcomes demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across studies. Therefore, confidence in the pooled estimates is limited, and the true association may differ meaningfully from the observed effect estimates.

Facet Orientation in Degenerative Lumbar Spondylolisthesis

Sixteen studies comprising 3,321 participants (1,428 DLS and 1,893 control patients) were included in the quantitative synthesis of facet orientation. Random-effects meta-analysis demonstrated a significant association between DLS and more sagittally oriented facet joints (SMD 1.30, 95% CI 0.78–1.82; p < 0.0001). However, heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 97.2%). The study level and standardized means differences in facet orientation are shown in Figure 3. Positive standardized mean differences indicate more sagittally oriented facets in the DLS group.

Sensitivity Analyses

Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated stable findings across all models, with pooled effect estimates ranging from SMD 1.16 to 1.40. No individual study altered the direction or statistical significance of the association. Heterogeneity remained high in all models (I2 range 95.4%–97.4%). Restriction to studies evaluating the L4–L5 level produced similar results (14 studies; SMD 1.37, 95% CI 0.78–1.96; p = 0.0002). Leave-one-out analysis is plotted in Supplemental Figure 1.

Subgroup Analyses

Imaging subgroup analysis identified significant differences between imaging modalities (p < 0.0001). The largest effect size was observed among 3D-CT studies (SMD 2.73, 95% CI 1.57–3.89), whereas MRI studies demonstrated a smaller and statistically nonsignificant pooled estimate (SMD 0.75, 95% CI −0.99 to 2.49). CT and mixed MRI/CT studies demonstrated intermediate effects. Exclusion of 3D-CT studies attenuated the pooled effect but did not eliminate the association (SMD 0.99, 95% CI 0.47–1.52; p = 0.0018). Imaging subgroup analysis data are summarized in Supplemental Figure 2. Subgroup analysis based on angle-definition harmonization demonstrated no significant subgroup difference (p = 0.6008).

Continuous Facet Tropism

Nine studies involving 1,913 participants (986 DLS and 927 controls) reported continuous measurements of facet tropism. Patients with DLS demonstrated significantly greater facet tropism than controls (MD 2.72°, 95% CI 2.07°–3.38°; p < 0.0001). Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 47.2%). Study level and mean differences in facet tropism are shown in Figure 4. Positive mean differences indicate greater facet tropism in the DLS group.

Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated stable findings, with pooled estimates ranging from MD 2.49° to 3.01°. No individual study materially influenced the overall result. Results are shown in Supplemental Figure 3. Restriction to L4–L5 studies yielded nearly identical results (MD 2.75°, 95% CI 1.98°–3.52°). Imaging subgroup analysis demonstrated significantly larger effects among 3D-CT studies (MD 3.24°) compared with other imaging modalities (MD 1.83°; p = 0.0009).

Binary Facet Tropism

Five studies comprising 1,249 participants were included in the binary facet tropism meta-analysis. Although the pooled estimate favored a higher prevalence of facet tropism among patients with DLS, the association did not reach statistical significance (OR 2.20, 95% CI 0.85–5.66; p = 0.0817). Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 74.3%).

Leave-one-out analysis and L4–L5 restriction produced similar findings, with all pooled estimates remaining nonsignificant despite consistently favoring increased tropism among DLS patients.

Sex-Specific Findings

Sex-specific facet orientation data were insufficient for quantitative synthesis. He et al. and Walker et al. provided direct female-versus-male comparisons within DLS cohorts. Both studies demonstrated numerically lower measured facet angles among females than males; however, differences in imaging modality and measurement methodology precluded meta-analysis.18,19 Sex-specific facet tropism data were sparse and did not support quantitative pooling. Only He et al. provided extractable sex-stratified continuous tropism measurements. In that study, females demonstrated slightly greater facet tropism than males at L4–L5, although the clinical significance of this isolated finding remains uncertain. 18

Facet Morphology and Degenerative Spondylolisthesis Severity or Instability

Data regarding slip severity, Meyerding grade, dynamic instability, facet fluid, facet opening, and reoperation risk were highly heterogeneous and therefore unsuitable for quantitative pooling, as shown in Supplemental Table 2. Narrative synthesis demonstrated inconsistent findings. Several studies reported significant associations between facet orientation and measures of slip severity or dynamic instability,20,21 whereas others found no meaningful relationship between facet morphology and instability-related outcomes.15,22 Overall, available evidence was insufficient to establish a consistent association between facet morphology and DLS severity or progression.

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the relationship between lumbar facet joint morphology and DLS, with particular emphasis on facet orientation, facet tropism, and potential sex-specific differences. Consistent with our protocol objectives, we found that facet orientation and facet tropism were associated with DLS, although the strength and consistency of evidence differed between outcomes. Across 16 studies comprising 3,321 participants, patients with DLS had significantly more sagittally oriented facet joints than controls (SMD 1.30, 95% CI 0.78–1.82), with the association remaining significant in leave-one-out analyses and when restricted to L4–L5. Continuous facet tropism was also greater in patients with DLS (MD 2.72°, 95% CI 2.07°–3.38°) and demonstrated lower heterogeneity and similarly stable sensitivity analyses. Binary analyses of facet tropism did not reach statistical significance. The discrepancy between continuous and binary tropism analyses likely reflects differences in study-specific tropism thresholds and loss of information associated with dichotomization of a continuous anatomical variable.

Although sex-specific differences were a planned secondary objective of this review, available evidence was insufficient for quantitative synthesis. Similarly, evidence regarding relationships between facet morphology and DLS severity, slip progression, or radiographic instability was heterogeneous and largely derived from isolated studies. Consequently, these findings were synthesized narratively and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Facet Morphology and Clinical Significance

The primary finding of this review was that patients with DLS demonstrated significantly more sagittally oriented lumbar facet joints than control populations. The observed association is consistent with the established biomechanical role of the lumbar facet joints in guiding and constraining spinal motion. 23 Facets that are oriented more coronally provide greater resistance to rotational and translational forces, 13 whereas sagittally oriented facets permit increased anterior-posterior motion. 24 As a result, sagittalization of the facet joints may reduce the stabilizing effect of the posterior spinal elements and increase susceptibility to vertebral translation under repetitive loading.7,25 The persistence of this association when analyses were restricted at the L4–L5 level, the level most commonly affected by DLS, further supports the potential biomechanical relevance of this morphology.26,27

This review also identified a significant association between facet tropism and DLS. In the pooled analysis of continuous measurements, patients with DLS demonstrated approximately 2.7° greater facet tropism than controls. When facet joints differ substantially in orientation, loading may become unevenly distributed across the motion segment, resulting in asymmetric rotational forces, altered stress concentrations, and accelerated degeneration of adjacent structures.33,34 The relatively consistent association observed for continuous tropism suggests that facet asymmetry may represent another important component of the biomechanical environment associated with DLS.7,35

Importantly, whether sagittal facet orientation and tropism are predisposing characteristics or consequences of degeneration remains unresolved. A pre-existing sagittal or asymmetric facet configuration could increase susceptibility to repetitive shear and vertebral translation. Conversely, progressive disc-height loss, altered posterior loading, facet subluxation, and osseous remodeling could modify facet morphology after degeneration has begun. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and the observed associations in patients with established DLS may reflect contributions from both baseline anatomy and subsequent degenerative remodeling.

This distinction is particularly important for translating the present findings into clinical practice. If prospective studies demonstrate that facet morphology precedes vertebral slippage, measurement of facet orientation and tropism could eventually contribute to identifying patients at increased risk for DLS before clinically significant instability develops. Facet morphology is readily assessable on imaging already obtained during evaluation of lumbar degenerative disease and could potentially complement other anatomical and clinical risk factors rather than requiring a separate diagnostic test. Earlier identification of higher-risk individuals could provide an opportunity for closer clinical surveillance and earlier attention to potentially modifiable contributors to functional decline. However, the present study does not demonstrate that management should currently be altered on the basis of facet morphology. Rather, our findings identify a potential anatomical risk characteristic and a population in whom preventive or risk-reduction strategies could be evaluated prospectively.

Interestingly, the binary facet tropism analysis produced a different pattern of results. Although the pooled odds ratio suggested that facet tropism was more common among patients with DLS, the association did not reach statistical significance. Studies used varying thresholds to define tropism, including cutoffs of 5°, 7°, and 8°, introducing substantial heterogeneity and limiting comparability across investigations. The stronger association observed when tropism was analyzed continuously suggests that facet asymmetry may be better conceptualized as a spectrum rather than a categorical abnormality. Standardized continuous measurement may therefore preserve clinically relevant information that is lost when patients are classified according to arbitrary thresholds and may be preferable for future risk-prediction models.

The present findings align with the hypothesis that asymmetric facet morphology contributes to degenerative instability, but they do not establish causality. Similarly to facet orientation, it remains unclear whether facet tropism precedes the development of DLS or evolves secondary to progressive degeneration and remodeling of the lumbar motion segment. Longitudinal imaging studies evaluating asymptomatic individuals over time would be necessary to determine whether facet tropism functions as a true risk factor for future vertebral slippage. Nevertheless, the relatively consistent association observed across studies suggests that facet tropism represents an important morphologic characteristic of DLS and may warrant consideration in future risk stratification models and biomechanical investigations.

Measurement Considerations

Imaging methodology significantly influenced the magnitude of the observed associations. Studies utilizing three-dimensional CT reported larger differences in facet orientation and facet tropism between DLS and control groups than studies using other imaging modalities. Because the facet joint is a curved three-dimensional structure, measurements obtained from a selected two-dimensional plane may not fully characterize its morphology, whereas three-dimensional techniques can incorporate a larger portion of the articular surface. Nevertheless, exclusion of 3D-CT studies did not eliminate the association between facet orientation and DLS, suggesting that the overall finding was not solely modality dependent. These differences also have direct implications for clinical translation. Before facet morphology can be incorporated into routine risk assessment, standardized definitions, imaging planes, measurement techniques, and reporting conventions are needed.

Sex-Specific Findings

A major secondary objective of this review was to determine whether associations between lumbar facet morphology and DLS differ between females and males. This question is clinically important because DLS demonstrates a well-established female predominance, suggesting that sex-specific anatomic or biomechanical factors may contribute to disease development. However, despite the frequent reporting of overall sex distributions, relatively few studies provided sex-stratified facet morphology measurements, and the available data were insufficient for quantitative synthesis.

For facet orientation, only two studies provided extractable female-versus-male comparisons within DLS cohorts. Although both studies reported numerically lower measured facet angles among females than males, differences in imaging modality, measurement methodology, and angle definitions limited meaningful quantitative comparison. Consequently, no pooled estimate was generated, and the available evidence remains insufficient to determine whether facet orientation contributes to the recognized female predominance of DLS.

Evidence regarding sex-specific differences in facet tropism was also limited. Only one study provided usable sex-stratified continuous tropism measurements, reporting slightly greater mean tropism values among females than males. However, the magnitude of these differences was small and derived from a single cohort. Several additional studies discussed sex-specific findings qualitatively or reported nonsignificant differences, but numerical data were generally unavailable. As a result, no quantitative synthesis could be performed.

The inability to adequately evaluate sex-specific associations should not be interpreted as evidence that such differences do not exist. Rather, it reflects the limited reporting practices of the existing literature. This represents an important knowledge gap because the female predominance of DLS suggests that disease susceptibility may reflect interactions among facet morphology, spinopelvic anatomy, soft-tissue stabilization, hormonal status, and age-related degeneration. Determining whether facet orientation and tropism differ by sex, or whether the biomechanical consequences of a given facet configuration differ by sex, may provide greater insight into why DLS develops disproportionately in women.

Several biological mechanisms could plausibly contribute to this sex disparity. The increasing prevalence of DLS among women after menopause raises particular interest in hormonal influences.8,36 Estrogen receptors have been identified within lumbar facet joint tissues, and declining estrogen levels may affect the homeostasis of bone, cartilage, intervertebral discs, ligaments, and other connective tissues involved in maintaining segmental stability.11,37 Postmenopausal changes may therefore contribute to disc degeneration and loss of disc height, altered facet loading, and diminished soft-tissue restraint.38,39 Reduced ligamentous and capsular support could be particularly important because the facet joints function as posterior restraints to anterior translation once disc and ligamentous structures begin to degenerate.40,41 A sagittally oriented or asymmetric facet configuration may become biomechanically more consequential, potentially creating an interaction between underlying facet morphology and acquired age or hormone-related changes rather than either factor acting independently.8,42

Sex-related differences in spinopelvic anatomy and spinal biomechanics provide another potential mechanism. Pelvic morphology strongly influences lumbar lordosis, sacral orientation, and the distribution of forces across the lumbar motion segments, and several studies have associated higher pelvic incidence and altered sagittal alignment with DLS.43,44 Differences in pelvic morphology or sagittal alignment could therefore alter the magnitude and direction of shear forces acting at L4–L5, potentially modifying the mechanical effect of facet orientation and tropism. Differences in bone quality, paraspinal muscle characteristics, and patterns of degenerative remodeling may further modify this relationship.15,45

These considerations highlight why sex-stratified investigation remains clinically relevant despite the limited data identified in the present review. Future studies should evaluate sex not merely as a demographic covariate but as a potential effect modifier of the relationship between facet morphology and DLS. Prospective longitudinal cohorts incorporating sex-stratified facet measurements, menopausal status, spinopelvic parameters, bone density, and measures of disc, ligamentous, and paraspinal muscle degeneration could help determine whether certain morphologic configurations confer different risks in females and males.

Facet Morphology, Severity, and Instability

Beyond the presence of DLS, our review sought to evaluate whether facet morphology was associated with disease severity, slip progression, or radiographic measures of instability. Although several studies investigated these relationships, substantial heterogeneity in outcome definitions, measurement techniques, and analytical approaches precluded quantitative synthesis. Reported outcomes included slip percentage, Meyerding grade, dynamic translation, angular motion, facet fluid, facet opening, and reoperation for instability, often using fundamentally different effect measures. Consequently, the available evidence was synthesized narratively.

Some studies supported a potential relationship between facet morphology and markers of disease severity or instability. Most notably, Cinotti et al. reported significant correlations between facet orientation and vertebral slip percentage, sagittal translation, and angular motion, suggesting that facet orientation may influence both the degree of vertebral displacement and dynamic segmental behavior. 20 These findings are biomechanically plausible, as more sagittally oriented facets may provide less resistance to translational movement and therefore permit greater motion under physiologic loading.7,13

However, evidence across studies was not uniform. While some investigations observed associations between facet morphology and measures of instability, others reported little or no relationship. For example, Zhao et al. found similar facet orientation and facet tropism measurements among patients classified as having stable and unstable DLS, whereas Dai et al. reported no significant correlation between facet tropism and slip percentage.15,21 Similarly, findings regarding Meyerding grade were limited and inconsistent, with reported associations varying according to vertebral level and study methodology. DLS progression is likely influenced by numerous interacting factors beyond facet morphology, including intervertebral disc degeneration, ligamentous laxity, sagittal alignment, paraspinal muscle function, and overall spinal balance.8,46

Limitations and Future Directions

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the primary facet orientation analysis (I2 = 97.2%), which remained high despite multiple sensitivity analyses. Although the association between sagittal facet orientation and DLS remained statistically significant across all analyses, the magnitude of the effect varied considerably among studies. Differences in imaging modality, measurement techniques, and study methodology likely contributed to this variability. In addition, definitions of facet orientation and facet tropism were not standardized across studies, and residual methodological differences persisted despite angle-direction harmonization. All included studies were observational in design, precluding causal inference and raising the possibility that altered facet morphology may represent either a predisposing anatomical characteristic or a consequence of progressive degeneration. Furthermore, evidence regarding sex-specific differences was limited, with few studies reporting extractable sex-stratified data, preventing quantitative synthesis. Finally, outcomes related to DLS severity, slip progression, and radiographic instability were too heterogeneous for meta-analysis, limiting conclusions regarding the prognostic significance of facet morphology.47-50

Future research should prioritize prospective longitudinal imaging of individuals before the development of DLS using standardized methods for measuring facet orientation and continuous tropism. Such studies could establish whether these morphologic characteristics independently predict incident DLS and clinically meaningful risk thresholds, potentially allowing facet morphology to be incorporated into multifactorial risk models to identify individuals who may benefit from closer surveillance or early risk-reduction strategies. Given the well-established female predominance of DLS, 3 future studies should routinely report sex-stratified morphologic data to clarify whether facet morphology contributes to sex-related differences in disease susceptibility. Alternative explanations, including differences in pelvic morphology, sagittal spinal alignment, ligamentous laxity, hormonal influences, bone quality, and patterns of degenerative change, may also play important roles and warrant further investigation.10,36,51-53 Finally, larger prospective studies evaluating clinically relevant outcomes, including slip progression, radiographic instability, symptom severity, and surgical outcomes, are needed to determine whether facet morphology provides prognostic value beyond its association with the presence of DLS.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that DLS is associated with more sagittally oriented lumbar facet joints and greater continuous facet tropism, with these associations remaining consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. Continuous measures of facet tropism demonstrated a robust association with DLS and more moderate heterogeneity, suggesting that facet asymmetry may represent a clinically relevant morphological characteristic of degenerative instability. Binary definitions of facet tropism showed a similar directional relationship but did not reach statistical significance.

Evidence regarding sex-specific differences, DLS severity, and radiographic instability was limited and insufficient for quantitative synthesis. Nevertheless, the available literature suggests that facet morphology may contribute to the pathogenesis of DLS through altered biomechanical loading and reduced resistance to anterior vertebral translation. Overall, these findings support the role of lumbar facet morphology as an important anatomical factor associated with DLS, particularly at the L4–L5 level, while highlighting the need for standardized measurement techniques and prospective studies to clarify causal relationships and sex-specific effects.
