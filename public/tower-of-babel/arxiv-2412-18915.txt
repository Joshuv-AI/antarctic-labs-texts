On decomposition problem for distribution functions of class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q}
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We consider a new class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} of distribution functions F F that have the property of rational-infinite divisibility: there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} such that F 1 = F ∗ F 2 F_{1}=F*F_{2} . A distribution function of the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} is quasi-infinitely divisible in the sense that its characteristic function admits the Lévy–Khinchine type representation with a “signed spectral measure”. The class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} , being a natural extension of the class 𝑰 \boldsymbol{I} of infinitely divisible distribution functions, is actively studied now and it finds various applications. In 2018, Lindner, Pan and Sato formulated the open question: is it true that if F ∈ 𝑸 F\in\boldsymbol{Q} and F = F 1 ∗ F 2 F=F_{1}*F_{2} with some distribution functions F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} , then F 1 ∈ 𝑸 F_{1}\in\boldsymbol{Q} and F 2 ∈ 𝑸 F_{2}\in\boldsymbol{Q} ? There are some positive results under special assumptions on the type of F F . In this paper, we answer the question in a general setting without any additional assumptions. We also consider the same question but with the stronger assumption that F ∈ 𝑰 F\in\boldsymbol{I} .

1
Introduction

It is well known that infinitely divisible distributions play a fundamental role in probability theory and stochastic processes. In particular, these probability laws are the limit distributions for the sums of independent random variables in the most general shemes of summation within the uniform asymptotic negligibility of summands (see
[
19
]
and
[
25
]
). They are also distributions of the Lévy processes (see
[
20
]
), which have further applications in the stochastic calculus (see
[
2
]
), insurance mathematics (see
[
21
]
) and teletraffic models (see
[
13
]
). In this article, we will consider one problem related to the new natural extension of the class of infinitely divisible distributions.

We now turn to precise descriptions. Let
F
be a distribution function on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with the characteristic function

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We recall that
F
(and the corresponding distribution) is called
infinitely divisible
if for every positive integer
n
there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” denotes the convolution operation, i.e.
F
is
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. It is a fundamental fact that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if the characteristic function
f
is represented by
the Lévy–Khinchine formula
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and with a bounded non-decreasing
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is assumed to be right-continuous at every point of the real line with the condition
G(-\infty)=0
. We choose
x\mapsto\sin(x)
as the “centering function” in the integral in (
1
) following to Zolotarev (see
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
), but some other variants are possible (see
[
20
]
, p. 38). We note that
the spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
(and hence by
F
). The class of all infinitely divisible distribution functions
F
will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{I}
.

We call a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding distribution)
rational-infinitely divisible
if there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
. Using characteristic functions, this definition is equivalent to the admission of the formula

f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are characteristic functions of some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. Let
\boldsymbol{Q}
denote the class of all rational-infinitely divisible distribution functions.

From the definition, it is not difficult to see that the characteristic function
f
of
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
admits the
Lévy–Khinchine type representation
, i.e. (
1
) is valid with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and with some
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
(in general, it is non-monototic), which is assumed to be right-continuous on
\mathbb{R}
with the condition
G(-\infty)=0
. Here
the spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
too that was already shown in
[
7
]
and
[
12
]
.

Now suppose that
f
is represented by formula (
1
) with some
\gamma
and
G
satisfying the latter conditions. Following Lindner and Sato
[
14
]
, the corresponding distribution function
F
(and the corresponding probability law) is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
. Let us fix any real
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
\gamma=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
. Due to the Hahn–Jordan decomposition, there exist some functions
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, which are bounded, non-decreasing, and right-continuous on
\mathbb{R}
with
G_{1}(-\infty)=G_{2}(-\infty)=0
, such that
G=G_{1}-G_{2}
. Therefore
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are characteristic functions of infinitely divisible distribution functions with the spectral pairs
(\gamma_{1},G_{1})
and
(\gamma_{2},G_{2})
in canonical Lévy–Khinchine representations for
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively, i.e.
F
is rational-infinitely divisible.

Thus
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f
admits (
1
) with some
\gamma
and
G
satisfying the conditions above. So
\boldsymbol{Q}
is an extension of the class
\boldsymbol{I}
of all infinitely divisible distribution functions. Namely,
F\in\boldsymbol{I}
if and only if
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
with spectral function
G
, which is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
. Actually, the set
\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
is non-empty and its representatives may be find in the classical monographs
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
, and
[
16
]
.

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is actively studied now. An extensive general analysis was done by Lindner, Pan, Sato in the paper
[
14
]
. Criteria of belonging to this class are investigated in
[
1
]
and
[
9
]
for discrete probability laws, in
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
for mixtures of discrete and absolutely continuous distributions, and in
[
11
]
for the general case. The problems on weak convergence within
\boldsymbol{Q}
were discussed in
[
10
]
,
[
14
]
, and
[
22
]
. There are some interesting results concerning applications of the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
(see, for instance,
[
5
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
23
]
).

This note is devoted to the decomposition problem of distribution functions of class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. Namely, Lindner, Pan and Sato formulated the following question (see
[
14
]
, Open Question 8.4):

Let
F
,
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
be distribution functions on
\mathbb{R}
such that
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
and
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Is it true that also
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
belong to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
?

Following the general theory (see
[
6
]
,
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
), the equality
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
yields a
decomposition
of
F
into the
components

F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. The problem is rather natural and important, because if the aswer on the question was yes, then, in particular, any component of infinitely divisible
F
would be rational-infinitely divisible and so the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
, according to the definition of the rational-infinite divisibility, would coincide with the class of all compoponents of infinitely divisible distribution functions.

There is a whole series of results on the problem. So the authors of
[
14
]
answered the question in the positive for the case of lattice distributions. For arbitrary discrete probability laws the similar conclusion can be easily done based on
[
1
]
and
[
9
]
. Berger and Kutlu essentially generalized the previous results in the paper
[
4
]
. Namely, they positively solved the problem for mixtures discrete and absolutely continuous distributions (with non-zero discrete part). Also it should be noted that, in the theory of decompositions of probability laws, special representatives of
\boldsymbol{Q}
serve as components of infinitely divisible distribution functions (see
[
7
]
p. 81–83, and
[
15
]
p. 165).

In this article, we will consider the decomposition problem for the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
in a general setting without any additional assumptions on the type of distribution.

We will use the following notation. We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integers. For any complex number
z
we denote by
\mathrm{Re}\,\{z\}
and
\mathrm{Im}\,\{z\}
the real and imaginary parts, respectively. The signum function is denoted by
\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot)
, i.e.
\mathrm{sgn}(x)=+1
for
x>0
,
\mathrm{sgn}(x)=-1
for
x<0
, and
\mathrm{sgn}(0)=0
. If
\psi
is a complex-valued continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\psi(0)=1
and
\psi(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then
the distinguished logarithm

\mathrm{Ln}\,\psi
is defined by the formula
\mathrm{Ln}\,\psi(t):=\ln|\psi(t)|+i\mathrm{Arg}\,\psi(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathrm{Arg}\,\psi(t)
is the argument of
\psi(t)
uniquely defined on
\mathbb{R}
by the continuity with the condition
\mathrm{Arg}\,\psi(0)=0
.

2
Results

Throughout this section, we construct an example of probability law with an interesting characteristic function, which will give the answer for the formulated open question and for one its stronger modification. We will try to describe the example rather explicitly as much as possible, but with a reasonable sufficiency. In the construction, we use the ideas from the papers
[
8
]
and
[
18
]
. All conclusions will be formulated at the end of the section.

2.1
The first auxiliary function

Let us consider the following triangular function:

\displaystyle\Delta(t):=\begin{cases}1-|t|,&|t|\leqslant 1,\\
0,&|t|>1.\end{cases}

It satisfies Pólya’s sufficient conditions (see
[
16
]
p. 83) and hence it is a characteristic function of some absolutely continuous probability law. The function
\Lambda(t):=\Delta(t)e^{-|t|}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is characteristic too as the product with characteristic function of the Cauchy law. Since
\Lambda\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, it corresponds to an absolutely continuous distribution with the following density function:

\displaystyle q(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\Lambda(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-itx}(1-|t|)e^{-|t|}\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular, we know that
q\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
q(x)\geqslant 0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}q(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

We need to find explicit expression for
q
. Observe that

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\cos(tx)(1-|t|)e^{-|t|}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{0}\cos(tx)(1+t)e^{t}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{0}(1+t)\bigl(e^{(1+ix)t}+e^{(1-ix)t}\bigr)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We next integrate by parts with any
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1+t}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{(1+ix)t}}{1+ix}+\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{1-ix}\biggr)\bigg|_{-1}^{0}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{(1+ix)t}}{1+ix}+\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{1-ix}\biggr)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{1+ix}+\dfrac{1}{1-ix}\biggr)-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{1-e^{-1-ix}}{(1+ix)^{2}}+\dfrac{1-e^{-1+ix}}{(1-ix)^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}-\dfrac{\ell(x)}{\pi e(1+x^{2})^{2}},

where we define

\displaystyle\ell(x):=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl((e-e^{-ix})(1-ix)^{2}+(e-e^{ix})(1+ix)\Bigr),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us express the function
\ell
in the real form. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\ell(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl((e-1)(1-ix)^{2}+(e-1)(1+ix)^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle{}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl((1-e^{-ix})(1-ix)^{2}+(1-e^{ix})\cdot(1+ix)^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e-1)\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl((1-ix)^{2}+(1+ix)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle{}+\bigl(1-\cos(x)\bigr)\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl((1-ix)^{2}+(1+ix)^{2}\bigr)+\sin(x)\cdot\dfrac{i}{2}\bigl((1-ix)^{2}-(1+ix)^{2}\bigr),

where

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl((1-ix)^{2}+(1+ix)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl(2+2(ix)^{2}\bigr)=1-x^{2},

\displaystyle\dfrac{i}{2}\bigl((1-ix)^{2}-(1+ix)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{i}{2}\,(-4ix)=2x.

Therefore we obtain the needed formula:

\displaystyle\ell(x)=(e-1)(1-x^{2})+\bigl(1-\cos(x)\bigr)\cdot(1-x^{2})+\sin(x)\cdot 2x,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

Using the latter expression for the function
\ell
, we will get lower estimates for the density
q
. So we have

\displaystyle q(x)=\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\biggl(e-\dfrac{\ell(x)}{1+x^{2}}\biggr),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(4)

If
|x|\leqslant 1
then

\displaystyle\ell(x)\leqslant(e-1)(1-x^{2})+1-\cos(x)+2|\sin(x)|\cdot|x|\leqslant(e-1)(1-x^{2})+\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}+2x^{2}.

Here we applied the well known inequalities for
\sin(x)
and
\cos(x)
. Hence

\displaystyle\ell(x)\leqslant e-1+(3.5-e)x^{2}\leqslant(e-1)(1+x^{2}).

Thus for the case
|x|\leqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle q(x)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\biggl(e-\dfrac{(e-1)(1+x^{2})}{1+x^{2}}\biggr)=\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}.

If
|x|>1
, i.e.
1-x^{2}<0
, then, according to (
3
), we estimate

\displaystyle\ell(x)\leqslant(e-1)(1-x^{2})+0+2|\sin(x)|\cdot|x|\leqslant(e-1)(1-x^{2})+2|x|.

Therefore

\displaystyle\ell(x)\leqslant e-1+(1-e)x^{2}+2|x|=2e-2+(1-e)(1+x^{2})+2|x|.

According to (
4
), we have

\displaystyle q(x)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\biggl(e-\dfrac{2e-2}{1+x^{2}}-(1-e)-\dfrac{2|x|}{1+x^{2}}\biggr)=\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\biggl(2e-1-\dfrac{2(e-1)}{1+x^{2}}-\dfrac{2|x|}{1+x^{2}}\biggr).

Since
1+x^{2}>2|x|>2
as
|x|>1
, we get

\displaystyle q(x)>\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\bigl(2e-1-(e-1)-1\bigr)=\dfrac{e-1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}>\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}.

Thus we have proved that

\displaystyle q(x)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(5)

By the way, it is seen from (
3
) that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle|\ell(x)|\leqslant(e-1)(1+x^{2})+2(1+x^{2})+2|x|\leqslant(e+2)(1+x^{2})

(here we used the inequality
2|x|\leqslant 1+x^{2}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
).
Therefore

\displaystyle q(x)\leqslant\dfrac{1}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\biggl(e+\dfrac{|\ell(x)|}{1+x^{2}}\biggr)\leqslant\dfrac{2e+2}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

This confirms that the estimate (
5
) has a right order.

2.2
The second auxiliary function

We next fix arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we introduce a function

\displaystyle\chi(t):=\exp\bigl\{-|t|+i\varphi(t)\bigr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle\varphi(t):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\bigl(t-\mathrm{sgn}(t)\bigr)^{2n+1}}{2n+1},&|t|>1,\\
0,&|t|\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

Observe that
\varphi
is an odd function, which is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, in any case,
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\bigl(t-\mathrm{sgn}(t)\bigr)^{2n},&|t|>1,\\
0,&|t|\leqslant 1,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}2n\bigl(t-\mathrm{sgn}(t)\bigr)^{2n-1},&|t|>1,\\
0,&|t|\leqslant 1,\end{cases}

where
2n-1\geqslant 1
.

Since
\chi\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we can define the following function:

\displaystyle h(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\chi(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}e^{-|t|+i\varphi(t)}\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(6)

We don’t assert that
h
is a density function and
\chi
is a characteristic function of some probability law. We need only an upper estimate of
|h(x)|
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We first write
h(x)=J_{-}(x)+J_{+}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where

\displaystyle J_{-}(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1-ix)t}e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt,\qquad J_{+}(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(1+ix)t}e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us consider
J_{-}
. Integrating twice by parts, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we get

\displaystyle J_{-}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{1-ix}\cdot e^{i\varphi(t)}\biggr)\bigg|_{-\infty}^{0}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{1-ix}\,i\varphi^{\prime}(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1-ix}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{(1-ix)^{2}}\cdot i\varphi^{\prime}(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}\biggr)\bigg|_{-\infty}^{0}

\displaystyle{}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\dfrac{e^{(1-ix)t}}{(1-ix)^{2}}\,\Bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime}(t)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi(1-ix)}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{1}\dfrac{e^{t-ixt}}{(1-ix)^{2}}\,\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt.

Here we used the equalities
\varphi(t)=\varphi^{\prime}(t)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)=0
for any
t\in[-1,1]
. The function
J_{+}
is handled in the similar way:

\displaystyle J_{+}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(-\dfrac{e^{-(1+ix)t}}{1+ix}\cdot e^{i\varphi(t)}\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{\infty}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-(1+ix)t}}{1+ix}\,i\varphi^{\prime}(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1+ix}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{-(1+ix)t}}{(1+ix)^{2}}\cdot i\varphi^{\prime}(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle{}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-(1+ix)t}}{(1+ix)^{2}}\,\Bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime}(t)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi(1+ix)}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-t-ixt}}{(1+ix)^{2}}\,\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt.

Thus for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle h(x)=J_{-}(x)+J_{+}(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]}\dfrac{e^{-|t|-ixt}}{(1+ix\,\mathrm{sgn}(t))^{2}}\,\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\,dt.

Therefore

\displaystyle|h(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]}\biggl|\dfrac{e^{-|t|-ixt}}{(1+ix\,\mathrm{sgn}(t))^{2}}\,\bigl(i\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\bigr)e^{i\varphi(t)}\biggr|\,dt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}+\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]}\dfrac{e^{-|t|}}{1+x^{2}}\,\bigl(|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)|+|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\bigr)\,dt=\dfrac{e+K}{e\pi(1+x^{2})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle K:=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]}\bigl(|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)|+|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\bigr)\,e^{1-|t|}\,dt.

(7)

Observe that for
|t|>1

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|=|t-\mathrm{sgn}(t)|^{2n}=(|t|-1)^{2n},\qquad|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)|=2n|t-\mathrm{sgn}(t)|^{2n-1}=2n(|t|-1)^{2n-1}.

Consequently, the integrand in (
7
) is an even function. Therefore

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigl(2n(t-1)^{2n-1}+(t-1)^{4n}\bigr)\,e^{1-t}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2n-1}e^{-u}\,du+\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{4n}e^{-u}\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n\cdot(2n-1)!+(4n)!=(2n)!+(4n)!.

Thus

\displaystyle|h(x)|\leqslant\dfrac{e+(2n)!+(4n)!}{e\pi(1+x^{2})}\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(8)

In particular, this means that
h\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and the following formula is valid:

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}h(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

It is important to note that the function
h
is real-valued. Indeed, returning to formula (
6
), it is easily seen that the integrand function

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}(t):=e^{-itx}e^{-|t|+i\varphi(t)}=\bigl(\cos(tx)+i\sin(tx)\bigr)\bigl(\cos\varphi(t)+i\sin\varphi(t)\bigr)e^{-|t|},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

has an odd imaginary part due to the oddness of
\varphi
. Therefore
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Im}\,\bigl\{\Lambda_{x}(t)\bigr\}\,dt=0
and

\displaystyle h(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda_{x}(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Re}\,\bigl\{\Lambda_{x}(t)\bigr\}\,dt+\dfrac{i}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Im}\,\bigl\{\Lambda_{x}(t)\bigr\}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Re}\,\bigl\{\Lambda_{x}(t)\bigr\}\,dt\in\mathbb{R}.

2.3
Construction of the principal distribution

We now fix an arbitrary
\delta>0
such that
\delta\bigl(e+(2n)!+(4n)!\bigr)\leqslant 1
and we define the function

\displaystyle p_{1}(x):=\dfrac{q(x)+\delta h(x)}{1+\delta},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
q
and
h
have real values, the function
p_{1}
is real-valued too. Moreover, due to (
5
) and (
8
), it is non-negative:

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)\geqslant\dfrac{q(x)-\delta|h(x)|}{1+\delta}\geqslant\dfrac{1-\delta\bigl(e+(2n)!+(4n)!\bigr)}{e\pi(1+x^{2})(1+\delta)}\geqslant 0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Next,
p_{1}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, because
q
and
h
are from this space as we showed above. Let us define the Fourier transform of the function
p_{1}
:

\displaystyle f_{1}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}p_{1}(x)\,dx=\dfrac{1}{1+\delta}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}q(x)\,dx+\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}h(x)\,dx\biggr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

According to (
2
) and (
9
), we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)=\dfrac{\Lambda(t)+\delta\chi(t)}{1+\delta},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

From this, by the definitions of
\Lambda
and
\chi
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{1}(x)\,dx=f_{1}(0)=\dfrac{\Lambda(0)+\delta\chi(0)}{1+\delta}=\dfrac{1+\delta\cdot 1}{1+\delta}=1.

Thus
p_{1}
is a density of some probability law and
f_{1}
is its characteristic function. We denote by
F_{1}
the corresponding distribution function.

Let us write the explicit expression for
f_{1}
using formula (
10
):

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)=\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta e^{i\varphi(t)}}{1+\delta}\,e^{-|t|}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{(1-|t|)+\delta}{1+\delta}\,e^{-|t|},&|t|\leqslant 1,\\
\tfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}\,\exp\Bigl\{i\,\tfrac{(t-\mathrm{sgn}(t))^{2n+1}}{2n+1}\Bigr\}\,e^{-|t|},&|t|>1.\end{cases}

From this, in particular, we find

\displaystyle|f_{1}(t)|=\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}\,e^{-|t|},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(11)

Since
\delta>0
and
\Delta(t)\geqslant 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
f_{1}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, observe that
f_{1}(t)=|f_{1}(t)|e^{i\varphi(t)}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and, consequently,
\mathrm{Arg}\,f_{1}(t)=\varphi(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, because
\varphi
is continuous and
\varphi(0)=0
. So the distinguished logarithm of
f_{1}
admits the following representation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln|f_{1}(t)|+i\mathrm{Arg}\,f_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\biggl(\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}\biggr)-|t|+i\varphi(t)=\begin{cases}\ln\bigl(\tfrac{(1-|t|)+\delta}{1+\delta}\bigr)-|t|,&|t|\leqslant 1,\\
\ln\bigl(\tfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}\bigr)-|t|+i\,\tfrac{(t-\mathrm{sgn}(t))^{2n+1}}{2n+1},&|t|>1.\end{cases}

We now show that
F_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
. Let us write explicitly the values of
\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{1}(t)
for
t>1
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{1}(t)=\ln\biggl(\dfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}\biggr)-t+i\,\dfrac{(t-1)^{2n+1}}{2n+1}.

This, in particular, implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{|\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{1}(t)|}{t^{2n+1}}=\dfrac{1}{2n+1},

where
2n+1\geqslant 3
. Therefore
|\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{1}(t)|/t^{2}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, which is impossible for characteristic functions of (rational-)infinitely divisible laws due to the following fact: if
f
is a characteristic function of some
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
(or
\boldsymbol{I}
), then
\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)/t^{2}\to c
as
t\to\infty
with some constant
c\leqslant 0
(see
[
14
]
, Lemma 2.7, or
[
20
]
, Lemma 43.11). Thus we proved that
F_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Let us consider the function
|f_{1}|(t):=|f_{1}(t)|
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which is described by formula (
11
). It is an interesting fact that
|f_{1}|
is a characteristic function of some distribution function
F_{1,a}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. We will prove it and find the corresponding spectral pair.

Recall that
t\mapsto e^{-|t|}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is the characteristic function of distribution function
F_{C}
of the Cauchy law, which is infinitely divisible, i.e.
F_{C}\in\boldsymbol{I}
. The corresponding Lévy-Khinchine representation is well known (see
[
24
]
pp. 4–8):

\displaystyle e^{-|t|}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\dfrac{\,dx}{\pi x^{2}}\biggr\}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{C}(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where the shift parameter equals zero and the spectral function
G_{C}
is defined by the formula:

\displaystyle G_{C}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{\,du}{\pi(1+u^{2})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Next, we mentioned above that
t\mapsto\Delta(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is a characteristic function of some absolutely continuous distribution. Therefore
t\mapsto\tfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
is a characteristic function of some distribution, which is a mixture of the previous one and the degenerate law concentrated at the point
x=0
. Let
F_{m}
denote its distribution function. Let us show that
F_{m}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and find the corresponding spectral pair. We write

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}=\exp\biggl\{\ln\biggl(\dfrac{\delta+\Delta(t)}{1+\delta}\biggr)\biggr\}=\exp\biggl\{\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{\Delta(t)}{\delta}\biggr)-\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{\delta}\biggr)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(12)

where the function
t\mapsto\ln\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Delta(t)\bigr)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and it has the support
[-1,1]
. So it belongs to
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and we can define the function

\displaystyle g_{m}(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{\Delta(t)}{\delta}\biggr)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

By the definition of
\Delta(t)
, we have

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-itx}\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1-|t|}{\delta}\biggr)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\cos(tx)\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1-t}{\delta}\biggr)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)

Observe that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle|g_{m}(x)|\leqslant\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1-t}{\delta}\biggr)\,dt\leqslant\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1-t}{\delta}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\delta}.

(14)

For
x\neq 0
we apply the integration by parts in (
13
):

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)=\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(tx)}{\pi x}\,\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1-t}{\delta}\biggr)\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{1}-\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\sin(tx)}{x}\cdot\dfrac{-1}{\delta+1-t}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi x}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\sin(tx)}{\delta+1-t}\,dt.

We again integrate by parts :

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\biggl(\dfrac{1}{\pi x}\cdot\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\delta+1-t}\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{1}+\dfrac{1}{\pi x}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{x}\,\dfrac{1}{(\delta+1-t)^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi x^{2}(1+\delta)}-\dfrac{\cos(x)}{\pi x^{2}\delta}+\dfrac{1}{\pi x^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{(\delta+1-t)^{2}}\,dt.

Thus we obtain that

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi\delta x^{2}}\,\biggl(\dfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}-\cos(x)+\delta\varepsilon(x)\biggr),\quad x\neq 0,

(15)

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{(\delta+1-t)^{2}}\,dt,\quad x\neq 0.

Since the function
t\mapsto\tfrac{1}{(\delta+1-t)^{2}}
is continuous and bounded on
[0,1]
, the function
\varepsilon
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. From the classical Fourier analysis we also know that
\varepsilon(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
. So, due to (
14
) and (
15
), we conclude that
g_{m}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Hence the following inversion formula holds

\displaystyle\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{\Delta(t)}{\delta}\biggr)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}g_{m}(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We note that

\displaystyle\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{\delta}\biggr)=\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{\Delta(0)}{\delta}\biggr)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g_{m}(x)\,dx.

Returning to (
12
), we write

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{itx}-1)g_{m}(x)\,dx\biggr\}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)g_{m}(x)\,dx\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
it\sin(x)
were formally added due to the evenness of the function
g_{m}
(see formula (
13
)). Thus we come to the Lévy–Khinchine type representation

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{m}(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with zero shift parameter and the spectral function

\displaystyle G_{m}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{u^{2}g_{m}(u)}{1+u^{2}}\,\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore
F_{m}
is quasi-infinitely divisible and, consequently,
F_{m}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
(see the introduction). By the way, the very fact that
F_{m}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
follows by Berger’s criterion from
[
3
]
. We didn’t apply it because we need the explicit expression for the spectral function.

We now return to
|f_{1}|
. According to (
11
), we see that
|f_{1}|
is the characteristic function corresponding to the distribution function
F_{1,a}:=F_{m}*F_{C}
and
|f_{1}|
admits the Lévy-Khinchine type representation:

\displaystyle|f_{1}(t)|=\dfrac{\Delta(t)+\delta}{1+\delta}\,e^{-|t|}=\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{1,a}(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(16)

with zero shift parameter and the spectral function
G_{1,a}(x):=G_{m}(x)+G_{C}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

\displaystyle G_{1,a}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{u^{2}g_{m}(u)}{1+u^{2}}\,\,du+\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+u^{2})}\,\,du=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1}{\pi(1+u^{2})}\,\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore
F_{1,a}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Actually, we can prove more that
F_{1,a}\in\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
. Indeed, according to (
15
), for any
u\neq 0

\displaystyle\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\,\biggl(\dfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}-\cos(u)+\delta\varepsilon(u)\biggr)+1=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\,\biggl(\delta+\dfrac{\delta}{1+\delta}-\cos(u)+\delta\varepsilon(u)\biggr).

Hence the inequality
\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1<\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,\bigl(2\delta-\cos(u)\bigr)
holds for all
u
with sufficiently large absolutely values and, in particular, for these
u
we have
\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1<0
on the intervals, where
\cos(u)>2\delta
. Therefore
G_{1,a}
cannot be monotonic. This means that
F_{1,a}\in\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
. By the way, as corollary,
F_{m}\in\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
too.

2.4
The statements

We now introduce a distribution function
F:=F_{1,a}*F_{1,a}
. Let
f
denote its characteristic function. On the one hand, we have that
f(t)=|f_{1}(t)|^{2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. On account of (
16
), the function
f
admits the Lévy-Khinchine type representation (
1
) with
\gamma:=0
and
G(x):=2G_{1,a}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and, consequently,
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and even more
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
. On the other hand,
f(t)=|f_{1}(t)|^{2}=f_{1}(t)\overline{f_{1}(t)}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which means that
F=F_{1}*\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}
, where
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}
denotes the conjugate distribution function of
F_{1}
(see
[
16
]
, p. 29), i.e.
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}(x):=1-F_{1}(-x-0)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is clear that
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
together with
F_{1}
(see description of
\boldsymbol{Q}
in the introduction).

Thus we come to the following assertion.

Theorem 1

There exist distribution functions
F
,
F_{1}
, and
F_{2}
such that
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
and
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
, but
F_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
and
F_{2}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
.

So we answer the open question from the introduction in the negative.

Will the answer change in the positive if we modify the formulation of the question replacing the assumption that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
by the stronger one that
F\in\boldsymbol{I}
? In order to answer this question, we continue to work with our example within the same notations. Recall that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
with the spectral pair
(0,G)
in the representation of its characteristic function
f
, where

\displaystyle G(x)=2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1}{\pi(1+u^{2})}\,\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us define non-negative functions

\displaystyle g_{+}(u):=\max\bigl\{0,\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{-}(u):=-\min\bigl\{0,\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1\bigr\},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

So we have
\pi u^{2}g_{m}(u)+1=g_{+}(u)-g_{-}(u)
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we write
G(x)=G_{+}(x)-G_{-}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where

\displaystyle G_{+}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{2g_{+}(u)}{\pi(1+u^{2})}\,\,du\quad\text{and}\quad G_{-}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{2g_{-}(u)}{\pi(1+u^{2})}\,\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

The functions
G_{+}
and
G_{-}
are non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
due to the non-negativity of
g_{+}
and
g_{-}
. Let
F_{+}
and
F_{-}
be infinitely divisible distribution functions with the spectral pairs
(0,G_{+})
and
(0,G_{-})
, respectively. Their characteristic functions, say
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
, respectively, satisfy the equality
f(t)=f_{+}(t)/f_{-}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Writing it in the form
f_{+}(t)=f(t)f_{-}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
F_{+}=F*F_{-}
. Then
F_{+}=(F_{1}*\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1})*F_{-}=F_{1}*(\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}*F_{-})
, where
F_{+}\in\boldsymbol{I}
and
F_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
. Actually,
(\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}*F_{-})\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
, since otherwise we can find infinitely divisible distribution function
F_{3}
and
F_{4}
such that
F_{3}=(\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}*F_{-})*F_{4}
and, consequently,

\displaystyle F_{+}*F_{4}=F_{1}*(\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-1.79993pt$F$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{1}*F_{-})*F_{4}=F_{1}*F_{3},

where
(F_{+}*F_{4})
and
F_{3}
are infinitely divisible, i.e.
F_{1}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
, a contradiction.

Theorem 2

There exist distribution functions
F
,
F_{1}
, and
F_{2}
such that
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
and
F\in\boldsymbol{I}
, but
F_{1}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
and
F_{2}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
.

So we answer the modified open question in the negative too.

The reason of the negative results, as we saw above, is that a component of a (rational-)infinitely divisible distribution function can have the characteristic function, whose argument has a very fast growth at the infinity. In our example, the argument of the component characteristic function
f_{1}
is a power function of arbitrarily high degree.

Thus Theorem 1 and 2 show that in order to get the positive solution of the decomposition problem for the classes
\boldsymbol{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{I}
we need to make some additional assumptions for the distribution function
F
.
