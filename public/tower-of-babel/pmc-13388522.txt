Association between chrononutrition patterns and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in adolescents: a population-based study
Authors: Yueju Wang, Mingxin Wang, Jingya He, Congwei You, Cai Tang, Lihong Zhu, Le Zhang, Xiaodie Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have primarily examined the benefits of calorie restriction and fasting for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, the impacts of eating timing and frequency on MASLD remain uncertain.MethodsThis study comprised 2128 participants aged 12 to 19 years, drawn from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data collected during the period 2017 to 2023. Chrononutrition patterns were determined with two 24-h dietary recalls. Logistic regression, accounting for potential confounders, was used to examine the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD. Subgroup analyses stratified by demographic and clinical variables were used to evaluate their potential impact on the associations observed. Mediation analysis was conducted to assess the indirect effects of metabolic indicators on MASLD risk.ResultsLogistic regression demonstrated that the timing of the first (continuous: OR: 1.07; 95% CI 1.02–1.14) and midpoint (continuous: OR: 1.11; 95% CI 1.00–1.22) food/beverage intake, as well as energy intake at dinner (OR: 1.01; 95% CI 1.00–1.01), were significantly associated with an increased risk of MASLD. Furthermore, the eating window (continuous: OR: 0.95; 95% CI 0.91–0.99) was significantly negatively correlated with the risk of MASLD. Subgroup analyses revealed that the chrononutrition pattern was particularly linked to an elevated risk of MASLD among males and Mexican Americans (P < 0.05). Additionally, HDL-C and NHHR (58.69% and 61.90%, respectively) partially mediated the association between the timing of the first food/beverage intake and MASLD risk.ConclusionLater timing of the first and midpoint food and beverage intake, as well as greater energy consumption at dinner, are linked to an elevated risk of MASLD. HDL-C and NHHR serve as mediators in these associations, particularly with respect to the timing of the first food or beverage intake.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40519-026-01874-0.Keywords: NHANES, MASLD, Chrononutrition patterns, Adolescents

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), was newly termed in 2023 to further emphasize its metabolic characteristics [1] MASLD is clinically defined by evidence of hepatic steatosis combined with at least one additional metabolic abnormality. The characteristic of hepatic steatosis is the deposition of excess triglycerides in the liver, involving ≥ 5% of hepatocytes [2] MASLD begins with the presence of hepatic steatosis and could progress into metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [3] MASLD is closely related to type 2 diabetes, overweight/obesity, and other metabolic risk factors, and could elevate the risk of cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease, malignancies both within and outside the liver, as well as liver-related outcomes [4] Research has shown that among adolescents aged 12–18 years, the prevalence of MASLD [controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) ≥ 248 dB/m] is currently estimated to be 24% [5] which has surfaced as the predominant etiology of chronic liver disease among adolescents. The latest international multidisciplinary consensus, released in 2024, emphasizes that lifestyle management, including a healthier dietary pattern and regular exercise, is fundamental to the treatment of pediatric MASLD [6]. In terms of diet, the focus is on dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet, hypocaloric diet, ketogenic diet, and intermittent calorie restriction, which aim to restrict calorie intake and promote fasting. However, the impact of eating timing and frequency on MASLD remains uncertain.

Recently, a new field called “chrononutrition,” which was introduced by Dr. Alain Delabos in 1986, has attracted widely interest [7] Chrononutrition denotes the alignment of food consumption timing with the body’s circadian system (the internal biological clock), encompassing the dimensions of timing (actual time of day of food intake), frequency (number of eating occasions per day), and irregularity (fluctuating energy intake across the day and varying meal times day after day) [8]. Among humans, every physiological process is synchronized with the light/dark cycle, which spans an approximate 24-h period and is known as the circadian rhythm. The circadian system consists of a central clock situated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, as well as numerous peripheral clocks distributed across various organs throughout the body [9]. The biological clock, which is marked by changes in metabolism that occur throughout the day, regulates various physiological processes [10]. Multiple studies suggest a strong connection between chrononutrition and metabolic diseases, including obesity, [11] diabetes, [12], and metabolic syndrome (MetS) [13]. This association also appears to persist in children and adolescents. Research indicates that more frequent eating may be related to a lower probability of developing obesity and MetS [14]. In addition, later eating times have been linked to a greater predisposition to adiposity, impaired insulin sensitivity, enlarged waist circumference, and elevated concentrations of inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP) [15, 16]. Given the continuity and stability of dietary behavior patterns from childhood into adulthood, [17] exploring the significance of chrononutrition during developmental stages may offer an effective approach to decreasing the risk of diet-associated diseases in later adult life.

Metabolic abnormalities, characterized by alterations in critical lipid parameters such as total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), are instrumental in the pathogenesis of MASLD [18]. NHHR (non-HDL divided by HDL-C) is an emerging lipid index that shows novelty and diagnostic reliability [19]. It reflects the balance between HDL-C and non-HDL-C and overcomes the limitations of previous studies focusing on single lipid parameters [20, 21]. Previous studies have shown that NHHR provides higher accuracy in predicting NAFLD in both Japanese and Chinese adults, as well as in children and adolescents, outperforming traditional lipid markers such as non-HDL-C [22–24]. In addition, multiple studies have demonstrated NHHR’s potential as a reliable predictor for various conditions, including type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, gallstone, and metabolic syndrome [25–27]. It is well established in the literature that chrononutrition patterns are closely related to metabolic indicators such as BMI, TC, and LDL-C [28–31]. Thus, metabolic indicators could function as intermediaries in the association between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD in adolescents.

This study utilizes nationally representative data obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which was collected during the period 2017 to 2023. The aim was to examine the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD among adolescents, along with further investigating the potential role of metabolic abnormalities in this relationship through mediation analysis.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study is based on an analysis of data from the NHANES 2017–2023. NHANES is a complex, multistage, stratified design survey of the health and nutrition status of adults and children in the United States. Initially, a total of 27,493 participants were included. Next, we excluded participants aged ≥ 20 years and those missing data on liver ultrasound transient elastography (n = 7591), two 24-h dietary recalls (n = 456), poverty to income ratio (PIR) (n = 268) or body mass index (BMI) (n = 9). Finally, 2128 adolescents were incorporated into the analysis (Fig. 1). The NHANES data were authorized by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and informed consent was acquired from all participants.

Flowchart depicting the selection process of study participants

Definition of MASLD

Transient elastography, an ultrasound-based elastography method, was performed using the FibroScan® 502 V2 Touch (Echosens) by well-trained health technicians. The CAP measured by this method is used to quantify the degree of steatosis. The examinations were deemed reliable only when a minimum of 10 valid measurements were taken after a fasting period of at least 3 h, with the interquartile range to median ratio of less than 30%. We applied a cut-off value of 248 dB/m for CAP as a marker of hepatic steatosis [32]. MASLD is diagnosed based on the presence of hepatic steatosis combined with at least one additional metabolic abnormality: (1) BMI ≥ 1 standard deviation (SD) based on age- and sex-specific World Health Organization (WHO) reference standards; [33] (2) Fasting serum glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or self-reported type 2 diabetes; (3) Blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mmHg if age was < 13 years, or ≥ 130/85 mmHg if age was ≥ 13 years; (4) Plasma triglycerides ≥ 100 mg/dL for age < 10 years or ≥ 150 mg/dL for age ≥ 10 years; (5) Plasma HDL-C ≤ 40 mg/L [34].

Evaluation of chrononutrition patterns

The corresponding data on chrononutrition patterns were derived from the two 24-h dietary surveys in the NHANES dataset. From 2017 to 2020, the first interview was provided in person at the mobile examination center, while the second interview occurred via phone 3 to 10 days later. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, both dietary recall interviews from 2021 to 2023 were conducted remotely via phone. The first food or beverage intake time refers to the moment when the first food or beverage with more than 0 kcal energy is consumed after midnight (0:00), while the last intake time is the latest instance before 23:59 when energy intake exceeds 0 kcal. The midpoint time of food/beverage intake was determined as ([the last food/beverage intake time—the first food/beverage intake time] / 2) + the first food/beverage intake time. The eating window is determined by the duration between the first and last intake times [35]. The duration of nighttime fasting was defined as the time interval between the last food/beverage intake and the first food or beverage intake [36]. Eating frequency was evaluated by counting the self-reported eating events with energy intake > 0 kcal at a single time. Additionally, the proportion of total energy intake from breakfast, lunch, and dinner was calculated. To ensure accuracy, all variables were averaged across the two 24-h dietary recall interviews.

Mediating variables

The levels of TC and HDL-C were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer, specifically the Roche Cobas 6000 Chemistry Analyzer, at the Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory. The non-HDL-C level, abbreviated as NHHR, is determined by calculating the ratio of non-HDL-C to HDL-C. Non-HDL-C was calculated by deducting HDL-C from TC [37]. BMI z-scores standardized for age and sex were calculated using R package “anthroplus,” according to the WHO reference guidelines [33]. BMI status was classified as normal or underweight (BMI z-score ≤ 1), overweight (1 < BMI z-score ≤ 2), or obesity (BMI z-score > 2).

Covariates

The covariates assessed in this study were gender (male and female), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races), PIR, total energy intake, and diabetes status. The total daily energy intake was derived from the dietary interview data, with the total energy intake of the first two days being averaged to represent the participants’ energy intake. In instances where data from the second day were not available, the data from the first day were used to estimate the typical daily total energy intake. Diabetes status was obtained through self-reported questionnaires and categorized into prediabetes/diabetes or non-diabetes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with R software (version 4.4.0). Continuous variables were characterized by mean and SD, while categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage. Group comparisons were performed using t-tests or chi-square tests. Weighted logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD. The chrononutrition patterns were as continuous variables or grouped as categorical variables for further analysis. Model A unadjusted covariates; Model B adjusted for gender, race/ethnicity, PIR, prediabetes/diabetes, and total energy intake. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential differences in demographic factors, including gender (male and female), BMI (normal or underweight, overweight, and obesity), and race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races). Cross-product terms involving chrononutrition patterns and the relevant stratification variables were added to the logistic regression models to examine potential interaction effects, and likelihood ratio tests were utilized to evaluate the statistical significance of these interactions.

To explore possible non-linear relationships between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD, restricted cubic spline (RCS) transformation with 4 knots was applied to the chrononutrition patterns as the independent variable, while adjusting for all covariates. The mediation analysis was carried out using the “mediation” package in R to assess the effect of metabolic factors on the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD. Covariates were incorporated into all regression analyses to enhance model estimation accuracy. To estimate effect sizes and obtain 95% confidence intervals, we used a quasi-Bayesian approximation with 1000 simulations to assess direct, indirect, and total effects, as well as the mediated proportion. To evaluate the robustness of the mediation analysis, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted by redefining MASLD after excluding lipid-related diagnostic components, namely, plasma triglyceride and HDL-cholesterol abnormalities. A two-sided P value below 0.05 was regard as indicative of a statistically significant result.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total sample size of 2128 adolescents were selected for inclusion in the study, of which 511 (24.0%) were identified as having MASLD. Among the 511 participants diagnosed with MASLD, overweight or obesity was observed in 493 participants (96.5%), glucose abnormalities in 152 participants (29.8%), blood lipid abnormalities in 146 participants (28.6%), and blood pressure abnormalities in 9 participants (1.8%). Regarding the number of metabolic abnormalities, 271 participants (53.0%) had one metabolic abnormality, whereas 240 participants (47.0%) had two or more metabolic abnormalities. The mean age the enrolled individuals is 15.4 years, with 51.3% being male. As expected, compared with participants without MASLD, those in the MASLD group had higher BMI z-scores and higher proportions of obesity and prediabetes/diabetes (P < 0.001). Participants in the MASLD group also had significantly lower PIR and total daily energy intake, as well as higher proportions of male participants and Mexican Americans, than those without MASLD (P < 0.05). In addition, the largest proportion of total daily energy intake was ingested at dinner (43.16%), followed by lunch (34.31%), while breakfast (23.22%) accounted for the smallest proportion of total daily energy intake. Compared with the non-MASLD group, a markedly greater proportion of total energy intake at dinner was observed in the MASLD group (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD

In Model A, we observed significant associations between MASLD and several factors, including the timing of first food/beverage intake, the intake midpoint, the eating window, the nighttime fasting window, eating frequency, and energy intake at dinner (P < 0.05). In Model B, after adjusting for all covariates, the timing of the first food/beverage intake (continuous: OR: 1.07; 95% CI 1.02–1.14), the intake midpoint (continuous: OR: 1.11; 95% CI 1.00–1.22), and energy intake at dinner (OR: 1.01; 95% CI 1.00–1.01) remained significantly positively correlated with MASLD risk. Notably, compared to food/beverage intake before 8:00, intake between 8:00 and 10:00 was additionally found to be positively corrected with MASLD risk (OR: 1.68; 95% CI 1.05–2.68). Furthermore, the eating window was significantly negatively related to the risk of MASLD (continuous: OR: 0.95; 95% CI 0.91–0.99). Similarly, an eating window of 8–12 h, compared with an eating window of 8 h or less, corresponded to an OR of 0.66 (95% Cl 0.46–0.94), suggesting a negative association with MASLD risk. However, no significant relationships were identified between the nighttime fasting window, eating frequency, and the risk of MASLD (Table 2). As illustrated in Fig. S1, the RCS results showed no significant non-linear association between the chrononutrition patterns and MASLD after adjustment for all covariates.

Associations between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD in various subgroups

A subgroup analysis was conducted to examine whether the associations differed when categorized by gender, BMI, and race. As shown in Fig. 3A, we observed that first food/beverage intake time was significantly associated with an increased MASLD risk among participants identified as male, Mexican American, and non-Hispanic Black (P < 0.05). Additionally, the midpoint time of food/beverage intake was also significantly associated with an increased MASLD risk among participants identified as male and Mexican American (P < 0.05). In Fig. 3B, the eating window was found to be significantly associated with a reduction in the risk of MASLD among participants identified as being Mexican American (P < 0.05). Besides, a significant positive association between nighttime fasting window and MASLD risk was observed among Mexican American participants (P < 0.05). In the BMI subgroups analysis, breakfast energy intake was found to be significantly linked to a lower MASLD risk among individuals classified as normal weight or underweight. However, no significant associations were observed between energy intake at lunch or dinner and the risk of MASLD across different subgroups (Fig.S2).

Mediation effects of HDL-C/NHHR on the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD

As depicted in Table S1, we found chrononutrition patterns were significantly correlated with BMI z-score, HDL-C, and NHHR (all P < 0.05), except for the last food/beverage intake time. Subsequently, we further explored the relationship between metabolic indicators and MASLD, and significant correlations were observed between all metabolic indicators and MASLD (P < 0.001) (Table S2). Finally, we performed a mediation analysis to examine the mediating effect of metabolic indicators on the connection between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD risk. We found that HDL-C and NHHR, respectively, mediated 58.69 and 61.90% of the relationship between the timing of the first food or beverage intake and MASLD (P = 0.030 for HDL-C and P = 0.042 for NHHR) (Fig. 4 and Table S3). Sensitivity analyses showed similar results (Table S4). After redefining MASLD by excluding lipid-related diagnostic criteria, HDL-C and NHHR mediated 57.75 and 61.79% of the association between the timing of the first food or beverage intake and MASLD, respectively (P = 0.032 for HDL-C and P = 0.048 for NHHR).

Discussion

This analysis explored the relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD among 2128 adolescents. The results suggest that later first and midpoint food/beverage intake times, as well as higher energy consumption at dinner, are related to a greater risk of MASLD. In contrast, an extended eating window is related to a decreased risk of MASLD. In addition, subgroup analysis showed that these associations were primarily observed among males and Mexican Americans. Furthermore, mediation analysis demonstrated that the association between the time of first food or beverage intake and MASLD was partially mediated by the HDL-C/NHHR.

In the current study, we found that a later timing of both the first and midpoint of daily food or beverage intake is corrected with a higher risk of MASLD. Similarly, a large-scale prospective cohort study involving 103,389 participants demonstrated that individuals with their first food intake after 9:00 AM showed a markedly greater risk of cardiovascular outcomes, in contrast to those who consumed their first meal before 8:00 AM [38]. In adolescents, an ecological momentary assessment study also reported that late breakfast timing (later than 9:00 AM compared with earlier than 8:00 AM) was associated with a higher cardiometabolic risk [39]. Moreover, it may disturb the body’s circadian rhythms, resulting in disrupted glucose metabolism, reduced insulin sensitivity, and disrupted lipid regulation, which can increase the likelihood of developing metabolic disorders [40]. As for the midpoint of intake, although few studies have specifically concentrated on the influence of lunch timing on MASLD, research has demonstrated a relationship between the midpoint of intake and body weight control. Early eaters—those consuming lunch before 3:00 PM—show greater reductions in body weight [41]. Considering the established relationship between obesity and MASLD, these results propose that the timing of intake could be an important factor in the disease’s progression [42]. However, further investigation is necessary to establish the optimal timing of the first and midpoint food or beverage intake in individuals with MASLD.

Dinner accounted for the largest share of total energy intake in our study, and a higher amount of energy consumed at dinner was related to an elevated risk of MASLD. This association may be influenced by cultural factors, as traditional Western dietary patterns typically designate dinner as the principal meal of the day, highlighting its social significance through family gatherings and communal dining practices [43]. Previous studies have also confirmed that higher energy intake during dinner, as opposed to breakfast, is more strongly linked to the development of insulin resistance [44] and an elevated risk of metabolic syndrome [45] and obesity [46]. Notably, a longitudinal study involving 1961 children reported that a relatively higher energy intake at dinner was associated with increased odds of becoming overweight or obese later in childhood [47]. Therefore, reducing energy intake at dinner may confer potential health benefits, particularly in relation to metabolic disease.

Apart from assessing the timing of food or beverage intake throughout the day, chrononutrition highlights the importance of the duration of eating. Our findings suggest a negative correlation between MASLD and the eating window. Similarly, evidence from the ALSPAC cohort showed that a longer eating window in adolescents was associated with lower BMI z-score, waist circumference, and fat mass, which are closely associated with MASLD risk [48]. However, previous research has shown that restricting the daily eating window to 8 h or less is related to a significantly reduced risk of MASLD compared to eating windows of 10 h or more [49]. The possible explanations for this inconsistency include the following: First, previous studies have typically restricted the 8-h eating window to a fixed time period, for example, between 8:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. [50], or have combined time-restricted eating with other dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet [51]. Second, the timing of the first and last dietary intake—including both food and beverage consumption—may not accurately reflect actual mealtimes. Additionally, the midpoint of our food and beverage intake times further supports this observation, indicating an average time of 14:57, which is notably later than the typical lunch period. Taken together, further large-scale, well-designed studies are warranted to determine the most effective eating window for the management of MASLD.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore the potential heterogeneity in chrononutrition patterns across different population strata. With the gender-specific subgroup, a significant relationship was detected between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD among male participants. The sex differences may be shaped by biological determinants, including genetic and hormonal influences, as well as surrounding and lifestyle factors [52]. The hepatoprotective effects of estrogen, mediated through its ERα (estrogen receptor alpha), have been consistently demonstrated in prior studies [53]. Therefore, males may exhibit a higher susceptibility to MASLD compared to females. Among all racial groups, only Mexican Americans exhibited a relationship between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD. This disparity is epidemiologically consistent with the prevalence of diseases like obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions [54–56]. This racial disparity may be attributable to differences in dietary habits as well as socioeconomic and cultural factors, underscoring the need for further investigation in future research.

Mediation analysis revealed that HDL-C and NHHR partially accounted for the correlation between the first food or beverage intake time and MASLD, suggesting a possible underlying mechanism. These findings were further confirmed in sensitivity analyses. While definitive evidence directly linking HDL-C or NHHR to MASLD and chrononutrition patterns remains limited, numerous studies have investigated the complex relationships between various cholesterol subtypes and the pathogenesis of MASLD. Findings from recent research suggest that both traditional lipid parameters (TC, HDL-C, LDL-C) and emerging lipid parameters [non-HDL-C, remnant cholesterol (RC), TC to HDL-C ratio, LDL-C to HDL-C ratio, and RC to HDL-C ratio] play significant roles in the development of MASLD [57]. Moreover, some studies have shown that chrononutrition patterns can alter lipid profile levels [28, 29]. Therefore, we propose that the timing of the first food or beverage intake may influence the development of MASLD by modulating blood lipid profiles.

The shortcomings of the present study are detailed as follows. First, due to the observational nature of the NHANES study, a cause-and-effect link between chrononutrition patterns and MASLD cannot be established. Second, since there is no standardized definition for chrononutrition patterns, the criteria used in this study are based on existing literature and common dietary habits. Third, detailed physical activity data, including exercise timing and intensity, were unavailable; therefore, residual confounding related to physical activity could not be fully excluded. Additionally, the absence of consecutive days of 24-h dietary data collection meant that fasting duration had to be estimated based on the last and first eating times. This method made it impossible to precisely determine the true fasting period from one night to the next morning, likely leading to measurement error. Therefore, future research should focus on developing a standardized definition of chrononutrition patterns.

Conclusion

The findings of this national cross-sectional study indicate that later timing of the first and midpoint food and beverage intake, as well as higher energy consumption at dinner, are linked to an elevated risk of MASLD, with HDL-C and NHHR serving as mediators—particularly in relation to the timing of the first food/beverage intake. However, the eating window exhibits a negative correlation with MASLD, which warrants further investigation. In the future, longitudinal studies may be employed to explore causal relationships, while animal research could offer insights into the underlying mechanisms. These efforts will ultimately facilitate the development of optimized dietary recommendations and targeted health interventions.
