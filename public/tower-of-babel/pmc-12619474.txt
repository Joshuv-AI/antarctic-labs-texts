Thoracoscopic management of recurrent tracheoesophageal fistula: a single-center outcome analysis
Authors: Cuizhu Feng, Long Li, Xiang Li, Yanxia Zhang, Haibin Yu, Mao Ye, Zhen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough thoracoscopic treatment of congenital esophageal atresia is widely accepted among pediatric surgeons, its application in recurrent tracheoesophageal fistula (RTEF) remains rare because of its technical difficulty. This study retrospectively analyzed thoracoscopic RTEF repairs at a single center to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and outcomes of this approach, providing a clinical reference.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 22 patients with RTEF who underwent thoracoscopic repair at the Capital Center for Children's Health between October 2020 and July 2024. All patients had a history of type III esophageal atresia repair, including 13 cases of type IIIa and 9 cases of type IIIb (Gross classification). The cohort included 8 male and 14 female patients, with an admission age of 2 to 30 months (mean: 8.0 ± 7.2) and weight of 2.3 to 11.0 kg (mean: 5.5 ± 2.4). There were 16 first recurrences, 5 s recurrences, and 1 fourth recurrence. Five patients had failed previous endoscopic fistula ligation, and 9 had a history of open thoracotomy. All 22 underwent thoracoscopic repair.ResultsThoracoscopic RTEF repair was successfully completed in all 22 patients (100%). The operation time ranged from 136 to 420 min (mean: 288.5 ± 93.1), hospitalization ranged from 15 to 124 days (median: 24), and postoperative invasive ventilator support lasted 0 to 480 h (median: 20). Esophageal leakage occurred in 6 patients (27.3%), all of whom recovered with 1 to 8 weeks of conservative treatment. Esophageal stenosis developed in 8 patients (36.4%), including 6 pre-existing and 2 new-onset cases; all improved after 1 to 6 sessions of endoscopic balloon dilation. During follow-up (0.5–4 years), only 1 patient (4.5%) experienced re-recurrence; the remaining patients showed good growth and development.ConclusionThoracoscopic treatment of RTEF following esophageal atresia is safe, feasible, and yields satisfactory clinical outcomes.

Background

Recurrent tracheoesophageal fistula (RTEF) is a serious complication following congenital esophageal atresia repair and often requires reoperation. Although endoscopic techniques—such as fistula ligation, drug injection, and covered stent placement—have been reported in the literature [1–4], their use remains limited because of small case numbers, high recurrence rates, and the need for multiple interventions. As such, these methods are still considered exploratory. Surgical repair remains the most definitive treatment [5–8]. Reoperations are typically performed via thoracotomy because of severe thoracic adhesions and unclear anatomical structures, with only a few pediatric surgical centers adopting thoracoscopic techniques for RTEF repair. Since 2020, the Capital Institute of Pediatrics has routinely employed thoracoscopy for RTEF treatment, achieving satisfactory outcomes. This study retrospectively analyzed 22 patients with RTEF treated thoracoscopically at our institute to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and outcomes of this approach, aiming to provide a reference for thoracoscopic management of RTEF.

Materials and methods

Patients

We retrospectively analyzed 22 patients with RTEF who underwent thoracoscopic repair at the Capital Institute of Pediatrics between October 2020 and July 2024. Among them, 13 were classified as type IIIa and 9 as type IIIb (Gross classification). The cohort included 8 male and 14 female patients, with a mean age at admission of 8.0 ± 7.2 months (range: 2–30 months) and a mean weight of 5.5 ± 2.4 kg (range: 2.3–11.0 kg). Recurrence details included 16 first recurrences, 5 s recurrences, and 1 fourth recurrence. Five patients had previously failed endoscopic fistula ligation, and 9 had a history of open thoracotomy. Among the children included, two were from our center; the remaining were referred from other institutions. This study did not include cases of missed proximal tracheoesophageal fistula. All 22 patients underwent thoracoscopic tracheoesophageal fistula repair.

Diagnostic methods

Diagnostic methods included gastroenterography (using a water-soluble contrast medium), bronchoscopy, gastroscopy, and chest computed tomography (CT). These preoperative examinations were used to comprehensively evaluate the esophagus, trachea, and lungs to aid surgical planning. In this study, all 22 patients underwent gastroenterography; 19 underwent bronchoscopy, 18 underwent gastroscopy, and 19 underwent chest CT.

Surgical methods

The patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position with the right arm elevated. A 5-mm trocar was inserted at the subscapular angle as the thoracoscopic port. Two 3-mm trocars were placed at the third and sixth intercostal spaces along the mid-axillary line as the main operating ports, and a 5-mm trocar was inserted at the seventh intercostal space near the spine as an assistant port. After successful trocar placement, pleural adhesions were dissected using an electric hook to expose the posterior mediastinum. Esophageal dissection began from the distal end, where adhesions were less severe, and continued through areas of dense adhesion. The esophagus was mobilized from its bed to facilitate dissection of the fistula. The most severe adhesions were typically located near the anastomosis, where the fistula was often found. The proximal esophagus and tracheal wall were dissected cranially from the fistula. Once identified, the fistula was divided, and the defects on both the tracheal and esophageal sides were closed separately with polydioxanone sutures. After the repair, surrounding tissue was interposed between the trachea and esophagus to prevent recurrence. Following confirmation of hemostasis in the thoracic cavity and the absence of lung injury, a chest drain was placed in the sixth intercostal space along the mid-axillary line, and the small chest wall incisions were sutured to complete the operation.

Results

All 22 patients (100%) were diagnosed with RTEF by gastroenterography. Bronchoscopy combined with methylene blue staining confirmed 18 of 19 cases (94.7%), and guidewire placement through the fistula under bronchoscopy was successful in 5 cases. Gastroscopy identified the fistula opening in 10 of 18 cases (55.6%) and confirmed esophageal stenosis in 12 of 18 cases (66.7%), with guidewire placement achieved in 2 cases. Chest CT detected the fistula in 10 of 19 cases (52.6%) and showed varying degrees of pneumonia in 18 of 19 cases (94.7%). When chest CT shows relatively severe pneumonia accompanied by cough, fever, and elevated infection markers, the repair operation can be postponed.

Thoracoscopic RTEF repair was successfully completed in all 22 patients (100%). The operation time ranged from 136 to 420 min (mean: 288.5 ± 93.1), the hospital stay ranged from 15 to 124 days (median: 24), and postoperative invasive ventilator support lasted 0 to 480 h (median: 20). Esophageal leakage occurred in 6 patients (27.3%), all of whom recovered with 1 to 8 weeks of conservative treatment. Esophageal stenosis developed in 8 patients (36.4%), including 6 with pre-existing stenosis and 2 new-onset cases. All were successfully treated with 1 to 6 sessions of endoscopic balloon dilation. During follow-up (0.5–4 years), only 1 patient (4.5%) experienced re-recurrence. The remaining patients demonstrated good growth and development.

Discussion

RTEF is a serious complication following congenital esophageal atresia repair. Preoperative diagnostic methods—including esophagography, bronchoscopy, gastroscopy, and chest CT—each offer distinct advantages for the comprehensive evaluation of RTEF in children.

Esophagography is a simple and convenient method with a high diagnostic rate (100% in this study). It allows dynamic observation of esophageal morphology, function, and reflux while also identifying the size, location, and shape of the tracheoesophageal fistula. Water-soluble contrast agents are preferred to avoid the risk of permanent lung residue associated with barium-based agents.

Bronchoscopy is a sensitive diagnostic tool for RTEF [9]. When combined with methylene blue testing, it can precisely confirm the site of recurrence. Methylene blue is injected into the esophagus via a gastric tube, and the appearance of blue dye in the trachea indicates a fistula (Fig. 1). In this study, 16 of 19 cases (84.2%) showed methylene blue leakage from the original fistula opening, and 2 cases (10.5%) from the apical segment of the right upper lobe. Following diagnosis, a guidewire can be placed through the fistula under bronchoscopy to assist intraoperative localization, which may help reduce the operative time [10]. During bronchoscopy, it is crucial to identify any proximal tracheoesophageal fistula missed during the initial surgery (Fig. 2). In such cases, thoracotomy is unnecessary because a cervical approach is more advantageous for repair. The present study excluded patients with this condition.

Gastroscopy allows direct visualization of the anastomosis and fistula: 12 of 18 cases (66.7%) showed stenosis, and 10 of 18 cases (55.6%) revealed the fistula opening. When guidewire placement under bronchoscopy is not feasible, it can be performed under gastroscopy, as was done in 2 cases in this study. Typically, bronchoscopy, gastroscopy, and thoracoscopy were carried out sequentially under a single anesthetic.

Chest CT had a diagnostic rate of 52.6% for RTEF in this study. While not highly sensitive for detecting RTEF, it is valuable for assessing lung conditions: 18 of 19 cases (94.7%) showed varying degrees of pneumonia. This information helps surgeons evaluate surgical risk and determine optimal timing for the procedure.

Notably, all 22 children successfully underwent thoracoscopic tracheoesophageal fistula repair without the need to convert to thoracotomy. In patients with RTEF, thoracic adhesions are often severe because of prior thoracic surgeries. Based on our experience, we highlight the following key considerations for thoracoscopic surgery:

Trocar placement: Safe thoracoscopic surgery begins with careful trocar placement. Given the severity of pleural adhesions, trocars must be inserted cautiously to avoid lung injury. The first trocar is typically placed at the seventh intercostal space near the spinal side, which is less affected by previous surgeries and has lighter adhesions. After incising the skin and subcutaneous tissue, vascular forceps are used to gently dissect into the thoracic cavity. A 5-mm trocar without a core is then inserted, followed by thoracoscope placement for initial exploration. Pleural adhesions are bluntly dissected under direct vision; once adequate space is created, the second trocar is inserted, and further dissection continues with an electric hook. For RTEF, the re-recurrence rate is a key outcome indicator because re-recurrent cases may require additional surgery. The causes of re-recurrence are multifactorial, involving surgical technique, infection, tissue quality, underlying disease, mechanical stress, postoperative management, and anatomical factors [11–13]. Reported re-recurrence rates in the literature range from 7.0% to 10.8% [14, 15], compared with 4.5% in this study. To help reduce re-recurrence, our center emphasizes the following:

Fistula localization: The location of the esophageal fistula varies (proximal, distal, anastomotic, anterior, or lateral wall), while the tracheal site tends to be relatively fixed, typically at the original fistula. Preoperative guidewire placement facilitates precise intraoperative localization (Fig. 4).

Esophageal anastomotic stenosis and leakage are common complications after RTEF repair. The anastomotic site is composed of scar tissue with limited healing capacity, and lateral esophageal fistulas near the anastomosis increase the risk of leakage. In this study, esophageal leakage occurred in 27.3% of cases, all of which resolved with conservative treatment involving adequate drainage and enhanced anti-infection therapy. Esophageal stenosis occurred in 36.4% of cases, most of which were pre-existing. All were successfully managed with endoscopic balloon dilation.

Compared with reports in the literature, thoracoscopic operation time for RTEF tends to be longer, primarily because of the need for careful dissection of dense thoracic adhesions. However, with continued advancement in surgical techniques, operation times are expected to decrease.
