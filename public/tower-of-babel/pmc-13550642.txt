Pseudomonas aeruginosa-derived volatile organic compounds modulate AhR signaling to dysregulate airway mucus homeostasis
Authors: Shanny Hsuan Kuo, Shi Qian Lew, Sook Yin Chong, Cong Wu, Allison B. Williams, Beata Kosmider, Scott H. Randell, Laurence G. Rahme, Gee W. Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudomonas aeruginosa frequently infects chronically diseased lungs and exacerbates airway mucus hypersecretion and failure in clearance. P. aeruginosa is metabolically versatile and secretes multiple volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which have been scrutinized as potential biomarkers for diagnosing acute exacerbation in diseased lungs. However, the pathogenic roles of VOCs in mucus dysregulation are unknown. By using air-liquid interface cultures of normal and diseased human bronchial epithelial cells and a mouse model of chronic VOCs exposure, we report that dominant species of P. aeruginosa VOCs in concentrations found in both the breath of cystic fibrosis patients and in the headspace of bacterial cultures significantly elevated the expression of the mucin MUC5AC. VOCs activate the AhR signaling to inhibit FOXA2, a key regulator of airway mucus homeostasis. AhR inhibitor restored FOXA2 expression to attenuate excessive mucin expression. Mechanistic studies revealed that P. aeruginosa VOCs activate the AhR-ARNT-CYP1-ROS signaling cascade, which in turn induces the EGFR-AKT/ERK signaling pathways to disrupt airway mucus homeostasis. Taken together, these findings highlight the critical role of P. aeruginosa VOCs in disrupting airway mucus homeostasis and implicate the pathogenic role of AhR signaling in chronic lung diseases.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonizes and infects the airways of chronically diseased lungs, including cystic fibrosis (CF), advanced stages of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchiectasis, and chronic bronchitis (CB), as well as ventilation-associated pneumonia (VAP)1. These diseased lungs exhibit several distinct pathological features, including goblet cell metaplasia (GCM), excessive mucus production and impaired clearance of both mucus and pathogens, reduced gas exchange, and deterioration of lung function, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality.

Microbial-derived volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and semi-VOCs (SVOCs) detected in the exhaled breath of patients with chronic lung diseases have been investigated as non-invasive biomarkers to support clinical diagnosis. At 25 °C, VOCs have saturation vapor pressures greater than 10−2 kPa, while SVOCs have saturation vapor pressures between 10−2 and 10−8 kPa, making them more likely to exist as liquids or solids at lower temperatures2. Researchers have extensively examined breath, sputum, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from CF patients and identified major P. aeruginosa VOCs and SVOCs species, including hydrogen cyanide (HCN)3,4, 2- aminoacetophenone (2-AA, a SVOC)5, 2-nonanone (2NA)6, 1-undecene (1UH)7, and methyl thiocyanate8. The presence of these VOCs has been associated with P. aeruginosa infection in CF, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis3,6,9–12.

Bacterial VOCs and SVOCs play distinct roles in virulence, biofilm formation, stress resistance, and microbial interactions within soils and mammalian guts13. VOCs biosynthesis is significantly upregulated by the cell-to-cell communication process called quorum sensing (QS)14. One notable QS-regulated SVOC is 2-AA, which gives P. aeruginosa cultures a distinctive “grape-like” fruity odor. 2-AA promotes chronic infection15,16 and enhances host tolerance towards P. aeruginosa by positively modulating histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1) activity, leading to alterations in histone acetylation of cytokines and epigenetic reprogramming of immune cells17. HCN, a potent inhibitor of cellular respiration, has been detected in the sputum of patients with CF and non-CF bronchiectasis infected with P. aeruginosa, where it is associated with impaired lung function4,18. Previously, we have shown that pyocyanin, a P. aeruginosa redox-active secondary metabolite, could robustly trigger mucus hypersecretion by activating STAT6 and EGFR signaling pathways that jointly inhibit the expression of FOXA2, a major transcriptional regulator of airway mucus homeostasis19–24. However, the biological impacts of P. aeruginosa VOCs and SVOCs on diseased lungs are poorly characterized.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor responsive to a wide range of structurally diverse compounds derived from the environment, microbiota, and host metabolism25–28. These AhR ligands include environmental pollutants such as dioxins, benzo(a)pyrene, and polycyclic aromatic compounds, as well as microbial metabolites, natural plant flavonoids, polyphenolics, indoles, and arachidonic acid. Upon binding to these ligands, AhR translocates to the nucleus, where it dimerizes with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) to transactivate genes encoding several detoxifying enzymes, including cytochromes P450 1A1 (CYP1A1) and P450 1B1 (CYP1B1). AhR exerts its effects by modulating gene expression, cell-cell adhesion, and the expression of Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg cells, which can have either protective or deleterious effects on the course of the disease29,30. Dioxins and benzo(a)pyrene promote MUC5AC expression through an AhR-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathway31, and SARS-CoV-2-induced interferons activate AhR to enhance mucin biosynthesis32. Conversely, epithelial AhR suppresses MUC5AC overexpression in allergic asthma by inhibiting ROS-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation33. Collectively, these observations reveal that AhR is an important regulator of airway mucus homeostasis during pulmonary inflammation.

We hypothesized that P. aeruginosa-derived VOCs and SVOCs might promote GCM by activating AhR in the diseased airways. We used air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures of normal and diseased human bronchial epithelial cells and a mouse model of chronic VOCs/SVOCs exposure to examine whether selective individual or cocktail VOCs/SVOCs exposure could disrupt airway mucus homeostasis. Dominant VOCs and SVOCs found in the CF patients, including 2-AA, 1-undecene, and 2-nonanone5,6,11,34, as well as 1-dodecene and 1-undecene detectable in the exhaled breath of a subset of cigarette smokers, hence potential biomarkers for P. aeruginosa infection in COPD34, were included. For simplicity, the term “VOCs” is used in the remainder of this presentation.

Results

P. aeruginosa VOCs inhibit FOXA2 expression and induce mucus hypersecretion in mouse airways

The concentrations of VOCs observed in nutrient-rich in vitro bacterial cultures are typically higher than those detected in the breath of human patients. However, the levels of VOCs in the sputum microenvironments of diseased lungs may be much higher, and when vaporized, may impact the larger area of airways. To ensure clinical relevance, we first compared the concentration of 2-AA found in the breath of CF patients [2-AA(H)]5 with that in Brain Heart Infusion broth [2-AA(B)]35 for their ability to disrupt mucus homeostasis in mouse airways. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed a moderate to strong mucin overexpression in mouse airways challenged with either concentration of 2-AA (Fig. 1a). The degree of mucin expression was quantified using a 4-tier grading system described by Grundström et al.36 (Fig. 1b). While the vehicle control group showed a goblet cell score of 0.3 ± 0.2, the goblet cell scores of both 2-AA treated groups were significantly higher, averaging 2.3 ± 0.6 (2-AA(H), p < 0.0001) and 2.8 ± 0.3 (2-AA(B), p < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 1c). The increased mucin expression was authenticated by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining where both MUC5AC and MUC5B, the two major glycoproteins of the airway mucus, were highly induced in the 2-AA-exposed mouse airways (Fig. 1a, d). A significant reduction in the nuclear FOXA2 expression in both 2-AA(H) (64%) and 2-AA(B) (82%) groups was observed when compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 1a, e). Additionally, secreted MUC5AC was also increased in murine BALF samples (Supplementary Fig. 1a). In line with these findings, both 2-AA(H) and 2-AA(B) also increased the expression of MUC5AC in the submerged SV40-transformed human bronchial epithelial 16HBE14o- cells (Fig. 1f, g). Because P. aeruginosa secretes multiple VOCs simultaneously in the diseased airways6,11,37, a VOCs cocktail of 2-AA(B), 1-undecene, 1-dodecene, and 2-nonanone would better mimic the more complex milieus in P. aeruginosa-infected lungs. We observed that in addition to 2-AA, all the other VOCs, individually or in a cocktail, significantly increased the number of goblet cells and the level of MUC5AC production (Fig. 2a–c), with a goblet cell score ranging from 2.7 to 3.0 compared to 0.3 in the vehicle control (Fig. 2a, b). We observed a marked depletion of nuclear FOXA2, ranging from 65% for 2-nonanone to 90% for 2-AA(B) (Fig. 2a, d). However, the additive or synergistic effect of the cocktail VOCs on FOXA2 repression and mucus hypersecretion was not apparent (Fig. 2a–d), most likely due to the high concentration of 2-AA(B) within the cocktail. Similar increases of MUC5AC were observed in murine BALF samples (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The 2-AA-deficient ΔpqsE mutant exhibits attenuated induction of mucus hypersecretion

The biosynthesis pathway of 2-AA has not been fully elucidated. The pqsE gene of P. aeruginosa functions as a pathway-specific thioesterase in the biosynthesis of alkylquinolone signal molecules in the PA alkylquinolone biosynthetic pathway, including HHQ (2-heptyl-4(1H)-quinolone), DHQ (2,4-dihydroxyquinoline), and 2-AA38. We compared the ΔpqsE mutant to its parental wild-type strain P. aeruginosa PA14 to validate the impact of 2-AA on airway mucus homeostasis. While P. aeruginosa PA14 infection induced robust mucin production in the mouse airways (goblet cell score 3.0 ± 0), there was a notable reduction in mucin production in the group challenged with ΔpqsE (goblet cell score 0.9 ± 0.2, p < 0.0001), which was similar to the vehicle control group (goblet cell score of 0.4 ± 0.2) (Fig. 3a–c). Previously, we had shown that a 40–50% reduction in FOXA2 expression would cause a significant increase in the MUC5AC and MUC5B expression21–24,39. The level of nuclear FOXA2 expression in the ΔpqsE-infected airways was similar to that of the control group; in contrast, the P. aeruginosa PA14-infected airways showed a 64% reduction in FOXA2 expression (Fig. 3a, d). The number of viable P. aeruginosa PA14 or ΔpqsE bacterial inoculum used to challenge mouse lungs was similar (Fig. 3e). Blockade of AhR restores FOXA2 expression and attenuates mucus hypersecretion in mouse lungs exposed to P. aeruginosa VOCs

Next, we investigated whether AhR regulates mucus homeostasis in mouse airways exposed to P. aeruginosa VOCs. We inhibited the function of AhR by intraperitoneally injecting the AhR-specific inhibitor, CH-223191. While the VOCs cocktail induced a significantly higher mucin expression than the vehicle control (goblet cell score 2.9 ± 0.1 versus 0.3 ± 0.2 respectively) (Fig. 4a–c), CH-223191 significantly attenuated GCM and MUC5AC and MUC5B expression (goblet cell score 1.2 ± 0.2, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4a–c), and restored the nuclear FOXA2 expression in airway epithelial cells to 84% of that in the vehicle control group (Fig. 4a, d). Similar attenuation in secreted MUC5AC was observed in murine BALF samples (Fig. 4e, f). P. aeruginosa VOCs induce AhR-regulated transdifferentiation of multi-potent secretory club cells into goblet cells

The airways are lined by a pseudostratified epithelium comprised of goblet cells, secretory club cells, ciliated cells, neuroendocrine cells, and basal cells40. The pristine airway epithelium contains only a few scattered goblet cells. However, in chronic lung diseases, increased numbers of goblet cells often arise as a result of proinflammatory stimuli. We hypothesized that P. aeruginosa VOCs increase mucin production through inflammation-induced transdifferentiation of resting multipotent epithelial cells, specifically Scgb1a1+ secretory club cells, into mucin-secreting goblet cells. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that chronic exposure of mouse airways to the VOCs cocktail induced the transdifferentiation of club cells into goblet cells (76.25%, CC10+, MUC5B+) and, to a lesser extent, of non-club cells into goblet cells (23.75%) (Fig. 5a–c). These findings aligned with a previous report indicating that club cells are the primary source of GCM following exposure to allergens41. Corroborating the biological impact of these VOCs, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining revealed mild to moderate inflammation within the airways characterized by peri-bronchiolar and intra-luminal inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of neutrophils and macrophages, with fewer lymphocytes and plasma cells, and occurred concurrently with GCM (Supplementary Fig. 2). Because AhR was previously reported to be essential in maintaining lung club cell homeostasis after exposure to inflammatory stimuli as well as epithelial damage42, we investigated the role of AhR in the VOCs-mediated goblet cell transdifferentiation process. AhR inhibitor restores nuclear localization and expression of FOXA2 in human bronchial epithelial cells

As a transcription factor, the active form of FOXA2 resides within the nuclei. Upon phosphorylation at the threonine residue T156, FOXA2 translocates to the cytoplasm, where it is degraded21,43–45. In 16HBE14o- cells exposed to the vehicle, FOXA2 was strongly expressed and predominantly localized within the nuclei. In contrast, in 16HBE14o- cells exposed to the P. aeruginosa VOCs cocktail, FOXA2 was distributed throughout the cytoplasm with a much-reduced fluorescence signal, suggesting its degradation (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, AhR inhibitor CH-223191 prevented degradation and restored the nuclear localization and expression of FOXA2. These findings indicate that AhR modulates the stability of FOXA2 upon VOCs challenge.

AhR-CYP1A1/CYP1B1-mitochondrial ROS signaling acts upstream of the EGFR-AKT/ERK axis during VOC-induced mucus hypersecretion

Building on the aforementioned findings, we delved deeper into the role of AhR and its downstream effectors CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 in shaping the airway pathophysiology in response to P. aeruginosa VOCs. Notably, treatment with the CYP1 inhibitor Alizarin47 decreased MUC5AC expression during exposure to VOCs while restoring FOXA2 expression (Fig. 8b). Alizarin also reversed the VOCs-induced generation of whole cell ROS (Supplementary Fig. 6A) and mitochondrial ROS (Supplementary Fig. 6b) and attenuated the activation of p-EGFR, p-AKT, and p-ERK (Fig. 8b). To further support the mechanistic pathway, we performed small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated genomic silencing of AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 genes, and examined the downstream EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 signaling cascades. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, knockdown of AHR, CYP1A1, or CYP1B1 genes attenuates P. aeruginosa VOC-induced EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 signaling and MUC5AC levels, and restores FOXA2 expression. These findings suggest that P. aeruginosa VOCs induce the expression of CYP1s, which in turn upregulate mitochondrial ROS levels to activate the EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 signaling cascades, forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates mucin overexpression by inhibiting FOXA2. Additionally, the AhR-ARNT-CYP cascade acts upstream of the EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 pathways.

Discussion

AhR binds aromatic compounds and dietary metabolites to induce molecular cascades that activate cytochrome P450s and the release of cytokines that modulate innate and adaptive immune responses51–53. The lung epithelium is especially sensitive to multiple AhR ligands49,54,55, including cigarette smoke extracts, 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD), or benzo(a)pyrene, which modify the expression of IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, matrix metalloproteases, retinoic acid signaling, and notably, increased ROS generation and MUC5AC expression49,56–59. Furthermore, these aforementioned AhR agonists upregulate the signaling of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, namely the Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK pathway, along with other signaling cascades56,60,61. These events collectively induce goblet cell differentiation, leading to elevated mucus biosynthesis and secretion in airways62. In line with these reports, P. aeruginosa VOCs induced a significant increase in AhR-dependent mucin production in mouse airways and ALI cultures of human bronchial epithelial cells.

Beyond mediating abnormal mucus production, pre-clinical studies have suggested that the activation of endogenous AhR is essential for safeguarding neonatal and adult lungs from injuries induced by hyperoxia and cigarette smoke32. Upon activation by environmental pollutants (e.g., benzo(a)pyrene), AhR initiates the transcription of CYP1A1 and CYP1B163, which are hemoprotein monooxygenases crucial for metabolizing xenobiotics, resulting in the generation of diverse metabolites that lead to ROS and MUC5AC production and cytotoxicity31,64. Our data showed that P. aeruginosa VOCs activate the AhR-ARNT-CYP1A1/CYP1B1 signaling, leading to increased ROS that activates EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 signaling pathways, which we have previously shown to inhibit the expression of FOXA221,22,24,46. Inhibition of CYP1s by Alizarin and siRNA effectively reversed the P. aeruginosa VOCs-induced accumulation of mitochondrial ROS and reduced the levels of p-EGFR, p-AKT, and p-ERK. Thus, we have established a mechanistic link by which P. aeruginosa VOCs trigger the AhR-ARNT-CYP1-mitochondria ROS signaling, which in turn activates the EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 pathways, forming a positive regulatory circuit that inhibits FOXA2 and exacerbates mucin overexpression. It could be argued that activation of AhR-ARNT-CYP1s may be an attempt by airway epithelial cells to metabolize and detoxify P. aeruginosa VOCs. Inadvertently, however, this exacerbates mucus pathology.

In line with a prior report that highlighted secretory club cells as the predominant source of GCM in response to allergen exposure41, our study revealed that chronic exposure to the VOCs resulted in 76% goblet cells that were transdifferentiated from club cells. The origin of the remaining non-club cell-derived goblet cells (24%) is not known yet. While lineage tracing studies have strongly suggested that they do not arise from the ciliated cells65, recent evidence has suggested that goblet cells may act as transitional intermediates co-expressing goblet and multiciliated cell markers during epithelial remodeling66. Our results further support club cells as the main cellular source of GCM while also emphasizing the cellular plasticity of the airway epithelium following chronic injury. Club cells are critically involved in xenobiotic metabolism, host defense, and maintenance of airway integrity and repair67,68, through the secretion of CC10 protein and CYP enzymes regulated by AhR42. Importantly, we found the AhR inhibitor CH-223191 strongly blocks the VOCs-induced GCM transdifferentiation process.

Recently, a variety of microbial molecules and virulence factors, including lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria, phenazines from P. aeruginosa, and the naphthoquinone phthiocol from Mycobacterium tuberculosis69,70, have been shown to activate AhR, which dampens inflammatory responses to primary LPS challenges, facilitates the degradation of virulence factors, and modulates cytokine and chemokine production69,70. Also, microbial volatile tryptophan metabolites, including indole71, N-formylkynurenine72, 2-oxindole, indole-3-acetic acid, and kynurenic acid73 are among the most abundant AhR activators present in mouse cecal contents and human stools that play important roles in maintaining host-microbe homeostasis in the gastrointestinal tract. However, detailed mechanistic studies underlying the activation of AhR remain limited. Our findings reveal a previously unexplored mechanistic link between AhR-CYP1A1/CYP1B1-mitochondrial ROS signaling cascade and EGFR-AKT/ERK axis in response to P. aeruginosa VOCs, leading to inhibition of FOXA2 expression and mucus dysregulation.

There is some confusion in the literature about the origin of 2-AA detected in human patients. P. aeruginosa uses alkylquinolones, in particular, PQS (2-heptyl-3-hydroxy-4(1H)-quinolone) and HHQ (2-heptyl-4(1H)-quinolone), as QS signaling molecules to synchronize the expression of virulence genes. Drees and Fetzner showed that 2-AA is formed during HHQ production38. Analysis of 52 P. aeruginosa isolates from clinical blood and sputum specimens indicates that 51 P. aeruginosa clinical isolates were capable of synthesizing 2-AA, with the single atypical isolate defective in QS74. These studies provide compelling evidence that 2-AA is an endogenous product produced by P. aeruginosa during infection.

Food, beverages, cosmetics, or medications may serve as exogenous sources of 2-AA contaminating patient breath samples, leading to a wrong diagnosis. A previous study detected low levels of 2-AA in a small proportion of healthy subjects5. To assess the potential confounding effects of these products on 2-AA breath analysis, Scott-Thomas et al.75 screened and found no detectable levels of 2-AA in cosmetics, an extensive list of common CF medications, and both alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, except for corn chips, canned tuna, egg white, and one of the three beers. A food challenge study involving ten healthy subjects revealed that by 2 hours post-consumption, 2-AA levels had returned to baseline for all subjects75. The researchers concluded that if 2-AA-rich foods are consumed, obtaining a breath sample 2 hours post-consumption will be unlikely to yield a false positive for the 2-AA breath biomarker.

A limitation of our study is that the production of P. aeruginosa volatilome was shown to be influenced by bacterial phenotypes such as strain mucoidy76, growth conditions, including biofilms versus planktonic states77, strain differences78, infection phases37, and host-associated factors such as dietary habits75, tobacco smoking34, and immune system status79. These variables, coupled with the different sample collection and analytical methods used for VOCs detection80,81, contribute to discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo findings of P. aeruginosa volatilome. The literature thus puts much emphasis on the characteristic “pattern” of VOCs, rather than a single compound, that indicates the presence of P. aeruginosa in clinical specimens9. In the present study, we focused on a specific panel of VOCs, namely 2-AA, 2-nonanone, and 1-undecene. These molecules have been consistently identified in vitro, including 2-AA10,35,78,82–85, 1-undecene10,34,84–86, 2-nonanone34,37,84,86,87, as well as in breath (2-AA)5, sputum (1-undecene and 2-nonanone)6,11, and sinus mucus (2-AA)10 from CF patients colonized with P. aeruginosa. Identification of such overlapping biomarkers among the corroborating reports, especially in human biological samples, gives confidence that these VOCs may be P. aeruginosa-specific. This makes them relevant indicators of the clinical disease status in patients infected by P. aeruginosa87, and hence, emphasizes the need for investigation into their role in mucus dysregulation and disease progression.

Another limitation of this study is the scarce direct in vivo measurements of absolute concentrations of P. aeruginosa VOCs within the human airway. Except for 2-AA(H)5, the VOC concentrations used in this study were based on levels measured in the headspace of bacterial cultures34,35. Most volatilome studies rely on analytical platforms such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) coupled with solid-phase microextraction (SPME), selected ion flow tube mass spectrometry (SIFT-MS), or proton transfer reaction mass spectrometry (PTR-MS)80,81. Although VOC analysis of respiratory specimens has been proposed as a minimally invasive approach for diagnosing and monitoring P. aeruginosa infection in CF patients5,6,10–12,88–91, quantitative assessment is infrequently performed, largely reflecting the lack of appropriate internal standards required for determining absolute compound concentrations. 2-AA has been detected in the breath of CF patients at 0.18 nM5. However, exhaled breath represents diluted end-expiratory gas rather than the actual microenvironment of the airway surface liquid and therefore likely underestimates local in situ concentrations. In addition, VOC measurements are susceptible to evaporation and loss during sample collection, transport, and processing prior to analysis. Evidence from HCN illustrates this discrepancy: sputum concentrations of HCN (130.03 μM to 400.07 μM) have been reported to be approximately 2.45 × 10⁵ to 7.6 × 10⁵ times higher than those measured in breath (0.529 nM)3,89,92. This compartmental gradient is comparable to the ~6-log difference observed in our study between breath-derived 2-AA [2-AA (H); 0.18 nM] and concentrations detected in bacterial culture headspace [2-AA (B); 192.4 μM]. This established precedent supports the physiological plausibility of the culture-based concentrations used in our experiments. Notably, the impaired mucociliary clearance and impaired airflow characteristic of diseased airways may trap and further concentrate VOCs within retained mucus, potentially amplifying local exposure. More importantly, we demonstrate that even at the low concentration detected in human breath [2-AA (H); 0.18 nM], 2-AA induces MUC5AC overexpression (Figs. 1, 7a, b; Supplementary Fig. 1a), suggesting that biologically relevant effects of P. aeruginosa VOCs occur across a wide concentration range.

Another factor to be considered is that all published measurements of VOCs were conducted in the gaseous phase. In contrast, in the current study, VOC exposure was conducted using an aqueous 0.1% DMSO-PBS solution. The mucus is comprised of 95–98% water, with the remaining components consisting of mucins and non-mucin proteins, lipids, salts, and cellular debris93. Thus, the instillation of aqueous-phase VOCs represents a valid model that more closely mimics the clinical scenario of a P. aeruginosa infection, where excessive mucus clogs the airways, and VOCs partition into this mucus, impacting the surrounding airway environment.

In summary, mucus is an integral part of lung innate immunity. However, overproduction of mucus and failure in clearance in diseased airways, including CF and non-CF bronchiectasis and COPD, creates a niche for continuous microbial colonization and infections, leading to a vicious cycle of additional obstruction and disease exacerbation94. We demonstrate that dominant species of P. aeruginosa VOCs, either individually or as a cocktail, are capable of inducing mucin overexpression. Furthermore, AhR was highly expressed in the areas of GCM in both mouse airways exposed to P. aeruginosa VOCs and in human CF and COPD airways. AhR and CYP inhibitors, as well as siRNA-mediated genomic silencing of AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 genes, attenuated the activation of EGFR/AKT/ERK1/2 signaling pathways and restored the expression of FOXA2 to inhibit mucin overexpression. These mechanistic studies suggest a hierarchical and epistatic link where PA VOCs-induced AhR-ARNT-CYP1-ROS signal acts upstream of the EGFR-AKT/ERK1/2 axis, leading to disruption of FOXA2-regulated airway mucus homeostasis.

Methods

Ethics statement

The current study complied with all relevant ethical regulations (see below).

Mouse study approvals

Mouse experiments were conducted in strict compliance with the guidelines outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” by the National Institutes of Health. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign under Protocols 20120 and 23132.

Healthy, CF, and COPD human lung tissues

The use of human lung sections was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and Temple University. Healthy and CF-diseased lung tissue sections from deidentified donors were provided by Dr. Scott H. Randell (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Marsico Lung Institute/UNC Cystic Fibrosis Center, IRB Study # 03-1396). Healthy and COPD-diseased lung tissue sections from deidentified donors were provided by Dr. Beata Kosmider (Temple University Katz School of Medicine, IRB protocol # 23201). The diseased lung sections demonstrated confirmed clinical infections with P. aeruginosa, characterized by 3+ mucoid and 3+ smooth forms.

Chemicals, reagents, and antibodies

VOCs, including 2-AA (W390607), 1-Dodecene (D221600), 1-Undecene (242527), and 2-Nonanone (108731), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Compounds were first suspended in DMSO and then serially diluted to desired concentrations in sterile PBS (final DMSO concentration 0.1%) for treatment. Specific inhibitors, including EGFR inhibitor AG1478 (658552, Sigma-Aldrich), STAT6 inhibitor Kaempferol (K0122, Sigma-Aldrich), AhR inhibitor CH-223191 (S7711, Selleck Chemicals), and CYP inhibitor Alizarin (S2526, Selleck Chemicals) were used at indicated concentrations and incubated for 1.5 hours before VOCs exposure. Antibodies utilized in this study include: from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (FOXA2, sc-101060 used in western blotting (WB); MUC5AC, sc-21701 used in WB; MUC5B, sc-20119 used in WB; EGFR, sc-373746 used in WB; phosphorylated EGFR, sc-101668 used in WB; AKT, sc-8312 used in WB; phosphorylated AKT, sc-16646 used in WB; ERK1/2, sc-514302 used in WB; phosphorylated ERK1/2, sc-81492 used in WB; histone H3, sc-10809 used in WB; GAPDH, sc-166656 used in WB; AhR, sc-133088 used in WB; CC10, sc-365992 used in IF/IHC); Invitrogen (ARNT, MA1-515 used in WB; CYP1B1, PA5-106878 used in WB; acetyl-α tubulin, 6-11B-1 used in IF/IHC); Proteintech (CYP1A1, 13241-1-AP used in WB); Cell Signaling Technology (FOXA2 D56D6; used in IF/IHC); Bioss Antibodies (MUC5AC bs-7166R; used in IF/IHC); and Novus Biologicals (MUC5B NBPI-92151; used in IF/IHC). Antibodies were diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin, used at dilutions recommended by manufacturers, and optimized as needed.

Bacterial cultures

The parental wildtype P. aeruginosa strain PA14 and the isogenic 2-AA-deficient mutant ΔpqsE were provided by Professor Laurence G. Rahme (Massachusetts General Hospital). P. aeruginosa strains were cultured from frozen −80 °C stocks in Lysogeny Broth (LB) for 16 hours at 37 °C to late stationary phase (OD 600 nm ≈ 3.0) and washed three times in sterile PBS. The size of the inoculum was verified by serial dilution plating on LB agar for viable bacterial colony-forming units (CFU), and verified in the infected mouse lungs (Fig. 3e).

Mouse models

All experiments were performed with 6–8 week-old, age and sex-matched C57BL6 mice procured from Charles River Laboratories, Inc. All mice were housed in positively-ventilated microisolator cages equipped with automatic recirculating water in a room with laminar, high-efficiency particle accumulation-filtered air, with the room temperature set at 20 °C, humidity at 58%, and at a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Mice were acclimated for one week before any experiments. The animals were provided with autoclaved food, water, and bedding. For P. aeruginosa infection, mice (groups of 8-10) were anesthetized with isoflurane and intranasally infected with either 106 CFU of the wild-type PA14 or the isogenic ΔpqsE mutant on Day 1, 3, 5, and 7 as previously published21,95,96 and verified by CFU counting in homogenized lungs (e.g., Fig. 3e). On Day 8, mouse lungs were harvested and processed for PAS, IHC, and IF staining. For the VOCs challenge, isoflurane-anesthetized mice (groups of 8) received intranasal instillation of specified concentrations of VOCs once daily for 3 weeks as previously published19,23,24. Control mice were exposed to the same volume of vehicle (0.1% DMSO in PBS) for the same duration. P. aeruginosa VOC species utilized include 2-AA, 1-undecene, 1-dodecene, and 2-nonanone. Except for the concentration of 2-AA detected in the breath of CF patients5 [0.18 nM, referred to as 2-AA(H)], the concentrations of the other VOCs were based on the maximum levels observed in the headspace of bacterial cultures34,35: 2-AA [192.4 μM, designated 2-AA(B)], 1-dodecene (56.4 nM, designated 1DH), 1-undecene (2.06 μM, designated 1UH), and 2-nonanone (157.5 nM, designated 2NA). Subsequently, mouse airways were evaluated for the degrees of GCM and mucin production using PAS stain, as well as IHC and/or IF analyses for MUC5AC, MUC5B, and FOXA2. Both the vehicle control (0.1% DMSO-PBS) and AhR inhibitor CH-223191 (AhR INH; 50 μg per mouse in 0.1% DMSO-PBS) were administered to mice on Day 0 and then once daily, 1 hour before exposure to VOCs. To further confirm the expression of MUC5AC in the murine airways, BALF was obtained from VOCs-exposed mice (n = 5). To collect BALF, the trachea was exposed and intubated with a 1.7-mm outer diameter polyethylene catheter, followed by the instillation of PBS in three successive 1-mL aliquots. Proteins from the pooled lavage samples were concentrated using the Amicon Ultra-4 columns (UFC805024) and quantified via BCA assay, and 15 μg of protein was subjected to Western blotting to assess the expression levels of secreted mucins on agarose-acrylamide hybrid gels.

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

Human airway cell cultures

Primary human airway epithelial cells, including NHBE, DHBE-CF, and DHBE-COPD cells, and their growth media were procured from Lonza (CC-2541, 00196979, and 00195275, respectively). Submerged primary cells were cultured using the bronchial epithelial growth medium (BEGM; CC-3170) supplemented with growth factors from the SingleQuots kit (CC-4175) from Lonza. The 16HBE14o- cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, maintained at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Cells at passage 3 were trypsinized, seeded onto 100 mm dishes, and cultured until reaching 70% confluency. Airway cells were exposed to VOCs with or without specific inhibitors and subjected to western blot analysis or fluorescence microscopy. For ALI cultures, 16HBE14o-, NHBE, CF-DHBE, and COPD-DHBE cells at passages 2-3 were trypsinized and seeded onto Costar Transwell inserts with a pore size of 0.4 μm (Corning™3460, Corning) at a density of 5 × 105 cells in BEGM or MEM. Cultures were maintained for 5 days with media added to both apical and basolateral chambers. On Day 6, media were removed, and complete PneumoCult-ALI media (05001, Stemcell) supplemented with PneumoCult-ALI supplement (05003), heparin (07980), and hydrocortisone (07925) were added to the basolateral chamber only to induce epithelial differentiation. In the experiments examining the effects of AhR and CYP inhibitors, cells were exposed to CH223191 (10 μM) or alizarin (10 μM) for 1.5 hours before the addition of an individual VOC or a cocktail of VOCs, comprising 2-AA (B) (192.4 μM), 1-dodecene (56.4 nM), 1-undecene (2.06 μM), and 2-nonanone (157.5 nM) for a 24 hours incubation. For studies involving STAT6 and EGFR inhibitors, 16HBE14o- cells were treated with 10 μM of kaempferol or AG1478 for 1.5 hours prior to the addition of 2-AA(H) for a 24 hours incubation.

siRNA transfections

16HBE14o⁻ cells were seeded at 8 × 10⁵ cells per well in 60-mm culture dishes containing 3 mL of MEM. Cells were incubated for 24 hours to achieve approximately 70-80% confluency. Cells were harvested using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA and transfected with sequence-validated siRNA plasmid constructs designed using the GenScript siRNA design tool. For optimization of transfection conditions, 30 pmol of siRNA per sample was delivered using the SF Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector™ X Kit (V4XC-2012; Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following transfection, cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 48 hours before subsequent experiments. Various siRNA oligonucleotides against AHR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1 genes are listed on Supplementary Table 1.

Measurement of intracellular and mitochondrial ROS

Intracellular ROS levels in 16HBE14o- cells exposed to P. aeruginosa VOCs were determined using the Cellular Reactive Oxygen Species Assay kit (ab186027, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Mitochondrial ROS was assessed using the Mitochondrial ROS Detection Assay Kit (#701600; Cayman Chemical), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

PAS, IF, and IHC staining

Following various indicated treatments, submerged or ALI cultures of 16HBE14o-, NHBE, CF-DHBE, and COPD-DHBE cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, blocked with 5% BSA containing goat serum, and incubated with specific primary antibodies. Samples were visualized with secondary anti-mouse antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor®568 (A11004, 1:500 dilution, Invitrogen) or with secondary anti-rabbit antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor®488 (A11008, 1:500 dilution, Invitrogen) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1023627001, 1:5000 dilution, Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 minutes.

For staining of mouse lungs, after euthanasia, a cannula was inserted into the trachea, and the lung was instilled with 1 mL of 10% neutral-buffered formalin at a constant pressure (25 cm H2O). After tracheal ligation, the inflated lung was immersed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 hours before paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded lungs were sectioned to a 5 μm thickness and stained with PAS reagent (395B, Sigma-Aldrich). For IHC, lung sections were stained with primary antibodies against specific proteins and visualized using the VECTASTAIN ABC Kit (PK-4000). For IF, tissue sections were probed with anti-mouse secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor®568 or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor®488. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. PAS and IHC images were captured using an Olympus DP70 light microscope. IF images were obtained using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope and quantified using ImageJ.

Histopathology staining and grading

Mouse lung sections were H&E-stained for histological assessment. Histological analysis of all slides was conducted in a blinded fashion by an American College of Veterinary Pathologists (ACVP)-board-certified veterinary pathologist. Semi-quantitative scoring of airway inflammation was graded on a scale of 0 to 4: 0, absent; 1, slight; 2, mild; 3, moderate; and 4, severe. Detailed grading criteria are present in Supplementary Fig. 2j. Each data point represents the median of individual animal scores. Statistical significance was determined using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison post hoc test and is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Whole-cell, cytosolic, and nuclear extract preparations

Whole-cell extracts were prepared from 16HBE14o- cells utilizing the M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (78501, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, airway cells were suspended in the M-PER extraction kit, followed by incubation on ice, sonication for 10 seconds, and vortexing every 15 minutes over 1 hour. The lysate was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 minutes, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the whole cell extract. For the fractionation of cytosolic and nuclear proteins, the Nuclear & Cytoplasmic extraction kit (786-182, G-Biosciences) and NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (78835, Thermo Scientific) were employed, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, airway cells were resuspended in SubCell buffer-I and lysis reagent, followed by a 10-minute incubation on ice. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes, the supernatant constituted the cytoplasmic fraction. The resulting pellets were then resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer, subjected to a 1-hour incubation on ice, and sonicated for 10 seconds every 15 minutes. The mixture was again centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 minutes and the supernatant was collected as the nuclear fraction. The protein concentration in each preparation was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23227, Thermo Scientific).

Western blot analyses

Agarose-acrylamide hybrid gels were used to resolve high molecular weight MUC5AC mucins. Regular 10-12% SDS-PAGE gels were used for all other proteins. To formulate agarose-acrylamide hybrid gels, a mixture of 1% agarose in the TBE buffer was combined with 3-6% acrylamide, 150 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.8), and 10% glycerol. The gel mixture was dissolved through repeated heating in a microwave. Before solidifying, SDS, ammonium persulfate, and TEMED were incorporated into the mixture. Gel electrophoresis was conducted using the TBE buffer, and proteins were subsequently transferred onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (88518, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For all other proteins separated on SDS-PAGE gels, the transfer was performed onto a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membrane (88018, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk dissolved in TBST and were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. The immune complexes were visualized with the ECL Western Blotting Detection System (34087, Thermo Fisher Scientific). In all Western blot analyses, primary antibodies were diluted as suggested by product datasheets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10, and quantitative data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m). In cases where equal variance was observed among samples, the significance between groups was assessed through a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For samples exhibiting unequal variance, a two-tailed Welch’s t-test was employed. In scenarios involving multiple comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post hoc Tukey’s test was implemented. Significance levels were established as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001.
