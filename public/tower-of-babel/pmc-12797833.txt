UBE2M inhibits neoplastic cell proliferation via MKK7–JNK–EGR1 axis in melanoma
Authors: Linlin Xu, Chenglong Pan, Yibo Wang, Li Ni, Piao Zeng, Xiang Lin, Xinxing Lyu, Shuhong Huang, Ningning Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysregulated neddylation is often associated with tumorigenesis and malignant progression in multiple kinds of cancers. However, tumor-specific neddylation substrates exist across cancer types, leading to distinct pathological and therapeutic implications. Therefore, precisely targeting tumor-specific neddylation substrates is of critical importance for achieving precision therapy in cancer treatment.MethodsThe expression of UBE2M was examined in human melanoma tissues using western blotting and immunofluorescence (IF). Functional assays evaluated its effects on melanoma cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo, as well as on cell migration in vitro. Mechanistic investigations integrated transcriptomic analysis, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays, and proximity ligation assays (PLA) to elucidate signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms underlying the role of UBE2M in melanoma.ResultsUBE2M, the predominant E2 enzyme in the neddylation pathway, was markedly downregulated in human melanoma tissues, implicating impaired neddylation in melanoma pathogenesis. Mechanistically, we identified mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 7 (MKK7) as a direct neddylation substrate of UBE2M. Neddylation of MKK7 inhibits its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby stabilizing MKK7 and enhancing its phosphorylation. Activation of MKK7 consequently triggers JNK signaling, induces early growth response factor 1 (EGR1), and suppresses CCND2 expression, collectively restraining melanoma cell proliferation.ConclusionsThis study establishes UBE2M as a critical tumor suppressor in melanoma through the MKK7 neddylation–JNK–EGR1 axis and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07531-x.Keywords: UBE2M, Neddylation, MKK7, Melanoma, EGR1

Introduction

Melanoma is an aggressive form of skin cancer characterized by poor prognosis, particularly at advanced stage [1, 2]. Its mortality rate is as high as 38% (1990–2021) [3]. Although surgical resection remains the primary treatment for early-stage disease, alternative therapies have emerged for advanced melanoma. In patients harboring BRAFV600 mutations, combined BRAF and MEK inhibition has demonstrated substantial clinical benefit [4, 5]. In parallel, immune checkpoint inhibitors have significantly improved outcomes in metastatic melanoma [6–8]. Nevertheless, the development of both primary and acquired resistance continues to limit long-term treatment efficacy, underscoring the urgent need to identify novel molecular mechanisms that suppress melanoma progression and to develop new therapeutic strategies [9–11].

Neddylation, one of ubiquitin-like modifications, regulates various cellular activities by conjugating the ubiquitin-like molecule NEDD8 to target proteins, plays a critical role in cellular homeostasis and cancer biology. Dysregulated neddylation affects key processes such as signal transduction, DNA repair, and protein degradation, thereby influencing tumor progression [12–14]. Although many studies have suggested that global inhibition of neddylation exerts anti-tumor effects, some conflicting evidence suggests a more nuanced role. For example, MLN4924 (pevonedistat), a first-in-class NEDD8-activating enzyme inhibitor, has paradoxically been shown to promote lung cancer cell proliferation [15]. These findings suggest that neddylation may have context-dependent, even opposing, effects across cancer types, underscoring its complexity and therapeutic potential.

Despite accumulating evidence implicating neddylation in various malignancies, its specific status, regulation, and biological function in melanoma remain largely undefined. In particular, the role of individual enzymatic components of the neddylation cascade—especially the E2 conjugating enzyme UBE2M—has not been systematically explored. Interestingly, lidocaine has been reported to inhibit melanoma proliferation and overcome vemurafenib resistance by targeting the neddylation pathway [16], further supporting the relevance of this modification in melanoma biology.

In this study, we investigated the functional significance of the E2 neddylation enzyme UBE2M in melanoma. We identified UBE2M as a suppressor of melanoma cell proliferation and uncovered its mechanistic link to the MKK7–JNK–EGR1 signaling axis. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized role for UBE2M in melanoma suppression and provide a mechanistic rationale for its potential as a therapeutic target.

Results

UBE2M suppresses melanoma proliferation

Neddylation has been implicated in the progression of various cancers [17, 18]. Among the two E2 enzymes involved in this post-translational modification pathway, UBE2M has garnered particular interest due to its central role in mediating neddylation [19]. To explore the involvement of UBE2M in melanoma, we first analyzed its expression in patient-derived human cutaneous melanoma tissues compared with normal skin. UBE2M expression was significantly downregulated in melanoma samples (Fig. 1A), a finding further confirmed by immunohistochemical staining of patient tissues using SOX10 as a melanoma cell marker (Fig. 1B).

To determine the functional consequences of UBE2M in melanoma, we generated A375 and M14 cell lines stably overexpressing UBE2M (Fig. 1C, D). The CCK8 assay showed that UBE2M overexpression significantly reduced melanoma cell proliferation (Fig. 1E). Consistent with this, the proportion of Ki67-positive cells—a hallmark of proliferation—was markedly decreased in UBE2M-overexpressing A375 and M14 cells (Fig. 1F, G). However, transwell migration assays revealed that UBE2M overexpression had no significant effect on cell migration relative to controls (Fig. 1H), suggesting a proliferation-specific effect.

To assess whether these findings extended in vivo, we cultured A375 cells with stable UBE2M overexpression and inoculated them into nude mice. Tumor growth was substantially inhibited in the UBE2M-overexpressing group, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and weight (Fig. 1I, J).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that UBE2M acts as a suppressor of melanoma cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo.

UBE2M promotes EGR1 expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which UBE2M suppresses melanoma proliferation, we conducted RNA sequencing on A375 cells with stable UBE2M overexpression. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a significant upregulation of EGR1 (Fig. 2A). This result was validated by RT-qPCR in both A375 and M14 cells, confirming the increased EGR1 transcript levels (Fig. 2B). Consistently, EGR1 protein levels were also elevated upon UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 2C).

EGR1, a zinc-finger transcription factor, has been reported to play multifaceted roles in cancer progression, including melanoma. While previous studies suggest that EGR1 may promote tumor migration and metastasis, other evidence indicates that it can function as a tumor suppressor in various contexts. Given our finding that UBE2M inhibits melanoma proliferation, we next investigated the relationship between EGR1 expression and patient survival using data from 401 melanoma samples in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high EGR1 transcription exhibited significantly better overall survival than those with low expression (Fig. 2D). Importantly, this association remained statistically significant after adjusting for age, sex, and disease stage (Fig. 2E), underscoring EGR1’s potential as a favorable prognostic marker in melanoma.

These findings suggest that UBE2M exerts its anti-proliferative effects in melanoma, at least in part, by promoting EGR1 expression.

UBE2M promotes EGR1 expression via MKK7-JNK phosphorylation

As a key conjugating E2 enzyme, UBE2M primarily mediates the neddylation and ubiquitination of substrate proteins [19]. To investigate how UBE2M influences EGR1, we examined whether EGR1 itself is a direct substrate of UBE2M-mediated neddylation or ubiquitination. Immunoprecipitation (IP) of Flag-tagged EGR1 under partially denaturing conditions, followed by probing with NEDD8- and ubiquitin-specific antibodies, showed no increase in neddylation or ubiquitination of EGR1 upon UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 3A, B). These results suggest that UBE2M does not directly modify EGR1 through either pathway.

EGR1 expression is known to be regulated by mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascades, including the ERK1/2, p38, and JNK pathways, all of which function through phosphorylation [20]. To determine whether UBE2M affects these signaling axes, we assessed their phosphorylation states. Notably, UBE2M overexpression selectively increased JNK phosphorylation without affecting p38 or ERK1/2 (Fig. 3C), and RT-qPCR analysis showed no change in JNK transcript levels (Fig. 3D), indicating a post-translational regulatory mechanism. Moreover, treatment with the JNK-specific inhibitor JNK-IN-8 abolished the UBE2M-induced upregulation of EGR1 (Fig. 3E, Fig. S1A), confirming that JNK activation is necessary for EGR1 induction by UBE2M.

JNK is activated by dual phosphorylation at threonine and tyrosine residues, primarily mediated by two upstream kinases: mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 4 (MKK4) and MKK7 [21]. To investigate which of these kinases mediates UBE2M’s effect, we performed Western blotting and found that UBE2M overexpression significantly increased both total and phosphorylated MKK7 levels, with no appreciable effect on MKK4 (Fig. 3F, Fig. S1B). Notably, RT-qPCR revealed no significant change in MKK7 mRNA levels upon UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 3G), suggesting post-translational stabilization. To further validate the UBE2M-MKK7-JNK-EGR1 signaling axis, MKK7 was silenced, which abolished the UBE2M induced phosphorylation of JNK and the upregulation of EGR1 (Fig. 3H, Fig. S1C).

Taken together, these results indicate that UBE2M enhances MKK7 stability and phosphorylation, thereby promoting downstream JNK activation. Activated JNK, in turn, upregulates EGR1 transcription and protein expression, establishing a novel regulatory axis by which UBE2M suppresses melanoma proliferation.

UBE2M mediates the neddylation of MKK7

To further elucidate the mechanism by which UBE2M upregulates MKK7 and its phosphorylated form, we first conducted co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. These revealed a physical interaction between exogenous UBE2M and endogenous MKK7, an association that was markedly attenuated by MLN4924, a potent small-molecule inhibitor of the NEDD8-activating enzyme (NAE) (Fig. 4A). This observation suggests that the interaction is neddylation-dependent and implicates UBE2M in the neddylation of MKK7.

To test this hypothesis, we performed a Co-IP assay using MKK7 to capture NEDD8. We detected a direct, covalent interaction between MKK7 and NEDD8, consistent with neddylation, which was effectively blocked by MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 4B). Conversely, when NEDD8 was used to pull down MKK7 in cells overexpressing UBE2M, a strong interaction was observed and similarly suppressed by MLN4924 (Fig. 4C). These results confirm that UBE2M mediates the covalent conjugation of NEDD8 to MKK7, verifying that MKK7 undergoes neddylation.

UBE2M is one of two known E2 enzymes involved in the neddylation cascade. To determine its specificity in modifying MKK7, we compared it with UBE2F, the other E2 enzyme in this pathway. While UBE2M overexpression significantly enhanced MKK7 neddylation, UBE2F had no detectable effect (Fig. 4D), indicating that UBE2M uniquely mediates MKK7 neddylation.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that UBE2M is the specific E2 enzyme responsible for catalyzing the neddylation of MKK7.

Neddylation of MKK7 inhibits its ubiquitin-proteasome degradation

As shown in Fig. 3F, UBE2M overexpression markedly increases MKK7 protein levels. Prior studies have suggested that neddylation can antagonize ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of target proteins [22]. To investigate whether UBE2M stabilizes MKK7 by preventing its degradation, we performed a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay in A375 and M14 cells. The results revealed that MKK7 degradation was significantly delayed upon UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 5A, Fig. S2A), indicating enhanced protein stability.

To determine the pathway responsible for MKK7 degradation, we employed MG132, a proteasome inhibitor [23, 24], and chloroquine (CQ), a lysosomal inhibitor [25]. MG132 treatment led to a clear accumulation of MKK7 in A375 and M14 cells, while CQ had little effect (Fig. 5B, Fig.S2B), suggesting that MKK7 is primarily degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome system.

To further examine whether UBE2M suppresses MKK7 ubiquitination via neddylation, we manipulated neddylation levels and assessed MKK7 ubiquitination. UBE2M overexpression, which enhances MKK7 neddylation, resulted in reduced ubiquitination. In contrast, MLN4924-mediated inhibition of neddylation led to elevated ubiquitination of MKK7 (Fig. 5C, Fig. S2C). These results were corroborated by proximity ligation assays (PLA), which showed reduced MKK7-ubiquitin interaction under UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 5D, Fig. S2D).

Together, these findings demonstrate that UBE2M promotes the neddylation of MKK7, thereby suppressing its ubiquitination, protecting it from proteasomal degradation, and stabilizing the protein.

Building on these results, we further investigated the downstream effects of stabilized and phosphorylated MKK7. As previously established, UBE2M enhances MKK7 phosphorylation, leading to activation of the JNK pathway, increasing EGR1 expression. EGR1 is a key transcription factor that regulates genes involved in cell proliferation and cycle progression [26]. Among its downstream targets, D-type cyclins (CCND1 and CCND2) and their associated cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK4 and CDK6) are critical regulators of the G1/S cell cycle transition [27, 28].

To delineate how UBE2M-driven upregulation of EGR1 contributes to melanoma suppression, we examined the expression of proliferation-associated proteins in UBE2M-overexpressing cells. Notably, CCND2 expression was significantly reduced following UBE2M overexpression (Fig. 5E, Fig. S2E). Given that CCND2 complexes with CDK4/6 to promote G1/S progression [29], its downregulation likely contributes to the observed anti-proliferative effects. Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of EGR1 partially restored CCND2 expression and melanoma cell proliferation (Fig. 5F-H, Fig. S2F), confirming that EGR1 acts downstream of UBE2M and is essential for repressing CCND2 mediated melanoma proliferation.

In conclusion, UBE2M functions as an E2 neddylation enzyme in melanoma cells, facilitating the covalent attachment of NEDD8 to MKK7. This modification protects MKK7 from ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation, increasing its abundance and phosphorylation. Enhanced MKK7 activity leads to JNK phosphorylation, activating the JNK-EGR1 signaling axis. Elevated EGR1 levels subsequently downregulate CCND2, suppressing cell cycle progression and inhibiting melanoma proliferation (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Protein neddylation is pivotal in regulating diverse oncogenic or anti-tumor processes across multiple cancer types [18, 30], making it an attractive target for anticancer therapies. However, its precise function in melanoma has remained elusive. In this study, we uncover a novel mechanism by which UBE2M promotes the neddylation of MKK7, thereby preventing its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Stabilization of MKK7 enhances its phosphorylation, activates the JNK signaling cascade, and upregulates EGR1 transcription and expression—culminating in the suppression of melanoma cell proliferation. These findings establish UBE2M-mediated neddylation of MKK7 as a tumor-suppressive mechanism in melanoma.

Previous reports have generally suggested that neddylation promotes cancer progression and therapy resistance. For instance, hyperactivation of the neddylation pathway facilitates tumor cell growth, enhances DNA damage tolerance, and promotes immune evasion [17, 18]. In addition, recent evidence indicates that elevated global neddylation in melanoma contributes to drug resistance by increasing the stability of oncogenic proteins such as Survivin [16]. At first glance, these findings appear to contradict our observation that UBE2M-mediated neddylation exerts tumor-suppressive effects. However, this discrepancy likely reflects the context-dependent nature of neddylation, which can be either oncogenic or tumor-suppressive depending on the substrates, enzymes, and signaling environments involved.

In our study, UBE2M-mediated neddylation of MKK7 inhibited its ubiquitin-dependent degradation, thereby sustaining JNK pathway activity and upregulating EGR1, a transcription factor associated with favorable prognosis in melanoma. Notably, MKK7 represents a previously unrecognized substrate of UBE2M in melanoma, distinguishing this mechanism from those reported in other cancer types. This substrate-specific and tumor-context-dependent effect distinguishes our findings from prior studies focusing on bulk neddylation activity. The divergence may also stem from differential usage of E2 conjugating enzymes (e.g., UBE2M vs. UBE2F) and E3 ligases that confer substrate specificity and cellular context sensitivity.

Early growth response 1 (EGR1) is a zinc-finger transcription factor whose dysfunction is associated with the initiation and progression of various tumors. Notably, EGR1 exerts diverse effects depending on the cancer type. In breast cancer, its suppression limits proliferation [31], while in colon, lung, and liver cancers, elevated EGR1 promotes apoptosis and tumor suppression [32, 33]. Our study demonstrates that UBE2M upregulates EGR1 at both the mRNA and protein levels, and high EGR1 expression correlates with better survival in melanoma patients, highlighting its tumor-suppressive potential in this context. These results further support the notion that UBE2M-driven neddylation selectively stabilizes signaling molecules that favor anti-tumor outcomes in melanoma.

UBE2M facilitates the covalent attachment of NEDD8 to MKK7, shielding it from proteasomal degradation and promoting its phosphorylation. This activates the JNK pathway, which in turn drives EGR1 expression, which are consistent with prior studies reporting MKK7 neddylation in breast cancer cells [34]. Such regulatory mechanism may be conserved across cancer types. Targeting MKK7 neddylation thus represents a promising, previously underexplored avenue for modulating JNK-EGR1 signaling and limiting melanoma progression.

The therapeutic implications of our findings warrant attention. Since UBE2M exerts a tumor-suppressive effect in melanoma by stabilizing MKK7 and activating the JNK–EGR1 pathway, restoring or enhancing its activity may represent a potential therapeutic approach. However, this strategy requires caution, as UBE2M displays oncogenic functions in other cancers, such as hepatocellular and colorectal carcinoma, where it promotes proliferation via neddylation of oncogenic substrates like SREBP-1 and LKB1 [14, 35]. These context-dependent effects emphasize the need for tumor-specific modulation of UBE2M activity. Selectively enhancing the UBE2M–MKK7 interaction, rather than broadly activating the neddylation cascade, may provide a safer and more precise strategy for melanoma treatment.

Interestingly, pharmacological inhibition of neddylation using MLN4924 has been reported to exert paradoxical effects: although it suppresses cullin-RING ligase activity and induces cell cycle arrest in some cancers, it may also stabilize oncogenic proteins such as c-MYC or activate the EGFR pathway, ultimately promoting tumorigenesis [15]. Conversely, in other malignancies like hepatocellular carcinoma, UBE2M-mediated neddylation of SREBP-1 has been shown to drive tumor proliferation and metastasis [35]. Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma, elevated neddylation correlates with increased CCL2 expression and reduced overall survival [36]. Together, these findings reinforce that neddylation’s role in cancer is multifaceted and highly dependent on the cellular context, signaling landscapes and target proteins involved.

This study has certain limitations. Our conclusions are mainly based on overexpression and in vitro models, which may not fully reflect in vivo regulation of UBE2M–MKK7 signaling. Moreover, the lack of patient-derived and immune-competent melanoma models restricts evaluation of UBE2M-mediated neddylation within the tumor microenvironment. Future in vivo validation will be crucial to confirm the translational relevance of targeting the UBE2M–MKK7 axis.

In summary, we identify a novel tumor-suppressive pathway in melanoma in which UBE2M-mediated neddylation of MKK7 enhances JNK signaling and upregulates EGR1, ultimately suppressing melanoma cell proliferation. This work not only elucidates a previously unrecognized role of UBE2M and neddylation in melanoma progression but also opens new therapeutic possibilities for targeting the UBE2M–MKK7–JNK–EGR1 axis in melanoma treatment.

Materials and methods

Mice

All mice were kept in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment under controlled room temperature and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Animals had free access to standard laboratory chow and water.

Cell culture

The A375, M14 and HEK293T cell lines were procured from ATCC and authenticated via STR profiling by GENEWIZ. Cells were verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use in experiments. They were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, #A3160802) and L-glutamine (Gibco, #35050-61), and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Plasmid transfection and RNA interference

Plasmid DNA was introduced into cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, #11668), whereas siRNA transfection was performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778), in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. Six hours post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium.

The siRNAs targeting EGR1 was 5’-ACCCUAAGCUGGAGGAGAU-3’.

Establishment of a stable UBE2M-overexpressing A375 and M14 cells

Puromycin-resistant plasmids of UBE2M and empty vector were transfected with the Lenti-Crispr vector and packaging plasmids into 293T cells. 48 h later, the medium was collected and filtered with 0.45 μm filter and infected A375 and M14 cells. Then the infected cells were selected by puromycin for 2 weeks. Halved puromycin was used in successive stable transfected cell culture.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO, #CW2333S) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, #HY-K011). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, mixed with SDS-PAGE sample buffer (Solarbio, #P1016), and boiled at 98 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, #ISEQ00010). Membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio, #A8020) in PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in PBST overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit (Proteintech, #PK10002). When the molecular weights of several target proteins were too similar, Western blotting were performed using separate membranes loaded with identical sample amounts. The antibodies we used were as follows: UBE2M (Proteintech, #14520-1-AP, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, #60004-1-Ig, 1:5000), NEDD8 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2745, 1:1000), EGR1 (ABclonal, #A7266, 1:600), HA (Proteintech, #51064-2-AP, 1:7000), Flag (Proteintech, #20543-1-AP, 1:20000), Ubiquitin (Proteintech, #10201-2-AP, 1:2000), JNK (Proteintech, #51153-1-AP, 1:1000), p-JNK (Proteintech, #80024-1-RR, 1:2000), ERK1/2 (Proteintech, #11257-1-AP, 1:3000), p-ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4370, 1:2000), p38 (Proteintech, #14064-1-AP, 1:5000), p-p38 (Proteintech, #28796-1-AP, 1:2000), MKK7 (Proteintech, #55030-1-AP, 1:500), p-MKK7 (Proteintech, #80357-1-RR, 1:5000), MKK4 (Proteintech, #17340-1-AP, 1:1000), p-MKK4 (Proteintech, #29250-1-AP, 1:1000), UBE2F (Proteintech, #17056-1-AP, 1:1000), CCND2 (Proteintech, #10934-1-AP, 1:1000), CCND1 (Proteintech, #26939-1-AP, 1:20000), CDK4 (Proteintech, #11026-1-AP, 1:4000), CDK6 (Proteintech, #14052-1-AP, 1:3000), goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, #SA00001-1), and goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, #SA00001-2).

Skin tissue protein extraction

Proteins from cutaneous melanoma and normal skin tissues were isolated using a skin-specific total protein extraction kit (Invent, #SA-01-SK), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting lysates were mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and analyzed by immunoblotting using designated antibodies.

Co-IP analysis

Cells were lysed using Lysis Buffer (containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.2% NP-40) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were directly mixed with magnetic Flag-beads (Thermo Scientific, #A36797) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were washed thoroughly with cold lysis buffer four times. Bound proteins were then eluted by boiling in SDS-PAGE sample buffer and subsequently analyzed by western blotting with the indicated antibodies.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Cellular total RNA was isolated using the RNA extraction kit (Sparkjade, #AC0205) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a cDNA synthesis kit (CWBIO, #CW2569M) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (CWBIO, #CW0957). Primer sequences are provided below.

The forward sequence targeting EGR1 was 5’-CCACGCCGAACACTGACATT-3’, the reverse sequence targeting EGR1 was 5’-GAGGGGTTAGCGAAGGCTG-3’. The forward sequence targeting JNK was 5’-TGTGTGGAATCAAGCACCTTC-3’, the reverse sequence targeting JNK was 5’-AGGCGTCATCATAAAACTCGTTC-3’. The forward sequence targeting MKK7 was 5’-CCACGTCATTGCCGTTAAGC-3’, the reverse sequence targeting MKK7 was 5’-GCACGATGTAGGGGCAGTC-3’.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For paraffin sections of the skin tissue, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated using an ethanol gradient. For melanoma cells, they were seeded onto glass coverslips in 6-well plates. Following this, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at RT and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min at RT.

To prevent non-specific antibody binding, tissues or cells were blocked with normal goat serum (CWBIO, #CW0130S) for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam, #ab104139). Image acquisition was performed using the Olympus BX63F microscope. The antibodies utilized in this study were as follows: Ki67 (Proteintech, #27309-1-AP, 1:200), UBE2M (Proteintech, #14520-1-AP, 1:100), SOX10 (Proteintech, #66786-1-Ig, 1:300), 488-goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, #RGAR002, 1:500), and 594-goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Proteintech, #RGAM004, 1:500).

CCK-8 assay

Melanoma cells were initially plated in 6-well plates and transfected with UBE2M for 24 h. Post-transfection, cells were harvested and reseeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well, followed by another 24-hour incubation. Subsequently, Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (Proteintech, #PF00004) was added and incubated for 40 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, #SUNRISE) to assess cell viability. All experimental conditions were tested in triplicate, and the assay was independently repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Transwell assay

The assay was performed in an invasion chamber consisting of 12 wells. A cell suspension in serum-free culture medium (2 × 104 cells per well) was added to the inserts, and each insert was placed in the lower chamber containing 10% FBS culture medium. Cells were allowed to migrate for 24 h in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, the images were captured using the microscope.

Tumor growth in nude mice

BALB/c nude male mice (8 ~ 10 weeks old) were obtained from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center (SLAC, China). The mice were divided into 2 groups, each group contained 8 mice. Each BALB/c nude male mouse was inoculated subcutaneously with 1 × 107 A375 cells into the left flank. Tumor size was measured 5 times. All animals were killed 3 weeks after injection, and the transplanted tumors were removed and weighed.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA was conducted following the manufacturer’s guidelines. A375 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 6-well plates and either mock-infected or infected with the specified plasmids. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization using 0.5% Triton X-100, cells were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies (MKK7, Proteintech, #55030-1-AP, 1:200; Ubiquitin, Cell Signaling Technology, #3936, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. PLA probe anti-rabbit PLUS (Sigma, #DUO92002–100RXN), anti-mouse MINUS (Sigma, #DUO92004-100RXN) were then added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After two washing steps, cells were treated with the ligation mix and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by signal amplification using a polymerase solution. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus BX63F fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained as results of a minimum of three independent replicates, analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9, and presented as mean ± SD. P < 0.05 represents statistically significant.
