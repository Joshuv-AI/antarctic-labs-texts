Volatile self-selective memristive neuron for millisecond-latency neuromorphic object detection at the edge
Authors: Zhejia Zhang, Jiahua Xu, Xuemeng Fan, Guobin Zhang, Zijian Wang, Pengtao Li, Qi Luo, Haoxiang Yu, Shuai Zhong, Yunyan Zhang, Wenzhang Fang, Weidong You, Daying Sun, Kun Ren, Qing Wan, Yishu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe deployment of artificial intelligence for real-time object detection in edge applications is constrained by the power and latency limitations of conventional computing architectures. Here we show a bio-inspired neuromorphic system built around a self-selective GaOx/ZnO memristor to address this challenge. The device exhibits a selection ratio and nonlinearity (both of ~10⁷), picoampere-level leakage currents, and microsecond-scale volatile dynamics. We integrate these memristors into a 32×32 array emulating the first-spike-time-coding mechanism of the frog visual system, enabling millisecond-scale pulse responses. When applied to aerial drone object detection, our hardware system achieves reliable recognition for pedestrians and vehicles, with only a 2.5% accuracy drop compared to software simulations. Furthermore, the array demonstrates a parallel processing scale of ~8.36×10¹² computational nodes under a 10% read margin. This work provides a tangible hardware solution for constructing fast-response neuromorphic computing systems at the edge, suitable for intelligent transportation and real-time monitoring.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The expanding deployment of drone technology in critical applications, such as intelligent traffic management, emergency response, and security surveillance creates an urgent demand for efficient, real-time object detection at the edge1–3. While artificial neural networks (ANNs) provide the software backbone for these tasks4,5, their implementations on conventional von Neumann architectures are fundamentally limited by the memory-processor bottleneck, leading to excessive latency and power consumption that are unsuitable for resource-constrained aerial platforms6–8.

Neuromorphic computing, which emulates the brain’s efficient, event-driven processing, offers a promising path forward9,10. Early neuromorphic systems based on complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology, however, often require complex circuits to mimic basic neuronal functions, resulting in poor scalability and energy efficiency11,12. Memristors, with their intrinsic ionic dynamics and synaptic/neuronal emulation capabilities, have emerged as a core building block for next-generation neuromorphic hardware13–17. In particular, volatile memristors are ideal candidates for constructing artificial neurons that mimic the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) mechanism18–22. Yet, existing volatile memristors face a critical trade-off: electrochemical devices offer low current and high on/off ratios but suffer from poor uniformity23,24. Additionally, the active electrodes relied upon exhibit inherent diffusion characteristics, which fundamentally conflict with the high stability and reliability demanded by CMOS back-end processes, posing severe challenges to their integration. While Mott memristors provide good uniformity and speed but at the cost of milliampere-level operating currents25,26. Moreover, a fundamental challenge in scaling up any two-terminal memristor is the debilitating issue of sneak-path currents in crossbar arrays, which compromises read/write fidelity27,28.

Self-selective memristors (SSMs), which integrate nonlinear current-voltage (I-V) characteristics directly into the device, provide an effective solution to the sneak-path problem, enabling the realization of large-scale, dense arrays29–34. However, the performance of existing SSMs has been hampered by high standby power (due to large static currents) and slow decay rates, which prevent the rapid reset of neuronal membrane potential and ultimately limit encoding speed35–37. Nature provides a blueprint for overcoming these limitations. Biological sensory systems have evolved for maximal efficiency, processing critical information with minimal energy38. A quintessential example is the visual system of the frog, which is exquisitely tuned to detect moving prey. Its retinal ganglion cells employ a time-to-first-spike (TTFS) coding scheme, where the first neuron to spike in response to a stimulus carries the most salient information, enabling recognition and reaction within milliseconds39. Emulating this efficient coding strategy in hardware could unlock similarly rapid and low-power object detection.

Here, we report a volatile SSM based on a GaOx/ZnO heterostructure that overcomes key material and device-level challenges to realize a high-performance, bio-inspired artificial neuron. And the selected device materials are compatible with CMOS technology and can be manufactured in back-end processes, enabling large-scale integration on silicon-based chips. Our device exhibits a selection ratio and nonlinearity ( ~ 10⁷), a picoampere-level leakage current, and a microsecond-scale decay time. These characteristics enable us to construct a 32×32 neuron array that demonstrates bio-realistic LIF dynamics and, by integrating the frog-inspired TTFS coding, achieves millisecond-scale pulse encoding. In a practical demonstration for drone aerial imaging, our system achieves 96.2% of the detection accuracy of software-based ANNs for pedestrians and vehicles. Concurrently, the intrinsic nonlinearity of the volatile SSMs suppresses sneak paths, supporting a large parallel computational scale of ~8.36×1012 nodes. This work thus provides a holistic hardware solution that bridges device engineering, bio-inspired algorithm implementation, and system-level validation, paving the way for high-efficiency neuromorphic computing at the edge.

Results

Bio-inspired neural encoding and system implementation

The efficiency of the frog’s visual system in detecting prey provides a powerful blueprint for designing rapid, low-power object detection systems40. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the process begins when a moving insect enters the frog’s visual field, creating local fluctuations in light intensity. The retina processes this information through a hierarchy of cells: photoreceptors (rods and cones) capture the initial signal, which is then relayed via bipolar cells to specialized retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). Crucially, RGCs, such as darkness detectors and contrast detectors act as feature-extraction filters. Their receptive fields are tuned to suppress static background information and amplify dynamic signals caused by moving objects41.

This selective filtering results in an accelerated depolarization rate for RGCs responding to the prey. Consequently, these cells generate their first spike significantly earlier than those processing the background, implementing a highly efficient TTFS coding scheme42. This TTFS-encoded information is transmitted to the optic tectum, which localizes the prey by comparing the relative timing of spikes from different retinal regions43. This mechanism bypasses the need for metabolically expensive high-frequency firing, enabling rapid prey recognition and reflex initiation with minimal energy expenditure.

Inspired by this core principle, we have developed a neuromorphic system that mimics the frog’s TTFS coding for efficient object detection in drone aerial imagery. Our hardware implementation, schematically depicted in Fig. 1b, is centered on a 32×32 array of artificial neurons built using volatile SSMs. A scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of a single fabricated GaOx/ZnO memristor device is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1, confirming the successful integration of the bilayer structure, with the full system integrated onto a printed circuit board (PCB) for functional validation.

The system operates by directly mapping the grayscale values of pre-processed aerial image pixels to the TTFS of corresponding neurons in the array. In this scheme, pixels with higher intensity changes, corresponding to key objects of interest like pedestrians and vehicles, trigger an earlier neuronal spike. This prioritizes critical information for downstream processing modules, dramatically accelerating the detection workflow. Furthermore, the memristors themselves, with their picoampere-level leakage currents (as will be shown in subsequent sections), inherently ensure low static power consumption. This bio-inspired architecture, combining TTFS-based temporal filtering with energy-efficient hardware, thus achieves a synergistic optimization of processing speed and power efficiency for edge-based aerial object detection.

Electrical characteristics of self-selective memristors

The fundamental electrical characteristics of our GaOx/ZnO bilayer SSM are presented in Fig. 2a. The device exhibits highly nonlinear and rectifying current-voltage (I-V) behavior under DC sweeps. Under positive bias, the current transitions from a pA-level off-state to a µA-level on-state, while under negative bias, the current remains suppressed at the pA-level due to the built-in potential from the asymmetric heterojunction. This intrinsic nonlinearity is quantified by a selection ratio (SR) and nonlinearity (NL), both approximately 10⁷ under a 1/6 read voltage scheme30 (see Methods for definitions, Supplementary Fig. 2). The voltage-dependent trends of SR and NL are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3. These properties are critical for suppressing sneak paths in crossbar arrays, enabling large-scale integration.

The volatility of the SSM, a key requirement for LIF neurons, is confirmed in Fig. 2b. The device consistently returns to its high-resistance state after each of 10 consecutive positive voltage sweeps without requiring an external reset operation. Furthermore, the volatile SSM demonstrates robust endurance, maintaining an SR of ~10⁷ and NL of ~10⁷ over more than 106 cycles (Fig. 2c). Additionally, we conducted endurance testing on the on/off ratio of this volatile SSM (Supplementary Fig. 4), demonstrating the reliability of the proposed volatile SSM for repeated programming. The uniformity of our 32×32 array is evidenced by the overlapping I-V curves of 128 randomly selected devices (Fig. 2d), with statistical distributions of resistance states and device-to-device variation detailed in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, respectively. The consistent performance across 16 randomly selected devices is further confirmed by cyclic I-V measurements in Supplementary Fig. 7.

We next characterized the transient response of the SSM to pulsed inputs, which is fundamental to its operation as an artificial neuron. The response current and decay time were probed using single pulses of varying amplitude and width. As shown in Fig. 2e, f, both higher amplitude and longer pulse width lead to a larger integrated charge and a progressively longer decay time (insets; statistical data in Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). A comprehensive mapping of the decay time (Fig. 2g) reveals that it remains on the microsecond scale across a wide range of pulse parameters. This rapid volatility is essential for the fast reset of neuronal membrane potential, directly enabling high temporal resolution in neural coding. Notably, compared with other volatile SSMs, our volatile SSM demonstrates improved performance (Fig. 2h), achieving the shortest decay time and a significantly low leakage current33–36,44,45.

The corresponding mapping of the peak response current (Supplementary Fig. 10) demonstrates a stronger dependence on pulse amplitude than on pulse width, highlighting the device’s sensitivity to input signal strength. Finally, we observed paired-pulse facilitation (PPF), a phenomenon that enhances the response to successive input pulses and thereby significantly modulates the integration of the membrane potential in artificial neurons. When two identical pulses are applied with a short inter-spike interval, the response to the second pulse is facilitated due to residual charge from the first (Fig. 2i). The PPF index, defined as (I₂ - I₁)/I₁ × 100%, where I₁ and I₂ are the peak currents of the first and second pulses, decays exponentially with the interval time46. This behavior is well-fitted by a double-exponential function with time constants of τ₁ = 25.97 and τ₂ = 281.52 μs, confirming the device’s capability for history-dependent temporal integration.

Conduction mechanism in bilayer self-selective memristors

To elucidate the physical origin of the observed electrical behavior, we conducted a systematic investigation of the material properties and electron transport mechanisms in our GaOₓ/ZnO bilayer SSMs. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was first employed to analyze the chemical states. The elemental distribution (Fig. 3a) confirms the successful formation of distinct GaOₓ and ZnO layers. Crucially, deconvolution of the O 1 s peaks (Fig. 3b, c) reveals a significantly higher concentration of oxygen vacancies (Ov) in the ZnO layer (42.9%) compared to the GaOₓ layer (10.3%). This gradient in Ov concentration is a key design feature for controlling charge dynamics. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. 3d, e) show that both films are smooth, with root-mean-square (RMS) roughness values of 0.699 nm (ZnO) and 0.367 nm (GaOₓ), ensuring uniform and reliable electrical characteristics across the device. Furthermore, cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals a well-defined vertical heterostructure with intact and sharp interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 11a). The corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping and line scan profiles further confirm the precise spatial confinement of each layer without severe interfacial mixing (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c).

AFM characteristics of d ZnO film and e GaOx film. f The electron transition mode corresponds to different voltage ranges under forward voltage, where the blue segment represents Schottky emission and the green segment represents Poole-Frenkel (P-F) emission. To decouple the roles of each layer, we fabricated and characterized control devices with single-layer structures: Pt/ZnO/ITO and Pt/GaOₓ/ITO (Supplementary Fig. 12). The Pt/ZnO/ITO device exhibits a transition from Schottky emission at low fields to Poole-Frenkel (P-F) emission at higher fields, as confirmed by linear fitting (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). This indicates that electron transport transitions from being limited by the electrode interface to being dominated by trap-assisted hopping within the Ov-rich ZnO bulk. In contrast, the Pt/GaOₓ/ITO device shows a transition from Schottky emission to Fowler-Nordheim (F-N) tunneling (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16), signifying direct electron tunneling through the thin GaOₓ layer under high bias47. However, neither single-layer structure exhibits significant nonlinearity or self-selection, underscoring the necessity of the bilayer design.

The synergistic conduction mechanism in the full Pt/GaOₓ/ZnO/ITO SSM is illustrated in Fig. 3f. The band alignment under equilibrium conditions (Supplementary Fig. 17) reveals a highly asymmetric structure48–51, with a large barrier at the Pt/GaOₓ interface and a smaller barrier at the ITO/ZnO interface. Under low forward bias (Fig. 3g), electron injection from the ITO electrode is limited by Schottky emission at the ITO/ZnO interface, resulting in the low-current, nonlinear region of the I-V curve (linear fitting in Supplementary Fig. 18a). As the bias increases, injected electrons populate the abundant shallow traps in the ZnO layer (from its high Ov concentration), facilitating a transition to P-F emission as the dominant transport mechanism through the ZnO bulk (linear fitting in Supplementary Fig. 18b). To further substantiate these conduction mechanisms, we conducted temperature-dependent I-V measurements at different temperatures. Supplementary Fig. 19 reveals a continuous increase in the current with elevating temperature, directly confirming a thermally activated transport process. Furthermore, the fitting results for both Schottky emission and P-F emission remain excellent across different temperatures, which systematically verifies the transition from Schottky emission to P-F emission. Subsequently, electrons tunnel through the GaOₓ layer via the F-N mechanism to reach the Pt electrode, completing the conductive pathway.

The distinctive switching mechanism and volatile nature of our device are fundamentally governed by an asymmetric band alignment coupled with the dual characteristics of intrinsic defects. Specifically, UV-Vis and UPS measurements (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21) experimentally confirm the highly asymmetric band structure driven by the distinct electron affinities of ZnO and GaOx. Furthermore, the extracted Urbach tail energy indicates that ZnO possesses substantially more abundant shallow potential wells (0.32 eV). These high-concentration shallow traps facilitate efficient electron transport under bias but cannot strictly confine the carriers52,53. Consequently, upon removal of the bias (Fig. 3h), the captured electrons rapidly relax and escape, fundamentally underpinning the electrical volatility of the device, which serves as the foundation of its LIF neuron function. Concurrently, the intense electric field during the set process drives the physical migration of these intrinsic defects, which is directly visualized via high-resolution TEM. Compared to the uniformly distributed Ov in the pristine lattice (Supplementary Fig. 22a), the post-set state exhibits distinct localized structural distortions (Supplementary Fig. 22b), which serve as direct residual footprints of localized defect aggregation. Under reverse bias (Fig. 3i), the operational symmetry is broken. Electron injection from the Pt electrode is severely limited by the large Schottky barrier at the Pt/GaOₓ interface. Furthermore, the low Ov concentration in the GaOₓ layer provides very few paths for trap-assisted conduction. This combination effectively suppresses current flow in the reverse direction, resulting in the high rectification ratio and excellent self-selective behavior that is critical for suppressing sneak paths in crossbar arrays.

Artificial neuron properties and time-to-first-spike coding

The LIF model is a canonical description of biological neuronal dynamics, capturing the core process where dendrites receive inputs, the soma integrates them, and the axon initiates a spike once a threshold is surpassed54 (Fig. 4a). We demonstrate that our volatile SSMs inherently emulate this behavior, serving as efficient hardware-based LIF neurons. When stimulated by a train of presynaptic-like electrical pulses (3.5 V, 200 μs width, 20 μs interval), the current through an SSM—analogous to a neuronal membrane potential—exhibits a gradual integration (Fig. 4b). Upon reaching a threshold, the neuron fires, after which the current rapidly decays back to its baseline within 1 ms, ready for the next integration cycle. Biologically, the soma integrates stimuli into a graded potential, while the axon strictly generates discrete ‘all-or-none’ spikes. Our SSM accurately emulates this somatic integration; thus, the observable sub-threshold current represents valid potential accumulation. Although abrupt ‘all-or-none’ firing can be triggered using step-increasing pulses (Supplementary Fig. 23), constant pulses are utilized here to maximize TTFS encoding efficiency. Crucially, this bio-realistic emulation preserves single-device scaling. By amalgamating self-selectivity ( ~ 107) and volatility into a single device, the SSM intrinsically eliminates array-level sneak-path current without requiring area-consuming local components at each cross-point. This LIF behavior is highly reproducible. Furthermore, the neuron’s firing timing can be precisely modulated by the input frequency (Pulse Frequency Modulation, Fig. 4c) and amplitude (Fig. 4d). Stronger or more frequent inputs lead to faster potential integration and earlier spiking, a fundamental property for temporal encoding.

The reliability of this conductance modulation is critical for system-level operation. The SSM shows consistent continuous conductance tuning over 50 pulses (Supplementary Fig. 24), with stable performance across multiple cycles (Fig. 4e). To evaluate the cyclic stability of the conductive states, we performed 100 repeated cycling tests. Under a stimulation of 50 pulses per cycle, we sampled and statistically analyzed 11 specific conductive states, which confirmed their excellent stability. (Supplementary Fig. 25). Most importantly, this functionality extends reliably across our full 32×32 crossbar array. The continuous conductance modulation of all 1024 devices is shown in Fig. 4f, with statistical uniformity across devices and states detailed in Supplementary Fig. 26. We quantified the variations as low cycle-to-cycle (C2C, 1.82%) and device-to-device (D2D, 3.58%) values (Supplementary Fig. 27), ensuring high encoding fidelity. A performance comparison with other artificial neuron technologies is provided in Supplementary Table 1, highlighting the advantages of our approach.

Inspired by efficient sensory processing in biology, we implemented a TTFS coding scheme, which stands in contrast to the more common but less efficient rate coding55. While rate coding transmits information via spike counts, consuming more time and energy, TTFS encodes stimulus strength in the latency to the first spike, enabling faster inference with drastically lower spike counts (and thus lower energy consumption)56. We leveraged the amplitude-dependent integration speed of our SSMs to implement this scheme (Supplementary Fig. 28). Setting a firing threshold of 12 μA, we recorded the neuron’s response to various input amplitudes (Fig. 4g). The corresponding temporal integration traces (Fig. 4h) show that sub-threshold inputs fail to trigger a spike, providing inherent noise suppression. Crucially, as the pulse amplitude increases from 3.0 V to 4.0 V, the time required to reach the threshold current decreases monotonically (Fig. 4i). This demonstrates that stronger inputs, representing more salient features in a data stream, are encoded and communicated significantly faster. This property is essential for rapid feature extraction in applications like drone imagery, allowing the system to prioritize critical information while ignoring minor perturbations. Furthermore, as detailed in Supplementary Note 4, this single-spike TTFS mechanism results in an average energy consumption of 42 nJ per neuron. A comprehensive benchmarking of the energy consumption per neuron against other representative memristive neurons is detailed in Supplementary Table 1. This culminates in approximately 43 μJ for the entire 32×32 array per encoding cycle. Consequently, our system enables much faster inference with a drastically reduced energy footprint compared to traditional coding strategies.

System-level demonstration for aerial object detection

To validate the practical utility of our bio-inspired system, we implemented a complete drone aerial object detection framework centered on the SSM-based neuron array (Fig. 5a). The TTFS encoding is deployed on a custom hardware platform constructed with a printed circuit board (PCB) and an FPGA (Supplementary Fig. 29). This hardware system converts the pixels of aerial images into spike times, achieving millisecond-level latency. The hardware-encoded temporal signals are subsequently utilized for downstream object detection, demonstrating the practical feasibility of our neuromorphic system in real scenarios.

The encoded temporal signals were then processed by a YOLOv8 deep neural network, which was trained on the VisDrone2019 dataset to detect five key object categories: pedestrians, cars, vans, buses, and motors57 (see dataset split and label distribution in Supplementary Fig. 30). A critical advantage of our hardware is its inherent scalability for large arrays. Using a one-bitline pull-up (OBPU) method to model array performance (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 31), we project that the volatile SSM array can support a maximum parallel processing scale of up to ~8.36×1012 computational nodes under a 10% read margin (Fig. 5b). This metric reflects the parallel computational density the system can sustain per cycle. Furthermore, we evaluated the practical impacts of interconnect resistance and parasitic capacitance (detailed in Supplementary Note 2). The voltage drop across the interconnect lines and the parasitic displacement current are strictly limited to negligible levels. Consequently, these parasitic elements exert no discernible impact on the array’s overall scalability. This scalability underscores the potential for highly compact, large-scale neuromorphic chips.

We quantitatively evaluated the system’s object recognition accuracy by comparing our hardware’s TTFS encoding against a software-simulated baseline. The SSM-based hardware achieved an overall accuracy of 62.9%, which is 96.2% of the software simulation’s accuracy (65.4%) (Fig. 5c). Although the software baseline of 65.4% might appear modest compared to standard object detection tasks, it is a deliberate result within the context of edge-computing hardware validation. Crucially, avoiding simpler datasets prevents the algorithmic fault tolerance from artificially masking hardware non-idealities. Therefore, successfully retaining 96.2% of this baseline rigorously validates the high signal fidelity of our hardware-based TTFS encoding. A comparison with other models simulated through software is provided in Supplementary Table 2, demonstrating that our model is effective for object detection. This near-parity demonstrates the fidelity of our hardware implementation. The confusion matrix (Fig. 5d) and precision-recall curves (Fig. 5e) confirm the system’s ability to reliably distinguish between all five object categories, with average precision ranging from 54% (vans) to 86% (cars). A detailed explanation of precision and recall is provided in Supplementary Note 3. The consistency between hardware results and software benchmarks (Supplementary Fig. 32) further validates the approach. To explicitly quantify our system-level advantages, we benchmarked the encoding pipeline against conventional CPU and GPU platforms performing identical image preprocessing tasks. Based on the analytical model detailed in Supplementary Note 4, the volatile SSM array achieves an average encoding latency of 2.012 ms and a low power consumption of 0.02 W. As shown in Fig. 5f, this translates to a 16.75× and 2.81× reduction in processing latency compared to the CPU and GPU baselines, respectively. Moreover, our hardware demonstrates a massive 1199× and 463× reduction in power consumption, directly confirming the efficiency of our bio-inspired architecture for edge computing.

The system’s performance is also robust to real-world dataset imbalances. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33, the VisDrone2019 dataset is dominated by cars (50.3%), yet our system maintains strong recognition across all categories. Qualitative results in diverse scenarios (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 34) demonstrate stable detection and accurate classification, even for distant small objects and in complex urban backgrounds. This showcases the system’s scene adaptability and its feasibility for real-world deployment in intelligent traffic management, emergency response, and other applications requiring low-power, high-speed edge computing.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a volatile SSM based on a GaOₓ/ZnO bilayer that demonstrates reliable performance, including a selection ratio and nonlinearity ( ~ 10⁷), picoampere-level leakage current, and microsecond-scale decay. These properties enabled the construction of a 32×32 neuromorphic array that successfully emulates bio-realistic LIF dynamics. By integrating a frog vision-inspired TTFS coding scheme, the system achieves millisecond-latency encoding for rapid object detection. Unlike standard short-term plasticity synapses, our proposed artificial neuron exhibits rapid decay dynamics, swiftly returning to its initial state after reaching the firing threshold. This intrinsic auto-reset mechanism effectively prevents temporal crosstalk between consecutive image frames during TTFS coding. Furthermore, the inherent self-selectivity of the device intrinsically suppresses array-level sneak-path current, thereby eliminating spatial crosstalk within dense crossbar arrays without requiring area-consuming auxiliary components. In a practical demonstration for drone aerial imagery, the hardware system achieved 96.2% of the software-based accuracy in identifying pedestrians and vehicles, while its intrinsic nonlinearity supports a parallel computational density of ~8.36×1012 nodes. This work provides a compelling hardware solution, demonstrating a synergistic combination of device physics and bio-inspired architecture for building efficient, fast-response neuromorphic computing systems at the edge, with promising applications in intelligent transportation and real-time monitoring.

Methods

Device fabrication and characterization of thin films

All films in this work were prepared using a magnetron sputtering system (DISCOVERY-635, Denton Vacuum). The deposition chamber was evacuated using a combination of turbomolecular and mechanical pumps, reaching a base pressure of better than 5×10⁻⁶ Torr after approximately 3 h of pumping. During all sputtering processes, high-purity argon gas (99.99%) was utilized as the sputtering medium with a constant flow rate of 37 sccm, precisely regulated by a mass flow controller (accuracy: ±0.1 sccm). The ignition pressure was strictly maintained at 99 mTorr for all deposited layers.

The device fabrication began with the ultrasonic cleaning of the SiO₂/Si substrate to remove surface contaminants. A 50 nm Pt bottom electrode was then deposited via DC sputtering at a power of 300 W onto the pre-patterned SiO₂/Si substrate defined by photolithography (AZ5214 photoresist, 1.1 μm). This step utilized a Pt target (99.9% purity, 3-inch diameter, 2 mm thickness). Following the Pt deposition, the photoresist was removed using an ultrasonic bath in acetone and isopropanol, followed by N₂ drying.

Subsequently, a second photolithographic patterning step was performed, after which the 10 nm GaOₓ and 40 nm ZnO functional layers were sequentially deposited. The GaOₓ layer was deposited via RF sputtering at 50 W using a Ga₂O₃ target (atomic ratio 2:3, 99.9% purity, 3-inch diameter, 3 mm thickness). Immediately after, the ZnO layer was deposited via RF sputtering at 50 W using a ZnO target (atomic ratio 1:1, 99.9% purity, 3-inch diameter, 3 mm thickness).

Finally, a 30 nm ITO top electrode was deposited onto the functional layers via DC sputtering at a power of 100 W, utilizing an In₂O₃:SnO₂ target (9:1 ratio, 99.9% purity, 3-inch diameter, 3 mm thickness). The remaining photoresist was then removed via an ultrasonic lift-off process in acetone and isopropanol, followed by N₂ drying to complete the device fabrication.

For material characterization, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of Ga, Zn, and O elements was conducted using a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi system equipped with an Al Kα (1486.6 eV) excitation source. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterization was performed using a Dimension ICON instrument.

Electrical measurements

The electrical characteristics of the Pt/GaOx/ZnO/ITO SSM were measured using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200A-SCS), with the ITO electrode connected to ground. A DC source module (4200-SMU) was employed for DC measurements. To emulate the functionality of biological neurons, a waveform generator module (4200-PMU) was used for pulse-mode measurements, while the pulse responses were recorded in real-time. All electrical tests were conducted at room temperature and under ambient atmospheric conditions.

Drive system design

A 32×32 SSM crossbar array was integrated onto a PCB using wire bonding. The key parameters for pulse modulation—including amplitude, pulse width, and pulse count—were transmitted to a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) serving as the main control unit via a communication interface. The FPGA is responsible for generating the corresponding pulse modulation signals and utilizes its internal programmable clock module to precisely regulate the pulse intervals, thereby achieving flexible frequency control. For pulse amplitude adjustment, a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) was employed to enable dynamic configuration of the amplitude. Simultaneously, a dedicated technical module integrated within the FPGA was used to accurately quantify the number of applied pulses. Based on the above methodology, we generated pulse trains with programmable amplitude (3 ~ 4 V), pulse width (200 μs), and pulse interval (20 μs) to drive the rows and columns of the SSM crossbar array. To further accomplish image encoding, the FPGA adopted a multiplexing strategy. Combined with a custom-designed decoding circuit, this strategy sequentially mapped the voltage modulation signals corresponding to each pixel of an aerial image to the respective units of the 32×32 crossbar array, ultimately achieving the encoding of the aerial image at the array level.

Drone aerial image object detection

In this work, aerial images were encoded using artificial neurons based on the GaOx/ZnO SSM, and object detection was performed using the YOLOv8 deep neural network architecture. The implementation was carried out in a Python environment, with aerial images sourced from the public VisDrone 2019 dataset. Deep neural networks, leveraging the combination of fully connected and convolutional layers, exhibit strong nonlinear mapping capabilities, making them particularly suitable for high-dimensional image feature extraction. In this study, a TTFS coding scheme was adopted to convert pixel information into spike timing: each pixel value corresponds to a spike, whose firing time decreases exponentially with increasing input voltage intensity. The resulting spike timing features are first processed by the backbone of YOLOv8, which consists of multiple RepConv layers and SiLU activation functions. Subsequently, the neck module fuses multi-scale features through cross-layer connections. Finally, the detection head outputs class labels, confidence scores, and bounding box locations. During training, the loss function comprises three components: bounding box regression loss, objectness loss, and classification loss. The network was optimized on the VisDrone 2019 dataset using stochastic gradient descent, with parameters updated via backpropagation to minimize the total loss. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU for 100 epochs.

Performance monitoring of central and graphics processors

We implemented a comprehensive performance monitoring framework to analyze the time delay and power consumption of both GPU and CPU during image preprocessing. The system utilized NVIDIA NVML and HWiNFO to monitor the real-time operation of the GPU and CPU, respectively. The monitoring setup incorporated Windows Performance Monitor along with various profiling tools to track system-wide resource utilization. NVIDIA NVML captures CUDA memory transfers and kernel execution, while HWiNFO analyzes CPU bus utilization and cache behavior. Specific Windows configurations included enabling Hardware Accelerated GPU Scheduling to improve measurement accuracy.
