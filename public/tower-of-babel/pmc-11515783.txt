Racial biases in clinical practice and medical education: a scoping review
Authors: Adil Mouhab, Rahmeth Radjack, Marie Rose Moro, Mathilde Lambert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth inequalities represent a major challenge in contemporary medicine, with some attributed to racial biases. Recently, in the United States, a call to combat discrimination in the field of health has resonated, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 crisis, in which minorities have been disproportionately affected. These calls echo recommendations from the Institute of Medicine dating back to 2001, urging the fight against inequalities in access to health care. In France, inequalities based on presumed origin persist, yet medical education on these issues is virtually nonexistent.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a scoping review literature review in accordance with the JBI recommendations for scoping review writing and applied the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist to explore existing conceptual and educational data on racial biases in medicine. The inclusion criteria were interventional studies or evaluations of existing educational programs in medical training addressing the fight against racial biases in clinical settings published in French or English between 2003 and 2023. Six databases were systematically consulted.ResultsOut of 748 initial studies, 28 were included in our study. The median number of participants in the studies was low, interventions were diverse, and participants were generally well received, most of whom were self-selected. No study has evaluated the clinical impact of these interventions. The highlighted concepts included levels of racism, cultural competence, cultural humility, and critical race theory.DiscussionThe authors most frequently referred to institutionalized racism, demonstrating the systemic nature of these issues. At an individual level, implicit and unconscious biases were most often emphasized. It appears that the concept of “race” is a sociopolitical construct without supporting biological determinants. Humility is central to this field of study, as it encourages questioning of individual or collective medical practices. In France, the context, including the prohibition of ethnic statistics, may hinder the objectification of discrimination. Immersion and virtual patient scenarios emerged as potential solutions for evaluating the clinical impact of interventions. A more horizontal medical pedagogy seems better suited to teach these sensitive issues.Keywords: Health inequities, Medical education, Implicit bias, Racism

Introduction

Health inequalities are one of the major issues in contemporary medicine. In the United States (U.S.), structural racism has been identified as one of the causes of these disparities [1]. Racism is recognized as a comprehensive health determinant correlated with a deterioration of physical and mental health [2]. The history of American medicine is marked by events demonstrating racism perpetuated by medical universities and health authorities themselves, such as the Tuskegee experiment, halted in 1972. During this study, the U.S. Public Health Service observed the progression of syphilis in untreated black men over a 40-year period without informing them of the diagnosis or lack of treatment [3].

In 2001, the American Institute of Medicine, now known as the National Academy of Medicine, was commissioned by the U.S. Congress to “assess disparities in the kinds and quality of healthcare received by U.S. racial and ethnic minorities and nonminorities.” In its report, a series of measures to combat health inequalities are recommended. Some include recommendations on physician training, reflecting significant awareness by health authorities [4].

More recently, in the United States, the COVID-19 crisis, which disproportionately affected minority communities, exacted a heavy toll in terms of mortality [5]. Additionally, the murder of George Floyd brought these deeply rooted discriminatory issues in American society to the forefront, prompting significant civil society mobilization to end these discriminations, mobilization that has spread globally as the event has gone global. The field of health was not spared, with numerous calls to address disparities in access to care resonating, including within medical faculties, echoing the initial recommendations issued two decades earlier by the Institute of Medicine.

Health inequalities stemming from presumed racial prejudices are not exclusive to the United States and affect numerous healthcare systems, including the French. An INSEE report revealed a “twice as significant increase in deaths for individuals born abroad compared to those born in France in March and April 2020,” at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2017, the case of Naomi Musenga, a young woman who was not taken seriously during her call to emergency services (SAMU) and died a few hours later, gained widespread attention and raised suspicions of discrimination and the so-called “Mediterranean syndrome.” Shortly before 2016, the sociological survey “Trajectoire et Origines” (TeO) highlighted differences in patient care based on skin color and origin [6].

During the French medical curriculum, education and training on dismantling racial prejudices in clinical practice are nearly nonexistent, and few scientific publications address the subject despite its current relevance and the imperative to break free from these inequalities.

The objective of this work is to analyze the scientific literature to shed light on existing conceptual and educational data regarding racial prejudices in medicine (see the research question below). We chose to conduct a scoping review, which is particularly suitable for this examination due to its broad exploratory nature, in this emerging field of study in France.

Materials and methods

We conducted a scoping review of the literature on racial biases in clinical settings and medical education, adhering to the JBI recommendations for scoping review writing and applying the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist. The protocol for this exploratory examination was deposited and registered with the Open Science Framework before the study’s commencement at the following address: https://osf.io/4w7ej/.

Hereafter, the identification of the research question and choice of methodology are outlined. The research question guiding this work was formulated as follows: “How does the literature inform us about the existence, within medical education for medical students, interns, residents, and faculty, of educational content on the subject of racial biases in healthcare?”

We opted for a scoping review due to the exploratory and descriptive nature of this type of study. Although the topic has been extensively addressed in North American academic circles, it remains underexplored and underpublished in the Francophone world.

The following databases were systematically consulted: PubMed, Web of Science, PsInfo, Cochrane, Cairn, and Thèses.fr. A literature search was performed using keywords and Boolean operators. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were extensively used in formulating our queries. For example, the following queries were used in PubMed: (“Racism“[Mesh] OR “Systemic Racism“[Mesh] OR “Antiracism“[Mesh] OR “Bias, Implicit“[Mesh] OR “racisme” OR “antiracisme”) AND (“Education, Medical“[Mesh] OR “formation médicale” OR “études médicales”). The article search was conducted during March 2023, and the query results were imported on March 20, 2023.

The selected articles met the following inclusion criteria: (i) were interventional studies or evaluations of an already implemented educational program, (ii) were within the context of medical education (medical studies), (iii) were addressing the fight against racial biases in clinical practice, and (iv) were published in French or English at any time between 2003 and 2023. The exploration period and inclusion criteria intentionally remained broad for this exploratory study, and articles on continuous education or paramedical studies were excluded because they focused on medical students and interns, the primary targets of our work at this stage. As recommended for exploratory studies, study quality was not used as an inclusion criterion.

After removing duplicates and using Rayyan software, two researchers (AM and ML) blindly assessed the eligibility of the extracted references, both at the title and abstract levels. After unblinding, any resulting disagreements were resolved through consensus between the two researchers. In Fig. 1, the flow diagram follows PRISMA recommendations, an international set of guidelines framing the publication of systematic literature reviews.

Systematic scoping review PRISMA 2020 flow chart

For the data extraction, a table containing relevant information on the topic was designed by two researchers (AM and ML). One researcher (AM) carefully read each included article and directly recorded the study characteristics and relevant data into predefined categories in an Excel data extraction form. This process ensured that the extraction was based on a detailed and thorough reading of the articles, in accordance with JBI recommendations. These categories included the study’s country, study type, type and purpose of the educational intervention, the targeted population and its size, main results, limitations, and the key concepts used or identified in the study.

The results were discussed by three researchers (AM, RR, ML) and the article was reviewed by all the co-authors (AM, RR, MRM, ML).

Results

In Fig. 1, the flow diagram illustrates the various stages of article selection, detailed below. This search yielded 748 results. After reviewing the titles and using Rayyan software, 103 duplicates were removed. Examination of the abstracts of the remaining 645 articles revealed that 37 met the eligibility criteria, while 608 articles were excluded based on the predefined relevance criteria. One article could not be located for full-text review, and of the remaining 36,8 articles were excluded after a thorough examination of the entire content. The reasons for exclusion are outlined in Fig. 1. This literature review included 28 articles.

The majority (86%) of the included studies originated from the U.S. No studies from France were identified, and no studies in the French language were included. Almost all the studies were observational studies, 39% were before-after studies, and none utilized a control group. The most common evaluation methods were self-assessments, and the majority of the studies were conducted in a single center. Although most related studies have targeted interventions for “undergraduate” students, corresponding to the first and second cycles of medical studies in France, several studies have focused their interventions on interns, residents, or faculty. Half of the included articles were published in a medical education journal, while the rest were distributed among general medical or specialty journals, with psychiatric journals being particularly represented.

Interventions varied and included lectures, courses or workshops, small group discussions, and cultural immersion programs, among others. The median number of participants in the studies was 59, and participants often self-selected. In 81% of the patients, the interventions were brief and one-time events.

As depicted in Fig. 2, the concepts of structural/institutionalized racism and levels of racism were most prominently featured in the studies, accounting for 71% of the cases. The concepts of cultural competence, cultural humility, critical race theory, empathy, and implicit racial biases were presented in descending order. The main concepts in question will be explored and defined in the next section to ensure a comprehensive understanding of these terms.

Discussion

Key concepts

Levels of racism: institutionalized, personally mediated, and internalized

The concepts most frequently highlighted in the studies are institutionalized racism (occurring within institutions), structural racism (synonymous with systemic racism, occurring on a broader societal scale) and racism. In 2000, the American physician and researcher Camara Jones, specialized in health inequalities and racism, published a groundbreaking article in which she outlined these concepts. Using a botanical allegory, she defined a conceptual framework that includes three levels of racism: institutionalized, personally mediated, and internalized [35].

Institutionalized racism is defined as “differential access to the goods, services, and opportunities of society by race,” and is “codified in our institutions of custom, practice, and law, so there need not be an identifiable perpetrator” and often manifests as “inaction in the face of need.” In a country such as the United States, the author argues that it perpetuates historical injustices and attributes to it “the association between socioeconomic status and race.”

Personally mediated racism, sometimes called interpersonal racism, is defined as " prejudice and discrimination, in which prejudice means differential assumptions about the abilities, motives, and intentions of others according to their race, and discrimination means differential actions toward others according to their race.” It can be " intentional as well as unintentional, and it includes acts of commission as well as acts of omission.” This level of racism is what people refer to when they speak of “racism.”

Internalized racism is defined as “acceptance by members of the stigmatized races of negative messages about their own abilities and intrinsic worth”, emerging through remarks, microaggressions, and lived racist experiences that lead individuals to adopt the racist viewpoint. Donna Bivens, an American academic, further elaborated on this concept in 2005, stating that " It is important to understand it as systemic because that makes it clear that it is not a problem simply of individuals. It is structural.” [36].

As evident from these studies, most authors overwhelmingly refer to institutionalized racism and much less to personally mediated or internalized racism. Regarding the healthcare system and medical education, it is interesting to focus on the “macro” aspects and demonstrate the systemic dimension to address issues more comprehensively and beyond a mere individual level.

The Concept of Race: combating disparities by rejecting racial taxonomy

Another concept frequently emphasized in these studies is race, which is challenging to define. Authors widely acknowledge race as a sociopolitical construct, and there is currently no argument supporting biological determinants of race. As Michael Yudell, an American expert in public health, medical ethics, and genomics, states, “phylogenetic and population genetic methods do not support a priori classifications of race, as expected for an interbreeding species like Homo sapiens. As a result, racial assumptions are not the biological guideposts some believe them to be, as commonly defined racial groups are genetically heterogeneous and lack clear-cut genetic boundaries.” He further added, “biological concepts of race in human genetic is problematic at best and harmful at worst” [37].

However, medical education still perpetuates the idea that individuals of different ethnicities are biologically distinct. For example, the CKD-EPI creatinine clearance formula includes a weighting factor for “African origin (black skin)”, a formula from 2009 that has been taught in medical faculties worldwide. This formula, published to address the limitations of previous formulas, attempts to include various criteria, but the “black skin” factor is at risk of approximation and scientific reductionism, not accounting for intraethnic complexity or environmental factors such as lifestyle and diet. According to Ritchie Witzig, an American physician who has published on the issue, “Despite modern proof that race is arbitrary biological fiction, racial taxons are still used widely in medical teaching, practice, and research.” [38].

Finally, according to Camara Jones, the race variable is ultimately a “social construct that precisely captures the impacts of racism,” making this variable interesting to study when addressing discrimination in the field of health.

One of the challenges of contemporary medicine is, therefore, to combat health disparities by providing optimal care to everyone, considering the particularities of each individual or human group, and promoting research on minorities, which are less studied [39], without falling into the trap of racial taxonomy.

Cultural competence: limits and the necessity of cultural humility

Numerous studies criticize the concept of “cultural competence”, which is widely used in medical education programs in the U.S., as recommended by the Liaison Committee on Medical Education and the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education following the 2001 report from the Academy of Medicine. As stated by Melanie Tervalon, an American professor in anthropology, history, and social medicine, “If we focus on acquiring knowledge about ‘other’ cultures and treat the concept of culture as static, we risk objectifying individuals whose appearance, language, national origin, religion, or sexual orientation differ from the majority, describing them in overly simplistic categorical terms of their character and behavior” [40]. The risk is thus the reification of the patient in a transcultural situation while overlooking issues of racism they may face.

Arno Kumagai, a Canadian medical education professor, suggested that in the field of health, there is less about the development of “competence” and more about a “critical awareness—a critical consciousness— of the self, others, and the world and a commitment to addressing issues of societal relevance in health care” [41]. The ultimate goal is the promotion of social justice, which refers to “the open acknowledgment of the dignity and autonomy of, and delivery of high-quality medical care to, all members of society, regardless of gender, race, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, language, geographic origin, or socioeconomic background” [41]. Acquiring cultural competence alone would not suffice to fulfill this mission and carries the risk of reinforcing stereotypes against minority groups.

The concept of cultural humility, on the other hand, proposes “a commitment and active engagement in a lifelong process that individuals enter into on an ongoing basis with patients, communities, colleagues, and with themselves”, involving a continual commitment to “self-reflection and self-critique as lifelong learners and reflective practitioners” [42]. This process inherently involves the humility of the learner. It is interesting to note that for individuals to identify and question their own patterns, they need to reflect on their own identities and cultures. According to Melanie Tervalon, “Recognition and respect for others’ cultural priorities and practices is facilitated by such initial and ongoing processes that engender self-knowledge” [42].

According to the included studies, cultural humility is frequently preferred over cultural competence. From our perspective, it is evident that humility, self-reflection, and self-critique are at the core of this field of study, as they invite a reconsideration of individual or collective medical practices.

Note that the concept of white supremacy, expounded by U.S. and South African physicians in 2024 in a medical education journal [43], was cited only minimally in the scoping review articles. This article insists that racial differences in treatment and outcomes are pervasive in teaching and, at least in part, linked to the racial hierarchy we see in the way disease prevalence and causation are taught across racial groups.

Situation in France: between the prohibition of ethnic statistics and objectified discrimination

In France, the principle of universalism prevails, making the Republic “one and indivisible.” It advocates for equality and does not distinguish individuals. Based on this principle, laws rigorously regulate the production of statistical data based on race or ethnicity. However, the issue arises regarding the approach to resolving the problem of discrimination based on the presumed origin of users within the healthcare systems in France. For instance, the “Mediterranean syndrome,” whose predecessor, “North African syndrome”, was already criticized in 1952 by Frantz Fanon, a Martinican psychiatrist [44], is still widely present in the French medical collective unconscious [45]. The French medical culture, shaped by a long history sometimes tinged with colonial history [46], must learn to recognize its biases and their consequences: discriminations, with the practical corollary being differentiated care. To quantify the phenomenon and address it in education, several researchers [47] in France advocate for an easier and less restricted use of ethnic statistics than is currently allowed.

The debate exists and sometimes appears dichotomously, even though recognized large-scale studies such as “Trajectoires et Origines” (TeO) [6] have been conducted, demonstrating that, at the same social position, discriminations based on appearance and origin exist. François Héran, a sociologist, anthropologist, demographer, and professor at the Collège de France, stated in a 2020 opinion piece in Le Monde: “Let us stop opposing republican principles to ethnic statistics. What is the use of brandishing our universalist ideals if we refuse to measure the gap that separates them from reality? Far from undermining the principle of equal treatment, ethnic statistics take it at its word, provided that it is not confused with ethnoracial statistics and not extended to management files. It is by facing realities that we can promote the ideals of the nation in a way other than in words” [48].

At the same time, it should be remembered that none of the articles in this study are French, given the paucity of teaching content on the issue of racial discrimination in French medical schools. The few courses that do exist are based on individual initiatives, and are by no means something globalized and included in curricula. One of the aims of this scoping review is to demonstrate the need for coordinated, collective action.

Design of studies, interventions, and evaluations: immersions and simulations to Counterimplicit biases?

In the design of these studies, several factors are noteworthy. The median number of participants was very low, and as a result, the results should be interpreted with caution. Participants were mostly self-selected, indicating awareness of the issue and raising suspicions of selection bias in most studies. Another element is the measurement of the clinical impact of programs, which should remain the ultimate goal of any intervention in the healthcare field but is never evaluated. We are not informed about criteria that would allow for a more nuanced detection of the effectiveness of interventions. Self-assessment provides much feedback. Evaluating clinical impact would likely require a large-scale study spanning a long period of time with complex yet highly interesting measures, for example, on the differences in impact according to gender, or according to previous experiences of racism. These experiences could also be a focus of attention when implementing educational content, which can potentially reactivate difficult or even traumatic memories.

These elements raise questions about the mismatch between the stated intention of health authorities to address the problem and the allocated resources. Globally, a recent consensus statement developed by an international group of medical educators described racism as “unacceptable and in breach of professional codes of medical practice” [49]. As shown in the introduction, in the U.S., many advocates call for the implementation of measures to combat racism, but actual measures are still challenging to implement, as evidenced by Ayana Jordan [50], a researcher, educator, and head of a Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) department at a major American university. In terms of interventions, “immersion” is used several times, either in real conditions or in virtual reality. The latter, for example, could promote empathy, defined as “the cognitive and emotional capacity to understand or feel another person’s experience from within that person’s frame of reference” [20]. Empathy could reduce implicit biases toward minority groups [51].

This aligns with recent advances in medical education. Virtual patients, medical teaching techniques that emerged in the early 1990s and were defined as “interactive computer simulations of real-life clinical scenarios for training, teaching, or medical assessment purposes,” have more recently been applied in transcultural psychiatry, with encouraging results, especially regarding practitioners’ confidence in provided care, clinical care, and diagnostic evaluation [52]. The virtual patient method has been described as effective at promoting empathy by some studies [53]. Although not explicitly mentioned in the selected studies, this is closely related, suggesting the hypothesis of effectiveness in terms of anti-racist medical education. Moreover, this method seems interesting for remote assessment of the clinical impact of an intervention, which seems complex in real-world conditions.

Interestingly, interventions in small groups, with facilitators promoting exchanges, are particularly appreciated by participants. This provides a sense of security in the exchange, which is necessary for addressing the intimate and collective theme of racial discrimination in health. These “safe places” within academic institutions constitute an alternative form to traditional teaching methods, which were still the norm until recently throughout medical studies, such as lectures in amphitheaters that were much more “vertical” in the way knowledge was delivered. Current medical education places much more emphasis on more horizontal teaching, using simulation and debriefing in small groups guided by the principles of benevolence, confidentiality, and learning from mistakes [54]. The studies indicate that facilitated exchange, in a safe space without judgment, allows for genuine engagement in learning on topics that emotionally and socially involve students.

A test frequently used in assessing intervention impact, especially in before-and-after studies, is the Implicit Association Test (IAT), which was developed by Harvard and Virginia universities and measures implicit preferences by bypassing conscious processing. Implicit biases originate from the fact that “Humans base their perceptions of reality on received information and experiences reinforced until they become automatic. This ability makes human decision-making efficient and likely provided an evolutionary advantage.” [55]. Implicit biases, or unconscious biases, develop early in childhood following repeated reinforcement of stereotypes and often differ significantly from explicit or conscious beliefs [56]. Physicians are not exempt, and “uncertainty and time pressure surrounding the diagnostic process may promote reliance on stereotypes for efficient decision-making” [55]. Several studies have provided " compelling data suggest that physicians make treatment decisions that divide patients with similar clinical presentations along lines of race or ethnicity” [55], despite the desire to provide equal care to all. Implicit biases are among the many factors that can create disparities in healthcare. Some researchers suggest sensitizing practitioners to their susceptibility to implicit biases to reduce their impact [57]. This finding promotes the idea that these issues should be discussed and explored during medical education.

Conclusion

This scoping review represents an initial exploratory phase aimed at initiating an assessment of various anti-racist pedagogical practices in medical faculties. The concepts emerging from these studies are consistent with each other, but no specific pedagogical strategy or tool emerges from these studies, which are almost all North American.

As most of the studies are North American, this also highlights the fact that no studies come from the Global South, even though pedagogy on combating racial discrimination could be rooted in pedagogies such as that of Brazilian Paulo Freire proposed by Razack et al. [43], to develop critical consciousness.

The complexity of the issue requires resources commensurate with the stakes, which are significant because they touch on the very foundations of medical practice and its ethics.

Among these issues, one also concerns the students themselves, some of whom may be confronted individually with racism at university or hospital. One of the challenges of this work is to equip them to deal with it, and to protect them from it by raising the awareness of every member of the institutions.

The goal is to continue this work in a larger-scale study to refine the literature review by adding to the psychological literature. Our objective is to create a tool to make the teaching of this issue relevant and tailored to the French context.
