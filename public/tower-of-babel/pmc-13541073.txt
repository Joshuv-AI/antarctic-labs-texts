Nanofiber-coated natural fibers for mechanical and durability improvement of sustainable cementitious systems
Authors: Yang Zhao, Hongxin Nie, Farzaneh Shamsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A hybrid reinforcement system based on rhubarb fiber (RF) and dendritic fibrous nanosilica (DFNS) was developed and incorporated into foamed concrete to examine its influence on structural performance. The study focuses on both mechanical properties and durability under impact loading as well as chemically aggressive exposure conditions. Rhubarb fiber, a sustainable cellulose rich material, was chemically modified using NaOH treatment and subsequently functionalized with DFNS to enhance its interfacial bonding and dispersion within the cement matrix. Structural and chemical transformations of the modified fiber were characterized using TGA, FTIR, and SEM analyses. The experimental findings indicate that incorporating the RF/DFNS hybrid reinforcement markedly enhances the impact resistance and energy dissipation capacity of the composite, improves its resistance to crack propagation, increases thermal stability, and effectively reduces shrinkage related deterioration. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of the bio based fiber and nanostructured DFNS contribute to enhanced impact resistance and extended service life of foamed concrete, promoting an eco friendly approach to advanced construction material design. These findings highlight the promise of integrating natural fibers with nanomaterials to develop sustainable, high performance cementitious systems. The hybrid reinforcement system enhanced both the mechanical strength and durability of the foamed concrete. In addition, the incorporation of bio based rhubarb fibers provides a potential pathway toward more sustainable reinforcement strategies, particularly through the use of renewable lignocellulosic resources. However, the overall environmental benefits of the RF/DFNS system require further verification through quantitative life cycle assessment.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of natural fibers in cement based composites has drawn considerable attention due to their environmental friendliness, cost effectiveness, and mechanical benefits. Rhubarb fiber, derived from the stalks of the Rheum plant, is a cellulose rich material that demonstrates good tensile strength, biodegradability, and effective bonding with the cementitious matrix, making it a promising candidate for reinforcing lightweight concrete.1,2 The incorporation of such natural fibers into foamed concrete improves properties such as crack resistance, toughness, energy absorption, and durability under various environmental loading conditions.3 The unique morphology of rhubarb fiber enables effective crack bridging within the cementitious matrix, thereby limiting shrinkage related deterioration and improving the long term durability of the composite.4 Moreover, when combined with advanced nanomaterials, the synergistic effect further enhances the mechanical performance and thermal stability of the composite system.5 This eco conscious approach not only promotes sustainability in construction but also contributes to the development of high performance building materials. The use of bio based rhubarb fiber as an alternative reinforcement may reduce reliance on conventional synthetic fibers and offers potential environmental advantages associated with the utilization of renewable lignocellulosic resources. Moreover, the utilization of plant derived RF is consistent with broader efforts to incorporate renewable bio based resources into cementitious composites. The use of natural RF and DFNS as hybrid reinforcement agents in foamed concrete offers substantial environmental benefits in line with sustainable construction objectives.6,7

Dendritic fibrous nanosilica possesses a distinctive radially oriented fibrous architecture that provides a high accessible surface area and abundant surface silanol (Si–OH) groups.8–10 Unlike conventional silica particles, its open dendritic morphology facilitates surface accessibility and provides numerous sites for interaction with surrounding phases.11 In cementitious systems, these characteristics are particularly relevant because the silica rich surface can promote interactions with cement hydration products, while the nanostructured morphology can contribute to particle packing, pore refinement, and modification of the interfacial region.12,13 When deposited onto lignocellulosic fibers, DFNS can also increase surface roughness and provide additional interaction sites at the fiber–cement interface, potentially improving mechanical interlocking and interfacial compatibility.14 These physicochemical characteristics make DFNS a suitable nanostructured modifier for developing hybrid natural fiber reinforced cementitious composites.15,16

Owing to its widespread availability, adaptability, and cost effectiveness, concrete continues to be the most extensively used construction material in modern infrastructure.17,18 Despite its widespread use, conventional concrete exhibits several inherent drawbacks, including brittle behavior, relatively high density, and vulnerability to deterioration caused by aggressive environmental agents such as sulfates, carbon dioxide, and chloride ions.19 The growing emphasis on sustainable construction has intensified the search for lightweight cementitious materials capable of lowering cement consumption without compromising mechanical properties or long term durability.20–28 Foamed concrete has emerged as an attractive lightweight cementitious material owing to its ease of casting, excellent thermal insulation, low density, and favorable acoustic performance. Despite its advantages, foamed concrete often suffers from poor tensile strength, increased porosity, and limited crack resistance, which hinder its structural applications.29 Therefore, advanced reinforcement strategies are required to mitigate these limitations and unlock its full potential.30–34

From a materials chemistry perspective, the key feature of the proposed RF/DFNS system is the integration of the hydroxyl rich surface of alkali treated lignocellulosic fibers with silanol rich DFNS, creating a chemically interactive bio–nano interface. Unlike conventional natural fiber or nanosilica modified cementitious systems, this hybrid strategy combines the crack bridging capability of renewable RF with the high specific surface area and dendritic architecture of DFNS. Such integration is expected to promote fiber–matrix interfacial interactions, hydration related microstructural development, and refinement of the pore network. Accordingly, this study establishes relationships between RF surface functionalization, interfacial characteristics, microstructural evolution, and the resulting mechanical and durability performance of foamed concrete. The hybrid system is comprehensively evaluated in terms of mechanical strength, impact and crack resistance, shrinkage, and resistance to aggressive environments, particularly sulfuric acid exposure and chloride ingress. Chloride transport modeling is further employed to relate the observed durability response to changes in the transport characteristics of the cementitious matrix. By combining a renewable lignocellulosic reinforcement with nanostructured silica, the proposed approach provides a materials chemistry based strategy for developing lightweight cementitious composites with improved performance and potential sustainability benefits.

Experimental

Materials

Ordinary Portland cement (Type PO 52.5), supplied by Fars Cement Company (Iran), served as the primary binder throughout this investigation. Its oxide composition, determined by X-ray fluorescence (XRF), is presented in Table 1. Standard silica sand complying with the required grading specifications was used as the fine aggregate, and its physical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. The reinforcing phase consisted of rhubarb fibers (RF) obtained from the stalks of Rheum spp. harvested in Neyshabur, Iran. After harvesting, the stalks were naturally air-dried and subsequently cut into short fibers of nearly uniform dimensions. Prior to incorporation into the cementitious matrix, the fibers underwent a chemical surface modification to improve their compatibility with cement hydration products. The treatment involved immersion in a 4 wt% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution at 60 °C for 6 h, which removed part of the hemicellulose and lignin, resulting in a rougher surface and greater exposure of cellulose hydroxyl groups. The alkali-treated fibers were repeatedly rinsed with distilled water until a neutral pH was reached and then oven-dried at 80 °C. To improve fiber dispersion and strengthen the fiber–matrix interface, the treated RF was dispersed in deionized water using ultrasonic agitation before being functionalized with DFNS. Surface modification was achieved by immersing the fibers in a DFNS colloidal suspension under simultaneous magnetic stirring and ultrasonication, enabling nanosilica particles to anchor onto the fiber surface. The resulting RF/DFNS hybrid reinforcement was finally sieved to obtain a uniform fiber length distribution (Fig. 1) before being incorporated into the foamed concrete mixtures for subsequent mechanical and durability evaluation.

Synthesis of DFNS

In the first step, 2.5 g of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) was introduced into an organic mixture containing 20 mL of cyclohexane and 2.5 mL of 1-pentanol. The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature until a clear and uniform precursor solution was obtained. Separately, 2.6 g of cetylpyridinium bromide (CPB) and 1.0 g of urea were dissolved in 35 mL of methanol under continuous stirring to form a homogeneous surfactant-containing phase. The CPB–urea solution was subsequently added dropwise to the TEOS-containing organic phase under vigorous agitation. After complete addition, stirring was continued for 90 min to establish a stable microemulsion and promote uniform distribution of the silica precursor within the surfactant assemblies. The resulting mixture was then transferred to a Teflon lined stainless steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 150 °C for 3 h. Under these conditions, controlled hydrolysis and condensation of TEOS occurred concurrently with surfactant directed growth, leading to the development of the characteristic radially oriented fibrous silica architecture. Following hydrothermal treatment, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The white solid product was recovered by centrifugation and repeatedly washed with deionized water and acetone to remove residual surfactant, solvent, and unreacted precursor species. The purified product was then dried overnight under ambient conditions.

Preparation of the RF

Fresh rhubarb stalks were collected, washed thoroughly to remove surface dust and impurities, and then cut into 2–3 cm segments. The stalks were subsequently dried at ambient temperature for 48 hours, followed by oven drying at 80 °C for 12 hours to reduce moisture content and inhibit microbial degradation. To enhance fiber matrix adhesion and modify surface characteristics, a chemical pretreatment process was conducted. The dried stalks were soaked in a 4 wt% sodium hydroxide solution at 60 °C for 6 hours. This process partially eliminated lignin and hemicellulose, resulting in greater exposure of cellulose microfibrils and improved adhesion to the cementitious matrix. After pretreatment, the rhubarb segments were rinsed multiple times with distilled water until a neutral pH was achieved. A mechanical stirring process was applied to initiate preliminary defibrillation. To further refine the fibrous structure and enhance homogeneity, the alkali treated stalks were subjected to ultrasonic dispersion in deionized water for 30 minutes. This process promoted the separation of individual cellulose fibers and prevented agglomeration.

Surface functionalization with DFNS

Subsequently, the treated rhubarb fibers were surface-functionalized with DFNS to produce an RF/DFNS hybrid reinforcement. The DFNS suspension was prepared in deionized water, and the extracted rhubarb fibers were immersed in the suspension and stirred magnetically for 2 hours, followed by 30 minutes of ultrasonic treatment to ensure uniform DFNS deposition on the fiber surfaces. This functionalization process aimed to improve the dispersion of the fibers within the cement matrix and enhance interfacial bonding due to the porous nature and high surface area of DFNS. After surface modification, the hybrid fibers were oven dried again at 70 °C and sieved to ensure a uniform length distribution. The final fiber properties, including average diameter and length, are presented in Table 1.

Mix design and sample preparation

Foamed concrete mixtures were produced with a target dry density of 1000 ± 50 kg m−3 using a protein-based foaming agent. The mixing procedure was carried out in a planetary mixer by first homogenizing the cement and fine aggregate, followed by the gradual addition of the mixing water together with the preformed foam. The RF/DFNS hybrid reinforcement was introduced during the final mixing stage to promote its uniform distribution throughout the cementitious matrix. The experimental program included an unreinforced control mixture and foamed concrete mixtures containing either RF or RF/DFNS at reinforcement dosages of 1, 2, 3, and 4 wt% relative to the cement mass. The same dosage levels were adopted for both reinforcement systems to enable a direct comparison between untreated RF and DFNS-functionalized RF. After mixing, the fresh material was cast into 100 × 100 × 400 mm prismatic molds and 100 × 200 mm cylindrical molds for the subsequent mechanical and durability tests. The specimens were kept in the molds for 24 h, after which they were demolded and transferred to a curing chamber maintained at 23 ± 2 °C and 95% relative humidity for 28 days. To ensure the statistical reliability of the experimental program, three identical specimens were tested for each mixture, and all reported results are presented as the mean value together with the corresponding standard deviation.

Acid resistance test

Acid resistance test

After 28 days of curing, three specimens from each mixture were immersed in a 5 wt% H2SO4 solution maintained at 23 ± 2 °C. The specimens were exposed for 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 weeks, after which they were removed, rinsed with deionized water, surface dried under identical conditions, and weighed. The residual compressive strength was subsequently determined using three replicate specimens. Mass retention and residual compressive strength were calculated relative to the corresponding values prior to acid exposure.

Results and discussion

The initial phase of this study involved the preparation and chemical pretreatment of rhubarb fibers (RF) using the procedures outlined earlier. These pretreated fibers were subsequently integrated with DFNS to form a hybrid RF/DFNS bio-nanocomposite. The DFNS particles were immobilized onto the surface of the RF, facilitating the development of a well dispersed and functionalized reinforcement system. The SEM micrographs in Fig. 2 reveal the surface morphology of DFNS, RF and RF/DFNS. As depicted in Fig. 2a, the DFNS sample exhibits parapet like structures with consistent proportions and dendrimer filaments with thicknesses ranging from 45 to 50 nm, arranged in three dimensions to form the boundaries. Also, the SEM images provided crucial insight into the microstructural evolution of the RF before and after functionalization with DFNS. In its untreated state, the RF exhibited a relatively compact, dense internal structure with limited visible porosity. The fibers appeared to be bundled, with smooth surfaces and minimal nano scale features, reflecting their native, lignocellulosic nature (Fig. 2b). Upon surface modification with DFNS, a significant transformation in fiber morphology was observed. The DFNS particles, characterized by their three dimensional, radially oriented fibrous branches and exceptionally high surface area, were uniformly anchored onto the RF surfaces. The SEM images revealed that DFNS formed a porous network on the fiber exterior, enhancing the roughness and creating a nanostructured shell around each fiber. This porous DFNS layer facilitates stronger mechanical interlocking and enables more effective stress transfer between the fiber and the surrounding cement matrix. Additionally, the open architecture of DFNS provides numerous active sites for interaction with cement hydration products, potentially improving the composite's microstructural integrity and crack resistance (Fig. 2c). This uniform coverage and intimate contact between DFNS and RF not only prevent nanoparticle aggregation but also ensure that the functionalized fibers maintain high dispersion stability within the cement paste an essential factor for consistent mechanical performance. The EDS spectrum of the RF/DFNS hybrid confirms the presence of C, O, and Si, corresponding to the lignocellulosic rhubarb fiber and the deposited dendritic fibrous nanosilica, respectively. The appearance of the Si peak provides direct evidence for the successful surface functionalization of RF with DFNS. The predominance of C and O is expected because the analyzed specimen consists primarily of cellulose-based fibers coated with a thin nanosilica layer (Fig. 3).

The FTIR spectrum of the RF/DFNS hybrid exhibited distinct spectral changes compared with untreated rhubarb fiber, indicating successful surface functionalization. The most prominent feature was the emergence of a new absorption band at approximately 1093 cm−1, assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si bonds, confirming the presence of DFNS on the fiber surface. From a materials chemistry perspective, the hydroxyl rich lignocellulosic surface provides active interaction sites for the silanol groups of DFNS, thereby creating a chemically interactive bio–nano interface rather than a solely physical nanoparticle coating. Together, the FTIR and TEM analyses clearly demonstrate that the DFNS functionalization process not only changes the physical texture of rhubarb fibers but also chemically modifies their surface. This dual enhancement leads to improved bonding with the cement matrix, reduced interfacial gaps, and superior mechanical load distribution in the final composite. These effects are critical for applications requiring high durability and impact resistance, especially in lightweight or eco friendly concrete formulations.

The impact test results presented in Fig. 5a and b reveal that incorporating RF and RF/DFNS reinforcement significantly increases the service impact energy of foamed concrete, with the DFNS functionalized fibers exhibiting the greatest improvement. The control specimens without reinforcement exhibit the lowest impact energy absorption, reflecting their inherent brittleness and low capacity to resist impact induced cracking. The incorporation of 1–2% RF significantly improves the impact resistance by effectively bridging microcracks and dissipating energy through fiber pull out mechanisms, highlighting the beneficial role of chemically treated natural fibers under dynamic loading. The beneficial effect became more evident at RF/DFNS dosages of 3–4 wt%, where the impact energy increased by approximately 170% relative to the control mixture, reaching a maximum value of about 496 J at 4 wt% reinforcement. This superior performance is attributed to the synergistic effect of DFNS nanoparticles, which enhance fiber dispersion, strengthen the fiber matrix interfacial bonding, and introduce nanoscale confinement that promotes uniform energy redistribution during impact. The open, porous morphology of DFNS not only increases surface roughness but also facilitates mechanical interlocking with the cementitious matrix, collectively leading to enhanced crack resistance and toughness. Fig. 5c further compares the maximum impact energy of slabs with varying RF and RF/DFNS contents (0–4%). The data indicate that while both reinforcements increase impact absorption, the hybrid RF/DFNS system exhibits superior performance, with the slab containing 4% RF/DFNS achieving the highest impact energy of 1194 J. This finding underscores the crucial role of nanostructured coatings in reinforcing the composite, improving load transfer efficiency, and enhancing resistance to dynamic loading beyond what is attainable with RF alone. The variation in impact energy with nanoparticle content, as shown in Fig. 5d, reveals a significant improvement within the 1–4% loading range. This enhancement stems from the combined mechanisms of flexible RF fibers acting as crack bridging agents and DFNS nanoparticles filling microvoids and refining the matrix microstructure. These effects increase tensile strength and toughness, enabling the composite to absorb greater impact energy. However, beyond 4%, the benefits plateau or slightly diminish, possibly due to nanoparticle agglomeration and fiber clustering, which may induce stress concentrations. These observations align with previous research emphasizing the necessity of optimized hybrid reinforcement proportions for maximum durability and impact resistance. Fig. 5e highlights a comparison of service crack resistance between reinforced and control specimens. All reinforced samples demonstrate marked improvements, with the specimen containing 4% RF/DFNS showing the highest resistance of 698 J. This enhanced performance is attributed to the combined action of fibrous reinforcement providing energy dissipation through crack bridging and the DFNS nanoparticles enhancing matrix densification and microstructural refinement. These complementary mechanisms corroborate prior findings that hybrid reinforcements outperform single component systems by offering more effective crack arrest and energy absorption capabilities. The results clearly indicate that incorporating optimized amounts of RF/DFNS nanoparticles substantially improves the longevity and resilience of foamed concrete structures under mechanical and environmental stresses. Crack resistance, a critical factor in durability, shows a consistent improvement across all specimens as RF and RF/DFNS contents increase (Fig. 5f). Notably, specimens with higher percentages of RF/DFNS achieve peak crack resistance, confirming the synergistic reinforcement effect. The data also indicate that boundary conditions significantly influence crack resistance; specimens with increased numbers of supports exhibit superior resistance by promoting more uniform stress distribution and limiting localized deformation. This interplay between reinforcement type and structural boundary conditions suggests practical design considerations for foamed concrete elements subjected to service loads.

As expected, ultimate crack resistance values were consistently higher than their service level counterparts, reflecting the material's ability to sustain greater loads before failure. The specimen containing 4 wt% RF/DFNS exhibited the highest ultimate crack resistance, reaching 1198 N mm−2 representing nearly a six fold increase compared to the unreinforced control. This remarkable improvement highlights the synergistic effects of natural fiber reinforcement combined with the nanostructured architecture of DFNS. The three dimensional fibrous structure of DFNS improves fiber matrix interfacial adhesion and enhances crack bridging. Meanwhile, the flexible RF fibers dissipate impact energy and redistribute stresses. As a result, crack initiation and propagation are delayed, leading to a tougher cementitious matrix. Fig. 6b presents the percentage variation in ultimate crack resistance under different support conditions for foamed concrete reinforced with varying contents of RF and RF/DFNS. The observed differences in crack resistance are primarily attributed to the ability of the fibrous RF/DFNS network to arrest microcracks in their early stages and to maintain crack stability as damage progresses. The nanostructured DFNS coating on the rhubarb fibers enhances their crack bridging efficiency by improving interfacial bonding and promoting stress transfer between the fiber and matrix. Additionally, the variation in boundary conditions influenced the manner in which impact energy was dissipated and how failure zones propagated, resulting in distinct mechanical responses. These findings emphasize the critical interplay between reinforcement type and structural constraints in determining the ultimate performance of lightweight concrete under extreme loading. Fig. 6c demonstrates that the inclusion of up to 4% RF/DFNS in foamed concrete results in an approximate 19% reduction in the average crack length formed under impact loading in slab specimens. This reduction remains relatively stable even with an additional 4% increase in RF/DFNS content in simply supported slabs. These observations suggest that the RF/DFNS hybrid bio nanocomposite effectively limits crack propagation length, enhancing the material's resistance to fracture under dynamic loading. However, the crack width does not exhibit a significant change with increasing RF/DFNS content (as shown in Fig. 6d), indicating that while the hybrid reinforcement substantially controls crack extension, its effect on crack opening size is comparatively limited. In contrast, Fig. 6d illustrates that the crack width remains nearly constant at approximately 1.8 mm across all RF/DFNS content levels, indicating that while the hybrid reinforcement effectively reduces crack length, it does not significantly affect the crack opening size.

Fig. 7a depicts the variation of the crack resistance ratio as a function of the volume fraction of RF/DFNS under both service and ultimate failure conditions across different support configurations. This ratio reflects the material's ability to resist cracking relative to its compressive strength. It is evident that increasing the RF/DFNS content, along with a higher number of supports, significantly enhances both the service level and ultimate crack resistance ratios, demonstrating the combined effect of hybrid reinforcement and structural constraints on improving durability. Drying shrinkage in concrete, caused by moisture loss, leads to volumetric contraction and is a primary contributor to crack initiation. The cementitious matrix containing RF/DFNS exhibits viscoelastic behavior, suggesting that the volume changes during drying are governed by a combination of viscous flow and elastic deformation mechanisms. This dual response helps mitigate shrinkage induced cracking, thereby enhancing the durability of the composite.

The influence of RF/DFNS incorporation on the compressive strength of lightweight foamed concrete is presented in Fig. 8a. Several factors contribute to this behavior. Increasing the RF/DFNS content enlarges the overall surface area within the concrete mixture, which requires more binder and a higher water to cement ratio. These modifications may reduce the compressive strength. Furthermore, the interaction among RF/DFNS particles restricts the movement of cement paste, creating localized stress concentrations that can lead to crushing of RF/DFNS particles. Excessive RF/DFNS also tends to agglomerate, causing void formation and weakening the bond between the cement matrix and the reinforcement. At higher dosages, reduced workability hinders uniform dispersion and proper orientation of RF/DFNS within the matrix, ultimately lowering compressive strength and diminishing load transfer efficiency. Although an increase in RF/DFNS content beyond the optimum level leads to a marginal decrease in compressive strength, this reduction does not necessarily preclude structural applicability. For lightweight and non load bearing applications, such as wall panels, insulation blocks, and prefabricated façade elements, the achieved compressive strengths remain well above the minimum thresholds (3–6 MPa) specified in international standards for structural grade foamed concrete. However, for higher load bearing components, optimizing the hybrid dosage to balance ductility and strength is critical. Future design strategies could involve combining RF/DFNS with supplementary cementitious materials or adopting hybrid reinforcement systems to maintain structural integrity while maximizing sustainability benefits. These findings are consistent with prior studies reporting compressive strength reduction with increased RF/DFNS content. Fig. 8b presents the drying shrinkage test results for concrete containing various weight ratios of RF/DFNS. As shown, shrinkage increases progressively over time in all mixtures. The control sample exhibits the highest moisture loss, resulting in the greatest shrinkage. Incorporation of RF/DFNS notably reduces drying shrinkage in the concrete. This improvement is attributed to the ability of RF/DFNS to dissipate tensile energy generated during shrinkage. Energy absorption occurs at the interface between RF/DFNS particles and the concrete matrix, transferring stresses into the surrounding material and thus alleviating excessive tensile stresses that could otherwise cause cracking. Moreover, when RF/DFNS is well dispersed within the cement paste, its high surface energy enhances the nucleation of hydration products, promoting the hydration process and further limiting shrinkage. Although the 4 wt% RF/DFNS mixture produced the highest impact and crack-resistance values, the 2–3 wt% range provided a more balanced overall performance when mechanical strength, durability, and workability were considered simultaneously. Within this range, the foamed concrete exhibited marked improvements in impact resistance, reduced shrinkage, and enhanced acid and chloride resistance, while maintaining adequate compressive strength for structural grade lightweight concrete. At higher dosages (>3 wt%), the marginal durability gains were accompanied by reduced workability and minor strength loss due to agglomeration and non uniform dispersion. Therefore, the 2–3 wt% range is recommended as the optimum hybrid reinforcement dosage to achieve a balanced combination of strength, ductility, and long term durability.

Fig. 9a depicts the influence of varying weight ratios of RF and its functionalized nanostructured counterpart (RF/DFNS) on the porosity of foamed concrete. The integration of these hybrid bio based reinforcements resulted in a noticeable reduction in porosity, with an average decrease of approximately 14% observed across optimized formulations. By day 30, specimens containing 4% RF/DFNS exhibited porosity levels of nearly 35%, compared to 47% in the unreinforced control sample. This marked reduction can be attributed to the dual action of the natural fiber and the high surface area DFNS nanoparticles. The porous yet highly structured morphology of DFNS enables it to act as a nanofiller, effectively occupying microvoids and refining the cement matrix. Simultaneously, RF serves as a bridging phase, promoting more homogeneous hydration and minimizing the formation of interconnected pores. The fibrous network introduced by RF enhances moisture retention and creates nucleation sites for hydration products, thus accelerating the development of a denser microstructure. Together, RF and DFNS improve particle packing, reduce pore interconnectivity, and promote better adhesion at the fiber matrix interface. This microstructural refinement plays a pivotal role in enhancing mechanical integrity and long term durability. The combination of cellulose-based fibers and DFNS provides a multiscale reinforcement mechanism. RF bridges macroscopic shrinkage cracks, whereas DFNS refines the pore structure and fills microscopic voids. The combined action of RF and DFNS produces a denser and more integrated cementitious microstructure, leading to enhanced matrix cohesion, reduced permeability, and improved resistance to chemically aggressive environments, including sulfuric acid and sulfate-rich media. The durability performance of the reinforced foamed concrete was assessed by exposing the specimens to sulfuric acid (H2SO4) for periods of 5, 10, 15, and 20 weeks. The corresponding changes in durability characteristics for mixtures containing RF and RF/DFNS are presented in Fig. 9b. The results highlight the relative acid resistance of different formulations, revealing a clear advantage for specimens incorporating RF/DFNS over both the control and RF only samples. After three weeks of immersion, only the RF/DFNS reinforced concrete demonstrated measurable resistance to acid attack, which is likely due to a combination of nanoconfinement effects from DFNS and fiber induced crack bridging, which limited the ingress of aggressive ions. Moreover, the reaction byproducts of sulfuric acid with calcium hydroxide, such as calcium sulfate, may have contributed to partial void filling within the matrix an effect more pronounced in DFNS modified systems due to their refined pore structure and higher density of active sites. By the 10 weeks mark, while the compressive strength of unreinforced and RF only specimens had notably declined due to sustained acid degradation, the RF/DFNS based composites retained superior structural integrity. This can be attributed to the robust interfacial bonding and enhanced matrix densification imparted by the hybrid reinforcement. DFNS nanoparticles likely delayed acid diffusion through gel like barrier formation and enhanced resistance to leaching, thereby protecting the cementitious matrix. At the 20 weeks exposure point, all specimens exhibited a decline in compressive strength; however, the decrease was significantly less severe in the RF/DFNS group. Their superior acid resistance underscores the effectiveness of combining naturally derived fibers with engineered nanostructures to create a multi barrier defense system against chemical attack. Notably, specimens reinforced with 4 wt% RF/DFNS exhibited the least deterioration, confirming the critical role of hybrid reinforcement in prolonging service life and maintaining performance under highly aggressive environmental conditions.

Fig. 10 presents the results of ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests conducted on concrete mixtures incorporating RF and RF/DFNS. Compared to the control sample, the reinforced concretes particularly those with RF/DFNS exhibited notably higher ultrasonic wave velocities, signifying improved internal density and reduced porosity. This enhancement indicates that the hybrid reinforcement system not only mitigates the formation of voids but also contributes to more efficient stress wave propagation through the cementitious matrix. The observed increase in wave velocity is closely aligned with the results from splitting tensile strength tests, where RF/DFNS reinforced samples also outperformed both the control and RF only specimens. This correlation underscores the beneficial role of RF/DFNS in developing a tightly packed and mechanically cohesive matrix, owing to its ability to bridge microcracks and introduce nanoscale roughness at the fiber matrix interface. The fibrous architecture of RF enhances energy dissipation and crack arresting, while DFNS particles with their highly porous and dendritic morphology act as nano fillers, penetrating microvoids and anchoring hydration products more effectively. This dual mechanism promotes a homogeneous microstructure, which translates to increased wave propagation speed and greater resistance to internal flaw development. Moreover, extended durability assessments conducted after 25 weeks of exposure to sulfate rich acidic environments revealed that specimens containing RF/DFNS sustained minimal mass loss, with the lowest recorded degradation being approximately 1.9%, a significant improvement over the control. This resistance is largely attributed to the dense interfacial zones created by DFNS around the fibers, which inhibit ion transport and chemical attack. The interaction between sulfate ions and the reactive components of the matrix was effectively buffered by the hybrid reinforcement, confirming the chemical shielding capacity of the RF/DFNS system. These findings highlight the potential of combining natural plant based fibers with advanced silica nanostructures to engineer concrete composites that exhibit both enhanced mechanical performance and superior durability under aggressive environmental conditions. Fig. 11 shows that concrete samples reinforced with RF/DFNS exhibited significantly lower weight loss under sulfuric acid exposure compared to control and RF only samples. Notably, after 10 weeks, the RF/DFNS specimens showed a slight increase in compressive strength, likely due to acid reaction byproducts filling internal voids. This trend continued through 15 weeks, indicating improved matrix densification and resistance to degradation. Results reveal that while the incorporation of RF led to a flexural strength close to that of the control specimen, the addition of RF/DFNS significantly enhanced flexural strength across all curing ages. This behavior can be attributed to the improved interfacial bonding and crack bridging mechanisms imparted by the hierarchical porous structure of DFNS and the fibrous morphology of RF, which together create a reinforcing network capable of arresting microcrack propagation. In terms of compressive strength, both RF and RF/DFNS enhanced performance compared to the control mortar, with RF/DFNS demonstrating a markedly superior effect. This enhancement is likely due to the synergistic contribution of the high surface area and porous architecture of DFNS, which improves pozzolanic reactivity and densifies the microstructure, along with the intrinsic tensile properties of the fibrous RF component. The DFNS matrix provides nucleation sites that accelerate the hydration of cement particles and facilitate the conversion of Ca(OH)2 into secondary C–S–H gels, especially in the interfacial transition zones. The relationship shown in Fig. 12b reveals a pronounced inverse exponential correlation between compressive strength and porosity, providing additional evidence for the observed mechanical behavior. Beyond the physical filling and packing effects, the silica rich DFNS phase can participate in secondary pozzolanic reactions with Ca(OH)2 generated during cement hydration. Such reactions may promote additional C–S–H formation, particularly near the fiber–matrix interfacial region, while simultaneously contributing to refinement and reduced connectivity of the pore network. This interpretation is consistent with the experimentally observed reduction in total porosity for the RF/DFNS modified mixtures. Therefore, the improved mechanical response can be associated with the combined effects of physical pore refinement, enhanced fiber–matrix interaction, and hydration related microstructural development. As presented in Fig. 13a, cement paste modified with RF and RF/DFNS exhibited a higher elastic limit than the reference mixture, indicating enhanced resistance to elastic deformation. This improvement is primarily attributed to the fibrous nature of rhubarb fibers and the unique dendritic architecture of DFNS, both of which contribute to enhanced water retention and particle interaction within the paste matrix. The hydrophilic surfaces of RF, and especially RF/DFNS, facilitated additional water adsorption, leading to a more pronounced shear thickening behavior. This rheological response suggests that the hybrid system delayed flow initiation under stress and promoted internal cohesion. The grafting of rhubarb fibers onto DFNS (in RF/DFNS composites) introduced more surface active sites, enabling stronger interactions with the cementitious matrix and water molecules. The ingress of chloride ions is widely recognized as a major factor governing the deterioration of cement-based materials exposed to marine and other chloride-laden environments. To assess the effectiveness of RF and RF/DFNS reinforcement in mitigating chloride transport, the chloride penetration resistance of cement mortar specimens was quantified after 30 days of curing using the rapid chloride diffusion coefficient (RCDC) test. As shown in Fig. 14a, mortar samples containing RF and particularly RF/DFNS demonstrated significantly reduced chloride movement indices compared to the control. The RF/DFNS modified mortars showed the lowest levels of chloride ion intrusion, indicating enhanced resistance to Cl− ingress. The unique porous structure of DFNS offered extensive internal pathways that acted as chloride traps, while the organic nature of RF contributed to the formation of a denser, less permeable microstructure. Furthermore, the hybrid RF/DFNS system benefited from the synergistic effects of the fibrous matrix and nanoscale silica framework, which improved interfacial bonding and pore refinement. These features collectively led to a notable decrease in the chloride diffusion coefficient (Fig. 14b), underscoring the superior durability of RF/DFNS reinforced mortars under aggressive ion attack. The improved chloride resistance is associated with the combined chemical and microstructural effects of the RF/DFNS hybrid. Hydration related pore refinement reduces the connectivity of capillary transport pathways, while the silica rich DFNS phase contributes to the development of a denser cementitious matrix. The resulting increase in transport path tortuosity and decrease in pore connectivity restrict chloride migration through the composite. In addition, the pozzolanic consumption of Ca(OH)2 by reactive silica may promote additional C–S–H formation, further refining the transport accessible pore network. Fig. 15 depicts the quantitative relationship between the chloride movement index and the overall permeability of the cement mortar samples. A clear positive correlation is observed: as the total permeability increases, the chloride ion diffusion coefficient correspondingly rises. This relationship highlights the critical role of permeability in governing chloride ingress. Notably, the cement samples containing RF exhibited a significant reduction in permeability, which directly contributed to their enhanced resistance to chloride penetration. In contrast, mortars modified solely with RF/DFNS despite their improved mechanical properties showed a relatively higher chloride diffusion coefficient. This phenomenon can be explained by the impact of RF/DFNS incorporation on the microstructure: the addition of RF/DFNS altered the cement paste rheology, reducing workability and potentially increasing the formation of pores and micro defects, as confirmed by Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (MIP) analysis. Since the RF and RF/DFNS particles do not chemically react with chloride ions, the enhanced chloride resistance of RF containing mortars is primarily due to their superior structural compactness and reduced permeability. The data suggest that while RF alone contributes to pore refinement and durability, higher loadings or combinations with DFNS may require optimization to avoid adverse effects on the microstructure that could counteract these benefits. The incorporation of DFNS significantly enhances microstructural densification and reduces chloride ion diffusivity by up to 72%, which prolongs the service life of concrete and reduces the frequency of maintenance or reconstruction both associated with high energy and resource consumption.

Conclusions

This work systematically examined the influence of a hybrid reinforcement system composed of RF and DFNS on the structural characteristics, durability, and mechanical behavior of foamed cementitious composites. Based on the experimental findings, the following conclusions are drawn:

(A) The incorporation of the RF/DFNS hybrid reinforcement refined the pore structure, reduced overall porosity, and strengthened the fiber–matrix interface. In addition, the high pozzolanic reactivity of DFNS promoted the formation of supplementary hydration products, resulting in a denser and more homogeneous cementitious matrix.

(B) Fickian diffusion modeling, calibrated using the 30-day chloride migration results, indicated that incorporating 1 wt% RF/DFNS reduced the effective chloride diffusion coefficient by approximately 72%. This reduction confirms the improved ability of the modified composite to resist chloride ingress and enhances its long-term durability in chloride-rich environments.

(C) The hybrid RF/DFNS reinforcement markedly enhanced the fracture energy of the composites, increasing the energy absorption capacity by more than 80%. This improvement is attributed to the combined effects of fiber bridging and the ability of DFNS to delay microcrack initiation and propagation, resulting in higher toughness and improved post-cracking performance.

(D) The simultaneous incorporation of RF and DFNS generated a hierarchical reinforcement system that improved both the mechanical performance and transport resistance of the foamed composites. This multiscale reinforcement approach effectively reduced the inherent brittleness and permeability of foamed concrete by promoting a denser and more interconnected microstructure.

(E) Overall, the findings indicate that the enhanced performance of RF/DFNS modified foamed concrete is closely associated with the coupling of bio fiber surface chemistry and nanosilica mediated interfacial and microstructural refinement, establishing a clear relationship between hybrid surface engineering and macroscopic durability.

(F) The use of renewable RF as a bio-based reinforcement, combined with the microstructural refinement provided by DFNS, demonstrates the potential of incorporating renewable lignocellulosic resources into high performance foamed cementitious composites. The developed hybrid system also shows promise for infrastructure applications requiring enhanced durability under aggressive service environments.

(G) Beyond the observed mechanical and durability improvements, the use of bio based RF highlights the potential of renewable lignocellulosic resources in the development of advanced cementitious composites. Nevertheless, the environmental performance of the proposed RF/DFNS system was not quantitatively assessed in this study, and future work should include life cycle assessment and embodied carbon analysis to determine its sustainability benefits relative to conventional reinforcement systems.
