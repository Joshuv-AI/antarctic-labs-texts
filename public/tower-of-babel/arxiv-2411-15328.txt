Dependence Induced Representations
Xiangxiang Xu, Lizhong Zheng

Abstract
We study the problem of learning feature representations from a pair of random variables, where we focus on the representations that are induced by their dependence. We provide sufficient and necessary conditions for such dependence induced representations, and illustrate their connections to Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) maximal correlation functions and minimal sufficient statistics. We characterize a large family of loss functions that can learn dependence induced representations, including cross entropy, hinge loss, and their regularized variants. In particular, we show that the features learned from this family can be expressed as the composition of a loss-dependent function and the maximal correlation function, which reveals a key connection between representations learned from different losses. Our development also gives a statistical interpretation of the neural collapse phenomenon observed in deep classifiers. Finally, we present the learning design based on the feature separation, which allows hyperparameter tuning during inference.

I
Introduction

Deep learning techniques
[
1
]
have demonstrated significant progress in extracting useful information from high-dimensional structured data. The learned feature representations can be easily adapted to different learning objectives, data modalities, or learning tasks. Compared with the practical successes, the understanding of the learned representations is rather limited: though it is widely acknowledged that such representations contain useful information for understanding the statistical behaviors of data
[
2
]
, theoretical analyses of learned representations are still challenging. In particular, the difficulty comes from the unknown complicated structures behind high-dimensional data and the huge design space of practical learning algorithms. It is often difficult to obtain analytical solutions to the representations, except for a few cases with principled designs
[
3
]
. Theoretical analyses often rely on
additional assumptions, making the results restricted to specific scenarios.

In contrast to the difficulty faced by theoretical characterizations, deep learning practices suggest the other extreme: despite the huge design space of loss functions and hyperparameter settings, the learned representations can still be reused or adapted to different setups; empirical studies also indicate
that representations obtained from completely different methods can be highly similar
[
4
]
. A better understanding of this similarity can provide
key insights on the learning mechanism and lead to more efficient learning designs.

In this paper, we tackle the representation learning problem from a statistical perspective. We consider learning representations from a pair of variables
X,Y
and focus on the representations determined only by the
X-Y
dependence. We refer to such representations as dependence induced representations, formalized as the representations invariant to transformations that preserve the dependence structure. We provide sufficient and necessary conditions for such representations and demonstrate their deep connections to the Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) maximal correlation functions and the notion of minimal sufficiency. Furthermore, we characterize a family of loss functions, termed D-losses, which can be used for learning dependence induced representations. Specifically, we demonstrate that the optimal feature is the composition of a loss-dependent term and the maximal correlation function, revealing the key connection between different feature representations.
We also show that D-losses cover a large class of practical loss functions, including cross entropy and hinge loss as special cases.
Our characterization also provides a statistical interpretation of the neural collapse behavior
[
5
]
in training deep classifiers. Based on our developments, we propose a design of feature adapters that allow hyperparameter tuning during inference.

The functions

Then, for all

\displaystyle(f^{*}_{i},g^{*}_{i})=\argmax_{f_{i},g_{i}}\,\corr(f_{i},g_{i})

Definition 1

We can also express the sufficiency in terms of CDK functions. Specifically, let

indicate the random variable
\gamma(X,Y)
where

is an abstraction of
(f(X),Y)

By symmetry,

and

•

•

Definition 3

\displaystyle\mathfrak{i}_{\hat{X};\hat{Y}}(\hat{x},{\hat{y}})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{i}_{X;Y}(\xi^{-1}(\hat{x}),\eta^{-1}({\hat{y}}))

III-C

Dependence Induced Representations and Learning

We then consider the impacts of dependence preserving transformations on representation learning. Specifically, we focus on the learned representations that are dependence induced, i.e., invariant to such transformations. Formally, Let
\mathsf{alg}\colon(X,Y)\overset{}{\mapsto}(f,g)
be a representation learning algorithm that learns

f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k},g\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
from
(X,Y)\sim P_{X,Y}

(\hat{X},\hat{Y})
are obtained by applying a dependence preserving transformation on
(X,Y)

\hat{X}=\xi(X,Z),\hat{Y}=\eta(Y,W)

\displaystyle(f^{(\xi)},g^{(\eta)})=\mathsf{alg}(\hat{X},\hat{Y}),

where we have denoted the learned feature functions by

and
g^{(\eta)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\hat{\mathcal{Y}}}^{\,k}

\hat{\mathcal{X}},\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
denoting transformed alphabets. Note that the transformations
\xi,\eta
are not separately input to the algorithm.

From Definition
3
, the learned representations are dependence induced if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{f^{(\xi)}(\hat{X})=f(X),g^{(\eta)}(\hat{Y})=g(Y)\right\}=1

for all possible generating processes of
\hat{X}
,
\hat{Y}
. We refer to the algorithm
\mathsf{alg}
as a

.

The collection
\mathscr{A}
of dependence learning algorithms is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\triangleq\{\mathsf{alg}\colon\mathsf{alg}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=(\phi\circ f^{*},\psi\circ g^{*}),

\displaystyle(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{alg}(f^{*}(X),g^{*}(Y))\},

(6)

where
f^{*},g^{*}
are the maximal correlation functions [cf. (
5
)].

Proof.

We first verify that each
\mathsf{alg}\in\mathscr{A}
for
\mathscr{A}
as defined in (

) is a dependence learning algorithm. It suffices to note that the representation pair learned from
\mathsf{alg}
is

. This is dependence induced since both
(f^{*}(X),g^{*}(Y))
and the mapping pair

are dependence induced [cf. Theorem

, Corollary
1
and (
6
)].

To see the reverse direction, suppose
\mathsf{alg}
is a dependence learning algorithm, and let
(\phi,\psi)=\mathsf{alg}(f^{*}(X),g^{*}(Y))
,
(\hat{f},\hat{g})=\mathsf{alg}(X,Y)

, we can obtain

g(Y)

Proposition 3
.

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y)=(\tau\circ\mathfrak{i}_{X;Y})(X,Y).

(7)

IV
D-Losses and Dependence Learning

Our discussions in the previous section have characterized the theoretical behaviors of dependence learning algorithms. It is, however, difficult to directly apply these results to practical deep representation learning, e.g., determining if a representation learning algorithm is a dependence learning algorithm or constructing dependence learning algorithms. To address these problems, it is necessary also to consider practical implementation and develop operable characterizations. To this end, we consider representation learning algorithms characterized by corresponding loss functions and characterize sufficient conditions on losses for constructing dependence learning algorithms. We investigate a family of loss functions, termed D-losses, and demonstrate their deep connections to learning practices.

IV-A

D-Loss Family

Given
(X,Y)\sim P_{X,Y}
, let
\Gamma=\Gamma(f,g;P_{X,Y})
denote a functional defined on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
. We will omit
P_{X,Y}
and write
\Gamma(f,g)
if the choice of
P_{X,Y}
is clear from the context. Specifically, when we evaluate
\Gamma
on a dataset

\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the
P_{X,Y}
corresponds to the empirical distribution
\hat{P}_{X,Y}(x,y)\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbb{1}_{\{x=x_{i},y=y_{i}\}}
, where
\mathbbb{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
denotes the 0-1 indicator function.

We define the D-losses as follows.

Definition 4

(D-loss)
.

Given
(X,Y)\sim P_{X,Y}
, a functional
\Gamma
defined on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
is a D-loss if

1.

suppose
\phi\circ\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}
and
\psi\circ\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
,
then we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(\phi\circ\xi,\psi\circ\eta;P_{X,Y})=\Gamma(\phi,\psi;P_{\xi(X),\eta(Y)});

(8)

2.

for all
s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,*}
and
t\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,*}
that satisfy the Markov relation
X-s(X)-t(Y)-Y
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(f|_{s},g|_{t})\leq\Gamma(f,g),

(9)

where
f|_{s}
and
g|_{t}
are as defined in (
1
).

Specifically, we say a D-loss
\Gamma
is regular,
if in (
9
),
\Gamma(f,g)=\Gamma(f|_{s},g|_{t})<\infty

implies
(f,g)=(f|_{s},g|_{t})
.

We can obtain dependence learning algorithms from the D-loss family, demonstrated as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Given
(X,Y)\sim P_{X,Y}
, suppose
L
defined on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
is a D-loss and
v_{k}(X,Y)\triangleq\min_{(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}}L(f,g;P_{X,Y})
exists. Then,

1.

v_{k}(X,Y)
is dependence induced;

2.

there exist dependence induced mappings
\phi,\psi
, such that
(f,g)=(\phi\circ f^{*},\psi\circ g^{*})
achieves the minimum
value
v_{k}
, where
f^{*},g^{*}
are as defined in (
5
);

3.

if
L
is regular, then the representation learning
algorithm
\left((X,Y)\mapsto\argmin_{f,g}L(f,g;P_{X,Y})\right)
is a dependence learning algorithm.

Proof.

We begin with the first two claims. Let
S=f^{*}(X),T=g^{*}(Y)
, and we use
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
denote the corresponding alphabets. Suppose
(\hat{f},\hat{g})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
achieves the minimum value
v_{k}(X,Y)
, and let
(\hat{\phi},\hat{\psi})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\,k}
be the mappings such that
\hat{f}|_{f^{*}}=\hat{\phi}\circ f^{*},\hat{g}|_{g^{*}}=\hat{\psi}\circ g^{*}
. Then, from the Markov relation

X-S-T-Y
and (
9
), we obtain

\displaystyle L(\hat{f},\hat{g};P_{X,Y})

\displaystyle\geq L(\hat{f}|_{f^{*}},\hat{g}|_{g^{*}};P_{X,Y})

\displaystyle=L(\hat{\phi}\circ f^{*},\hat{\psi}\circ g^{*};P_{X,Y})=L(\hat{\phi},\hat{\psi};P_{S,T}),

where the last equality follows from (
8
). Note that since
(\hat{f},\hat{g})
achieves the minimum value of
L
, the inequality must hold with equality, so the minimum value
v_{k}
can be achieved by
(f,g)=(\hat{\phi}\circ f^{*},\hat{\psi}\circ g^{*})
. In addition, we have

\displaystyle(\hat{\phi},\hat{\psi})\in\argmin_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\,k},\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\,k}}L(\phi,\psi;P_{S,T}),

(10)

since otherwise we get an
L
value smaller than
L(\hat{f},\hat{g};P_{X;Y})
. From Corollary
1
, both
v_{k}
and
(\hat{\phi},\hat{\psi})
and dependence induced.
Finally, when
L
is regular, the equality
L(\hat{f},\hat{g})=L(\hat{f}|_{f^{*}},\hat{g}|_{g^{*}})
implies
(\hat{f},\hat{g})=(\hat{\phi}\circ f^{*},\hat{\psi}\circ g^{*})
. Therefore, it follows from
(
10
) and Theorem
2
that
\left((X,Y)\mapsto\argmin_{f,g}L(f,g;P_{X,Y})\right)
is a dependence learning algorithm.
∎

Combining Theorem
3
and Theorem
2
, if
L
is a regular D-loss, it suffices to solve
\minimize_{\phi,\psi}L(\phi,\psi;P_{S,T})
instead of the original problem
\minimize_{f,g}L(f,g;P_{X,Y})
, where we have defined
S=f^{*}(X),T=g^{*}(Y)
. In particular, the optimal solution takes a composition form
(f,g)=(\phi\circ f^{*},\psi\circ g^{*})
, where only
\phi,\psi
depend on the form of
L
. This separation between loss-dependent functions
(\phi,\psi)
and loss-invariant
(f^{*},g^{*})
makes it possible to design efficient implementation for D-loss minimization problems, which we will detail in the next section.

Comparing the first claim with the second claim, a difference between D-losses and regular D-losses is that minimizing D-losses can give solutions not in the form
(\phi\circ f^{*},\psi\circ g^{*})
. However, from the first claim, restricting to such forms does not affect the optimality. Therefore, it is without loss of generality to restrict to such forms. Once we have this restriction, the D-loss minimization algorithm is also a dependence learning algorithm. Moreover, we can always convert a D-loss into a regular D-loss by introducing regularization terms. One example is demonstrated as follows. We omit its proof.

Property 3
.

If
\Gamma
is a D-loss, then for all
\lambda>0,\mu>0
,
\left(\Gamma(f,g)+\lambda\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|^{2}\right]+\mu\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g(Y)\|^{2}\right]\right)
is a regular D-loss.

We can interpret the regularization terms in Property
3
as a weight decay
[
13
]
on feature representations, which can be implicitly introduced in optimization, e.g., stochastic gradient descent. Due to these connections, we will consider general D-losses in the discussions that follow, and
restrict to the optimal solutions of the form
(\phi\circ f^{*},\psi\circ g^{*})
. Specifically, we introduce several useful properties for constructing D-losses. The proofs can be obtained by applying Jensen’s inequality and noting the Markov relation
X-S-T-Y
.

Property 4
.

Suppose
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex with respect to both arguments, then
(f,g)\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y}}\left[\gamma(f(X),g(Y))\right]
and
(f,g)\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}P_{Y}}\left[{\gamma(f(X),g(Y))}\right]
are D-losses.

Property 5
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a D-loss on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
, and
\nu\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
is nondecreasing with respect to the first argument, then
\nu\left(\Gamma(f,g),{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X)\right],{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(Y)\right],\Lambda_{f,g}\right)
is also a D-loss, where
\Lambda_{f,g}={\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X)g^{\mathrm{T}}(Y)\right]
.

Given
l
losses, we can obtain a new loss by using a mapping
\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}
that aggregates the losses. We refer to the operation as a monotonic aggregation if the mapping is nondecreasing with respect to each argument.

Property 6
.

A monotonic aggregation of D-losses is a D-loss.

D-losses in Learning Practices

[

\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that for all
\underline{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{D}}(\underline{v})\triangleq\begin{cases}0,&\underline{v}\in\mathcal{D}\\
\infty,&\underline{v}\notin\mathcal{D}\end{cases}.

Log Loss (Softmax & Cross Entropy)

We demonstrate the log loss minimization problem is equivalent to a D-loss minimization problem. Our development will use the following fact.

Fact 1
.

The functional
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\log{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Y}}\left[\exp(f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y))\right]\right]
defined on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
is a D-loss, where the inner expectation is taken w.r.t.
Y\sim P_{Y}
, and the outer expectation is taken w.r.t.
X\sim P_{X}
.

In particular, let
X
and
Y
denote the data variable and categorical label, respectively. We use
h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the feature representation, which corresponds to the feature in the last hidden layer, and denote the weight and bias term corresponding to
Y=y
as
w(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b(y)\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively. By applying the softmax function, we get a posterior parameterized by
h,w,b
:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{(h,w,b)}_{Y|X}(y|x)=\frac{\exp(h^{\mathrm{T}}(x)w(y)+b(y))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(h^{\mathrm{T}}(x)w(y^{\prime})+b(y^{\prime}))}.

(12)

Then, the log loss is given by the expected value of
-\log\tilde{P}^{(h,w,b)}_{Y|X}
, i.e.,

{\mathbb{E}}_{(\hat{X},\hat{Y})\sim P_{X,Y}}\left[-\log\tilde{P}^{(h,w,b)}_{Y|X}(\hat{Y}|\hat{X})\right]
. Let
k=d+1
, and we define the calibrated bias term
\beta(y)\triangleq b(y)-\log P_{Y}(y)
, and features

\displaystyle f=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
h\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k},\quad g=\begin{bmatrix}\beta\\
w\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}.

(13)

Then, we can rewrite the log loss as

-{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\log{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Y}}\left[\exp(f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y))\right]\right]+H(Y)
, where
H(Y)
denotes the entropy of
P_{Y}
. Therefore, minimizing log loss is equivalent to the minimization of the following functional defined on
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
:

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\log{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Y}}\left[\exp(f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y))\right]\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}_{\{1\}}(f_{1}(X))\right],

(14)

where
f_{1}
denotes the first dimension of
f
, and where
\mathbb{I}
is the characteristic function as defined in (
11

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}_{\{1\}}(f_{1}(X))\right]<\infty

\mathbb{P}\left\{f_{1}(X)=1\right\}=1

13

From Fact

the extended log loss (

are dependence induced. It is worth mentioning that though

is not dependence induced in general, the calibrated bias
\beta(Y)
is dependence induced.

We then consider the loss applied in support vector machine (SVM). In particular, we consider a binary classification problem on data
X

Y\in\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}

h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\underline{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and bias
b\in\mathbb{R}

\langle\underline{w},h(x)\rangle+b=0

[

]

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y}}\left[\ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}(Y,\langle\underline{w},h(X)\rangle+b)\right]+\lambda\cdot\|\underline{w}\|^{2},

(15)

where
\ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the hinge loss, defined as
\ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}(y,z)\triangleq(1-yz)^{+}
with
x^{+}\triangleq\max\{0,x\}
, and where
\lambda>0
is a hyperparameter. Let
k=d+1
and define feature functions
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k},g\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
as

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}h(x)\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad g(y)=y\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\underline{w}\\
b\end{bmatrix}.

Then, we have
\ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}(Y,\langle\underline{w},h(X)\rangle+b)=(1-\langle\underline{w}Y,h(X)\rangle-bY)^{+}=(1-\langle f(X),g(Y)\rangle)^{+}
and

where

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bigl(1-\left\langle f(X),g(Y)\right\rangle\bigr)^{+}\right]+\lambda\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g_{[d]}(Y)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}_{\{1\}}(f_{k}(X))\right]+\mathbb{I}_{\{0\}}({\mathbb{E}}\left[g(Y)\right]),

(17)

where
f_{k}(X)
denotes the

Learning Techniques: Regularization, Constrained Features, and Feature Nesting

We consider several learning techniques that preserve the D-loss structure. We first consider the regularization. Given a D-loss
L(f,g)
, we can construct the regularized loss
L(f,g)+\lambda\cdot R(f,g)
, where
R(f,g)
is the regularization term and
\lambda>0
is the hyperparameter. Then, from Property
6
, the regularized loss is a D-loss if
R(f,g)
is a D-loss. From Property
4
, the
squared distance
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)-g(Y)\|^{2}\right]
, and generally
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)-g(Y)\|_{p}\right]
for
p\geq 1
are D-losses, where
\|\underline{v}\|_{p}\triangleq\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
denotes the
p
-norm.
Similarly,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|_{p}\right]
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g(Y)\|_{p}\right]
,
p\geq 1
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|^{2}\right]
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g(Y)\|^{2}\right]
are also D-losses. During implementation, the expectation is estimated by the corresponding empirical averages over each mini-batch.

In contrast to the batch-wise regularization terms, another common practice is directly introducing constraints on each instance of feature representations, e.g., force features to be nonnegative. With the characteristic function (
11
), we can use an extended loss to characterize such constraints. For example, suppose the original loss
L(f,g)
is a D-loss and
f(X)
is constrained to be within a set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then we can consider the extended loss
L(f,g)+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{C}}(f(X))\right]
, which leads to the same optimal solution. From Property
4
, the extended loss is a D-loss if
\mathcal{C}
is convex. Two important examples of such convex sets are: 1)
p
-norm balls, e.g.,
\mathcal{C}=\{\underline{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\colon\|\underline{v}\|_{p}\leq 1\}
, where each feature instance is projected onto the norm ball; 2) nonnegative orthant, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=\{\underline{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\colon v_{i}\geq 0\}
, corresponding to learning nonnegative features.

The regularization methods are also used to combine multiple training objectives by optimizing their weighted sum. From Property
6
, if each objective is a D-loss, the weighted sum will also be a D-loss. A special example is
Feature Nesting
, which constructs the weighted sum of the same loss applied on a set of nested features. Specifically, suppose the loss
L
is defined for all
d
-dimensional feature function pair
(f,g)
, e.g.,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{\mathrm{T}}(X)g(Y)\right]
. Then, give
(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,k}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,k}
, we consider the nested loss

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\cdot L(f_{[i]},g_{[i]}),

(18)

where
c_{i},i=1,\dots,k
are non-negative weights, and where for each
i\in[k]
, we have defined
f_{[i]}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\,i},g_{[i]}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\,i}
as

\displaystyle f_{[i]}\triangleq(f_{1},\dots,f_{i})^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad g_{[i]}\triangleq(g_{1},\dots,g_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}.

(19)

Such construction is applied to induce structures on the representations
f,g
, referred to as the
nesting technique

[
3
, Section 4]
, also called
matryoshka representation learning

[
17
]
. The nested loss (
18
) is a D-loss if
L
is a D-loss.

Representations Induced by Strict Dependence

We conclude this section by discussing a special case where
X
and

are strictly dependent, and we have the following characterization [cf. Corollary

Proposition 4
.

Given a regular D-loss

\displaystyle\mathsf{alg}(X,Y)=\{(\phi\circ\mathtt{y},\psi)\colon(\phi,\psi)\in\mathsf{alg}(Y,Y)\}.

Note that a specific case of Proposition
