Chemically triggered bioorthogonal activation of antimicrobial peptide mimic prodrugs
Authors: Hao Luo, Rhiannon P. Kuchel, Kevin Neumann, Edgar H. H. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of antimicrobial resistance has necessitated the urgent development of new and effective antimicrobial agents. One promising class of compounds is antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and their mimics, although this type of membrane-active agent is also prone to cause toxicity issues. To circumvent the associated toxicities of AMPs, we herein developed a novel AMP mimic prodrug where the cationic ammonium residues are caged with trans-cyclooctene (TCO) units that can be bioorthogonally activated by tetrazine via an inverse-electron-demand Diels–Alder click-to-release reaction. The prodrug, TCO-Dendron, was inactive but exhibited antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative pathogens, especially Pseudomonas aeruginosa, in the presence of dimethyl tetrazine. Crucially, through detailed correlation of NMR, MS and antimicrobial assays, we obtained strong indications that the intermediate dihydropyridazine tautomer that persists due to incomplete release of the original AMP mimic also demonstrates antimicrobial activity, thus overcoming the often rate-limiting step in many click-to-release systems. In addition, TCO-Dendron was significantly less toxic to red blood cells and mouse embryonic fibroblast cells than the original AMP mimic, further demonstrating the on-demand control of toxicity enabled by the bioorthogonal trigger. Overall, this study thus not only reports the development of a new click chemistry-responsive AMP mimic prodrug but also reveals important mechanistic insights that help to inform the development of future antimicrobial prodrug systems.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health threat and has caused approximately 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019 alone.1,2 The death toll could yet rise to 10 million casualties per year by 2050 if the clinical development of new antimicrobial agents continues to fall behind.3 According to the World Health Organization, Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii have been deemed as top priority pathogens that urgently require new treatment options.4 Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), which are typically rich in cationic (ammoniums) and hydrophobic residues in their molecular structure, have shown great promise in combating multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, most notably because of their ability to act broadly on the bacterial cell membrane and other cellular targets, which makes it more difficult for bacteria to develop resistance compared with traditional antibiotics.5–22 Despite their excellent antimicrobial potency, AMPs (and synthetic mimics thereof) often exhibit toxicity issues, hence limiting their translation into clinical settings as the same key functional groups that give rise to antimicrobial activity are also responsible for causing toxicity to mammalian cells.16,23 Over the years, research efforts have been made to overcome this root cause of toxicity, mostly by balancing the overall cationic and hydrophobic characteristics or through combination therapy with antibiotics.5,6,14,22,24–32 Meanwhile, the prodrug approach, which involves the caging and subsequent triggered uncaging of functional groups for precise antimicrobial activation in the presence of bacteria cells, has been less explored.

Recently, we have reported proof-of-concept systems where the cationic amines of AMP mimics are initially caged to render them inactive and less toxic, which can later be uncaged by specific environmental triggers such as light (external trigger) or enzymes like galactosidase and penicillinase (biological trigger) to confer on-demand antimicrobial activity.33–35 This approach, termed the amine uncaging strategy (AUS), focusses on controlling the presentation of amine functionalities to govern molecule–cell interaction events, and by default selectivity and toxicity, given that cationic groups tend to bind indiscriminately with both bacteria and mammalian cells. We speculate that the use of bioorthogonal triggers with high selectivity and efficiency could provide excellent alternatives to environmentally responsive antimicrobial prodrugs in complex biological environments. Therefore, in this study, we describe the development of a novel AMP mimic prodrug (TCO-Dendron) where the cationic amines are caged with trans-cyclooctene (TCO) moieties that can be subsequently activated by tetrazines via the inverse-electron-demand Diels–Alder (IEDDA) click reaction to yield in situ antimicrobial activity (Fig. 1).

(b) Simplified scheme of the inverse-electron-demand Diels–Alder click-to-release reaction between a trans-cyclooctene derivative and dimethyl tetrazine to yield cationic functional groups that ultimately give rise to activated antimicrobial nanoparticles for killing Gram-negative bacteria.

This click-to-release method was pioneered by Robillard and co-workers in 2013 for anticancer applications where the drug doxorubicin was caged with TCO before being released via the IEDDA reaction with tetrazines.36–38 Since then, numerous systems have been developed based on this chemistry for the oncology field,39–45 but only a few examples on small molecule antibiotic modification exist.46–48 Most recently in 2024, Brönstrup and co-workers reported the development of a TCO-modified AMP prodrug for activation via IEDDA chemistry for the first time. Specifically in their work, the primary amines of colistin B, which is a natural AMP from the polymyxin family, were caged with TCO derivatives.48 Prior to that, Oneto, Royzen, Trampuz and co-workers reported the development of TCO-vancomycin and TCO-daptomycin prodrug systems that can be activated via IEDDA. Antimicrobial activation was achieved when these prodrugs encountered a judiciously designed tetrazine-modified alginate gel.49 While it has been the consensus that near complete release is required to attain the desired activated product for relevant bioactivity, we crucially show in the present study that this is not necessary and the intermediates that persist because of incomplete release can exhibit bioactivity, at least for antimicrobial purposes. Our findings have direct implications on the design of a releasable system since the cargo release upon initial clicking is commonly the bottleneck.49,50 This study hence not only reports the development of a potentially new AMP-mimicking prodrug but also reveals important and novel mechanistic insights pertaining to the click-to-release prodrug strategy that could help inform the design of future antimicrobial agents.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

For this study, the development of the tetrazine-responsive antimicrobial prodrug, TCO-Dendron, was based on the judicious modification of a model AMP mimic, Ref-Dendron, which is a cationic amphipathic macromolecule that consists of two piperidine derivatives (secondary amine) and a lipid tail (C12). Our earlier study has shown that cationic amphipathic molecules like Ref-Dendron have good antimicrobial activity but only moderate biocompatibility,32 which makes the TCO-Dendron prodrug an excellent example to showcase the advantage of the AUS approach in improving the selectivity of the original AMP mimic.34

The synthesis of Ref-Dendron and the corresponding TCO-Dendron was readily achieved (SI, Schemes S1–S3), and the chemical structures of the main products and precursors were verified by 1H and 13C NMR analysis (SI, Fig. S1–S8). For example, the resonance from the strained alkene protons of the TCO units at δH 5.5 to 5.8 ppm was detectable as expected in the NMR spectra of TCO-Dendron, thus indicating the successful synthesis of the prodrug. The formation of TCO-Dendron was also further confirmed by detailed 2D NMR analysis, specifically HSQC, HMBC and COSY (SI, Fig. S9–S11). High resolution MS analysis also provided positive indication of the formation of the dendrons (Fig. 2a and b). The sodium ion adduct of TCO-Dendron was clearly visible in the mass spectra at 862.60 Da as the most abundant peak, whereas in the mass spectra of Ref-Dendron, the singly and doubly charged proton adducts of the unsalted version of the dendron were detected as the most abundant ion species at 536.45 and 268.74 Da, respectively. It is worth noting that possible fragmentation of Ref-Dendron at one of the amide junctions occurred during MS analysis that resulted in a lesser peak at 425.39 Da that represented the proton adduct of a ‘half-cleaved’ dendron. The chemical structures corresponding to these peaks can be found in the SI (Fig. S12).

(d) Critical micelle concentration (CMC) determination plots based on the DLS count rate method. (e and f) TEM micrographs of the dendrons.

DLS analysis was subsequently performed to check if the dendrons can undergo self-assembly in aqueous solution, especially for Ref-Dendron, given its amphipathic characteristic (Fig. 2c). Indeed, the DLS intensity distribution of Ref-Dendron was monomodal with a low polydispersity (PDI) value of 0.29 and an average hydrodynamic diameter (DH-avg) of 190 nm, indicating self-assembly behavior in water. Interestingly, TCO-Dendron, which is less polar than Ref-Dendron because of the more hydrophobic TCO caging motifs compared to the free cationic groups, also appeared to undergo self-assembly in water, yielding a monomodal intensity distribution with PDI and DH-avg values of 0.14 and 182 nm, respectively. Using the DLS count rate method, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) values of Ref-Dendron and TCO-Dendron were approximated as 45 and 22 µg mL−1, respectively, which were comparable to those obtained for similar self-assembling dendrons in our previous studies.34,35 It is also worthwhile noting that the integrity of the self-assembled structure was not affected upon treatment with dimethyl tetrazine (Tet 1). DLS analysis of the TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 sample revealed monomodal intensity distribution with DH-avg and PDI values of 204 nm and 0.21, respectively, which were similar to those of the other two dendrons (SI, Fig. S13a). Furthermore, the CMC value of the clicked sample was estimated as 32 µg mL−1, which was again comparable to those of the other two dendrons (SI, Fig. S13b). The morphology of the nanoconstructs was visualized under a microscope via TEM analysis (Fig. 2e and f). For Ref-Dendron, clear individual spherical morphologies were observed, which are typical of most amphipathic macromolecules, indicating particle stability during TEM characterization in the dried state. In contrast, for TCO-Dendron, aggregation of smaller spherical constructs was observed. Despite TCO-Dendron displaying apparent colloidal stability under aqueous conditions, the removal of surrounding water molecules, like in the case of TEM sample preparation where the samples are cast onto a substrate and dried for analysis, destabilizes the nanoparticles and drives the aggregation of hydrophobic TCO groups. This suggests that the nanoparticles of TCO-Dendron in water were stabilized by moderately hydrophilic groups on the outer shell, most likely the carbamate functionalities, but this stabilization effect was insufficient in preventing the coalescence of the nanoparticles in the dried state. Nonetheless, TCO-Dendron is considered stable in an aqueous environment as the molecule does not precipitate (at the highest tested concentration of 256 µg mL−1) despite the hydrophobic TCO termini and instead undergoes self-assembly to form uniform nanoparticles, thus making it suitable for subsequent biological evaluation.

Antimicrobial activity

The initial assessment of the antimicrobial activity of TCO-Dendron was based on a bacteriostatic assay where the dendron was co-administered with Tet 1 at 1.6 mole equivalent per TCO unit along with bacteria culture in ‘one-pot’ and left to stand for 20–24 h. Tet 1 is a commonly employed tetrazine for decaging chemistries within a biological environment.36,45,49,50 This standard assay enables the determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of a compound required to halt bacteria growth by more than 90%. We focused our efforts on testing Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and especially P. aeruginosa (including MDR strains) because of their classification as top priority pathogens.4 In the presence of Tet 1, TCO-Dendron displayed MIC values of 32 µg mL−1 (or 2-fold lower) across all tested bacteria, which were comparable to those of the original uncaged AMP mimic Ref-Dendron (Fig. 3a). In fact, to our surprise, the sample containing TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 performed 4× better than Ref-Dendron against wild-type E. coli, suggesting that the click-activated sample may have converted into products that possess stronger antimicrobial activity than Ref-Dendron against E. coli (vide infra). Meanwhile, for TCO-Dendron alone in the absence of Tet 1, that is without any chemical trigger to react with the TCO moieties, no bacterial inhibition was observed even at the highest tested concentration of 256 µg mL−1. It is also important to note that Tet 1 alone did not exhibit any antimicrobial activity against the tested Gram-negative bacteria. The MIC results thus conclusively showed that the TCO-Dendron prodrug undergoes in situ bioorthogonal activation with tetrazine, even in complex biological media, to produce bacteriostatic activity. It is worth noting that the MIC values of Ref-Dendron and TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 samples coincided closely with their CMC values, thereby implying that the compounds need to be in their self-assembled nanoparticle form to exert antimicrobial activity. We postulate that the nanoparticle format produces a strong multivalent effect where multiple dendrons can collectively target and concentrate on the bacteria cell membrane to induce antimicrobial activity.

Next, the bactericidal activity of TCO-Dendron with Tet 1 against planktonic and young biofilm cells of wild-type P. aeruginosa was assessed via colony-forming unit (CFU) analysis. As can be seen in Fig. 3b, no viable planktonic bacteria cells were detected after 2 h of treatment with 1× MIC of TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 within the detection limit of the assay (i.e., 2 log10 CFU mL−1). The killing efficiency was greater at 2× MIC where the same observation was made after just 1 h of treatment. Unlike planktonic cells, bacterial biofilms are harder to eradicate because the cells are encased in a network protected by a matrix of extracellular substances. Generally, a higher concentration of antimicrobial agents is required to combat biofilms than planktonic cells.33,51,52 For this antibiofilm assay, young biofilms of P. aeruginosa were grown for 6 h according to our previous protocol and subsequently treated for 1 h at 1×, 2×, and 4× the MIC value,33 followed by CFU analysis to determine the amount of viable biofilm cells remaining (Fig. 3c). Unsurprisingly, at 1× MIC, hardly any effect was observed for both TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 and Ref-Dendron samples, and even at 2× MIC, less than a 1 log10 reduction in CFU mL−1 was attained relative to the untreated bacteria control sample. It was not until at 4× MIC that an appreciable reduction in CFUs was observed. For Ref-Dendron, ca. only 1 log10 reduction in CFU mL−1 was achieved, however, the in situ activated prodrug sample resulted in a nearly 3.5 log10 reduction in CFU mL−1, which was significantly better than the original AMP mimic. The superior antibiofilm activity of TCO-Dendron in the presence of Tet 1 was unexpected and to discount any antimicrobial effects contributed by Tet 1, a control experiment was performed by pitting Tet 1 against the P. aeruginosa biofilms at the same dosages as in the TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 samples. The Tet 1 control samples had no effect on the biofilm cell viability. The results hinted that the click-activated sample had better antimicrobial performance than the original Ref-Dendron and prompted us to investigate further.

Mechanism of action

To understand why the click-activated sample appears to have stronger antimicrobial potency, we had to investigate the click-to-release mechanism in detail. While the reaction scheme shown in Fig. 1b is a simplified version, the full extent of the reaction pathway is more complex and involves the transformation of the initial 4,5-dihydropyridazine tautomer X1 into the following intermediate tautomers, the 2,5- and 1,4-dihydropyridazines X2 and X3, prior to being released into the final desired cationic species X4 and other pyridazine by-products X5 and X6 as well as aromatized but non-released X7 (Fig. 4a). It is worthwhile noting that X4 is the HCl salt-free version of Ref-Dendron after undergoing the IEDDA reaction, but nevertheless, the antimicrobial activity and solubility of the salted and unsalted forms of the cationic species were the same. Moreover, for simplicity, only the transformation of the reactive functional groups is depicted rather than the complete chemical structures, highlighting that each TCO moiety present in the molecules can undergo the same reaction pathway, as will be described in detail below.

(b) 1H NMR spectra of the clicked samples at different time points as measured in DMSO-d6 solvent. The initial molar ratio of TCO units to Tet 1 was kept the same as in the antimicrobial assays at 1 : 1.6. The extent of the click-to-release reaction between TCO-Dendron and Tet 1 was monitored by 1H NMR analysis for up to 24 h (Fig. 4b). As can be seen in Fig. 4b, the integrals that correspond to the species X1 to X7 were clearly evolving over the 24 h duration. First, the resonance of strained alkene protons (signals a and b) of TCO-Dendron at δH 5.5 to 5.8 ppm disappeared almost entirely after 10 min of mixing with Tet 1. The disappearance of these signals was accompanied by the appearance of resonance g at δH 5.1 to 5.2 ppm that corresponds to the proton of the O-α carbon adjacent to the carbamate group in X1, as well as the disappearance of resonance c. Only faint amounts of X4 and X5 could be detected at this 10 min mark. As the reaction progresses, resonance g disappeared after 24 h, while resonances y and t that are unique to the N–H hydrogens of X2 and X4, respectively, grew and plateaued. It is noteworthy that the presence of the other dihydropyridazine intermediate tautomer X3 was negligible as resonance m at δH 5.4 to 5.5 ppm that corresponds to the proton of the O-α carbon adjacent to the carbamate group in X3 was barely detectable compared to resonance j of X2 at δH 4.8 ppm. The formation and disappearance kinetics of X1 to X7 are summarized in Fig. 4c, where it was clear that only approximately half of the TCO units triggered release of the cationic piperidine derivative X4 after 24 h and the other half remained as the dihydropyridazine intermediate tautomer X2. It is also worth noting that the release of X4 also led to the generation of pyridazine by-product X6, hence the sample at 24 h consisted of all three species (X2, X4 and X6) in almost equal molar proportions. Thus, NMR results suggest that the observed antimicrobial activity above may be contributed by X2 and/or X6, besides solely by the expected X4.

It is worthwhile noting that the mechanistic investigation by 1H NMR was performed in DMSO solvent and not in water because the self-assembly behavior of TCO-Dendron causes the aqueous sample to become turbid at >1 mg mL−1 concentration, thus making any NMR analysis in water unreliable. Although the antimicrobial assays were performed in aqueous media and the release kinetics of X1 to X7 may be different compared to when they are in the DMSO solvent, we do not envisage that there will be a large disparity between the DMSO and water systems as both have similar polarity. Furthermore, the literature has shown that pH rather than solvent type has a significant impact on the release kinetics.49 Nonetheless, we have repeated the entire 1H NMR analysis in a protic organic solvent, namely methanol, for comparison (SI, Fig. S14 and S15). In the methanol system, only a third of the TCO units triggered release of X4, which was lower than that in the DMSO system (half converted), while most of the rest remained as the dihydropyridazine tautomers X2 and X3. Although the concentration of the functional groups in both DMSO and methanol samples was not identical, it is beyond doubt that the dihydropyridazine tautomer persisted and the conversion to the desired piperidine derivative X4 was incomplete in both cases regardless of solvent type. The incomplete release via this particular click-to-release system is not abnormal, as other studies have also observed similar incomplete conversions in their examples.36,49 It is important to note that attempts have been made to isolate the intermediate tautomers but to no avail.

To determine if the small molecule pyridazine X6 has any antimicrobial activity, an additional control experiment was performed where a model TCO small molecule, (E)-cyclooct-4-enol, was reacted with Tet 1 in the same manner as above in a bacteriostatic assay against P. aeruginosa. No antimicrobial activity was recorded even at the highest tested concentration of 256 µg mL−1. The reaction between this model TCO and Tet 1 will generate a similar pyridazine compound to X6 albeit with an extra alcohol group in the 4-position (SI, Fig. S16). 1H NMR as well as high resolution MS analysis confirmed the presence of the pyridazine compound after the click reaction (SI, Fig. S16). Based on this control experiment, we can rule out X6 as having any contributing factor to the antimicrobial activity of the TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1 sample, and that the said activity must derive from the uncaged cationic amphipathic agent and the intermediate dihydropyridazine tautomer.

Apart from 1H NMR analysis, in-depth high resolution MS analysis was also conducted for the TCO-Dendron and Tet 1 reaction in methanol at different time points (10 min, 3 h and 5 d) and the mass spectra are shown in Fig. 5a–c. The most intense signals found in these mass spectra have been identified and their potential molecular ion adducts are listed in Fig. 5d. In essence, at the early stages of the reaction, that is for the 10 min and 3 h mass spectrum, the most abundant molecular ion adducts observed belonged to the dihydropyridazine tautomers that were either a combination of the 4,5-, 2,5- and/or 1,4-tautomers (i.e., X1, X2 and/or X3). Given that the tautomers share the same elemental mass, it was impossible to distinguish which tautomer was truly present by MS analysis. Nevertheless, it was evident that only these tautomers were identifiable in the 10 min and 3 h mass spectra. This corroborated with the NMR results in Fig. S14 and S15 that showed the presence of a large number of tautomers in the early stages. It was not until longer reaction times that molecular ion adducts containing the uncaged piperidine derivative group were observed in abundance (as XIII and XIV in the 5 d mass spectra of Fig. 5c), and even then, the dihydropyridazine tautomers were still present, confirming their persistence in the click reaction sample. It is worth mentioning that there were two relatively intense peaks in the 5 d mass spectra at 548.45 and 774.57 Da that were unassignable, which were coincidentally close to XIII and XIV and differed by 12 and 6 Da, respectively. While we are unsure about these two unknown peaks, we speculate that they consist of the uncaged piperidine derivative functionality, given that they produced the same isotopic patterns and the decimal parts of their mass-to-charge ratio values (0.45 and 0.57 Da) coincided with those of XIII and XIV. Taking the antimicrobial, NMR and MS results together, it was concluded that TCO-Dendron did not undergo full release upon reaction with Tet 1, and that the intermediate dihydropyridazines possess the capability to deactivate bacterial cells with similar or perhaps greater efficiency compared to the original AMP mimic Ref-Dendron.

Mechanism investigation of the click-to-release system by MS analysis. (a–c) Mass spectra of the clicked samples in methanol at 10 min, 3 h and 5 d. (d) Identification of the potential molecular ion adducts corresponding to the observed peaks in the mass spectra.

Given the success with Tet 1, we subsequently explored the use of a bis-pyridyl tetrazine Tet 2 as a potential trigger to uncage TCO-Dendron. Unfortunately, the bacteriostatic assays involving TCO-Dendron and Tet 2 did not yield any appreciable MIC values (>256 µg mL−1) against P. aeruginosa, indicating that Tet 2 failed to activate the prodrug. A timepoint 1H NMR analysis in DMSO was then performed, and identical spectra were obtained throughout the different timepoints, confirming that the click reaction occurred to form the initial tautomer but stopped prematurely without undergoing further transformation (Fig. 6). High resolution MS analysis in methanol verified the formation of the initial tautomer along with excess Tet 2 as the most abundant species in the sample (SI, Fig. S17). The combined MIC assay, NMR and MS analysis implies that the initial tautomer lacks the ability to deactivate bacteria. The unsuccessful pairing release of TCO-Dendron by Tet 2 is not entirely surprising as studies have shown that the bis-pyridyl tetrazine is highly reactive in clicking with TCO units to form the highly stable initial tautomer (4,5-isomer).53,54 However, we have performed extra preliminary antimicrobial experiments on the TCO-Dendron and Tet 2 system where small portions of HCl were added. By lowering the pH, the HCl-doped sample exhibited an MIC value of 32 µg mL−1 against P. aeruginosa. This is in line with what other studies have shown where the release could progress better under more acidic conditions.50 We aim to explore this point further in more detail with the TCO-Dendron prodrug but in a future study, as this is beyond the scope of the current work.

Biocompatibility

The AUS approach was meant to improve biocompatibility and thus, the toxicity of the amine-caged TCO-Dendron towards model mammalian cells, namely sheep red blood cells (RBCs) and murine embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells, was assessed and compared to the original Ref-Dendron (Fig. 7). First, in terms of hemolytic activity against RBCs, the HC50 value, defined as the concentration that resulted in the lysis of 50% of RBCs, of Ref-Dendron was 102 µg mL−1 while TCO-Dendron only lysed ca. 20% of RBCs at 1024 µg mL−1, thereby proving to have far better hemocompatibility than the unmodified AMP mimic. In addition, the click-activated sample, TCO-Dendron plus Tet 1, has a HC50 value of 378 µg mL−1, which was also not as hemolytic as Ref-Dendron (about 3.7 times less toxic to RBCs). The excellent hemocompatibility of TCO-Dendron is essential in ensuring that the prodrug will not cause toxicity during circulation if it was to be administered as a therapeutic.

Biocompatibility of dendrons. (a) Hemolytic activity of the dendrons toward sheep red blood cells after 2 h of incubation at 37 °C. (b) Cell viability curves of mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C.

Lastly, the cytotoxicity towards MEF cells was compared in terms of the half-maximal concentration that reduces the cell viability by half (i.e., IC50 value), which was estimated based on the metabolic activity of the cells after 24 h of exposure to the dendrons. For the prodrug TCO-Dendron, no reduction in metabolic activity was observed even at 512 µg mL−1, whereas Ref-Dendron had an IC50 value of 140 µg mL−1. Despite not being as hemolytic as the original AMP mimic, the click-activated sample exhibited higher toxicity towards MEF cells than the former, registering an IC50 value of 64 µg mL−1. This finding was interesting and seems to suggest that the intermediate dihydropyridazine due to incomplete release and/or the released clicked pyridazine by-product has a different toxicity profile to that of Ref-Dendron. Regardless, it was evident that the biocompatibility experiments demonstrated the advantage of the AUS approach in reducing the overall toxicity of Ref-Dendron in the form of the TCO-Dendron prodrug. For future practical applications, it is envisaged that the TCO-Dendron prodrug can be used in tandem with a bacteria-targeting ligand-functionalized-tetrazine molecule to achieve localized antimicrobial activation.

Conclusion

In summary, a new AMP mimic prodrug TCO-Dendron that can be chemically activated with dimethyl tetrazine via a bioorthogonal click reaction to confer on-demand antimicrobial activity was developed. Despite possessing hydrophobic trans-cyclooctene termini, TCO-Dendron can undergo self-assembly in water to form uniform nanoparticles and reacts efficiently with dimethyl tetrazine to produce bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects against planktonic and biofilm cells of Gram-negative bacteria, namely P. aeruginosa. While the click reaction proceeded in an ultrafast fashion, the release of the original cationic AMP mimic compound was slow and incomplete, which is something commonly observed and considered to be the bottleneck for click and release systems. However, detailed correlation of NMR, MS and antimicrobial analysis strongly indicated that dendrons containing the intermediate dihydropyridazine tautomer functionality (X2 or X3) that persists due to incomplete release could exhibit antimicrobial activity. Given that the general notion has always been that complete release is necessary to cause the desired biological response, this piece of new information will hopefully provide a better mechanistic understanding of the activation of AMP prodrugs and direct future AMP design. Additionally, TCO-Dendron demonstrated significantly lower hemolytic activity and cytotoxicity towards mammalian cells than the unmodified AMP mimic, thus confirming the validity of this prodrug approach in improving overall biocompatibility whilst enabling the capability for orthogonal antimicrobial activation.
