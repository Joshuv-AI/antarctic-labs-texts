Improving the stability of the covariance-controlled adaptive Langevin thermostat for large-scale Bayesian sampling
Jiani Wei, Xiaocheng Shang

Abstract
Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics and its variants approximate the likelihood of an entire dataset, via random (and typically much smaller) subsets, in the setting of Bayesian sampling. Due to the (often substantial) improvement of the computational efficiency, they have been widely used in large-scale machine learning applications. It has been demonstrated that the so-called covariance-controlled adaptive Langevin (CCAdL) thermostat, which incorporates an additional term involving the covariance matrix of the noisy force, outperforms popular alternative methods. A moving average is used in CCAdL to estimate the covariance matrix of the noisy force, in which case the covariance matrix will converge to a constant matrix in long-time limit. Moreover, it appears in our numerical experiments that the use of a moving average could reduce the stability of the numerical integrators, thereby limiting the largest usable stepsize. In this article, we propose a modified CCAdL (i.e., mCCAdL) thermostat that uses the scaling part of the scaling and squaring method together with a truncated Taylor series approximation to the exponential to numerically approximate the exact solution to the subsystem involving the additional term proposed in CCAdL. We also propose a symmetric splitting method for mCCAdL, instead of an Euler-type discretisation used in the original CCAdL thermostat. We demonstrate in our numerical experiments that the newly proposed mCCAdL thermostat achieves a substantial improvement in the numerical stability over the original CCAdL thermostat, while significantly outperforming popular alternative stochastic gradient methods in terms of the numerical accuracy for large-scale machine learning applications.

1.1
Stochastic gradient

In a typical setting of Bayesian sampling
[
4
,
32
]
, we are interested in sampling from a posterior distribution defined as

\pi({\bm{\theta}}|\mathbf{X})\propto\pi(\mathbf{X}|{\bm{\theta}})\pi({\bm{\theta}})\,,

(1)

where
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}}
denotes the parameter vector of interest,
\mathbf{X}
is the entire dataset, and
\pi(\mathbf{X}|{\bm{\theta}})
and
\pi({\bm{\theta}})
respectively represent the likelihood and prior distributions. As in molecular dynamics, a potential energy function
U({\bm{\theta}})
is introduced by setting
\pi({\bm{\theta}}|\mathbf{X})\propto\exp(-\beta U({\bm{\theta}}))
, where
\beta
is a positive parameter that is proportional to the reciprocal temperature in an associated physical system, i.e.,
\beta^{-1}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, with
k_{\mathrm{B}}
being the Boltzmann constant and
T
the temperature.
By taking the logarithm of (
1
), we can rewrite the potential energy function as

U({\bm{\theta}})=-\log\pi(\mathbf{X}|{\bm{\theta}})-\log\pi({\bm{\theta}})\,.

(2)

Based on the assumption that the data points are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the negative gradient of the potential energy can be written as

-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla\log\pi({\mathbf{x}}_{i}|{\bm{\theta}})+\nabla\log\pi({\bm{\theta}})\,,

(3)

where
N
denotes the size of the entire dataset. However, in many large-scale machine learning applications with a very large
N
, evaluations based on the entire dataset at each iteration, for example in MCMC and HMC methods, are computationally infeasible. In order to reduce the computational cost, random (and typically much smaller) subsets (also known as “subsampling”) are preferred in stochastic gradient methods, in which case the log likelihood of the dataset is approximated as

\log\pi(\mathbf{X}|{\bm{\theta}})\approx\frac{N}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\pi({\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}}|{\bm{\theta}})\,,

(4)

where
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a subset of the entire dataset
\mathbf{X}
, randomly selected at each iteration, and
n\ll N
denotes the size of the subset. In this case, (
3
) becomes a “noisy force”
\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}}
, which can be written as

\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})=-\nabla\tilde{U}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{N}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\log\pi({\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}}|{\bm{\theta}})+\nabla\log\pi({\bm{\theta}})\,.

(5)

As in
[
6
,
7
,
35
]
, we assume the stochastic gradient noise to be Gaussian with mean zero and unknown covariance, in which case the noisy force (
5
) can be written as

\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})=-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}})+\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}\,,

(6)

where
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N_{\mathrm{d}}}
represents the covariance matrix of the noisy force,
{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N_{\mathrm{d}}}
is typically a diagonal mass matrix, and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}}
denotes a vector of i.i.d. standard normal random variables.
As explained in
[
24
]
, in a typical setting of numerical integration with an associated stepsize
h
, we have

h\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})=h\left(-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}})+\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}\right)=-h\nabla U({\bm{\theta}})+\sqrt{h}\left(\sqrt{h\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})}\right){\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}\,.

(7)

1.2
Related work

Proposed by Welling and Teh, the stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD)
[
38
]
combines the ideas of stochastic optimisation
[
31
]
and traditional Brownian dynamics, with a sequence of stepsizes decreasing to zero. However, a fixed stepsize is often preferred in practice, which we also adopt in this article, as in Vollmer et al.
[
37
]
, where a modified SGLD (mSGLD) method was proposed in order to reduce the sampling bias.

Popular alternative methods include the stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (SGHMC) method proposed by Chen et al.
[
6
]
and the stochastic gradient Nosé–Hoover thermostat (SGNHT) proposed by Ding et al.
[
7
]
, both of which will be included in numerical comparisons in this article. Based on a second order Langevin dynamics, SGHMC includes a parameter-dependent diffusion matrix in order to effectively offset the stochastic perturbation of the gradient. As a result, SGHMC is able to maintain a desired stationary distribution. In contrast, it has been demonstrated in
[
24
]
that first order dynamics, such as SGLD (with a fixed stepsize), is unable to dissipate excess noise in gradient approximations, in which case first order dynamics is unlikely to be able to maintain a desired stationary distribution. However, it turns out that, the additional diffusion matrix in SGHMC is difficult to accommodate in practice, which may lead to a significant adverse effect on the sampling of the target distribution
[
7
]
. SGNHT adopts the “thermostat” idea that is widely used in molecular dynamics
[
10
,
22
]
. It adjusts the kinetic energy during the simulation in order to maintain a constant (average) temperature, leading to the preservation of the canonical ensemble. SGNHT is also able to dissipate excess noise in gradient approximations and also maintains a desired stationary distribution
[
24
]
. However, it relies on the assumption that the covariance matrix of the noisy force is constant, which may not be the case in practice.

1.3
Covariance-controlled adaptive Langevin thermostat

It is worth mentioning that, in SGNHT, the covariance matrix is assumed to be constant, i.e.,
\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}
, where
\sigma
is a positive constant and
{\mathbf{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N_{\mathrm{d}}}
is the identity matrix.
However, as explained in
[
35
]
, it is essential to consider parameter-dependent noise as the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})
is expected to be naturally dependent on the parameter.
To this end, proposed by Shang et al.
[
35
]
, the CCAdL thermostat generalises the idea of SGNHT and, as in SGHMC, also incorporates an additional term involving the covariance matrix of the noisy force, which is intended to effectively offset the stochastic perturbation of the gradient. CCAdL can be written as a standard Itō stochastic differential equation (SDE) system:

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,,

(8a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}}){\rm d}t+\sqrt{h\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}{\rm d}\mathbf{W}-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t-\xi{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2A\beta^{-1}}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}{\rm d}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{A}}\,,

(8b)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle={\mu}^{-1}\left[{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}-N_{\mathrm{d}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right]{\rm d}t\,,

(8c)

where
{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}}
represents the associated momenta,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
is an auxiliary variable governed by a Nosé–Hoover device
[
15
,
30
]
via a negative feedback mechanism (see more discussions in the original article
[
35
]
),

\mu
is a coupling parameter that is referred to as the “thermal mass” in the molecular dynamics setting, and
{\rm d}{\mathrm{{\bf W}}}
and
{\rm d}{\mathrm{{\bf W}}_{\rm A}}
respectively represent vectors of independent Wiener increments (often informally denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\rm d}t{\mathbf{I}})

[
6
]
). Note that the coefficient
\sqrt{2A\beta^{-1}}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}
represents the strength of the artificial noise added into the system in order to improve the ergodicity, where the “effective friction”
A
is a positive parameter that is proportional to the variance of the artificial noise. The incorporation of the additional term,
-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t
, involving the parameter-dependent covariance matrix, is intended to offset the parameter-dependent noise also involving the covariance matrix (
6
). As a result, it can be shown that the CCAdL thermostat (
8
) preserves a modified Gibbs stationary distribution
[
35
]
:

\hat{\rho}_{\beta}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathbf{p}},\xi)=Z^{-1}\exp\left({-\beta H({\bm{\theta}},{\mathbf{p}})}\right)\exp\left(-\beta\mu(\xi-A)^{2}/2\right)\,.

(9)

where
Z
is the normalising constant and
H({\bm{\theta}},{\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}/2+U({\bm{\theta}})
is the Hamiltonian.

In practice, we do not know
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})
a priori. Therefore, we have to estimate
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})
during the simulation.
In the original CCAdL article
[
35
]
, we have
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=N^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{t}/n
and
g({\bm{\theta}};{\mathbf{x}})=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log\pi({\mathbf{x}}|{\bm{\theta}})
. It was assumed that
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
does not change dramatically over time and a moving average
[
1
]
was used to estimate
{\mathbf{I}}_{t}
:

\hat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{t}=(1-\kappa_{t})\hat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{t-1}+\kappa_{t}{\mathbf{V}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,,

(10)

where
\kappa_{t}=1/t
and

{\mathbf{V}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}})-\bar{g}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\right)\left(g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}})-\bar{g}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

(11)

is the empirical covariance of the gradient, with
\bar{g}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
being the mean gradient of the log likelihood computed from a subset

\bar{g}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}})\,.

(12)

One potential issue of the moving average (
10
) is that the covariance matrix will converge to a constant matrix in long-time limit (i.e.,
\hat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{t}\to\hat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{t-1}
as
t\to\infty
in (
10
)). However, the covariance matrix is naturally parameter-dependent. Moreover, it is computationally infeasible to estimate the full covariance matrix in high dimension, for which a diagonal approximation of the covariance matrix was employed in high dimension in the original CCAdL article
[
35
]
. Although the diagonal approximation of the covariance matrix worked well in some of the numerical experiments in high dimension in
[
35
]
, we could numerically integrate the subsystem involving the additional term without estimating the full covariance matrix in order to reduce the computational cost.

1.4
Contributions

In this article, we propose a modified CCAdL (mCCAdL) thermostat that does not rely on the moving average. Moreover, mCCAdL does not need to explicitly estimate the full covariance matrix in integrating the subsystem involving the additional term; instead, it makes use of the fact that the exact solution to the subsystem involving the additional term is essentially a product of a matrix exponential and a vector, which can be solved more efficiently in practice. We also propose a symmetric splitting method for mCCAdL, instead of an Euler-type discretisation used in the original CCAdL thermostat. Our numerical experiments in large-scale machine learning applications demonstrate that the newly proposed mCCAdL thermostat substantially outperforms popular alternative methods in terms of the numerical stability (measured by the largest usable stepsize) and accuracy (measured by the test error), leading to significantly improved computational efficiency.

The rest of the article is organised as follows.
In Section
2
, we propose the mCCAdL thermostat that uses a novel approach to integrate the subsystem involving the additional term; we also propose a symmetric splitting method for the mCCAdL thermostat. Various numerical experiments are reported in Section
3
to evaluate the performance of mCCAdL in large-scale machine learning applications. Finally, Section
4
summarises our findings.

2
Modified covariance-controlled adaptive Langevin thermostat

In this section, we introduce the mCCAdL method, including the estimation of the parameter-dependent covariance matrix in Section
2.1
, the integration of the subsystem involving the additional term in Section
2.2
, and the design of splitting methods in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Estimating the parameter-dependent covariance matrix

In addition to the assumption that the stochastic gradient noise follows a normal distribution (
6
), we further assume that the size of the random subset
n
is large enough for the central limit theorem to hold, in which case we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}}_{r_{i}})\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})],\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Cov}[g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})]\right)\,,

(13)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})]
and
\mathrm{Cov}[g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})]
are the expectation and covariance of the gradient
g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\log\pi({\mathbf{x}}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
, respectively. We can easily see that the expectation of the noisy force (
5
) is equal to that of the clean (full) force (
3
), that is,
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[-\nabla\tilde{U}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})]={\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}}_{t})]
, and therefore the noisy force can be written as

-\nabla\tilde{U}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}}_{t})+{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\right)\,,

(14)

where the covariance matrix of the noisy force is calculated as

\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\equiv\frac{N^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Cov}[g({\bm{\theta}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}})]=\frac{N^{2}}{n}{\mathbf{V}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,.

(15)

Note that we no longer use the moving average (
10
) and instead estimate the covariance matrix “directly” as in (
15
).

2.2
Integrating the subsystem involving the additional term

Let us now consider the subsystem involving the additional term in (
8
):

{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}=-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,.

(16)

It turns out that we do not need to estimate the full covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})
before numerically integrating the subsystem (
16
) using for instance an Euler-type integrator as in the original CCAdL article
[
35
]
. Instead, we can make use of the fact that there exists an exact solution to (
16
):

{\mathbf{p}}(t)=e^{t\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\theta}})}{\mathbf{p}}(0)\,,

(17)

where
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\theta}})=-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N_{\mathrm{d}}}
is a symmetric matrix. Subsequently, following
[
2
]
, we can use the scaling part of the scaling and squaring method together with a truncated Taylor series approximation to the exponential to numerically approximate the exact solution in (
17
), which is essentially a product of a matrix exponential and a vector.

In order to reduce the computational cost, we first shift the matrix
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
to obtain
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}=\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}-\tilde{\mu}{\mathbf{I}}
, where
\tilde{\mu}=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}})/N_{\mathrm{d}}
and
{\mathbf{I}}
is again the identity matrix. The shifting allows us to rewrite the exact solution in (
17
) as

e^{t\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\mathbf{p}}(0)=e^{t(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\tilde{\mu}\mathbf{I})}{\mathbf{p}}(0)=e^{t\tilde{\mu}}e^{t\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\mathbf{p}}(0)\,.

(18)

We further introduce a scaling parameter
s\geq 1
, which is an integer. This allows us to “scale” the exact solution in (
17
) as

e^{t\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\mathbf{p}}(0)=e^{t\tilde{\mu}}\left(e^{\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}}\right)^{s}{\mathbf{p}}(0)\,,

(19)

where each scaled matrix exponential
e^{\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}}
is approximated by using a truncated Taylor series of degree
m
:

e^{\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)^{j}\,.

(20)

Subsequently, starting from
v_{0}={\mathbf{p}}(0)
, we iteratively compute

v_{k+1}=e^{\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}}v_{k}\,,\quad k=0,1,\dots,s-1\,.

(21)

In the end, we multiply
v_{s}
by
e^{t\tilde{\mu}}
to rewrite the exact solution in (
17
) as

e^{t\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\mathbf{p}}(0)=e^{t\tilde{\mu}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)^{j}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)^{j}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{t}{s}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)^{j}\right)}_{s~\mathrm{times}}{\mathbf{p}}(0)\,.

(22)

Note that we can determine an optimal choice of
s
and
m
by exploiting the backward error analysis of Higham
[
13
,
14
]
, as refined by Al-Mohy and Higham
[
3
]
. Moreover, one particular advantage of the current approach (
22
) is that we do not need to explicitly forming the matrix exponential,
\exp(t\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}})
, before computing its product with the vector,
{\mathbf{p}}(0)
. In what follows we refer the newly proposed approach as the mCCAdL thermostat.

2.3
Splitting methods

The optimal design of splitting methods in ergodic SDEs has been an active area of research in the mathematics community
[
20
,
23
,
24
,
22
,
25
,
33
,
34
,
36
,
9
,
40
]
. It has been demonstrated in
[
24
]
that one particular type of symmetric splitting methods for the SGNHT method (equivalent to the adaptive Langevin thermostat
[
17
,
24
]
) with a clean (full) gradient (i.e., without subsampling) inherits the superconvergence property (i.e., fourth order convergence to a modified Gibbs stationary distribution for configurational quantities) recently proved in the setting of Langevin dynamics
[
21
,
20
]
. However, a naive nonsymmetric splitting method was used for CCAdL in the original CCAdL article
[
35
]
. In what follows we propose a symmetric splitting method for mCCAdL. More specifically, the vector field of the CCAdL/mCCAdL system (
8
) is decomposed into five parts, which we label as “A”, “B”, “C”, “O”, and “D”, respectively:

\displaystyle{\rm d}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\theta}}\\
{\mathbf{p}}\\
\xi\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\\
\mathbf{0}\\
0\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{A}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})\\
0\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{B}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{p}}\\
0\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{C}}

(23)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-\xi{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2A\beta^{-1}}{\mathbf{M}}^{1/2}{\rm d}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{A}}\\
0\end{array}\right]}_{\mathrm{O}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}\\
{\mu}^{-1}\left[{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}-N_{\mathrm{d}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right]\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{D}}\,.

As explained in detail in
[
24
]
, parts “A”, “B”, “O”, and “D” can all be solved “exactly”, while part “C” can be approximated as described in the previous subsection. Note that part B is written slightly differently here as compared in (
8
); this is to emphasise that in practice we do not need to explicitly estimate the covariance matrix appearing in part B—the noisy force
\tilde{{\mathbf{F}}}({\bm{\theta}})
is generated by the subsampling procedure (
5
). The generators associated with each part are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\,,

(24a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla U({\bm{\theta}})\cdot\nabla_{{\mathbf{p}}}+(h/2)\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{M}}\nabla^{2}_{{\mathbf{p}}}\right)\,,

(24b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{C}}

\displaystyle=-(h/2)\beta\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}}){\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{{\mathbf{p}}}\,,

(24c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle=-\xi{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{{\mathbf{p}}}+A\beta^{-1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\mathbf{M}}\nabla^{2}_{{\mathbf{p}}}\right)\,,

(24d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\left[{\mathbf{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}-N_{\mathrm{d}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right]\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\,.

(24e)

Overall, the generator of the CCAdL/mCCAdL system (
8
) can be written
as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{C}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}}\,.

(25)

The flow map (or phase space propagator) of the system,
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\exp\left(t\mathcal{L}\right)
, is used to formally denote the solution operator. It turns out that a large number of approximations of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
can be obtained as products (taken in different arrangements) of exponentials of the splitting terms. It has been demonstrated that different splittings and/or combinations give dramatically different performance in practice
[
24
]
. It has also been well documented that symmetrical splitting methods often outperform their nonsymmetric counterparts (e.g., in the case of stochastic gradient methods
[
5
,
24
]
). In this article, we propose the following symmetric splitting method, which we refer as the “BAODCDOAB” method:

e^{h\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{BAODCDOAB}}}=e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}}}e^{h\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{C}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}}e^{\frac{h}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}\,,

(26)

where
\exp\left(h\mathcal{L}_{f}\right)
denotes the phase space propagator related to the corresponding vector field
f
. Note that the steplengths associated with various operations are uniform, spanning the interval
h
. Therefore, each of the B, A, O, and D steps in (
26
) is taken with a steplength of
h/2
, while a steplength of
h
is used in the C step. Note that the C step only appears once in the sequence, meaning that we only need to approximate the associated C part of the system once at each iteration. The procedure of the BAODCDOAB method is summarised in Algorithm
1
.
It is worth mentioning that the noisy force computed at the end of each iteration can be reused at the start of the next iteration; therefore only one (noisy) force calculation is needed in the BAODCDOAB method at each iteration. Based on the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion (e.g., in
[
24
,
36
]
), it can be demonstrated that the newly proposed symmetric splitting method associated with mCCAdL has a weak second order convergence to the modified Gibbs stationary distribution (
9
), if the approximation (
22
) in the C part is an at-least-second-order approximation to its exact solution (
17
). Note that it is nontrivial to achieve a weak second order convergence to a stationary distribution when the stochastic gradient noise is present (even for symmetric splitting methods). In contrast, the methods associated with SGHMC, SGNHT, and CCAdL are all expected to have a weak first order convergence to their respective stationary distributions.

3.1
2-Wasserstein distance evaluation with Bayesian linear regression

We begin with a Bayesian linear regression problem and evaluate the samplers by considering the 2-Wasserstein distance between an empirical distribution and the true posterior distribution
[
12
]
. We generated
N=10{,}000
data points with
N_{\mathrm{d}}=100
according to

{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{I}})\,,\quad y_{i}={\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}+\delta_{i}\,,

(27)

where
\delta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are i.i.d. standard normal variables. We used a likelihood function of

\pi\left(\mathbf{y}|{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{X}\right)\propto\exp\!\left[-\left(\mathbf{y}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{X}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{y}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{X}\right)/2\right]\,,

(28)

where
\mathbf{X}=\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N}
and
\mathbf{y}=\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
, and a prior distribution of
\pi({\bm{\theta}})\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\lambda{\mathbf{I}})
, where we used
\lambda=10
in our numerical experiments. We then derived the posterior distribution as a Gaussian distribution
\pi({\bm{\theta}}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{m}},\bm{\Sigma})
, where the mean vector is given by
{\mathbf{m}}=\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
with the covariance matrix being

\bm{\Sigma}=\left[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}+(\lambda{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}\right]^{-1}\,.

(29)

At each iteration
k
, we randomly selected a subset of size
n=500
with replacement. We then formed an empirical Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k},\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}\right)
and computed the 2-Wasserstein distance between the empirical distribution and the true posterior distribution, using an explicit formula that characterises the 2-Wasserstein
distance between any two Gaussian distributions

]

4
Conclusions

In this article, we have proposed a modified version of the covariance-controlled adaptive Langevin (CCAdL) thermostat originally proposed in
[
35
]
. While CCAdL relies on a moving average to estimate the covariance matrix of the noisy force generated from subsampling, in the newly proposed mCCAdL method we have used the scaling part of the scaling and squaring method together with a truncated Taylor series approximation to the exponential to numerically approximate the exact solution to the subsystem involving the additional term (i.e., the C part). We have further proposed a symmetric splitting method for mCCAdL. We have demonstrated in large-scale machine learning applications that the newly proposed mCCAdL method achieves a substantial improvement in numerical stability (measured by the largest usable stepsize) over the original CCAdL method, while significantly outperforming popular alternative stochastic gradient methods in terms of accuracy (measured by the test error). The methodologies presented in this article could be of use in more general settings of large-scale Bayesian sampling and optimisation, which we leave for future work.
