A unilateral presentation of papillary renal cell carcinoma in a pediatric patient with tuberous sclerosis complex: A rare case report
Authors: Farah Budairy, Reshdy Almaghoush, Mohammad Obada Alsadi, Fadi Tanous, Judy Abdelrahim, Alaa Almoarawi, Qamar Salloum, Manal Mouhamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is a multisystem genetic disorder predominantly identified by benign hamartomas; however, it harbors a hidden risk for early-onset malignancies. We report a 15-year-old female presenting with extensive mucocutaneous hallmarks, including facial angiofibromas and Koenen's tumors. Comprehensive systemic surveillance revealed neurological tubers, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, and pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis. Crucially, imaging identified asymptomatic right renal mass, histopathologically confirmed as papillary renal cell carcinoma following nephrectomy. This rare pediatric malignancy underscores the dermatologists role as the first line of defense. Benign skin lesions in TSC can mask aggressive internal cancers, necessitating immediate multidisciplinary screening for life-saving intervention.

Introduction

Tuberous sclerosis complex is a rare multisystem genetic disorder characterized by the development of benign hamartomatous lesions involving multiple organs, most commonly the skin, brain, kidneys, lungs, and heart.1,2 The disease exhibits marked clinical heterogeneity, with manifestations ranging from isolated cutaneous findings to significant neurological, renal, and pulmonary involvement, which may evolve over time.2 Cutaneous features are often among the earliest and most visible manifestations and frequently serve as the initial clue leading to clinical suspicion of the disorder.3

The dermatological spectrum of tuberous sclerosis complex includes facial angiofibromas, fibrous cephalic plaques, periungual fibromas, gingival fibromas, Shagreen patches, and other connective tissue lesions.1,3,4 Facial angiofibromas are considered one of the most characteristic findings and typically present during childhood, gradually increasing in number and size with age.3 Gingival fibromas and periungual fibromas, although less frequently reported, are well-recognized manifestations and may present as part of the mucocutaneous involvement of the disease.1,4 These lesions can be asymptomatic but often cause cosmetic concern and may prompt medical consultation.

Beyond cutaneous manifestations, Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is frequently associated with neurological abnormalities, including cortical and subcortical tubers and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas, which are often linked to seizures and neurocognitive or learning difficulties. Renal involvement constitutes a major source of morbidity, potentially manifesting as angiomyolipomas, renal lipomas, or, less frequently, renal cell carcinoma. Furthermore, pulmonary involvement—particularly lymphangioleiomyomatosis—is described predominantly in female patients and may be detected incidentally during systemic imaging.2

This report presents a female adolescent with extensive dermatological involvement and associated systemic findings consistent with tuberous sclerosis complex, highlighting the role of dermatological assessment in initiating comprehensive evaluation and surveillance.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old female presented with multiple asymptomatic red-to-pink facial papules, which first appeared at age seven and gradually increased in number and size, causing significant cosmetic concern. Her medical history included three seizures occurring between the ages of four and eight, and learning difficulties without global developmental delay. She had no known chronic illnesses or allergies; however, she was the product of a consanguineous marriage. Her surgical history was notable for the excision of a lower lip lesion, though no histopathological evaluation was performed at the time.

Physical examination revealed normal vital signs. Dermatological assessment identified multiple firm, smooth, pink-to-red papules on the nasolabial folds, cheeks, and chin, consistent with facial angiofibromas (Fig. 1A). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed findings consistent with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC), including early-stage lymphangioleiomyomatosis, multiple hepatic and renal lipomas, bilateral renal angiomyolipomas, bilateral adrenal hyperplasia, and a right renal mass suspected to be renal cell carcinoma (Fig. 4). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain demonstrated cortical and subcortical tubers, along with a contrast-enhancing mass in the left lateral ventricle, suggestive of subependymal giant cell astrocytoma. Following the neurological assessment, the diagnosis of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) was confirmed. Ophthalmological evaluation revealed bilateral optic disc elevation; however, cardiac assessments, including ECG, were unremarkable, and an EEG was not performed. Following a thorough multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent an open surgical exploration. The right renal mass was in the mid-pole, measuring (6∗5∗5) cm. An intraoperative frozen section biopsy was performed, which definitively confirmed a malignant renal cell carcinoma. Due to the large size and central mid-pole location of the tumor, a nephron-sparing surgery was technically unfeasible and carried a high risk of positive surgical margins, necessitating a total right nephrectomy to achieve complete oncological clearance.Fig. 4Contrast-enhanced CT scan showing a right renal mass consistent with renal cell carcinoma.

At the most recent follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved renal function and no new neurological or respiratory manifestations. She has since been enrolled in a structured multidisciplinary surveillance program, consisting of biannual renal imaging, periodic neurological monitoring, and annual ophthalmological evaluations.

Discussion

Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by a predisposition to hamartoma formation, primarily resulting from mutations in either the TSC1 or TSC2 genes. While these mutations drive tumor pathogenesis, the majority of cases occur sporadically with no prior family history, often resulting in delayed diagnosis.5,6

Cutaneous and mucosal manifestations are cardinal features of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC). While these lesions, such as facial angiofibromas, Shagreen patches, fibrous cephalic plaques, and ungual fibromas, may often be dismissed as aesthetic concerns, they serve as critical diagnostic clues pointing toward systemic involvement. Beyond the skin, the disease frequently presents with dental enamel pits, gingival fibromas, and retinal astrocytic hamartomas. Our patient displayed these same classic signs, which guided her systemic diagnosis.6

In addition to these dermatological manifestations, the patient's clinical history was notable for recurrent seizures, which affect approximately 80% to 90% of TSC patients. Furthermore, neurodevelopmental delays and behavioral issues are seen in up to 90% of patients with TSC.6,7 Subependymal nodules are present in 80% to 90% of patients, while about 10% to 20% develop subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs),8 which were detected during our patient's brain MRI evaluation.

Pulmonary involvement, specifically lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is typically identified in adult women upon symptomatic presentation, more than 75% of TSC patients were diagnosed with LAM only after the onset of clinical symptoms. Although LAM affects approximately 30% of TSC patients, it carries a risk of significant morbidity, including spontaneous pneumothorax and progressive respiratory failure.9,10 Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed pulmonary findings in our patient consistent with LAM.

Most intriguingly, systemic evaluation extended beyond the pulmonary findings to uncover complex renal abnormalities, including multiple angiomyolipomas and a suspicious right renal mass, which was histopathologically confirmed as renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Although rare, malignant transformation is a significant concern in TSC; RCC is the most frequent malignancy observed in this population, occurring in approximately 2–5% of cases. Classically, RCC in TSC presents at a younger age than in the general population, with a predilection for bilateral and multifocal involvement.2,7,11 In contrast, our patient exhibited a unique, unifocal, and unilateral presentation. Notably, she remained asymptomatic, emphasizing that the absence of symptoms does not preclude underlying malignancy. Consequently, based on a multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient underwent a total right nephrectomy. Although nephron-sparing surgery (partial nephrectomy) is always the preferred gold standard in TSC patients to mitigate the risks of future contralateral renal events, the large (6∗5∗5) cm size and mid-pole location—confirmed as RCC on intraoperative frozen section—left no safe alternative to a radical open approach.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case uniquely highlights an exceptionally rare renal presentation of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC): a unifocal, strictly unilateral Papillary Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) in an asymptomatic adolescent. This asymmetric oncological behavior proves that aggressive, silent malignancies can develop independently alongside benign hamartomas, demonstrating that early mucocutaneous lesions remain vital diagnostic clues that prevent missing such critical internal pathologies. Therefore, managing this dual clinical challenge requires swift intraoperative frozen section differentiation followed by a radical open approach.
