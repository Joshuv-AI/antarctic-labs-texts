Parent Partnership in Neonatal Research: Facilitators, Barriers and Contributions of the Irish PADDINGToN Parent Advisory Group
Authors: S. Giva, B. Cleary, M. Daly, L. Bracken, P. Bradley, B. Carter, J. Clark, A. Gill, C. Sutton, N. McCallion, F. O'Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPatient and public involvement (PPI) is increasingly recognised as integral to high‐quality neonatal research, yet practical guidance on its implementation remains limited.PurposeThe primary aim of this paper is to describe the establishment and operation of a Parent Advisory Group (PAG) embedded within a national neonatal medicines feasibility study, including facilitators and barriers for ongoing PPI involvement in research. The second aim is to describe how the group's involvement informed study design, recruitment and retention strategies to optimise the research study.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was undertaken and reported in accordance with the GRIPP2 checklist. Parents with recent neonatal unit experience were recruited through national neonatal networks and institutional PPI platforms. Feedback from meeting transcripts, reflective notes and questionnaires was analysed using an inductive descriptive thematic approach.ResultsSeven parents contributed to study materials, recruitment strategies and interpretation of findings through group meetings and asynchronous feedback. Flexible engagement approaches supported participation, although sustaining involvement over time and managing the emotional impact of participation presented challenges.ConclusionStructured and flexible models of engagement, paired with appropriate supports, can support meaningful parental involvement in neonatal research. This study highlights how parental involvement can inform study design, recruitment and retention planning, and strategies for sustaining parent engagement.

Introduction

Healthcare has, in recent years, become more inclusive and professionals are actively engaging patients in medical decision‐making (Westerink et al. 2023). With widespread access to information, patients are increasingly aware of their rights (Smailhodzic et al. 2016; Benetoli et al. 2018). The same is true for healthcare research, where patient and public involvement (PPI) have evolved towards more active collaboration, with contributors bringing lived experiences to inform study design (Westerink et al. 2023). This collaboration has recognised benefits (Brett et al. 2014; Agyei‐Manu et al. 2023). PPI can provide valuable insights into barriers and facilitators that may affect study delivery and acceptability (Westerink et al. 2023; Ocloo et al. 2021; Melchior et al. 2021), promote transparency and inclusivity in funded research and enable patients and the public to influence healthcare development more directly (Baumann and Brütt 2021; Gilfoyle et al. 2022).

In Ireland, national funding bodies have increasingly promoted the inclusion of PPI in health‐related research by prioritising funding for studies that formally incorporate PPI within their design (Gilfoyle et al. 2022).

PPI involvement is essential to bridge the gap between academic expertise and lived experience; its effectiveness depends on adequate communication and support, including appropriate financial resources. Investment in researcher training and resources is necessary to ensure that PPI becomes embedded within routine research practice (Finderup et al. 2022).

The role of PPI in paediatric and neonatal research is particularly relevant (Menzies et al. 2016). In neonatal research, parents have been involved in planning, design and interpretation of research studies, improving acceptability, feasibility and relevance (Molloy et al. 2019; Levene et al. 2022; Bjerregaard et al. 2025; Darby et al. 2025).

This group has previously reported on the experiences of parents of neonates as part of the development of a medicines information resource—the PADDINGToN‐1 study (PArent co‐Designed Drug and INformation resources for parents and Guardians Taking Neonates home); this study was a co‐collaboration between Ireland and UK researchers and parents (Bracken et al. 2022; Bracken et al. 2023). Parents actively contributed to co‐design of the resources to support safe medicine administration after discharge. This collaboration highlighted the value of involving parents as active contributors, resulting in outputs that are both evidence‐based and family‐centred.

Building on these foundations, the group established an Irish PADDINGToN‐2 Parent Advisory Group (PPAG) to guide the design and implementation of a feasibility study in Ireland, embedding parental perspectives to ensure the project's acceptability within the Irish healthcare context. In parallel, a similar group was also established in the United Kingdom (led by the group in Alder Hey Children's Hospital). This manuscript describes the establishment and operation of the PPAG, including factors that supported and challenged ongoing involvement, in line with the GRIPP2 (Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public) (Staniszewska et al. 2017). We also describe how parent involvement informed study design and implementation of the PADDINGToN‐2 feasibility study.

Methodology

Study Design

This was a qualitative descriptive study exploring the formation, operation and contributions of the Irish PPAG established as part of the Irish PADDINGToN‐2 feasibility study.

The feasibility study recruited parents of newborns admitted to the neonatal unit during the study period; the aim of the feasibility study is to determine whether previously designed medicine information resources are beneficial to parents and carers of neonates discharged home on medications.

The PPAG was an independent group comprising parent representatives with lived experience; the PPAG was formed prior to the onset of the feasibility study period in order to inform the design of study materials for the feasibility study, including patient information leaflets, consent forms, survey instruments and recruitment materials, and to guide recruitment and retention strategies.

Formation and Composition of the Irish PPAG

Parent representatives with lived experience were recruited between November 2024 and March 2025. Recruitment materials, including posters, were disseminated through the Irish Neonatal Health Alliance (INHA) social media platforms and the PPI Ignite website. Eligibility criteria for the PPAG included: (1) being a parent or caregiver of a child previously admitted to an NNU within the previous 5 years, and (2) that same child being discharged home from the NNU on prescribed medications. Members of the PPAG did not take part in the feasibility study and instead were essential in informing the design of the feasibility study materials and developing strategies to optimise the feasibility study.

Parents fulfilling both eligibility criteria met with a member of the research team individually for an introductory meeting to outline the purpose and expectations of participation in the group and outline the goals of the research study. Parents were given time to consider participation before confirming involvement.

Structure and Operation of the PPAG

The aim was to conduct a meeting with the group every 3 months; meetings were scheduled according to members' availability.

Communication was conducted exclusively via email and all meetings held virtually through Microsoft Teams, with recordings made for transcription and documentation purposes and subsequently deleted.

The first group meeting took place in April 2025 and a subsequent meeting took place in July 2025.

During the first meeting, the research team provided an overview of the PADDINGToN‐2 study. Members received a booklet outlining the purpose of the PPAG. An agenda was circulated in advance and detailed minutes and reflective notes, taken by one of the researchers during the meetings, were shared subsequently.

Due to members' scheduling constraints, further full‐group meetings were not feasible. Regular communication was maintained asynchronously, with the research team providing updates and PPAG members offering feedback and suggestions to improve recruitment and retention strategies.

To facilitate structured input, brief questionnaires were developed to guide members' feedback on study materials and procedures.

After conclusion of the recruitment to the feasibility study, a pre‐designed questionnaire was also shared with the PPAG to capture their perspectives on their motivations for joining the PPAG and their perceived role within the group.

PPAG members contributed to interpretation of findings through feedback on emerging themes and manuscript drafts.

Data Analysis

To ensure trustworthiness, feedback from meeting transcripts, reflective notes and questionnaire responses was considered collectively during analysis. An inductive descriptive thematic approach was used for final analysis. Themes were refined iteratively to capture recurring patterns relating to PPAG members' motivations, experiences and perceived contributions to the study. To enhance credibility of the reporting, PPAG members reviewed manuscript drafts and provided feedback to ensure their experiences and contributions were accurately represented.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the recruitment and formation of the Irish PPAG was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee at the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland. Written consent for participation was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and members were reminded that they could withdraw at any stage without consequence. No formal financial remuneration was provided; however, to acknowledge participants' time and contributions, a voucher was offered in line with institutional PPI involvement protocols.

All data derived from recorded meetings were stored securely in accordance with institutional data protection policies and ethical standards.

Results

A total of seven parents were recruited. The group encompassed a broad spectrum of neonatal care experiences. The PPAG was ethnically diverse: two members identified as Caucasian; three identified as Black (including Black‐Irish, Caribbean or African backgrounds) and two identified as having mixed or multiple ethnicities.

All members identified as mothers or birthing parents. Five participants were between 35 and 44 years of age, while the remaining two were between 25 and 34 years. English was the first language for all members, enabling full participation in reading study materials. Six of the seven members had experienced preterm birth, with gestational ages ranging from 24 to 33 weeks, while the remaining member had given birth at term. Each parent's infant had required admission to a NNU for more than 1 week, with the mean duration of admission approximately 3 months.

The average number of medications prescribed on discharge was two to three.

Two overarching themes were identified. The first theme—Establishing and Sustaining Parent Engagement captured parents' motivations for joining the PPAG and the factors that facilitated or challenged their ongoing participation. The second theme, Parent Partners Influencing Research Design and Delivery, captured parents' perceptions of their role as well as the objective impact of their suggestions and contributions to optimise the feasibility study.

Establishing and Sustaining Parent Engagement as PPI in Research

Motivations for Participation

Parents expressed a strong desire to support other families navigating similar challenges. Their lived experiences served as both emotional and practical motivators. Several members described their involvement as an opportunity to contribute to meaningful research and change the healthcare system while ensuring that parents' voices and needs are recognised as valuable. As one member explained, ‘I wanted to make a change …’ adding that ‘it feels great to be part of something and to give back’ while another mentioned ‘to be able to help other families and carers. We were in the same situation 6 years ago… Having other parents' knowledge and input gives a different perspective.’ One individual highlighted the value of shaping research through lived experience: ‘I was motivated by the opportunity to share my lived experience and contribute to research that directly supports parents and families. I wanted to help make sure parents' voices are heard and reflected in how neonatal care and research are shaped.’

Facilitators and Challenges for Continued Engagement

Clear communication, flexibility in participation and providing materials in advance were identified as important facilitators to continued engagement. Parents noted that other commitments often made scheduled meetings difficult to attend, and that the option to contribute offline enabled them to remain engaged without the added pressure of fixed times. As one member explained, ‘sometimes there isn't the time there, but I felt that it wasn't too taxing on my time, and it definitely felt that I contributed something to it and it was worth my time.’

Some parents also reflected on the emotional dimension. One parent shared that the experience ‘… brought back memories, but also made me feel seen’, highlighting both the emotional challenge and the personal value associated with involvement.

Parent Partners Influencing Research Design and Delivery

Perceived Influence on Study Design and Delivery

When asked whether they felt their contributions had meaningfully informed the study, one member affirmed this strongly: ‘Yes, I do. It's clear that the team genuinely takes on board feedback from the members. Seeing changes made to the study design based on our input shows that our perspectives are making a real difference.’ This sentiment reflects the central role of the PPAG in ensuring that the study remains grounded in the lived experiences and priorities of parents.

Objective Impact—PPAG Members Suggestions and Contributions to the Optimisation of the Research Study

PPAG members made important contributions to the design and implementation of the feasibility study. Their lived experience and insights have informed decisions across multiple components of the research process.

Their input helped refine the wording and acceptability of study documents. One member highlighted the importance of accessibility of the written materials, advising that ‘not much technical terms … which would make it really hard to understand. … words … simple to understand’. Such comments demonstrate the importance of co‐design in ensuring materials are comprehensible and meaningful to parents. Other relevant contributions included wording to improve inclusivity: ‘the questionnaire [has to be] inclusive … as a same‐sex family, I like the terminology used of birth parent and non‐birth parent.’

PPAG members also contributed to recruitment strategies offering practical recommendations. A recurring theme in their feedback was the importance of trust and relationship‐building between researchers and parents. Successful engagement was described as depending on opportunities to ‘getting to know the researcher’ and taking time to ‘build familiarity’ with families and ‘win the parent's trust’. This advice led to the incorporation of the researcher into everyday clinical activities.

Other members highlighted the valuable role of clinical staff in facilitating recruitment suggesting the involvement of ‘champion nurses’ to introduce the study in a supportive manner and ‘have … nurses … promote it …’. Due to time constraints and staff shortages, this recommendation could not be implemented within PADDINGToN‐2, but it will inform the next phase of the study.

PPAG members also recommended making study information more accessible within everyday parent spaces in the NNU by leaving ‘a poster or leaflet with contact information for parents to get in touch if they want to sign up’ such as ‘the parents' room…’. This recommendation was implemented where feasible, within the parameters of the existing ethics approval.

Retention strategies were also an important area of contribution: members' feedback highlighted the importance of reducing participant burden and offering flexible options for continued engagement, emphasising the need for adequately designed surveys to ‘make sure the survey is short and mobile‐friendly’. They further suggested exploring in‐person engagement such as ‘a researcher based in the paediatric outpatient clinic’ to improve response rates, as ‘they might engage better in person.’ While some suggestions were incorporated, others will help inform the design of the next phase of the PADDINGToN studies.

Other members proposed strategies that increase convenience and autonomy for parents such as keeping virtual meetings flexible and short and allowing the participant to schedule meetings at their convenience; this recognises that flexibility is essential when engaging in research with parents caring for medically vulnerable infants.

Collectively, these contributions highlight the value of parent‐centred recruitment and retention strategies that prioritise accessibility, flexibility and trust. A summary of the key themes, findings and contributions identified through PPAG involvement is presented in Table 1.

Discussion

This study describes the formation and contributions of a parent advisory group in Ireland established to guide neonatal research and illustrates the practical value of involving parent partners in the research process. Parents brought experience‐based insights that enriched multiple components of the research. Previous work has highlighted that PPI not only enhances study relevance but also strengthens the ethical foundations ensuring that those most affected have a voice in shaping it (Brett et al. 2014; Ocloo et al. 2021; Melchior et al. 2021; Menzies et al. 2016; Bjerregaard et al. 2025). Our findings add to this evidence by showing how parental involvement can help improve the clarity, inclusivity and accessibility of research materials.

The Irish PPAG members described both emotional and practical motivators for participation, emphasising their desire to support families and to contribute to system‐level improvements, aligning with previous reports (Bjerregaard et al. 2025; Bracken et al. 2023). It is important to acknowledge the emotional cost that participation and reliving past experiences may have. Similar reflections have been reported in other studies (Melchior et al. 2021; Darby et al. 2025), highlighting the need for appropriate supports for participants.

Facilitators of engagement included flexible and voluntary meeting structures, clear communication and advance sharing of materials; similar factors have been recognised previously (Westerink et al. 2023; Finderup et al. 2022; Menzies et al. 2016). The ability to contribute offline was particularly valued, enabling parents with demanding responsibilities to continue to participate, echoing prior findings (Molloy et al. 2019; Bjerregaard et al. 2025).

Parental involvement meaningfully informed the feasibility study design through identification of potential barriers to recruitment and development of practical solutions including relationship‐building with families in keeping with principles of family‐centred care (Gooding et al. 2011; O'Brien et al. 2013). Their recommendations on improving retention, such as shortening surveys and accommodating parents through flexible booking systems, highlight the necessity of reducing participant burden, a barrier frequently cited in neonatal research (Bates et al. 2024).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The PPAG comprised seven members, which, although acceptable for qualitative advisory work, may not capture the full diversity of neonatal parent experiences in Ireland. While ethnic diversity within the group was strong, participation was limited to English‐speaking parents, potentially overlooking perspectives of families with limited English. As a descriptive study embedded within a single feasibility project, these findings may not be fully transferable to other neonatal research settings. Ongoing PPAG involvement and PADDINGToN research may benefit from translation services.

Practical Recommendations for Establishing a Neonatal Parent Advisory Group

Based on our experience, practical strategies for future neonatal PPI work include recruiting through trusted parent networks, offering asynchronous opportunities for feedback, arranging meetings in advance and according to participants' availability, keeping meetings short and flexible, sharing materials in advance, recognising the emotional burden that participation may cause, simplifying study documents and building trust through regular engagement and feedback on how contributions have informed the study.

Members of the Irish PPAG reviewed and provided feedback on this manuscript and will continue to collaborate in the following phases of the research project.

Conclusion

Parents' lived experience and practical insights informed the relevance and acceptability of study materials and contributed to parent‐centred approaches to optimise recruitment and retention. Flexible opportunities for participation, clear communication and recognition of the emotional cost of participation were key to sustaining engagement.

This work suggests that meaningful PPI in neonatal research is both feasible and valuable. The contributions of parents helped ensure that the study remained grounded in the real‐world needs of families transitioning home with infants on multiple medications. As the PADDINGToN programme progresses, continued collaboration with parent partners will remain important.
