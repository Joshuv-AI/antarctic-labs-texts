Interactive Query Answering on Knowledge Graphs with Soft Entity Constraints
Daniel Daza, Alberto Bernardi, Luca Costabello, Christophe Gueret, Masoud Mansoury, Michael Cochez, Martijn Schut

Abstract
Methods for query answering over incomplete knowledge graphs retrieve entities that are likely to be answers, which is particularly useful when such answers cannot be reached by direct graph traversal due to missing edges. However, existing approaches have focused on queries formalized using first-order-logic. In practice, many real-world queries involve constraints that are inherently vague or context-dependent, such as preferences for attributes or related categories. Addressing this gap, we introduce the problem of query answering with soft constraints . We propose a Neural Query Reranker (NQR) designed to adjust query answer scores by incorporating soft constraints without disrupting the original answers to a query. NQR operates interactively, refining answers based on incremental examples of preferred and non-preferred entities. We extend existing QA benchmarks by generating datasets with soft constraints. Our experiments demonstrate that NQR can capture soft constraints while maintaining robust query answering performance.

Introduction

Knowledge graphs (KGs) are graph-structured databases that store information about a domain using triples of the form
(subject, predicate, object)
. Such a compact representation enables efficient algorithms for answering queries about entities over the domain encoded by the KG
[
1
,
2
]
.
In principle, answers to these queries can be obtained by traversing the graph and collecting the entities that satisfy the given conditions. However, this strategy misses answers due to connections that are not explicit in a KG that may be incomplete, but that are
likely
to satisfy a query, for example because of their similarity to known answers.
The problem of finding likely answers to simple queries over KGs has been studied in areas such as rule mining
[
3
,
4
,
5
,

]
and link prediction
[
7
,

]
, and complex query answering
[

,

,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Given a query, these methods score all the entities in the KG, indicating the likelihood of meeting all conditions specified in the query.
Notably, prior works have focused on conditions that can be specified using different subsets of first order logic.

We identify a relevant and complementary problem: answering queries with constraints that cannot be specified formally as further conditions in a query. We refer to these as
soft constraints
, to contrast them with the
hard
constraints that can be specified using first order logic. Consider the following query: “
What are award nominations received by movies starring Leonardo DiCaprio?
”. A user might be additionally interested in nominations “
related to the audio-visual design of the movie
”, such as
Best Costume Design
, or
Best Sound
, and not in nominations about the artistic vision of a film. Such a constraint cannot be specified using the language of first order logic, simply because such an idea of relatedness is too vague to be formalized into precise logical statements. Furthermore, we note that the same set of answers to a query can accommodate different types of soft constraints, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Our goal is thus to broaden the expressivity of queries on KGs which are specified using first order logic, by incorporating additional soft constraints to refine the set of answers.

Figure 1
:
A query over a KG can be written in a logical form that specifies
hard constraints
that an entity must meet. We investigate the problem of incorporating
soft constraints
that cannot be specified in logical form, such as “
awards given to performers
.” Such constraints correspond to subsets of entities, shown in shaded clusters, that share some similarities.

Addressing this challenge requires a practical definition of how soft constraints are specified. Since soft constraints are based upon similarity relations, a natural way to capture them is to provide specific examples of entities meeting a soft constraint, which we call
preferences
. The goal is then to identify other similar entities that should be given priority. Following our previous example, a possible preference set is
\{
Best Costume Design, Best Sound
\}
. A system that captures the soft constraint underlying this preference set should give a higher score not only to the entities in this set, but also to similar ones such as
Best Original Song
, and ideally without requiring larger and more exhaustive preference sets. The result is an
interactive
formulation of query answering, in which for a given query, sets of one or more entities can be provided incrementally as examples that meet the implicit requirements of the user. We are therefore interested in evaluating what the performance of the system is with respect to the number of interactions.

The challenge of incorporating soft constraints lies in adjusting answer scores with minimal deviation from the original distribution of scores, so that hard logical constraints imposed by the query are still met. Training a model for this task requires datasets that capture the relationships between query answers and soft constraints – data that is not available in existing query answering benchmarks over incomplete KGs. Additionally, the reranking process must be efficient and integrate seamlessly with state-of-the-art QA models.

To tackle these challenges, we propose a Neural Query Reranker (NQR) designed to capture soft preferences while preserving the global structure in an original list of scores for a given query. In summary, our work makes the following key contributions:

1.

We introduce the problem of query answering on knowledge graphs with soft entity constraints, defining it as an interactive process where preferences are specified incrementally.

2.

We present the NQR model, which learns to rerank query answers based on an initial list of scores from an existing query answering system. The model is optimized to capture soft constraints while maintaining the original ranking structure.

3.

We extend existing benchmarks for query answering with automatically generated data of soft constraints. Through comprehensive evaluations, we show that NQR effectively captures these constraints without significantly compromising global ranking performance compared to baselines.

Preliminaries

We denote a KG as a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of entities,
\mathcal{R}
is the set of relations, and
\mathcal{E}
is the set of edges of the form
(h,r,t)
where
h,t\in\mathcal{V}
are the head and tail entities, and
r\in\mathcal{R}
is the relation between them. A triples
(h,r,t)
in a KG implies the truth of the statement
r(h,t)
in first-order logic, where
r\in\mathcal{R}
is a binary predicate, and
h,t\in\mathcal{V}
its arguments.

Logical queries over KGs.
A query over
\mathcal{G}
defines a set of constraints that an entity must satisfy to be considered an answer. For example, consider the query
What are award nominations received by movies starring Leonardo DiCaprio?
The
target
variable of this query is an award, and the query places two constraints on it: 1) the award nomination is given to movies, and 2) the movie features Leonardo DiCaprio. Formally, we can express the set
A_{q}
of answers to the query using first order logic as follows:

A_{q}=\{v_{2}\mid\exists v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}:\texttt{performer}(\texttt{Leonardo DiCaprio},v_{1})\land\texttt{nominated}(v_{1},v_{2})\}.

(1)

Answering this query requires assigning entities from
\mathcal{V}
to variables
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
such that the conjunction specifying the constraints is true according to the information contained in the KG. More generally, and following the notation in
Bai et al. 2023
, we consider first-order logic (FOL) queries that can include conjunctions, disjunctions, and negations, which we write in disjunctive normal form as follows:

\mathcal{A}=\{v_{t}\mid\exists v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\in\mathcal{V}:\,(c^{1}_{1}\land\dots\land c^{1}_{m_{1}})\lor\dots\lor(c^{n}_{1}\land\dots\land c^{n}_{m_{n}})\},

(2)

where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
are variables in the query, and
v_{t}
is the
target
variable. The constraints
c_{j}^{i}
of the query consist of binary relations between two variables, or between a variable and a known entity in
\mathcal{V}
, while optionally including negations:

\displaystyle c^{i}_{j}=\begin{cases}r(e,v)\text{ or }r(v^{\prime},v)\\
\lnot r(e,v)\text{ or }\lnot r(v^{\prime},v)\end{cases}\hskip 30.00005pt\text{with }e\in\mathcal{V},v,v^{\prime}\in\{v_{t},v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\},\text{ and }r\in\mathcal{R}.

Approximate Query Answering.
In order to provide answers to queries that are not limited to the information explicitly stated by the edges in the graph, recent works have introduced machine learning models for
approximate
query answering (QA)
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
23
,
24
]
. For a given query, these methods provide a score for all the entities in the KG according to how likely they are to be answers to a query. While the specific mechanisms for achieving this vary among methods, in general we denote a
QA model
as a function
a
that maps a query to a vector
a(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
of real-valued scores, with one score for each entity in the KG.

4
Query Answering with Soft Constraints

FOL queries over KGs allow imposing a broad range of constraints over entities, but this approach is still limited by the expressivity of first-order logic, and the schema of the KG. In some applications one might require incorporating notions of similarity, which can be hard or impossible to express using FOL. We address this limitation by introducing the concept of
soft constraints
. A soft constraint is a loose requirement based on semantic similarity, which can guide answers to a query towards desirable types of answers without imposing strict logical criteria. For example, the soft constraint “
awards involving performers
” suggests prioritizing answers related to acting awards, without strictly excluding other awards.

Given that a soft constraint is intended to capture similarity, a natural way to incorporate it into a query is through specific examples of entities that represent it. We formalize this via labeled
preferences
. A preference is a tuple
(e,l)
, where
e\in\mathcal{V}
is an entity in the KG, and
l\in\{0,1\}
is a label indicating whether entities “like”
e
should be preferred (
l_{i}=1
) or non-preferred (
l_{i}=0
).

Since specifying an exhaustive list of preferences can be challenging or costly (for example, if elicited from humans), and the types of soft constraints formulated can be different for the same query, we investigate the setting of
interactive query answering with soft constraints.
We define an iterative process where an initial ranked set of answers to a query is refined progressively through the incorporation of preferences. Concretely, we start with the hard constraints of a logical query, which we run through a QA model to obtain an initial list of scores
a^{(0)}(q)
. On the first interaction the system receives a preference
(e_{i},l_{i})
that exemplifies a soft constraint, which is used to adjust the initial scores to incorporate the provided preference. After
t
interactions, we obtain a
preference set
:

P(t)=\{(e_{1},l_{1}),\ldots,(e_{t},l_{t})\}.

(3)

The adjusted scores at any time
t\geq 1
are therefore a function of the original scores and the set of preferences at time
t
:
a^{t}(q)=f(a^{(0)}(q),P(t))
.

5
Learning to Incorporate Soft Constraints

We now turn our attention to the challenge of learning to adapt answer scores based on user preferences. The goal is to develop a model that can dynamically update the ranking of answers in response to incremental feedback of preferences, while maintaining the structure of the initial scores. Following our running example, this is desirable because, given a soft preference like “
awards involving performers
”, we would like awards such as “
Best Actress
” to rank higher than “
Best Sound
”, but both entities should still rank higher than incorrect entities, such as “
Nobel Prize
”.

To achieve this, we propose a learning-based approach that leverages a dataset of query-answer pairs and preference sets as
\mathcal{D}=\{(q_{i},\mathcal{A}_{i},P(T_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{N}
. We propose to use such a dataset to learn a reranking model for computing score updates according to preferences provided incrementally. Let
a^{(t)}[e]
denote the score of entity
e
at time step
t
, for some query
q
(omitted for brevity). Our proposed model updates this score as follows:

a^{(t)}[e]=a^{(0)}[e]+f_{\theta}(e,P(t)),

(4)

where
f_{\theta}
is a score adaptation function with learnable parameters
\theta
. The task of the model is thus to compute an additive adjustment to the original score as a function of the preference set observed so far until interaction
t
.

Neural Query Reranker
To compute the score adjustment
f_{\theta}(e,P(t))
we implement a Neural Query Reranker (NQR) that is trained separately from an existing QA model. We assume access to entity embeddings
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each of the entities in the KG, which are already trained by the majority of existing QA models
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
23
,
24
]
. NQR is composed by a
preference embedding module
and a
score adjustment module
.

The preference embedding module computes a vector representation that captures the information provided by a preference set
P(t)
. Given such a set, we form a matrix where each row contains the embeddings of the
t
entities in the set, concatenated with their label value, resulting in a matrix
\mathbf{M}
of size
t\times(d+1)
. The preference embedding is then computed by a function
\mathbf{m}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
g_{\theta}
is a function that maps the matrix
\mathbf{M}
to single vector. We employ a self-attention layer over the rows of
\mathbf{M}
, since it supports variable lengths and it is invariant to permutations of the input
[
45
]
that should not affect the embedding of a preference set.

In the score adjustment module, we concatenate the preference embedding
\mathbf{m}
, the embedding of the entity
e
whose score is being adjusted, and its original score into a single vector. This vector is the input to a two-layer MLP that computes the score adjustment
f_{\theta}(e,P(t))
(see Fig.
2
).

This embedding is concatenated with the embedding of the entity whose score is being adjusted together with the original score, and passed to the second the second module for computing and adjustment score. The function
emb
(\cdot)
retrieves the embedding of an entity.

Training Objective
The main goal of NQR is to adjust scores of entities in a way that the information conveyed by the preference data is reflected, while maintaining overall ranking quality. This imposes a two-fold objective: (1) preferred entities should be ranked higher than non-preferred ones, and (2) all correct answers to a query should still be ranked higher than other entities in the KG that do not meet the logical constraints of the query.

We start by defining a partition of a preference set
P(t)
into the following sets of preferred and non-preferred entities (we omit
t
for brevity):

P^{+}=\{e\mid(e,l)\in P(t),\,l=1\},

(5)

P^{-}=\{e\mid(e,l)\in P(t),\,l=0\}.

(6)

We note that the objective of capturing preferences has been the focus of extensive work in the field of learning to rank, in which for a given query, a set of documents is annotated with a label indicating their relevance
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. In RankNet, a neural network is optimized to rank relevant items above others using a binary cross-entropy loss where the logits are differences in scores for relevant and non-relevant items
[
32
]
. In our context, the RankNet loss can be computed as
\mathcal{L}_{R}=-\log\sigma(a^{(t)}[e^{+}]-a^{(t)}[e^{-}])
, with
e^{+}\in P^{+}
,
e^{-}\in P^{-}
. In practice, we observe that this loss aggressively pushes the scores of preferred and non-preferred entities apart, which helps to capture preferences, but harms the quality of the initial ranking provided by the QA model. To address this, we resort to a margin loss for capturing preferences:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pref}}(t)=\sum_{e^{+}\in P^{+}}\sum_{e^{-}\in P^{-}}\max(0,\gamma+a^{(t)}[e^{-}]-a^{(t)}[e^{+}]),

(7)

where
\gamma
is a margin hyperparameter. Once this margin exists between the scores the loss provides no gradients, preventing NQR from pushing the scores further apart as we observe in the RankNet loss. Additionally, to ensure that NQR does not drift far from the distribution of scores computed by the QA model, we minimize the KL divergence between the distribution of scores before and after adjusting them:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ans}}(t)=D_{\text{KL}}(u\|v)
, with
u
a categorical random variable parameterized by
\operatorname{softmax}(a^{(0)})
and similarly for
v
, which is parameterized by
\operatorname{softmax}(a^{(t)})
.

The total loss that we compute is
\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{pref}}(t)+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{ans}}(t)
, where
\lambda
is a hyperparameter for controlling the strength of the KL divergence term. For each instance
(q_{i},\mathcal{A}_{i},P(T_{i}))
in the dataset, we sample preference subsets from
P(T_{i})
by choosing a random number of interactions
t
from the interval
[1,T_{i}]
, which we then use to compute the loss
\mathcal{L}(t)
. We use gradient descent on this loss to update the parameters of the preference embedding and score adjustment modules in NQR.

Computational complexity.

The computational complexity of NQR when adjusting the scores for a given query is firstly determined by the preference embedding module, that computes an embedding of a preference set of size
t
. When done using self-attention, this step requires
O(t^{2})
operations. Once the preference embedding is obtained it is used to adjust the scores of the
|V|
entities in the KG. The overall complexity is therefore
O(|V|+t^{2})
, which is efficient when
t\ll|V|
, as is the case in our experiments where
1\leq t\leq 10
.

6
Experiments

We aim to evaluate whether NQR can effectively incorporate soft constraints as expressed in preference sets, while preserving overall query answering performance. We extend current benchmarks for query answering with automatically generated preference data, and we run experiments comparing the performance of baselines with respect to NQR.

6.2
Evaluation

In order to compute an initial list of scores for complex queries, we employ QTO
[
15
]
, a state-of-the-art method that only requires training a link prediction model for computing scores for complex queries. We then proceed to evaluate performance on the task of interactive query answering with soft constraints as follows: given an instance
(q_{i},\mathcal{A},P(T_{i}))
in the test set and a list of initial scores
a^{(0)}
computed by QTO, we select subsets
P(t)\subseteq P(T_{i})
incrementally, with
1\leq t\leq 10
and alternating between preferred entities and non-preferred entities at each step. For each subset we compute the adjusted score (Eq.
4
), and then we evaluate the following:

1. Pairwise accuracy:
Measures the extent to which preferred entities are ranked higher than non-preferred entities. Let
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
be as defined in Eqs.
5
and
6
, and let
\texttt{r}(e,a^{(t)})
be the position of entity
e
when sorting the list of scores
a^{(t)}
in descending order. We define the pairwise accuracy at interaction
t
, as follows:

\operatorname{PA}(t)=\sum_{e^{+}\in P^{+}}\sum_{e^{-}\in P^{-}}\mathbbm{1}\left[\texttt{r}(e^{+},a^{(t)})>\texttt{r}(e^{-},a^{(t)})\right],

(8)

where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
is an indicator function.

2. General ranking quality.
Prior work on approximate query answering has relied on ranking metrics to evaluate performance. For an answer
e\in\mathcal{A}
to a query, the reciprocal rank is
1/\text{r}(e,a^{(t)})
, and the Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) is the average over all queries
2
2

2

We compute
filtered
ranking metrics, where other known answers to a query are removed before computing
\text{r}(e,a^{(t)})
.
. Hits at
k
(H@k) is defined by verifying if, for each answer
e
, its rank
\text{r}(e,a^{(t)})
is less than or equal to
k
(in which case H@k = 1, and H@k = 0 otherwise), and averaging over all queries. We compute these ranking metrics using the original and adjusted scores to determine any effect on overall query answering performance.

6.3
Baselines

We consider the following methods for comparing the performance of NQR:

Cosine similarity:
This is a baseline that is not trained with preference data, but uses entity embeddings to rerank a list of scores based on similarity. Let
\text{sim}(e_{1},e_{2})
be the cosine similarity between the embeddings of entities
e_{1},e_{2}
. This baseline adjusts the score as a combination of the initial ranking score, and an additive or subtractive term for preferred or non-preferred entities in a preference set:

a^{(t)}[e]=a^{(t)}[e]+\alpha_{p}\sum_{e_{i}\in P^{+}}\text{sim}(e_{i},e)-\alpha_{n}\sum_{e_{i}\in P^{-}}\text{sim}(e_{i},e)

(9)

We search for the best the value of
\alpha_{p},\alpha_{n}\in(0,1)
based on performance in the validation set.

RankNet:
This baseline uses the same architecture as NQR and is trained with preference data, but is instead optimized with the original RankNet loss.

We observe that the cosine similarity baseline works very well for clustering similar entities, with pairwise accuracy increasing monotonically with the number of interactions. While this baseline preserves a part of the original QTO scores by adding or subtracting to it, as the number of interactions increases the similarity scores take priority and start to degrade ranking quality, as evidenced by the decreasing MRR and H@k metrics. We also observe an oscillating effect that directly correlates with the way preferences are selected during evaluation, that alternates between preferred entities (leading to an increase in ranking performance) and non-preferred entities (leading to a decrease). This result shows that it is more difficult to capture a soft constraint when a non-preferred entity is provided.

The results with RankNet and NQR show that an architecture for learning to encode preferences can lead to an improved balance in the trade-off between capturing preferences and maintaining ranking quality. Even though RankNet and NQR result in lower pairwise accuracy than the cosine similarity baseline on FB15k237, they are more robust to the provided preference sets as their size keeps increasing, regardless of whether they correspond to preferred or non-preferred entities. On FB15k237 we note that in spite of this robustness, RankNet presents a significant drop in overall ranking performance, which we attribute to the loss function optimizing for pairwise margins only. On both datasets, NQR results in higher ranking quality, showing that the loss used during training is effective at preventing large deviations from the original list of scores.

Effect of KL divergence.

In Fig.
5
we show the performance of the baselines and NQR after 10 interactions, comparing MRR vs. pairwise accuracy. The results indicate that the problem of incorporating soft constraints implies a tradeoff, where achieving very high pairwise accuracy comes at the expense of reduced overall ranking performance. For NQR, we plot several runs corresponding to different values of the weight used in the KL divergence term of the loss. We observe that the different weight values tend to expand the feasible region in the MRR vs. pairwise accuracy space, achieving better combinations of both metrics compared to the baselines. This suggests that the KL divergence term plays a crucial role, not only in balancing the two metrics but also in enhancing overall performance.

8
Limitations and future work

Our focus during the design of NQR was on adjusting a list of scores efficiently together with provided preference sets. However, it is also possible to provide additional information to NQR by including the query itself, for example in the form of a query graph, which can be encoded using a graph neural network as done in prior work
[
10
]
, which we plan to explore in future work.

Our study of soft constraints is limited to the target variable of the query, but some interesting constraints can be formulated over intermediate variables. An example is the query “
What are award nominations received by movies starring Leonardo DiCaprio?
” A soft constraint can be specified not only for the award, but also for the movies (e.g. “
science fiction movies
”). This requires considering combinations of constraints, and possibly a more elaborate architecture, which constitutes an interesting direction for future research.

The preference data used in our experiments is generated through clustering methods applied to embedding models, which capture intuitive notions of similarity among entities. This automated approach allows us to study the problem without requiring extensive human input. However, incorporating human evaluations could complement and potentially enhance the validity of our findings. While conducting such an evaluation would require significant resources, it represents a promising direction for future research.

9
Conclusion

We have introduced the problem of interactive query answering on KGs. We formulate it as an extension that addresses the limitations of logical queries over knowledge graphs, which are restricted by the expressivity of first order logic and the schema of the graph, and we propose NQR: an efficient neural architecture and a training objective for solving the task. We extend knowledge graphs containing encyclopedic and biomedical knowledge with preference data. In our experiments, we find that the problem poses a trade-off between capturing soft constraints accurately, which NQR can learn to balance.
With soft constraints, we explore a new and flexible way to interact with graph databases, which can allow users to specify their preferences by providing examples interactively, leading to query answering systems that can quickly adapt to user feedback.

In our main experiments, we report the performance of different methods when presented with incremental feedback with preference sets of size 1 up to 10, averaged over all queries. This requires queries that have at preference set (combining both preferred and non-preferred entities) of at least 10 entities, such that we can evaluate methods over the entire range of preference set sizes.

In some cases, the number of answers to a query is small, leading to preference sets smaller than 10. To compute an aggregated metric that considers preference sets of different sizes for the same query, we compute the mean pairwise accuracy, defined as follows:

\text{AvPA}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{PA}(t),

(10)

where

is the size of the preference set, and
\text{PA}(t)
is the pairwise accuracy defined in Eq.
8
. Similarly, we compute the mean MRR over preference sets of different sizes:

\text{AvMRR}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\text{MRR}(t),

(11)

where
\text{MRR}(t)
is the resulting MRR when considering the preference set of size
t
.

We present results for these metrics, stratified by query structure (illustrated in Fig.
