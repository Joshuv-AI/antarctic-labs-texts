Nafamostat mesylate in renal replacement therapy: balancing efficacy with emerging risks of hyperkalemia
Authors: Niroj Mali, YouLi Tang, Saroj Mali, Xiankun Sun, Ling Zhang, Ping Fu
Published: 2025

We read with interest the recent studies by Li et al. ‘Nafamostat mesylate versus regional citrate anticoagulation for chronic hemodialysis in patients at high risk of bleeding: a single-center, retrospective study’ that comparing nafamostat mesylate (NM) with regional citrate anticoagulation in high-bleeding-risk patients undergoing hemodialysis, alongside Lin et al.’s ‘Efficacy and safety of nafamostat mesilate anticoagulation in blood purification treatment of critically ill patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis’ that provides valuable insights into NM’s clinical utility [1,2]. Nafamostat mesylate (NM), a serine protease inhibitor, has garnered attention as a safer anticoagulant for patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), particularly those at high risk of bleeding. These works underscore NM’s advantages as a short-acting anticoagulant but also highlight underappreciated risks, particularly hyperkalaemia [3]. We wish to augment this discussion with findings from our original case report and propose actionable strategies for mitigating NM-associated electrolyte disturbances, especially hyperkalemia. Our case report highlights hyperkalemia as a critical adverse effect during NM use in CRRT. This commentary synthesizes these findings, urging a balanced approach to NM’s adoption in clinical practice.

Hyperkalemia: an underestimated risk

While NM’s short half-life (5–8 min) minimizes systemic anticoagulation risks, its metabolism releases guanidino compounds that inhibit renal tubular potassium secretion, predisposing to hyperkalemia [5]. Our case report provides critical insights into NM’s potassium-related risks during CRRT. Two critically ill patients developed hyperkalemia (peak K+: 5.5 mmol/L and 6.8 mmol/L) despite standard dialysate potassium adjustments (0–2.0 mmol/L) and absence of potassium-containing medications.

Patient 1: A 66-year-old male presented with pneumonia, respiratory failure, and was placed on mechanical ventilation. Chest CT revealed multiple lung infiltrates, and Aspergillus fumigatus was identified in sputum cultures. He was admitted for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) on March 26, 2024. Despite initial potassium levels of 3.04 mmol/L, potassium levels increased during NM anticoagulation. Discontinuing NM successfully lowered his potassium levels and prevented severe hyperkalemia.

Patient 2: A 57-year-old male with cough, fever, and respiratory distress was intubated and placed on mechanical ventilation. He suffered a pulmonary embolism and cardiac arrest, later stabilized. Sputum tests showed carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae. He was transferred for CRRT on March 26, 2024, his potassium level was initially 4.57 mmol/L. NM anticoagulation was started, and although potassium levels fluctuated, they normalized with ongoing CRRT.

Clinical implications: risk-benefit stratification

While NM avoids hypocalcemia and bleeding risks, hyperkalemia poses a significant threat, particularly in critically ill patients with:

Renal impairment

Reduced potassium excretion capacity.

Multiorgan failure

Concurrent acidosis or tissue breakdown (e.g. rhabdomyolysis) may amplify potassium release.

Prolonged NM use

Tang et al. suggest duration-dependent risk, necessitating time-limited NM protocols.

Comparative considerations

RCA: Preferred in hyperkalemic patients but requires intensive ionized calcium monitoring.

NM: Safer in bleeding-prone patients but mandates frequent potassium checks (e.g. every 2–4 h initially).

Conclusion

The interplay between NM’s anticoagulant benefits and hyperkalaemia risks demands a nuanced approach. While NM remains a cornerstone anticoagulant for CRRT in bleeding-prone patients, its hyperkalaemia risk demands recalibration of clinical protocols. The original case report and recent studies underscore that while NM is valuable in CRRT, its use requires tailored protocols and heightened electrolyte surveillance. Future research should explore NM’s pharmacodynamics in diverse populations and develop predictive models for hyperkalaemia risk, ensuring safer application in critical care. Also, Li et al. and Lin et al. collectively advance NM as a viable anticoagulant for high-bleeding-risk patients, offering comparable efficacy to RCA with fewer metabolic complications. For clinicians, NM represents a pragmatic option in settings where citrate is logistically challenging or contraindicated. However, transitioning NM into practice requires RCTs validating its benefits and addressing protocol heterogeneity. As dialysis populations grow increasingly comorbid, NM’s potential to improve patient safety and simplify care demands urgent prioritization in research and guideline development.
