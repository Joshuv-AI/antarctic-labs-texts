Resting-State Heart Rate Variability and Symptom-Level Conditional Associations in Hospitalized Patients with Musculoskeletal Pain: An Exploratory Cross-Sectional Network Analysis
Authors: Xi Chen, Guangyuan Ma, Jinchao Hu, Tao Yan, Jingchi Li, Lin Luo, Zhipeng Xi, Xubin Shan, Cheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPain, depressive and anxiety symptoms, sleep disturbance, and autonomic regulation may coexist in hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain. This study integrated item-level symptoms with resting-state photoplethysmography-derived heart rate variability (HRV) to characterize their regularized association structure.MethodsThis exploratory cross-sectional study retained 172 adults hospitalized with musculoskeletal pain-related conditions. Pain-duration information was available for all participants, and 169 complete cases entered the primary 58-node network. Regularized partial rank associations were estimated from Spearman correlations using EBICglasso (γ = 0.50) without additional thresholding. Sensitivity analyses examined participants with pain lasting at least 3 months and the influence of overlapping insomnia items.ResultsMedian pain duration was 4.00 months (IQR, 0.57–24.33; range, 0.50–365.00), and 91 of 172 participants (52.9%) had pain lasting at least 3 months. The primary network retained 332 of 1653 possible edges. Prominent within- and cross-domain connections involved pain intensity and mood, gastrointestinal, cognitive, somatic, and insomnia symptoms. HRV indices formed a comparatively distinct physiological cluster; only 24 of 441 possible HRV–symptom edges were retained, all with absolute weights below 0.029. Strength and expected-influence estimates showed low bootstrap stability (CS coefficients = 0.000 and 0.207, respectively). In the ≥3-month subgroup, correlations with the primary network were 0.868 for edge weights, 0.552 for strength, 0.729 for expected influence, and 0.925 for bridge expected influence. Network structure and bridge patterns were partly preserved, but centrality rankings were more duration-sensitive. After overlapping insomnia items were removed, the global structure remained similar, but pain-related sleep interference fell from fourth to 27th in bridge expected influence.ConclusionItem-level network analysis identified closely connected pain and psychological symptom patterns, whereas short-term PPG-derived HRV showed sparse and weak direct network connectivity with clinical symptoms. Sleep-related bridging was partly attributable to overlapping insomnia content. These exploratory findings support multidimensional inpatient assessment but require validation in larger longitudinal studies.

Introduction

Chronic pain is a highly prevalent clinical problem that not only causes persistent physical discomfort but also affects patients’ sleep, functional capacity, emotional status, and quality of life.1–4 Many patients with chronic pain experience depressive and anxiety symptoms in addition to pain itself.5 Previous studies have shown that depressive and anxiety symptoms are common among individuals with chronic pain, suggesting that the co-occurrence of pain with emotional symptoms is an important consideration in chronic pain management.6 Compared with patients experiencing pain alone, those with higher levels of depressive or anxiety symptoms often report more severe pain, greater functional impairment, and increased treatment difficulty.7 Therefore, understanding the relationships among pain, emotional symptoms, and related physiological indicators is important for the comprehensive management of patients with chronic pain. In the present study, depressive and anxiety symptoms were conceptualized as dimensional symptom domains assessed using the HAMD-24 and HAMA-14, rather than as clinically diagnosed psychiatric comorbidities.

Hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain represent a clinically important population whose pain, functional interference, or related treatment needs may warrant inpatient management. The present sample primarily comprised patients hospitalized for musculoskeletal pain conditions, particularly low back pain, with conservative treatment planned as the main therapeutic approach. Because pain duration varied substantially, the entire sample was not assumed to meet a uniform duration-based definition of chronic pain.

Pain, sleep disturbance, emotional symptoms, and autonomic regulation are closely interrelated in chronic pain. Persistent pain may interfere with sleep through nocturnal discomfort and heightened arousal, whereas insufficient or fragmented sleep may be associated with increased pain sensitivity and impaired emotional regulation. Sleep disturbance is also closely associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, while sleep problems and emotional symptoms may both be accompanied by alterations in autonomic regulation. These relationships are likely to be reciprocal and multifactorial. Consequently, sleep-related symptoms may constitute an important area of overlap among pain, emotional symptoms, and autonomic function, although cross-sectional data cannot establish their temporal or mechanistic direction.

From a psychophysiological perspective, depressive and anxiety symptoms may be associated not only with the subjective experience of chronic pain but also with somatic discomfort and autonomic nervous system function.8–10 HRV provides information about cardiac autonomic regulation that is conceptually complementary to symptom ratings. Compared with conventional electrocardiography, photoplethysmography-based HRV assessment is convenient and noninvasive, making it suitable for standardized bedside assessment in hospitalized patients.11–13 Integrating HRV indices with item-level symptoms may therefore help determine whether physiological measures show broad associations with psychological distress or selective conditional associations with particular pain, sleep, somatic, or emotional symptoms.

Nevertheless, individual HRV indices should not be assigned overly specific physiological meanings. In particular, LF/HF reflects complex contributions from sympathetic, parasympathetic, baroreflex, respiratory, and other regulatory processes and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of sympathovagal balance.14 Accordingly, LF/HF, LFnorm, and HFnorm were not included as nodes in the primary network because of their mathematical overlap with LF and HF and the debated physiological interpretation of LF/HF. Although 5-minute recordings are commonly used for short-term HRV assessment, VLF estimated from recordings of this duration has limited stability and physiological interpretability18. VLF was therefore retained only as an exploratory physiological node. Because respiratory rate was not recorded or experimentally controlled, frequency-domain HRV findings should also be interpreted cautiously.

Previous chronic pain network studies have examined item-level relationships among pain, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and related psychosocial factors.15,16 However, limited research has integrated item-level pain and emotional symptoms with resting-state HRV indices within a unified network. Most previous studies examining pain, depression, anxiety, and HRV have relied on total-score correlations, group comparisons, or regression models. Although these approaches can identify associations between summary variables, they provide limited information about which specific symptoms occupy relatively prominent network positions, which symptoms show cross-domain connections, and whether HRV indices are conditionally associated with the overall symptom structure or only with selected symptom nodes.

Network analysis provides an exploratory approach to addressing these questions. By estimating associations between nodes while accounting for the remaining variables in the network, it can characterize conditional relationships among symptoms and physiological indices and identify relatively central nodes and nodes showing cross-domain connectivity.17 Compared with analyses based solely on total scores, this approach may provide a more detailed description of the symptom-level organization of pain, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and HRV indices. However, centrality and bridge metrics describe the estimated cross-sectional network structure and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal influence, symptom transmission, or intervention targets.

Accordingly, the present exploratory cross-sectional study integrated resting-state PPG-derived HRV indices with item-level measures of pain, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms in hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain. A duration-based sensitivity analysis was additionally conducted among participants whose pain had lasted at least 3 months.

Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible if they: (1) were aged ≥20 years; (2) were hospitalized for musculoskeletal pain-related conditions; (3) reported pain lasting for more than 2 weeks before admission; (4) had clear consciousness, adequate insight, and the capacity to understand the study procedures and complete the assessments; and (5) provided written informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants were excluded if they: (1) had acute trauma or another acute pain condition requiring emergency management; (2) had clinically documented cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, including conditions likely to substantially affect HRV measurement, such as clinically significant arrhythmias or implanted cardiac pacing devices; (3) had severe medical illness, cognitive impairment, or communication difficulties that precluded reliable assessment; (4) declined participation or withdrew consent; or (5) did not complete the HRV recording or the primary symptom assessments.

Instruments and Scales

Instruments

Resting cardiac variability was assessed using a fingertip photoplethysmography (PPG)-based system (LOONGRAY LR-HK-A02 probe with LR-HK-A30 analyzer, China). Because PPG measures pulse-to-pulse intervals rather than electrocardiographic R–R intervals, the recordings are more precisely described as PPG-derived pulse rate variability (PRV).12 For consistency with the clinical literature and the device output, the term PPG-derived HRV is used throughout this article; however, these measures should not be considered fully interchangeable with ECG-derived HRV.

A 5-minute resting PPG recording was obtained on the day of admission, before the planned inpatient conservative treatment had begun. Measurements were conducted in a quiet, temperature-controlled ward environment (22–24°C) at a fixed morning time window (9:00–11:00 AM). Participants were assessed in a semi-recumbent seated position and were instructed to remain still and breathe spontaneously during the recording. Respiratory rate was not paced, recorded, or adjusted for in the analyses. Participants were asked to avoid caffeine, nicotine, and vigorous physical activity for at least 2 hours before assessment. No surgical or interventional procedure was initiated before the study assessment.

PPG signals were initially processed using the device’s built-in artifact-detection and correction procedures to derive interbeat interval series. Signal quality was subsequently reviewed by trained study personnel. Isolated abnormal intervals were corrected according to the device-supported procedure, and the stationarity of the recording was checked before HRV parameters were calculated. When a recording did not meet the prespecified signal-quality requirements, the measurement was repeated whenever feasible. Only recordings containing an analyzable 5-minute segment were retained for analysis.

The device output included time-domain, frequency-domain, composite, normalized, and geometric measures of pulse-interval variability. For the primary network, nine PPG-derived HRV indices were selected: CV, SDNN, RMSSD, TINN, TRI, TP, VLF, LF, and HF. LF/HF, LFnorm, and HFnorm were not included as primary network nodes because of their mathematical dependence on LF and HF and concerns regarding their physiological interpretation. The retained HRV indices were treated as exploratory physiological variables rather than direct biomarkers of specific autonomic processes. In particular, VLF was interpreted cautiously because it was estimated from a 5-minute recording. HRV parameters were calculated with reference to established recommendations for short-term HRV assessment.

Scales

Pain symptoms and pain-related functional interference were assessed using the Chinese version of the Brief Pain Inventory–Short Form (BPI-SF). The BPI-SF uses 0–10 numeric rating scales to assess worst, least, average, and current pain intensity during the past 24 hours and assesses pain interference in seven domains: general activity, mood, walking ability, work or household work, relations with other people, sleep, and enjoyment of life. The mean of the four pain-intensity items was calculated as the pain-intensity score, and the mean of the seven interference items was calculated as the pain-interference score. The pain-relief item was retained as a separate item because it assesses the percentage of pain relief attributed to medication or treatment during the preceding 24 hours. The Chinese BPI-SF has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in clinical populations.18–21 Cronbach’s α for the BPI-SF in the present sample was 0.921.

In the study, P1 to P12 are a set of indicators for assessing pain and its impact on life: P1 represents the worst pain intensity in the past 24 hours, reflecting the peak of pain; P2 represents the least pain intensity in the past 24 hours, reflecting the lowest level of pain; P3 represents the average pain intensity in the past 24 hours, providing an overall assessment of pain strength; P4 represents the current pain intensity, reflecting the real-time pain state; P5 represents the percentage of pain relief due to medications or treatments in the past 24 hours, assessing treatment effects; P6 to P12 evaluate the impact of pain on different aspects of life, where P6 is the impact on daily life, P7 is the impact on mood, P8 is the impact on walking ability, P9 is the impact on daily work (including both outside work and housework), P10 is the impact on relationships with others, P11 is the impact on sleep, and P12 is the impact on interest in life.

The pain-relief item (P5) was retained for descriptive purposes but was not included as a symptom node in the primary network because it reflects treatment-related pain relief rather than pain intensity or pain interference.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the clinician-rated HAMD-24. Ratings were conducted on the day of admission by one of three assessors who had received unified professional training in standardized administration and scoring. Item anchoring and scoring were conducted with reference to Williams’ Structured Interview Guide for the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (SIGH-D). Formal inter-rater reliability was not quantified. The HAMD-24 was used to assess dimensional depressive symptoms rather than to establish a psychiatric diagnosis.22–26 Cronbach’s α for the HAMD-24 in the present sample was 0.887.

In the study, D1 to D24 are a set of indicators for assessing mental health status, potentially related to the evaluation of depressive disorder or other psychiatric symptoms: D1 represents depressed mood, reflecting an individual’s sad or low mood state; D2 is guilt, referring to feelings of guilt or self-blame; D3 is suicide, involving suicidal ideation or behavior; D4 is difficulty falling asleep, referring to problems with initiating sleep; D5 is shallow sleep, indicating poor sleep quality or light sleep; D6 is early awakening, referring to waking up too early from sleep; D7 is work and interests, assessing reduced participation in work or interest activities; D8 is retardation, referring to slowness in movement or thinking; D9 is agitation, manifesting as restlessness or excessive excitement; D10 is psychic anxiety, referring to psychological tension or worry; D11 is somatic anxiety, referring to bodily symptoms induced by anxiety; D12 is gastrointestinal symptoms, such as loss of appetite or digestive issues; D13 is general somatic symptoms, such as fatigue or bodily discomfort; D14 is genital symptoms, referring to changes in sexual function or interest; D15 is hypochondriasis, referring to excessive worry about one’s health; D16 is weight loss, involving unintentional weight decline; D17 is insight, referring to an individual’s awareness of their mental state; D18 is diurnal variation, referring to fluctuations in symptoms throughout the day; D19 is depersonalization or derealization, referring to loss of self-sense or reality sense; D20 is paranoid symptoms, such as unreasonable suspicion or persecutory delusions; D21 is obsessional symptoms, referring to obsessive thoughts or behaviors; D22 is sense of reduced capacity, referring to the subjective experience of diminished abilities; D23 is sense of hopelessness, referring to feelings of despair about the future; D24 is feelings of worthlessness, referring to reduced self-worth.

HAMA-14 ratings were conducted on the day of admission by one of the same three trained assessors. Scoring followed the standard interview-based item anchors of the HAMA-14; the Structured Interview Guide for the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (SIGH-A) was not used. Formal inter-rater reliability was not quantified. The HAMA-14 was used to assess dimensional anxiety symptoms rather than to establish a psychiatric diagnosis.27–30 Cronbach’s α for the HAMA-14 in the present sample was 0.884.

In the study, A1 to A14 are a set of indicators for assessing anxiety and related psychological and somatic symptoms: A1 represents anxious mood, referring to persistent worry or unease; A2 is tension, reflecting psychological or physical tension; A3 is fears, referring to specific fears or panic attacks; A4 is insomnia, referring to difficulties falling asleep, shallow sleep, or early awakening; A5 is cognitive function, assessing changes in attention, memory, or clarity of thinking; A6 is depressed mood, referring to sadness or low mood accompanying anxiety; A7 is somatic anxiety (muscular), involving symptoms such as muscle tension or soreness; A8 is somatic anxiety (sensory), referring to sensory abnormalities like numbness or tingling; A9 is cardiovascular symptoms, such as palpitations or chest tightness; A10 is respiratory symptoms, such as shortness of breath or suffocation; A11 is gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea, abdominal pain, or indigestion; A12 is genitourinary symptoms, such as reduced sexual function or urinary frequency; A13 is autonomic symptoms, such as sweating, dry mouth, or tremors; A14 is behavior at interview, referring to observed behavioral characteristics during clinical assessment, such as restlessness or gaze avoidance.

The BPI-SF, HAMD-24, HAMA-14, and PPG-derived HRV assessments were completed on the day of admission before inpatient treatment commenced. The exact interval between the symptom assessments and HRV recording was not prospectively standardized or recorded.

To distinguish true zero scores from missing values during data entry, observed BPI-SF, HAMD-24, and HAMA-14 item scores were temporarily stored as the original item score plus one, whereas missing values were coded as zero. Before all descriptive and network analyses, zero codes were converted to missing values and one point was subtracted from every nonmissing item score. Consequently, all analyses and reported descriptive statistics were based on the original scale metrics.

Study Design

Study Population and Design

This cross-sectional study recruited hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain-related conditions between March 31, 2025, and September 28, 2025. Participants were aged 20 years or older and reported pain lasting for more than 2 weeks before admission. The sample predominantly comprised patients with low back pain (approximately 75%), while the remaining participants had pain involving other musculoskeletal sites. Participants were admitted primarily for conservative management of pain-related symptoms and functional impairment.

Pain and symptom assessments, including the Brief Pain Inventory–Short Form (BPI-SF) and clinician-rated Hamilton Depression Rating Scale-24 (HAMD-24) and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale-14 (HAMA-14), were conducted on the day of admission. Resting-state PPG-derived HRV recordings were obtained on the same day, before planned inpatient conservative treatment had begun. No surgical or interventional procedure was initiated before the study assessment. Participants with clinically documented cardiovascular or cerebrovascular conditions that could substantially influence cardiac variability were excluded.

A 5-minute PPG recording was obtained at the bedside in a quiet, temperature-controlled ward environment (22–24°C), with participants in a semi-recumbent seated position. Measurements were scheduled within a fixed morning time window (9:00–11:00 AM). Participants were instructed to avoid vigorous physical activity, caffeine, and nicotine for at least 2 hours before measurement. PPG signal acquisition, artifact processing, and derivation of PPG-derived HRV indices are described in the Instruments section.

A total of 213 patients initially underwent study assessment. Following the data-quality procedures applied during the original data-preparation stage, 172 records were retained in the archived screened dataset. Detailed participant-level records linking each excluded case to a specific screening criterion were not retained; therefore, the contribution of individual criteria to the reduction from 213 to 172 could not be retrospectively reconstructed.

All 172 archived records were reconsidered in the analysis, and pain-duration information was available for all retained participants. After the temporary data-entry codes had been converted back to the original scale metrics, three records did not meet the complete-case requirement for the primary network variables. Therefore, 169 participants were included in the revised primary network analysis.

HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 were used to assess depressive and anxiety symptoms as dimensional symptom domains rather than to establish psychiatric diagnoses. Participants were not recruited or excluded on the basis of psychiatric diagnoses established from these scales or predefined HAMD-24/HAMA-14 score thresholds. For network estimation, individual HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 item scores were retained in their original ordered numeric form and entered as symptom-level variables. Total scores and severity categories were calculated only for descriptive purposes and were not used to define psychiatric comorbidity.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, Southwest Medical University (BY2025046).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3. The number of available observations was reported for each variable.

The primary network combined ordered HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 symptom items, 0–10 BPI numerical-rating items, and continuous PPG-derived HRV indices. Spearman rank correlations were selected as a common rank-based association measure because they preserve ordinal ordering without requiring equal intervals between response categories and reduce sensitivity to skewness and extreme physiological values. Selected positively skewed HRV indices were log1p-transformed before estimation; because this transformation was monotonic, it did not alter their rank ordering.

The primary network combined ordered HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 symptom items, 0–10 BPI-SF numeric-rating items, and continuous PPG-derived HRV indices. Spearman rank correlations were selected as a uniform rank-based association measure because they preserve ordinal ordering without requiring equal intervals between response categories and are less sensitive to skewness and extreme physiological values than Pearson correlations. Selected positively skewed HRV indices were log1p-transformed; because this transformation is monotonic, it did not affect their Spearman rank ordering.

A regularized Gaussian graphical model was estimated from the Spearman correlation matrix using EBICglasso as implemented in the qgraph package, with γ = 0.50 and no additional thresholding. Edges were therefore interpreted as exploratory regularized partial rank associations between pairs of nodes after conditioning on the remaining nodes, rather than as parameters of a fully distribution-specific mixed graphical model.

As an additional diagnostic sensitivity analysis, HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 items were explicitly treated as ordered variables, whereas BPI-SF and HRV variables were retained as numeric. A heterogeneous correlation matrix was estimated using qgraph::cor_auto, which applies polychoric, polyserial, or Pearson correlations as appropriate, followed by the same EBICglasso specification. Positive definiteness was evaluated before estimation, and a nearest positive-definite projection was applied when required. Because this adjustment was required for the mixed-correlation matrix, that analysis was interpreted as an exploratory diagnostic rather than as a definitive alternative model.

For the reduced-sleep network, D4–D6 and A4 were removed while P11 was retained. For the reduced-HRV network, TP and VLF were removed. In addition to global correlations of corresponding edge weights and centrality estimates, the raw estimates and ranks of D19 strength, P3 expected influence, and the bridge expected influence of A4, A6, A11, P11, A1, and A5 were directly compared across networks.

Node strength and one-step expected influence were examined as the primary centrality indices. Bridge strength and one-step bridge expected influence were calculated using four prespecified domains: PPG-derived HRV, pain, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms. Bridge strength was defined as the sum of the absolute edge weights linking a node to nodes outside its prespecified domain, whereas bridge expected influence was defined as the signed sum of these cross-domain edge weights.

As a separate exploratory analysis, Louvain community detection was applied to the absolute regularized edge-weight matrix because the algorithm does not directly accommodate negative edge weights. The procedure was repeated 1000 times using a prespecified random seed (20250713). The solution with the highest modularity was retained. Community consistency was evaluated by calculating adjusted Rand indices between each repeated solution and the retained best solution. Louvain-derived communities were reported descriptively and were not used to define the four prespecified domains for bridge analysis.

Potentially redundant symptom nodes were screened using the goldbricker procedure (p = 0.05; difference threshold = 0.25). This procedure was used as a diagnostic screen and did not automatically determine node deletion. Node response variability was additionally examined.

The accuracy of edge weights was evaluated using 1000 nonparametric bootstrap samples, from which 95% bootstrap confidence intervals and edge-difference tests were obtained. Case-dropping bootstrapping with 1000 repetitions was used to assess the stability of strength and expected-influence estimates and to calculate correlation-stability coefficients. CS coefficients ≥0.25 were considered acceptable and those >0.50 were considered good. Centrality-difference tests were also performed.

Bridge expected-influence stability was evaluated separately by dropping 10%, 25%, 50%, 60%, or 70% of cases. One hundred resamples were generated at each drop proportion. The bridge stability coefficient was defined as the largest drop proportion at which the fifth percentile of correlations between resampled and original bridge expected-influence estimates remained ≥0.70, provided that at least 90% of the requested resamples yielded valid estimates.

Pain duration was retrospectively extracted from the medical records and recorded in days. For descriptive presentation, duration in months was calculated by dividing the recorded number of days by 30. The median, interquartile range, range, and prespecified duration categories were summarized for all 172 retained participants. Because established duration-based definitions generally refer to pain lasting longer than 3 months, the number with pain lasting >90 days was reported separately. The primary 58-node network was additionally re-estimated among complete cases with pain lasting at least 90 days, using the same Spearman-correlation and non-thresholded EBICglasso specification with γ = 0.50. Similarity with the revised primary network was evaluated using correlations of corresponding edge weights, strength, expected influence, bridge strength, and bridge expected influence, together with the Jaccard similarity of nonzero edges. The subgroup correlation matrix had a numerically negligible negative minimum eigenvalue and was projected to the nearest positive-definite matrix before network estimation.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by re-estimating the network after: (1) excluding VLF because of its limited physiological interpretability in a 5-minute recording; (2) excluding TP and VLF to obtain a reduced HRV set; and (3) excluding the overlapping sleep-related items D4–D6 and A4 while retaining the BPI-SF sleep-interference item P11. Additional diagnostic analyses included a high-specificity thresholded EBICglasso model with γ = 0.50, a non-thresholded EBICglasso model with γ = 0.25, and an analysis applying the historical HAMD-24/HAMA-14 total-score exclusion thresholds. Similarity with the primary network was quantified using correlations between corresponding edge weights, node strength estimates, and expected-influence estimates for the nodes and edges common to both networks. For the mixed-correlation, reduced-sleep, and reduced-HRV networks, bridge-strength correlations, bridge-expected-influence correlations, and the Jaccard similarity of nonzero edges were additionally examined. The raw estimates and ranks of D19 strength, P3 expected influence, and the bridge expected influence of A4, A6, A11, P11, A1, and A5 were directly compared across the primary and diagnostic networks.

To evaluate whether the primary findings were sensitive to the correlation specification used for the mixed measurement levels, an additional mixed-correlation sensitivity network was estimated. HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 items were explicitly treated as ordered variables, whereas BPI items and HRV indices were retained as numeric variables. The qgraph::cor_auto function was used to estimate polychoric, polyserial, or Pearson correlations as appropriate, after which the network was estimated using the same non-thresholded EBICglasso specification with γ = 0.50.

Several relatively strong cross-domain correlations were concentrated around conceptually overlapping sleep, somatic, and affective symptoms. Network Analysis

Network Structure

The primary Spearman-based regularized Gaussian graphical model comprised 58 nodes and was estimated using 169 complete cases. Of the 1653 possible edges, 332 nonzero regularized partial associations were retained, corresponding to a network density of 20.08%. Of these edges, 297 were positive and 35 were negative. The network contained 174 within-domain edges and 158 cross-domain edges (Figure 2). Community Detection

The repeated Louvain analysis yielded a highly reproducible retained partition. Across 1000 repetitions, both the best and median modularity values were 0.577. The median adjusted Rand index relative to the retained best solution was 1.000, the minimum adjusted Rand index was 0.789, and 85.9% of the repetitions produced a partition identical to the best solution. These results suggest that the detected community structure was highly consistent across repeated runs.

Redundancy Diagnostics

The goldbricker diagnostic identified six potentially redundant node pairs: P8–P6, P4–P1, P9–P8, P12–P10, A14–D11, and A6–D7. These findings were treated as diagnostic indications of potential content overlap rather than as automatic node-deletion criteria. Given the clinical distinction between pain intensity, pain interference, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms, all nodes were retained in the primary network. The potential influence of overlapping sleep-related items was evaluated separately in a sensitivity analysis.

Sensitivity Analyses

Compared with the primary Spearman-based network, the mixed-correlation sensitivity network showed correlations of 0.833 for edge weights, 0.356 for strength, 0.736 for expected influence, and 0.928 for bridge expected influence. Thus, the broader edge-weight and bridge-connectivity patterns were reasonably preserved, whereas strength rankings were more sensitive to the correlation specification. These findings support the use of the Spearman-based network as an exploratory common-scale representation while indicating that exact centrality rankings should be interpreted cautiously.

The overall network structure was highly consistent across most sensitivity analyses. After removing VLF, correlations with the primary network were 0.999 for edge weights, 0.993 for strength, and 0.991 for expected influence. Removing VLF and TP to obtain a reduced HRV set produced corresponding correlations of 0.992, 0.935, and 0.910.

The mixed-correlation matrix was not positive definite before adjustment (minimum eigenvalue = −3.718) and was therefore projected to the nearest positive-definite matrix before network estimation. The resulting network retained 399 nonzero edges, compared with 332 in the primary Spearman network. Correlations with the primary network were 0.833 for edge weights, 0.356 for strength, 0.736 for expected influence, 0.747 for bridge strength, and 0.928 for bridge expected influence. P3 remained among the leading expected-influence nodes but changed from rank 1 to rank 3, whereas D19 changed from rank 1 to rank 7 in strength. A4, A6, A11, A5, and A1 remained the five highest-ranked bridge-expected-influence nodes, while P11 changed from rank 4 to rank 10. Thus, the broader bridge pattern was more consistent across correlation specifications than the exact general-centrality rankings.

The reduced-sleep network, which removed D4–D6 and A4 while retaining P11, showed correlations of 0.998 for edge weights, 0.951 for strength, 0.954 for expected influence, 0.987 for bridge strength, and 0.988 for bridge expected influence. D19 remained the highest-strength node (1.588), and P3 remained the highest expected-influence node (1.341). A6, A11, A1, and A5 became the four highest-ranked bridge-expected-influence nodes. In contrast, P11 decreased from fourth in the primary network (0.763) to 27th in the reduced-sleep network (0.236). A4 and D4–D6 were absent by design and could not be evaluated as retained nodes. These findings indicate that the broader network structure and non-sleep bridge pattern were retained, whereas the prominence of P11 as a sleep-related bridge node was substantially attenuated after removal of the overlapping insomnia indicators.

After TP and VLF were removed, the reduced-HRV network showed correlations of 0.992 for edge weights, 0.899 for strength, 0.902 for expected influence, and approximately 1.000 for both bridge strength and bridge expected influence. D19 and P3 remained ranked first in strength and expected influence, respectively, and the bridge-expected-influence ordering of A4, A6, A11, P11, A1, and A5 was unchanged. Thus, the principal symptom-level and bridge findings were not dependent on retaining the two conceptually or statistically redundant HRV indices.

Similarly, reducing the EBIC tuning parameter from γ = 0.50 to γ = 0.25 produced correlations of 0.997 for edge weights, 0.937 for strength, and 0.994 for expected influence. These findings indicate that the broader organization of the network was largely preserved after removing VLF, reducing the HRV node set, removing overlapping sleep items, and modifying the EBIC tuning parameter.

The analysis applying the historical HAMD-24/HAMA-14 total-score exclusion thresholds showed correlations of 0.965 for edge weights, 0.841 for strength, and 0.725 for expected influence, indicating that the general edge structure was retained but that centrality estimates were more sensitive to the score-based exclusion procedure. The high-specificity thresholded EBICglasso model showed an edge-weight correlation of 0.860 with the primary network but lower correlations for strength (0.543) and expected influence (0.416). This model retained 50 nonzero edges, compared with 332 in the primary network, and did not retain any HRV–symptom edges. Thus, the broader symptom-domain structure was supported across the principal sensitivity analyses, whereas small edges, HRV–symptom connections, and exact centrality rankings were more sensitive to the use of additional thresholding.

The duration-based sensitivity analysis included all 91 primary-network complete cases with pain lasting at least 3 months. The subgroup network retained 288 of 1653 possible edges, compared with 332 edges in the revised primary network. Correlations between the subgroup and primary networks were 0.868 for edge weights, 0.552 for strength, 0.729 for expected influence, 0.891 for bridge strength, and 0.925 for bridge expected influence. The networks shared 190 nonzero edges, all of which had the same sign, although the nonzero-edge Jaccard similarity was 0.442. Discussion

The present study integrated item-level pain, depressive, and anxiety symptoms with resting-state PPG-derived HRV indices in a unified network among hospitalized patients with chronic pain-related musculoskeletal conditions. Three principal findings emerged. First, pain, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms formed a densely interconnected affective–somatic symptom region, whereas the HRV indices constituted a comparatively distinct physiological cluster. Second, average pain intensity occupied a prominent position in the symptom network, while sleep-related, mood-related, gastrointestinal, and other somatic symptoms contributed substantially to cross-domain connectivity. Third, HRV–symptom associations were limited and selective rather than broadly distributed across the clinical symptom network. Taken together, these findings extend conventional total-score analyses by showing how specific pain, emotional, sleep, somatic, and physiological variables are organized after conditioning on the remaining variables. The study therefore provides an item-level account of the multidimensional clinical presentation of hospitalized patients with chronic pain and clarifies the incremental role that short-term physiological measurements may play within, rather than in place of, symptom assessment.

The pain-duration analysis clarified the population to which these findings apply. Although the median duration exceeded 3 months, 81 of the 172 retained participants (47.1%) reported pain lasting less than 3 months. The full sample should therefore be characterized as hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain rather than uniformly as patients with chronic pain. Restricting the network to the 91 participants with pain lasting at least 3 months yielded relatively high similarity in edge weights and bridge expected influence, suggesting that the broader conditional-association and cross-domain bridge patterns were partly preserved. However, the lower strength correlation and changes in centrality rankings indicate that exact node rankings were not invariant to the duration criterion.

Within the symptom network, average pain intensity showed the highest expected influence, indicating that it was strongly connected to the surrounding network when both the magnitude and direction of its conditional associations were considered. This finding is broadly consistent with previous evidence that pain intensity is closely related to emotional distress, sleep disturbance, and functional impairment in individuals with chronic pain.31,32 However, the present analysis adds an important symptom-level perspective. The prominence of average pain intensity was driven mainly by its position within the pain domain, including its associations with other pain-intensity and pain-interference items, rather than by uniformly strong direct connections with every emotional or physiological variable. Pain intensity may therefore be understood as an important clinical anchor within a broader symptom system, while the emotional and functional consequences experienced by individual patients may depend on additional sleep, somatic, and affective features. This pattern supports comprehensive pain assessment that considers not only the maximum or current level of pain but also the patient’s typical pain experience and the extent to which pain interferes with daily functioning.

The network also revealed substantial overlap between depressive and anxiety symptom domains. The strongest cross-domain edge connected gastrointestinal symptoms measured by the HAMD and HAMA, while additional prominent connections were observed between corresponding sleep, cognitive, mood, and somatic items. These results are consistent with the clinical observation that depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with chronic pain frequently share somatic and affective expressions rather than appearing as completely separate symptom clusters. Importantly, the present study conceptualized depression and anxiety as dimensional symptom domains rather than as diagnosed psychiatric comorbidities. The network findings therefore do not imply that all participants had depressive or anxiety disorders; instead, they indicate that variations in specific emotional and somatic symptoms were conditionally related within this inpatient pain population. This dimensional approach may be particularly useful in general medical and rehabilitation settings, where clinically relevant emotional distress may be present even when the criteria for a formal psychiatric diagnosis are not met.

In the primary network, the sleep-related items formed a prominent cross-domain region: BPI sleep interference was connected with HAMD and HAMA insomnia indicators, and A4 and P11 ranked first and fourth, respectively, in bridge expected influence. However, the reduced-sleep analysis qualified this finding. After D4–D6 and A4 were removed, P11 decreased from fourth to 27th in bridge expected influence, although P3 and D19 remained the leading expected-influence and strength nodes and A6, A11, A1, and A5 remained the highest-ranked non-sleep bridge nodes. The high global similarity of the two networks therefore did not imply preservation of every node-level finding. Rather, the broader symptom structure and non-sleep bridge pattern were retained, whereas the apparent prominence of sleep-related bridging was partly attributable to overlapping insomnia content across the BPI-SF, HAMD-24, and HAMA-14. Sleep interference remains clinically relevant,33 but its role as a uniquely prominent bridge node should be treated as exploratory rather than robust.

The physiological findings differed from the comparatively dense symptom-domain structure. HRV indices showed strong internal organization, including prominent connections between TINN and the HRV triangular index, TP and LF, and SDNN and RMSSD. These relationships indicate coherence among physiological measures reflecting related aspects of short-term pulse-interval variability. By contrast, only eight HRV–symptom edges were retained, and their weights were considerably smaller than the strongest symptom–symptom and HRV–HRV edges. This pattern differs from interpretations based solely on bivariate correlations or total symptom scores, which may suggest a broad relationship between autonomic regulation and psychological distress. After conditioning on the remaining physiological and symptom variables, the present network instead indicated that HRV was largely organized as a distinct physiological dimension with limited connections to selected pain, depressive, and anxiety symptoms.

This relative separation does not mean that PPG-derived HRV lacks potential clinical value. Rather, it suggests that HRV should not be treated as a direct physiological substitute for pain, depression, anxiety, or sleep assessment. Its potential contribution may lie in providing complementary information about physiological regulation that is not redundant with clinician-rated and patient-reported symptoms. This distinction is clinically meaningful because an objective physiological measure does not need to reproduce a symptom scale to contribute to multidimensional phenotyping, repeated bedside monitoring, or the identification of subgroups with differing physiological profiles. At the same time, the weak HRV–symptom connections indicate that individual HRV indices should not currently be interpreted as symptom-specific biomarkers. This is particularly relevant because the physiological meaning of commonly used composite measures such as LF/HF remains debated, especially in short-term recordings.34 Accordingly, LF/HF was not emphasized as a mechanistic marker in the present analysis. Future research combining repeated HRV recordings with longitudinal symptom assessments may determine whether physiological changes precede, follow, or occur independently of changes in pain and emotional symptoms.

The centrality and bridge analyses further differentiated general network connectivity from cross-domain connectivity. Average pain intensity had the highest expected influence, whereas HAMD depersonalization/derealization had the highest strength. In contrast, bridge expected influence was most pronounced for HAMA insomnia, depressed mood, gastrointestinal symptoms, BPI sleep interference, cognitive symptoms, and anxious mood. Thus, the node most connected to the network overall was not necessarily the node most responsible for connecting different clinical domains. This distinction is important for interpreting symptom networks. Highly central pain symptoms may characterize the overall burden of the pain network, whereas sleep, mood, gastrointestinal, and other somatic symptoms may be more relevant to the clinical overlap among pain, depressive, and anxiety domains. These nodes should not be regarded as proven intervention targets on the basis of cross-sectional centrality alone;32 nevertheless, they provide empirically grounded hypotheses for future longitudinal studies examining how changes in one symptom domain may relate to changes in another.

The repeated Louvain analysis also supported a reproducible community organization. Across 1000 repetitions, the best and median modularity values were both 0.577, the median adjusted Rand index relative to the retained solution was 1.000, and 85.9% of repetitions reproduced the best partition exactly. This consistency supports the broader structural distinction visible in the network: the physiological indices were relatively cohesive, while pain and emotional symptoms were more extensively interconnected. The community findings add to the edge-level results by showing that the observed organization was not solely dependent on isolated pairwise relationships. At the same time, the prespecified pain, depressive-symptom, anxiety-symptom, and HRV domains remained clinically useful for interpreting bridge connectivity, because the data-driven communities and clinically defined domains address related but different questions.

From an exploratory clinical perspective, the findings have several implications. First, average pain intensity remains a useful starting point for characterizing symptom burden, but assessment limited to pain intensity is unlikely to capture the full clinical presentation of hospitalized patients. Second, sleep-related interference, insomnia, mood symptoms, and somatic complaints may help identify patients whose difficulties extend across conventional pain and emotional domains. Incorporating brief sleep and emotional-symptom assessments into inpatient pain care may support earlier recognition of complex symptom presentations and facilitate referral to psychological, sleep, or multidisciplinary services. Third, bedside PPG-derived HRV offers a feasible way to collect physiological information under routine inpatient conditions, but its most appropriate role may be complementary phenotyping and longitudinal monitoring rather than direct psychological diagnosis. At a broader service level, integrating symptom-level assessment with scalable physiological measurement may help reduce fragmented care in which pain, sleep disturbance, emotional distress, and physical symptoms are considered separately. These hypotheses are especially relevant for inpatient populations undergoing treatment planning, where a more complete characterization of symptom burden may inform individualized management and the allocation of supportive resources.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design does not establish temporal or causal relationships among pain, sleep, emotional symptoms, and HRV indices34. Second, the primary network included 58 nodes but only 171 complete cases. Although the strongest edges had comparatively consistent bootstrap estimates, the correlation-stability coefficients for strength and expected influence were both 0.205, below the recommended minimum of 0.25, and the bridge stability coefficient of 0.25 only reached the minimum prespecified criterion. Exact centrality and bridge rankings should therefore be regarded as exploratory, particularly for nodes with similar estimates. The principal network structure was highly consistent after removing VLF, reducing the HRV node set, removing overlapping sleep items, and modifying the EBIC tuning parameter. However, the lower similarity of centrality estimates between the primary and high-specificity networks indicates that small edges, HRV–symptom connections, and exact node rankings remained sensitive to additional thresholding. Third, HRV was derived from a 5-minute fingertip PPG recording rather than standard electrocardiography and may have been affected by peripheral vascular factors, device characteristics, artifact-processing procedures, and recording conditions. Respiratory rate was neither paced nor recorded, and the physiological interpretation of VLF derived from a 5-minute recording is limited. Fourth, conceptual and measurement overlap existed among the sleep, somatic, depressive, and anxiety items, which may have strengthened some cross-domain associations. Although three assessors received unified professional training in HAMD-24 and HAMA-14 administration, formal inter-rater reliability was not quantified. The primary model used a Spearman rank-correlation matrix as a pragmatic approach to the combination of ordered symptom items and continuous physiological variables. This approach does not explicitly model ordinal thresholds or the complete joint distribution of the mixed-scale variables. Moreover, the mixed-correlation sensitivity matrix required a nearest positive-definite adjustment, and exact strength rankings were not preserved across correlation specifications. The resulting edges and centrality estimates should therefore be interpreted as exploratory partial rank associations. The reduced-sleep analysis also showed that the high bridge ranking of P11 was substantially attenuated after removal of overlapping insomnia indicators. Fifth, pain duration varied substantially across participants, and 47.1% of the retained sample reported pain lasting less than 3 months. The full sample was therefore described as having musculoskeletal pain rather than uniformly meeting a duration-based definition of chronic pain. Although the ≥3-month sensitivity analysis showed relatively high similarity in edge weights and bridge expected influence, strength estimates and exact centrality rankings were less consistent. Moreover, subgroup bootstrap stability estimates could not be obtained because the resampled correlation matrices were not consistently positive definite. The duration-based subgroup findings should consequently be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, extreme-value screening was applied before the present reanalysis. Of the 213 patients originally assessed, 172 remained in the archived screened dataset, and 171 complete cases were included in the primary network. Because participant-level exclusion logs and excluded raw records were unavailable, the effect of the original screening procedure could not be fully evaluated and some selection bias cannot be excluded. Despite these limitations, the study provides an exploratory item-level framework linking pain, emotional, sleep, somatic, and physiological information and identifies specific, testable hypotheses for larger longitudinal and interventional research.

Conclusion

This exploratory cross-sectional network analysis demonstrated that pain, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms formed an interconnected affective–somatic symptom structure in hospitalized patients with musculoskeletal pain, whereas PPG-derived HRV indices constituted a comparatively distinct physiological cluster. The ≥3-month sensitivity analysis partly supported the broader network and bridge-connectivity patterns but indicated that exact centrality rankings were sensitive to the duration-based sample restriction. These findings support multidimensional inpatient assessment while underscoring the need for larger studies specifically designed for patients meeting established chronic-pain criteria.
