Numerical simulation and experimental investigation of bubble behaviour during pool boiling in the coiled wire
Authors: Ahmad Jalali, Jamshid Khorshidi, Younes Bakhshan, Saeid Niazi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Under the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) framework, an interface tracking method has been used to perform several multiple simulations of coiled wire boiling in a pool. This study includes simulations of the boiling regime, including the Critical Heat Flux (CHF), ranging from nucleate boiling to film boiling. The purpose of this research was to investigate using coiled wire to increase the rate of heat transfer under constant heat flux conditions. The range of the applied heat flux is 130–1075 kW/m2. This research delves into the formation of bubble shapes, bubble diameter, and bubble departure frequency. The vapour bubble grows in size as the heat flux increases; with a heat flux of q'' = 600 kW/m2, it reaches its maximum measured diameter of about 9 mm. Additionally, the heat transfer coefficients and wall temperature distribution were determined using the simulation findings. The heat flux was gradually increased in the critical heat flux investigation until burnout of the coiled wire was observed and a bubble layer formed. This led to the conclusion that q'' = 1075 kW/m2 represents the CHF for this particular geometry. It was observed that the CHF conditions occur in the coil portion, which emphasises the importance of considering the critical heat flux more carefully while designing this geometry.

Introduction

Recently, pool boiling heat transfer has attracted research attention due to its excellent heat dissipation capacity at low-temperature differences. Boiling heat transfer has long been a particularly attractive field of study because it is the most efficient way to remove heat from a solid body with liquids and achieve the maximum heat fluxes at the lowest wall superheat. Pool boiling removes heat effectively because it incorporates both sensible and latent heat, maintaining greater heat flux values in addition to vapour-liquid exchange, natural convection, and transient conduction. Owing to these benefits, it is used for nuclear reactor cooling, microelectronic cooling, quenching procedures in the metallurgical industries, and cooling techniques in space applications [1]. Even now, a lot of the time, accurate boiling heat transfer forecast depends on empirical data. Transitory, transitory boiling and film boiling-nucleate are the three types of boiling-nucleate that are generally thought to be the most complex [2]. The most crucial step in the boiling process is nucleate boiling [3]. A nucleate boiling bubble life cycle includes nucleation, bubble growth, departure, bubble collapse, and waiting for the next renucleation [4]. The size and form of the liquid-gas interface must be taken into account in the boiling simulation since vapourization takes place there. Thus, numerical methods capable of resolving the interface are considered suitable for phase transition process simulation. The volume of the fluid method [[5], [6], [7]], level-set method [8,9], arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian approach [2,10], phase field method [[11], [12], [13]], colour function method [[14], [15], [16]], lattice Boltzmann method [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], and front tracking method [23] are interface-resolving techniques used in boiling simulations. Son and Dhir simulated nucleate pool boiling close to the Critical Heat Flux (CHF) using the level set approach. As opposed to Son and Dhir [24], who employed a depletable micro-layer model [25] and a sharp-interface phase-change model [26]. The calculated heat transfer coefficient and the experimental results agreed well, despite the applied heat flux being less than CHF. To detect the thin liquid film behind a developing bubble, optical techniques are used to boil water at air pressure in a process known as nucleate boiling [[27], [28], [29]], which is explained by Son and Dhir's depletable micro-layer model. A micro-layer is believed to form as the developing vapour bubble is unable to exclude the liquid on the wall, but the bubble expands more quickly in the bulk due to fewer restrictions. Fischer et al. [30]investigated the circumstances necessary for the formation of a micro-layer. The working fluid was refrigerant, which was tested. Evaporative latent heat, wall superheat, and liquid-vapour interface velocity all affect microlayer formation. Boiling and the application of heat flux on the wire have been the subject of numerous research to date [31]. DolatiAsl et al. [32]examined the boiling of coiled wire at atmospheric pressure; it examined the pool boiling purely visually and noted the bubble shape; the research did not include a simulation or experimental setup. An experimental setup has been created for carrying out the experiment and validating the simulation results. In this study, using the simulation approach, we model pool boiling on the coiled wire from nucleate boiling to the film boiling regime [32].

Once the temperature reaches the activation point, a small vapour bubble, also known as a seed bubble, is inserted into the nucleation location. Simulation

The nucleate regimes of pool boiling are covered by heat fluxes ranging from q'' = 130 kW/m2 to q'' = 1075 kW/m2. The wall superheat can be expressed as Tw−Tsat, where Tw represents the temperature at the heated transfer surface and Tsat represents the saturation temperature at atmospheric pressure. There are two divisions in the computational domain: solid and fluid. Fig. 1 shows the computational domain for coiled wire and simulation. Boundary condition

As a boundary condition, the coiled wire is considered to be evenly heated with a constant heat flux. Five simulation cases with various heat flows were investigated in this study. At the fluid-solid interface, a well-established non-slip boundary condition is present. Regardless of whether the domain is fluid or solid, the side boundaries are subject to the periodic boundary condition. The velocity field's output boundary is the fluid domain's upper plane. For the top plane of the fluid domain, where the temperature is equal to the saturated temperature (T=Tsat), a Dirichlet boundary condition is used.

Results and discussions

As shown in section 2.4, the most reasonable value of Cslop for the depletable microlayer model [17] employed here ought to be applied to the subsequent computer simulations. Utaka et al.'s measurement indicates [37] that Cslop=4.46×10−3 is suitable for water boiling at atmospheric pressure. However, four simulations with different Cslop have been run to evaluate this effect, Cslop=2.26×10−3,3.51×10−3, 4.46×10−3 and 5.71×10−3 have been run to evaluate this effect, as this parameter hasn't had any bearing on the outcomes. Cslop=4.46×10−3 has been selected for this study. The simulations were conducted using the parameter, and they were run until a pseudo-steady state or saturation was achieved.

Experimental setup and validation

As seen in Fig. 3, an experimental setup was designed to validate the simulation results. A Schematic view of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 3(a) and the actual image is shown in Fig. 3(b). The experimental conditions were appropriately set to mirror the parameters defined in the simulation. Fig. 1(b) shows that the coiled wire length is 25 mm and the coiled portion length is 5 mm. Stainless steel is the material used for the wire. The working fluid was deionized water, with a volume of 5 L in the pool. To maintain the pressure in the pool equivalent to atmospheric pressure, a pressure transducer was placed on the upper side. In the experiments, the power supply's maximum output power was 1500 W. Using two reflux condensers, water vapour was condensed and the water level was maintained. Two calibrated K-type thermocouples with a precision of 1 °C were inserted at two distinct locations in the pool to measure the fluid temperature. This allowed for simultaneous temperature monitoring and guaranteed pool-wide temperature constancy. We used a high-speed camera to record the development of bubble shapes. Two 1500 W supplemental heaters were used to keep the isothermal water bath's liquid temperature at saturation (373 K). An electrical controller integrated with a data logger was used to maintain the stability of the water bath. A K-type thermocouple was used to measure the water bath's temperature. Conclusions

Investigating the behaviour of bubbles during pool boiling on a coiled wire is the aim of this investigation. To achieve this, five simulation instances with different heat fluxes were conducted to observe the nucleate boiling to film boiling regime through CHF. To validate the simulation results, an experimental setup was implemented. The summarized results of this study are as follows: Due to the higher fluid temperature in the middle region of the wire compared to other regions, more bubbles are formed on the inner side of the coiled wire. As bubbles grow and merge, larger bubbles are created and eventually rise and depart from the top side of the coiled portion. The largest bubble diameter was observed at a heat flux of q″=600kW/m2, approximately 9 mm in diameter. With increasing heat flux, the bubble diameter continues to increase until q″=1000kW/m2, at which point, the observed bubbles merge and individual bubbles cannot be distinguished. With increasing heat flux, the vapour bubble also grows in size. Bubble departure can be accelerated by increasing the departure frequency and decreasing the departure diameter. Bubble frequency is 4.35, 3.57, and 2.86 for q″=600,300and130kW/m2, respectively. Since film boiling in the inner region of the coiled portion is most prominent, burnout is likely to initiate from the inner side of the coiled portion. At low heat flux, single bubbles form and depart similarly to a regular wire. However, at high heat flux, due to the high rate of phase change, a continuous vapour column forms, and individual bubbles do not appear.
