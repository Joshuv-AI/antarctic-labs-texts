Cardioneuroablation for Vagally Mediated Coronary Artery Spasm With High-Grade Atrioventricular Block and Vasovagal Syncope
Authors: Hongyi Zhao, Zemin Yang, Bohua Li, Shilei Deng, Zhibin Peng, Qingyan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery spasm (CAS) may cause ischemia, malignant arrhythmias, and syncope without obstructive coronary disease; a parasympathetic-dominant phenotype is increasingly recognized.Case SummaryA 64-year-old man with >7 years of recurrent syncope had Holter documentation of transient inferior ST-segment elevation during sleep followed by high-grade atrioventricular block. Coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis, and head-up tilt testing was positive (mixed type). Cardioneuroablation (CNA) targeting atrial ganglionated plexi abolished vagal responses and improved electrophysiologic indices. Postprocedural Holter showed no recurrent ST-segment elevation or atrioventricular block, and he remained symptom-free at 1-month follow-up.DiscussionThe close sequence of ST-segment elevation and functional atrioventricular block supports a unified vagally mediated mechanism linking CAS and reflex syncope; CNA may be a mechanistically targeted option in selected refractory cases.Take-Home MessagesNocturnal ST-segment elevation with bradyarrhythmia suggests parasympathetic-dominant CAS. CNA can interrupt this autonomic cascade in carefully selected patients.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old man presented with recurrent syncope for more than 7 years, with a recent relapse accompanied by palpitations. His first event occurred at night after micturition and was associated with nausea and vomiting after spontaneous recovery. On presentation, vital signs were stable, and the physical examination was unremarkable except for a diastolic murmur consistent with known aortic regurgitation.

Past Medical History

He had no known coronary artery disease, cardiomyopathy, or previous sustained ventricular arrhythmias. Multiple previous hospitalizations had not identified structural heart disease or neurologic causes of syncope.

Differential Diagnosis

Obstructive coronary artery disease, primary conduction system disease, epilepsy or other neurologic causes of transient loss of consciousness, and reflex syncope with cardioinhibitory features.

Investigations

During the initial emergency evaluation years earlier, bedside monitoring suggested high-grade atrioventricular (AV) block and a temporary pacemaker was implanted. Nocturnal ST-Segment Elevation Followed by Atrioventricular Block

Preprocedural ambulatory electrocardiography captured transient inferior ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF during sleep, followed by high-grade atrioventricular block within the same event. This sequence supported a shared autonomic mechanism linking coronary spasm and functional conduction disturbance.

Head-up tilt testing was positive (mixed type), reproducing presyncope. Transthoracic echocardiography showed moderate aortic regurgitation and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 58%. Routine laboratory testing and neurologic assessment were unremarkable.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Given the tight temporal association between transient ischemic ECG changes and functional AV conduction disturbance in the absence of obstructive coronary disease, a vagally mediated mechanism was suspected. After multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making, cardioneuroablation (CNA) targeting atrial ganglionated plexi (GP) was performed.

Under local anesthesia and conscious sedation, a 3-dimensional electroanatomic map of the left atrium and pulmonary veins was created (CARTO 3, Biosense Webster). Baseline electrophysiology showed AH 125 ms, HV 45 ms, sinus node recovery time 1,800 ms, and a Wenckebach point of 90 beats/min. GP sites were identified by anatomical landmarks and confirmed by vagal response during stimulation (≥50% R–R interval increase). Radiofrequency applications were delivered with an irrigated catheter (Thermocool SF) at up to 40 W (maximum temperature 43 °C; irrigation 20 mL/min; ablation index 550), with lesions applied at each GP region until vagal responses were abolished (Figure 3).Figure 3Electroanatomic Mapping and Vagal Response During CardioneuroablationCARTO electroanatomic mapping showed atrial ganglionated plexus target regions. The ablation strategy consisted of focal, clustered radiofrequency lesions targeting the major atrial GP regions rather than pulmonary vein isolation or linear left atrial ablation. The lesion set included the left superior GP near the left superior pulmonary vein/anterior left atrial ridge, the left inferior GP near the inferior left pulmonary vein region, the right anterior GP near the anterior right pulmonary vein region, and the right inferior GP near the inferior/posterior right pulmonary vein region. Blue tags on the electroanatomic map indicate sites where test stimulation elicited a vagal response, whereas red tags indicate radiofrequency ablation applications. Ablation was continued at each GP region until stimulation-induced vagal responses were abolished (Figure 3).

Immediately after CNA, AH shortened to 85 ms, HV remained 45 ms, sinus node recovery time decreased to 980 ms, and the Wenckebach point increased to 140 beats/min. Resting sinus rate increased from 65 to 75 beats/min.

Outcome and Follow-Up

A repeat 24-hour ambulatory ECG within 24 hours after the procedure showed no pauses >2 seconds and no recurrence of ST-segment elevation or high-grade AV block. At 1-month follow-up, the patient reported no syncope or presyncope. Discussion

Vasovagal syncope is a common form of reflex syncope characterized by transient autonomic imbalance that can produce profound cardioinhibition, including functional AV block.1 Contemporary statements support CNA as an autonomic-modulation strategy for carefully selected patients with recurrent, refractory reflex syncope or functional bradyarrhythmias, using procedural endpoints that reflect parasympathetic attenuation (eg, shortened AH interval, reduced sinus node recovery time, and higher Wenckebach point).2

CAS is traditionally attributed to endothelial dysfunction and vascular smooth muscle hyperreactivity, but a clinically important subset appears to be strongly influenced by autonomic regulation.3, 4, 5 Nocturnal/rest angina with a stereotyped pattern aligns with periods of heightened vagal tone, and acetylcholine can provoke spasm in susceptible coronary segments.6 Recent reports suggest that parasympathetic modulation may be disease-modifying in selected medication-refractory CAS.7

This case adds direct clinical evidence for a unified vagally mediated cascade by capturing, on ambulatory monitoring, a close sequence of inferior ST-segment elevation followed by high-grade AV block during sleep. The finding supports the concept that a vagal surge can simultaneously drive coronary vasoconstriction and suppress AV nodal conduction, providing a parsimonious explanation for recurrent syncope without obstructive coronary disease or intrinsic conduction system abnormality. After CNA, objective electrophysiologic indices indicated reduced parasympathetic influence, and postprocedural Holter demonstrated complete resolution of both vasospastic and bradyarrhythmic events with clinical improvement. Atropine testing performed before CNA may provide additional evidence for an atropine-sensitive, vagally mediated mechanism and may help differentiate functional AV block from intrinsic conduction system disease. In this case, atropine testing was not performed. However, the overall clinical and electrophysiologic profile was consistent with a parasympathetic-dominant phenotype, including nocturnal inferior ST-segment elevation followed closely by high-grade AV block, absence of obstructive coronary artery disease on repeated angiography, a positive head-up tilt test, preserved HV interval, and improvement in sinus and AV nodal electrophysiologic parameters after CNA. The lack of preprocedural atropine testing should therefore be considered when interpreting the mechanistic attribution in this single case. Although causality cannot be proven from a single case and longer follow-up is needed, the concordant physiologic and clinical response supports CNA as a mechanistically targeted option for carefully selected patients with suspected parasympathetic-dominant CAS associated with functional bradyarrhythmias.

Conclusions

In a patient with recurrent syncope, ambulatory ECG captured nocturnal inferior ST-segment elevation followed by high-grade AV block, suggesting a shared vagal mechanism linking CAS and functional conduction disturbance.
