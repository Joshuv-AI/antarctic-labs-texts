Reliability and validity of two shuffle-based change-of-direction tests in basketball players: A smartphone-based assessment
Authors: Hongyao Huang, Kai Xu, Peng Wang, Siyu Dai, Yuming Zhong, Mengde Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the test-retest reliability and concurrent validity (with T-test and Lane test) of the Shuffle-Change of Direction (SCOD) test and the Shuffle-Shuttle Test (SST) using a smartphone-based timing application.MethodsForty-four elite collegiate basketball players (20 males, 24 females; age: 20.30 ± 1.19 years; training experience ≥ 3 years) completed the SCOD (left and right), SST, T-test, and Lane test in Session 1, and repeated the SCOD and SST in Session 2. Timing was performed via the CODTimer app. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1), coefficient of variation (CV), standard error of measurement (SEM), minimal detectable change (MDC), and Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsTotal-sample test-retest reliability was good-to-excellent (single-measures ICC2,1 = 0.81–0.91, CV = 2.43%–4.08%, SEM = 0.04–0.65 s, MDC = 0.12–1.80 s; mean bias: −0.062 to −0.009 s, all p > 0.05). After controlling for sex, the SST remained significantly correlated with the T-test (partial r = 0.43, p = 0.004) and Lane test (partial r = 0.44, p = 0.003). Most sex-adjusted SCOD correlations were non-significant, except the left SCOD with the Lane test (partial r = 0.32, p = 0.03).ConclusionBoth tests are reliable using a smartphone app. The SCOD assesses side-specific shuffle performance, whereas the SST provides a reliable assessment of sustained multi-directional shuffle performance. The concurrent validity of the SCOD remains insufficiently established.

Introduction

Basketball is a sport characterized by frequent actions in the frontal plane.1 Studies have shown that players may execute more than 300 lateral movements during a single game, representing approximately 18.1% to 42.1% of total playing time, with each movement typically covering a distance between 2.28 and 4.24 m.2 These lateral actions involve rapid start-and-stop motions primarily occurring in the frontal plane and are essential for quick directional shifts within brief intervals.3 Such movements include shuffles, cutting, and changes of direction (COD). Among these, the shuffle is a core element of lateral movement,4 playing a critical role in both mobility and defensive effectiveness on the court.5,6

Currently, commonly used COD tests for basketball players that include shuffle movements, such as the T-test and the Lane agility test, require athletes to perform a combination of movement patterns (e.g., sprinting, backpedaling, and shuffling).7 However, these mixed-movement protocols may not accurately isolate or reflect shuffle-specific ability. Therefore, to fully capture basketball-specific shuffle capacities, assessments must be developed to target complementary domains. Specifically, there is a need for a protocol isolating single-cut execution to assess side-specific shuffle performance, as well as a multi-directional continuous protocol to assess sustained high-intensity shuffle performance and repeated movement adjustments. To assess the former performance domain, Wang and colleagues modified the traditional 505 test by replacing sprinting with lateral shuffling, resulting in the Shuffle-Change of Direction (SCOD) test.8 However, they did not report the test–retest reliability of this modified protocol. Moreover, in actual basketball gameplay, movements are executed at various angles and directions.9 At the same time, in most regions, the game is played under a 24-s shot clock rule, with a requirement to advance the ball into the frontcourt within 8 s.10,11 In actual match play, a complete offensive possession typically involves multiple consecutive shuffling movements, defensive adjustments, and COD, rather than a single short movement.2 From a performance perspective, the cumulative duration and high-intensity multi-directional directional changes within a single offensive possession require sustained rapid shuffling and repeated decelerations/accelerations, rather than merely representing an instantaneous single-cut explosion. Based on this, we developed a new multi-angled shuffle test, termed the Shuffle-Shuttle Test (SST), aimed at better capturing the demands of basketball-specific shuffle performance.

With the advancement of mobile technology, smartphone apps equipped with high-speed cameras and motion analysis algorithms have emerged as practical and cost-effective tools for sports performance assessment.12,13 These apps allow for reliable measurement of movement time and kinematic variables in a variety of testing environments. Recent studies have demonstrated good validity and reliability of such apps for assessing sprint and linear agility performance.12,14,15 Specifically, validation studies on the CODTimer application have demonstrated near-perfect concurrent validity (ICC = 0.97, r > 0.96) against standard timing gates during COD tasks.14,16 While the CODTimer application itself has previously demonstrated high concurrent validity against timing gates in COD tasks, its application for measuring lateral shuffle-specific testing protocols remains to be evaluated.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the reliability and validity of both the SCOD test and the SST using this smartphone-based timing application. We hypothesized that both SCOD and SST would demonstrate high test-retest reliability. Furthermore, based on movement specificity, we hypothesized that the multi-directional SST would exhibit moderate-to-strong positive correlations with the composite T-test and Lane test, whereas the isolated SCOD would display weak-to-moderate correlations.

Methods

Participants

Forty-four collegiate basketball players, consisting of 20 males and 24 females, volunteered to participate (Table 1). All participants were elite student-athletes competing in the Chinese university Basketball Association Division I leagues. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) actively training for a minimum of 3 sessions per week with a consistent volume of ≥ 120 min per session, and (b) possessing a minimum of 3 years of official regional or national tournament competitive history. According to Walter et al., a sample of 40 or more participants provides adequate power (≥0.80) to detect an expected intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.80, assuming a minimally acceptable ICC of 0.60, a significance level of 0.05, and two repeated measurements per participant.17 Therefore, the current sample size (n = 44) ensures robust statistical power to assess the reliability of the proposed test. The study was approved by the Sports Science Experiment Ethics Committee of University (approval number: 2024409H). Procedure

This study consisted of two testing sessions separated by a minimum of seven days. In Session 1, participants completed the LSCOD, RSCOD, SST, T-test, and Lane test, with the order of the five testing protocols randomized. In Session 2, participants repeated the LSCOD, RSCOD, and SST, with the order of the three testing protocols also randomized. Each test was performed three times. Prior to each testing session, participants completed a standardized 10-min warm-up consisting of dynamic stretching and familiarization trials for each test. A 3-min recovery period was provided between consecutive trials of the same test, and a 5-min passive recovery period was provided between different testing protocols.

All video analyses were performed by the same investigator, who was blinded to session order and to all previous trial times during the analysis process. All videos were recorded using an iPhone 14 Pro Max at 240 fps, and timing analyses were performed using the CODTimer application (version 3.0.3).16

Shuffle-change of direction test

In the SCOD test, the acceleration shuffle distance was set at 5 m, with a timing point and a marker pole positioned at the 5-m mark to accurately capture the start and end frames (Fig. 1), and the actual COD distance was 2.5 m.8 During video acquisition, the smartphone camera was mounted on a tripod at a fixed height of approximately 1.0 m and positioned 4.0 m from the timing point shown in Fig. 1, maintaining a perpendicular orientation to the plane of motion. Taking the left SCOD as an example, participants began behind the starting line in a standardized ready position: feet shoulder-width apart, knees flexed at approximately 60°, body slightly leaning forward, and center of gravity lowered.1,4 Participants then shuffled to the left with maximal effort until their left foot crossed the COD line. Immediately afterward, they shuffled to the right with maximal effort until they passed the timing point (YouTube video links: https://youtu.be/xMhlE38DEyc). Each participant performed three trials on both the left and right sides. The order of trials was randomized, and a 3-min recovery period was provided between attempts. Performance was timed using the CODTimer system, which has previously demonstrated validity for timing change-of-direction tasks against optical timing gates.16 The start and end frames were defined as the moments when the participant's body mid-line aligned with the marker poles at the respective endpoints.Fig. 1Layout of SCOD and SST test.

Shuffle-Shuttle Test

The SST was conducted within the center circle of a basketball court. Participants were required to perform shuffle movements in eight directions, with both the endpoints of each shuffle and the center of the circle marked on the floor. For video acquisition, the smartphone camera was mounted on a tripod at a fixed height of approximately 1.0 m and positioned 4.0 m in front of the center circle. The distance from the center to each directional endpoint was 2.5 m (Fig. 1). Participants began at the center of the circle in a standardized ready position: feet shoulder-width apart, knees flexed at approximately 60°, body slightly leaning forward, and center of gravity lowered,1 with one hand touching the ground to initiate the test. Upon the start signal, participants shuffled to each designated endpoint in the order shown in Fig. 1. At each endpoint, they touched the floor marker with their hand before returning to the center and touching the center marker. They then proceeded to the next direction. After completing all eight directions, participants returned to the center for a final touch (YouTube video links: https://youtu.be/jqDYcesTCgo). Timing was recorded from the first frame when the participant's hand left the ground to the first frame when the hand touched the ground at the end of the final return.

T-test

The T-test was conducted on a basketball court. A central cone was placed 10 m from the starting line, with two additional cones positioned 5 m to the left and right of the central cone. Participants began in a standing position at the starting line and sprinted forward to the central cone, touching the top of the cone with their left hand. They then performed a 5-m lateral shuffle to the left cone and again touched the top of the cone with their left hand. Next, they shuffled 10 m to the right cone and touched the top with their right hand. Following this, participants shuffled 5 m back to the central cone and completed the test by backpedaling to the starting line.18 Each participant completed three trials, aiming to finish as quickly as possible, with a 3-min rest interval between trials. The fastest time was recorded for subsequent data analysis. During testing, an iPhone was mounted on a tripod positioned 5 m perpendicular to and 5 m horizontal to the starting line to record the entire test. The tripod height was adjusted individually to match the vertical distance from the floor to the participant's greater trochanter. A 1.5-m-high vertical marker pole was placed at the starting line, and the start and end frames were determined based on the moment the participant's greater trochanter aligned with the vertical marker.

Lane test

Participants began the test from the upper left corner of the free-throw line, sprinting 5.8 m forward to the baseline, followed by a 4.9-m lateral shuffle to the right along the baseline. They then performed a backward run to the upper right corner of the free-throw line and shuffled 4.9 m to the left, touching the designated spot with their foot. Immediately afterward, participants reversed direction and repeated the same movement sequence.18 Timing stopped once the participant returned to the starting point. Each participant completed three trials, with a 3-min rest interval between trials. The fastest time was recorded for subsequent data analysis. During testing, an iPhone was mounted on a tripod positioned 5 m perpendicular to the starting point and 5 m horizontally from the start line to capture video footage. The tripod height was individually adjusted to match the distance from the floor to the participant's greater trochanter. A vertical marker pole (1.5 m in height) was placed at the starting point, and the start and end frames were defined as the moments when the center of the participant's hip aligned with the vertical marker.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution and the homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. Test-retest reliability was assessed by comparing the results from Session 1 and Session 2 (separated by 7 days) using single-measures ICC (ICC2,1) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and between-session coefficients of variation (CV). Interpretation of ICC values followed the guidelines by Koo and Li,19 where ICC > 0.90 was considered excellent, 0.75 – 0.90 good, 0.50 – 0.75 moderate, and < 0.50 poor.19 Detailed ICC specifications and estimates, including the model, type, unit, estimate and 95% CI, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The between-session coefficient of variation (CV%) was calculated from the standard error of measurement (SEM) divided by the pooled mean of the two sessions: CV% = (SEM/Mean_pooled) × 100, with values ≤ 10% considered acceptable. For the LSCOD, RSCOD, and SST, the best of three trials in each session was used for the reliability analyses. For the validity analyses, the best Session 1 trial for the LSCOD, RSCOD, and SST and the best of three trials for the T-test and Lane test were used. Selecting the best trial aligns with established protocols in COD speed and agility testing20,21 and was used to represent each participant's best observed performance; however, it is noted that utilizing the best trial can occasionally produce slightly higher reliability estimates than using trial means. Effect sizes between sessions were calculated using Cohen's d, where values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively.22 The standard error of measurement (SEM; SEM = SDdiff/√2) and minimal detectable change (MDC95; MDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM) were calculated to quantify absolute measurement error and the magnitude of change required to exceed measurement error. Pearson correlation coefficients with 95% CIs were used to examine associations between the LSCOD, RSCOD, and SST and the T-test and Lane test in the total sample and within male and female subgroups. To account for potential confounding by sex, partial Pearson correlation analyses controlling for sex were additionally performed for all associations between the LSCOD, RSCOD, and SST and the T-test and Lane test in Session 1. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for the partial correlation coefficients were calculated using Fisher's z-transformation. Correlation magnitudes were interpreted as follows: ≤ 0.1 (trivial), 0.1 – 0.3 (small), 0.3 – 0.5 (moderate), 0.5 – 0.7 (large), 0.7 – 0.9 (very large), and 0.9 – 1.0 (almost perfect).23 To assess absolute agreement between sessions, Bland–Altman plots were constructed.24 The mean difference (bias) and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated as mean difference ± 1.96 × SD of the differences. One-sample t-tests of paired mean differences against zero were performed to determine whether systematic bias differed significantly from zero. Due to differences in test duration, percentage bias and percentage limits of agreement (%bias and %LoA) were also computed. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare performance between male and female participants within each session. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Table 2 presents the reliability indices for the three tests. No statistically significant between-session differences were observed for any of the three repeated protocols (all p > 0.05; ES = 0.04–0.12). All three tests demonstrated good to excellent test-retest reliability in the total sample (ICC2,1 = 0.81 – 0.91, CV < 5%), and moderate to excellent reliability across sex-stratified subgroups (ICC2,1 = 0.70 – 0.92, CV < 5%), with LSCOD showing the highest reliability (ICC2,1 = 0.91, CV = 2.43%, SEM = 0.04 s, MDC = 0.12 s), and SST exhibiting the largest MDC values (1.80 s) due to its longer duration. Discussion

This study aimd to examine the reliability and validity of SCOD and SST. The findings indicate that both tests demonstrate acceptable test–retest reliability while capturing different aspects of shuffle performance in basketball players.

No statistically significant between-session differences were observed for the three repeated tests, and the ICC estimates indicated good-to-excellent test–retest reliability in the total sample. This may be attributed to the high degree of standardization in test procedures and familiarity of participants with the movement patterns. Regarding absolute measurement error, the LSCOD and RSCOD exhibited relatively low SEM and MDC values. The MDC represents the magnitude of change required to exceed measurement error at the 95% confidence level; however, responsiveness to meaningful performance changes cannot be determined because a smallest worthwhile change or responsiveness analysis was not conducted. In contrast, the SST showed larger SEM and MDC values, likely due to greater test complexity or variability in participant performance, warranting cautious interpretation in practice. Bland–Altman analyses showed small mean biases with no statistically significant systematic bias for any protocol, although the SST exhibited wider absolute limits of agreement than the two SCOD conditions. These findings support the feasibility and test–retest reliability of smartphone-based timing for lateral shuffle protocols in field settings, extending previous applications from linear sprinting and 505 tests to lateral shuffle movements.14,16

After controlling for sex, the associations between the SST and both conventional agility tests remained significant, whereas most associations involving the SCOD were attenuated and no longer significant. This suggests that sex contributed partly to the pooled associations, while also highlighting differences in the concurrent validity of the two shuffle-based tests. This divergence may stem from movement specificity, the SCOD is performed exclusively in the frontal plane over a short distance without sprinting or backpedaling, whereas the T-test and Lane test incorporate multiple locomotor patterns. In contrast, the SST more closely resembles these composite tests in movement structure due to its repeated multi-directional shuffling, deceleration, and re-acceleration demands, which may explain the persistence of its associations after adjustment for sex. The divergent correlation patterns between sexes may also be influenced by underlying mechanical, strength, or anthropometric differences.25,26 However, because these physiological and mechanical variables were not measured in the present study, these potential explanations remain speculative and require direct verification in future biomechanical studies. Therefore, the present findings provide preliminary support for the concurrent validity of the SST, whereas the concurrent validity of the SCOD remains insufficiently established. Furthermore, because all protocols shared the same timing system, common method variance may have contributed to the observed between-test associations. Consequently, the observed relationships should be interpreted cautiously as indicators of construct overlap rather than definitive criterion validity.

The SCOD may provide a reliable measure of side-specific unilateral shuffle performance. Conversely, the SST incorporates multi-directional shuffles over an extended duration (∼15–17 s) matching a typical basketball possession timeframe.27,28 Rather than directly reflecting specific energetic system contributions, SST performance is best interpreted as evaluating the capacity to maintain high-intensity shuffle performance under repeated COD demands. Future studies incorporating blood lactate or heart rate monitoring are warranted to further examine its underlying physiological demands. For practical implementation, because the testing protocol retained the best of three trials, performing three trials after adequate familiarization is recommended to ensure performance stability. Overall, the SCOD and SST may provide complementary information on shuffle performance. The time-efficient SCOD may be useful for repeated assessment of side-specific shuffle performance. The SST may be useful for periodic assessment of sustained multi-directional shuffle performance.

This study has several limitations. The present study did not perform direct concurrent timing comparisons between the CODTimer application and optical timing gates or gold-standard motion capture systems in this specific athletic sample. Future studies should directly compare smartphone-based timing against optical timing gates or gold-standard 3D motion capture during shuffle movements to evaluate absolute timing precision in this specific movement pattern. All four testing protocols shared a single timing methodology, introducing common method variance that may artificially inflate between-test correlations. Although the sample size met the requirements for the primary reliability analysis, the statistical power for secondary analyses may be limited. Session 1 involved a greater overall testing load than Session 2 because the T-test and Lane test were administered only during the first session. Although the order of the testing protocols was randomized and standardized inter-trial and inter-test recovery periods were provided, the possibility that differences in session workload influenced the test–retest reliability estimates cannot be excluded. In addition, the overall SST ICC was higher than the sex-stratified ICCs, suggesting that the pooled reliability estimate may be influenced by baseline performance differences between sexes, consistent with results observed in the validity analysis, indicating that caution is warranted when interpreting overall reliability estimates and further supporting the need for sex-stratified analyses in future research. Compared to the SCOD, the SST requires participants to touch the ground with their hand at each marker, although this mimics game-like actions, it nevertheless introduces additional trunk flexion and body coordination components, which may confound the assessment of pure shuffle ability. Although the same rater was used across sessions to maintain procedural consistency, inter-rater reliability was not evaluated in the present study. Camera positioning and timing reference points varied across protocols to optimize visual capture for each test, which may introduce subtle methodological variation when comparing absolute performance across different tests. The T-test and Lane test are themselves composite agility measures rather than pure shuffle-specific gold standards, which inherently limits the interpretation of criterion-related validity.

Conclusion

Both the SCOD and SST demonstrated good test–retest reliability in the total sample when assessed using smartphone-based timing. After adjustment for sex, the SST retained moderate associations with both the T-test and Lane test, supporting preliminary evidence of concurrent validity for sustained multi-directional shuffle performance. In contrast, most associations between the SCOD and the criterion tests were attenuated and non-significant after controlling for sex. Therefore, although the SCOD provides a reliable assessment of side-specific shuffle performance, its concurrent validity remains insufficiently established and requires further investigation using more movement-specific criterion measures.
