Towards a few percent measurement of the Hubble constant with the current network of gravitational wave detectors without using electromagnetic information
Tom Bertheas, Vasco Gennari, Nicola Tamanini

Abstract
Gravitational waves provide a novel and independent measurement of cosmological parameters, offering a promising avenue to address the Hubble tension alongside traditional electromagnetic observations. In the absence of electromagnetic counterparts or complete host galaxy catalogs, current measurements rely on population-based methods that statistically combine black hole merger events. Building on recent models that incorporate additional structure in the primary black hole mass distribution, using public data from the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA (LVK) collaboration third observing run (O3), we obtain a 30% accuracy improvement on the measurement of the Hubble constant with respect to the result reported by LVK. Employing a realistic simulation that includes full Bayesian single-event inference, we present forecasts for the upcoming LVK observational runs, O4 and O5. Using a three power-law mass model, we project a measurement of the Hubble constant with 20% accuracy at O4 sensitivity, improving to 2.7% accuracy at O5 sensitivity. Our findings demonstrate the potential for gravitational waves to provide a substantial contribution to solving the Hubble tension within the next decade of observations.

Introduction

In the last ten years since their first direct detection
Abbott et al. 2016a
, gravitational waves (GWs) have firmly established a new observational window onto the Universe.
This plethora of new observations is yielding original insights into different physical and astronomical domains
Abbott et al. 2019a
;
Abbott et al. 2024
;
Abbott et al. 2021a
;
Abbott et al. 2023a

GWs are in particular becoming a new instrument for cosmology, providing independent inference on the dynamics of the Universe uniquely complementary to standard electromagnetic (EM) probes
Abbott et al. 2017a
;
Abbott et al. 2021c
;
Abbott et al. 2023c

In fact, GWs naturally provide a direct measurement of the source’s

;
Dalal et al. 2006

Obtaining complementary redshift information to a GW source is the main challenge of performing cosmological inference with GWs signals, which per se do not directly deliver a redshift measurement.
Several approaches have so far been developed to provide redshift information.
The confident detection of an electromagnetic (EM) counterpart, allowing for a single galaxy to be associated to the GW signal (a so-called
bright siren
event), provides exquisite cosmological constraints at low redshift
Abbott et al. 2017a
;
Abbott et al. 2019b
;
Palmese et al. 2024
.
Around 50 of such bright sirens, could provide a 2% measurement of the
Hubble constant
, the parameter that determines the present rate of expansion of the Universe
Chen et al. 2018
.
Such a result would deliver a new, concrete insight into the so-called
Hubble tension

Di Valentino et al. 2021
;
Verde et al. 2024
, possibly hinting at new physics beyond the current standard cosmological model.
Unfortunately in the three GW observational campaigns completed so far by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) collaboration
Abbott et al. 2019a
;
Abbott et al. 2024
;
Abbott et al. 2021a
;
Abbott et al. 2023a
, only one bright siren has been detected
Abbott et al. 2017b
, and no new ones have so far been reported in the ongoing fourth observational run.

In the absence of an EM counterpart, cosmological inference with GWs can be performed accumulating information from multiple detections (generally referred to as
dark sirens
) combined with archival EM data.
One such approach consists in using galaxy catalogs to statistically assign a host to GW events
Schutz 1986
;
Holz and Hughes 2005
;
Del Pozzo 2012
;
Gray et al. 2023
, or to cross correlate GW events to the matter distribution in the Universe

;
Oguri 2016
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024

Combining multiple observations in this way require however to consistently infer the underlying events population, implying that a joint analysis of both cosmological and population parameters is required
Mastrogiovanni et al. 2023
;
Gray et al. 2023
.
Remarkably, even in the absence of any EM information (direct counterparts or archival catalogs), a suitable modelling of the underlying GW population can still be exploited to infer cosmological parameters.
Such a method was initially proposed for binary neutron star mergers
Markovic 1993
;
Chernoff and Finn 1993
;
Finn 1995
;
Taylor et al. 2012
, and later extended to binary black holes (BBHs)
Farr et al. 2019
;
Ezquiaga and Holz 2022
;
Mastrogiovanni et al. 2022
;
Mali and Essick 2024
.
This approach is commonly referred to as
spectral sirens

Given the lack of GW events with counterparts and being current all-sky galaxy catalogs limited by low completeness
Abbott et al. 2023c
, spectral siren methods are fundamental to guarantee effective cosmological constraints with GWs from forthcoming LVK observations
Abbott et al. 2016b

III
Prospects for O4 and O5

Based on the current knowledge on the BBH population, we provide simulation-based prospects for the upcoming LVK observing runs, simulating mock datasets at O4 and O5 sensitivities for the two mass models,
PLG
and
3PL
.
According to the official LVK plans
Abbott et al. 2016b
, we set
\sim 2
yrs of observation time for O4 and
3
yrs for O5, assuming realistic duty cycles for the detectors.
We thus consider a four detector network for both observing run, including the two LIGO
Aasi et al. 2015
, the Virgo
Acernese et al. 2015
and the KAGRA interferometers
Akutsu et al. 2021
(the latter joining the network late in O4).
Additional information can be found in App.
A
.

We generate a synthetic population from the BBH rates corresponding to the maximum likelihood values obtained from O3 analyses with a fixed cosmology
Gennari et al. 2025
.
The injected population parameters are reported in App.
B
, alongside with the priors used in the hierarchical inference.
For our simulated data, we adopt the Plank 2015 results
Ade et al. 2016
for a flat
\Lambda\rm CDM
model.
Additional details can be found in App.
B
.

We evaluate the detectability of each GW event based on its network matched-filter SNR (computed using
bilby

Ashton et al. 2019
;
Romero-Shaw et al. 2020
;
Smith et al. 2020
), setting the detection threshold at
\rm SNR=12
.
Noise is simulated for each GW event in all detectors of the LVK network from the PSDs at O4 and O5 design sensisivities
Abbott et al. 2016b
, and the GW signal is modelled with the
IMRPhenomXHM
waveform
García-Quirós et al. 2020
.
While the masses and the redshift follow the population distribution, the three-dimensional spin components are drawn uniformly in magnitude and isotropic in orientation.
This allows us to remain agnostic about the spins distribution, which is not included in our population models.
The remaining extrinsic parameters are also drawn uniformly from the same priors used for single event parameter estimation (PE), except for the geocentric time which is drawn uniformly over the entire observing run.
The priors on the single event parameters are further discussed in App.
C
.
To make our analysis as realistic as possible, we perform full Bayesian PE on the detected events using
bilby
, to obtain the posterior samples required for the hierarchical inference.

Using
ICAROGW
, we carry out joint hierarchical inference of the population and cosmological parameters with the simulated datasets generated with the procedure described above.
We account for selection effects in the analysis by generating
injections
which cover a sufficiently large portion of the parameter space, and assess their detectability with the same procedure used for the mock catalogues.
Details about the injections can be found in App.
D
.
We adopt for the hierarchical inference the same population model used to generate the dataset.
By doing so, we assume any form of mismodelling in the population to be negligible.

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a new GW-only measurement of the Hubble constant using a novel BBH primary mass distribution with three distinctive features (
3PL
), that is preferred to the fiducial
PLG
by LVK O3 data.
We improve current constraints on
H_{0}
from GWs alone by a factor
\mathchar 21016\,1.5
compared to previous LVK analyses
Abbott et al. 2023c
, with a measurement of
H_{0}=84^{+35}_{-25}\penalty\ \rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}
.
This result highlights how properly resolving intrinsic structure in the BBH population can dramatically enhance cosmological inference with GWs.
By projecting the
3PL
model to upcoming LVK observing runs, we provide forecasts for the constraints on
H_{0}
and
\Omega_{m,0}
in the near future.
We report an expected constraint on
H_{0}
at 2.7% by the end of O5.
Such a result constitutes the tightest forecast on
H_{0}
so far estimated for O5, showing that a GW solution of the Hubble tension is within reach of the current second-generation detector network, even without relying on complementary EM information.

The results obtained in our investigation strongly depend on the
3PL
BBH population model we considered, which presents three sharp features in the primary mass distribution driving the precise cosmological inference.
Even if
3PL
is currently preferred by O3 data, nothing guarantees that it will be still favoured by future observations.
Nevertheless if sharp features in the BBH mass distribution similar to the ones of
3PL
are indeed present, even if another model will be preferred we may expect similar cosmological constraints
Farr et al. 2019
.

Furthermore
3PL
assumes a stationary, i.e. not evolving in redshift, BBH mass distribution.
An unmodelled redshift evolution of the BBH mass function may provide a substantial cosmological bias, if not properly taken into account
Mukherjee 2022
;
Pierra et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2024
;
Mali and Essick 2024
.
However the observed mass distribution does not show any evidence of redshift evolution so far
Karathanasis et al. 2023
;
Lalleman et al. 2025
;
Gennari et al. 2025
, and an improved
3PL
model capable of correctly capturing potential redshift evolution of one or more features, is not expected to considerably degrade the results provided here, barring extreme deviations from stationarity at high redshift.

Finally, future GW observations could actually not provide the number of detections considered in our analyses (e.g. because of a lower BBH merger rate or a shorter observational time), or the preferred population model results to be one with (slightly) broader mass features.
In such cases, we can expect a moderate degradation of the cosmological results presented here.
Such a loss could however be more than compensated by the use of complementary EM information, either in the form of galaxy catalogs or by the observations of more EM counterparts.

Even considering all these possible issues, the general conclusion we can draw from our investigation is that a solution of the Hubble tension is within reach of the current network of GW detectors.

Appendix C
Events parameter estimation

For each of the
detected
event in our simulated datasets, we estimate their parameters using
bilby

Ashton et al. 2019
;
Romero-Shaw et al. 2020
;
Smith et al. 2020
.
We simulate colored Gaussian noise in the detectors and consequently employ a Gaussian likelihood in frequency domain, bandpassed between
[20,1024]\penalty\ {\rm Hz}
.
We use the
IMRPhenomXHM
waveform model
García-Quirós et al. 2020
which includes higher angular harmonics but neglects precession (thus assuming aligned spins binaries).
We generate the signal and noise at sampling frequency
2048\penalty\ {\rm Hz}
, starting from
20\penalty\ {\rm Hz}
.
The signal duration is
t_{\rm seglen}=\Delta t_{\rm mrg}+4\penalty\ {\rm s}
, with
\Delta t_{\rm mrg}
being the time difference between the moment the binary’s frequency enters the detector’s band (
20\penalty\ {\rm Hz}
) and the frequency at merger.
The analysis start time is set to be at
t_{\rm seglen}+1\penalty\ {\rm s}
before the geocent time of the event (see below).

We sample over the following 11 parameters:
m_{1},m_{2}
(primary and secondary masses, such that
m_{1}>m_{2}
),
d_{L}
(luminosity distance),
\theta_{JN}
(inclination angle),
\psi
(polarization angle),
\phi
(phase of the signal),
\alpha,\delta
(right ascension and declination, i.e. the sky localization of the binary),
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
(projected component of the spin vectors of each BH along the orbital angular momentum) and
t_{\rm geocent}
(the time at which the merger signal reaches the Earth’s center).

We employ uniform priors for the component masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, within a range mildly tailored to the injected value
m_{1,\rm inj}
, i.e. between
1\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot}
and an upper bound linearly interpolated from the following reference values:
60\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot}(300\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot})
for
m_{1,\rm inj}=10\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot}(90\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot})
.
We employ a uniform prior for the luminosity distance
d_{L}
in the range
[0,16]\penalty\ {\rm Gpc}
for O4-like datasets and
[0,30]\penalty\ {\rm Gpc}
for O5-like datasets.
We assume a spin prior uniform in magnitude and orientation (see Eq. A7 of
Lange et al. 2018
), similar to what is done for real events
Abbott et al. 2019a
;
Abbott et al. 2024
;
Abbott et al. 2023a
.
For the sky localization we use a prior uniform on the celestial sphere.
The prior on
\cos\theta_{JN}
is uniform in
[-1,1]
so that the prior on the binary orientation is uniform on the unit sphere.
The priors for
\phi,\psi
is uniform in
[0,2\pi]
.
Finally, the prior for
t_{\rm geocent}
is uniform in the range
[t_{\rm g,inj}-0.1\penalty\ {\rm s},t_{\rm g,inj}+0.1\penalty\ {\rm s}]
where
t_{\rm g,inj}
is the injected geocentric time value.

We sample the likelihood with the
nessai
sampler
Williams 2021
;
Williams et al. 2021
;
Williams et al. 2023
.
To ensure a stable PE across the entire parameter space, we use 5000 live points for each event.
If 2000 live points would be sufficient for most events, we found the sampler to struggle with low mass events, a problem which was solved by increasing the number of live points.
Note that the computational cost was only mildly impacted by this choice.
As for the hierarchical inference, we use
\texttt{dlogZ}<0.1
as the stopping criterion.

Appendix D
Evaluating selection effects

Population analyses with GWs are heavily influenced by
selection effects
due to the finite detectors sensitivity
Gaebel et al. 2019
;
Mandel et al. 2020
;
Kapadia et al. 2020
;
Vitale et al. 2020
.
This effect is directly taken into account in the likelihood, by generating a large set of simulated events–normally called
injections
–and evaluate their detectability in realistic detector noise
Essick and Fishbach 2024
.
For O3 analyses, we use the publicly available injection by LVK computed at O3 sensitivity in real noise
Collaboration et al. 2023
, that have been used in the LVK publications for GWTC-3
Abbott et al. 2023b
;
Abbott et al. 2023c
.
For simulations, we generate our own injection sets at O4 and O5 sensitivities, following a procedure similar to Ref.
Agarwal et al. 2024
.
We draw events from a wide population distribution that largely covers the target one, to ensure that a sufficient number of points is available to evaluate selection effects by Monte Carlo integration during the inference (e.g. see
Mastrogiovanni et al. 2024
).
We adopt a truncated power-law with index
\alpha=-3
for the primary mass between
2\penalty\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
and
150\penalty\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
, smoothed at its lower bound with a wide tempering of
\delta_{m}=17\penalty\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
; a truncated power-law with index
\alpha_{q}=1
for the mass ratio; a power-law with index
\gamma=0
for the rate evolution.
To consistently estimate the detector sensitivity, we process the simulated injections through the same detection procedure as the events in our simulated datasets, i.e. computing the matched-filter SNR in colored Gaussian noise and labelling as detected those above the
\rm SNR>12
threshold, as described in Sec.
III
.
Following the aforementioned method, we generate
5\cdot 10^{5}
(
6\cdot 10^{6}
) detected injections for O4 (O5) simulations.
We comment on the numerical stability in App.
E
.

In our analysis, the requirements above need to be carefully addressed, given the sharp features involved in the

In Ref.

Talbot and Golomb 2023
.
For this reason, we use the same criteria on the effective samples and injections adopted in Ref.
Gennari et al. 2025
, for both O3 data and simulations.
We note, however, for O4 and O5 simulations involving a large number of events, that the
10M_{\odot}
peak of the
3PL
is prone to numerical instability from
\texttt{Neff}_{\texttt{PE}}
, driven by the few events whose noise realization statistically shifts
m_{1}
posteriors to lower values, leaving
10M_{\odot}
in the tail of the distribution.
Such few events have been removed from the analysis, but found not to affect the results.
Specifically, for the
3PL

\mathchar 21016\,2\%
of the total number of detected events in the dataset.
By generating several realizations of the same population, we checked that such variation is negligible compared to the Poisson noise from the events draws.
These limitations concerning the
3PL
model will hopefully disappear once a better parametrization for the primary mass is found.
