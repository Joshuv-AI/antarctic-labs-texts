Exploring the reasons behind induced abortion among Iranian couples: a qualitative study
Authors: Zari Dolatabadi, Farnaz Farnam, Maryam Moridi, Maryam Damghanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInduced abortion remains a significant reproductive health issue in Iran, shaped by complex socio-economic, cultural and legal factors. Despite its importance, limited evidence exists on the underlying motivations for seeking abortion in a context of restrictive laws and pronatalist policies.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the reasons underlying couples’ decisions to seek induced abortion within the constraints of the current legal framework.DesignQualitative interview study, co-designed with couples with a history of induced abortion.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with a purposively selected sample of couples. Data analysis followed a content analysis approach and was iterative, occurring concurrently with data collection until thematic saturation was reached.SettingBaharloo, Arash and Imam Khomeini hospitals, as well as private gynaecology and midwifery clinics in Tehran, Iran, between January and November 2024.Participants32 adults participated, including 18 women who had experienced an induced abortion and 14 of their male partners, representing diverse socio-economic backgrounds.ResultsParticipants’ decisions about abortion were shaped by multiple, interconnected factors. Economic pressures, including job instability and high costs of childbearing, intersected with relationship concerns, such as lack of partner support and unstable marital life. Social norms influenced choices through expectations of parenting, emphasis on individual autonomy and attitudes toward fertility. Pregnancy-related fears, including past traumatic experiences, health concerns and worries about fetal well-being, further contributed to decision-making. Finally, social uncertainty and anxieties about children’s futures, along with perceived inadequacy of government support, created conditions in which continuing a pregnancy often seemed unfeasible.ConclusionCouples’ decisions to seek induced abortion in Iran emerge from complex interactions between economic pressures, relational dynamics, cultural shifts, embodied experiences and structural constraints. Pro-natalist policies that fail to address these underlying factors are unlikely to reduce abortion rates. Findings underscore the need for comprehensive interventions addressing socioeconomic insecurities, relationship support and reproductive health services, while respecting couples’ decision-making autonomy.

Introduction

Induced abortion is defined as the intentional termination of a pregnancy before the 22nd week of pregnancy or before the fetus reaches 500 grams in weight, at which point it cannot survive outside the uterus.1 According to the WHO, nearly half of all pregnancies worldwide each year are unintended. Of these, six out of ten unintended pregnancies and three out of ten of all pregnancies end in induced abortion.2 In Iran, induced abortion also occurs at significant rates, with reported statistics estimating between 370 000 and 530 000 cases annually, while some unofficial sources suggest figures as high as one million per year.3

A substantial body of international evidence has demonstrated that induced abortion, when performed safely using evidence-based methods recommended by WHO, is a low-risk medical procedure that is not associated with long-term physical or mental health harms. However, the safety of abortion is highly dependent on the legal and clinical context in which it is provided. When access to safe abortion is restricted, individuals with unintended pregnancies may resort to unsafe methods, which remain a major preventable cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide.4 Unsafe abortion accounts for an estimated 4.7%–13.2% of maternal deaths annually and can result in severe physical complications, as well as significant social and economic burdens for women, families, communities, and healthcare systems.5

In Iran, induced abortion is permitted only under specific legal circumstances, such as when the mother’s life is at risk or in cases of severe fetal abnormalities.6 Within this restrictive legal environment, decisions to terminate a pregnancy are typically influenced by a complex interplay of economic, familial, cultural and health-related factors.7 Economic challenges, including unemployment and poverty, may heighten the desire to improve living conditions while reducing the perceived financial capacity to raise children.8 Unstable or strained marital relationships also play a significant role. Furthermore, unplanned pregnancies, concerns about fetal abnormalities and parental health issues contribute to abortion decisions.9 Cultural factors, such as the social stigma associated with pregnancies outside of marriage or pregnancies at advanced maternal age, as well as gender preferences for male children, further shape the prevalence of induced abortion.6

In an effort to curb population decline and prevent population ageing, Iran enacted the Family and Youth Protection Law.9 A key strategy of this law aimed to reduce induced abortions in order to promote population growth. However, strict measures, including the elimination of routine fetal health screenings, restricted access to family planning services, bans on permanent sterilisation for men and women, and limitations on the distribution and promotion of contraceptives, have paradoxically led to an increase in unintended pregnancies and, consequently, induced abortions.10

Reproductive behaviour, including fertility choices, is shaped by multiple cultural, social and personal factors.11 Amid rapid economic and social change, Iran’s traditional family structure has undergone significant transformation.12 The expansion of educational opportunities for women, their increased participation in the workforce and improved access to contraceptives are among the key factors contributing to declining fertility rates and shifting attitudes toward childbearing.13 As a result, traditional values favouring large families have gradually been replaced by new patterns of family planning. This shift reflects the rationalisation of marriage and marital relationships in modern society, with one notable outcome being the rise in the prevalence of induced abortion.14

Given recent social, economic and cultural changes in Iran, understanding the factors underlying couples’ decisions regarding induced abortion is essential. While previous studies have examined abortion, there is limited qualitative evidence focusing specifically on couples’ lived experiences and perspectives. Such understanding is critical for designing culturally sensitive and effective health policies and interventions that prioritise reproductive health and safety. Therefore, this study aims to explore the factors influencing Iranian couples’ decisions to pursue induced abortion despite legal restrictions.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study represents the first phase of a mixed-methods study, and its protocol has already been published.15 It aimed to explore the reasons behind induced abortion from the perspective of Iranian couples. We conducted in-depth interviews with 18 women and 14 men residing in Tehran, Iran, who had recently experienced an induced abortion. It is noteworthy that four husbands of the participating women either declined to participate or their wives preferred that they not be included in the study. Methods and results are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (see online supplemental file).

We used a qualitative content analysis approach, which is appropriate for exploring sensitive research topics and for gaining an in-depth understanding of the experiences of Iranian couples within their sociocultural context. This approach systematically interprets qualitative data through structured coding, categorisation and the identification of patterns and themes.16 17

Participant recruitment

The study included women aged 15–49 years who had experienced induced abortion, along with their husbands. For the purposes of this study, induced abortion was defined as an abortion performed at the request of the couple and without any medical indication. All participants were residents of Tehran, able to speak and understand Persian and capable of providing informed consent.

Purposive sampling was conducted in medical centres affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, including Arash Hospital, Baharloo Hospital and Imam Khomeini Hospital. The interviewer identified eligible participants by visiting these centres and reviewing patient records to identify women diagnosed with induced abortion. Although induced abortion is prohibited in Iran and providers who perform illegal abortions (such as gynaecologists or midwives) may face legal consequences, couples are not punished for attempting an abortion. Consequently, they often seek care at public or private healthcare centres if complications arise following the procedure.

Because women visiting these centres were predominantly seeking care for abortion-related complications, part of the sampling was also conducted in specialised gynaecology and midwifery clinics to include women who had experienced uncomplicated induced abortion. In these clinics, purposive sampling was carried out by asking physicians or midwives to identify potential participants who had recently undergone induced abortion and visited the clinic. After the study objectives were explained and informed consent was obtained, these individuals were referred to the researcher for participation. At the conclusion of each interview, participants were asked to refer others who might meet the inclusion criteria, thereby enabling snowball sampling.

Participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study objectives, assured of complete confidentiality, informed that their responses would be used solely for research purposes, and made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Following the acquisition of informed consent, each interview was documented through audio recording and supplementary written notes.

Interviews were conducted either individually or as couples, depending on participants’ preferences, in order to facilitate open discussion. The interviews took place in locations where participants felt comfortable, ensuring both privacy and the ability to freely disclose sensitive information. The sampling strategy aimed to ensure diversity in terms of age, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, education level and employment status.

Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted by the first author (ZD), a PhD candidate in Reproductive Health with experience in several research projects in Gynaecology and Obstetrics, between January and November 2024, using a semi-structured guide developed based on the methodological framework by Rubin and Rubin.18 This framework emphasises flexible interviewing and organises questions into main questions, follow-up questions, probing questions and clarification questions, allowing for in-depth exploration of participants’ experiences.

Given the criminalised nature of abortion in Iran, participants were assured that all information provided would remain strictly confidential. This assurance was emphasised at the beginning of the interviews to build trust and encourage open sharing of experiences. Prior to the interviews, participants received a study information sheet describing the purpose of the study and were asked to complete a demographic and obstetric questionnaire. Written informed consent was obtained. Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent.

All interviews began with an open-ended question: ‘Why did you decide to have an induced abortion?’ Follow-up and probing questions such as ‘What do you mean?’, ‘Could you tell me more about that?’ and ‘Could you clarify this?’ were used to elicit detailed responses.

The duration of individual interviews ranged from 30 to 90 min, while couple interviews lasted between 50 and 120 min. A total of 36 interviews were conducted. Of 18 women and 14 men, four interviews were conducted jointly with couples, while the remaining participants were interviewed individually (14 women, 10 men). To ensure that participants could freely express their personal perspectives, a short follow-up individual interview was conducted with each member of the four couples after the joint interview. This approach captured both individual perspectives and interactional dynamics of couples.

Data analysis and reporting

The analysis was based on qualitative content analysis, with an inductive approach.16 Content analysis provides a systematic method for examining written, oral or visual communication.19 In qualitative content analysis, the process is structured but non-linear, requiring researchers to move iteratively between the original text and its components throughout the analytic process.16

The analysis was conducted collaboratively among the research team. First, audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed verbatim by the first author and imported into MAXQDA 20 for data management and coding.20 Second, the narratives were carefully read several times to gain a thorough understanding of their content. Third, individual sentences within each narrative were examined to identify contextual cues and thematic relationships, with a focus on shared concepts, emotions or events indicating thematic relevance. Fourth, condensed units of meaning were captured in the form of codes, which succinctly represented the essence of the content and facilitated the organisation of the data. Fifth, similar codes were grouped into categories, yielding 13 subcategories and 5 main categories that encompassed the emerging findings. Finally, these subcategories and categories were further refined to develop coherent subthemes and overarching themes. Throughout the process, the research team engaged in discussion and reached consensus, adopting alternative perspectives when disagreements arose.16

Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, meaning that new interviews no longer yielded new categories or subcategories. After the 15th interview with women and the 10th interview with men, no new codes or categories emerged, and subsequent interviews were confirmatory in nature. To ensure saturation, three additional interviews were conducted. Saturation was confirmed when several consecutive interviews did not provide any novel information, indicating that the main categories and subcategories had been fully identified.

Rigor

To ensure the study’s rigour, Guba and Lincoln’s criteria were applied.21 Credibility was established through the first author’s (ZD) prolonged engagement with the participants and the study settings, where a longstanding relationship helped build trust and provided a deeper understanding of the research context. The coded interviews were shared with three participants for feedback. Furthermore, obtaining information from a diverse range of stakeholders, including women and men, enhanced the credibility of the data.

To enhance dependability, two authors (ZD and MM) independently conducted an analysis of the interviews, and then resolved any inconsistencies to agree on the initial codes. Confirmability was achieved through an independent researcher (not part of the study) who had prior experience with qualitative research, and who reviewed and validated the data analysis process.

Researchers’ reflexivity and positionality were considered throughout all stages of the research process. Researchers remained aware of their prior assumptions and professional experiences in the field of induced abortion, and sought to manage their potential influence on data collection and analysis. Coding decisions and theme development were conducted collaboratively through team discussions and repeated review of the data, with continual reference to the raw data to ensure that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ lived experiences.

Results

Based on participants’ perspectives, five main categories and thirteen subcategories were identified as reasons for choosing induced abortion (figure 1). These categories included: economic challenges; marital and partner relationship concerns; the influence of contemporary social norms on reproductive choice; pregnancy fears; and social uncertainty and fears for the child’s future. Although presented separately, these categories were deeply interconnected in participants’ narratives, with economic hardship often exacerbating marital tensions and social insecurity intensifying concerns about children’s futures.

Reasons of induced abortion among Iranian couples.

Category 1: economic challenges

Economic hardship emerged as a fundamental concern underlying couples’ abortion decisions, intersecting with nearly every other category. Participants described how financial instability created conditions in which childbearing felt untenable. This category comprised two subcategories: occupational and economic instability and high costs of childbearing.

Occupational and economic instability

Job insecurity undermined couples’ sense of stability and their capacity to plan for the future. Participants described stable employment not merely as a source of income, but as a prerequisite for feeling psychologically and materially prepared for parenthood. For many, the absence of secure work transformed childbearing from a desired possibility into an unmanageable risk. One man articulated this directly:

If I were confident about my job security, I might not have made this decision. (Male participant No. 1)

This account illustrates how employment insecurity shaped reproductive decision-making by weakening participants’ confidence in their ability to provide long-term support for a child. Beyond individual employment concerns, broader economic conditions—particularly inflation and rising living costs—further intensified couples’ sense of financial instability. These pressures were experienced even among dual-income households, suggesting that employment alone was no longer perceived as sufficient to ensure economic security:

Really, with just a salaried job, it’s impossible to live in today’s society. Inflation and the rising cost of living are overwhelming. Even with two people working as employees, life becomes unsustainable. (Female participant No. 5)

Participants’ narratives reflected not only concern about current expenses but also anxiety regarding their diminishing capacity to maintain an acceptable standard of living after the birth of another child. Economic strain was especially evident in relation to housing. Rising rents and housing costs limited couples’ ability to secure adequate living space, reinforcing perceptions that expanding the family was materially unrealistic:

We are renters living in a small house. Even with five people, space is tight, and adding another person is not possible. Rent for a bigger place is high, and with our current income, we can’t afford it. (Male participant No. 1)

For these participants, housing insecurity functioned as more than a practical limitation; it symbolised broader instability and uncertainty about the future. Overall, job insecurity, inflation and housing constraints were experienced as interconnected dimensions of economic hardship that collectively reduced couples’ sense of stability. Rather than isolated problems, these factors reinforced one another and shaped a shared perception that providing adequate conditions for raising children was increasingly unfeasible.

High costs from childbearing

Participants described the financial demands associated with pregnancy, childbirth and child-rearing as major obstacles to continuing pregnancy. The anticipated costs of prenatal care—including diagnostic tests, ultrasounds and specialist visits—were often perceived as exceeding families’ financial capacity, particularly among participants already experiencing economic strain:

We are not in a good financial situation and cannot afford the costs of pregnancy. Even during my previous pregnancy, I couldn’t afford to have the tests and ultrasounds. (Female participant No. 11)

This concern extended beyond isolated medical expenses and reflected broader anxieties about the cumulative financial responsibilities associated with parenthood. Participants described childbearing as initiating a long-term chain of economic obligations that they felt unable to sustain.

Childbirth itself was also associated with unexpected and informal costs. Some participants reported additional payment requests from healthcare providers, experiences that reinforced perceptions that even access to maternity care involved hidden financial burdens:

In addition to the fee charged by the hospital, my doctor requested an additional payment, which was a considerable amount. (Female participant No. 7)

Such experiences not only created immediate financial strain but also eroded trust in healthcare institutions, reinforcing perceptions that childbearing in the current economic climate was prohibitively expensive:

When you can barely cover the costs of these two children, how can you even think about having another one? (Male participant No. 1)

This quotation reflects how participants evaluated childbearing not as a single event, but as a continuing financial commitment extending into the future. Notably, economic concerns were rarely described as standalone reasons for abortion. Instead, participants portrayed financial strain as interacting with other domains of life—particularly marital tensions and perceptions of insufficient social support. Financial hardship often intensified conflict within relationships, while limited governmental support amplified concerns about the affordability of raising children. Together, these interconnected pressures contributed to a broader perception that expanding the family was economically unfeasible and reinforced couples’ decisions to avoid additional childbearing.

Category 2: marital and partner relationship concerns

The quality and stability of marital relationships played a central role in couples’ reproductive decisions. Two subcategories captured these dynamics: lack of male support and unstable marital life. Importantly, men’s and women’s perspectives on these issues sometimes diverged, revealing gendered expectations and tensions.

Lack of male support

Participants described male support in childbearing as encompassing emotional availability and active involvement in childcare, yet many women reported feeling largely alone in caregiving responsibilities. Women’s accounts frequently reflected emotional exhaustion and a perception that parenting responsibilities were distributed unequally within the relationship:

All the responsibility of raising and taking care of the children was on me, and my husband did not help me at all. (Female participant No. 1)

This perceived imbalance contributed to emotional exhaustion and frustration among women, leading many to perceive further childbearing as increasingly difficult to manage. Participants framed partner support not only in practical terms but also as emotional acknowledgement of the physical and psychological demands associated with childbearing and caregiving.

One of the hardest things is taking care of two young kids, especially when you don’t have anyone to help you. My husband has no understanding of how hard it is to raise children. (Female participant, No. 11)

These accounts illustrate how inadequate partner support was experienced as both a practical and emotional burden. Several women described feeling that their exhaustion and caregiving efforts were minimised or dismissed by their husbands:

He does not help me at all. When I tell him that I am exhausted and that taking care of the children alone is very difficult, he says, ‘You’ve been sitting at home all day—why are you tired? (Female participant No. 10)

This perceived lack of understanding intensified women’s feelings of emotional isolation and reinforced concerns that additional childbearing would be difficult to manage. While women primarily described insufficient support as emotionally exhausting and unequal, some men attributed their limited involvement to work-related fatigue and competing responsibilities:

When I come home from work, I’m truly exhausted and just want to rest. I know my wife needs help too, but I honestly don’t have the energy to assist with childcare or household chores. (Male participant No. 9)

Beyond work-related exhaustion, some men also framed childcare and domestic responsibilities through traditional gender-role expectations, viewing these tasks as primarily belonging to women regardless of women’s employment status. One man explained:

My wife is more experienced in childcare, so I believe she handles it better than I could. From long ago, men work outside, and women take care of the children. I think sticking to these roles makes things run more smoothly. (Male participant No. 11)

Rather than reflecting emotional disengagement alone, such accounts suggest that unequal involvement in childcare was also normalised through culturally embedded beliefs about gender roles.

Unstable marital life

Instability in marital relationships—including infidelity, substance use, violence and uncertainty about the future of the relationship—played a significant role in abortion decisions. Participants described these conditions as creating environments in which childbearing felt unsafe, unstable or irresponsible. Infidelity eroded trust and undermined couples’ ability to envision a shared future that included children:

I really love children. Unfortunately, I found out that my husband was cheating on me, and that caused our relationship to completely fall apart. After that, I stopped thinking about having children. (Female participant No. 13)

This account illustrates how the breakdown of trust within the relationship led to a withdrawal from considering continued pregnancy, as childbearing became incompatible with the perceived instability of the marital bond.

Substance use disorders also constituted a major source of instability. Women whose husbands were addicted to drugs or alcohol described unpredictable behaviour, financial insecurity and concerns about the safety and well-being of children. In this context, addiction refers to substance dependence that significantly impairs functioning and creates an unsafe environment for child-rearing:

At the beginning of our marriage, my husband was employed and not addicted, and I had two children. But later, when he became addicted and I got pregnant again, I decided to have an abortion because the situation was not suitable for another child. (Female participant, No. 10)

In this narrative, the decision to terminate the pregnancy is embedded within a broader context of escalating instability, where addiction disrupted both economic security and family functioning, making further childbearing appear untenable.

Domestic violence created similarly untenable conditions. Women described household environments characterised by ongoing conflict, which they did not want additional children to witness or experience:

When my husband is home, there is always conflict and violence. My sons constantly witness these scenes and get very upset. I don’t want another child to grow up in such an environment. A child needs peace, not a home filled with conflict. (Female participant, No. 13)

This account highlights that women’s concerns extended beyond their own safety to include the psychological well-being of existing and potential children, positioning continued pregnancy as potentially exposing another child to harm.

For women in temporary marriages, the absence of a secure legal and social status created profound uncertainty about the future, making childbearing feel impossible:

We had a temporary marriage contract, but it was still unclear when our marriage would become official. When you don’t know what the future holds, you can’t easily decide to have a child. (Female Participant, No. 18)

This uncertainty destabilised reproductive decision-making by limiting participants’ ability to plan long-term family formation.

On the other hand, some couples’ concerns were not based on present instability but on anticipated changes in their relationship after having a child. Fears of reduced emotional closeness, limited shared time and declining relationship quality influenced their decisions:

I thought that if we had a child at the beginning of our marriage, we wouldn’t spend as much time together as we should, and the quality of our relationship would decrease. (Female Participant, No. 2)

These concerns suggest that childbearing was also evaluated in terms of its potential impact on marital intimacy and relationship quality. For others, previous experiences of emotional distance during a previous pregnancy created apprehension about repeating that pattern:

In my previous pregnancy, although my husband tried to support me, I felt that he had become emotionally distant. This affected our relationship, and we both felt pressured. (Female Participant, No. 12)

This narrative indicates that even subtle emotional shifts during pregnancy could be interpreted as destabilising the marital relationship and influencing future reproductive decisions.

Overall, these accounts demonstrate that marital dynamics—both actual and anticipated—were deeply intertwined with reproductive decision-making. In particular, relationship instability amplified concerns related to economic hardship and intensified fears regarding the well-being and future of children, thereby contributing to decisions to terminate pregnancy.

Category 3: influence of contemporary social norms on reproductive choice

Over time, attitudinal and cultural changes have reshaped how couples approach family formation and childbearing. This category encompasses idealised expectations of parenting and child upbringing, rising emphasis on individual autonomy and self-fulfilment, immunity from others’ blame and individual attitudes toward fertility. Together, these factors illustrate how contemporary values and social pressures influence reproductive decisions.

Idealised expectations of parenting and child upbringing

Some participants held highly idealised and extreme views regarding childbearing. Participants emphasised the importance of providing not merely adequate but optimal financial, emotional and developmental conditions for their children. As a result, many participants viewed childbearing as something that should occur only when they felt fully prepared to meet these expectations.

Having a child is not that simple; it’s a lifelong responsibility. I will decide to have a child only when I am certain that I can handle everything—financially, emotionally, psychologically, and in terms of upbringing. (Male participant No. 3)

Similarly, another male participant emphasised the importance of being able to fully meet a child’s needs before deciding to become a parent:

I believe my child should have everything they need. I will only decide to have a child when I am certain that I can meet all of their needs—both big and small—so they will not have to experience hardship in life. (Male participant No. 4)

For some participants, these heightened parenting expectations were also shaped by their own difficult childhood experiences. Rather than reflecting idealised parenting alone, their concerns centred on preventing their future children from experiencing similar hardships:

I had a difficult childhood that still affects my life today. I don’t want my child to face the same struggles. Until I’m sure I can provide the best conditions, I won’t have a child. (Female participant No. 16)

This narrative illustrates how personal childhood experiences contributed to elevated standards for childbearing and intensified concerns about the potential consequences of raising a child under unfavourable conditions.

Rising emphasis on individual autonomy and self-fulfilment

In contemporary Iranian society, traditional family roles increasingly compete with individual aspirations. Many participants, particularly women, prioritised educational and career goals, viewing childbearing as a choice to be made only after achieving personal milestones:

I hadn’t reached my goals yet. I wanted to continue my studies, find a job, and only think about having a child once I had achieved my goals and aspirations. (Female participant No. 2)

This suggests that reproductive decisions were increasingly embedded within broader life-course planning, where pregnancy continuation was postponed in favour of self-development trajectories. For working women, concerns about the impact of pregnancy on career continuity were particularly salient. Employment interruptions were not only temporary disruptions but were perceived as producing long-term structural disadvantages in competitive labour markets:

In previous pregnancies, I quit my job to fully focus on my children. However, leaving work created a significant gap in my career. When I returned, I realised I had missed out on advancement opportunities. (Female participant No. 8)

Body image concerns also emerged as an additional dimension of self-fulfilment. For some women, the physical consequences of pregnancy were not merely aesthetic concerns but part of a broader sense of loss of bodily autonomy:

Pregnancy changes your appearance, your body no longer looks the same. Before I had a child, my body was in great shape, and I was fit. Afterwards, I didn't like looking at myself in the mirror, and I truly missed my old body. (Female Participant No. 5)

Immunity from others' blame

Social judgement and fear of criticism shaped some couples’ decisions regarding whether to continue pregnancies. When pregnancies deviated from dominant social expectations—such as short interpregnancy intervals, older maternal age or gender preference—participants described feeling exposed to scrutiny that made continuation of pregnancy socially and emotionally difficult.

The sarcastic remarks from my mother-in-law, after I became pregnant shortly after giving birth, were unbearable. (Female Participant No. 11)

This account demonstrates how social surveillance within family networks could intensify emotional pressure and contribute to decisions to terminate pregnancy.

Pregnancy at an older age and having older children can be accompanied by judgmental views and social stereotypes. Women who become pregnant later in life not only face medical concerns and physical challenges but are often subject to criticism and judgement from those around them.

The constant remarks and sarcastic comments from others were unbearable for me. I didn’t want people talking behind my back, saying that I got pregnant at this age. My children were older and did not accept it. (Female participant No. 15)

Gender preference, where culturally salient, further intensified these pressures. In contexts where having daughters was devalued, women described anticipating social mockery, which influenced decisions regarding continuation of pregnancy:

I live in an environment where I've seen that those who have multiple daughters are often mocked. When I found out my baby was a girl, it made it easier for me to decide to abort. (Female participant No.14)

Overall, these findings show that social judgement functioned as a form of normative pressure shaping reproductive decisions. Anticipated criticism from family and community created emotional strain and heightened sensitivity to social surveillance. In this context, continuing pregnancy was often perceived as exposing women to ongoing stigma and social disapproval, thereby contributing to decisions to terminate pregnancy.

Individual attitudes toward fertility

Beyond social expectations, couples also expressed personal preferences regarding family size and birth spacing that shaped decisions about continuing pregnancies. For some, achieving a desired number of children marked the endpoint of acceptable childbearing:

We have three children, and I think this number is enough. We don’t feel the need to have another child. (Male participant No. 13)

Others had specific preferences about age gaps between children, viewing certain intervals as optimal for meeting each child’s needs:

My partner and I want a second child, but we wanted there to be a gap of about 6–7 years. We planned to have another child when our daughter started school. (Male participant No.10)

Short intervals between pregnancies created particular challenges, especially when parents felt overwhelmed by the demands of closely spaced children:

Both of my children are infants and were born one after another. Given my current situation, it was impossible for me to continue the pregnancy. (Female participant No.11)

This response directly links perceived caregiving overload with the decision to terminate pregnancy, highlighting how fertility preferences interacted with lived parenting capacity. Taken together, these narratives highlight the tension between personal fertility preferences, perceived parenting capacity and social expectations, which shaped decisions to continue or terminate pregnancy.

Category 4: pregnancy fears

Past negative experiences with pregnancy and childbirth profoundly shaped participants’ attitudes toward subsequent pregnancies. This category included three subcategories: past traumatic experiences from pregnancy and childbirth, fear of harm due to poor physical and mental condition, and concerns about fetal health.

Past traumatic experiences from pregnancy and childbirth

For many women, previous pregnancies had left lasting physical and emotional scars. These experiences created aversion to future pregnancies and influenced abortion decisions:

I never want to experience pregnancy again because, for me, it was nothing but bad memories. who would miss nausea and vomiting, sleepless nights, constipation, frequent urination, or that excruciating leg and back pain? (Female participant No. 6)

This finding illustrates how cumulative physical burden transformed pregnancy into an experience that participants actively sought to avoid, thereby influencing decisions to terminate subsequent pregnancies. In addition to physical strain, some women described long-term health consequences that reshaped their perceptions of their bodily capacity:

I don’t have a healthy back, knee, or proper nerves anymore. One may be in their early adulthood, but their physical strength feels like that of someone much older. (Female participant No. 1)

These narratives indicate that pregnancy-related physical deterioration was perceived by participants as reducing their capacity to cope with future pregnancies. Beyond physical experiences, some women described mistreatment during childbirth that left psychological wounds. Disrespectful care in maternity settings created negative associations with childbearing:

When I went to the maternity ward for my third delivery, I was treated very badly. Instead of feeling calm, I was left with nothing but sadness and frustration. (Female participant No. 1)

Such experiences extended the impact of childbirth beyond the physical event, shaping long-term emotional associations with pregnancy and increasing reluctance towards continuation of future pregnancies. Similarly, difficult postpartum experiences—such as infant illness or prolonged caregiving stress—created enduring anxiety about repeating such conditions:

My son had jaundice and constant colic crying for a long time, which caused me endless stress and many doctor visits. This experience made me really afraid of having another child. (Female Participant No. 6)

Fear of harm due to poor physical and mental condition

Concerns about the potential negative impact of pregnancy on women’s physical and psychological well-being played an important role in abortion decision-making. Participants often evaluated pregnancy in relation to existing health conditions, previous experiences of illness and perceived vulnerability to future complications. Women with pre-existing medical conditions frequently described fears that continuing the pregnancy would aggravate their health problems and compromise their well-being. One participant who had previously undergone major surgery explained:

Because of the surgery I had undergone, my doctor had advised me not to become pregnant. I felt that continuing the pregnancy under those circumstances could have caused serious complications and negatively affected my health. (Female participant No. 5)

This finding illustrates how concerns about potential medical complications following prior health interventions shaped perceptions of pregnancy risk and contributed to the decision to terminate the pregnancy. Similarly, participants with chronic illnesses assessed pregnancy through the lens of previous experiences of physical suffering. As one woman with severe asthma recalled:

If I had continued the pregnancy, my asthma would have become much worse. I had experienced that in my previous pregnancy and was even hospitalized because of it. I suffered a lot during my last pregnancy because of my asthma, and I didn't want to go through that again. (Female participant No. 7)

This finding highlights how concerns about psychological well-being directly contributed to decisions to terminate pregnancy, particularly when pregnancy was perceived as a risk factor for deterioration.

Rather than reflecting a hypothetical concern, this decision was informed by prior experiences of illness exacerbation during pregnancy, leading participants to view continuation of the pregnancy as a significant threat to their health.

Advanced maternal age also heightened perceptions of physical vulnerability and pregnancy-related risk. Women often viewed pregnancy at older ages as less compatible with their physical condition and life circumstances. As one participant explained:

Age was an important factor in my decision. If I had been younger, I might have accepted the pregnancy more easily. But at this stage of my life, I felt that pregnancy could harm my body. (Female participant No. 15)

Concerns about fetal health

Anxiety regarding fetal health and potential abnormalities was another powerful driver of abortion decisions. When medical examinations indicated possible risks, couples often interpreted these signals as justification for terminating the pregnancy:

At the ultrasound, the doctor said the baby’s heartbeat was slow and irregular. I got really scared and thought, ‘I already have two kids; I don’t want a sick one,’ and figured something must be wrong. (Female Participant No. 14)

Unintentional medication uses during early pregnancy created similar fears. Participants worried that medications taken before pregnancy was known might have harmed the fetus:

I was most afraid my baby would be born with a disability because I was taking Accutane for my skin. When I found out I was pregnant, I immediately thought about abortion due to my fear of having a sick child. (Female participant No. 9)

Across these subcategories, pregnancy-related fears were not isolated experiences but interconnected with broader contextual factors. Concerns about fetal health were amplified by economic constraints related to caring for a potentially disabled child, while past traumatic experiences shaped expectations of future pregnancies. In addition, health-related fears often intersected with marital dynamics, as partners’ responses to physical and psychological risks influenced final decisions regarding pregnancy continuation.

Category 5: social uncertainty and fears for the child’s future

Participants described how broader social and political conditions shaped their reproductive decisions. This category comprised two subcategories: fears for the child’s future and weakness in government support policies. Together, these subcategories illustrate how structural factors create conditions in which childbearing may be perceived as risky or socially irresponsible.

Fears for the child’s future

Perceptions of economic instability, social decline and limited upward mobility generated persistent anxiety about the future well-being of potential children. Rather than focusing only on the immediate circumstances of pregnancy, participants evaluated childbearing in relation to long-term life prospects and societal conditions.

In many cases, this resulted in a sense of uncertainty and discouragement regarding bringing a child into the world:

Society is in a really bad state, and social problems are getting worse. I’m scared my kid would get into trouble, and that’s why I don’t want to have kids. (Male participant No. 1)

This perspective reflects how perceptions of social deterioration extend beyond present conditions and are translated into anticipatory fears about future harm and social vulnerability.

Similarly, concerns about long-term stability—particularly financial security and occupational success—were central to participants’ reasoning. The decision to avoid childbearing was often conditional on the perceived absence of stable life opportunities for future generations:

If we were sure about the financial, social, and career stability of our children, and their success, we really wouldn't do this. (Male Participant No. 7)

Among younger participants, these concerns were further reinforced by their own experiences of constrained opportunity structures. This generational perspective linked personal life chances to expectations about their children’s future environment:

Our society doesn’t give real chances to get ahead, and as a young person, I don’t see any place to grow. Living like this makes me wonder why I should have a kid. (Female Participant No. 3)

Overall, these accounts indicate that reproductive decision-making was strongly shaped by perceived structural instability, with participants framing childbearing as potentially exposing children to long-term social and economic disadvantage.

Weakness in government support policies

Participants generally perceived governmental support policies as insufficient and misaligned with the practical realities of raising children. Although pronatalist policies existed, they were often viewed as symbolic rather than addressing structural barriers such as housing, childcare infrastructure and service accessibility. Financial incentives such as loans were frequently criticised as inadequate forms of support, as they did not address core concerns related to long-term child well-being and institutional support systems:

Giving loans isn't real support. True support means solving core problems like affordable, quality daycare and helping with housing. Loans help temporarily, but don't secure the child’s future. (Female Participant No. 5)

This reflects a broader critique that financial assistance alone cannot compensate for the absence of reliable social infrastructure necessary for sustainable childrearing.

In particular, the lack of accessible and trustworthy childcare services emerged as a significant concern. Participants emphasised that the absence of safe daycare options increased perceived risks associated with employment and parenting responsibilities:

The problem is there aren’t any good daycare centres where you can safely leave your kid. Kids often get sick or pick up bad habits. (Female Participant No. 3)

Overall, these findings suggest that structural uncertainty operates as a central driver of reproductive decision-making. Perceived inadequacy of governmental support not only intensifies economic and caregiving concerns but also reinforces fears about children’s future prospects.

Discussion

The present qualitative study sought to develop an in-depth understanding of the factors shaping couples’ decisions to seek abortion in Iran, within a context characterised by religious, legal and socio-cultural constraints. The findings suggest that abortion decision-making was a complex and multidimensional process shaped through the interaction of economic insecurity, relationship dynamics, social expectations, prior reproductive experiences and uncertainty regarding the future well-being of children and families.

Economic hardship emerged as a central concern shaping participants’ decisions regarding pregnancy continuation. Consistent with previous studies,6 22 23 participants described how rising living costs, unstable employment, housing insecurity and the financial demands of child-rearing created significant pressure on family life. Importantly, participants did not describe abortion as a freely preferred option, but rather as a response to circumstances in which continuing the pregnancy was perceived as financially and emotionally unmanageable. Concerns extended beyond direct medical expenses to broader structural conditions, including insufficient social support, informal healthcare costs and uncertainty about the future. These findings suggest that reproductive decisions were deeply embedded within wider socio-economic realities that constrained couples’ perceived options.23–25

Relationship dynamics also played an important role in participants’ narratives. Women particularly described situations involving lack of emotional support, unequal childcare responsibilities, marital conflict or partner indifference. Consistent with previous literature,6 26 27 these experiences shaped women’s perceptions of whether they could cope with another child. Participants’ accounts highlighted the gendered nature of reproductive responsibility, with women frequently describing themselves as bearing the primary burden of childcare and domestic labour.28 In this context, decisions regarding pregnancy continuation were closely connected to perceptions of relational stability, support and care within the family.29 30 Some studies have shown that perceived support from a spouse and their involvement in household chores and child-rearing influence women’s intentions regarding childbearing.14 30

Social norms and anticipated judgement further shaped reproductive decision-making. Some participants described fears of criticism from family members or the wider community when pregnancies occurred under socially disapproved circumstances, such as shortly after a previous birth, at advanced maternal age or when the fetus was female. These findings indicate that abortion decisions were influenced not only by personal preferences, but also by concerns regarding social acceptability and conformity to prevailing cultural expectations.31–33 At the same time, several participants described prioritising educational, occupational or personal aspirations when considering childbearing, reflecting broader social changes that have also been documented in previous studies.29 30 34 Rather than representing a simple shift toward individualism, the findings suggest that reproductive decisions were negotiated within competing pressures between personal aspirations, family responsibilities and social expectations. Despite some narratives of personal autonomy, this section highlights the strong role of social pressure and stigma in abortion decisions. Some women choose abortion not purely based on personal preference, but due to anticipated disapproval from family, peers or society. Social norms about the “appropriate” timing and conditions for childbearing strongly influence these decisions. Pregnancies occurring soon after a previous birth, at advanced maternal age or when the fetus is female are often met with social scrutiny and pressure. As a result, decisions about continuing or terminating a pregnancy are shaped not only by individual choice, but also by the need to conform to socially accepted reproductive expectations.

Negative experiences of pregnancy and childbirth profoundly shaped participants’ attitudes toward subsequent pregnancies. Women described physical suffering, loss of dignity, complications and mistreatment during previous births—experiences that created lasting aversion to further childbearing. These findings align with research documenting how traumatic birth experiences can diminish women’s desire for future pregnancies.35–37 When women felt unable to cope with the challenges associated with pregnancy or childbearing, and when fears and anxieties contributed to a perceived loss of control over different aspects of their lives, abortion was often framed as a means of regaining control over their circumstances.

Concerns about fetal health, particularly when screenings raised questions or when medications had been used inadvertently, added another layer of complexity. Here, the non-maleficence principle—the ethical obligation to prevent harm—took on particular significance, as couples weighed potential risks against their capacity to care for a child with special needs.38 Iran’s restrictions on prenatal screenings, implemented as part of pronatalist policies, intensified these dilemmas by limiting access to information that couples needed for informed decision-making.3

The social insecurities reported by participants play an important role in shaping reproductive decisions and may lead to delayed fertility intentions or decisions regarding abortion. These concerns about children’s future in an uncertain social and political environment show how structural conditions penetrate deeply personal reproductive choices; fears of social harm and limited opportunities for children’s success are not abstract worries, but rather realistic assessments of the environments in which future generations would grow up. In line with this finding, Lutz et al also argue that declining social security and increasing social risks are among the factors influencing fertility intentions, ultimately contributing to reduced willingness to have children.39

The majority of participating couples reported not using effective contraceptive methods at the time of conception. Participants’ narratives suggested that limited access to contraceptive information and services, insufficient awareness regarding emergency contraception and restrictions associated with recent pronatalist policies may have increased vulnerability to unintended pregnancy. Consistent with previous studies,32 35 the findings highlight the importance of accessible reproductive health education and family planning services in supporting informed reproductive decision-making.

This study offers several important strengths. Including both women and men provides insight into how reproductive decisions are negotiated within couple relationships, a perspective often underrepresented in abortion research. The qualitative design enabled an in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences and the meanings attached to abortion decision-making within the socio-cultural and legal context of Iran. Recruiting participants from Tehran, a socio-demographically diverse urban setting, further allowed the study to capture a wide range of social and economic perspectives.

Several limitations should also be considered. Recruitment through healthcare settings and snowball sampling may have limited access to individuals who obtained abortions outside formal healthcare networks. In addition, as the study was conducted in Tehran, findings may not be fully transferable to rural or more traditional regions due to differing cultural and structural contexts. The sensitive legal and moral context surrounding abortion may have influenced participants’ willingness to disclose certain experiences. Some couple-based interviews may also have limited participants’ expression of divergent views, although individual follow-ups were conducted when necessary. Finally, as with all qualitative research, interpretation of data was shaped by the researchers’ perspectives, despite efforts to enhance reflexivity through team discussions and analytic memo-writing.

Future research should further examine abortion decision-making among underrepresented groups, including adolescents, unmarried women, rural populations and individuals who access clandestine abortion services. Further studies on the long-term psychological, relational and socio-economic consequences of abortion in restrictive contexts, as well as comparative cross-country research on how reproductive policies shape unintended pregnancy and abortion, may provide a deeper understanding of the structural factors influencing reproductive decision-making.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that induced abortion cannot be understood as a purely individual phenomenon detached from broader social and structural contexts. Rather, it emerges at the intersection of multiple interrelated factors, including economic pressures, gendered family roles, cultural expectations, prior reproductive experiences and inequalities in access to healthcare services, contraception and social support. In this context, decisions regarding the continuation or termination of pregnancy are often made within a space characterised by constrained choices, uncertainty and competing responsibilities toward partners, existing children and future family well-being.

Unintended pregnancy and abortion are deeply embedded in women’s everyday lived realities, including social insecurity, unequal caregiving burdens, limited social support and concerns about the future. Alongside these factors, restricted access to reproductive health information and services further shapes experiences of unintended pregnancy.

Overall, abortion within such a complex context requires a more comprehensive understanding of the social and structural determinants involved—an understanding that can inform more effective reproductive health policies and interventions that take into account both individual circumstances and the broader contexts shaping fertility-related decisions.
