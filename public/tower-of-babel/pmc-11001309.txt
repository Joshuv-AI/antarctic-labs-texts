First reported case of ANCA-associated vasculitis induced by oxaliplatin, capecitabine, and trastuzumab
Authors: Bengang Huo, Lirong Lin, Lei Zhao, Rongjie Yu, Jurong Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 68-year-old male, who was undergoing XELOX plus trastuzumab therapy for gastric cancer, developed proteinuria, hematuria, and progressive increase in creatinine after 3 months. Subsequently, the patient also experienced hemoptysis, nasal bleeding. Chest CT examination shown pulmonary hemorrhage. The MRI of the nasopharynx ruled out nasopharyngeal cancer recurrence. The MPO and PR3 were elevated, and renal biopsy confirmed ANCA-related vasculitis, which affected the lungs, kidneys, and nasopharynx. Based on the review of the patient’’s medical history and medication, it is believed that ANCA-related vasculitis was caused by XELOX plus trastuzumab chemotherapy, but it is difficult to confirm which specific drug caused it. After stopping XELOX plus trastuzumab chemotherapy, glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide was given, the patient’’s pulmonary hemorrhage and nasal bleeding stopped, and the lung lesions were absorbed. The renal function also improved. The patient later experienced pulmonary infection again, and tNGS indicated Legionella pneumophila and pulmonary tuberculosis infection. Despite anti-infection treatment, steroid dose was rapidly reduced. Ultimately, the patient gave up on treatment and eventually died.Keywords: Oxaliplatin, capecitabine, trastuzumab, vasculitis, ANCA

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a type of autoimmune disease that affects small blood vessels and causes inflammation, leading to organ damage. Drugs are one of the important causes of AAV (Supplementary References 1, 2). Here, we present a patient who developed AAV after receiving XELOX plus trastuzumab for the treatment of gastric cancer.

A 68-year-old male had a medical history of nasopharyngeal carcinoma and had undergone multiple rounds of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, resulting in complete remission three years prior. He was later diagnosed with gastric malignancy and underwent distal gastrectomy, followed by Billroth II anastomosis and jejunojejunostomy. The postoperative pathology indicated poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with some signet ring cell carcinoma. Following the surgery, he was treated with XELOX (oxaliplatin 200 mg IV + capecitabine 1 g twice a day OS) and trastuzumab 400 mg IV for three cycles. Before chemotherapy, his kidney function (creatinine was 57 µmol/L and blood urea nitrogen was 4.73 mmol/L) and urinary test were normal. However, the patient’s therapy was interrupted due to increased creatinine at 346 µmol/L and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) at 9.58 mmol/L. An abdominal CT scan showed no evidence of urinary tract obstruction. Urine analysis revealed trace proteinuria (1+) and microscopic hematuria (1600/µL); microscopy showed heterogeneous erythrocytes, without gross hematuria, and an elevated urine microalbumin/urine creatinine ratio (53.04 mg/g.crea). A nephrology consultation was requested due to concerns of AKI. However, no further treatment was supplied based on the patient’s decision. Two weeks later, the patient developed cough, yellow phlegm with blood in the sputum, intermittent epistaxis, and fever, with a body temperature up to 39 °C, accompanied by chills. His blood pressure was 152/84 mmHg. Laboratory tests showed a high white blood cell count (WBC) at 12.03*109/L, low hemoglobin at 60 g/L, a high neutrophil percentage at 95.4%, elevated levels of hypersensitive C-reactive protein (HS-CRP) at 157.14 mg/L, high levels of procalcitonin (PCT) at 7.47 ng/ml and interleukin 6 at 113.10 pg/ml. Creatinine was increased to 537 µmol/L, and BUN was increased to 22.06 mmol/L. PaO2 was 62 mmHg (without oxygen inhalation). Coagulation tests revealed high levels of fibrinogen at 6.04 g/L and D dimer at 950 ng/ml. The antinuclear antibody, anti-dsDNA, rheumatoid factor, C3, C4, anti-GBM, and anti-SSA/SSB were negative. However, anti-myeloperoxidase (anti-MPO) antibody was elevated at 152.82 RU/ml, and anti-protease 3 (anti-PR3) antibody was elevated at 98.48 RU/ml. Sputum culture revealed the presence of Klebsiella pneumoniae. Chest CT showed multiple lesions in the lower lobe of the right lung and the upper and lower lobes of the left lung, with diffuse pulmonary alveolar hemorrhage mainly located around the pulmonary hilum and bilateral ground glass opacities (Figure 1A–D). Nasopharynx MRI showed chronic inflammation of the bilateral maxillary sinuses and ethmoid sinuses. A kidney biopsy (4 months after the first chemotherapy) showed capillary loop necrosis, cell crescents, fibrocellular crescents, and fibrous crescents, and immunofluorescence was negative (Figure 2) (Supplementary Data 1).

The patient was diagnosed with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage related to AAV and bacterial pneumonia. He received treatments including blood transfusion, antibacterial therapy with piperacillin/tazobactam, and posterior nasal canal tamponade hemostasis. After infection control, methylprednisolone (0.25 g) for 3 days followed by a 40 mg intravenous drip was administered. Additionally, the patient was given cyclophosphamide (0.6 g/day) intravenously for two days. After 1 week of treatment, chest CT reexamination showed that the pulmonary infiltration lesions in both lungs of the patient had significantly decreased (Figure 1E–H). The anti-MPO decreased to 64.65 RU/ml, the anti-PR3 decreased to 49.38 RU/ml, and the PaO2 increased to 84 mmHg (without oxygen inhalation). The CT scan after 2 weeks showed that most of the lung lesions had been absorbed (Figure 11–L). The patient’s symptoms of nasal bleeding and hemoptysis disappeared, and BUN decreased to 19.9 mmol/L, while creatinine decreased to 335 µmol/L. Subsequently, the patient was administered oral prednisone at 40 mg/day, along with sulfamethoxazole to prevent Pneumocystis carinii infection. Prednisone was reduced by 4 mg every 2 weeks. After one month of treatment, the patient experienced fever and coughed up yellow sticky sputum again. The results of the laboratory tests revealed a WBC of 2.45*109/L, hemoglobin level of 83 g/L, neutrophil percentage of 95.1%, HS-CRP level of 244.92 mg/L, and PCT level of 66.99 ng/ml. Sputum culture revealed Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and chest CT showed pulmonary infection in both lungs. The patient was given meropenem and moxifloxacin for anti-infection treatment. The dose of prednisone was reduced to 16 mg/day and was decreased by 4 mg every week. After 6 weeks of treatment, renal function tests showed that the BUN was 20.75 mmol/L, creatinine decreased to 246 µmol/L, anti-MPO was 55.41 RU/ml and anti-PR3 was 30.79 RU/ml. However, the patient’s pulmonary infection remained severe, and bronchoalveolar lavage targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) examination revealed a sequence count of 573 for Legionella pneumophila, 2 for the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex group, 211 for Yeast-like Pneumocystis jirovecii, and 108 for Klebsiella pneumoniae. Although the tuberculosis culture and acid-fast staining were negative, tuberculosis infection was still considered, and the patient was given ethambutol hydrochloride 0.75 g/day, isoniazid 0.3 g/day, rifampicin 0.45 g/day, and moxifloxacin 0.4 g/day for anti-tuberculosis and anti-Legionella pneumophila treatment. Prednisone was reduced to 8 mg/day, and sulfamethoxazole was given at a dose of 0.96 g twice daily to prevent Pneumocystis carinii infection. However, the patient gave up on treatment and was discharged from the hospital and did not return for follow-up. The patient died at home after 3 months.

Malignant tumors can also lead to vasculitis, but the patient’s renal function and urine routine were normal before and after the resection of his malignant tumors, and vasculitis occurred after chemotherapy. Therefore, we believed that the vasculitis was not caused by malignant tumors but by drugs. In most cases of drug-induced AAV, patients exhibit MPO-ANCA, often in combination with antibodies to other neutrophil cytoplasmic proteins such as PR3 and human neutrophil elastase (HNE), as well as anti-nuclear antibodies. If a patient tests positive for both MPO and PR3-ANCA, it suggests that the disease may be drug-induced (Supplementary References 1, 2). After reviewing the patient’s medical history and medication, it is believed that the XELOX plus trastuzumab chemotherapy regimen may have triggered the development of ANCA-related vasculitis. However, it is challenging to pinpoint which specific drug within the regimen caused this reaction. Currently, there are no reports of AAV induced by oxaliplatin or capecitabine. However, multiple reports have indicated that oxaliplatin can cause cutaneous vasculitis [1,2] and leukocytoclastic vasculitis [3,4]. Capecitabine-induced leukocytoclastic vasculitis has also been reported [5,6]. There were also reports of vasculitis caused by trastuzumab [7–9]. However, there have been no reports of AAV causing damage to multiple systems. This is the first report of XELOX plus trastuzumab-induced ANCA-associated systemic vasculitis affecting the lungs, kidneys, and nasal mucosa.

Regrettably, a kidney injury had not been considered until there was a significant increase in the serum creatinine level. Then, a nephrology consultation was sought, resulting in a delay in diagnosis and treatment. This patient had rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and massive pulmonary alveolar hemorrhage, and glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapy were used. Therefore, after stopping XELOX plus trastuzumab chemotherapy, glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide were given, the patient’s pulmonary hemorrhage and nasal bleeding stopped, and the lung lesions were absorbed. Renal function also improved. Pulmonary infection is one of the most common complications of AAV and one of the main causes of death. High-dose glucocorticoids contribute to the risk of infection. The patient later experienced pulmonary infection again, and tNGS indicated Legionella pneumophila and pulmonary tuberculosis infection. Despite active anti-infection treatment, the steroid dose was rapidly reduced. Ultimately, the patient gave up on treatment and eventually died.
