Preventive effects of combined live Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Bacillus cereus tablets on radiation pneumonitis-associated with cyberknife treatment for pulmonary malignancies
Authors: Wei Wang, Yingchun Zhao, Ying Guo, Qian Wang, Lichao Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudTo investigate the preventive effects of combined live Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Bacillus cereus tablets (CBLEBTL) on patients with radiation pneumonitis associated with CyberKnife treatment for pulmonary malignancies.MethodsIn total, 130 patients who received CyberKnife treatment for pulmonary malignancies at the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University between August 2023 and February 2025 were enrolled. Patients were randomly categorized into the experimental group (n = 65) or control group (n = 65). While both groups received CyberKnife treatment for pulmonary lesions, the experimental group additionally received CBLEBTL (0.5 g/dose, thrice daily). Introduction

Pulmonary malignancies encompass primary lung cancer and associated secondary metastatic malignancies. Primary lung cancer ranks first in incidence among all cancers and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Radiotherapy is a crucial treatment modality for pulmonary malignancies. In comparison with conventional radiotherapy, CyberKnife treatment, a type of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), has been associated with improved local tumor control and enhanced protection to the surrounding critical organs [2]. Despite these advantages, adverse effects of CyberKnife treatment have been well documented. One such adverse event is radiation pneumonitis (RP) that involves inflammation of the lung tissue primarily resulting from radiation-induced disruption of the alveolar mucosal barrier. This condition often triggers a cascade of inflammatory events, causing persistent lung tissue damage and chronic injury to the lungs [3]. Studies thus far have reported that the incidence of symptomatic RP (grade 2 or higher) ranges from 10 to 30% [4]. RP can considerably restrict the radiation dose that can be safely delivered with CyberKnife treatment, eventually compromising the therapeutic benefits in terms of treatment efficacy. Research evidence has underscored an association between reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium in the neonatal gut and increased risk of respiratory inflammation [5]. The genus Enterococcus has been shown to improve the host’s resistance to radiation-induced damage through the production of short-chain fatty acids [6], and Lactobacillus has been reported to mitigate the toxic effects induced by radiations [7]. The bacterium Bacillus cereus consumes oxygen and creates an anaerobic environment that encourages the growth and proliferation of anaerobic bacteria such as Bifidobacterium [8]. Combined live Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Bacillus cereus tablets (CBLEBTL) comprises Bifidobacterium infantis, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Enterococcus faecium, and B. cereus [8]. To investigate the protective effects of these bacterial strains against RP, we performed a prospective randomized controlled clinical trial by recruiting patients with pulmonary malignancies. We observed and compared the incidence and onset time of RP, as well as changes in the quality of life between patients from the experimental and control groups.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included in the study if they met the following criteria: (1) 35 to 80 years of age; (2) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of primary lung cancer or secondary metastatic malignancy of the lung, with a planned CyberKnife treatment; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0 or 1; (4) granted consent to receive CBLEBTL adjunctive treatment and comply with follow-up requirements; (5) an expected survival of at least 6 months; (6) both patient and their family provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) concurrently receiving chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy; (2) those with chest computed tomography (CT) findings indicative of any pulmonary infection or interstitial lung fibrosis; (3) those with comorbidities such as thromboembolism, heart failure, or uncontrolled hypertension; (4) those simultaneously enrolled in other clinical trials.

General information

Sample size estimation was performed using PASS 15 software. By assuming a significance level (α) of 0.025 (one-sided test) and a power (β) of 0.2, the incidence rate of RP was set at 0.1 in the experimental group and 0.3 in the control group. The rate of loss to follow-up was assumed to be 0.1. As such, the required sample size was calculated to be 65 patients per group.

We applied the randomization method wherein each participant was assigned a number from 1 to 130. Then a random number was generated for each participant using SPSS 27 software. Patients were then allocated either to the experimental or control group depending on their corresponding random number.

A total of 130 patients who underwent SBRT for lung malignancies at the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University between August 2023 and February 2025 were enrolled in the study. Patients were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group, leading to 65 patients per group. Four patients from the experimental group were lost to follow-up (one died of pulmonary embolism, and three failed to adhere to the prescribed CBLEBTL medication). In addition, three patients from the control group were lost to follow-up (one died of acute myocardial infarction, and two had switched to other treatments owing to distant metastasis). Therefore, the final analysis was conducted on 123 patients who met the inclusion criteria.

The general demographic characteristics of the study patients from both groups are shown in Table 1 (located at the end of the document). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (all P-values > 0.05).

Treatment method

Patients from both groups received treatment with the CyberKnife system as per the American M6 model along with the treatment planning system for stereotactic radiotherapy. The dose ranged from 40 to 50 Gy that was administered over 3 to 5 sessions. The target area was carefully delineated through strict protection of surrounding vital organ tissues. One week prior to the CyberKnife treatment, the experimental group was orally administrated CBLEBTL (Hangzhou Yuanda Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval No. S20227006) at a dose of 1 tablet per session, thrice daily. This regimen was continued until 1 month following completion of CyberKnife treatment, after which the treatment was discontinued.

Each tablet of CBLEBTL weighed 0.5 g and contained not less than 1.5 × 106 colony-forming units (CFUs) of Bifidobacterium infantis, L. acidophilus, and Enterococcus faecalis, and not less than 1.5 × 105 CFU of B. cereus [8].

Observation time points and items

For each patient, signs and symptoms were monitored, quality of life was investigated, and imaging findings studied before and 1, 2, 4, and 6 months after CyberKnife treatment. The primary endpoint was to determine the incidence of RP, while the secondary endpoints included onset time of RP and ECOG PS score.

Assessments

Diagnosis and grading (grade 1–4) of RP was conducted as per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Grade 2 indicates symptoms such as cough, chest tightness, and fever that affect daily life and work, requiring treatment, with imaging showing 25%-50% involvement of lung parenchyma. The proportion of patients with symptomatic grade 2 or higher RP and onset time of RP were recorded at CyberKnife treatment and 1, 2, 4, and 6 months after treatment. In addition, their clinical status was scored depending on their ECOG PS score before and 1,2,4, and 6 months post CyberKnife treatment. Additionally, to evaluate the potential impact of antibiotic exposure (Abx exposure) on the study results, we retrospectively collected data on systemic antibiotic usage for all patients from the time of randomization until the diagnosis of RP (or until the last follow-up for patients who did not develop RP), and defined it as a binary variable (yes/no).

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. For continuous variable that were skewed, such as age and actual radiation dose, were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables such as gender, staging, pathological type, smoking history, ECOG PS, and incidence of RP were analyzed using χ2or Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Comparison of the incidence of RP

The incidence of RP was significantly lower in the experimental group (6/61 [9.8%]) than in the control group (16/62 [25.8%]) (χ2 = 5.340, P = 0.021).

Onset time of RP

The average onset time of RP after CyberKnife treatment was 176.9 days for the experimental group as compared with 166.6 days for the control group. Therefore, the onset of RP was delayed in the experimental group as compared with that in the control group (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.34, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.15–0.80, Fig. 1).

ECOG PS scores

There was no significant difference in the ECOG PS score between the two treatment groups prior to CyberKnife treatment (P > 0.05). However, the proportion of patients with ECOG PS score of 0 tended to increase with an increase in the follow-up time from 1 month to 6 months after CyberKnife treatment among patients from the experimental group. Conversely, the proportion of patients with ECOG PS score of 0 in the control group decreased over the same period. Furthermore, there was a significant difference in ECOG PS scores between the two patient groups (P < 0.05), which is indicative of improvement in the quality of life among patients from the experimental group (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

To further elucidate the relationship between CBLEBTL and RP, a series of subgroup analyses were performed. As shown in Fig. 2, the association between CBLEBTL and reduced incidence of RP was not altered in all subgroups, including age, gender, tumor stage, pathological type, smoking status, and actual radiation dose. In addition, during the observational period, antibiotics were used by 5 patients (8.2%) in the CBLEBTL group and 8 patients (12.9%) in the non-CBLEBTL group. These patients were categorized into antibiotic-exposed (AE) and non-antibiotic-exposed (NAE) subgroups for analysis. As shown in Fig. 2, the point estimates for the HR favored CBLEBTL in both the AE (HR = 0.914, 95%CI 0.225–3.717; p = 0.900) and NAE (HR = 0.282, 95%CI 0.076–1.050, p = 0.059) subgroups, although the results were not statistically significant. Notably, the point estimate of the treatment effect was more substantial in the NAE group. This observation suggests that antibiotic use might influence the ability of CBLEBTL to achieve its full preventive potential.

Discussion

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of treatment for lung cancer and other malignancies. Technological advancements in radiation therapy have progressed from three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) to intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), and proton beam therapy [9]. CyberKnife treatment, popularly known as stereotactic radiosurgery, is considered safe and effective for treatment of lung malignancies and lung metastases. With a high local control rate, this treatment modality has shown a 2-year local control rate of approximately 90% [10]. RP is a known side-effect frequently reported among patients undergoing chest CyberKnife treatment. Symptomatic RA typically occurs after 3 to 6 months of CyberKnife treatment, although in some cases it may manifest years later [11]. The mechanism underlying RP primarily involves series of inflammatory responses manifested through an increase in the oxidative stress, release of cytokines, and activation of chemokines [9]. The occurrence of RP limits the radiation dose for some patients, thus hindering tumor control.

The human gut microbiome has been the focus of scientific interest in recent years. It has been well established that the gut microbiome plays a critical role in the respiratory system [12] through its effects via the gut-lung axis [13] wherein the beneficial bacteria and their metabolites secreted in the intestinal submucosal layer or mesenteric lymph nodes bypass the mesenteric lymphatic system into the lacteals to enter the pulmonary circulation. This phenomenon results in the activation of lung macrophages and T cells [14]. Hence, the lung-gut axis is thought to shape the immune response in the lung tissue by interfering with the progression of respiratory diseases. In their study, Zhang et al. [15] reported the production of short-chain fatty acids such as acetate by Bifidobacterium through the fermentation of dietary fibers. Acetate was found to inhibit the activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome in a mouse model of bronchopulmonary dysplasia and exhibit protective functions against lung injury. Another research study found that Enterococcaceae promoted hematopoiesis through the production of short-chain fatty acids, particularly propionate, as well as specific tryptophan metabolites, which improved resistance to radiation-induced damage and death [16]. Riehl et al. [17] reported that L. reuteri can induce the expression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells prior to irradiation, thereby promoting their migration and consequently escalating the process of wound repair. This phenomenon in turn can contribute to the protection of the mucosa from radiation-induced toxicity. Clostridium perfringens was shown to produce short-chain fatty acids that induced the expression of specific lipid factors known for their radioprotective properties and consequently alleviating radiation-induced lung injury [6].

CBLEBTL is a mixture of Bifidobacterium infantis, L. acidophilus, Enterococcus faecalis, and B. cereus [8]. Herein, CBLEBTL was designated as a factor influencing study outcomes. We show that the incidence of RP was significantly lower in the group treated with CBLEBTL than in the control group of patients who did not receive this treatment. Further, the onset of RP was also significantly delayed among patients receiving this treatment than in those from the control group. Considering the ECOG PS scores, CBLEBTL clearly improved the physical condition of patients and ameliorated their quality of life. Additional subgroup analyses confirmed that the positive effects of CBLEBTL on reduced incidence of RP were not influenced by factors such as age, sex, and disease stage. Therefore, CBLEBTL can serve as a potential therapeutic agent capable of preventing RP associated with radiotherapy among patients with lung malignancies. Our findings provide a strong basis for the application of this therapy for the prevention and treatment of radiation-induced pneumonia.

To further elucidate the treatment effects, we specifically analyzed the impact of antibiotic use. Our analysis revealed that CBLEBTL maintained a beneficial trend regardless of Abx exposure. This finding suggests that antibiotic use did not negate the positive effects of CBLEBTL under our study conditions, thereby strengthening the reliability of our primary conclusions. Furthermore, the more pronounced benefit observed in patients without Abx exposure indicates that while antibiotics do not completely abolish CBLEBTL’s efficacy, avoiding their use may optimize treatment outcomes. These findings hold implications for clinical practice and underscore the necessity of detailed antibiotic documentation in future research.

Several limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. First, the retrospective trial registration, initiated after patient enrollment began, deviates from international standards for prospective registration, affecting the study’s transparency. Second, the single-center design with a limited sample size constrains the generalizability of our findings. Third, the 6-month follow-up period may be insufficient to capture all late-onset RP cases. Fourth, the absence of mechanistic data on gut microbiota, microbial metabolites, or inflammatory biomarkers precludes direct biological verification of CBLEBTL’s mechanism of action. Fifth, although the Bacillus cereus strain in CBLEBTL was a non-toxigenic probiotic and no related adverse events were observed, its long-term safety requires continued monitoring in broader populations, given the safety concerns associated with some pathogenic strains of the same species. Sixth, the lack of blinding and placebo control may have introduced potential assessment bias. Finally, the retrospective nature of the antibiotic usage data limited detailed analyses of antibiotic types and treatment duration, which is a limitation that should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

This study provides clear directions for future research. Subsequent work should comprise prospectively registered, multicenter, large-scale, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trials with extended follow-up periods and detailed documentation of antibiotic use. Future studies should incorporate the collection of biospecimens (e.g., blood and stool) to directly analyze microbial, inflammatory, and immune markers, thereby elucidating CBLEBTL’s mechanism of action. Such comprehensive studies are needed to generate robust evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of CBLEBTL, ultimately guiding its clinical application.
