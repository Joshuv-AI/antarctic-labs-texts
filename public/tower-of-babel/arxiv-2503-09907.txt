PLRD: Partially Linear Regression Discontinuity Inference
Aditya Ghosh, Guido Imbens, Stefan Wager

Abstract
Regression discontinuity designs have become one of the most popular research designs in empirical economics. We argue, however, that the widely used approaches to building confidence intervals in regression discontinuity designs often exhibit suboptimal behavior in practice. We propose a new estimator, the partially linear regression discontinuity (PLRD) estimator that, in set of a simulation studies carefully calibrated to twelve high-profile applications of regression discontinuity designs, has substantially lower estimation error than available comparison methods. Throughout our experiments, the confidence intervals built using PLRD are both valid and short. We also provide large-sample guarantees for PLRD. Our simulation study serves as a general template for how new econometric methods can be credibly evaluated relative to the existing alternatives by constructing simulation designs that generate synthetic data indistinguishable from the original data using the Wasserstein generative adversarial network methodology.

These semi-synthetic simulation findings leave open a pragmatic question: Is it possible to design automated confidence intervals that reliably achieve nominal coverage
in realistic settings, while remaining competitive with the conventional intervals in terms of interval width? We emphasize here the “realistic” qualifier. Even if it is not feasible to achieve uniformly better properties, it may be that most real-world applications are sufficiently smooth to allow for nominal coverage and narrower confidence intervals than the existing methods.

The main contribution of this paper is a method, partially linear regression discontinuity
inference (PLRD), that provides an affirmative answer to this question—at least in the context
of the numerical settings we consider. As described further below, algorithmically, the PLRD estimator
adapts
RDHonest

(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
to accommodate a partially linear treatment effect
model; conceptually, however, our full approach fits within an asymptotic econometric
framework as used for
rdrobust

(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
. We implement our method in the package
plrd
for

R

(
R Core Team, 2023
)
, which provides end-to-end functionality for regression discontinuity inference
without relying on the user to specify hard-to-choose tuning parameters. As illustrated in
Tables

1
and
3
, our proposed
plrd
method achieves nominal and near-exact coverage throughout. It also has the most accurate point estimates across all settings considered here (
Figure

2
).

We emphasize that our semi-synthetic simulation setup, central to argument, comes with two qualifications. First, it relies on the availability of datasets where regression discontinuity methods are applicable. We apply it to twelve such data sets, but we encourage researchers to apply it to additional ones. Second, it relies on our generative adversarial nets yielding realistic data generating processes given the provided datasets. Well-known results in non-parametric inference imply that, fixing a sample size and given any dataset where standard regression-discontinuity estimators work well, one can construct another adversarial dataset that is statistically indistinguishable from the first but where the same estimators fail to get coverage
(
Low, 1997
)
. Our approach to evaluation implicitly relies on an assumption that the real datasets we are calibrated to aren’t adversarial in this sense.
Again, we encourage researchers to explore the sensitivity of our results to other generative models that are tied to existing data sets.
Finally, our estimation process involves some choices of tuning parameters for which different researchers may make different choices; we seek to mitigate concerns of researcher bias by using a single set of tuning parameters across all datasets.

The PLRD Estimator

Consider the sharp RDD formalized using
potential outcomes
(
Imbens and Lemieux, 2008
)
. For each unit
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
, we
observe IID-sampled pairs
(X_{i},\,Y_{i})
, where
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the running variable and

Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the outcome of interest. Following the sharp discontinuity design, we assume
there exists a cutoff
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that treatment is assigned as
W_{i}=\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{X_{i}\geq c\right\}}
.
We posit potential outcomes
\{Y_{i}(0),\,Y_{i}(1)\}
such that
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(W_{i})
, and our
goal is to estimate the conditional average treatment effect at the cutoff,

\tau_{c}:=\mu_{1}(c)-\mu_{0}(c)
where
\mu_{w}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w)|X_{i}=x]
.

It is well known that, provided the
\mu_{w}(x)
are continuous in
x
and that the
running variable has continuous support around
c
, our target
\tau_{c}
is identified
as a discontinuity in the conditional response surface at
X_{i}=c

(
Hahn et al., 2001
)
:

(1)

\tau_{c}=\lim_{x\downarrow c}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i}=x]-\lim_{x\uparrow c}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i}=x].

In order to obtain practical estimation or inference results for
\tau_{c}
, though, one
needs to make further regularity assumptions on the
\mu_{w}(x)
; our PLRD method relies
on the following two assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

\mu_{w}(x)
are 3 times continuously differentiable in
x
.

Assumption 2
.

The conditional average treatment effect function
\tau(x)=\mu_{1}(x)-\mu_{0}(x)

has a linear dependence in
x
, i.e.,
\tau(x)=\tau_{c}+\beta(x-c)
for some

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assumption

1
is a standard smoothness assumption; this is exactly the
same as the main smoothness assumption used by
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
to justify

rdrobust
.
Assumption

2
implies that the treatment effect
has a simpler dependence on the running variable than the baseline effect
\mu_{0}(x)
.
This assumption is well in line with assumptions often made in the literature on
heterogeneous treatment effect estimation
(e.g.,
Wager, 2024
, Chapter 4)
;
however, it has not typically been used in the RDD literature to date, which is why
we emphasize this assumption in naming our method. Given these two assumptions, PLRD
proceeds as follows:

1.

Under
Assumption

1
there exists a finite constant
B

such that the second derivatives
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{w}(x)
are
B
-Lipschitz in a neighborhood
of
c
; and it is possible to identify such a
B
from data in the large-sample limit.
PLRD starts by estimating

B
via a cubic regression
(
Imbens and Kalyanaraman, 2012
;
Calonico et al., 2014
)
.

\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}(c)=0
, our cubic polynomial estimator
\hat{B}
(in step 6 of
Algorithm

1
)
converges to 0, but the exact way it does so is left underspecified under
Assumption

1
.
Our use of the lower bound
\varepsilon
guarantees validity of our confidence intervals at
the expense of some excess conservativeness when
\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}(c)=0
. We note that existing
methods, including
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
and
(
Imbens and Kalyanaraman, 2012
)
, also do not
offer guidance on how to potentially benefit from “superefficiency” in
similar critical cases; i.e., our approach is again in line with current best practices.

2.1
Discrete running variables

Discrete running variables arise frequently in regression discontinuity applications.
Our formal results are stated under the assumption that the running variable has a continuous density bounded away from zero near the cutoff, which does not accommodate the discrete case. In particular, when the running variable is discrete,
Step
1
is no longer guaranteed to consistently recover
B
under
Assumption

1
alone, and thus
Theorem

1
does not provide a formal guarantee for the discrete case. However,
Steps
2
and
3
of our procedure remain valid, i.e., given an appropriate
B
they yield partial identification intervals for
\tau_{c}
with a guarantee of the type (
7
) conditional on that bound
(
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
;
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
.
Thus, PLRD is robust to discrete running variables in the following sense: The interval logic (
7
) is sound as long as the practitioner is willing to commit to a curvature bound—whether from the pilot regression or from background knowledge.

Table

1
illustrates the empirical performance of PLRD when the running variable is discrete: Five of our twelve benchmark cases—Matsudaira (math and reading), Jacob & Lefgren (math and reading), and Oreopoulos—use assignment rules based on test-scores, where the running variable takes values on a finite grid. This suggests that the pilot-based curvature estimate continues to behave reasonably even in the case of discrete running variables, where one would need further assumptions to formally justify the data-driven detection of
B
in
Algorithm

.

3
Relationship to Existing Methods

Regression discontinuity designs were originally introduced by
(
Thistlethwaite and Campbell, 1960
)
.
The modern econometric literature goes back to
(
Hahn et al., 2001
)
, who emphasized the identification
result (
1
) and recommended estimation
\tau_{c}
via local linear regression,

\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}
.
In the following decade, local linear
(or polynomial) regression developed into the prevalent approach to inference in RDDs, with confidence
intervals produced by applying off-the-shelf tools for heteroskedasticity-robust inference directly to the
regression
(
Imbens and Lemieux, 2008
;
Lee and Lemieux, 2010
)
.

A limitation of this approach, however,
is that it leads to contradictory guidance on the bandwidth choice under a non-parametric
specification as given in,
e.g.
,
Assumption

1
. The accuracy of

\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}
depends delicately on the bandwidth
h_{n}
, with a first systematic proposal given in
(
Imbens and Kalyanaraman, 2012
)
.
For choices of
h_{n}
that optimize this accuracy, the bias and
standard error of
\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}
are of the same order, so off-the-shelf
local linear regression inference will not have valid coverage for this choice of
h_{n}
.
The practitioner is thus forced to invoke “undersmoothing”, whereby they voluntarily
use a non-accuracy-optimizing choice of
h_{n}
to justify validity of their inferential
procedure.

In a major advance,
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
and

(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
introduced paradigms for inference in RDDs that formally
account for the bias of local linear regression.
These methods offer material improvements in the credibility of regression-discontinuity
analyses over the earlier practice
(
Stommes et al., 2023
)
. The main idea in
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
is to
estimate and correct for the bias of local linear regression by leveraging higher-order smoothness;
we refer to this approach as the ‘‘bias-corrected’’ approach;
11
11

11

When
reporting results using the bias-correct approach, we use robust standard error
estimates as recommended by
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
and as implemented in the package

rdrobust

(
Calonico et al., 2015
;
Calonico et al., 2017
)
.

this approach as further been extended in a number of directions
(
Calonico et al., 2019
;
Cattaneo and Titiunik, 2022
)
.
In contrast, the “bias-aware” approach developed by

(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
involves conservatively widening
intervals to account for worst-case bias (without needing to actually estimate the bias);
this approach has also been recently developed further

(
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
;
Imbens and Wager, 2019
;
Noack and Rothe, 2024
)
.

Interestingly, not only do the above papers under the bias-corrected vs. bias-aware paradigms
explore different methodological strategies for inference in RDDs; they also focus on proving
formal results under different formal paradigms.
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
and follow-ups focus
entirely on asymptotic validity results—as we do here. They fix a data-generating process, and
guarantee that they eventually get coverage for this data-generating process as the sample
size gets large (as is also done in (
7
)). In contrast, most papers written
under the bias-aware paradigm specify a quantitative smoothness class for the
\mu_{w}(x)

(
e.g.
, all functions with a second derivative bounded by a pre-specified constant
B
), and
then seek guarantees that hold in finite samples and uniformly over this whole class.
Getting uniform guarantees in finite samples of course requires stronger assumptions
than getting pointwise asymptotic guarantees; and for this reason papers associated with the
bias-aware paradigm often start by making stronger assumptions than those associated with the
bias-corrected paradigm. We emphasize, however, that this is due to the sought formal
guarantees; and it’s not that bias-aware methods inherently require stronger assumptions to work
than bias-corrected methods.

Our proposed method, PLRD, straddles these two literatures. Our confidence intervals
are rooted in the bias-aware paradigm
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
, and
we use numerical convex optimization to construct our estimator
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
.
In contrast, our formal results are focused on pointwise asymptotics, and
the assumptions and guarantee type given in our
Theorem

1
mirror
those in Theorem 1 of
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
. One notable paper that also straddles
these literatures is
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2020
)
, who propose bias-aware confidence intervals
for local linear regression with pointwise asymptotic guarantees. In particular, they show
that if one runs local linear regression with a mean-square-error
optimal bandwidth choice, then under generic
conditions we can build valid, bias-aware 95% confidence intervals for
\tau_{c}
as

\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}\pm 2.18\times\text{standard errors}
rather than the conventional

\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}\pm 1.96\times\text{standard errors}
. We provide a comparison of
PLRD to this approach in the Supplementary Material.

Wrapping up, PLRD can best be understood within the context of earlier developments in
the bias-aware paradigm; and in fact
Steps (
2
)
and
(
3
)
of our algorithmic outline fall
squarely within the roadmap for minimax linear inference via numerical convex optimization
as in
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
. However, in a deviation from existing work:

•

We rely on higher-order smoothness and a preliminary cubic fit to initialize
tuning parameters of our algorithm; in
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
,
(
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
)
and
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
, a parameter analogous to the
B
derived in our
Step (
1
)
would
need to be pre-specified using subject matter knowledge or chosen using a heuristic.

•

Because of this data-driven choice of
B
in
Step (
1
)
, we are not able to provide
uniform, finite-sample inferential guarantees;
12
12

12

As shown in
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
,
doing so would in fact be impossible under smoothness assumptions like
Assumption

1
alone.
We do note that proving uniform bounds for methods involving data-driven smoothness guarantees
can be possible under more restricted function classes, e.g.,
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2020
, Appendix E)

consider such results over function classes where global polynomial approximations are guaranteed
to capture worst-case local curvature of the underlying functions. We leave a study
of uniformity guarantees for PLRD over restricted function classes to future work.

thus, like in
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
,
we focus on pointwise asymptotic inferential statements.

•

The specific worst-case class we use in our
Step (
2
)
, i.e., with
B
-Lipschitz
curvature bounds and a linear treatment effect assumption, has not been previously
considered in the literature. The most standard choice in applications of

(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
,
(
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
)
and/or
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)

has been to only assume that the
\mu_{w}(x)
have second derivatives bounded by
M
.

Regarding this last point, the earlier focus on the
M
-bounded curvature class appears
to have been largely guided by intuitive simplicity of this class rather than any
conceptual or empirical arguments. Given the empirical performance of PLRD on calibrated
simulations, we suggest that our
B
-Lipschitz curvature class with linear treatment effects
may be a good default choice in many applications.

To further examine the effect of different choices on the behavior of our estimator, we conduct an ablation analysis, reported in the Supplementary Material,
comparing four alternatives to PLRD: Bias-aware intervals for local linear regression
with the inflated critical value of
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2020
)
; the analogous construction for local quadratic regression; PLRD with
Assumption

2
removed while retaining the
B
-Lipschitz curvature structure (so that separate curvature bounds are allowed on each side of the threshold); and PLRD with
Assumption

1
relaxed to second-order smoothness, which essentially recovers the optrdd construction of
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
augmented with
Assumption

2
. The ablation analysis empirically demonstrates that the efficiency of PLRD is a joint consequence of both
Assumptions

2
and
1
.

A fundamental challenge in assessing methods for causal inference on real-world data is that we
typically do not have access to a ground truth we can use for evaluation. In light of this
challenge,
(
Athey et al., 2024
)
proposed using Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Networks (WGANs)
to generate synthetic data that mimic benchmark datasets, such as to obtain simulation instances
that enable comparing methods in realistic settings.
Our WGAN evaluation pipeline involves the following three steps:

1.

Training:
We train WGANs on each benchmark dataset to mimic the underlying data generating process
for both treated and control units.

2.

Generation:
Once trained, the WGANs generate multiple synthetic datasets that reflect the characteristics of the original data.

3.

Evaluation:
We apply the various methods, including our proposed
PLRD
method, to the synthetic datasets. The performance of these methods is then evaluated based on the empirical coverage and the average width of the confidence intervals.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, a trained WGAN is able to simulate data whose
distribution is very similar to that of the original data; thus suggesting that if a method
gets reliable coverage on the simulator we can also trust it on the real data.

Our WGAN training procedure consists of the following steps. First, apply marginal CDF transforms to the data to convert
(X_{i},Y_{i})
to
(U_{i},V_{i})
, where
U_{i}=F_{x}(X_{i})
and
V_{i}=F_{y}(Y_{i})
. For discrete data we break ties by randomization, and for additional stability we use a Gaussian transformation. We train two generators
\widehat{g}_{w}(u,z)
(
w=0,1
), one for treated units and one for controls, that mimic the conditional distribution of the CDF-transformed outcome
V
given the CDF-transformed running variable
U
. Finally, we invert the CDF-transforms to obtain
(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})
from the pair
(\widetilde{U}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{i})
generated by the trained WGAN.

We calculate the ground truth
\widetilde{\tau}
for the GAN estimated distribution as follows. We first calculate the cutoff
c^{\prime}
under the applied CDF transforms. We then sample random noise
Z_{0,q},Z_{1,q}\in\mathbb{R}
for
q=1,\dots,Q
, where
Q
is a large number (
10^{7}
in our simulations), generate CDF-transformed outcomes at the threshold
c^{\prime}
, and invert them:

\widetilde{\tau}:=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\left(F_{y}^{-1}(\widehat{g}_{1}(c^{\prime},Z_{1,q}))-F_{y}^{-1}(\widehat{g}_{0}(c^{\prime},Z_{0,q}))\right).

We generate
N=1,000
simulated datasets indexed by
i=1,\dots,N
, and compute confidence intervals
[a_{i,k},b_{i,k}]
, for each method
k
. The empirical coverage for the
k

\widehat{p}_{k}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{a_{i,k}\leq\widetilde{\tau}\leq b_{i,k}\}
.

6
Extensions

It is often of interest to incorporate auxiliary covariates that are predictive of the outcome but unrelated to the treatment assignment. One approach to doing so is by augmenting (
8
) with covariate-balance conditions as linear constraints
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
, provided that exact balance is feasible. Another approach is to fit a flexible predictive model and replace the outcomes with the residuals from this fit
(
Noack et al., 2026a
)
.
Investigation of these approaches is left for future work.

Extending PLRD to fuzzy RDDs (see
(
Imbens and Lemieux, 2008
)
for a review) requires an analogue of
Assumption

2
for the local average treatment effect. A linear shape restriction on this imposes a joint constraint on the numerator and the denominator, and is less transparent than in the sharp-design. An alternative formulation would instead impose partial linearity on the intent-to-treat function and treat the first-stage discontinuity as a separate object subject to its own smoothness constraint, similar in spirit to
(
Noack and Rothe, 2024
)
.

Adapting PLRD to clustered data requires replacing the heteroskedasticity-robust sandwich variance in the last step of
Algorithm

1
with a cluster-robust analogue, and optimizing the minimax weights to account for within-cluster correlation in the objective
(
Noack et al., 2026b
)
.

Appendix B
Safeguard against Curvature Changes

While
Assumption

2
provides a parsimonious and empirically realistic model for treatment effect heterogeneity in many applications, it can in principle be violated. To safeguard against such violations, our software incorporates an automatic diagnostic that tests for difference in curvatures.
The diagnostic works as follows. Before running the main PLRD procedure, we conduct an ANOVA test comparing a restricted cubic polynomial model (where treatment interacts only linearly with
X_{i}-c
, consistent with
Assumption

2
) against an unrestricted model (where treatment interacts with all polynomial terms in
X_{i}-c
). If the test rejects at significance level
\alpha^{\prime}
(default 0.1%), the method proceeds with a more flexible specification that only assumes
Assumption

1
but not
Assumption

2
. Otherwise, it uses the default specification under both assumptions. This adaptive approach helps explain the strong empirical performance of
plrd
across all simulation settings in
Table

3
, including those where
Assumption

2
fails.

When the flexible specification is selected, estimation of the Lipschitz constant
B
changes to accommodate potentially different curvature on either side of the threshold. Rather than fitting a single cubic polynomial as in (
9
), we fit
separate
cubic regressions for treated and control units:

Proposition 6

(Consistent estimation of the Lipschitz constant
B
under different curvatures)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds and that
X_{i}
has a density bounded away
from 0 in a neighborhood of
c
, and
B:=\max\{|\mu_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}(c)|,|\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(c)|,\varepsilon\}
. Estimate
\widehat{B}
via local cubic regression: For
w\in\{0,1\}
run

(24)

\widehat{\beta}_{w}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\beta_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W_{i}=w\\
|X_{i}-c|\leq\ell\end{subarray}}\left(Y_{i}-\beta_{w,0}-\beta_{w,1}(X_{i}-c)-\frac{\beta_{w,2}}{2}(X_{i}-c)^{2}-\frac{\beta_{w,3}}{6}(X_{i}-c)^{3}\right)^{2},

with bandwidth

scaling as in (
6
), and set

\widehat{B}=\max\{|\beta_{0,3}|,|\beta_{1,3}|,\varepsilon\}
.
Then,
\widehat{B}\rightarrow_{p}B
as

.

With this estimated

adjusts the constraint set to allow for different curvature functions

and

on either side of the cutoff (see (

Bias-Corrected Confidence Intervals

Define the local polynomial estimator of order
p
as

\widehat{\tau}_{p}(h_{n}):=\widehat{\mu}_{1,\,p}(h_{n})-\widehat{\mu}_{0,\,p}(h_{n}),

where
\widehat{\mu}_{1,\,p}(h_{n})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0,\,p}(h_{n})
are the intercepts from local polynomial regressions of order
p
fitted separately on each side of the threshold. More precisely, for
w\in\{0,1\}
, compute

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{w,\,p}(h_{n})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\prime}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{w,\,p}(h_{n}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{w,\,p}(h_{n})

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}}\sum_{i\,:\,W_{i}=w}K(|X_{i}-c|/h_{n})\left(Y_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{p}(X_{i}-c)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{2},

where
\mathbf{e}_{0}=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
is the first unit vector,
\mathbf{r}_{p}(x)=\left(1,x,\ldots,x^{p}\right)^{\top}
,
K(\cdot)
is a kernel, and
h_{n}
is a bandwidth. The first-order bias of
\widehat{\tau}_{p}(h_{n})
is
(
Calonico et al., 2014
;
Imbens and Kalyanaraman, 2012
)

B_{p}(h_{n})=\frac{h^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}\left[\mu_{1}^{(p+1)}(c+)-\mu_{0}^{(p+1)}(c-)\right],

where
\mu_{w}^{(p+1)}
denotes the derivative of
\mu_{w}(\cdot)
of order
p+1
.
Calonico et al. (2014)
estimate the above bias using a higher-order local polynomial of order
q>p
with a separate pilot bandwidth
b_{n}
, thus compute the bias-corrected estimator as

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{bc}}_{p,q}(h_{n},b_{n}):=\widehat{\tau}_{p}(h_{n})-h_{n}^{p+1}\widehat{B}_{n,p,q}(h_{n},b_{n}),

where
\widehat{B}_{n,p,q}(h_{n},b_{n})
is the estimated bias term
B_{p}(h_{n})
. The confidence interval they construct based on the bias-corrected estimator takes the following form:

(25)

\widehat{\text{CI}}_{1-\alpha}^{\text{bc}}(h_{n},b_{n})=\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{bc}}_{p,q}(h_{n},b_{n})\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\sigma}(h_{n})\right),

where
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the critical value from the standard normal distribution, and
\widehat{\sigma}(h_{n})
is the standard error (for which
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
provide two options: plug-in and fixed matches). The above CI construction is implemented in the
rdrobust
package, with
p=1
and
q=2
as default.

The CI constructed in (
25
) might not perform well in finite samples because the estimator of the bias introduces additional variability. To address this,
(
Calonico et al., 2014
)
also propose an alternative, more robust CI construction using the bias-corrected estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{bc}}_{p,q}(h_{n},b_{n})
as a starting point. They construct this robust CI by inflating the CI in (
25
) with a more conservative estimator of the standard error:

(26)

\widehat{\text{CI}}_{1-\alpha}^{\text{rbc}}(h_{n},b_{n})=\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{bc}}_{p,q}(h_{n},b_{n})\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}^{\text{bc}}_{n,p,q}}\right),

where
\widehat{V}^{\text{bc}}_{n,p,q}
is an estimator of the asymptotic variance that accounts for both the variability in estimation and the bias-correction. In
Tables

1
,
2
and
3
, the columns marked as
rdrobut
correspond to the confidence intervals computed using (
26
).

C.2
Bias-Aware Confidence Intervals

The bias-aware inference approach
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
;
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2020
;
Imbens and Wager, 2019
, e.g.,)
provides a different approach for obtaining confidence intervals for
\tau_{c}
. Note that the LLR estimator takes the form
\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,\texttt{LLR}}Y_{i}
for some weights
\gamma_{i,\texttt{LLR}}
that are functions of the running variable. In fact, the same also holds more generally for the estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{p}(h_{n})
defined in
Section

C.1
. Next, one makes the observation that although
\widehat{\tau}_{\texttt{LLR}}
is motivated by
a regression problem, the rest of our inference procedure hinges upon on properties that hold for any linear estimators of the form
\widehat{\tau}(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}Y_{i}
. Given by this observation, we can systematically do better by directly finding weights
\gamma_{i}
that minimize the mean squared error (MSE) of the estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\gamma)
uniformly over a class
\mathcal{M}
of conditional response functions
\mu_{w}
, i.e.,

(27)

\widehat{\gamma}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\gamma}\sup_{\mu_{w}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}}\text{MSE}\left(\widehat{\tau}(\gamma)\mid\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right).

The two most widely considered classes to date
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
;
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
;
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
are

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\text{T2},\,M}=\left\{\mu_{w}(\cdot):|\mu_{w}(x)-\mu_{w}(c)-\mu_{w}^{\prime}(c)x|\leq M(x-c)^{2}/2\text{ for every }x,\text{ and }w=0,1\right\},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\text{H2},\,M}=\left\{\mu_{w}(\cdot):\mu^{\prime}_{w}(x)\text{ is $M$-Lipschitz for all $x$ and }w=0,1\right\},

where
M>0
is a constant. The first option, considered in early work by
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
,
justifies accuracy of a 2nd-order Taylor expansion at
c
and allows for explicit, closed-form solutions
to the minimax linear estimation problem. The second option, which simply takes all functions to belong
to a 2nd-order Hölder class, was used by
(
Kolesár and Rothe, 2018
)
and
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
and also
adopted for the
RDHonest
package by
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
.

Fixing a value of
M
, we numerically solve the optimization problem (
27
) and obtain an upper bound on the worst-case bias of the resulting estimator as a by-product. We then follow
(
Imbens and Manski, 2004
)
to construct a bias-aware confidence interval by inflating the Gaussian confidence interval accounting for the worst-case bias of
\tau(\widehat{\gamma})
under the function class
\mathcal{M}
. These bias-aware confidence intervals provide uniformly valid coverage in finite samples,
provided the function class
\mathcal{M}
and the distribution of
Y_{i}
given
X_{i}
is Gaussian
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2018
)
.
15
15

15

If the conditional distribution of
Y_{i}
is non-Gaussian, one can recover exact finite-sample results using the bias-aware approach by using finite-sample concentration results instead of Gaussian approximation.
In practice, however, smoothness constant
M
is typically unknown; both
(
Armstrong and Kolesár, 2020
)
and
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
suggest using some rule-of-thumb estimates.

C.3
The Choice of Function Class

One of the key insights our paper provides is that the choice of the function class
\mathcal{M}
in the minimax problem (
8
) plays a crucial role in determining the conservativeness (and thus the practical usefulness) of the resulting bias-aware method.
The function classes used in the existing bias-aware literature (
e.g.
, the classes
\mathcal{M}_{\text{T2},\,M}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\text{H2},\,M}
defined in (
28
) and (
29
) respectively) are much modest in terms of assumed smoothness as compared to the the bias-corrected methods used in practice (see,
e.g.
,
(
Calonico et al., 2014
, Theorem 1)
). Here, on the other hand, use the class of all functions
\mu_{w}(\cdot)
that satisfy
Assumption

2
and for which
\mu_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
is
\widehat{B}
-Lipschitz, where we obtain
\widehat{B}
via a global cubic regression and cross-fitting. While this might appear as a minor modification from a theoretical point of view, it materially affects the width of our intervals in practice, as we demonstrate in
Tables

1
,
2
and
3
.

To illustrate the difference between the conservativeness of our function class versus the function classes used by existing bias-aware approaches, we plot in
Figure

3
the regression functions corresponding to the worst-case bias obtained by our method and
\mathcal{M}_{\text{H2},\,M}
as used in
RDHonest
. This plot demonstrates that the classes
\mathcal{M}_{\text{H2},\,M}
can often be unreasonably broad, and include regression functions that are
ex ante
implausible or rarely occur in practice. This explains why the resulting bias-aware confidence intervals can be substantially more conservative than the bias-corrected ones (see,
e.g.
,
Tables

1
,
2
and
3
). The empirical success of our PLRD method that we demonstrate in
Section

4
is, in fact, rooted in this choice of the function class over which one computes the worst-case MSE.

Functions
\mu_{w}(\cdot)
with worst-case bias in the RDHonest and PLRD function classes. The top two plots use the
m_{\text{quad}}
example from
(
Imbens and Kalyanaraman, 2012
)
, while the bottom two plots use the data from
(
Lee, 2008
)
. In each plot, black lines show
\mu_{w}(\cdot)
functions with maximum (solid) or minimum (dotted) bias in the respective function class. This figure illustrates how the RDHonest function class may accommodate in-hindsight-unreasonable functions for several practical settings.

\sigma_{i}^{2}={\rm Var}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i})
to be the
conditional variance of the response
Y_{i}
given the running variable
X_{i}
, then the
problem below is exactly the minimax linear estimation problem.
When we run this the optimization as specified in
Algorithm
1
, we set
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
via cross-fitting, as
described in Section
2
.

(30)

\mathop{\rm{minimize}}_{\gamma}\left[\sup_{\mu_{w}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}_{B}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\mu_{W_{i}}(X_{i})-(\mu_{1}(c)-\mu_{0}(c))\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\gamma_{i}^{2}\right].

Here, we take
\mathcal{M}_{B}
of all conditional response functions
\mu_{w}(\cdot)
satisfying
Assumption

2
and such that
\mu_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)
is globally
B
-Lipschitz.
Since
\gamma_{i}
are measurable functions of
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the bias of the estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}Y_{i}
conditional on the running variable is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}Y_{i}\,\bigg|\,\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]-\tau_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\mu_{W_{i}}(X_{i})-\tau_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(W_{i}(\tau_{c}+\beta(X_{i}-c))+\mu_{0}(X_{i}))-\tau_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\left(\mu_{0}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(c)-\mu_{0}^{\prime}(c)(X_{i}-c)-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}^{\prime\prime}(c)(X_{i}-c)^{2}\right),

provided

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}W_{i}=1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}W_{i}(X_{i}-c)=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(1-W_{i})(X_{i}-c)=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(X_{i}-c)^{2}=0,

which are implicit constraints in our optimization procedure. Indeed, at values of
\gamma
for which these constraints are not all satisfied, we can construct
\mu_{w}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{M}_{B}
that forces the squared conditional bias to blow up to
\infty
. Thus, the solution
\widehat{\gamma}
to (
30
) must satisfy the constraints. Next, define

\rho(x):=\frac{1}{B}\left(\mu_{0}(x+c)-\mu_{0}(c)-\mu_{0}^{\prime}(c)x-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}^{\prime\prime}(c)x^{2}\right).

Note that for any
\mu_{w}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{M}_{B}
, the function
\rho(\cdot)
belongs to the function class

\mathcal{R}:=\{f:f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0,f^{\prime\prime}\text{ is }1\text{-Lipschitz}\}.

We can therefore rewrite the optimization problem (
30
) as:

\widehat{\gamma}:=\mathop{\rm{minimize}}_{\gamma}\left[B^{2}\left(\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\rho(X_{i}-c)\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\gamma_{i}^{2}\right].

Finally, we make the implicit constraints explicit and use an auxiliary variable
t
and rewrite the above optimization problem as:

\displaystyle(\widehat{\gamma},\widehat{t}\,):=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm{minimize}}_{\gamma,t}\left\{B^{2}t^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right\}

(31)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\ \sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\rho(X_{i}-c)\leq t,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}W_{i}=1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\left(1-W_{i}\right)=-1,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(X_{i}-c)=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(1-W_{i})(X_{i}-c)=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(X_{i}-c)^{2}=0.

An immediate consequence of this modified optimization problem is that we get an upper bound on the conditional bias of our PLRD estimator.
Next, we discuss how one can solve the above problem using convex duality. The dual problem is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm{maximize}}_{\rho(\cdot),\lambda}\inf_{\gamma,t}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+B^{2}t^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\lambda_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\rho(X_{i}-c)-t\right)+\lambda_{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}W_{i}-1\right)+\lambda_{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\left(1-W_{i}\right)+1\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left.+\lambda_{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}W_{i}(X_{i}-c)+\lambda_{5}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\left(1-W_{i}\right)(X_{i}-c)+\lambda_{6}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(X_{i}-c)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\lambda_{1}\geq 0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5},\lambda_{6}\in\mathbb{R},\text{and},\rho\in\mathcal{R}.

The inner minimization problem is simply a quadratic one, which we can explicitly solve to reduce the dual problem to the following.

(32)

\widehat{\theta}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta=(\widetilde{\rho},\lambda)\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{G^{2}(\theta;X_{i}-c,W_{i})}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{4nB^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}{n}\right),

where
\theta=(\widetilde{\rho},\lambda)
denotes the dual variable that varies in the set

(33)

\Theta:=\left\{(f,\lambda)\mid f:[-\ell,\ell]\to\mathbb{R},f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0,f^{\prime\prime}\text{ is $\lambda_{1}$-Lipschitz},\lambda\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{5}\right\},

and

G(\widetilde{\rho},\lambda;x,w):=\widetilde{\rho}(x)+\lambda_{2}w+\lambda_{3}(1-w)+\lambda_{4}wx+\lambda_{5}(1-w)x+\lambda_{6}x^{2}.

The function
\widetilde{\rho}(\cdot)
in the above formulation corresponds to the function
\lambda_{1}\rho(\cdot)
in the original problem. Thus, we can recover the optimal variables
(\widehat{\gamma},\widehat{t}\,)
for the primal problem (
31
) from the dual optimal variable
\widehat{\theta}
(i.e., solution of (
32
)), as follows.

\widehat{\gamma}_{i}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}G(\widehat{\theta};X_{i}-c,W_{i}),\quad\widehat{t}=\frac{1}{2B^{2}}\widehat{\lambda}_{1}.

Since our optimization problem differs from
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
)
essentially in the choice of the function class,
(
Imbens and Wager, 2019
, Proposition 2)
provides a basis for approximating (
32
) using a finite-dimensional quadratic program, in a way that the resulting discrete solutions converge uniformly to the continuous solution as the grid size becomes small. In our software implementation, we adopt this finite-dimensional approximation approach to compute the optimal weights using
quadprog
, a function for quadratic programming in
R

(
R Core Team, 2023
)
.

Supporting Lemmas

To set the stage, we recall the key concepts of entropy with and without bracketing for function classes, which will be central to the results presented in this section.
Consider a class of real-valued functions
\mathcal{F}
defined on a set
\mathcal{Z}
, equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|
. The
covering number

N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|)
is the minimum number of balls of radius
\varepsilon
needed to cover
\mathcal{F}
. The centers of the covering balls need not belong to
\mathcal{F}
, but they are required to have finite norms. The
entropy (without bracketing)
is defined as the logarithm of the covering number.
An
\varepsilon
-bracket is a pair of functions
(l,u)
such that
\|u-l\|<\varepsilon
and
l\leq f\leq u
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The
bracketing number

N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|)
is the minimum number of
\varepsilon
-brackets needed to cover
\mathcal{F}
. The upper and lower bounds
u
and
l
of the brackets need not belong to
\mathcal{F}
, but they are required to have finite norms. The
entropy with bracketing
is defined as the logarithm of the bracketing number.

Following
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Section 2.7)
, we consider the class of functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a bounded set
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
the derivatives of
f
up to order
k
are uniformly bounded, and

f^{(k)}
is Lipschitz continuous. We can define a norm on this class as follows:

\|f\|_{k+1}:=\max_{j\leq k}\sup_{x}|f^{(j)}(x)|+\sup_{x,y}\frac{|f^{(k)}(x)-f^{(k)}(y)|}{|x-y|},

where the last supremum is over all
x,y
in the interior of
\mathcal{Z}
with
x\neq y
. Finally, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{M}^{k}(\mathcal{Z})
the set of all continuous functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|f\|_{k}\leq M
. Throughout, we use
A\lesssim_{\eta}B
to denote that
A\leq CB
for some arbitrary constant
C>0
that can depend on
\eta
. For a vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{p})
we denote
\lambda_{-1}:=(\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{p})
. We use
\|x\|_{\infty}
to denote
\max_{i}|x_{i}|
when
x
is a vector, and
\sup_{t}|x(t)|
when
x
is a function.

Lemma 7
.

Consider the following class of functions:

\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k}=\{\rho\mid\rho:[-\ell,\ell]\to\mathbb{R},\ \rho(0)=\rho^{\prime}(0)=\cdots=\rho^{(k)}(0)=0,\ \rho^{(k)}\text{ is $B$-Lipschitz}\},

where
k\geq 0
is an integer and
0<\ell\leq 1
.
It holds that

\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\lesssim\ell(B/\varepsilon)^{1/(k+1)},\quad\text{and}\quad\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k},L_{r}(P))\lesssim\ell(B/\varepsilon)^{1/(k+1)},

for any
r\geq 1
and probability measure
P
on
\mathbb{R}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k}
is universally Donsker.

Proof of
Lemma

7
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k}
, the derivatives of
\rho
upto order
k
are all bounded by
B\ell
. Thus,
\|\rho\|_{k+1}\leq B(1+\ell)\leq 2B
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{C}^{k+1}_{2B}([-\ell,\ell]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq N(\varepsilon/2B,\mathcal{C}^{k+1}_{1}([-\ell,\ell]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}).

For any
\delta>0
, note that
N(\delta,[-\ell,\ell],|\cdot|)\lesssim\ell/\delta
. We can thus slightly tweak the proof of
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Theorem 2.7.1)
, with
\alpha=k+1
and
\lambda(\mathcal{X}_{1})=2\ell
(in their notation), to conclude that

\log N(\varepsilon/2B,\mathcal{C}^{k+1}_{1}([-\ell,\ell]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}))\lesssim\ell(\varepsilon/2B)^{-1/(k+1)},

as desired to show. This gives us the first entropy bound (without bracketing).

Next, we can adapt the proof technique of
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Corollary 2.7.2)
to deduce that the last display also yields the desired bound on entropy with bracketing. Finally, the bound on
\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k},L_{2}(P))
implies that
\mathcal{F}_{B,\,\ell,\,k}
is
P
-Donsker for any probability distribution
P
(see,
e.g.,

(
van der Vaart, 1998
, Theorem 19.5)
).

\square

For the rest of this section, we assume without loss of generality that the treatment threshold is at
c=0
.
Before stating the next few lemmas, we recall some notation. The empirical dual problem is given by

\widehat{\theta}_{n}(\sigma^{2},B)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta=({\rho},\lambda)\in\Theta(\ell)}\widehat{L}_{n}(\theta;B),\quad\widehat{L}_{n}(\theta;B):=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{G^{2}(\theta;X_{i},W_{i})}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{4nB^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}{n},

where

G(\theta;x,w)=G(\rho,\lambda;x,w)={\rho}(x)+\lambda_{2}w+\lambda_{3}(1-w)+\lambda_{4}wx+\lambda_{5}(1-w)x+\lambda_{6}x^{2}
, with
w=1(x>0)
,
and

\Theta(\ell):=\left\{(f,\lambda)\mid f:[-\ell,\ell]\to\mathbb{R},f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0,f^{\prime\prime}\text{ is $\lambda_{1}$-Lipschitz},\lambda\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{5}\right\}.

Define the dual problem at the population level as

\theta_{n}^{*}(\sigma^{2},B)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta(\ell)}L_{n}(\theta;\sigma^{2},B),\quad\text{where}\quad L_{n}(\theta;\sigma^{2},B):=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{2}(\theta;X,W)\right]+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{4nB^{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}{n}.

In
Algorithm

1
, we sample splitting to estimate the unknown Lipschitz constant
B
and the homoskedastic variance
\sigma^{2}={\rm Var}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i})
. However, for ease of exposition, we assume throughout this proof that an estimate
\widehat{B}
of
B
and an estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of
\sigma^{2}
is available independently of the data using which we solve the empirical dual problem. We define

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\widehat{\theta}_{n}(\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{B}),\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{n}^{*}=\theta_{n}^{*}(\widehat{\sigma}^{2},B).

Lemma 8
.

Define

\Theta_{n}(\ell,\delta,\varepsilon):=\{(\rho,\lambda)\mid(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta(\ell),\ |\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n,1}^{*}|\leq\delta\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\text{ and }\|\lambda_{-1}-\lambda^{*}_{n,-1}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}\},

where
\ell,\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
are fixed. Then, the following maximal inequality holds.

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\theta\,\in\,\Theta_{n}(\ell,\delta,\varepsilon)}|\widehat{L}_{n}(\theta;\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{B})-L_{n}(\theta;\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{B})|\lesssim(\lambda_{n,1}^{*})^{2}\frac{\ell^{6}}{\sqrt{n}}+\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{n}}+\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}\frac{\ell^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof of
Lemma

8
.

We begin by noting that

\left|\widehat{L}_{n}(\theta;\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{B})-L_{n}(\theta;\widehat{\sigma}^{2},\widehat{B})\right|=\frac{1}{4\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})G^{2}(\theta;X,W)|.

We prove a maximal inequality for the above RHS using tools from empirical process theory. Write
G(\rho,\lambda;x,w)={\rho}(x)+\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(x,w)
where
v(x,w):=(w,1-w,wx,(1-w)x,x^{2})^{\top}
, and note that
\|v(x,w)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{1+\ell+\ell^{2}}\leq 2
. To reduce notation, we write
\Theta_{n}=\Theta_{n}(\ell,\delta,\varepsilon)
for the rest of this proof. Using triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})G^{2}(\theta;X,W)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\rho^{2}(X)|+\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W))^{2}|

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\rho(X)\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W)|.

We will bound each of the above three quantities in order. First, note that for any
(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}
,
\rho^{(j)}(0)=0
for
j=0,1,2
, and
\rho^{\prime\prime}
is
B_{1}:=(1+\delta)\lambda_{n,1}^{*}
-Lipschitz. This gives
|\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)|=|\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)-\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq B_{1}\ell
. Moreover, a Taylor expansion of
{\rho}(x)
about
x=0
yields the following

(34)

|{\rho}(x)|=|\rho(x)-\rho(0)-\rho^{\prime}(0)x|=\frac{|\rho^{\prime\prime}(\xi)-\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)|}{|\xi-x|}\left|\xi-x\right|\frac{x^{2}}{2}\leq B_{1}\frac{\ell^{3}}{2}.

A similar argument gives
|\rho^{\prime}(x)|\leq B_{1}\ell^{2}
. Putting these all together, we can say that the function
r=\rho^{2}
satisfies
r(0)=0
,
r^{\prime}(0)=0
,
r^{\prime\prime}(x)=2\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)\rho(x)+2\rho^{\prime}(x)^{2}
, and thus

\|r^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\lesssim B_{1}^{2}\ell^{4}\lesssim(\lambda_{n,1}^{*})^{2}\ell^{4}.

Consequently, using the notation of
Lemma

7
, we can say that
\mathcal{A}:=\{\rho^{2}:(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}\text{ for some }\lambda\}
is a subset of the class
\mathcal{F}_{\ell,h,1}
with Lipschitz constant
B\lesssim(\lambda_{n,1}^{*})^{2}\ell^{4}
. Therefore,
Lemma

7
implies that

\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{A},L_{2}(P))\leq\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{\ell,h,1},L_{2}(P))\leq\ell(B/\varepsilon^{\prime})^{1/2}\lesssim\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1/2}.

Finally, note that (
34

F=(\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3})^{2}
works as an envelope to the class
\mathcal{A}
.
We can now apply
(
van der Vaart, 1998

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\rho^{2}(X)|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{r\in\mathcal{A}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})r|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\|F\|_{P,2}}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{A},L_{2}(P))}\,d\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{F}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1/4}\left(\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}\right)^{1/2}\,d\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}F^{3/4}F^{1/4}=(\lambda_{n,1}^{*})^{2}\frac{\ell^{6}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Next, we deal with

\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W))^{2}|=\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W))^{2}|,

where
\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{5}:\|v-\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|\leq\varepsilon\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|\}\subseteq[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{5}

Therefore for any fixed

\displaystyle|\langle\lambda,v(x,w))\rangle^{2}-\langle\widetilde{\lambda},v(x,w))\rangle^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq 8\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}|\langle\lambda,v(x,w))\rangle-\langle\widetilde{\lambda},v(x,w))\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 16\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}\|\lambda-\widetilde{\lambda}\|_{2},

(where

\displaystyle\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\Lambda_{n},L_{2}(P))

We can now apply
(
van der Vaart, 1998

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W))^{2}|

It only remains to prove a maximal inequality for the following supremum:

\sup_{(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}}|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\rho(X)\lambda_{-1}^{\top}v(X,W)|.

Next, we fix
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}:=\{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{N_{1}}\}

-cover for
\pi_{1}(\Theta_{n})=\{\rho:(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}\text{ for some }\lambda\}

,

\log N_{2}\leq 5\log\left(1+\frac{c\varepsilon\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|}{\varepsilon^{\prime}/4\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|\ell^{3}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}.

On the other hand,

7

\log N_{1}\lesssim(\varepsilon^{\prime}/8\|\lambda_{n,-1}\|_{2})^{-1/3}(2\lambda_{n,1}^{*})^{1/3}\ell=(\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2})^{1/3}\ell(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1/3}.

Now, for any
\theta=(\rho,\lambda)\in\Theta_{n}
, pick
\theta^{0}=(\rho^{0},\lambda^{0})\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle|\rho(x)\langle\lambda_{-1},v(x,w)\rangle-\rho^{0}(x)\langle\lambda_{-1}^{0},v(x,w)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\rho(x)|\cdot|\langle\lambda_{-1},v(x,w)\rangle-\langle\lambda_{-1}^{0},v(x,w)\rangle|+|\langle\lambda_{-1}^{0},v(x,w)\rangle|\cdot|\rho(x)-\rho^{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}\cdot\|\lambda_{-1}-\lambda_{-1}^{0}\|_{2}\|v(x,w)\|_{2}+4\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}\cdot|\rho(x)-\rho^{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{4\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\ell^{3}}\cdot 2+4\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Therefore, the class
\mathcal{B}
of functions

\displaystyle\log N(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{B},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq\log N_{1}N_{2}\lesssim(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1/3}(\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|_{2})^{1/3}\ell+(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}\lambda_{n,1}^{*}\|\lambda_{n,-1}^{*}\|\ell^{3}\varepsilon.

This also gives us a bound for the entropy with bracketing (cf.
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996

\widehat{b}=\widehat{B}\widehat{t}_{n}
, and thus the goal here is to show that
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\,2}/\widehat{V}_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
. Recall the population-level problem with externally fit nuisance parameters:

(\gamma^{*}_{n},t_{n}^{*}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\gamma,t}\left\{\widehat{B}^{2}t^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{2}(X)]\right\}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\rho(X)]\leq t.

It suffices to prove that
t_{n}^{*}/\sqrt{V_{n}^{*}}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
, where
V_{n}^{*}=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{n}^{*,2}(X)]/n
, since we have consistency (i.e., that
\widehat{t}_{n}/t^{*}_{n}\rightarrow_{p}1
and
\widehat{V}_{n}/V_{n}^{*}\rightarrow_{p}1
).
It follows from
Lemma

12
that

\widehat{B}^{2}t_{n}^{\,*,\,2}+V_{n}^{*}\leq_{p}a_{+}n^{-6/7}
for some
a_{+}\in(0,\infty)
, for all sufficiently large
n
. On the other hand, it follows from the minimax lower bound (see,
e.g.
,
(
Cheng et al., 1997
)
) that
\widehat{B}^{2}t_{n}^{\,*,\,2}+V_{n}^{*}\geq_{p}a_{-}n^{-6/7}
for some
0<a_{-}\leq a_{+}
for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, to claim that
t_{n}^{*}/\sqrt{V_{n}^{*}}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
, it suffices to show that there exists some
c>0
such that
V_{n}^{*}\geq_{p}cn^{-6/7}
for all sufficiently large
n
.

To prove this by contradiction, assume that for any
c>0
, we have
V_{n}^{*}\leq_{p}cn^{-6/7}
infinitely often. We examine the MSE of
\gamma_{n}^{*}
for the objective

R_{m}(\gamma):=B^{2}\left(\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{E}\gamma(X)\rho(X)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{2}(X)],

where
m=n/k
(assume without loss of generality that
n
is divisible by
k
). Note that

\displaystyle R_{m}(\gamma_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\widehat{B}^{2}\left(\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{E}\gamma_{n}^{*}(X)\rho(X)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{*,2}_{n}(X)]

\displaystyle=\widehat{B}^{2}(t_{n}^{*})^{2}+kV_{n}^{*}\leq_{p}(a_{+}+kc)k^{-6/7}m^{-6/7}.

Choose
k
large enough so that
a_{+}k^{-6/7}\leq a_{-}/2
, and then choose
c>0
small enough such that
ck^{1/7}<a_{-}/2
. This, along with the above display, implies that
R_{m}(\gamma_{m}^{*})\leq R_{m}(\gamma_{n}^{*})\leq_{p}a_{-}m^{-6/7}
, which contradicts the minimax lower bound (we choose
n
is large enough so that
m=n/k
satisfies the
minimax lower bound
\widehat{B}^{2}t_{m}^{\,*,\,2}+V_{m}^{*}\geq_{p}a_{-}m^{-6/7}
). This contradiction completes the proof.

\square

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that
Z\sim N(0,1)
. Then, for any
s
,
h
,
t
and
\delta>0
, we have

\inf_{|b|\,\leq\,t+\delta s}\mathbb{P}(|b+sZ|\leq h)\geq\inf_{|b|\,\leq\,t}\mathbb{P}(|b+sZ|\leq h)-\delta.

Proof of
Lemma

14
.

Fix any
s
,
h
,
t
and
\delta>0
. Denote

g(b)=\mathbb{P}(|b+sZ|\leq h)=\Phi\left(\frac{h-b}{s}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{-h-b}{s}\right).

Note that,

g^{\prime}(b)=-\frac{1}{s}\left(\phi\left(\frac{h-b}{s}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{-h-b}{s}\right)\right)\implies\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\phi(0)}{s}\leq\frac{1}{s}.

Next, fix any
b_{1}
with
|b_{1}|\leq t+\delta s
. There exists
b_{0}
such that
|b_{0}|\leq t
and
|b_{1}-b_{0}|\leq\delta s
. Consequently,
|g(b_{1})-g(b_{0})|\leq\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}|b_{1}-b_{0}|\leq\delta
. This implies that
g(b_{1})\geq g(b_{0})-\delta\geq\inf_{|b|\leq t}g(b)-\delta
. Taking infinimum over
b_{1}
, we get the desired conclusion.

\square

Lemma 15
.

Under the conditions of
Theorem

3
, it holds that
\widehat{s}(\widehat{\gamma})/{s}(\widehat{\gamma})\rightarrow_{p}1
as
n\to\infty
, where
{s}^{2}(\widehat{\gamma})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}
with
\sigma_{i}^{2}={\rm Var}(Y_{i}\mid X_{i})
, and
\widehat{s}^{2}(\widehat{\gamma})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
with
\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}=(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{W_{i}}(X_{i}))^{2}
as defined in
Algorithm

1
.

Proof of
Lemma

15
.

In
Algorithm

1
, we use sample-splitting and fit regression on either side of the threshold; here we assume for simplicity in notation that we fit the regression of
Y_{i}
on
X_{i}
globally (our argument applied on either side of the threshold will then complete the proof). Also assume without loss of generality that
c=0
. Write
\widetilde{X}_{i}=(1,X_{i})
,
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}=Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}(X_{i})=Y_{i}-\widetilde{X}_{i}\widehat{\beta}
and
\varepsilon_{i}^{*}=Y_{i}-\mu(X_{i})=Y_{i}-\widetilde{X}_{i}\beta^{*}
where
\widetilde{X}\beta^{*}
is the best linear projection of
\mu(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]
onto
\text{span}\{1,X\}
. It follows from
(
White, 1980
)
that
\widehat{\beta}\rightarrow_{p}\beta^{*}
.
Next, write

(43)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta}))^{2}.

On the other hand, note that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq\sigma^{2}_{\min}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}
. Therefore,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta}))^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\lesssim\left\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})^{\top}\frac{|\widehat{\gamma}_{i}|\widetilde{X}_{i}}{\|\widehat{\gamma}_{i}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=o_{p}(1),

since
\widehat{\gamma}_{i}=0
for
|X_{i}|>\ell
. For the cross-term, we apply Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to write

\frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta})\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})^{\top}\frac{|\widehat{\gamma}_{i}|\widetilde{X}_{i}}{\|\widehat{\gamma}_{i}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}.

We can handle the second term exactly as above. For the first part, we show below that

(44)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}\;\big/\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\rightarrow_{p}1.

As a consequence, we can continue from (
43
) to write

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}+o_{p}(1)+o_{p}(1),

which completes the proof.
To prove (
44
), fix any
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
. Define
a_{n,i}=\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and write
\Delta_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}
. It follows from the assumption
\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-\mu(X_{i})|^{q}\mid X_{i}=x]<\infty
and Jensen’s inequality that
\mathbb{E}[|\Delta_{i}|^{q/2}|X_{i}=x]\leq C
for some universal constant
C\in(0,\infty)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\Delta_{i}\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|>M\right\}}\right|\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta_{i}\right|\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|>M\right\}}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right]

(Hölder’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta_{i}\right|^{q/2}\mid X_{i}\right]\right)^{2/q}\mathbb{P}\left(|\Delta_{i}|>M\mid X_{i}\right)^{1-2/q}

(Markov’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\frac{1}{M^{q/2-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta_{i}\right|^{q/2}\mid X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{M^{q/2-1}}.

Since
q>2
, we can pick
M
large enough so that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\Delta_{i}\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|>M\right\}}\right|>\varepsilon/2\right)<\eta/2,

and
|\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|\leq M\right\}}|\leq\varepsilon/4
(the latter is possible since
\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|\leq M\right\}}]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}]=0
by Dominated Convergence Theorem).
Consequently, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}\Delta_{i}\text{$\mathbf{1}$}{\left\{|\Delta_{i}|\leq M\right\}}\right|>\varepsilon/2\,\bigg|\,\{X_{i}\}\right)\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,i}^{2}M^{2}\leq M^{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{n,i}<\eta/2,

for all sufficiently large
n
, since we have
\max_{i\leq n}a_{n,i}=o_{p}(1)
from
Corollary

5
. Putting these together, we obtain
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}(\varepsilon_{i}^{*2}-\sigma_{i}^{2})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\gamma}_{i}^{2}=o_{p}(1)
. Since
0\leq\sigma^{2}_{\min}\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}
, this finishes the proof of (
44
).

\square
