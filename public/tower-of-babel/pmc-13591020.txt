Surgical Treatment for Hemorrhagic Infarction of Small Intestine Caused by Organophosphate Poisoning
Authors: Yi‐Yun Sun, Ren‐Jie Song, Wen‐Rui Wang, Xiao‐Fei Huang, Yuan‐Lan Lu, An‐Yong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute organophosphorus pesticide poisoning (AOPP) may cause intestinal infarction via intra‐abdominal hypertension (IAH) and non‐occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI). Although this complication carries high mortality, early laparotomy, resection of necrotic bowel, and comprehensive therapy can improve survival, as supported by our successfully treated case.

Introduction

Acute organophosphorus pesticide poisoning (AOPP) represents a major global public health concern in rural regions of developing countries, responsible for roughly 200,000 deaths annually [1]. Because the poisoning route is mainly oral, the first damage is to the intestinal mucosal barrier. Intra‐abdominal hypertension (IAH) may develop if the damage continues to worsen and gastrointestinal failure develops. IAH disrupts systemic hemodynamics, triggering mesenteric vasospasm and hypoperfusion, which further progresses to nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI) [2, 3]. In the absence of intervention, the disease progresses to intestinal infarction. This condition carries a high mortality rate ranging from 50% to 83% [4, 5]. To date, only one surgically managed case of organophosphorus‐induced small intestinal infarction has been documented, and the patient ultimately succumbed despite active supportive care [6]. The present article reports a rare case of severe organophosphorus poisoning, which was successfully managed with laparotomy for resection of the infarcted small intestine, aiming to summarize clinical experience in organophosphorus poisoning treatment and elevate the therapeutic success rate.

Case Materials

Case History/Examination

A 46‐year‐old female patient was admitted to the EICU, due to confusion caused by oral administration of 100 mL (77.5% concentration) of the organophosphate pesticide over the course of 12 h. The patient was examined for the following: APACHE II score of 20 points, poisoning severity score (PSS) of 7 points, RASS score of −1 point, P 120 beats/min, BP 87/62 mmHg, SpO2 99%. A garlic odor was detected during gastric lavage. Bilateral pupil diameter of 4 mm, light retardation, abdominal softness, painful face with epigastric compression, bowel sounds 2 times/min, and cardiopulmonary (−). Blood gas analysis revealed the following: pH 7.242, Lac 2.7 mmol/L, HCO3‐10.1 mmol/L, and BE −17.2 mmol/L; CHE: 0.2 kU/L (3.9–10.8kU/L); Toxicological analysis: trimethyl phosphate was detected in gastric juice, blood (475 ng/mL), and urine (734 ng/mL). Past medical history: Previously healthy, With no history of intestinal disease.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The diagnosis was acute severe organophosphate pesticide poisoning. The following treatments were used: (1) removal of poisons from the body: gastric lavage was performed with tap water (three sessions, 12,000 mL per session), mannitol catharsis, and hemoperfusion; (2) combined application of antidote and reactivator: intramuscular injection of pentethylquine hydrochloride (1 mg, q8h) and intravenous injection of pralidoxime iodide (1 g, q8h); (3) symptomatic supportive treatment: invasive ventilator‐assisted ventilation (P‐A/C mode), precise fluid rehydration and antishock (8 h, total 2940 mL) under the guidance of pulse indicator continuous cardiac output (PICCO), correction of acidosis. After treatment, the patient's was slightly better than before, and 500 mL of yellow loose stools were discharged.

At 8 h after admission, the condition of the patient changed. Abdominal distention, generalized abdominal tenderness (presenting with a painful face), muscle tension, tympanic resonance on percussion, and bowel sounds 1 times/min. The patient developed an intra‐abdominal pressure (IAP) of 13.5 mmHg, aggravated acidosis (Table 1), circulatory failure, and renal failure. Bedside ultrasound showed intestinal edema and a small amount of pelvic effusion. Diagnostic abdominal puncture (see pale yellow clear liquid), artificial anal dilation, anal exhaust, CRRT correction of acidosis and other treatments should be performed immediately, and emergency trauma surgery consultation should be urgently requested to evaluate whether the abdominal cavity needs to be opened.

At 20 h post‐admission, the acidosis could not be corrected, the bowel sounds disappeared, the knee joints appeared to have mottling (skin mottling score: 2 points), the IAP was 15 mmHg, and the PICCO index was abnormal (CI 1.58 L/min/m2, PVPI 2.5, SVI 12 mL/m2, GEDI 361 mL/m2, ELWI 10 mL/kg, SVRI 3403 dyn•s/cm‐5/m2). Under ultrasound positioning, a dark yellow transparent liquid was withdrawn from the abdominal cavity. Enhanced abdominal CT with 3D reconstruction (28 h post‐admission) showed fluid pneumoperitoneum (Figure 1). In this clinical context, although the patient's intra‐abdominal pressure (IAP) did not meet the diagnostic criteria for abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS), persistent intra‐abdominal hypertension (IAH) may still have contributed to intestinal ischemia and necrosis. Therefore, laparotomy was performed 22 h after admission. During the operation, intestinal avascular necrosis was evident approximately 10 cm to 160 cm from the ligament of Troch (Figure 2). After decompression of the small intestine, the necrotic intestine was excised, and the end was anastomosed.

Conclusion and Results

After the operation, the PICCO significantly improved (CI 3.68 L/min/m2, PVPI 1.7, SVI 41 mL/m2, GEDI 579 mL/m2, ELWI 8 mL/kg, SVRI 1056 dyn•s/cm‐5/m2), acidosis was corrected (Table 1), and the functions of various organs were gradually restored. Postoperative pathological results revealed small intestinal hemorrhagic infarction, which was consistent with what was observed during surgery (Figure 3). After active treatment, the endotracheal tube was successfully removed on Day 9. On Day 11, the patient was transferred to the emergency ward for further treatment. After observation, the patient's condition was stable and serum cholinesterase activity returned to normal. Therefore, she was discharged 24 d after admission.

Intestinal pathological section. Full‐thickness necrosis and edema of the small intestinal wall were observed, accompanied by infiltration of numerous neutrophils, eosinophils, and plasma cells; necrosis and detachment of the mucosal layer of the intestinal wall; and dilation and congestion of submucosal venules, with necrosis and local bleeding. The muscularis propria is disrupted and discontinuous, with large patches of hemorrhagic infarction. The intestinal submucosa exhibited abundant fibrinous exudate, severe focal hemorrhage, and tissue necrosis.

Discussion

Because of a lack of clinical experience supporting this therapeutic strategy, the clinical management proved complicated. The reasons for the hemorrhagic infarction of the small intestine in this patient were analyzed: In addition to the direct toxic damage of the poison on the gastrointestinal mucosa, the condition was compounded by systemic factors. Stress responses secondary to poisoning further depress gastrointestinal peristalsis, compromise gut motor activity, and derange systemic acid–base homeostasis. These disturbances consequently resulted in increased toxin absorption and aggravated intestinal edema [7]. Concurrently, organophosphates inhibits AChE, resulting in acetylcholine accumulation and elevated release of adrenaline and norepinephrine. This neurohumoral cascade induces intestinal wall vasospasm, microcirculatory dysfunction, and heightened vascular permeability, ultimately exacerbating ischemic injury to the intestinal wall [8]. Once gastrointestinal failure develops, intra‐abdominal pressure (IAP) increases. Persistent IAP ≥ 12 mmHg can lead to intra‐abdominal hypertension (IAH), which compromises mesenteric perfusion When IAP reaches 15 mmHg, intestinal mucosal microcirculatory blood flow is reduced by approximately 50% [2, 8]. As the duration of exposure lengthens and pressure rises, mitochondrial cristae become separated and disintegrate, resulting in mitochondrial degeneration. Furthermore, microvilli detach from the cell membrane and tight junctions dissociate. Collectively, these changes lead to intestinal ischemia and hypoxia [8]. Mesenteric artery embolism was ruled out on contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT. Hence, we consider that persistent IAH reduces mesenteric blood flow, resulting in NOMI and intestinal infarction. In NOMI, the body prioritizes perfusion to vital organs at the expense of mesenteric perfusion. A mismatch between supply and demand occurs due to impaired intestinal mucosal perfusion. This mismatch can be exacerbated by increased IAP and administration of vasoactive agents, ultimately leading to ischemia, inflammation, and intestinal wall infarction [3].

Lessons learned: During the management of this patient, the following two aspects are worthy of further discussion: (1) Should routine or repeated gastric lavage be performed in patients with severe poisoning and delayed presentation? According to the guidelines for self‐poisoning, gastric lavage is recommended only for patients who arrive within 1 h of ingestion and are hemodynamically stable. After a single gastric lavage, it is unlikely that large amounts of organophosphates remain in the stomach [1]. In this case, the patient presented 12 h after ingestion and received repeated gastric lavage; however, organophosphates was still detected in gastric aspirate 7 d after admission. Therefore, in patients with severe massive intoxication and gastrointestinal dysfunction who arrive outside the effective time window for conventional gastric lavage, the clinical value of repeated lavage, targeted endoscopic gastric lavage, or even open gastrolavage remains to be further explored. (2) In this patient, the gastrointestinal mucosal barrier function was severely impaired, and abdominal signs deteriorated progressively after admission. Despite active medical treatment, the response was unsatisfactory. As IAP increased progressively, systemic hemodynamics and mesenteric perfusion were compromised. Although emergency laparotomy was performed immediately, roughly 150 cm of small bowel was found to be necrotic. In the absence of clear clinical evidence, the optimal timing of laparotomy for poisoned patients who do not meet the diagnostic criteria for abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS) remains uncertain. Judging from the range of intestinal necrosis observed in this case, laparotomy was performed with mild delay. Therefore, further research is needed to delineate the role that IAH without ACS plays in intestinal ischemia; the optimal surgical timing for poisoned patients also warrants additional investigation [2].
