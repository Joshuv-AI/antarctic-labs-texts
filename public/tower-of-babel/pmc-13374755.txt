Guidance from an informed public on collecting and sharing PGx test results for major depressive disorder: “It’s no different from your blood type”
Authors: Colene Bentley, Louisa Edwards, Kieran C. O’Doherty, Stirling Bryan, Jehannine (J9) Austin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMany people with major depressive disorder (MDD) undergo a lengthy process of trial-and-error before finding a medication that works well for them. Pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing for MDD can help find a suitable antidepressant faster. However, concerns about health equity, and data privacy and security need to be considered before implementing the test.MethodsIn Fall 2024, 30 individuals attended a four-day public deliberation in Vancouver, British Columbia (BC) to provide direction on implementing PGx testing for MDD in BC’s publicly funded healthcare system. Participants were recruited to represent a diversity of perspectives and experiences of British Columbians. Information supports included expert speakers and a background booklet. Event transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis (both inductive and reflexive), while also accounting for participants’ knowledge gains over time.ResultsParticipants adopted four policy-relevant positions on implementation: 1) PGx test results should be treated like other lab results; 2) stored in electronic medical records; 3) shared with patients and healthcare professionals in the circle of care; and 4) education about the test will facilitate its widespread use.ConclusionParticipants’ positions represent informed, civic-minded, and policy-relevant priorities for the acceptable adoption of PGx testing for MDD in the BC healthcare system and elsewhere.

Introduction

Depression affects more than 300 million people worldwide and is the leading mental health contributor to the global burden of disease [1,2]. It is also on the rise. From 2007–2017, the number of years living with disability (YLD) attributed to depression increased by 14.3% globally (from 13.1% to 15.6%) [1]. More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic fueled further spikes in psychiatric conditions, including a 27.6% increase (from 25.1% to 30.3%) in the prevalence of depression worldwide [3]. A recent systematic review of longitudinal cohort studies examining mental health before and during the pandemic found that, unlike other psychiatric conditions, the increase in depression and mood disorder symptoms did not return to pre-pandemic levels [4].

Major depression or major depressive disorder (MDD) is not one disorder but a constellation of symptoms, severity levels, and trajectories of illness. It is characterized by depressed mood, loss of pleasure and interest, low self-esteem, inability to concentrate, fatigue, sleep disturbances, suicidal ideation, and is highly correlated with alexithymia [5–8]. Compared to the general population, people with depression have higher mental and physical comorbidities [9,10], lower quality of life [11], and substantially lower personal earnings and household income [12,13]. The most common first-line treatment for MDD is antidepressant medications, often because they are less expensive and more accessible than psychotherapy and there’s no wait time for treatment. However, antidepressants typically take 4–6 weeks to determine efficacy [14], and up to 50% of people prescribed an antidepressant do not respond initially [15–18]. These challenges contribute to low adherence to medication, which itself is a predictor of poorer health outcomes, including an increased risk of relapse [18–20]. The need to improve therapeutic approaches to treat depression is, therefore, of necessity. (It is important to emphasize that medication is only one modality for depression treatment, and it cannot address root causes of depression and other psychiatric conditions. The legacies of colonization and slavery, for instance, and low socio-economic status, have resulted in higher prevalence of psychiatric conditions, including depression, amongst Indigenous [21], African American [22], and low-income populations [23]. These social, economic, and political determinants of mental health cannot be remedied by pharmaceutical intervention.)

For people taking medication to treat MDD, pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing can help them find an efficacious and tolerable antidepressant more quickly [24]. Currently, antidepressant selection can lead to cycles of standard-of-practice prescribing and observing for effects over several months or years [14,25,26]. In many cases, this process subjects patients to undue hardship in search of an appropriate medication with tolerable side effects; it also increases healthcare expenditures due to multiple medication appointments and prescription changes [27,28]. PGx testing can help shorten the trial-and-error period in many cases. By analyzing the influence of specific genes (i.e., variation in three cytochrome P450 genes: CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP2B6) on the rate at which a person metabolizes antidepressants, PGx testing provides guidance on which medications and doses are more likely to benefit each patient [29]. Systematic reviews on PGx-guided prescribing for antidepressants have reported improved remission and response rates, but the variable quality of clinical trials presents interpretation challenges [30,31]. This remains an active area of research that is of interest to people with lived experience (PWLE) of depression [31–35], and may have greater clinical utility in some situations. For example, the 2023 update to the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatment guidelines stated that PGx testing may be beneficial for those experiencing unusual adverse effects at low doses or those who do not respond to therapeutic doses [14].

PGx testing for depression treatment is not currently available through Canada’s publicly-funded healthcare system, meaning those who wish to access the test must pay privately. Over the past decade, in the Canadian province of British Columbia (BC), primary care physicians and the Ministry of Health have called for new regulation, standardized testing, and public education on PGx testing for depression [36,37]. In 2023, a BC-specific cost-effectiveness analysis showed PGx testing to guide antidepressant use yielded significant health gains and generated cost savings of close to $1 billion CAD over 20 years [28].

Given public relevance as well as policy interest in PGx testing for MDD in BC, in-depth public consultation on the topic was warranted since residents of BC are both payers for and end-users of BC’s public healthcare system. Research shows that PWLE of depression and some members of the public are apprehensive about data privacy and security of genetic data in mental health contexts [32,38], especially because these are stigmatized conditions [38] (Concerns about privacy, security of patient data, and stigmatization are evident in the genetic testing literature more broadly as well [39,40].). PWLE and members of the public also have misgivings about the impact of test costs on equity of access to PGx testing in the absence of universal coverage [32,33,35]. The acceptability of PGx testing will depend, in part, on addressing these concerns.

The purpose of the current study was to gauge the social acceptability of implementing PGx testing for MDD in BC, and to understand British Columbians’ concerns and supports for this policy initiative. To this end, in Fall 2024, thirty British Columbians were invited to a four-day, in-person public deliberation on implementing PGx testing for MDD. Public deliberation is a specific form of public discussion in which participants are informed about the topic and collectively seek solutions to challenging social issues through reasoned engagement [41]. Public deliberation is highly suited to address topics in healthcare that require incorporating value trade-offs and evidence, and when priorities must be set. In Canada, publics ought to be consulted on the use of collective resources across diverse health and social needs, since they are both payers and end-users of these services. Deliberative public engagements have been held in Canada to advise policy-makers on funding for expensive cancer drugs [42,43], allocating kidneys for transplant [44], childhood vaccination policy [45], and biobanks [46].

Participants at the BC public deliberation were tasked with reaching civic-minded solutions to sharing PGx test results with other healthcare providers (HCPs)—including issues of data privacy and security related to broadening access to mental health data—equity of access, and secondary use of PGx test results for ethics-approved research (See Box 1). “Civic-minded solutions” refers to societal positions on a given issue that go beyond individual opinion and preference; they are developed collectively to reflect what all or most participants are willing to accept on the issue. At the BC deliberation, participants made 15 explicit recommendations—or solutions—to guide BC policy-making [47]. Overall, participants’ views coalesced around four key policy-relevant positions regarding PGx testing for MDD. This paper provides a detailed qualitative analysis of their policy-relevant positions.

Methods

Participant recruitment, event structure, and the process of developing recommendations are described in detail in Edwards et al. [47]. The study was approved by the University of British Columbia Behavioural Research Ethics Board (#H24-01354) and Vancouver Island Health Authority Research Ethics & Compliance Office.

Recruitment

The goal of recruitment was to assemble a mini public [48,49] of 30 individuals who represented a diversity of perspectives and experiences of the people of BC. Participants were recruited to ensure representation on key socio-demographic characteristics based on 2021 population estimates [50], with demographic diversity used as a proxy for lived experience. A letter of invitation to participate was mailed to 15,000 randomly selected households across BC. Interested individuals were directed to the study website, where they completed a brief demographic survey. Eligible participants were adults (18+ years) living in BC, fluent in English, and able to attend all four days of the event. To ensure a representatively-stratified sample, we selected at least two individuals from each of five regional health authorities, and then relatively more from more highly populated areas, and sought to maximize diversity of key socio-demographic variables (i.e., gender, age group, self-reported ancestry, education, and urban/rural location). We also included individuals with varying experience with MDD (direct, indirect, no known close contacts with MDD) to ensure this experience was represented. Exclusion criteria were: being employed as a health professional, policymaker, lobbyist, and working or volunteering for a mental health organization to avoid vested interests unduly influencing the deliberative process. Thirty individuals attended the deliberation on Days 1–2, 29 on Day 3, and 28 on Day 4. Participant withdrawal was for medical reasons unrelated to the event. All participants signed a detailed consent form, were compensated $125/day to participate, and all travel, meals, and accommodation expenses were covered. Event structure

The format of the deliberation followed methods developed by Burgess and O’Doherty [46]. The four-day in-person event was held on two nonconsecutive weekends in November-December 2024. Day 1 was dedicated to introducing the topic to participants. They heard from six expert speakers-two people with lived/living experience of MDD, a pharmacogeneticist, a bioethicist, an Indigenous knowledge keeper, and a family physician—followed by a question and answer session. A brief description of the topics covered by each speaker is found in Table 2. Days 2–4 focused on deliberating about the event topic, making collective recommendations, and voting on them. Participants met in small- and large-group sessions led by trained facilitators. Recommendations were made and voted on collectively in the large group only. Voting (i.e., for, against, abstention) was used to gauge the degree of collective support for each recommendation, and as a technique to identify points of disagreement that could be documented and explored more fully [51]. On Day 4, a panel of three policy makers (one head of a mental health association, two directors of pharmacy services at the provincial and health authority levels) and two clinical experts (a psychiatrist, a family physician) participated in a question and answer period with participants. Afterward, participants reviewed, revised (if necessary), and then re-voted on each of their recommendations. Deliberative questions and information support

Over the course of a year, the study team met with provincial policy makers charged with implementing PGx testing for MDD, should it be adopted and funded by the province of BC. This was to ensure the deliberative questions brought to participants were policy-relevant and involved key trade-offs on which public guidance was needed. An information booklet was shared with participants prior to the event (see Supplementary File S1). The booklet provided information on: MDD and approaches to treatment, benefits to prescribing with PGx testing, potential risks to storing electronic health information, and that PGx test implementation would not displace other therapeutic approaches to MDD. The booklet also clearly stated that the public deliberation was about whether PGx testing should be made publicly available or not, rather than PGx testing versus any other depression treatment or funding treatments for any other health conditions. Participants also received a handout describing three pieces of information involved PGx testing for MDD: biologic samples, genetic data, and PGx test results.

Participants deliberated on four questions (see Box 1). It was up to participants to determine the direction they wanted to take with each question, which is in keeping with public deliberation methods. To avoid perceptions that the event was designed to simply ‘rubber stamp’ a foregone policy decision, the first question asked participants whether or not PGx testing for MDD should be publicly funded in BC. The research team was fully prepared to pivot to a different set of questions if participants were strongly against adopting PGx testing, since their concerns, too, would be useful input to policy makers. The remaining questions were about how PGx testing for MDD should be implemented in an acceptable and trustworthy manner. The qualitative data generated by deliberative public engagements are fundamentally different from the data generated by interviews or focus groups, and thus require a slightly different approach to analysis [52]. A purpose of public deliberation is to provide a forum in which participants engage in learning and an iterative exchange of perspectives with each other to collectively develop recommendations on an issue. Over the course of a four-day event, participants are expected to be willing to change their views in light of new learning, hearing other participants’ perspectives, and through aiming to reach civic-minded conclusion that are responsive to a wide range of societal views. Consequently, participants’ views on the issues being discussed are expected to change over time; in particular, they tend to become more informed, more considerate of other perspectives, and tend toward convergence on an issue. Even when there is disagreement, participants’ opinions tend to become more clearly articulated over time and convergent on two or more distinct positions. Thus, analysis of deliberation transcripts needs to consider the context and relative time point during the deliberation in which certain statements were made. In particular, participants’ statements and reasons articulated on Weekend 2 carry more weight—because they are more deliberatively informed and people may have changed their minds as a result—than those made on previous days, and their ratified recommendations on Day 4 are the recommendations of record. Analysis of transcripts from deliberative events must take into account this weighting [53].

All event sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim using Temi (Temi 2025©). Transcripts were then reviewed and corrected against audio recordings by research team members. Participants were de-identified and assigned pseudonyms. NVivo software (Lumivero, Version 14, 2023) was used to manage the data set and facilitate transcript coding. The transcripts were read numerous times by CB (a qualitative researcher, also the large group facilitator for the deliberation) and LE (a social psychologist, also the research lead on the public deliberation and overall research program manager) to gain a holistic sense of the data prior to analysis. Preliminary codes were developed by the two analysts, based on their familiarity with the data and topics explored in the deliberative questions. Transcripts were then independently coded by CB (all transcripts) and LE (one-third of transcripts), with each analyst acknowledging their own subject-positions as informing their approach to coding and interpretation. The purpose of having two research team members engage in coding was not to assess consensus between coders, but rather “to engage in collaboration … to enhance reflexivity and interpretative depth” [54]. In weekly meetings and through ongoing discussion, CB and LE explored their respective interpretations to gain a deeper understanding of the participants’ perspectives and how they changed over the process of deliberation, and how to weigh statements made at different points in time. This process resulted in the addition of new codes, collapsing of some codes, and the development of sub-codes, and/or refining existing codes that informed subsequent understandings of the data. Thus coding was an organic process that reflected the dynamic interactions of the researchers’ particular experience and training, and their growing familiarity and insights about the data [54]. Our analytic process aligns broadly with what Braun & Clarke [55] refer to an inductive approach to thematic analysis and also draws on elements of reflexive approaches to thematic analysis [54].

Position 1. PGx genetic data are “benign”

Participants ultimately considered the genetic data collected through PGx testing for MDD as fairly innocuous. They saw it as a “very benign sort of data point … no different from … your blood type, your hair color or your eye color” (Vinh, Large Group, Day 3). They felt the data were unremarkable, although potentially useful to physicians, like knowing whether a patient was diabetic or allergic to penicillin (Thariq, Blue Group, Day 3). An expert speaker described PGx test results as “more like a compass than a GPS” to guide more efficacious prescribing. This metaphor resonated with participants, who often adopted it in their deliberations. They also referred to PGx test results as giving HCPs “another tool” in their toolkit to inform prescribing practices. A few participants raised concerns that PGx testing might promote medication as a first resort over other therapeutic approaches. As one participant commented: I feel that in society, [there is] a general kind of over-reliance or pushing of antidepressants … .I’m not sure how much PGx testing is going to radically change what’s already a very prevalent attitude towards depression. (Karen, Red Group, Day 2)

Position 2. Include PGx test results in EMRs

Participants were comfortable including PGx test results in EMRs. They felt the three genes assessed to determine medication metabolizer status “d[id]n’t seem to show as much information as we originally thought” (Richard, Large Group, Day 3), and believed test results should be treated the same as other types of lab tests or health information. Apprehensions about potential data breaches were raised (e.g., how might the breached data be used against individuals, particularly by insurance companies?); however, participants placed a higher value on having a comprehensive medical history available in their EMR than the possibility of a data breach, which they considered to be low. Ultimately, they felt the genetic data were unexceptional enough that no additional security and/or legal measures were required to protect genetic data and patient confidentiality in EMRs (Recommendations 7 and 8): We understood that the PGx test, and it sort of was reiterated by other people … was not extraordinary enough an entity to warrant any special treatment. (Thariq, Large Group, Day 3) I think [genetic data] should be similar to how blood type is added to your chart. It is just another unique identifier that impacts how you’re treated and how certain medical procedures are conducted, just like blood type does. (April, Blue Group, Day 3) You’ll be diagnosed with depression and maybe have been given one antidepressant already. That data is in your file. Is it different than having PGx testing that also shows that you have depression? Why is it more scary to have PGx test results in your file when you’ve already got depression diagnosis in your file? Position 3. PGx test results can be shared with HCPs

Participants unanimously supported sharing PGx test results for MDD between HCPs directly involved in the patient’s care (Recommendation 12). This reflected their growing comfort with the role and availability of PGx data to support patient-centered care. According to participants, emergency care teams, specialists (e.g., psychiatrists, surgeons, cardiologists), and doctors at walk-in clinics warranted access to PGx test results, as did patients themselves. Once again, participants placed a high value on having their EMRs–including their PGx test results–widely accessible to HCPs in their circle of care, despite concerns about any stigma associated with having a mental health diagnosis. At times they struggled to find reasons not to share results with HCPs and saw little difference between sharing PGx and other test results with HCPs: [T]reat the [PGx] lab result as a lab result. There was some disagreement on whether pharmacists should have access to PGx test results. All participants saw pharmacists having a key role in identifying potential drug interactions with prescribed antidepressants. A few argued that pharmacists didn’t need to know PGx lab results to advise on drug interactions and thus should not be included in the circle of care. On multiple occasions, participants supported expanding pharmacists’ scope of practice to include ordering PGx tests. As one participant remarked: My concern is access to [PGx testing]. I think that one way that could potentially be solved is by allowing you to request and get the testing by going to your local pharmacy and saying … .’I think I have problems. Can you refer me? Or like, can you start this process?’ (April, Blue Group, Day 3)

Participants saw pharmacists’ expanded role as creating a path toward depression care for the unhoused, individuals without a family doctor, and those living in rural and/or remote areas with no resident physician, and as a way to reduce the workload of busy family physicians.

Position 4. Public information about PGx testing is needed to reduce sigma, reach target population

Participants endorsed having a targeted awareness/education campaign about the possibility of PGx testing as one of the options for depression care (Recommendation 10). They said that having PGx testing for MDD as part of a broader public discourse about wellbeing would help reduce public anxiety about genetic testing. In addition, describing the test as medication compatibility testing or something similar (Recommendation 9) would make it “less sci-fi scary” (Nora, Large Group, Day 3) and more approachable to those seeking depression care. On multiple occasions, participants wanted to “normalize” conversations about mental health in an effort to reduce its stigma: In our group, we discussed stigma. I think that at least [knowing about] the test … allows for people that are reluctant to take antidepressants [to take them]. That might help [them]. (Thariq, Large Group, Day 3)

Discussion

This study presents policy-relevant positions taken by 30 members of the BC public on whether the provincial health system should adopt PGx testing for MDD and, if so, how best to implement it. Participants’ positions represent the collectively-held views of an informed public on the topic of implementing PGx testing in BC; they are based on participants’ engagement with the expert speakers and event materials, and through their deliberations with one another over the four-day event. As evidenced by their recommendations, participants strongly supported implementing PGx testing for MDD in the healthcare system. After four days of deliberation, participants were comfortable with the type and amount of genetic information reported in PGx test results, which they came to view as “benign” information. They regarded metabolizer status as an important but unexceptional piece of genetic data, not unlike one’s blood type, that did not contain any harmful or identifying information. Sharing PGx test results for MDD with other HCPs via EMRs, without the need for additional security protections, was strongly supported by participants, while acknowledging the potential for data breaches. Participants were conscious of the stigma associated with mental illness. They identified the need to raise public awareness about the availability of this new technology for anyone with depression who may need it and to counter public biases toward those with potentially stigmatizing diagnoses. Taken together, these views represent policy-relevant priorities for the acceptable adoption of PGx testing for MDD in the BC healthcare system and elsewhere.

To some extent, participants’ positions align with an emerging body of research on PGx testing in mental health care from the perspectives of PWLE and the public [27,32–35,38,56]. With respect to PGx testing for MDD in particular, participants in the present study were optimistic about the potential for testing to shorten the trial-and-error period needed to find an effective medication and dose, as other studies have reported [32–35,38]. Some participants also indicated they would re-seek treatment after a history of medication challenges, recognizing that PGx test results might explain why previous medications were ineffective for them, echoing results from earlier studies [33,35,38]. Finally, participants’ receptiveness to PGx testing for MDD was tempered by strong concerns about the potential for stigmatization and the need to educate HCPs, PWLE, and the public about testing, which is consistent with previous work [32,34,35,38,57].

Participants’ positions on genetic data in PGx testing for MDD also stand out from the abovementioned body of research. Participants in the current study decided that metabolizer status was a small amount of genetic data that did not carry personally identifying information, which eased early concerns about data privacy and security and led to the acceptance of storing PGx test results in EMRs. Participants also likened PGx test results to routine lab test results and, as such, recommended they be placed in patients’ EMRs to inform care. Few expressed reservations about doctors placing too much weight on genetic results to the exclusion of patient experience. These findings are notable. They contrast with prior calls by PWLE and the public for greater transparency and/or safeguards regarding how genetic data are used and stored in depression care [32,38] and healthcare generally [57], and apprehensions about doctors’ overreliance on genomic results [35,38]. Providing participants with information supports and enabling them to engage with the material and one another may account for why early concerns about the collection, storage, and sharing of genetic data lessened over the course of their deliberations.

Participants endorsed sharing PGx data for clinical purposes. Access to this data by HCPs directly involved in a patient’s care–such as doctors at walk-in clinics, ER physicians, specialists, and possibly pharmacists–aligns with continuity of patient care. However, standard practice in BC is for lab test results to be shared only with the prescribing physician and other doctors identified on the test requisition. In addition, participants felt patients, too, should have access to their genetic test results. At present, genetic test results are not electronically available to patients. Participants’ advice invites policy makers to (re)consider access to genetic test results for patients and HCPs, consistent with patient-centered care. Studies have reported that incorporating PGx reports into mental health consultations can support patient-provider engagement and decision making, and help alleviate patients’ concerns about medications [58,59]. It may also be appropriate for pharmacists to play a role in implementing PGx testing beyond conventional dispensing activities. Pharmacists are well-positioned in community settings to educate patients, monitor for drug interactions, and recommend therapies based on PGx test reports [60].

The positions developed by participants are those of an informed public, which may account for the emphases they placed on some considerations over others. For instance, participants’ deliberations were premised on universal coverage of PGx testing for MDD in BC. They understood from event materials that a recent economic evaluation showed adopting the test would generate cost-savings without displacing other treatment options for depression, like counseling [28]. Consequently, the impact of test costs on equitable access to treatment was not a prominent concern among participants in this study, in contrast to other PWLE and public views on PGx adoption [32,33,35,57]. A second point of differentiation relates to how well participants grasped differences between types of genetic tests and what they uncover. Reports of people mistaking genomic testing for disease risk testing or whole genome sequencing are common in the literature [35,38,57]. For participants in this study, these early misunderstandings were cleared up during the first weekend. They understood that test results provided a “compass” rather than a ‘silver bullet’ to finding an appropriate antidepressant, and that the genes analyzed in the test do not provide information beyond medication metabolism (i.e., no incidental findings). Ultimately, they recognized the benefits of engaging with expert speakers, event materials, and one another during the deliberation. This prompted their recommendation for a public education campaign in tandem with PGx implementation to raise awareness of the test and its possibilities for people experiencing depression. Participants’ felt that an effective public education strategy would help assuage fears about this type of genetic test, facilitate outreach to underserved groups (including those previously alienated by treatment), and potentially reduce social stigma around mental illness. As noted above, the need for public, PWLE, and physician education to support implementing PGx testing for MDD is echoed in the literature; the current study offers preliminary evidence of the impact education could have on test uptake and acceptability.

This study has several strengths. The deliberation event provided an opportunity for a diverse public to engage in participatory democracy and contribute to imminent policy decisions. Other key strengths are the identification of actionable policy positions from an informed public, and a rigorous methodology. The four-day event structure enabled participants to engage in a process of learning and reasoned deliberation on the topic of implementing PGx testing for MDD; this process is also accounted for in the transcript analysis. Despite these strengths, there are limitations. First, while the research team endeavored to allow participants to direct the discussion and decide for themselves what was important about PGx testing for MDD, it is possible the information and questions provided were framed in such a way as to influence the deliberation outcomes. Relatedly, the information provided to participants was inevitably limited. A balance was sought between providing enough relevant information to support quality deliberation and not overwhelming participants with too much information or certain expert knowledge (e.g., in-depth explanations of PGx testing in other medical areas and clinical trials data) that could frustrate their efforts and/or steer the discussion away from the policy topic. Second, while our representatively-stratified sample of 30 participants adheres to the public deliberation methodology we follow, the study findings cannot be generalized to other publics and locations. As is the case with all qualitative research, the purpose is to gain insights that are locally relevant, rather than universally applicable. Also, because the deliberation was conducted in English, we may have missed important viewpoints of non-English speaking British Columbians. However, this was a socio-demographically diverse group of participants, in which 57% had non-European ancestry and 23% were born outside of Canada.

Conclusion

Over the course of their deliberations, a diverse public came to common understandings about the collection, storage, and sharing of genetic data collected through PGx testing for MDD. They saw metabolizer status as an important but unexceptional piece of health data; as such, it should be treated like any other lab result, shared with patients and HCPs in the circle of care, and stored in EMRs without additional security measures. Participants at the public deliberation also wanted patients, HCPs, and the public to be educated about the test to encourage its widespread use. Taken together, these shared understandings–or positions–add to the base of relevant input into a range of policy decisions regarding the formal adoption of PGx testing for MDD into a publicly-funded healthcare system. They represent the concerns and supports of an informed public on the policy topic; as such, they can help inform implementation decisions to enable broader public uptake of and trust in the decisions made.
