A cross-sectional study on dentists’ learning preferences for learning about light-curing units and resin-based composites
Authors: Afnan O Al-Zain, Khlood Baghlaf, Omar Abdulwassi, Reem Almukairin, Maram Alanazi, Elaf Alshomrani, Sultan Alftaikhah, Richard B Price
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Most participants lacked knowledge about their LCU type.•Wavelength is rarely considered when choosing the LCU.•Hands-on workshops are preferred for education.•YouTube was the preferred social media platform for learning.•Graduate/postgraduates had better knowledge about LCUs than those with a only a bachelor’s degree.Keywords: Curing lights, Resin-based composites, Learning preferences, Social media, Dental education, YouTube

Introduction

The dental light-curing unit (LCU) is an essential equipment in dental practice (Price et al., 2020, Rueggeberg et al., 2017). The correct light-curing technique is also essential as it greatly influences the success and performance of light-activated materials (Maktabi et al., 2021, Price et al., 2020, Rueggeberg et al., 2017).

For a dental resin restoration to function properly, it must be optimally polymerized. This depends on delivering the correct energy and the correct wavelengths of light to activate the photoinitiators in resin-based composites (RBCs). The most commonly used LCUs are light-emitting diode (LED) units, either single-emission-peak LEDs emitting blue light (420–520 nm), or multiple-emission-peak LEDs that emit additional violet light (380–425 nm). Blue light activates camphorquinone (CQ) photoinitiators (peak absorption = 470 nm), whereas violet light primarily activates the alternative photoinitiators such as diphenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO) (peak absorption = 409 nm) (Al-Zain et al., 2018, Price et al., 2020, Rueggeberg et al., 2017). Clinicians should therefore select the LCU type depending on the type of photoinitiator used within the RBC. A lack of understanding and improper light-curing techniques, including LCU emission spectrum compatibility with RBC photoinitiators, can negatively affect physical and mechanical properties such as compressive strength, bond strength, degree of conversion, microleakage, and color stability, potentially leading to restoration failure (Leprince et al., 2013, Maktabi et al., 2021, Price, 2017, Rueggeberg et al., 2017).

Dental students, dentists, and dental assistants often lack adequate knowledge about the LCU, its use and maintenance. This has prompted recommendations that the dental school curricula address this knowledge gap and enhance educational programs (Al-Senan et al., 2021, Alhaddad et al., 2022, Alsenan et al., 2022, Frazier et al., 2020, Ismail et al., 2024, Kopperud et al., 2017). Some universities now use patient simulators to teach correct light-curing techniques. This simulator is an effective educational tool (Al-Zain and Al-Osaimi, 2021, Al-Zain et al., 2023, Price et al., 2014, Suliman et al., 2020). Despite the reported knowledge gap among dentists, interns, and students and attempts to bridge this gap, the knowledge level among dentists about the LCU that most use many times every day is still inadequate.

Dentists prefer learning from skilled colleagues during face-to-face meetings and are more likely to seek further information through discussions with specialists and respected experts (Isham et al., 2016, Landry, 2006). Therefore, it is important to investigate preferred educational methods to address knowledge gaps about LCUs. Understanding these preferences will help develop tailored educational approaches to improve clinical outcomes and dental care quality. To the author’s knowledge, no study has yet determined the preferred methods for learning about LCUs or RBCs.

This study aimed to identify the key factors considered by dentists, interns, and students as important when selecting an LCU and RBC, and to determine the most effective educational methods to learn about LCUs.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of King Abdulaziz University, Faculty of Dentistry (proposal no. 087-09-22). This questionnaire-based cross-sectional study included dentists, interns, and students from private and government hospitals, clinics, and academic institutions in Saudi Arabia. Dentists outside Saudi Arabia and those who chose not to participate were excluded.

Study instrument

The questionnaire was developed using original and modified questions from previous studies (Alhaddad et al., 2022, Alqabbaa et al., 2018, Santini and Turner, 2011) and underwent content and face validation. Five experts assessed its clarity, relevance, simplicity, and ambiguity using a five-point Likert scale. In-person validation involved interviews with 12 target population representatives, and their feedback was incorporated into the final version.

The 4-section questionnaire, consisting of 30 open- and closed-ended questions, was distributed electronically via convenience sampling from September to December 2022 (Supplementary Material 1). Knowledge about LCUs was assessed based on brand reputation, irradiance, and wavelength compatibility with the RBC photoinitiator's absorption spectrum (Price et al., 2020). RBC knowledge was evaluated through factors such as esthetics, polishability, handling properties, brand reputation, and the compatibility between the LCU emission spectrum and the photoinitiator's absorption characteristics. Sample size/power calculation and statistical analyses

The sample size was calculated using the G. Power software from a publication on the number of registered dentists in Saudi Arabia (AlBaker et al., 2017). A sample size of 384 was adequate (α = 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics described the outcomes. The relationships between demographic variables and outcomes were examined using chi-squared tests, Fisher's exact tests, and independent samples t-tests (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Content and face validity

Five experts assessed the scale and calculated its content validity index (CVI) as 0.88 for both versions. To ensure clarity, the questionnaire was pilot-tested with 12 participants.

Demographics

Of the 635 individuals who responded, 420 completed the online questionnaire (response rate = 66.1 %). Table 1 reports the characteristics of the participants. The average age was 27 years. Most of the respondents were from the Western, Northern, and Central regions of Saudi Arabia. Regarding the dental license rankings by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), more General Dentists participated in the study than specialists or consultants (Table 1).

Practice experience

Table 1 shows that most respondents were dental students (34.3 %) who had not yet gained clinical experience beyond dental school, followed by dentists with 1–5 years of clinical experience (20 %). Most respondents worked part-time (56 %). Participants’ practices were evaluated based on how often they used an LCU. Most participants (68.3 %) reported using LCUs 1–4 times daily.

Knowledge perception

In this study, 52 % of the respondents considered themselves sufficiently knowledgeable about the LCU. Of these, 77.2 % had a bachelor's degree, and 22.8 % were graduates/postgraduates. Fig. 2 shows that many participants lacked knowledge of the specific type of LCU they used. Specifically, 31.2 % of participants were unaware of the type of LCU they were using. 29.8 % identified their units as LED but were unsure whether they were single- or multiple-emission-peak LED units.

LCU and RBC selection parameters

As for the parameters used when selecting the LCU, Fig. 3A indicates that exposure time (44 %), brand reputation (43 %), and battery life (36 %) were the top three parameters selected by the participants and shows the percentages of participants who ranked the parameters from 1 to 10. Notably, only 29 % knew that matching the LCU wavelength to the absorption spectrum of the photoinitiators in the RBC was even important. 17 % of participants selected irradiance as one of the top three parameters to consider when selecting an LCU, regardless of their status or ranking level. Among the participants, 28 % of postgraduate students and 27 % of bachelor's degree graduates correctly matched the RBC photoinitiator wavelength to the LCU wavelength. This association was statistically significant (p = 0.009).

When selecting an RBC, 91.9 % of participants prioritized esthetics and polishability, brand reputation, and handling properties among the top three parameters. Specifically, 72 % selected esthetics and polishability, 49 % brand reputation, and 49 % handling properties. In contrast, only 20 % of participants considered the compatibility between the RBC photoinitiator absorption spectrum and the LCU wavelength important, regardless of their status or ranking level (Fig. 3B).

Regarding the question “Do you consider the characteristics of the LCU when using or purchasing a resin that must be light cured, and vice versa?” 20.5 % selected that they considered the LCU and RBC characteristics, and the same respondents were knowledgeable about the top LCU selection parameters. In contrast, 67 % selected that they considered the LCU and RBC characteristics and were knowledgeable about the top RBC selection parameters.

Fig. 3C illustrates that graduate and postgraduate participants had significantly more knowledge than the participants who only had a bachelor's degree regarding selecting LCUs, particularly regarding the compatibility of the emission spectrum from the LCU with the absorption spectrum of the photoinitiator used in the RBC (p = 0.009). Dentists who frequently used LCUs had significantly greater knowledge about the key RBC selection factors (p = 0.037).

Preferred educational method

Fig. 4 shows that 57.6 % of the participants had gained most of their previous knowledge about LCUs from undergraduate lectures, followed by scientific articles, books, colleagues, and social media. Fig. 5A shows that 45.5 % of the participants preferred hands-on workshops, 26 % favored in-person lectures, and 42.6 % chose a 30-minute lecture to learn about LCUs. Moreover, in Fig. 5B, 49.9 % of the participants preferred ‘YouTube’ as their primary social media platform, with Instagram coming in at 24 % and ‘X’ at 20.2 %. Regarding video length preference, 69 % preferred 2–5-minute videos over longer or live videos. Finally, Fig. 5C indicates that specialists and consultants significantly preferred YouTube as their top social media educational resource compared to general dentists, interns, and students (p = 0.036). Nevertheless, some respondents noted that dentists should not depend on social media for scientific information.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the knowledge and selection practices for LCUs and RBCs among dentists, interns, and students in Saudi Arabia. The diverse sample was selected to illustrate the evolution of knowledge and practice at various stages of dental care. The study identified significant knowledge gaps, particularly in understanding the interaction between LCUs and RBCs, and reveals the preferred educational methods for learning about these topics. These findings will help to guide improvements in the dental curricula and enhance the generalizability of the results across various educational and clinical settings.

The predominance of dental students and early career dentists as study participants presented a significant opportunity for targeted educational interventions regarding information about LCUs and RBCs. This finding aligns with previous research showing that academic environments promote structured training and access to educational resources, although gaps remain in the understanding of the technical aspects of LCUs (Castanheiro et al., 2024, Suliman et al., 2020). The lack of awareness regarding specific LCU types and emission peaks highlights the need for comprehensive training on using the LCU and its impact on restorative procedures.

This study included many dental students and early career clinicians. This presents an opportunity to target educational strategies that enhance LCU knowledge among our participants. The prevalence of part-time clinicians, who may limit their exposure to diverse experiences and advanced LCU techniques, underscores the importance of practical training and hands-on workshops. Additionally, the lack of awareness regarding specific LCU types and emission peaks that they emit indicates the need for better education on the technology used in LCUs and its role in restorative procedures.

The preference for practical LCU parameters, such as exposure time, brand reputation, and battery life, over compatibility of the emission spectrum from the LCU with the photoinitiators used in the RBC highlights a significant gap in the knowledge of the participants in this study. The fact that only 29 % of the respondents recognized that it was important to match the emission spectrum from the LCU to the absorption spectrum of the photoinitiator was consistent with other studies that report a lack of knowledge about the LCU among dentists (Al-Senan et al., 2021, Binalrimal et al., 2020, Kopperud et al., 2017, Santini and Turner, 2011).

The improved understanding among graduate/postgraduate dentists compared with bachelor's degree holders that the emission spectrum from the LCU must be compatible with the photoinitiators in the LCU highlights the value of advanced education. These findings highlight the need for continuous education to equip dental professionals with the technical knowledge to optimize restorative outcomes (Al-Zain et al., 2024, Price et al., 2020, Suliman et al., 2020).

Only 20 % of participants considered the photoinitiator absorption spectrum and the LCU emission spectrum compatibility important when selecting RBCs. Dentists who frequently used LCUs showed significantly greater knowledge of critical RBC selection parameters (p = 0.037), emphasizing the need for continuous training to enhance theoretical knowledge and practical skills. The importance of this compatibility for effective polymerization and restoration longevity is based on the Grotthuss–Draper law (First Law of Photocuring) (King and Laidler, 1984).

The study found that less experienced respondents reported higher knowledge of LCUs than more experienced counterparts, potentially reflecting the Dunning-Kruger effect, in which individuals with limited knowledge overestimate their competence (Canady and Larzo, 2023). This underscores the need for continuous education to maintain high standards of patient care. Graduate and postgraduate students had significantly greater knowledge about LCUs than those with a bachelor's degree, highlighting the impact of advanced education on understanding LCU parameters. Nearly half of the respondents gained LCU knowledge from undergraduate lectures, emphasizing the importance of formal education and comprehensive LCU training in dental curricula. Additionally, alternative strategies are needed to enhance LCU education further and reinforce theoretical knowledge.

The study revealed that participants across all educational levels and professional rankings preferred hands-on workshops, in-person lectures, and online lectures for learning about LCUs. Both novice and experienced dental professionals valued practical, real-time engagement in mastering technical skills, a preference that aligns with previous research highlighting a preference for face-to-face learning and direct consultation with experienced colleagues (Isham et al., 2016, Landry, 2006, Toema et al., 2024).

Most participants preferred lectures lasting no more than 30-minutes. This emphasized the need for concise and focused educational sessions and aligned with cognitive research suggesting that adult learners maintain optimal attention for 15–30 min (Middendorf and Kalish, 1996). Shorter lectures are more effective for information retention and fit better into busy schedules, facilitating continuous learning without interrupting clinical duties. Educational strategies should adopt brief and well-structured formats to improve engagement and knowledge retention when educating dental professionals about LCUs.

This study found that dental professionals prefer traditional and interactive learning methods over social media for learning about LCUs, likely due to skepticism about social media's reliability (Al-Obaidi, 2024, Gross et al., 2023). Respondents emphasized the importance of scientific articles and books, favoring evidence-based practice. While social media can Supplement education, foundational knowledge should be rooted in evidence-based sources.

This study found a significant preference among specialists and consultants for using YouTube for LCU education. Another study found similar results, stating that YouTube was the preferred educational method (Shah et al., 2024). The participants favored short videos (2–5 min) over long or live videos. This may highlight time constraints among practitioners and that concise content is more attractive (Hamasha et al., 2019, Isham et al., 2016). Platforms such as YouTube are effective educational dental resources as they align with current knowledge-seeking behaviors. However, caution should be taken when using social media to obtain scientific information and the viewer should ensure that only academic and scientifically validated sources are used. Currently, many videos on social media are the personal opinions of the presenter and thus represent the lowest form of scientific evidence.

Targeted education is needed to improve dentists' understanding of the relationship between the LCU and RBC, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes. Given the limited availability of hands-on workshops, future research should focus on broader representation and explore different strategies for keeping dentists updated on LCUs.

Conclusions

This study identified significant knowledge gaps among dentists, interns, and students when selecting which LCU and RBC to use. The preferred learning methods are hands-on workshops, in-person lectures, and concise YouTube content, emphasizing the need for practical and interactive learning. Educators should focus on brief, impactful training and prioritize evidence-based resources.
