Universality and Optimal Architectures for Layered Programmable Unitary Decompositions Preprint: APS/123-QED
Javier Álvarez-Vizoso, David Barral

Abstract
The decomposition of arbitrary unitary transformations into sequences of simpler, physically realizable operations is a foundational problem in quantum information science, quantum control, and linear optics. We establish a 1D Quantum Field Theory model for justifying the universality of a broad class of such factorizations. We consider parametrizations of the form U = D 1 V 1 D 2 V 2 ⋯ V M − 1 D M U=D_{1}V_{1}D_{2}V_{2}\cdots V_{M-1}D_{M} , where { D j } \{D_{j}\} are programmable diagonal unitary matrices and { V j } \{V_{j}\} are fixed mixing matrices. By leveraging concepts like the anomalies of our effective model, we establish criteria for universality given the set of mixer matrices. This approach yields a rigorous proof grounded on physics for the conditions required for the parametrization to cover the entire group of special unitary matrices. This framework provides a unified method to verify the universality of various proposed architectures and clarifies the nature of the “generic” mixers required for such constructions. We also provide a geometry-aware optimization method for finding the parameters of a decomposition.

Map of the problem to QFT.

We study when a physical architecture can generate arbitrary special unitary transformations. For
\mathrm{SU}(N)
matrices, we consider factorizations

U(\phi)=D_{1}(\phi)V_{1}D_{2}(\phi)V_{2}\cdots V_{M-1}D_{M}(\phi),

(1)

where
V_{j}
are fixed mixers and
D_{j}
are programmable diagonal unitaries with phases
\phi_{j,\mu}\in S^{1}
, forming a parameter space
\mathbb{T}^{k}
with
k=M(N-1)
.

The architecture is universal if the induced map
f:\mathbb{T}^{k}\to\mathrm{SU}(N)
is surjective. Since
\mathrm{SU}(N)
has dimension
N^{2}-1
, a necessary condition is
k\geq N^{2}-1
, i.e.,
M\geq N+1

(
Lee2012
, Cor. 6.11)
. When
k=N^{2}-1
, the solution set for a target matrix is generically finite
(
Lee2012
, Th. 5.12)
, while for
k>N^{2}-1
it forms a continuous manifold.

A direct proof of surjectivity is challenging. Instead, we adopt a 1-dimensional QFT approach to model our matrix
U(\phi)
as the
S
-matrix
\langle\text{out}|S|\text{in}\rangle
of a one-dimensional model
Brezin1978
;
Marino2005
. We introduce
N
-component complex vector fields
\psi_{j}
at discrete lattice sites
j=1,\dots,M
, see Fig.
1
. The dynamics are governed by a transfer matrix action constructed to reproduce the propagator-vertex structure of the original map: each mixer
V_{j}
component contributes as an interaction vertex and each phase from a diagonal factor
D_{j}
contributes as a propagator, both coupled by the indices of the matrix product summation in Eq. (
1
). The action linking adjacent sites is given by the inverse of the transfer matrix
T_{j}=V_{j}D_{j+1}(\phi)
:

S[\psi,\bar{\psi};\phi]=\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\bar{\psi}_{j+1}^{T}\left(V_{j}D_{j+1}(\phi)\right)^{-1}\psi_{j}.

This is a quadratic action where the mixer entries in
V_{j}
act as fixed coupling constants and the phases
\phi_{j,\mu}
in
D_{j}
are external parameters.

The generating functional for all
S
-matrix elements is the partition function in the presence of sources,
J_{in},J_{out}
, defined by the path integral over all intermediate fields, which yields

Z[J_{in},J_{out};\phi]\propto\exp\left(\bar{J}_{out}^{T}f(\phi)J_{in}\right).

The
S
-matrix elements are the 2-point correlation functions of this theory for a fixed set of external parameters
\phi
, recovered by functional differentiation (assuming usual normalization):

\langle b|S(\phi)|a\rangle=U_{ab}(\phi)=\frac{\delta^{2}Z}{\delta\bar{J}_{out,b}\delta J_{in,a}}\bigg|_{J=0}.

The universality of the factorization map
f
is a global property, determined by the full range of outcomes accessible by tuning the theory’s parameters
\{\phi\}
.
This question, however, is not about a single scattering event, but about the structure of the entire set of possible scattering events. To probe this structure, we must move from two-point functions to higher-order correlators that measure the relationships between different scattering channels, averaged over all possible internal dynamics. An effective field theory
Wilson1971
;
Wilson1971b
;
Weinberg1979
;
Weinberg2021
;
Zinn2021
, describes the low-energy behavior of a system by integrating out high-energy degrees of freedom. For our specific
1D
QFT model of the matrix factorization map, the effective field theory characterizes the statistical fluctuations of the
S
-matrix across the parameter space.

To construct the effective field theory

;
Wilson1971b
;
Weinberg1979
;
Weinberg2021
;
Zinn2021
, we introduce a background field
B_{\mu\nu}
coupled to the
S
-matrix elements
U_{\mu\nu}(\phi)=f(\phi)_{\mu\nu}
. The generating functional for connected correlation functions is:

Z[B]=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}\frac{d^{k}\phi}{(2\pi)^{k}}\exp\left(\mathrm{Tr}(B^{\dagger}U(\phi)+U^{\dagger}(\phi)B)\right).

The effective action
\Gamma_{\text{eff}}[B]
is the Legendre transform of
\log Z[B]
.
For small fluctuations around the mean (which vanishes due to phase averaging), we can expand to quadratic order but, to preserve the constraints, we must expand the source
B=\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}-1}B_{a}T^{a}
in an orthonormal basis

\mathfrak{su}(N)

\text{Tr}(T^{a}T^{b})=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{ab}

\Gamma_{\text{eff}}[B]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{N^{2}-1}B_{a}(C^{-1})_{ab}B_{b},

where

is the correlation matrix. Its entries physically measure the covariance between two different
S
-matrix elements, averaged over the entire parameter space:

\displaystyle C_{ab}=\frac{\partial^{2}\log Z[B]}{\partial B_{a}\partial B_{b}}\bigg|_{B=0}\!\!\!\!=\langle\mathrm{Tr}(T^{a}U(\phi))\text{Tr}(T^{b}U^{\dagger}(\phi))\rangle_{c}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\sum_{\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma=1}^{N}\!\!\!(T^{a})_{\nu\mu}(T^{b})_{\sigma\rho}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{k}}\frac{d^{k}\phi}{(2\pi)^{k}}U_{\mu\nu}(\phi)\overline{U_{\sigma\rho}(\phi).}

(2)

The diagonal entries of the integral measure the total power or variance of the
(\mu\nu)
scattering channel across all experimental tunings. Its off-diagonal entries measure the degree to which the fluctuations in the
(\mu\nu)
and
(\rho\sigma)

The covariance matrix
C
serves as a definitive test for universality. A universal system must be unconstrained, meaning no linear dependencies exist among its fundamental observables. The determinant of
C
—the Gram matrix of the
S
-matrix components—detects exactly this:
\det(C)\neq 0
ensures linear independence, indicating an anomaly-free, universal theory. If
\det(C)=0
, the matrix is singular, and any vector
v
in its null space corresponds to a composite operator
\mathcal{O}_{\text{eff}}=\sum v_{\mu\nu}f(\phi)_{\mu\nu}
with identically zero fluctuation
\langle|\mathcal{O}_{\text{eff}}|^{2}\rangle=v^{\dagger}Cv=0.

A non-trivial observable with zero variance is a zero mode

Ward1950
;
Takahashi1957
. From the perspective of the
1D
QFT, this corresponds to an accidental symmetry—a symmetry that emerges due to fine-tuning of the coupling constants (the mixer matrices
\{V_{j}\}
). When such a symmetry exists,
\det(C)=0
, the set of reachable
S
-matrices must be restricted to a non-symmetric subspace of
\mathrm{SU}(N)
, breaking the global symmetry of
\mathrm{SU}(N)
,
G=\mathrm{SU}(N)_{L}\times\mathrm{SU}(N)_{R},
given by the action
U\mapsto LUR^{\dagger}
. This is precisely the definition of a ’t Hooft anomaly
Wess1971
;
hooft1980recent
. Conversely, when
\det(C)\neq 0
, all
S
-matrix elements fluctuate independently, indicating the absence of accidental symmetries. The symmetry
G
is thus preserved. This forces the image of
f
, the set of all the accessible
S
-matrices from our model, to be
G
-invariant: since
G
acts transitively on
\mathrm{SU}(N)
, i.e., the action transforms any possible outcome
U_{1}
to any possible outcome
U_{2}
, the only
G
-invariant subset is
\mathrm{SU}(N)
itself. To prove this, take any matrix
Y
in the image, since the group-action is transitive, any other matrix
W
in
\mathrm{SU}(N)
can be reached via
LYR^{\dagger}
for some
L,R

f
is assumed
G

W
must also be in the image. Therefore, the image of

is all of
\mathrm{SU}(N)

universality is equivalent to anomaly freedom
.

The theoretical origin of the function

Global Riemannian Optimization Algorithm
. When a sequential peeling algorithm is not available, since for
M\sim N+1
all the phases are highly-coupled in any entry of the final matrix so a sequential isolated partial solver for each phase is unlikely to exist for
V_{j}
generic, the factorization parameters must be found by a global numerical search.
Standard optimization methods often minimize a cost function based on the Euclidean distance, such as
\|U(\phi)-U_{target}\|_{F}^{2}
using the Frobenius norm. However, a more natural and efficient approach is to work directly on the manifold of unitary matrices, minimizing the true geodesic distance
Absil2007
. This ensures the search proceeds along the shortest possible paths on the curved space of
\mathrm{SU}(N)
, leading to optimal on-manifold search for a solution. The algorithm seeks to find the tuple of all phases,
\phi
, that minimizes a cost function given by the squared geodesic distance between the factorization
U(\phi)
and the target matrix
U_{target}
.

Let
\Omega(\phi)=U(\phi)^{\dagger}U_{target}
. The cost function
F(\phi)
is the squared Frobenius norm of the matrix logarithm of
\Omega
:
F(\phi)=\|\log(\Omega(\phi))\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{k}^{2}
,
where
\lambda_{k}=e^{i\theta_{k}}
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
\Omega(\phi)
. This cost is zero if and only if
U(\phi)=U_{target}
.
Our cost function is the squared geodesic distance—the shortest path between our estimate and our target on the manifold itself. This is the most natural and direct measure of error for this problem. While algorithms like Levenberg-Marquardt, basin-hopping or L-BFGS
Zelaya2024
;
Saygin2020
;
Markowitz2023
;
Pereira2025
;
Zelaya2025
are powerful, best performance is supplied with an analytical Jacobian and, at least, finite-difference estimates of the Hessian from it. Our method provides the exact, analytical gradient of the true manifold distance:

\!\!\!\!\!\!(\nabla F)_{j,\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}2\theta_{k}\cdot\text{Im}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\left(\vec{v}_{k}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}\right)\vec{v}_{k}\right)\right],

(4)

where
\vec{v}_{k}
are the eigenvectors of
\Omega(\phi)
. This avoids the inaccuracies of numerical gradient estimation and exploits the full geometric structure of the problem every step.
For the partial derivatives of
\Omega
we need the standard Jacobian matrix (of matrices) of the factorization map
f
. We provide the explicit formulas needed in Appendices
C
and
D
.

Discussion and outlook.
In this work, we have established a complete mathematical framework for analyzing the universality of layered unitary decompositions of the form
U=D_{1}V_{1}D_{2}V_{2}\cdots V_{M-1}D_{M}
. Our contributions are threefold. First, we have provided a physical proof of the conditions for universality, moving beyond numerical evidence using concepts from QFT. Second, we have translated the vague requirement of “generic” mixers into a deterministic condition based on a matrix determinant computation that can certify any given architecture. Third, we have introduced a highly efficient, geometry-aware optimization algorithm that uses an analytical gradient to find factorization parameters by minimizing the true geodesic distance on the
\mathrm{SU}(N)
manifold.

Our framework provides definitive answers to several open questions and apparent paradoxes in the field: i)
Difficulties for a peeling algorithm:
unlike conventional architectures solvable with sequential “peeling” algorithms
Reck1994
;
Clements2016
, the MPLC architecture introduces a global, non-linear coupling of all phase variables. This entanglement is likely to preclude a layer-by-layer factorization in the minimum number of layers and necessitate the global optimization methods analyzed in this work. ii)
Confirmation of numerical results:
prior works, e.g.
Saygin2020
;
Zelaya2024
, have provided strong numerical evidence that
M\geq N+1
layers are sufficient for universality. Our work provides the first justification of this condition, confirming these numerical findings and precisely delineating the mathematical requirements on the mixing matrices for this to hold. iii)
Numerical failures:
Some studies report lack of numerical convergence for certain architectures, even with
N+1
layers
Pereira2025
. Our framework explains why. For the minimal depth
M=N+1
there is only a finite number of solutions expected, whereas for
M>N+1
there is a positive-dimensional submanifold of solutions. Thus, examples to factorize special limit cases such as reducible matrices form a proper lower-dimensional subvariety of measure zero, so lack of convergence to a solution serves as evidence of numerical ill-posedness but does not disprove universality. Moreover, anti-diagonal or reducible matrices often lie near symmetry loci where the Jacobian becomes nearly singular, even if not exactly singular so derivative-based optimization methods are expected to stall: Certain targets
U
may be close to the image of a critical point of the map
f
, making the local inverse highly sensitive.
iv)
Optimal mixers:
From our analysis we deduce that the entropy maximizer mixer of our model, and thus the mixer expected to be the most robust, is the DFT or complex Hadamard matrices (see Appendix
B
).

In summary, this work provides a robust theoretical foundation for the design, verification, and optimization of a broad and physically important class of programmable unitary devices with multiple applications

C
, is an
(N^{2}-1)\times(N^{2}-1)
matrix whose entries are given by:

\tilde{C}_{ab}=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu\nu}(T^{a})_{\nu\mu}(T^{b})_{\mu\nu},

where
\{T^{a}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{su}(N)
.

As an example, for
N=3,M=4
, the DFT matrix is:

V_{\mathrm{DFT}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&\omega&\omega^{2}\\
1&\omega^{2}&\omega\end{pmatrix},\quad\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}.

The squared-modulus matrix is
P_{\mathrm{DFT}}=\frac{1}{3}J
. Since
P_{\mathrm{DFT}}
is idempotent (
P_{\mathrm{DFT}}^{2}=P_{\mathrm{DFT}}
), the
M-1=3
step matrix is
Q=P_{\mathrm{DFT}}^{3}=P_{\mathrm{DFT}}
, i.e.,
Q_{\mu\nu}=(P_{\mathrm{DFT}}^{3})_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{3},\quad\text{ for all }\mu,\nu.

We now compute the physical correlation matrix
C_{\mathrm{DFT}}
:

\displaystyle(C_{\mathrm{DFT}})_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{3}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)(T^{a})_{\nu\mu}(T^{b})_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}(T^{a}T^{b}).

Using the standard
\mathfrak{su}(3)
normalization
\text{Tr}(T^{a}T^{b})=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{ab}
, we get:

(C_{\mathrm{DFT}})_{ab}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta^{ab}\right)=\frac{1}{6}\delta^{ab}

The determinant of this
8\times 8
matrix is:

\displaystyle\det(C_{\mathrm{DFT}})=\det\left(\frac{1}{6}\mathbf{I}_{8}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{8}=\frac{1}{1,679,616}

This is non-zero, confirming the map is universal.

Now consider the cyclic permutation matrix:

V_{\mathrm{perm}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The associated stochastic matrix is
P_{\mathrm{perm}}=|V_{\mathrm{perm}}|^{2}=V_{\mathrm{perm}}
.
The
M-1=3
step matrix is
Q=P_{\mathrm{perm}}^{3}
. Since
P_{\mathrm{perm}}
is a permutation matrix representing a 3-cycle,
P_{\mathrm{perm}}^{3}=\mathbf{I}
(the identity matrix).
Thus, we have
Q_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
We compute the physical correlation matrix
C_{\mathrm{perm}}
similarly as above:

(C_{\mathrm{perm}})_{ab}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}(T^{a})_{\mu\mu}(T^{b})_{\mu\mu}

This matrix correlates only the diagonal entries of the generators. For
\mathfrak{su}(3)
, only
T^{3}
and
T^{8}
have non-zero diagonal entries. All other generators (
a=1,2,4,5,6,7
) are purely off-diagonal.
For any
a\in\{1,2,4,5,6,7\}
, we have
(T^{a})_{\mu\mu}=0
for all
\mu
. This implies:

(C_{\mathrm{perm}})_{ab}=0\quad\text{if }a\text{ or }b\in\{1,2,4,5,6,7\}.

The resulting
8\times 8
matrix
C_{\mathrm{perm}}
has at least 6 all-zero rows and columns. Therefore, its determinant is exactly zero. This mixer is not universal, as its correlation matrix is singular.

It is then advisable, when designing mixers, to normalize
\det(C)
with respect to
\det(C_{\text{DFT}})
to measure “how numerically far” from universality they may be.

Application to Clements factorization.

As shown in Figs. 1 and 4 of ref.
Kuzmin2021
, Clements factorization
Clements2016
can be “visually” mapped to a MPLC factorization. We demonstrate the universality of this map to Eq. (
1
) in
M=2N+1
layers using the criterion of Eq. (
3
). We exemplify it for
N=3
.

For
N=3
, the factorization is built from two types of mixers:

V_{\mathrm{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&i/\sqrt{2}&0\\
i/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad V_{\mathrm{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{2}&i/\sqrt{2}\\
0&i/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The total transition probability matrix
Q
for the
M-1=6
mixer sequence
P_{1}\cdot P_{1}\cdot P_{2}\cdot P_{2}\cdot P_{1}\cdot P_{1}
simplifies to
Q=P_{1}^{2}P_{2}^{2}P_{1}^{2}=P_{1}P_{2}P_{1}
.

Q=\frac{1}{8}\begin{pmatrix}3&3&2\\
3&3&2\\
2&2&4\end{pmatrix}.

To test for universality requires computing the determinant of the
8\times 8
physical correlation matrix
C_{ab}
. Due to the block-symmetric structure of
Q
and the structure of the
\mathfrak{su}(3)
generators
\{T^{a}\}
,
C
is block-diagonal. Its determinant is the product of the determinants of these blocks: Block
\{T^{1},T^{2},T^{3}\}
(in
1,2
subspace):
\det(C_{1-3})=(3/16)^{3}
; block
\{T^{4},T^{5}\}
(in
1,3
subspace):
\det(C_{4,5})=(1/8)^{2}
; block
\{T^{6},T^{7}\}
(in
2,3
subspace):
\det(C_{6,7})=(1/8)^{2}
; block
\{T^{8}\}
(diagonal):
\det(C_{8})=11/48
.
The determinant of the correlation matrix is:

\det(C_{\text{Clements}})=\left(\frac{3}{16}\right)^{3}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{11}{48}\right)=\frac{99}{2^{28}}

Since
\det(C_{\text{Clements}})\neq 0
analytically, the criterion is satisfied. This correctly shows that the Clements factorization is universal. It is a type of MPLC factorization with
N^{2}-1
phase shifts distributed in
2N+1
layers, interleaved with a specific family of sparse mixers in
2N
layers. Note that the sparsity of the mixers is compensated by an increase in the number of layers. Thus, Clements’ peeling algorithm remains a composable factorization with minimum depth for this type of map.

Entropy maximization.

One would like to find the mixer
V
that maximizes the one-loop partition function
Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\propto\sqrt{\det(C)}
. This is equivalent to maximizing
\log\det(C)
. This is a constrained optimization problem, as the entries of
P
must satisfy the doubly stochastic constraints (
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\beta}p_{\alpha\beta}=1
). Solving this optimization using Lagrange multipliers for the constraints shows that the constrained derivative vanishes precisely when the derivative
\partial\log\det(C)/\partial p_{\alpha\beta}
is a constant independent of
\alpha,\beta
. This condition is met when
P
is the flat matrix
P_{ab}=1/N
. This condition corresponds to maximizing the Shannon entropy of the classical transitions. The unique doubly stochastic matrix that achieves this maximum is the flat matrix
P_{ab}=1/N
, which is generated by any complex Hadamard matrix, such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) mixer
V_{\mathrm{DFT}}
with
|(V_{\mathrm{DFT}})_{ab}|=1/\sqrt{N}
.
At this optimal point, the correlation matrix becomes isotropic. As we saw in the
N=3
DFT example,
Q=\frac{1}{N}J
, which leads to
C_{ab}\propto\text{Tr}(T^{a}T^{b})\propto\delta_{ab}
. This means
C
is diagonal and proportional to the identity matrix,
C\propto\mathbf{I}
. This minimizes the condition number and maximizes robustness. Consequently, the DFT mixer, by setting the constrained derivative of
\log\det(C)
to zero, maximizes the one-loop partition function and provides the largest margin of anomaly freedom—making it the optimal choice for stable, universal control.

Appendix C

Partial Derivatives of the Factorization.

The entries of the Jacobian matrix (of matrices) of the factorization map
f
have a physical observable interpretation in our QFT, as the expectation value of an operator insertion
\mathcal{O}_{j,\alpha}=-\frac{\partial S}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}
,
i.e., the derivative of the
S
-matrix itself is the transition amplitude of the observable
\mathcal{O}_{j,\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\langle b|\mathcal{O}_{j,\alpha}|a\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}\frac{\delta^{2}Z}{\delta\bar{J}_{out,b}\delta J_{in,a}}\bigg|_{J=0}=\frac{\partial\langle b|S|a\rangle}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}.

Every Jacobian matrix row is hence given by the matrices:

[J_{f}]_{j,\alpha}=\frac{\partial U(\phi)}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}=[\mathcal{O}_{j,\alpha}]=L_{j}D_{j}X_{j\alpha}R_{j}.

(5)

The columns of the Jacobian are the set of the theory’s “probed” 3-point correlation functions, representing the system’s fundamental linear responses. Here,
L_{j}
is the product of all matrix factors to the left of
D_{j}
:
L_{j}=D_{1}V_{1}D_{2}V_{2}\cdots V_{j-2}D_{j-1}V_{j-1},
and
R_{j}
is the product of all matrix factors to the right of
D_{j}
:
R_{j}=V_{j}D_{j+1}V_{j+1}\cdots V_{M-1}D_{M}.
And where
X_{j\alpha}
is the corresponding
\mathfrak{su}(N)
Lie algebra generator. It is a diagonal, anti-hermitian, and, for
\mathrm{SU}(N)
, traceless matrix, given by

X_{j\alpha}=i(E_{j\alpha,j\alpha}-E_{(j,N),(j,N)}),

where
E_{ab}
is the standard matrix basis element that is 1 at row
a
and column
b
and 0 elsewhere.
Each column of the Jacobian matrix corresponds to a derivative with respect to a single independent phase
\phi_{j,\alpha}
, This column is an
N\times N
matrix representing the probed correlation function.

Appendix D

Gradient of the Global Riemannian Optimization Algorithm.
The main challenge is computing the gradient of the cost function
F(\phi)
. While differentiating the matrix logarithm is complex, we can find the gradient by focusing on the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=e^{i\theta_{k}}
of the error matrix
\Omega
. The core of our method relies on an elegant result from matrix perturbation theory for differentiating eigenvalues
(
Magnus1985
, Th. 2)
, a technique not commonly used in optimization contexts but which provides an exact analytical gradient “almost everywhere”, yielding optimal performance.

Let
\Omega(t)
be a family of matrices depending smoothly on a parameter
t
. If at
t=0
,
\Omega(0)
has a simple eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
with corresponding orthonormal eigenvector
\vec{v}_{0}
, then the derivative of the eigenvalue is given by:

\frac{d\lambda}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}=\vec{v}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{d\Omega}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\right)\vec{v}_{0}.

The cost function is
F(\phi)=\sum_{k}\theta_{k}^{2}
. Using the chain rule, its partial derivative with respect to a phase parameter
\phi_{j,\alpha}
is

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}=\sum_{k}2\theta_{k}\frac{\partial\theta_{k}}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}.

We can relate the derivative of the phase
\theta_{k}=\text{Im}[-i\log(\lambda_{k})]
to the derivative of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
and apply the lemma:

\frac{\partial\theta_{k}}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}=\text{Im}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\frac{\partial\lambda_{k}}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}\right]=\text{Im}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\left(\vec{v}_{k}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}\vec{v}_{k}\right)\right].

This gives the final, explicit formula for the components of the gradient, Eq. (
4
).
Substituting

\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi_{j,\alpha}}\right)^{\dagger}U_{target}

gives a complete formula for the gradient, where the partial derivatives are just our Jacobian entries studied in Eq. (
5
).

The optimization proceeds by iteratively taking steps in the direction of the negative gradient from a random initial vector of phases
\phi_{0}
. The algorithm terminates when the value of the cost function
C(\theta_{k})
is below a desired tolerance, indicating that
U(\theta_{k})
is sufficiently close to
U_{target}
.

The Fidelity Cost Function and its Gradient.
Another numerically efficient and physically meaningful objective for optimizing the factorization
U(\phi)
towards a target
U_{\text{target}}
is the infidelity. It is defined based on the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, which measures the overlap between the two matrices.
The fidelity between two unitary matrices is given by
\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}[\operatorname{Tr}(U_{\text{target}}^{\dagger}U(\phi))]
. A value of
\mathcal{F}=1
signifies a perfect match. The cost function to be minimized is the infidelity,
\mathcal{I}(\vec{\phi})=1-\mathcal{F}=1-\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}[\operatorname{Tr}(U_{\text{target}}^{\dagger}U(\phi))].

Figure 3:
(a) Plateaus of high fidelity (blue) optimization for the mixer
V=\exp(-iHz)
with
H_{k,k+1}=0.25
,
N=4
,
M=5
, and varying parameter
z
. The maximum value of
\det(C)
(red) clearly distinguishes the feasibility region. (b) Robustness analysis of the fidelity for a fixed optimized phases solution against perturbations
\epsilon
around
H_{k,k+1}=0.25
for
N=10
,
M=11
, and
z=15
. The original phases are not re-optimized but the mixers are perturbed. The
\epsilon
used in each simulation is sampled from a normal distribution centered in
H_{k,k+1}
with standard deviation
\Delta\epsilon
.

To find the direction of steepest descent, we compute the gradient of
\mathcal{I}(\phi)
with respect to each phase parameter
\phi_{r,s}
(the
s
-th phase in the
r
-th diagonal matrix
D_{r}
). Using the linearity of the trace and derivative operators, we have:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{I}}{\partial\phi_{r,s}}=-\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(U_{\text{target}}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi_{r,s}}\right)\right].

Substituting Eq. (
5
) back into the gradient formula yields the explicit expression for each component:

(\nabla\mathcal{I})_{r,s}=-\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(X_{r,s}R_{r}U_{\text{target}}^{\dagger}L_{r}D_{r}\right)\right].

While minimizing the squared Frobenius norm,
||U(\vec{\phi})-U_{\text{target}}||_{F}^{2}
, is a valid approach, the infidelity cost function is often superior for this class of problems. Its gradient calculation is computationally simpler and avoids terms involving
U(\phi)^{\dagger}
, which can be complex to differentiate.
