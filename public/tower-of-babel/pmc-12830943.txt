Exploiting a centrally powered coherent microcomb for lightweight optical transmission
Authors: Junhao Han, Guofeng Yan, Kang Li, Bitao Shen, Haowen Shu, Yimeng Wang, Yuchen Zhang, Jiong Xiao, Yichen Wu, Huajin Chang, Chengkun Cai, Xuguang Zhang, Xingjun Wang, Jian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe exponential growth of data capacity in intelligent terminals drives higher data traffic toward network edges. Compact I/O systems are essential to support space-constrained infrastructures at the computing edges or modular data centers. However, scaling high-capacity transmission via increasing physical channels is constrained by limited source coherence and low carrier-to-noise ratios (OCNR), hindering lightweight, efficient applications like distributed edge computing. Here, we exploit an integrated self-injection-locked dark-pulse microcomb to achieve 1 Tbps/λ/core transmission and characterize the constraints among OCNR, linewidth, and transmission rate. Furthermore, a multi-dimensional transmission architecture for multi-nodes aggregation is explored, boosting the transmission rate to 200 Tbps with 16 comblines at 70 Gbaud. Combining with integrated waveshapers and semiconductor optical amplifiers, a chip-level parallel carrier generator is explored, reducing system size a hundredfold while delivering 5 Tbps. Our results highlight significant potential for compact and resource-conserving transmission systems in data centers and distributed high performance computing applications.Subject terms: Integrated optics, Fibre optics and optical communications

Introduction

With the explosive growth of artificial intelligence (AI) and the internet of things (IoT)1, the demand for efficient data transmission within the network has reached unprecedented levels. In addition to backbone network communications, the devices at the network edges, required to perform real-time monitoring2 and high accuracy sensing3, are also expected to simultaneously transmit massive information across distributed nodes or back to the data center for system coordination4. Recent advances in optical cloud computing architectures have demonstrated significant potential for enabling AI workload offloading to edge networks5, with experimental validation confirming seamless integration between distributed edge nodes and centralized cloud computing infrastructure across metropolitan-area optical networks. To meet these challenges, compact data links with lightweight infrastructures capable of supporting high throughput have become more essential6.

Integrated photonics can provide high-density data link solutions. Despite the achievement of ultra-high capacity integrated parallel transmission systems, conventional schemes still struggle to deliver the required data throughput while maintaining compact structure and energy efficiency in space-limited and power-constrained scenarios. The challenges stem from two main aspects. Firstly, it is difficult for wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) parallel light sources to obtain both low noise and compact forms simultaneously. While distributed feedback (DFB) laser diode arrays have been commercially successful7,8, they exhibit limited phase noise and complexity in synergistic frequency control. Quantum dot mode-locked lasers9–13 can generate multiple channels with a single laser diode, but the inherent high phase noise reduces the maximum transmission rate for coherent communication. Other integrated multiwavelength sources, such as electro-optical combs14–16 and Kerr microcombs17–21, relying on high-power, high-purity optical or microwave sources for low-noise wideband comb generation, which results in bulky and costly setups for edge nodes. Self-injection locked (SIL) Kerr microcombs offer high-coherent multiwavelength sources with a laser diode directly coupled to a high-quality optical nonlinear microcavity. However, the relatively low combline powers of microcombs degrade the OCNR with the optical amplification, limiting the data rates per channel. Optical-injection amplified combs22,23 have been proposed to avoid the OCNR degeneration, while the employment of laser diode arrays hinders their application for lightweight systems.

Another issue lies in the subsequent system. As the channel count often spans multiple optical bands (S/C/L) for high-capacity integrated optical demonstrations, the link setup of each wavelength, comprising modulators, amplifiers, and passive couplers, grows dramatically in real-world deployments. This increase occurs not only due to the need to scale the same devices within each band24, but also the differing design structures and operating principles required across bands25. The use of excessive wavelengths in high-capacity transmission will further exacerbate nonlinear signal crosstalk, which may degrade overall signal quality and limit system throughput, particularly in tightly packed wavelength grids26,27. Therefore, merely increasing the number of physical channels in the system makes it difficult to realize lightweight, cost-effective high-capacity signal transmission. To date, although achieves over 1 Pbps high-throughput transmission28, wavelength resources are often underutilized, resulting in reduced communication conversion efficiency and increased device overhead. Ideally, a compact transmission system at edge nodes needs a parallel light source covering a single band with high coherence and adequate OCNR. Such a source is essential to maximize single-channel capacity, enable multi-dimensional multiplexing for even higher aggregate rates, and tolerate the performance degradation inherent in lightweight systems.

In this work, we achieve high-capacity optical transmission and a streamlined system architecture by employing an integrated SIL comb source. The highly integrated source provides both high coherence with ~40 dB frequency noise suppression and high per-channel power with >60% on-chip conversion efficiency. Among 16 channels within the C-band, each channel exhibits an intrinsic linewidth below 600 Hz with power exceeding −10 dBm. To establish an overarching framework for our study, we perform a detailed investigation of the constraints among OCNR, linewidth, and achievable transmission rate. The experimental results indicate that a narrow linewidth <1 kHz is essential for elevated Gbaud single-channel transmission in our system, positioning the SIL microcomb as a key enabler of high-capacity lightweight systems. We experimentally demonstrated short-reach campus/edge networks and metropolitan area network transmission separately. In the campus/edge networks, we achieve a transmission capacity of 1 Tbps/λ across all carriers using a self-homodyne system. Furthermore, we perform multi-dimensional multiplexing by incorporating with WDM methods across various schemes, such as spatial mode, linear polarization (LP) mode, and orbital angular momentum (OAM), with the highest baud rate in comb-based transmission at 70 Gbaud. Specifically, in the WDM-spatial division multiplexing (SDM) scheme, the maximum transmission capacity exceeds 200 Tbps using only 16 high-quality comb carriers in the C-band. This reduces both wavelength resource requirements and channel count by more than ten times compared to previous demonstrations with the same capacity. Finally, for more compact deployment scenarios, a chip-level multicarrier generator is demonstrated, replacing bulky erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) and wavelength-selective switches (WSS) with integrated semiconductor optical amplifiers and silicon photonic waveshapers. Contributing to the original narrow linewidth and high OCNR, optical carriers provided by the SIL microcomb can tolerate the higher noise figures and insertion losses of integrated devices. The proposed scheme shrinks the system size by a hundredfold with an aggregation rate of 5 Tbps. By fully exploiting the integrated parallel high-coherence optical carriers, the proposed transmission scheme paves the way for high-capacity, ultra-lightweight solutions in next-generation edge computing networks (Fig. 1).

Results

Light source effects on transmission system

To fulfill the demand for a compact, highly coherent source, the SIL microcomb scheme is implemented for frequency noise compression and carrier-to-noise enhancement in this work, as shown in Fig. 2b. A laser diode chip, which exhibits a wall-plug efficiency of 13.45%, is directly coupled with the designed silicon nitride (SiN) microresonator chip. The output spectrum from the laser chip through the SiN chip to the fiber link is given in Fig. 2a, with the output wavelength around 1555.37 nm and the output power of 14.86 dBm. Considering the laser diode direct output power of 18 dBm, the total optical insertion loss is estimated as 3.14 dB. The SiN microresonator consists of a rectangular waveguide with a height of 300 nm and a width of 3 μm for both high-quality and normal dispersion conditions. Figure 2c shows one of the measured resonances around 1555.37 nm, where the loaded quality factor is measured as 1.7 × 107. Such high quality factor is significant for microcomb generation with limited optical power without amplification, and for noise suppression with self-injection locking. In this way, both frequency noise and spontaneous emission noise can be minimized. Figure 2d exhibits the generated coherent microcomb. Thanks to the normal dispersion condition, the spectrum is highly concentrated within C-band with high conversion efficiency. For adequate OCNR, we select the central 16 comblines with power exceeding −10 dBm as parallel optical carriers. The on-chip conversion efficiency from the pump laser to the 15 generated comblines (excluding the residual pump laser) is calculated to be 61.5%, with the center 16 high-power comblines accounting for ~99.7% of the converted energy. In addition, the frequency noise of the generated comblines is lower than the free-running laser, where the noise is suppressed by ~40 dB for the pump laser. Due to the repetition rate fluctuation, the combline coherence degenerates as apart from the pump laser. Owing to the self-injection locking, all 16 comblines express the intrinsic linewidth below 600 Hz.

To quantitatively verify the necessity of such high-performance sources for high-capacity transmission, we conduct a systematic investigation of the impact of linewidth and OCNR on optical communication systems under back-to-back (BTB) conditions. The experimental link transmits DP-16QAM signals as described in the Methods, wherein a Keysight N7714A external cavity laser (ECL) with 10-kHz linewidth serves as the local oscillator (LO). Optical carrier linewidths are varied from 1 Hz to 10 MHz, provided by different commercial lasers and comblines of the SIL microcomb. The OCNR is adjusted by controlling the laser intensity via a variable optical attenuator. Intuitively, high-speed transmission systems require coordinated optimization of both OCNR and laser linewidth characteristics. While the degradation of OCNR critically impacts transmission, light sources with superior coherence properties, could partially alleviate such degradation. With narrow linewidth (200 Hz for example), an OCNR of ~45 dB suffices to achieve transmission rate exceeding 100GBaud, where a higher OCNR provides negligible improvement. Importantly, maintaining a linewidth <1 kHz is essential to achieve 1 Tbps in our system, as coherent receivers exhibit heightened sensitivity to phase noise under high-speed signal conditions. As coherence progressively deteriorates, the accumulated phase noise not only elevates the bit error rate (BER) at the receiver but also disrupts frequency offset estimation in the offline digital signal processing. This impairment leads to failed carrier phase recovery and ultimately complete system failure.

Parallel node-to-node data transfer

Currently implemented distributed edge computing systems primarily focus on short-reach campus/edge networks and metropolitan area network transmission, and avoid excessive hardware overhead to maintain edge computing capabilities. Thus, a high single-carrier capacity facilitates efficient data aggregation with minimal scaling requirements. To validate this, parallel wavelength multiplexing data transmission is performed, employing the central 16 comblines. The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 3a. SIL comblines are divided into odd and even banks to simulate real-world communication scenarios. First, we build up a 1 km self-hemodyne coherent transmission system to explore the maximum capacity of the comelines. Limited by the channel spacing of 100 GHz, we artificially doubled the spectral interval through WSS, as shown in Fig. 3b, and distinguish the channels by their transparency. Thanks to the high OCNR with power levels above −10 dBm, all channels can support 110 GBaud DP-32QAM signal transmissions in the WDM configuration, indicating a single-channel rate of 1.1 (net 1.008) Tbps below the 9.1% forward error correction (FEC) threshold with a BER of 7 × 10⁻³.

Despite self-hemodyne coherent system can support ultrahigh transmission rate, it is not suitable for metropolitan area network transmission systems, considering the requirements of precise fiber-length alignments and extra channels for LO transmission. Thus, the transmission results based on the homodyne coherent scheme are also provided in this section. The symbol rate is limited to 70 Gbaud due to constraints from the power level and linewidth characteristics of the edge comblines, while simultaneously satisfying requirements for fiber transmission loss and component insertion losses. DP-16QAM signals are then modulated onto the parallel carriers, which are subsequently combined and transmitted through a 160-km standard single mode fiber (SMF). At the receiver end, the LO source and transmitted comblines are detected and mixed by a coherent receiver. Overall, this 16-channel WDM scheme achieves a raw (net) aggregated rate of 8.96 (8.37) Tbps below the 7% hard decision (HD)-FEC BER threshold.

Extending beyond previous work showing minimal degradation from a soliton microcomb verses a 10 kHz narrow-linewidth ECL18, we demonstrate a substantial improvement in transmission quality by introducing the SIL scheme here. Figure 3d shows the error vector magnitude (EVM) results for each carrier in WDM transmission with DP formats. Compared with a high-performance tunable ECL1 with a ~10 kHz linewidth (gray dots), the self-injection locking scheme (purple dots) exhibits a 3 dB lower OSNR requirement at the same EVM level due to the high coherence. Figure 3e compares the ECL1 and the 1550.72 nm combline (−6 channel) for 160-km transmission. The SIL comb with better noise performance delivers superior BER levels. The OSNR values at a BER of 3.8 × 10−3 are calculated for the −6, +6, −3, +3, and 0 channels and compared with ECL at the same wavelength, as illustrated in Fig. 3f. This comparison also validates the lower OSNR requirements for the narrower linewidth carriers. It also can be seen that the OSNR penalty difference between the referenced ECL carrier and the narrow-linewidth comblines increases from 1 dB at BTB transmission to 3 dB after 160-km. This demonstrates during long-haul transmission, the pulse-like transmission format of the microcomb solution can better resist nonlinear distortions, potentially optimizing signal quality compared to traditional WDM schemes29, hinting at superior performance compared to commercial ECLs.

Multi-dimensional transmission aggregation

SDM transmission over a field-deployed fiber has been practically demonstrated recently30. Multiplexing dimensions31, including spatial, LP mode, and OAM mode, hold promising prospects in next-generation edge networks. Such cascaded multiplexing schemes can aggregate information from distributed nodes operating with the same standardized wavelength configuration. Compared to continuously expanding the wavelength channels, this approach results in a more compact and cost-effective system architecture.

To simulate the aforementioned multi-node aggregation scenario, a 2-km 24-core multi-core fiber (MCF) with a mode field diameter of 9 μm was used. The crosstalk of 24-core MCF is ~28 dB for nearest neighbors, ~49 dB for next-nearest neighbors, and ~62 dB for next-next-nearest neighbors and beyond, as shown in Fig. 4b. In this case, we achieve a raw (net) transmission rate of 215.04 (200.97) Tbps below the 7% HD-FEC BER threshold by loading 70 Gbaud DP-16QAM signals, with only 16 wavelength channels, as shown in Fig. 4c. As far as we are aware, this represents the highest rate realized with an on-chip light source over the entire C-band, significantly simplifying the cumulative device requirements while avoiding saturation of the EDFAs.

Besides, we show the first proof-of-concept mode-division multiplexed (MDM) multi-dimensional transmissions includes LP modes in a few-mode fiber (FMF) and OAM modes in a ring-core fiber (RCF) driven by the integrated photonic light source. Two modes are considered here for each scheme, where FMF supports the LP01 and LP02 and RCF supports OAM11 and OAM21, as shown in Fig. 4a. Nonlinear losses and crosstalk across all channels are measured in both schemes, as shown in Fig. 4d. Although higher-order modes exist more crosstalk, the fibers maintain inter-coupling below −15 dB, making them suitable for seamless linking with the front WDM for boosted capacity. Figure 4e shows the WMD-MDM transmission results for LP01 and LP02 mode carrying 40-Gbaud DP-16QAM signals transmitted over a 25-km FMF, with raw (net) capacity of 10.24 (9.57) Tbps. Compared to back-to-back transmission, MDM signals incur an additional OSNR penalty of ~6 dB after transmission over 25-km FMF. OAM modes are multiplexed and demultiplexed by a dedicated functional device32 operating in C-band, with over 95% generating purity and <3 dB insertion loss. Similarly, the WDM-MDM transmission performance of 16 comblines carrying 70-Gbaud SP-16QAM signals with raw (net) rate reaching 8.96 (8.37) Tbps in a 50-m RCF using OAM11 and OAM21 modes, as shown in Fig. 4f. It is observed that MDM transmission of OAM modes in this scenario brings about 8-dB OSNR penalty around 7% HD-FEC threshold, compared with BTB transmission. The results validate the feasibility of microcomb-based multi-dimensional cascaded multiplexing schemes using passive specialty fiber links, offering a budget-friendly solution without the need for additional wavelength-dependent active devices.

Highly integrated lightweight transmission

Under stringent cost and footprint constraints, bulky equipment impedes system integration, particularly for achieving lightweight transmission in distributed edge nodes. In this section, beyond integrated light sources, we present a chip-level parallel carrier generator where bulky WSSs and EDFAs are replaced by SOAs and silicon photonic integrated waveshaping chips (IWCs). Figure 5a illustrates the architecture of the chip-level transmitter module, with photographs of all modules provided in Fig. 5b. The IWC is fabricated using mature silicon photonics technology, featuring cascaded microring resonators with a free spectral range (FSR) of ~1.3 THz. Microheaters placed above the microrings allow tuning of resonant frequencies to adjust the output spectrum, as detailed in Supplementary Information Note 5. To suppress thermal crosstalk, adjacent microrings are physically separated by etched trenches, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. Since not all comblines require power equalization, we achieve uniform power distribution across all spectral lines using only 12 microrings. After the IWC, all 16 comb lines are equalized to −18 dBm per carrier, accounting for an 8 dB insertion loss. All carriers are then amplified by an SOA to a total output power of 18 dBm and routed through another IWC for odd-even channel separation. The separated carriers are modulated by 35-GHz-bandwidth DP-IQ modulators (which introduce 20 dB of insertion loss), combined, and then amplified by an SOA to an output power of 2 dBm before transmission. As demonstrated in Fig. 5e, a 10-km transmission at 5.12 Tbps (net 5.015 Tbps, 5 channels under 7% HD-FEC threshold condition) is achieved using 40-GBaud DP-16QAM signals. The BER trend per channel aligns consistently with the linewidth values as shown in Fig. 5d, with comblines below 300 Hz linewidth achieving error-free operation.

It is noteworthy that the capacity of the present chip-level transmitter is lower than that of systems based on bulky devices. This primarily stems from carrier performance degradation caused by integrated components, which feature higher noise figures and insertion losses than their benchtop counterparts. Specifically, the IWC contributes an additional 3 dB insertion loss compared to benchtop WSS. SOAs, while far more compact than EDFAs, introduce excessive amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) noise. Meanwhile, their high α-factor and nonlinear effects (notably XPM) further induce cumulative phase noise and waveform distortion in high-speed systems33,34. Figure 5f presents the corresponding noise degradation after an SOA amplification. When amplified to 10 dBm, the linewidth of channel 6 increases by approximately threefold and tenfold via EDFA and SOA respectively. After two SOAs, IWC and modulators, the OCNR decreases by 13 dB at the 2 dBm output operating point, as shown in Fig. 5g. We find that when the input optical carrier has an OCNR < 25 dB, the ASE noise from the dual SOAs completely overwhelms the carrier, rendering it incapable of supporting signal transmission. Despite these impairments, the SIL light source still enables >5 Tbps transmission within the chip-level lightweight system, due to its original superior coherence and OCNR. For comparison, we implement a reference system using a 45-GHz-spacing electro-optic frequency comb pumped by an N7714A ECL (Fig. 5d). Under identical conditions, severely degraded constellation diagrams are obtained due to its poor original coherence (Fig. 5e black pentagrams).

Discussion

In general, by exploiting spectral energy and coherence in the parallel light source, we propose a compact integrated photonic solution aimed at lightweight and high capacity network interconnection. Moreover, the concentrated power conversion within target band provides a sufficient carrier-to-noise ratio for parallel channels, enabling multidimensional multiplexing in frequency and spatial domain at high rate operation. This allows transmission capacity to scale to hundreds of terabits using only 16 optical channels operating at a peak baud rate of 70 Gbaud. This miniaturized light source holds significant promise for deployment in edge computing node networks, compared to current transmission systems primarily designed for backbone transmission, as shown in Fig. 6. Owing to the high conversion efficiency and coherence of each combline, the highest single channel net data rates of 1 Tbps/(λ·core) and 523 Gbps/(λ·core) are achieved in WDM and WDM-SDM schemes, respectively. It means that the single-carrier rate of comb-based transmission has reached the level of high-power bulk lasers35. Although the output power of the integrated frequency comb remains lower than that of traditional bulk laser arrays, this limitation is compensated during transmission by the comb’s high conversion efficiency and narrow linewidth characteristics. Compared to other integrated multiwavelength light sources, our approach demonstrates superior coherence and noise performance while achieving the highest level of integration - requiring neither bulk laser drivers nor microwave excitation. To accommodate distributed edge computing nodes, we further implemented IWCs and SOAs as cost-effective replacements for conventional WSSs and EDFAs, achieving transmission capacities exceeding 5 Tbps while dramatically reducing both physical footprint and implementation costs. Although the total capacity is below the petabit-per-second scale compared to state-of-the-art demonstrations, a higher channel capacity and streamlined nature of this architecture would significantly reduce the footprint and overhead associated with channel scaling, making it well-suited for compact edge infrastructure.

Additional efforts could be taken to further optimize and enhance the system’s performance. Due to the limitations of the modulator bandwidth, our modulation format is limited to 70 Gbaud, which mismatches the 100 GHz combline FSR, resulting in reduced spectral efficiency. The higher baud transmission adopted in the text requires more complex DSP (Details in Methods). With the advanced integrated modulator design36,37, a single-channel baud rate exceeding 100 Gbaud could seamlessly operate with the proposed light source, enabling fully spectral resource utilization under limited DSP overhead.

Additionally, despite high conversion efficiency, the comb power is still constrained by the limited pump power, making it challenging to adopt higher-order modulation formats, which can be mitigated by further optimization in coupling loss38,39 and integrated optical amplifier with high output power and low noise figure40. Advancing towards enhanced system integration, our architecture incorporates both multidimensional on-chip multiplexers41,42 and chip-scale fiber-to-chip couplers43,44, establishing a comprehensive and space-efficient multiplexing architecture. Extending this approach to the receiver side is equally feasible, where the proposed chip-level parallel optical source can serve as local oscillators in combination with integrated photodetectors and demultiplexers45. The co-integration of transmitters and receivers would enable a lightweight platform for bidirectional transmission. Furthermore, higher cascade level of multiplexing techniques46 could further increase the total capacity, boosting the transmission capacity towards the scale of tens of petabits. To summarize, the proposed system allows low hardware overhead while meeting high-capacity transmission demands, providing lightweight solution for future greener and higher-density compute-and-transport architectures.

Methods

Detailed characterization of microcomb

The silicon nitride resonator was fabricated with a standard integrated photonic foundry process. A 300 nm silicon nitride layer (refractive index 1.95 @ 1550 nm) is encapsulated by highly transparent silica cladding, with the bottom oxide layer thickness of 4.4 µm and the top oxide layer of 3.5 µm. The silicon substrate is 725 µm thick. To achieve low loss and adequate dispersion, the microresonator waveguide width was designed to be 3 µm, with the bus waveguide of 1 µm. The fabricated microresonator exhibits a free spectral range of 100 GHz (aligned with the DWDM ITU grid spacing standard), a Q-factor of ~1.7 × 10⁷, and a coupling loss of ≤2.5 dB/facet @ 1550 nm. The microring resonator adopts a racetrack configuration, with Euler bends on both curved sections. The coupling gap is 1 μm, and the coupling length is 110 μm, forming an overall weakly overcoupled structure.

Our DFB laser outputs ~18 dBm, which is reduced to 14.86 dBm after traversing the microresonator. The DFB laser was mounted on a six-axis translation stage (Thorlabs MAX602D/M) and controlled by a benchtop laser diode driver (Thorlabs ITC4001). The SiN microresonator, maintained at room temperature, was placed on a three-axis translation stage (Thorlabs MAX313D). Alignment between the DFB laser and the SiN microresonator is achieved using a piezoelectric controller to adjust the six-axis stage. The output was collected by lensed fibers, with 1% of the light directed to an optical spectrum analyzer (Yokogawa AQ6370C) and the remaining 99% connected to subsequent communication systems or noise measurement setups. When the DFB laser diode was powered on, the current was set around 300 mA, and the temperature was maintained at ~30 °C. By fine-tuning the position of the six-axis stage, the generation of dark pulse soliton frequency combs can be observed on the optical spectrum analyzer (as shown in Fig. 2d). Stable microcomb generation is sustained for over 1000 min.

We employed delayed self-heterodyne interferometry to measure the spectral phase noise. The desired wavelength was filtered out using an optical filter. The signal was then split into two paths with equal power: one path was frequency-shifted by a 200 MHz acousto-optic modulator (AOM), while the other passed through a 10-km (10 m for MHz Laser) fiber delay line. Both paths were subsequently combined using a 2 × 2 50/50 optical coupler (OC) and directed to two photodetectors (PD). The output signals were captured by an oscilloscope at a sampling rate of 500MSa/s, collecting 80 million data points, which were then processed using custom software.

Fabrication and characterization of the SDM (de)multiplexers

The optical fibers used in the experiments (a 160-km SMF, a 2-km 24-core MCF, a 25-km FMF and a 50-m RCF) are all provided by YOFC. We fabricate all the (de)multiplexers needed for SDM experiments. The 24-core MCF (de)multiplexer are self-made fan-in/fan-out 3D photonic devices based on femtosecond laser writing technology. The glass chip (dimension: 40 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm) is inscribed by a high repetition rate Ytterbium-based laser to form 24-channel (de)multiplexer. The measured insertion losses of (de) multiplexer are all lower than 1.5 dB in C band, with the maximum and minimum inter-channel crosstalk of −19.8 dB and −30.6 dB respectively. For LP modes (de)multiplexing, we employed all-fiber mode-selective couplers (MSCs) based on the side-polishing technique47. The MSC consists of a SMF and a FMF. When the effective refractive index of the fundamental mode in the SMF matches that of the higher-order modes in the FMF, the fundamental mode is coupled into the FMF to generate the corresponding LP02 mode. The fundamental mode in the FMF maintains its mode nature as it passes through the MSC. As a result, the MSC successfully enables the multiplexing of the LP01 and LP02 modes. The LP multiplexer and demultiplexer are connected by an FMF to characterize their desirable performance. Across the entire C-band, they exhibit a loss of less than 1.5 dB and a crosstalk of less than −15 dB. For OAM modes (de)multiplexing, we utilized MSCs similar to those used for LP mode (de)multiplexing32. The difference lies in the use of the fused biconical taper technique for OAM modes (de)multiplexing. Additionally, a multimode fiber (MMF) is used instead of an FMF. Furthermore, we implemented two cascaded MSCs to achieve the multiplexing of OAM11 and OAM21 mode. The cascaded OAM multiplexer demonstrates a loss of less than 2.5 dB and a crosstalk of less than −15 dB across the entire C-band.

Detailed coherent experimental setup

The experimental results presented in Fig. 2e were obtained using multiple light sources with varying linewidth characteristics: NKT Photonics (1 Hz), Comb0 (25 Hz), Comb1 (81.64 Hz), Comb5 (220 Hz), SANTEC TSL-510 (1.576 kHz), RIO ORION-1550-30-15-M (5 kHz), KEYSIGHT N7714A ECL (10.4 kHz), ALNAIR TLT-200 (20 kHz), APEX AP3350A (238 kHz), and CONQUER CUSTOMIZED (628 kHz), along with two cases that failed demodulation - an InP DFB laser (3 MHz) and BOXOPTRONICS CUSTOMIZED (8.8 MHz). Throughout the transmission experiments, the KEYSIGHT N7714A ECL served as the LO to maintain phase reference stability. It must be emphasized that all OCNR measurements were performed with the optical spectrum analyzer’s resolution bandwidth set to 6.25 GHz.

In our work, we employed self-homodyne and homodyne coherent detection schemes for optical communication demonstration. The homodyne scheme modifies the self-homodyne setup by replacing LO path with an additional light source, while other configurations remain unchanged. Notably, for a WDM system with a single-wavelength rate of 1 Tbps, self-homodyne detection system demands precise alignment of the signal and LO path lengths, exhibiting a trend where lower linewidths of light source result in higher tolerance levels. At the transmitter, integrated comb chip generates 16 comblines as the WDM channels while 16 ECLs are used to load channels for comparison experiment. The channel spacing of both solutions is 100 GHz. The WSS (Finisar Waveshaper 4000 s) (with 5 dB insertion loss) splits these channels into odd/even paths. Subsequently, the light source carrying 16 wavelengths is amplified by an amplifier to boost the power of each channel, with the aggregate power reaching ~13 dBm prior to entering two 35-GHz 3 dB bandwidth IQ modulators (Sumicem T.SBXH1.5PL-35PD-ADC). Both paths are simultaneously modulated by data generated from a 128-GSa/s Arbitrary Waveform Generator (AWG-M9505A). Depending on the data rate, the output optical signal power varies within the range of −7 dBm to −14 dBm. The odd and even signals decorrelating by a 10-ns optical delay lines are coupled with similar power levels and fed into the transmission link. When transitioning to single-polarization transmission, only one polarization of the dual-polarization modulator is utilized, with the corresponding modulator drive voltage applied to that polarization. The modulator’s bias voltage is dynamically adjusted by an automatic bias controller to ensure stable high-speed signal performance and efficient operation.

The transmission link consists of an EDFA (KEOPSYS) for signal amplification, multiplexers/demultiplexers, and multidimensional multiplexing fiber to validate the optical comb’s transmission performance. The SDM channels are fully decorrelated to each other, with 10-ns optical delay lines between each channel. The power injected into the fiber is kept below 10 dBm to mitigate fiber nonlinear effects. At the receiver, amplification, filtering, attenuation and amplification scheme is employed for WDM demultiplexing and OSNR adjustment. The LO light, amplified to 18 dBm by an EDFA, is coherently detected with the signal light (around 0 dBm) by a dual-polarization coherent receiver consisting of a polarization-diversity 90-degree hybrid and four 70-GHz 3 dB bandwidth balanced photodiodes. A 256-GSa/s real-time oscilloscope (KEYSIGHT UXR0594AP) with 59-GHz bandwidth captures waveforms from four channels and sends them to a computer for DSP. In the context of DSP, at the transmitter side, we initially generate a PRBS sequence for QAM mapping, followed by 0.01 Root Raised Cosine pulse shaping, and then resample the signal before sending it to the AWG. On the receiver side, the captured signal undergoes sequential processing including resampling, frame synchronization, dispersion compensation, carrier frequency offset estimation using the fourth-power algorithm, adaptive MIMO equalization, carrier phase recovery through a two-stage blind phase search algorithm (with filter lengths of 32 and 8 for the first and second stages, respectively), demodulation, and BER calculation. It is noteworthy that during the verification of the relationship between OCNR and linewidth versus maximum net rate, DSP section utilized an 81-tap adaptive MIMO equalizer. In other experimental scenarios, the use of a 41-tap MIMO equalizer with reduced compensation capability and the decreased received OSNR per combline caused by WDM collectively lead to a degradation in achievable symbol rate compared to the former configuration.
