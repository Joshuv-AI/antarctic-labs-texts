Full Body Embolic Protection During Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in High Risk Patients
Authors: Omran Abukhalaf, Rajiv Das, Mohammad Alkhalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke remains one of the most feared complications of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). We report our initial experience using a dedicated full-body embolic protection device during TAVR in 3 patients with severe aortic stenosis and high-risk embolic features. This included large thrombus on the aortic valve, a left ventricular outflow tract mass, and persistent left atrial thrombus despite anticoagulation. Device deployment and retrieval were successful in all procedures, with no device-related complications or neurologic events at 30-day follow-up. Macroscopic debris was retrieved in 2 patients, and histopathologic analysis in 1 case demonstrated atheromatous plaque components. This initial experience demonstrates the feasibility of full-body embolic protection in selected patients with high-risk undergoing TAVR and supports further evaluation in prospective studies.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become first-line therapy for severe aortic stenosis across a different spectrum of surgical risks.1 Despite continued procedural refinement, stroke remains one of the most feared complications, associated with a 6-fold increase in 30-day mortality and substantial long-term morbidity.2

Embolic debris generated during TAVR is presumed to be the main mechanism of procedural stroke.3 They may originate from calcified valve tissue, aortic atheroma, or thrombus mobilized during valve crossing, balloon valvuloplasty, or prosthesis deployment.3 However, recent studies assessing the role of cerebral protection using the Sentinel device have failed to show clinical benefits in all-comer patients undergoing TAVR.4,5

The full-body embolic protection device (FLOWer) is a full-arch capture system designed to provide comprehensive supra-aortic branches and full-body protection using a 60-μm mesh.6 It was recently reported to be feasible and safe in patients undergoing TAVR; however, its utility in high-risk patients is yet to be determined. We report our initial experience using FLOWer in patients considered at high embolic risk.

Case 1

A 67-year-old woman was admitted with heart failure secondary to severe bicuspid aortic valve stenosis (mean gradient of 43 mm Hg). Her workup included transesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography, which highlighted an echogenic mass of 11 × 14 mm attached to the aortic valve, which did not respond to intensive anticoagulation treatment (Figures 1A and 1B). She had a prior ischemic stroke, previous surgical pulmonary endarterectomy with residual pulmonary hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, and elevated body mass index. The procedure was performed via the right common femoral artery. An 18-F long sheath (65 cm) was introduced within the previously deployed FLOWer device, and a 26-mm Evolut FX + was directly implanted without immediate complications (Figure 2C, Video 2). The tortuosity of the aorta in the setting of using the FLOWer device made it challenging to navigate the aortic arch. Therefore, a long sheath was used to ensure safe passage of the Evolut FX + platform. The FLOWer device captured at least 6 pieces of tissue upon withdrawal from the patient (Figure 2D). Subsequent histopathologic analysis highlighted features of atheromatous plaque components. The patient underwent postimplant computed tomography (CT), which highlighted good valve position above the left ventricular outflow tract mass, which remained visible on the repeat CT. There were no recorded neurologic events at 30-day follow-up.

Case 3

A 68-year-old woman with severe low-flow aortic stenosis, atrial fibrillation, and previous ischemic stroke with residual weakness. She underwent workup, which highlighted a large left atrial thrombus measuring 25 mm (Figure 3A). The case was discussed in the multidisciplinary team, and the patient elected to undergo TAVR with full body protection using the FLOWer device. The procedure was performed via the right common femoral artery. The native aortic valve was predilated, and a subsequent 26-mm Evolut FX + was deployed. The transcatheter heart valve was postdilated with good procedural results (Figure 3B). The FLOWer device retrieved 2 large pieces of tissue (Figure 3C), but histologic analysis was not available to confirm their nature. The patient underwent repeat CT, which did not reveal any evidence of left atrial thrombus (Figure 3D). The disappearance of left atrial mass on repeat CT may reflect the captured debris within the FLOWer device or a treatment response to prior oral anticoagulation. No neurologic events occurred within 30 days post-TAVR procedure.

Procedural Technique

All FLOWer cases are planned using preprocedural CT. The device is sized according to the measured diameter of the ascending aorta at 10 mm proximal to the innominate artery. A 12-F sheath is inserted into the contralateral common femoral artery. The FLOWer device is inserted into the sheath and positioned in the ascending aorta proximal to the innominate artery (Figures 4A and 4B). Following full deployment of the device, the debris retention funnel is visible on fluoroscopy (red arrow, Figure 4A). An integrated pigtail is advanced to the aortic annulus to perform an aortography for valve deployment. A second pigtail catheter is navigated and advanced within the device structure inside the descending aorta. An interaction between the retention funnel and pigtail catheter is needed to ensure that the latter is not abluminal and is advanced inside the FLOWer device (Figure 4C, yellow arrow). The pigtail catheter is subsequently exchanged to AL1 catheter, and the native valve is crossed accordingly. The transcatheter heart valve using either a balloon- or self-expanding valve(Figures 4D and 4E) is advanced over the stiff wire and deployed as per standard practice.Figure 4Procedural Steps in Using FLOWer Device(A) Fluoroscopic image of deployed full-body embolic protection device (FLOWer) highlighting the distal structure close to the nosecone and the debris retention funnel (red arrow). Discussion

In this single-center experience involving patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing TAVR with high risk of embolic events, the use of an adjunctive full-body protection device with the FLOWer device was feasible, safe, and achieved 100% technical success without neurologic events at 30 days.

Multiple embolic protection systems have been developed to reduce procedural stroke risk.3 These devices are broadly categorized as capture or deflection systems. While they consistently demonstrate their ability to capture embolic material and possible reduction in magnetic resonance imaging-defined lesion burden, translation into reproducible reductions in clinical events has not been consistent.3, 4, 5 Therefore, routine use of a stroke protection device is currently not supported, and its widespread use is limited.

FLOWer is a full-body protection device that filters and removes debris that are potentially released during the TAVR procedure without compromising normal patient blood flow. It has a mesh pore size of 60-μm to capture small embolic debris. The CE-mark Trial of the FLOWer Cerebral Embolic Protection Device (NAUTILUS) captured on average 420 particles per filter,7 supporting the concept that embolic load during TAVR may be underestimated by partial protection strategies.6 While causality cannot be inferred from this small series, the findings are consistent with the hypothesis that full-arch capture strategies may be particularly relevant in selected high-risk phenotypes. In our series, we retrieved large materials in 2 of 3 patients, and this debris was likely to cause embolic, including stroke events. Importantly, periprocedural stroke was recently linked to the presence and severity of aortic arch atheroma.7,8 The interaction between devices, including stroke protection ones, and aortic atheroma can itself lead to procedural stroke. Therefore, it is important to assess the landing zone when using full-arch capture strategies. Notably, FLOWer is of atraumatic nature as the mechanism of deployment requires unsheathing of the inflow of the device to minimize stroke risk. Nonetheless, attention needs to be paid to avoid large aortic atheroma of “shaggy” nature.8 Deployment may be required to be distal (more aortic) to avoid entrapping and subsequently dislodging such atheroma upon retrieving or during deployment of the FLOWer device. In our case series, the landing zone for the stroke protection device was clear of any significant atheroma, which enabled safe deployment before the TAVR procedure.

The risk of vascular complications should be taken into consideration as the FLOWer device is deployed using a 12-F sheath. In other words, it is important not to substitute stroke with vascular complications risks. Therefore, selective use of such a device guided by clinical and imaging assessment should guide decision-making until randomized data is available.

Our study is limited by its small sample size and the absence of postprocedural neuroimaging. The data are not randomized, nor was it powered to evaluate clinical efficacy.

Conclusions

In this high-risk cohort of embolic events, patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing TAVR, the adjunctive use of FLOWer as a full-body protection device was feasible and not associated with neurologic events at 30 days.
