The Hubble Tension in Light of the Symmetry of Scale Invariance
Frédéric Courbin, André Maeder *

Abstract
When the expansion rate of the Universe at recombination is used to infer the present expansion rate H 0 H_{0} , the value derived in the Λ \Lambda CDM model, H 0 = 67.4 H_{0}=67.4 km s -1 Mpc -1 , is about in 6 σ \,\sigma tension with the value measured locally, H 0 = 74 H_{0}=74 km s -1 Mpc -1 . In this work, we consider instead the expansion history in the context of the symmetry of scale-invariant vacuum (SIV model). We first perform two major cosmological tests: the Hubble diagram for type-Ia supernovae and the fundamental relation between H 0 H_{0} , the age of the Universe, and the total density of matter, Ω m \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} . This allows us to constrain Ω m \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} in SIV, with both tests giving the best agreement for Ω m \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.20. We then study the physical connections of the dynamical and thermal states of the Universe at recombination with the present Hubble constant, H 0 H_{0} , and the present temperature, T T , in the Λ \Lambda CDM and SIV contexts. We find that, in SIV, the properties at recombination may be conveyed to the present ones ( T = 2.726 T=2.726 and H 0 H_{0} at z = 0 z=0 ) without any tension, indicating H 0 = 74 H_{0}=74 km s -1 Mpc -1 in spite of the anchoring on the CMB. This is due to the slightly different expansion and temperature histories of the two cosmological models. Importantly, this happens to occur for Ω m \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.20, as constrained in SIV with supernovae and cosmic age. This suggests that the Hubble tension currently found between H 0 H_{0} values in the early and late Universe may simply be the result of Λ ​ C ​ D ​ M \Lambda CDM ignoring the small but still measurable effects of scale invariance.

Introduction

Hubble tension, the difference between the value of the Hubble parameter
H_{0}=74
km s
-1
Mpc
-1
determined in the late universe
(
Breuval et al. 2024
)
and the value of
H_{0}=67.4
km s
-1
Mpc
-1
, determined from the CMB fluctuations in the early universe
(
Planck Collaboration 2021
)
, has progressively gained a high level of significance. This has been strengthened even further with recent CMB observations from SPT-3G in E-mode polarization and high spatial frequencies, giving
H_{0}=66.66\pm 0.60
km s
-1
Mpc
-1

(
Campuis et al. 2025
)
. This is in 6.2
\sigma
tension with local measurements.

The tension now seems clearly established and acknowledged as the signature of a severe problem in cosmology and physics rather than an observational fluke. Thorough reviews by
Verde et al. 2019
,
Kamionkowski & Riess 2023
, and
Di Velentino et al. 2025
examine the main observational constraints, the possibly discrepant points in the physics of the early and late Universe, as well as possible suggestions of new physical effects to be considered in the future. Considering the many methods to measure
H_{0}
,
Verde et al. 2019
emphasizes that, “This shows that the discrepancy does not appear to be dependent on the use of any one method, team, or source”. Also, these authors point out that the discrepancy seems to concern the early physics and the possible changes affecting the expansion phase preceding the end of the radiative phase.

On the observational side, the validity of the determination of the Hubble constant has been further supported by the Large Magellanic Cloud Cepheid Standards with great accuracy
(
Riess et al. 2019
)
. Potential errors in the Cepheid calibration or about the possibility of local density voids affecting measurements of the local
H_{0}
have been disregarded by new Gaia DR3 parallaxes
(
Riess et al. 2018
;
Riess et al. 2021
)
. Moreover, JWST observations of a sample of more than 1000 Cepheids as an anchor of the distance ladder confirm within 0.01 magnitude the distance scale previously adopted by SH0ES
(
Riess et al. 2024
)
. New observations
(
Scolnic et al. 2025
)
with the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) of the distance to the Coma Cluster support a rather high value
H_{0}=76.5\pm 2.2
km s
-1
Mpc
-1
, even more differing from the CMB Planck value. Very recently,
a study with detailed error audit of the measurements even reported a 7.1
~\sigma
tension
Casertano et al. 2025
. All these observations call for cosmological developments beyondthe
\Lambda
CDM model.

Numerous original and inventive cosmological models have been studied regarding the physics of the end of the radiative phase, such as the “Early Dark Energy (EDE)” model, which proposes a transitory background peak of some unknown dark energy phase being active just before recombination and essentially insignificant in earlier and later phases. Further theoretical models are proposed with evolving fluid densities playing a significant role at some cosmological stages and also new models where either transitory, oscillating, or rolling new scalar field are envisaged in the radiative phase
(
Kamionkowski & Riess 2023
)
. However, alternatives to the current
\Lambda
CDM paradigm should at least respect two crucial rules:

•

“A mathematical principle, logical and simple, (must) totally or nearly determine the equations (of gravitation)”
, according to Einstein
(
Einstein 1949
)
.

•

”The new model should not do worse than standard
\Lambda
CDM in describing all other cosmological observations”
, as emphasized by
Verde et al. 2019
.

In the present work, we explore scale-invariant models (“Scale Invariant Vacuum”; SIV models)
(
Maeder 2017a
;
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
)
. SIV clearly obeys the first requirement, as the whole theory flows naturally when considering the question: In addition to Galilean invariance, to Lorentz invariance and to general covariance,
is there still another invariance to consider in the gravitation theory? In the empty space, general relativity is also scale-invariant.
However, the presence of mass generally suppresses scale invariance
(
Feynman 1963
)
. How small has to be the density not to destroy scale invariance? Cosmological models based on the scale-invariant field equation by
Dirac 1973
indicate a drastic reduction of the effects of scale invariance
as soon as even some negligible amount of matter is present
(
Maeder 2017a
)
. However, although very much reduced,
these effects totally vanish only at the
critical density
\rho_{\mathrm{c}=}\frac{3H^{2}_{0}}{8\pi G}
(
\sim 10^{-29}
g cm
-3
) and above. This limit is consistent
with considerations about causal connection in the Universe
(
Maeder and Gueorguiev 2021a
)
.

The second of the above two requirements has been progressively verified by confronting SIV with key observational tests. Noticeable examples include the growth of density fluctuations
(
Maeder and Gueorguiev 2019
)
, the dynamics of galaxies
(
Maeder 2023
)
and of galaxy clusters
(
Maeder & Courbin 2024
)
. In addition, the departures from classical mechanics measured in the motion of very wide binary stars
(
Hernandez 2023
;
Chae 2023
)
were found consistent with the predictions of SIV
(
Maeder & Courbin 2024
)
. In the latter work, detailed tests were also performed with strong gravitational lensing, showing that scale-invariant effects were able to reconcile the lensing and stellar mass of lens galaxies, both at low redshift in the SLACS sample and at redshift z
\sim
2 in the JWST-ER1 system.

In the present work, we explore the expansion history of the Universe,
H(z)
as described in SIV and show that the Hubble tension is nicely explained even when anchoring
H(z)
on the CMB. Section
2
presents the basic analytical properties of the SIV models, and some relevant model properties of the early Universe. Section
3
discusses the SNIa observations
and the relation between
H_{0}
,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, and the age of the Universe, in light of SIV. Section
4
explores the origin of the Hubble tension and Section
5
presents our conclusions.

2
The Scale-Invariant Models

Although the full SIV theory has already been published
(
Maeder 2017a
;
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
)
along with all the details of equation derivations, we briefly summarize the main points relevant to Hubble tension.

2.1
Short Basics

The scale-invariant theory assumes general covariance like general relativity; in addition, it also assumes scale covariance to a form

ds^{\prime}\,=\,\lambda\,ds\,.

(1)

ds^{\prime}
is the line element in the space-time of GR, while
ds
is the scale-transformed line element in a new space-time, endowed with the possibility of scale transformation by a factor of
\lambda
. The notion of cotensor was introduced by Dirac
(
Dirac 1973
)
: mathematical bodies possibly subject to general covariance and to scale covariance of the form
Y^{\prime}=\lambda^{n}Y
. For
n=0
one speaks of scale invariance and of intensor, invector or inscalar. A cotensoral expression of the scale-invariant field equation has been developed by Dirac
(
Dirac 1973
)
and Canuto et al.
(
Canuto et al. 1977
)
, it was further confirmed by an action principle
(
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
)
. In this context, it is worth to emphasize some remarks by
Einstein 1949
in his Autobiographical Notes of 1949:
“…the existence of rigid standard rulers is an assumption suggested by our approximate experience, assumption which is arbitrary in its principle”
.

A gauging condition is necessary to constrain the scale factor.
Dirac 1973
and
Canuto et al. 1977
adopted the so-called large number hypothesis. It was preferred to adopt as a gauge condition that “the macroscopic empty space must be scale invariant”
(
Maeder 2017a
)
, which implies a gauge factor
\lambda\sim 1/t
. Moreover, this gauging condition is also demanded by the above mentioned action principle
(
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
)
.

Cosmological equations have been obtained with a geometry described by the FLWR metric and with the gauging assumption that the macroscopic empty space is scale-invariant
(
Maeder 2017a
)
,

\frac{8\,\pi G\varrho}{3}=\frac{k}{a^{2}}+\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a^{2}}+2\,\frac{\dot{a}\dot{\lambda}}{a\lambda}\,,

(2)

-8\,\pi Gp=\frac{k}{a^{2}}+2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a^{2}}+4\frac{\dot{a}\dot{\lambda}}{a\lambda}\,.

(3)

The combination of these two equations leads to

-\frac{4\,\pi G}{3}\,(3p+\varrho)=\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{a}\dot{\lambda}}{a\lambda}\,.

(4)

Each equation contains only one additional term with respect to Friedmann’s.
The last equation shows that this additional term produces an acceleration proportional to the velocity. Of importance for the Hubble expansion, we see that the above system of scale-invariant
cosmological equations naturally contains an acceleration term, which results from the (small) variations in the scale factor
\lambda

with time. In the context of the scale-invariant models, it is therefore not necessary to call for different possible forms of dark energy.
An additional acceleration is also present in the weak field approximation, thus leading to a modified Newton equation
(
Maeder & Bouvier 1979
)
(see also
Maeder 2023
;
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
).
Such an acceleration has also been found to favor the growth of density fluctuations
and thus the early formation of galaxies at very high redshifts
(
Maeder and Gueorguiev 2019
)
.
The above gauging condition based on the properties of the empty space also implies that the scale factor
\lambda(t)
is independent of the matter content.

A specific conservation law for SIV results from the above cosmological equations,

\varrho\,a^{3(w+1)}\,\lambda^{(3w+1)}\,=const,

(5)

with
p=w\varrho\,c^{2}
(c=1 in the three above cosmological equations). This conservation law, together with the condition of the scale invariance of the energy-momentum
T_{\mu\nu}
, imposes that the potential
\Phi=GM/r
is a conserved quantity rather than the mass,
M
, which varies in
\lambda^{-1}=t

(
Maeder 2017a
;
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2023
)
. Thus, as in special relativity, the mass is not a conserved quantity. However, in current models, the amplitude of these variations over the age of the Universe is very limited.

The additional effects resulting from scale invariance would be quite large if there were no matter in the Universe (
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0
), with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=\varrho/\varrho_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\varrho_{c}=\frac{3H^{2}_{0}}{8\pi G}
. However, a
noticeable property of these equations is that the effects of scale invariance are drastically reduced as soon as some matter is considered in the models (see Figure 3 in
(
Maeder 2017a
)
). The effects completely vanish for models with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=1
(identical to the EdS model) and above. Moreover, in models with
0<\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}<1
, the scale-invariant effects in current weak-field dynamics behave like
\sim\sqrt{\varrho_{c}/\varrho}
, implying a further drastic reduction in scale-invariant effects with the increasing density of the systems considered
(
Maeder 2017c
)
. Remarkably, the above results are in agreement with the statements by
Feynman 1963
about the occurrence of scale invariance in empty space, and with its rapid suppression in the presence of matter.

The solutions of the above equations for
k=0
and
\pm 1
are close to those of the
\Lambda
CDM models when
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
>0.3

according to the numerical models
(
Maeder 2017a
)
. For matter-dominated models with
k=0
, there is a simple analytical solution
(
Jesus 2018
)
,

a(t)\,=\,\left[\frac{t^{3}-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}\right]^{2/3}\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\;\;t_{\mathrm{in}}=\Omega^{1/3}_{\mathrm{m}},

(6)

where
t_{\mathrm{in}}
is the initial time for
a(t_{\mathrm{in}})=0
. The solution for models in the radiative phase of the Universe has been studied in

(
Maeder 2019
)
. For the present time
t_{0}=1
, one also has
a(t_{0})=1
. It is somehow surprising that the addition of one more invariance in the theory of gravitation leads to simpler equations than in the
\Lambda
CDM models (cf. Appendix
A
).

A word of caution is necessary about
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. Although its definition in SIV is similar to that in
\Lambda
CDM, it does not mean that the numerical values of the SIV density, temperature, and other properties are identical to those of the
\Lambda
CDM models. The reason is that
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
indirectly influences, through several equations, other quantities such as
H_{0}
and the critical density
\varrho_{\mathrm{c}}
. It also influences the age of the Universe,
\tau_{0}
, the times of various important events, such as recombination, and eventually all model parameters and properties. For example, if we consider the cases of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.3 and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.2 in the
\Lambda
CDM models, the same values of the critical density,
\varrho_{\mathrm{c}}
, occur for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.28 and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.17 in the SIV models. Thus, there is no exact numerical correspondence of all parameters between models of the same
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
value in
\Lambda
CDM and SIV, particularly for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\leq 0.30
. This is why when we refer to
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
we often speak of specific SIV
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
values. The cosmological models being different, all their variables evidently have (often slightly) different numerical values.

The derivative of (
6
) leads to the Hubble expansion rate in the
t
scale,

H(t)=\frac{2\,t^{2}}{t^{3}-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}},\quad\mathrm{thus}\;\;H(t_{0})=\frac{2}{1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}.

(7)

The term
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
represents the value at the present time
t_{0}=1
, alike in the
\Lambda
CDM.
At present, we have for
k=0

(
Maeder 2017a
)
,

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\Omega_{\lambda}=1,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\Omega_{\lambda}=1-\frac{2}{H(t)\times t}.

(8)

The dependence in
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{1/3}
of
t_{\mathrm{in}}
(Eq.
6
) produces a rapid increase in
t_{\mathrm{in}}
for low
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. For
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0,0.01,0.1,0.3,0.5
, the values of
t_{\mathrm{in}}
are 0, 0.215, 0.464, 0.669, and 0.794, respectively. This strongly reduces the possible range of
\lambda(t)=t_{0}/t
, which varies between
1/t_{\mathrm{in}}
and
1/t_{0}=1
for increasing
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

(cf. Figure 3 in
(
Maeder 2017a
)
). For
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\geq 1
, there are no possible scale-invariant models, consistent with causality relations in an expanding Universe
(
Maeder and Gueorguiev 2021a
)
.

The SIV models,
a(t)
vs.
t
, are relatively close to the
\Lambda
CDM models for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

>0.3
up to
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 1,
where they become identical to the EdS model. For smaller
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
departures become larger and larger with decreasing
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
values.

2.2
Evolution of Scale-Invariant Properties with Redshift
z

By expressing the redshift
z+1=a(t_{0})/a(t)
, one may obtain the dimensionless time
t
as a function of
z
,

t^{3}=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})a^{3/2},\;\mathrm{thus}\;t=\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})(1+z)^{-3/2}\right]^{1/3}.

(9)

To transfer time
t
to the current time
\tau

19
),
which give

\tau=\tau_{0}\,\frac{\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})(1+z)^{-3/2}\right]^{1/3}-\Omega^{1/3}_{\mathrm{m}}}{1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{1/3}}.

(10)

The current

H(\tau)=\frac{da}{dt}\frac{dt}{d\tau}\frac{1}{a}=H(t)\frac{1-\Omega^{1/3}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\tau_{0}},

(11)

which gives

H(\tau_{0})=\frac{2\,(1-\Omega^{1/3}_{\mathrm{m}})}{\tau_{0}\,(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})}.

(12)

Thus, one also has from (
10
)

\tau(z)=\frac{2\,\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})(1+z)^{-3/2}\right]^{1/3}-\Omega^{1/3}_{\mathrm{m}}}{H_{0}\,(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})}

(13)

For

) and (

\mathrm{and}\quad H(z)\,=\,H_{0}\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{9/4}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})(1+z)^{3/4}\right]^{2/3}

(15)

3
Confronting SIV with Observations

We now confront the expansion history of the Universe predicted in the SIV theory to two key tests: 1- The Hubble diagram of type Ia supernovae, 2- The relation between
H_{0}
, the age of the Universe
\tau_{0}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. Further tests of the cosmological expansion history are being performed and will be the subject of future works to comply with our second requirement in the introduction “The new model should not do worse than standard
\Lambda
CDM in describing all other cosmological
observations”.

3.1
Testing SIV with the Hubble Diagram of SN Ia

Type Ia supernovae are standard candles used in the distance ladder method. They allowed us to measure the accelerated expansion rate of the Universe
(
Riess et al. 1998
;
Perlmutter et al. 1999
)
. Because type Ia supernovae are part of a ladder, the method is subject to potential errors propagating in the consecutive steps of the ladder. For this reason, they do not stand alone as a measurement method for
H_{0}
. However, they constrain the density of matter
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
well. This is what is performed in the SIV paradigm without trying to measure
H_{0}
.

The data set considered is the one compiled by
Pierel et al. 2024
that includes Pantheon + SH0ES
(
Brout et al. 2022
)
and two lensed high-redshift supernovae extending the redshift range to
z=2.9
. Using Equation (
15
), we perform a Bayesian fit to the distance modulus model for SIV, where the free parameters are the matter density,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, and an arbitrary constant,
K
, in the distance modulus. The results are shown in Figure
2
, leading to
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
=0.18\pm 0.03
. We obtain a reduced
\chi^{2}_{\rm SIV}=0.44
, pointing to overfitting but this likely due to the error bars on the supernovae being over-estimated. For comparison, the best fit in
\Lambda
CDM with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
=0.315
, as in
Pierel et al. 2024
, gives
\chi^{2}_{\rm CDM}=0.44
as well. We further explore the robustness of our fit. We first artificially normalize the error bars to obtain a
\chi^{2}_{\rm SIV}\sim 1.0
. This results in the same value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. Dividing the error bars by 2 increases the
\chi^{2}
values to
\chi^{2}_{\rm SIV}=1.75
and
\chi^{2}_{\rm CDM}=1.77
but does not change the values of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. Increasing our prior on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
to the range [0, 1] gives the exact same fit as with [0, 0.5] used in Figure
2
, although with slightly slower convergence. Using a prior that does not include the value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
=0.2 results in hitting the edge of the prior without proper convergence.

Our previous results with strong gravitational lensing

(

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

=0.05

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
\sim

that indicate values for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
that are significantly smaller than in
\Lambda
CDM. The present
use of
H(z)
in establishing the theoretical expression of the distance modulus of supernovae leads to the first direct observational measurement of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
in the SIV paradigm. The value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

=0.18\pm 0.03
represents a good starting point for further observational tests in SIV. It is also consistent with previous works, but now with error bars.

The value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
obtained in SIV is different from the classical one of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.30, and this may appear surprising. But the
two cosmological models are different: the equations of the
\Lambda
CDM model contain an additional constant with respect to Friedmann’s, while the SIV models contain an additional term varying with time.
All relations between the different physical variables are different. Thus, different models applied to the same observational data do not necessarily give the same values of the parameters. For example, the baryon contents of the two types of models are not necessarily identical
(
Gueorguiev & Maeder 2025
)
.

The above remark in fact does not only apply to Figure

below involving the age of the Universe,

-

The above considerations are preliminary and will be developed in detail in an upcoming paper, but they show that the angular scales in the SIV model are already compatible with the observations and that the remaining difference maybe explained by doing more precise calculations and/or by determining

We finally note that the present results appear to leave room for some amount of dark matter. The amount of it is still uncertain in view of the remaining uncertainties in the predictions of the SIV nucleosynthesis
(

Our analysis, based on the study of the thermal and dynamical properties in the
\Lambda
CDM and SIV models, suggests that the origin of the Hubble tension resides in the way the physical conditions at recombination are connected by the
\Lambda
CDM model to the present ones in terms of
H(z)
and temperature evolutions. The account of an additional symmetry, scale invariance, introduces rather minor but measurable effects and appears as an interesting possible solution to the tension.

5
Conclusions

We conclude that if the thermal and dynamical properties of the CMB at
T=3000
K
are used to infer the present expansion rate,
H_{0}
, the value derived within SIV is
H_{0}=74
km s
-1
Mpc
-1
for a specific SIV matter density of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
= 0.2. This last value is supported by the interpretation of the Hubble diagram of type Ia supernovae in the SIV context and independently by the diagram that compares
H_{0}
,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and the age of the Universe. For that value of
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, the expansion rates of the Universe at
T=3000
K in SIV and
\Lambda
CDM coincide almost exactly, implying a similar thermal and dynamical state. Thus, connecting this state to the present time using the
\Lambda
CDM model leads to
H_{0}
=67.4 km s
-1
Mpc
-1
, while the connection using the SIV model instead gives
H_{0}
=74 km s
-1
Mpc
-1
.

About the major question raised in the introduction whether some scale-invariant effects are present in our low density Universe, the present results
suggest a positive answer. For the above mean density, some, in fact very limited, effects remain of what they would be in the empty space. As a consequence, the Hubble tension currently found between
H_{0}
values in the early and late Universe may simply be the result of
\Lambda
CDM ignoring the small but still measurable effects of scale invariance.

Appendix A
The Analytical
\Lambda
CDM Models

Analytical solutions would often be useful, in particular for the resulting functions, such as time
t
, expansion rate
H
, temperature
T
, matter and radiation densities, as a function of redshift
z
. Here, we are considering the case of a flat geometry with
k=0
, which forms a basis for comparison with the SIV models.

A.1
Basic Relations

Let us start from the basic cosmological equation of the
\Lambda
CDM models,

\frac{8\,\pi G\varrho}{3}=\frac{k}{a^{2}}+\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a^{2}}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}.

(21)

Considering a flat model with
k=0
and accounting for the conservation law
\varrho\,a^{3}=const.
, we may write,

\dot{a}^{2}\,a-\frac{\Lambda\,a^{3}}{3}-C=0,\quad\quad\mathrm{with}\quad C=\frac{8\,\pi G\varrho\,a^{3}}{3}.

(22)

The cosmological constant
\Lambda
is expressed here as the inverse of the square of a time. With the critical density
\varrho_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{3H^{2}_{0}}{8\,\pi G}
and the matter density parameter
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=\varrho/\varrho_{\mathrm{c}}
. The constants
C
can be further specified by its value at present time
t_{0}
,
C\,=\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,H^{2}_{0}\,a(t_{0})^{3}
.

We have from (
21
)

\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=1,\,\quad\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\Omega_{\Lambda}=\frac{\Lambda}{3\,H^{2}},

(23)

where
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}
,
\rho_{\mathrm{c}}
are all varying with time. However, these quantities are usually considered at the present time, unless specified.
We may relate the constants
\Lambda
and
C
thanks to values at present,

\Lambda=3H^{2}_{0}(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})=3H^{2}_{0}\left(1-\frac{C}{H^{2}_{0}\,a^{3}(t_{0})}\right).

(24)

We choose the units so that at present we have
t_{0}=1
and
a(t_{0})=1
.

The initial time
t_{\mathrm{in}}
of the Universe is defined by
a(t_{\mathrm{in}})=0
.
The cosmological equation can now be written,

\dot{a}^{2}-H^{2}_{0}\,\Omega_{\Lambda}\,a^{2}-\frac{H^{2}_{0}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{a}=0.

(25)

A.2
A Time Versus a(t) Solution of the
\Lambda
CDM Models

The equations of
\Lambda
CDM models are generally solved numerically. Here we are first examining a useful analytical relation. From (
25
), we get

\dot{a}\,=\,\frac{H_{0}}{a^{1/2}}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}\,a^{3}+\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}},

(26)

\mathrm{Setting\;}x=a^{3/2},\;\mathrm{we\;have}\quad dt\,=\,\frac{2}{3}\frac{dx}{H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\sqrt{\,x^{2}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}.

(27)

\mathrm{Integration\;gives}\quad t\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\left[\,\ln\left(a^{3/2}+\sqrt{a^{3}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\,\right)+\tilde{C}\,\right]

(28)

where
\tilde{C}
is a constant. Since
\frac{2\,\tilde{C}}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}

is also a constant
C^{\prime}
, we may write simply

t\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\ln\left(a^{3/2}+\sqrt{a^{3}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\,\right)+C^{\prime}\,.

(29)

The condition
a(t_{0})=1
at
t_{0}=1
imposes

C^{\prime}\,=\,1-\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\ln\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\,\right).

(30)

This establishes a relation between
H_{0}
and
C^{\prime}
for a given
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. From (
28
), we have at the initial time
t_{in}
when
a(t_{in})=0
,

t_{in}=\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\ln\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}+C^{\prime}

(31)

If we choose
t_{in}=0
, we get a second relation between
H_{0}
and
C^{\prime}
. Combining it with (
30
) gives

1=\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\ln\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\right)\,-\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\ln\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}.

(32)

We then get an expression for
H_{0}
and
C^{\prime}
,

H_{0}=\frac{2}{3\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}{\ln\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}{{\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}}\right)}\,\quad\quad C^{\prime}\,=\,-\frac{\ln\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}{\ln\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}{{\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}}}\right)}

(33)

As an example for a cosmological model with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.3
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
, we get

H_{0}\,=\,0.9640994\,\quad\quad\mathrm{and}\quad C^{\prime}\,=\,0.3501419\,.

(34)

Apart from the choice of the conditions
a=1
at
t=1
at present, we are free to choose one among the three values
t_{in}
,
C^{\prime}
,
H_{0}
. Above, we have taken
t_{in}=0
, but we could have chosen
H_{0}
or
C^{\prime}
and this would have determined another couple of values. Taking
H_{0}
similar to that of another kind of model may sometimes be useful, as permitting direct comparison of their expansion rates
a(t)
and

H(z)/H_{0}
evolution; see Figure 2 by
Maeder 2017a
, where
\Lambda
CDM and SIV solutions for
a(t)
are compared.

A.3
The General Analytical
\Lambda
CDM Solution

It is necessary to have a relation expressing the expansion factor
a(t)
as a function of time. We use an alternative expression of the above solution (
29
) in terms of
\mathrm{argsinh}

(
Bronstein & Semendiaev 1974
)
,

t\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\mathrm{argsinh}\frac{a^{3/2}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}+C_{1}.

(35)

Constant
C_{1}
is different from the previous constants
\tilde{C}
and
C^{\prime}
.
The inversion of argsinh provides the expansion factor
a(t)
as a function of
t
,

\frac{a^{3/2}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}=\sinh\frac{3}{2}\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\;(t-C_{1}),

(36)

\mathrm{thus,}\quad\quad a=\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\right)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}\,\left[{\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\;(t-C_{1})}\right]

(37)

\mathrm{or}\quad a=\left(\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{e^{\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}(t-C_{1})}-e^{-\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}(t-C_{1})}}{2}\right)^{2/3}.

(38)

These are two equivalent forms of the expansion factor
a(t)
as a function of time.
The solutions for a given
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
depends on
C_{1}
and
H_{0}
, which may be constrained by the limiting conditions.
Condition
a=1
at
t=1
in (
36
) is leading to

H_{0}\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,(1-C_{1})\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\mathrm{argsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}.

(39)

Relation (
36
) at the origin
a(t)=0
with
t_{in}=0
is leading to

\sinh\,\left(-\frac{3}{2}\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\,C_{1}\right)=0.

The argument is also zero and thus
C_{1}=0
, and we finally have for the solution of the
\Lambda
CDM models,

a=\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\right)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}\,\left[{\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\,\,\;t}\right]\,,

(40)

and the present expansion rate
H_{0}
becomes

H_{0}\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\mathrm{argsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}.

(41)

As an example, for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.3
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
, we get
H_{0}\,=\,0.9640994
, which is in agreement with (
34
).
For
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.2
,
H_{0}=1.07602236
and for
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.1
,
H_{0}=1.27787391
. These values are
specific to the cosmological models with
a(t_{0})=1
,
t_{0}=1
and
t_{in}=0
.

As in Appendix
A.2
,
instead of fixing
t_{in}=0
, we could have fixed the value of
H_{0}
(for example for comparison of different kinds of models with observations having an already fixed
H_{0}
). In this case, the initial time
t_{in}
is no longer zero, but according to (
37
) the initial time
t_{in}=C_{1}
as obtained from (
39
),

t_{in}=C_{1}=1-\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\,\mathrm{argsinh}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}.

(42)

Checks have be performed on the exact correspondence between the results of Equation (
37
) and those of numerical
\Lambda
CDM models.

Time
t
is running from
t_{in}
at the Big-Bang to 1 at present. Converting the time unit
t
into the current units
\tau
in Gyr or seconds or vice versa is straightforward, by expressing that the age fraction with respect to the present age is the same in both timescales. This gives

\tau\,=\,\tau_{0}\,\frac{t-t_{in}}{1-t_{in}}\,,\quad t\,=\,t_{in}+\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}(1-t_{in})\,\;\;\mathrm{and}\;\;\frac{d\tau}{dt}=\frac{\tau_{0}}{1-t_{in}}\,.

(43)

A.4
The Expansion Rates
H(z)

We need to express the expansion rate
H=\dot{a}/a
as a function of redshifts
z
. From Equation (
26
), we have

\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\,=\,\frac{H_{0}}{a^{3/2}}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}\,a^{3}+\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}},

(44)

which gives with
\frac{a_{0}}{a}=1+z
,

H(z)=H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{a^{3}}}=H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}+\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,(1+z)^{3}}.

(45)

For model calculations of
a(t)
with
a(t_{0})=1
with
t_{0}=1
, the expression of
H_{0}
given by (
41
) is quite appropriate.
For
H(z)
in km s
-1
Mpc
-1
, we must use the appropriate value of
H_{0}
in these units.
Expression (
45
) is quite convenient, we could also use the derivative
\dot{a}
of (
37
):

\dot{a}=\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\right)^{1/3}\sinh^{-1/3}\,\left[{\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\;t}\right]\,H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}},

leading to
H
as a function of time,

H=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}=H_{0}\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\,\coth\left[{\frac{3}{2}H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\;t}\right].

(46)

It shows that for
t\rightarrow 0
, the corresponding
H
-value tends towards infinity.
A relation between
t
and
z
is often useful, from (
40
) we get

t\,=\,\frac{2}{3\,H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\lambda}}}\mathrm{argsinh}\;\left[(1+z)^{-3/2}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}\right].

(47)
