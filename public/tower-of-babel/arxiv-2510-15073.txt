On mixing and dense periodicity on spaces with a free arc
Dominik Kwietniak, Filip Wierzbowski

Abstract
. We study the dynamics of continuous maps on compact metric spaces containing a free interval (an open subset homeomorphic to the interval ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) ). We provide a new proof of a result of M. Dirbák, Ľ. Snoha, V. Špitalský [Ergodic Theory Dynam. Systems, vol. 33 (2013), no. 6, pp. 1786–1812] saying that every continuous and transitive, but non-minimal map of a space with a free interval is relatively mixing, non-invertible, has positive topological entropy, and dense periodic points. The key simplification comes from short proofs of two facts. The first says that every weakly mixing map of a space with a free interval must be mixing and have positive entropy. The second says that a transitive but not minimal map of a space with a free interval has dense periodic points and is non-invertible.



1.
Introduction

The topology of compact one-dimensional spaces like the interval
[0,1]
or the unit circle influences the dynamics of self-maps of these spaces. As a result, there are theorems in one-dimensional dynamics that are not true for general dynamical systems. Important examples are Sharkovsky’s Theorem or results describing transitive maps on these spaces, see
[
3
,
29
]
. A natural way of generalising the theory of iterations of interval maps is to extend these results first from interval maps to circle maps, then to self-maps of topological trees and graphs, see
[
3
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
29
]
. It is then natural to consider maps of dendrites as the next step, see
[
20
,
23
,
31
,
32
]
, but many results known for the interval are false on dendrites, see
[
12
,
20
,
26
,
32
]
.

Spaces with a free interval (that is, compact metric spaces containing an open subset homeomorphic with the open interval
(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
) form an alternative class of spaces for extending the theory of one-dimensional dynamical systems. These spaces are only somewhere one-dimensional.
A paper of M. Dirbák, Ľ. Snoha, V. Špitalský
[
14
]
and its continuation
[
13
]
, present a systematic study of dynamical properties of self-maps of spaces with a free interval. Earlier results concerning the dynamics of such spaces are contained in
[
1
,
18
,
21
]
, see the discussion in
[
14
]
, cf. the more recent paper
[
28
]
and T. Drwięga’s PhD thesis
[
16
]
. These papers show that many features of one-dimensional dynamics actually depend on the presence of an interval-like component in the phase space rather than its global structure. Furthermore, there are uncountably many pairwise non-homeomorphic spaces with a free interval (for example, this family contains all dendrites whose branch points are not dense, in particular all dendrites with a closed set of endpoints or all compactifications of a ray
[0,\infty)
, see
[
4
,
27
]
). An easy example of the space that is covered by these results is the Warsaw circle.

In particular,
[
14
, Theorem C]
provides the following dichotomy for transitive maps on a space with a free interval (we provide definitions of all terms used here in the forthcoming sections): every transitive map
f
of such a space is either non-minimal, relatively strongly mixing, non-invertible, has positive topological entropy and dense periodic points (
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
) or it is minimal and invertible, with the map cyclically permuting a disjoint union of
k\geq 1
circles and
f^{k}
is topologically conjugate to an irrational rotation of the unit circle (
[
14
, Theorem C(2)]
).

The main aim of the present paper is to provide a short and almost self-contained proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
, see the proof of Theorem
5
below.
We call this proof
almost self-contained
because we still need two results from the general theory of topological dynamical systems. One is a variant of a result of Kinoshita that guarantees the existence of a dense set of non-transitive points in a non-minimal transitive system (see Theorem
3
,
[
15
, Fact 2]
,
[
24
, Theorem 4.3.1]
, or
[
22
]
for the original proof), the second is a result describing the structure of transitive but not totally transitive systems using the notion of a regular periodic decomposition (RPD), see Lemma
6
and
[
5
]
. We note that these results, together with
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
are also used in the original proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
.
Our proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
is based on two observations that are relatively easy to prove (Theorems
1
and
4
). We show in Theorem
1
that every weakly mixing map of a space with a free interval must be mixing and have positive entropy. Theorem
1
generalises
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
. Theorem
4
says that a transitive but not minimal map of a space with a free interval has dense periodic points and is noninvertible. The proof of the latter combines ideas from
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
30
]
.

Finally, note that although Theorem
1
is a direct corollary of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
, it was proved independently by the first named author of the present paper around the same time that the results of
[
14
]
were being written (this is acknowledged in
[
14
]
, see reference [Kw11] therein). We also note that the original proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
uses
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
and that a reference to Theorem
1
may replace the reference to
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
. Therefore, our proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
seems to be more direct than the original one. It also yields
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
as an easy corollary, see Theorem
2
.

2.
Terminology and notation

Let
(X,\rho)
be a compact metric space. A
map
(of
X
) means here a
continuous
map (from
X
to itself). Given
n\geq 0
, we write
f^{n}
for the
n
-th iterate of the map
f
with the convention that
f^{0}=\text{id}_{X}
. The
orbit
of a point
x
is the set
\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)=\{f^{n}(x):n\geq 0\}
. A
transitive point
is a point
x\in X
whose orbit is dense in
X
. We call a map
f\colon X\to X

transitive
if for every two nonempty open sets
U,V\subseteq X
there is
k\geq 0
such that
f^{k}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset
. We write
\Trans(f)
for the set of all transitive points and
\Intrn(f)
for its complement
X\setminus\Trans(f)
. If
X
has no isolated points, then
f
is transitive if and only if
\Trans(f)
is a dense
G_{\delta}
-set, see
[
24
, Theorem 2.2.2]
. Note that if
f
is transitive, then the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)
of an intransitive point
x\in\Intrn(f)
is nowhere dense. Indeed, if
U=\interior\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)}\neq\emptyset
, then
U
would contain a transitive point
y
but then the orbit of
y
would never intersect the nonempty open set
X\setminus\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)}
, which gives a contradiction. A map
f
is
totally transitive
if for every
n\geq 1
the map
f^{n}
is transitive. We say that
f\colon X\to X
is
weakly mixing
if the product map
f\times f\colon X\times X\to X\times X
is transitive. Note that weak mixing is equivalent to the fact that for every
m\geq 1
the
m
-fold product map
f^{\times m}
on the
m
-fold Cartesian product

X^{m}=\underbrace{X\times\ldots\times X}_{\text{$m$ times}}

is transitive (see
[
6
]
).
Finally,
f
is
mixing
if for every nonempty open sets
U,V\subseteq X
there exists
N\geq 0
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset
for every
n\geqslant N
. For a map
f\colon X\to X
we have the following implications:

\text{$f$ mixing}\Longrightarrow\text{$f$ weakly mixing}\Longrightarrow\text{$f$ totally transitive}\Longrightarrow\text{$f$ transitive}.

Only the middle implication is a non-trivial one (see e.g.
[
29
, Theorem 2.7]
for the proof). None of the implications above can be reversed.

A map
f
is
sensitive to initial conditions
(or simply
sensitive
) if there is a constant
\alpha>0
, called a
sensitivity constant
, such that for each
x\in X
and every
\delta>0
there is
y\in X
with
d(x,y)<\delta
and
d(f^{k}(x),f^{k}(y))>\alpha
for some
k>0
.

A set
I\subseteq X
is an
arc
if
I
is homeomorphic to the unit interval
[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. In this paper we will follow the terminology used in
[
14
]
. We indicate here in parentheses differences with the terminology used in
[
18
]
.

An open subset
J\subseteq X
is a
free interval
(
free arc
) if
J
is homeomorphic to the open interval
(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. If, in addition,
X
is connected and for each
x\in J
the set
X\setminus\{x\}
has exactly two connected components, then we say that
J
is a
disconnecting interval
. The latter definition comes from
[
1
]
. In
[
14
, Appendix B]
relations between several ‘natural’ definitions of a
disconnecting interval are discussed.

An arc
I
is a
free arc
(
closed interval
) (in
X
) if there is a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to I
such that the set
\varphi((0,1))
is a free interval, that is, it is open in
X
. In fact, if
I
is a free arc in
X
, then for every homeomorphism
\psi\colon[0,1]\to I
we have that the set
\psi((0,1))
is open in
X
. (Let
\varphi
be a homeomorphism from the definition of a free arc (closed interval). Then

\Psi=\psi^{-1}\circ\varphi\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a homeomorphism, so
\Psi((0,1))=(0,1)
, hence
\varphi((0,1))=\psi((0,1))
.)

The closure of a free interval in
X
need not be a free arc. For example, there is a free interval which is an open dense subset of the topologist sine curve (
\sin\frac{1}{x}
continuum). But a free interval always contains a free arc; therefore, a space
X
has a free arc if and only if it has a free interval.

Given two free arcs
I,J\subseteq X
and a map
f\colon X\to X
we say that
I

f
-covers

J
if there exists a free arc
K\subseteq I
such that
f(K)=J
. Below, we recall basic properties of the
f
-covering relation.
These results are based on the following elementary observation.

Lemma 1
.

If
g\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a continuous surjection, then for any choice of
0\leqslant c<d\leqslant 1
there exist
0\leqslant a<b\leqslant 1
such that
g([a,b])=[c,d]
.

Proof.

Since
g
is a surjection, we find
x_{0},x_{1}\in[0,1]
such that
g(x_{0})=c
and
g(x_{1})=d
. Without loss of generality
x_{0}<x_{1}
. Take

y_{0}:=\sup\{x\in[x_{0},x_{1}]:g(x)=c\}.

The point
y_{0}
is well-defined as the supremum of a nonempty set bounded from above.
Because
g
is continuous, we have
g(y_{0})=c
, so
y_{0}<x_{1}
. Now take

y_{1}:=\inf\{x\in[y_{0},x_{1}]:g(x)=d\}.

By continuity of
g
, we have
g(y_{1})=d
, so
y_{0}<y_{1}
. Furthermore,
[c,d]=[g(y_{0}),g(y_{1})]\subseteq g([y_{0},y_{1}])
by the intermediate value theorem.

If there existed
x
such that
y_{0}<x<y_{1}
and
g(x)<c
, then using again the intermediate value property we would obtain
y\in(x,y_{1})
such that
g(y)=c
and
y_{0}<y
, which contradicts the choice of
y_{0}
. By a similar argument there is no
x
satisfying
y_{0}<x<y_{1}
and
g(x)>d
. Hence
g([y_{0},y_{1}])=[c,d]
.
∎

The next result, Lemma
2
is adapted from
[
2
, p. 590]
. The result is well-known for graph maps, but since we will apply it in a more general setting, we provide a proof.

Lemma 2
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space,
I,J,K,L\subseteq X
be free arcs in
X
, and let
f,g\colon X\to X
be maps. Then

(i)

If
I

f
-covers
I
, then there exists
x\in I
such that
f(x)=x
;

(ii)

If
I\subseteq K
,
L\subseteq J
and
I

f
-covers
J
, then
K

f
-covers
L
;

(iii)

If
I

f
-covers
J
and
J

g
-covers
K
, then
I

(g\circ f)
-covers
K
;

(iv)

If
\psi\colon[0,1]\to J
is a homeomorphism and
0\leq a<b\leq c<d\leq 1
are such that for some

0\leq t_{0}\leq a
,
b\leq t_{1}\leq c
, and
d\leq t_{2}\leq 1
we have
\psi(t_{i})\in f(I)
for
i=0,1,2
, then
I

f
-covers
\psi([t_{0},t_{1}])
or
\psi([t_{1},t_{2}])
(possibly both);

(v)

If
J\subseteq f(I)
and
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq J
are two free arcs such that
K_{1}\cap K_{2}
is at most one point, then
I

f
-covers
K_{1}
or
I

f
-covers
K_{2}
.

Proof.

We begin with a proof of
(i)
. Let
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to I
be a homeomorphism such that
\varphi((0,1))
is open in
X
. Since
I

f
-covers
I
, there exists
J\subseteq I
such that
f(J)=I
. Take
a,b\in[0,1]
such that
J=\varphi([a,b])
. Then
g:=\varphi^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi\colon[a,b]\to[0,1]
is a continuous surjection.
If
g(x)<x
for all
x\in[a,b]
, then
1\notin g([a,b])=[0,1]
, which is a contradiction; similar reasoning shows that it is not possible that
g(x)>x
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Thus there are
x_{1},x_{2}\in[a,b]
such that
g(x_{1})-x_{1}\geq 0
and
g(x_{2})-x_{2}\leq 0
. From the intermediate value property, there exists
x_{0}\in[x_{1},x_{2}]\subseteq[a,b]
such that
g(x_{0})=x_{0}
. Then
f(\varphi(x_{0}))=\varphi(x_{0})
.

Next, we prove
(ii)
. Let
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to K
,

\psi\colon[0,1]\to J
be homeomorphisms. Take
0\leq a<b\leq 1
and
0\leq c<d\leq 1
such that
\varphi([a,b])=I
and
\psi([c,d])=L
. Consider
g\colon[a,b]\to[c,d]
given by

g=\psi|_{[c,d]}^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi|_{[a,b]}
. It follows from our assumption that
g
is a surjection. Applying Lemma
1
we get the desired conclusion.

Note that
(iii)
is an immediate consequence of
(ii)
.

For a proof of
(iv)
take a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to I
. By assumption for each
i\in\{0,1,2\}
there exists
s_{i}\in[0,1]
such that
\psi(t_{i})=f(\varphi(s_{i}))
. We define

C=\bigcup\{[s_{0}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}]\subseteq[0,1]:s_{1}\in[s_{0}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}],\ f(\varphi([s_{0}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}]))\subseteq J\}.

Recall that
U=\interior J
is a neighbourhood of
\psi(t)
for every
0<t<1
. In particular,
U
is a neighbourhood of
\psi(t_{1})
. Therefore, by continuity of
f\circ\varphi
, the set
(f\circ\varphi)^{-1}(U)
is an open neighbourhood of every
s\in[0,1]
such that
f\circ\varphi(s)\in U
. It follows that the set
C
is nonempty, closed and has nonempty interior.
Notice also that
C
is connected as a union of connected sets with nonempty intersection. Therefore,
C=[s_{0}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}]
for some
s_{0}^{\prime}<s_{2}^{\prime}
and
s_{0}^{\prime}\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
1
it is now enough to show that there is
i\in\{0,1\}
such that
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]\subseteq g(C)
where
g:=\psi^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi|_{C}
. If
C=[0,1]
, then we are done. Otherwise, if
s_{0}^{\prime}>0
, then
f(\varphi(s_{0}^{\prime}))\in\{\psi(0),\psi(1)\}
, by maximality of
C
. Thus
\varphi([t_{i},t_{i+1}])\subseteq f(\varphi(C))
for some
i\in\{0,1\}
. An analogous reasoning applies when
s_{1}^{\prime}<1
. Using Lemma
1
we obtain that
I

f
-covers
\psi([t_{i},t_{i+1}])
for some
i\in\{0,1\}
.

To finish the proof, note that
(v)
follows from
(iv)
.
∎

We refrain from recalling the definition of topological entropy, we refer to
[
3
]
. We will just need the following easy criterion for positivity of topological entropy, see

Lemma 3

(Horseshoe lemma)
.

If
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq J
are two disjoint free arcs such that
K_{1}\cup K_{2}\subseteq f(K_{1})\cap f(K_{2})
,
then the topological entropy
h(f)
of

is positive.

3.
When does weak mixing imply mixing?

We recall
[
18
, Lemma 3.1]
and provide a proof for completeness.

Lemma 4
.

If
f\colon X\to X
is a transitive map and
x\in X
is a transitive point such that for each neighbourhood
W
of
x
we can find an
N
with
f^{n}(W)\cap W\neq\emptyset
for all
n\geqslant N
, then
f
is mixing.

Proof.

Fix nonempty open sets
U,V\subseteq X
.
Let
l\geq 0
be such that
W^{\prime}=U\cap f^{-l}(V)\neq\emptyset
. Since
W^{\prime}
is open and
x
is a transitive point, there is
k\geq 0
such that

W=f^{-k}(W^{\prime})=f^{-k}(U\cap f^{-l}(V))

is a neighbourhood of
x
. Take
N\geq 0
such that
f^{n}(W)\cap W\neq\emptyset
for
n\geqslant N
. Hence, for
n\geq N
and
y\in W\cap f^{-n}(W)
we have

f^{k}(y),f^{n+k}(y)\in W^{\prime}
, in particular
f^{k}(y)\in U
and
f^{n+k+l}(y)\in V
. Therefore,
f^{m}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset
for
m\geqslant N+l
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

If
X
has a free interval, then every weakly mixing map of
X
is mixing and has positive topological entropy.

Proof.

Let
J
be a free interval in
X
and let
\varphi\colon(0,9)\to J
be a homeomorphism. Define

\displaystyle A=\varphi([1,2]),\quad B=\varphi([3,4]),\quad C=\varphi([5,6]),\quad D=\varphi([7,8]),

\displaystyle I_{j}=\varphi((2j,2j+1))\ \text{for}\ 0\leqslant j\leqslant 4,

\displaystyle I^{*}=I_{0}\times I_{1}\times\ldots\times I_{4}\subseteq X^{5}.

Since
g=f^{\times 20}\colon X^{20}\to X^{20}
is transitive and
A,B,C,D
have nonempty interiors, we can find
k\geqslant 0
such that

g^{k}(A^{5}\times B^{5}\times C^{5}\times D^{5})\cap I^{*}\times I^{*}\times I^{*}\times I^{*}\neq\emptyset.

We conclude from Lemma
2
(iv)
that each of the arcs
A,B,C,D
has to
f^{k}
-cover at least three distinct arcs among
A,B,C,D
. In particular, there are
K_{0},K_{1}\in\{A,B,C,D\}
with
K_{0}\cap K_{1}=\emptyset
such that
A
, respectively
D
,
f^{k}
-covers
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
.

Now, note that
K_{i}
(for
i=0,1
) also has to
f^{k}
-cover at least one arc in
\{A,D\}
.
It follows from Lemma
2
(iii)
that

K_{0}\cup K_{1}\subseteq f^{2k}(K_{0})\cap f^{2k}(K_{1}).

Hence
f^{2k}
has a two horseshoe, so the entropy is positive.

To prove mixing, we need to modify this reasoning. We use transitivity of
h\times h
, where
h=f^{\times 5}\colon X^{5}\to X^{5}
to find
n\geqslant 0
such that

(h\times h)^{n}(K_{0}^{5}\times K_{0}^{5})\cap\left(I^{*}\times h^{-1}(I^{*})\right)\neq\emptyset.

In other words,
f^{j}(K_{0})\cap I_{i}\neq\emptyset
for
j=n,n+1
and
i=0,1,\ldots,4
. It follows from Lemma
2
(iv)
that there are arcs
E,E^{\prime}\in\{A,D\}
such that
K_{0}

f^{n}
-covers
E
and
f^{n+1}
-covers
E^{\prime}
. But, as we have seen above,
K_{0}
is
f^{k}
-covered by both
A
and
D
. Therefore by Lemma
2
(iii)

K_{0}

f^{n+k}
-covers and
f^{n+k+1}
-covers itself. Applying Lemma
2
(iii)
again, we see that
K_{0}

f^{m}
-covers
K_{0}
for every integer
m
in the cofinite set

\{\alpha\cdot(n+k)+\beta\cdot(n+k+1):\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Now let
x\in\Trans(f)
belong to a free arc.
Fix an open neighbourhood
W
of
x
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
W=J
is a free interval in the above reasoning. Since we know from the above that the set

\{n\geq 0:f^{n}(W)\cap W\neq\emptyset\}
is cofinite, we conclude that
f
is mixing by Lemma
4
.
∎

We can now provide a simple proof of
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 2
.

If
X
is a compact and connected metric space with a disconnecting interval, then each totally transitive map of
X
is mixing.

Proof.

By
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
each transitive map of
X
has a dense set of periodic points. Every totally transitive map with a dense set of periodic points is weakly mixing, see
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
. The result now follows from Theorem
1
.
∎

4.
Transitivity and dense periodicity

A point
x\in X
is
periodic
if there is
n>0
such that
f^{n}(x)=x
. Let
\Per(f)
denote the set of periodic points of
f
. We will need the following lemma saying that if periodic points for a transitive map are somewhere dense, then the set of periodic points is dense in
X
. The result is probably well-known, but we were unable to find a proof other than the proof of
[
16
, Lemma 8.5]
, so we provide our own.

Lemma 5
.

If
f\colon X\to X
is a transitive map and for a nonempty open set
W\subseteq X
we have
W\subseteq\overline{W\cap\Per(f)}
, then
\overline{\Per(f)}=X
.

Proof.

Fix a nonempty open set
V\subseteq X
. By transitivity, there is
k\geq 0
such that
U=W\cap f^{-k}(V)\neq\emptyset
. Using our assumption, we find
x\in\Per(f)\cap U
. Hence
f^{k}(x)\in\Per(f)\cap V
, which ends the proof.
∎

A map
f\colon X\to X
is
minimal
if
\Trans(f)=X
. It is known that a transitive map is either minimal or the set
\Intrn(f)
of points whose orbits are non-dense is dense in
X
. This is a result due to Kinoshita
[
22
]
, see
[
24
, Theorem 4.3.1]
or
[
15
, Fact 2]
for other proofs.

Theorem 3
.

If
X
has no isolated points and
f\colon X\to X
is a map, then
\Intrn(f)
is either empty or dense in
X
.

The main purpose of this section is to provide a direct proof of the following theorem:

Theorem 4
.

A set
D\subset X
is
regular closed
if it is equal to the closure of its interior. A
regular periodic decomposition
(RPD) for a map
f\colon X\to X
is a finite sequence
\mathcal{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{k-1})
of nonempty regular closed sets that cover
X
and satisfy
f(D_{i})\subset D_{i+1\ \text{mod}\ k}
for all
0\leqslant i<k
and
D_{i}\cap D_{j}
is nowhere dense for
i\neq j
. We call a RPD
terminal
if it is of maximal length among all RPDs for
f
. By
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
a RPD is terminal if and only if
f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
is totally transitive for
0\leqslant i<k
. We recall the following criterion for the existence of terminal RPD when the space has a free interval, see
[
14
, Lemma 7]
.

Lemma 6
.

If
X
has a free interval
J
and
f\colon X\to X
is transitive map with
\Per(f)\cap J\neq\emptyset
, then
f
has a terminal RPD.

We say that
f
is
relatively mixing
if there is a RPD
\mathcal{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{k-1})
such that
f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
is mixing for each
0\leqslant i<k
. We are ready to provide a new proof of
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
. Recall that
[
14
, Theorem C]
describes a dichotomy for transitive maps on spaces containing a free interval; the first part referred to as
[
14
, Theorem C(1)]
(presented below) describes non-minimal maps, while the second alternative
[
14
, Theorem C(2)]
describes minimal maps of such spaces.

Theorem 5
.

If
X
has a free interval and
f\colon X\to X
is a transitive non-minimal map, then
f
is relatively mixing, non-invertible, has positive topological entropy, and dense periodic points.

Proof.

By Theorem
4
we have
\Per(f)
is dense in
X
.
By Lemma
6
there exists a terminal RPD for
f
, say
\mathcal{D}=(D_{0},\ldots,D_{k-1})
. Thus
f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
is totally transitive for each
0\leqslant i<k
. Then

f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
for each
0\leqslant i<k
is a totally transitive map that has dense periodic points, so by
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]

f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
is weakly mixing. Finally, at least one
D_{i}
contains a free interval, let it be
D_{0}
. Applying Theorem
1
to
f^{k}|_{D_{0}}
, we see that
f^{k}|_{D_{i}}
is mixing and has positive topological entropy.
∎

It is natural to ask if the results presented here can be generalised even further.

Problem 1
.

For which continua do the appropriate analogs of Theorems
1
and
2
hold?

Theorem
1
is false for general dendrite maps. It follows from the existence of a zero entropy, weakly mixing, non-mixing, and proximal dendrite map. Recall that proximality of a map is equivalent to the fact that the only periodic point of that map is a fixed point which is also the only point that is a minimal point for the system. Such a map of the Ważewski dendrite was constructed in
[
20
]
and proved to have zero entropy in
[
12
]
.

Benjamin Vejnar asked whether the assumption of the existence of a disconnecting interval in
X
in Theorem
2
could be relaxed to:
X
is a compact and connected space with uncountably many cut-points (i.e. points whose removal disconnects
X
). The answer is negative already for dendrites. It is known that every point in the dendrite which is not an endpoint is a cut-point. Therefore, the examples from
[
20
]
discussed above show that assuming the existence of uncountably many cut-points cannot replace the existence of a free arc in
X
in Theorem
2
.

Nevertheless, it is possible that every weakly mixing map with a dense set of periodic points on a space with uncountably many cut points is mixing (that is,
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
generalises to spaces with uncountably many cut points).

Another intriguing question concerns the specification property introduced by R. Bowen
[
10
]
, see also
[
25
]
. Specification is a kind of stronger, uniform variant of mixing. A. M. Blokh proved that an interval map has the specification property if and only if it is mixing and later extended this result to maps of topological graphs, see
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. For other proofs, see
[
2
,
11
,
19
]
.

Therefore, a natural problem is how far this result can be generalised.

Problem 2
.

For which continua does every mixing map have the specification property? Does every mixing map of a space with a free arc have the specification property?

C. Mouron (personal communication) informed us that the answer is negative for dendrites, but to the best of our knowledge, the problem seems to be open even for the simplest continua with a free interval, like the Warsaw circle.

In
[
33
]
there is an example of a Cantor set homeomorphism with a dense set of periodic points and zero entropy, whose only ergodic invariant measures are concentrated on periodic orbits (see
[
17
]
for more details). This motivates our last problem. We conjecture that the answer to the first half of the question is positive.

Problem 3
.

Does there exist a mixing dendrite map with a dense set of periodic points and zero entropy? Can the only ergodic invariant measures of such a map be the ones concentrated on periodic orbits?
