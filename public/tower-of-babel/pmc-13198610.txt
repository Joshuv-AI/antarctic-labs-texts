Dual roles of two Tfdps in germline nuclear meiosis and somatic nuclear fragmentation during sexual reproduction in the unicellular organism Paramecium tetraurelia
Authors: Xinpeng Zuo, Zihui Wang, Mengyao Liu, Therese Solberg, Danxu Tang, Zhiwei Wen, Chundi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeiosis is regulated by phase-specific genes to orchestrate nuclear and cytoskeletal dynamics essential for sexual reproduction. The ciliate Paramecium tetraurelia exhibits nuclear dimorphism, harboring two germline micronuclei (MICs) and one somatic macronucleus (MAC) within a single cell during vegetative growth. During sexual reproduction, the MICs undergo meiosis and the MAC deforms and fragments, providing a unique model to study the regulation of diverse nuclear events. Transcription factor DP (TFDP), which heterodimerizes with E2Fs, can bind to specific DNA motifs in promoters of cell cycle-regulated genes to activate or repress their expression. Here, we identified 16 TFDP homologs in P. tetraurelia, representing an exceptional gene family expansion accompanied by functional domain diversification. The functions of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b, which are specifically expressed during sexual reproduction and localize in the old and new MACs, were further investigated. Their depletion resulted in meiotic arrest at metaphase in the MIC, failure of old MAC fragmentation, and abortive cytokinesis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that TFDP1A/1B knockdown primarily causes gene downregulation, with > 60% of downregulated genes being specifically highly expressed during sexual reproduction. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses demonstrated significant downregulation of proteins involved in meiosis, DNA replication, and DNA repair. Critically, multiple downregulated meiotic regulators are essential for proper homologous chromosome segregation and sister chromatid separation, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed MIC meiotic arrest. This study uncovers Tfdp1a/1b as essential regulators of the distinctive meiotic process in P. tetraurelia, providing crucial insights into nuclear dynamics and the regulation of sexual reproduction in binucleate systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s42995-026-00378-1.Keywords: TFDPs, Ciliate, Meiotic cell cycle, DNA repair, Nuclear fragmentation

Introduction

Sexual reproduction harnesses genetic recombination to produce offspring with diverse genotypes, conferring adaptive advantages that sustain evolutionary diversity across generations. Central to this process, meiosis executes ploidy halving through two sequential rounds of divisions (meiosis I and II), introducing genetic variation via programmed homologous chromosome pairing and recombination (Kleckner 1996; Moens 1987; Zickler and Kleckner 2015). Both meiosis I and II are subdivided into four phases: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase (Wilkins and Holliday 2009). Each transitional stage is governed by checkpoint mechanisms and phase-specific regulatory genes that orchestrate sequential control of chromosome and cytoskeletal dynamics, such as cyclins, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), and transcription factors (Farini and De Felici 2022; Gao et al. 2024; Handel and Schimenti 2010; Palacios-Blanco and Martín-Castellanos 2022).

Transcription Factor DP (TFDP) proteins, conserved partners of E2F transcription factors, are key regulators of the cell cycle and DNA replication (Helin 1998; Müller and Helin 2000; Ramirez-Parra et al. 2007). Both TFDP and E2F possess a DNA binding domain (E2F/DP family winged-helix DNA-binding domain) that can recognize DNA motifs in promoters of cell cycle-regulated genes (Blake and Azizkhan 1989; Thalmeier et al. 1989). Eight E2F proteins have been well characterized and although all of them regulate the cell cycle, they do so in different ways: E2F1 to E2F3 act as transcriptional activators and E2F4 to E2F8 as transcriptional repressors (reviewed in Kent and Leone 2019). In humans and primates, three TFDPs have been identified, but only two in other mammals. The association of TFDP1 and TFDP2 with E2Fs directly enhances the DNA binding affinity and transcriptional activation/repression of the heterodimers (Bandara et al. 1993; Helin et al. 1993; Wu et al. 1995). TFDP3 differs from TFDP1 and TFDP2 in that TFDP3/E2F complexes have a diminished DNA binding ability and fail to interact with the E2F consensus site; moreover, TFDP3 inhibits E2F-mediated transcriptional activation (Huang et al. 2021; Milton et al. 2006; Qiao et al. 2007). Both E2Fs and TFDPs contribute to the functional complexity of the E2F/TFDP complex.

Ciliates, a diverse group of unicellular eukaryotes, have become highly valuable research models in biological fields ranging from ecology and taxonomy to genome evolution and epigenetics (Gao et al. 2025; Lyu et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2022, 2025a, b; Ye et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Their nuclear dimorphism, as well as distinctive sexual reproduction processes, also provides a unique opportunity to study the coordination of nuclear events and their regulation during the meiotic cell cycle. Ciliates have two different types of nuclei within a shared cytoplasm: a transcriptionally active polyploid somatic macronucleus (MAC) responsible for gene expression, and a transcriptionally silent diploid germline micronucleus (MIC) that preserves genetic integrity (Prescott 1994). During sexual processes (conjugation or autogamy) of some species (e.g. Paramecium tetraurelia), the MIC undergoes meiosis and mitosis to generate haploid gametic nuclei, following which the MAC deforms and fragments; however, the molecular mechanisms coordinating these spatiotemporally synchronized events in the MIC and MAC remain poorly characterized.

Despite extensive characterization of E2F/TFDP functions in mitotic regulation, their roles in meiosis and nuclear dynamics remain largely unexplored in ciliates. In this study, we searched for Tfdp proteins in P. tetraurelia by blastp and identified 16 Tfdp homologs with different expression patterns. Characterization of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b, which are specifically expressed during meiosis, revealed that they simultaneously regulate MIC meiosis and MAC fragmentation. Notably, we found the expression of many meiosis, DNA replication and DNA repair related genes to depend on Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b. Our results provide insights into the processes that regulate MIC meiosis and MAC fragmentation during sexual reproduction in P. tetraurelia.

Materials and methods

Paramecium tetraurelia cultivation and autogamy induction

Paramecium tetraurelia, strain 51, mating type seven, was cultured in medium made by wheat grass powder (WGP, Pine international, Lawrence, KS) as previous described (Beisson et al. 2010b), which was then bacterized with Klebsiella pneumoniae and supplied with 0.8 mg/L β-sitosterol (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology). Autogamy was induced by starvation.

Gene silencing

DNA fragments of the full length of TFDP genes were amplified and cloned into the L4440 plasmid, followed by transformation into the HT115 (DE3) strain of Escherichia coli. Primers used for PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNAi-mediated gene silencing was carried out as described in Beisson et al. (2010c). Briefly, bacteria with specific silencing constructs were cultured overnight in LB medium, and then 1:100 diluted in 1 × WGP medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The culture was further diluted 1:5 with 1 × WGP medium and grown at 37 °C for 1.5 h to allow the OD600 to reach between 0.07 and 0.1. Isopropyl-β-D-Thiogalactoside (IPTG) was added to the medium at a final concentration of 0.4 mmol/L and the culture was incubated overnight to induce the production of double-strand RNAs (dsRNAs). Then, β-sitosterol was added to the prepared silencing medium and P. tetraurelia was collected, washed with 10 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), and seeded into the silencing medium at a density of ~ 200 cells/mL.

Survival test

Cells that have undergone autogamy were transferred into individual wells containing 0.2 × WGP (K. pneumoniae bacterized and supplied with 0.8 mg/L β-sitosterol) to recover to the vegetative stage with one cell per well. The growth of the cells was monitored for three consecutive days. Cells growing similarly as the control were categorized as healthy, otherwise as sick (fewer cells than control) or dead (only one cell or no living cell).

DNA preparation and microinjection

The entire gene of TFDP1A or TFDP1B, as well as the flanking sequences (431-bp upstream and 218-bp downstream of TFDP1A; 200-bp upstream and 275-bp downstream of TFDP1B; primers in Supplementary Table S1), was cloned into the pCE2 vector using the 5 min™ TA/Blunt-Zero Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotechnology). A codon-optimized Flag-HA (sequence shown in Supplementary Table S1) was inserted before the stop codon TGA of the gene. GFP tagged CENH3A and PTIWI09 plasmids were constructed as previously published (Bouhouche et al. 2011; Lhuillier-Akakpo et al. 2016). The plasmids were extracted from bacteria and linearized by enzyme digestion in the backbone region. Then, the DNA was purified with phenol chloroform (pH 8.0) and cleaned up using Ultrafree-MC Centrifugal Filters (Millipore) as described in Wang et al. (2024).

Paramecium tetraurelia that had undergone autogamy was recovered to the vegetative stage and grown for four to six divisions. Purified DNA was injected into the macronucleus as previous described (Beisson et al. 2010a). Construct-specific regions were amplified by PCR to screen for successfully injected cells, which were then used for follow-up experiments.

Immunofluorescence

Around 150,000 cells were collected and washed twice with 10 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 7.4). Cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in 1 × PHEM buffer (10 mmol/L EGTA, 25 mmol/L HEPES, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 60 mmol/L PIPES (pH 6.9)) for 10 min and then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 in 1 × PHEM buffer for 10 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris buffered saline (0.15 mol/L NaCl, 10 mmol/L Tris–HCl) containing 1% Tween 20, 10 mmol/L EGTA, and 2 mmol/L MgCl2 (TBSTEM) for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight with a 1:500 diluted Rabbit anti-HA primary antibody (3724S, Cell Signaling Technology) at 4 °C, followed by three washes with 3% BSA in TBSTEM and incubated with a goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 546 Secondary antibody (A-11071, Invitrogen) at a dilution of 1:4000 for an hour. Next, cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS buffer and spread onto glass slides, which was then mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant containing the blue DNA stain NucBlue (P36980, Invitrogen).

Imaging

For cells injected with GFP-tagged constructs, around 150,000 cells were collected and fixed in 75% EtOH. Before imaging, cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS, stained with DAPI, and spread onto glass slides.

Cells on glass slides, either after immunofluorescence or with a GFP-tagged construct, were imaged using an Axio Imager D2 (Zeiss) and processed using the Zen 2 software.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR kit (R333-C1, Vazyme Biotechnology) to generate first-strand cDNA. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was then performed with the ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q312, Biotechnology) on the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system. The housekeeping gene ACT1-1 was used as an internal reference control. Primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Amino acid sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

The amino acid sequences of TFDP and E2F in Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Drosophila melanogaster, and Caenorhabditis elegans were downloaded from the Uniprot database with the accession number shown in Fig. 1A. Tfdps in Tetrahymena thermophila (Dpl1: TTHERM_00107000, Dpl2: TTHERM_00047010, and Dpl3: TTHERM_00016410) and P. tetraurelia (gene ID shown in Fig. 1A) were downloaded from the Tetrahymena database (https://tet.ciliate.org/index.php) and the Paramecium database (https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/), respectively. The website of Phylogenetic analysis pipeline by ETE3 (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/ete) was used to align the amino acid sequences and build the maximum likelihood (ML) tree with default parameters (Huerta-Cepas et al. 2016). The tree was visualized using the ITOL website (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork 2007).

Protein domain and structure prediction

The domains of TFDP proteins were predicted using InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). The protein structures were predicted using the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) (Abramson et al. 2024) and visualized using PyMol (Schrodinger 2015).

RNA extraction and mRNA-seq analysis

Approximately 300,000 cells were centrifuged, washed twice with 10 mmol/L Tris (pH 7.4), and frozen in liquid nitrogen until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted with RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa) following the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted RNA was sent to the Novogene company for library preparation and sequencing to obtain paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads.

Paired-end reads were filtered using fastp (v0.24.0) with default parameters to trim the adapters and remove low-quality reads (Chen et al. 2018). Then, the reads were mapped against the MAC genome of P. tetraurelia, strain 51 (https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/files/Paramecium/tetraurelia/51/sequences/ptetraurelia_mac_51.fa) using Hisat2 (v2.2.1) with parameters of -t –dta –no-mixed –no-discordant –min-intronlen 20 (Kim et al. 2019). Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.6) (Liao et al. 2014). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.44.0), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with an adjusted p-value less than 0.1 and at least a two-fold change relative to the control (fold change > 2 or < 0.5) (Love et al. 2014).

Principal component analysis (PCA), heatmaps, and volcano plots were constructed with ggplot2 (v3.5.1). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost) (Kolberg et al. 2023). For Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, the protein sequences of P. tetraurelia, strain 51 (https://paramecium.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr/files/Paramecium/tetraurelia/51/annotations/ptetraurelia_mac_51/ptetraurelia_mac_51_annotation_v2.0.protein.fa) were first annotated using eggnog-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) and then analyzed using the online platform OmicShare (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/) (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021; Mu et al. 2024). Visualization of GO and KEGG analyses were done using the WeiShengXin website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). GSEA (v4.4.0) was used to conduct the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Subramanian et al. 2005).

Results

Transcription factor DPs in Paramecium tetraurelia

To identify Transcription Factor DPs (TFDPs) in P. tetraurelia, we used TFDPs from Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans and Tetrahymena thermophila to perform blastp searches against the protein sequences of P. tetraurelia. In total, 16 homologs of Tfdps in P. tetraurelia were identified (Fig. 1A). Based on whether they originated from whole-genome duplication (WGD) events annotated in the ParameciumDB (Aury et al. 2006), the 16 Tfdps were classified into nine groups (Tfdp1-9). Tfdps belonging to the same group cluster together in the phylogenetic tree and present higher amino acid sequence similarity (Fig. 1A).

In H. sapiens, M. musculus, and D. melanogaster, TFDPs have conserved lengths ranging from 405 to 446 amino acids (aa), with the exception of C. elegans (598 aa) (Fig. 1A). The three Dpls (TFDP-like proteins) in T. thermophila display a marked elongation (776, 816, and 836 aa). In contrast, Tfdps in P. tetraurelia are substantially shorter, spanning 229–356 aa, with the extreme case of Tfdp6 (94 aa). The E2F/DP family winged-helix DNA-binding domain coexists with a heterodimerization domain in TFDPs of human, mouse, Drosophila, and C. elegans, while those in T. thermophila retain only the DNA binding domain. In P. tetraurelia, six Tfdps preserve the two domains, while the remaining members retain solely the DNA binding domain. Multiple sequence alignment of the DNA binding domain revealed significant conservation, with amino acid sequence similarities exceeding 50% among orthologs (Supplementary Fig. S1; Table S3).

Expression profiling of the 16 TFDPs revealed stage-specific divergence (Fig. 1B). Transcriptomic data from the ParameciumDB demonstrated that half of the TFDPs exhibited low constitutive expression (mean expression level < 500) across both vegetative and sexual phases (Arnaiz et al. 2020). Among the highly expressed TFDPs, most showed sustained transcription during asexual proliferation and sexual reproduction (autogamy). Striking exceptions were TFDP1A and TFDP1B, which displayed negligible expression during vegetative growth but were dramatically upregulated during autogamy, especially at the meiosis stage. RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated gene silencing of these 16 TFDP genes (single gene knockdown or double and triple knockdowns of ohnologs) showed that none of them affected cell viability or proliferation during vegetative growth (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). For autogamy (sexual reproduction), only the knockdowns of TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B resulted in phenotypic defects (Supplementary Fig. S2C-F). Therefore, we focused subsequent studies specifically on TFDP1A and TFDP1B.

TFDP1A and TFDP1B are duplicates from a WGD event, sharing 82.97% amino acid sequence identity (Fig. 1A). Both proteins exclusively retain the E2F/DP family winged-helix DNA-binding domain, with no other discernible domains (Fig. 1A). Structural predictions generated by AlphaFold3 further demonstrated a high degree of similarity in their tertiary structures, particularly within the core domain region (Fig. 1C). The DNA binding domain comprises three N-terminal α-helices and two C-terminal β-strands (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. S1B). Superimposition of the predicted structures showed a near-complete topological overlap between P. tetraurelia and other species (H. sapiens, M. musculus, D. melanogaster, C. elegans, and T. thermophila), with root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values below 1.5 Å across all pairwise comparisons, suggesting strong evolutionary constraints to maintain the functional structure.

Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are specifically expressed during sexual reproduction

The life cycle of P. tetraurelia comprises a vegetative stage and sexual reproduction (conjugation or autogamy, with autogamy shown as an example) (Fig. 2A). According to their expression profiles, TFDP1A and TFDP1B are both exclusively expressed during sexual reproduction (Fig. 1B). We further investigated their protein localization by engineering C-terminal Flag-HA-tagged constructs of both paralogs, microinjecting them into the MAC and performing immunofluorescence staining. Neither Tfdp1a nor Tfdp1b was observed during vegetative growth, consistent with their transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2B, C). Upon starvation, both Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b emerged in the MAC (to distinguish it from the new MAC generated later, this MAC is referred to as “old MAC”). This localization persisted with the initiation and progression of autogamy, during which the old MAC underwent threading and fragmentation. In later stages of autogamy when the new MACs are visible, Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b exist in both old MAC fragments and the new MACs, although the signal in the old MAC is fainter than in earlier stages. As autogamy progresses further, Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b eventually disappear. Throughout the entire process, no signal was observed in the MICs (Fig. 2B, C). Taken together, we conclude that Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are expressed specifically during sexual reproduction.

Deficiency of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b affects MAC fragmentation and cytokinesis

In TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B KDs, no discernible phenotypic abnormalities were observed during vegetative growth, indicating that they are dispensable for vegetative growth. When the cells are starved, EV (empty vector) control cells can enter autogamy normally and undergo old MAC fragmentation and new MAC development (Fig. 3A). In contrast, severe defects emerged in the knockdown groups. When the EV control reached 100% in the late stage of autogamy with visible new MACs, only 7.94% of TFDP1A KD and 4.05% of TFDP1B KD cells had undergone normal MAC fragmentation and new MAC development; TFDP1A/1B KD cells completely failed this process (Fig. 3B, C).

In TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B KD, 18.77%, 31.08% and 68.58% of cells retained a single ovoid old MAC without fragmentation at the late stage of autogamy, respectively, indicating that they arrest at the beginning of autogamy (Fig. 3B, C). Moreover, the old MAC of cells that were able to fragment in the knockdown groups exhibit abnormal disintegration, producing several irregular nuclear fragments with heterogeneous sizes and morphology, distinct from the MAC fragmentation pattern of the control (Fig. 3A, B). The percentage of cells with more than two MAC fragments in TFDP1A/1B KD (7.51%) is much lower than TFDP1A (55.95%) and TFDP1B (43.69%) knockdown, and there were no morphologically normal nuclei in the double knockdown, indicating that the effect of the double knockdown is more severe than single knockdowns (Fig. 3C).

We then recovered cells after silencing to monitor their survival and ability to enter the vegetative stage. Eight independent biological replicates involving 184 clonal cultures revealed severe survival defects in TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B KD, with the majority of cells dead or sick (Fig. 3D). This mass mortality starkly contrasted the EV control which exhibited normal proliferation.

Deficiency of Tfdp1a and/or Tfdp1b also induced severe cytokinetic defects during karyonidal division, a critical stage at the end of autogamy which distributes the two new MACs into daughter cells (Fig. 2A). 31.9% of TFDP1A KD cells, 44.3% of TFDP1B KD cells, and 26.7% of TFDP1A/1B KD cells were arrested mid-cytokinesis, characterized by a V-shaped morphology with interconnected daughter cells (Fig. 3E, F). This phenotype was entirely absent in the EV control. DAPI staining revealed that the nucleus of V-shaped cells is abnormal and that even cells whose MACs were arrested still received a signal to divide, even though they had not undergone the necessary nuclear events. Hence, the V-shaped cells exhibit an asynchrony of nuclear events and cytokinesis, suggesting that Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are essential coordinators of nuclear and cell division dynamics during sexual reproduction in P. tetraurelia.

Loss of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b disrupts MIC meiosis in P. tetraurelia

At the beginning of autogamy, the maternal MAC undergoes fragmentation, while the MICs participate in a series of meiotic events (Fig. 2A). We therefore investigated whether the knockdown of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b also affects MIC meiosis. Due to the small size of MICs and frequent obscuration by the larger MAC, direct observation via DAPI staining proved challenging. To overcome this, we introduced and expressed CenH3a-GFP, a fusion protein that localizes in the MICs, enabling visualization of MIC dynamics via GFP fluorescence (Lhuillier-Akakpo et al. 2016). In the EV control, the GFP signal associated with CenH3a initially appeared compact (Fig. 4A). Then, the MICs underwent volumetric expansion and adopted a spindle-like morphology, followed with an elongation phase of the first meiotic division, yielding four daughter MICs. These four nuclei then underwent a second division, resulting in a total of eight haploid MICs.

In the EV control, the highest proportion of cells contained a single MIC, corresponding to either pre-meiotic cells or post-fertilization individuals (Fig. 4B). Paramecium tetraurelia typically has two MICs, and occasional variations (one or three MICs) and rare MIC loss have been observed (Supplementary Fig. S3) (Lhuillier-Akakpo et al. 2016). Cells with ≥ 8 micronuclei, representing completion of meiosis II, accounted for 6.14% of the population (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast, the MIC number was affected in the TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B KD. TFDP1A KD and TFDP1B KD cells predominantly contained two MICs and the proportion of cells with ≥ 8 micronuclei was markedly reduced (3.15% and 3.66%, respectively) (Fig. 4C, D). TFDP1A/1B KD showed a dominant single-micronucleus phenotype (39.35%), most of which were in the spindle-shaped phase (Fig. 4E, F). Concomitantly, the number of cells with four MICs (completion of meiosis I) and ≥ 8 MICs (completion of meiosis II) plummeted (12.57% to 4.44% and 6.14% to 0.3%, respectively) (Fig. 4E).

In the TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B KD, MIC meiosis exhibited significant defects, resulting in a substantial proportion of cells containing MICs arrested at the spindle-shaped stage, with a pronounced signal observed at the equatorial plate, corresponding to the period of chromosome alignment at metaphase (Fig. 4F–I). MICs in the EV control had a balanced morphological distribution, with spindle-shaped and elongation-stage nuclei accounting for 21.2% and 32.28% of the population, respectively (Fig. 4F). TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B knockdowns showed 65.74%, 54.75%, and 88.51% of cells with MICs in the spindle-shaped phase, respectively. Furthermore, the proportion of MICs in the elongation stage was significantly lower in the knockdowns, particularly in the TFDP1A/1B KD, where only 3.72% reached this phase (compared to 32.28% in the EV control).

As autogamy proceeded to the stages in which EV control cells have two new MICs and two visible new MACs, the MICs in TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B knockdowns still could not complete meiosis. These nuclei re-condensed, rather than permanently keeping the spindle shape, and a proportion of cells lost the MICs (Supplementary Fig. S4). Taken together, Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are critical for MIC meiosis and their depletion arrests MICs at metaphase.

Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b deficiency primarily affect the expression of genes differentially expressed during autogamy

Since TFDPs are important transcriptional regulators that control the cell cycle by regulating gene expression, we speculated that the effects of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b depletion on the autogamy of P. tetraurelia also occur via altered gene expression. We therefore investigated their roles in transcriptional regulation by performing mRNA-seq of TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B knockdowns. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptomic data revealed distinct clustering patterns between the control (EV) and the knockdown groups, which segregated into two separate clusters (Fig. 5A). Further analysis also revealed distinct gene expression patterns between the control and the knockdown samples, in which the three knockdowns clustered together in a heatmap, indicating higher similarity in their transcriptional profiles compared to the control, consistent with the PCA results (Fig. 5B).

By analysing gene expression changes between the knockdown groups and the EV control, we firstly confirmed efficient target gene silencing. The level of TFDP1A was reduced 21.6-fold (Log2 Fold change = – 4.43) in the TFDP1A KD, and TFDP1B showed a 22.2-fold (Log2 Fold change = – 4.47) decrease in the TFDP1B KD (Supplementary Table S4). In the TFDP1A/1B KD, TFDP1A and TFDP1B were downregulated 14.2-fold (Log2 Fold change = – 3.83) and 9.3-fold (Log2 Fold change = – 3.21), respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, no cross-targeting effects were observed between TFDP1A and TFDP1B knockdowns, as evidenced by the absence of reciprocal gene expression changes in single-knockdown experiments.

By comparing the knockdowns to the EV control, 481, 454 and 658 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected in TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B KD, respectively, with 450, 428 and 639 of these downregulated (Fig. 5C–E). A total of 344 genes were found to be downregulated in all three knockdown groups, accounting for a significant fraction of the DEGs (Fig. 5F). Genes in P. tetraurelia are categorized into seven distinct classes based on their expression profiles during vegetative growth versus autogamy: Early peak, Early repression, Intermediate peak, Late induction, Late peak, Late repression, and None (indicating no significant expression change between stages) (Arnaiz et al. 2017). Classes other than "None" exhibit specific expression pattern alterations during autogamy. Strikingly, in the TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B knockdowns, the downregulated DEGs were substantially enriched (over 90%) within these autogamy time course differentially expressed classes (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, the Intermediate peak class constituted the largest proportion of DEGs in each knockdown (63.11% in TFDP1A KD, 67.99% in TFDP1B KD, and 48.70% in TFDP1A/1B KD). Genes functionally critical for autogamy in P. tetraurelia appear to primarily be those within the Intermediate peak, Early peak, and Late peak classes, and these three key functional classes represented over 60% of the DEGs in all knockdowns. The significant dysregulation of these developmentally important gene cohorts might underlie the observed failure of the knockdown cells to complete autogamy.

Transcription and cell cycle related genes are downregulated when Tfdp1a and/or Tfdp1b are absent

We then performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for the DEGs. There were few upregulated DEGs in the TFDP1A, TFDP1B, and TFDP1A/1B knockdowns, and no GO terms were enriched. Therefore, only the results for downregulated DEGs are presented (Fig. 6A–C). Significant enrichment was observed for numerous transcription-related GO terms across all three knockdowns, such as transcription by RNA polymerase II, regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, DNA-templated transcription, regulation of DNA-templated transcription, and regulation of gene expression. The DEGs in the TFDP1A/1B KD exhibited an enrichment for GO terms associated with the cell cycle and DNA damage response (including cell cycle, cell cycle process, regulation of cell cycle, mitotic cell cycle, mitotic cell cycle process, and DNA damage response) (Fig. 6C).

We further performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to determine the function of the DEGs. Given that the TFDP1A/1B KD exhibited the most severe phenotypic defects, we performed the subsequent KEGG analysis on the DEGs from this knockdown. A prominent enrichment for cell cycle and meiosis-related pathways was observed within the top 30 significantly enriched KEGG pathways, including Cell cycle (ko04110), Cell cycle—yeast (ko04111), Meiosis—yeast (ko04113), and Oocyte meiosis (ko04114) (Fig. 6D). Within these pathways, we found homologs of genes that are essential for these processes, such as SMC1, Ycs4, Cyclin B, Cyclin E, Cdh1, Cdc20 and Cks, to be downregulated (Fig. 7A). Additionally, multiple pathways central to DNA replication and repair were significantly enriched, including DNA replication (ko03030), Base excision repair (ko03410), Mismatch repair (ko03430) and non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) (ko03450) (Fig. 7B–F). This included homologs of genes such as Orc1, Mcm4, Mcm5, Dna2, ExoI, Lig1, Ku80, DNA Pol λ, HMGB1, PARP and PARG, all of which were downregulated (Fig. 7A). We selected 13 of these genes for validation by RT-qPCR and confirmed that they were significantly downregulated in the TFDP1A, TFDP1B and TFDP1A/1B KDs (Fig. 7G).

Both the GO and KEGG analyses were performed specifically on differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To obtain a more comprehensive view of global gene expression patterns, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) with the data from the TFDP1A/1B KD. Corroborating our findings from the GO and KEGG analyses, processes directly or indirectly related to meiosis, including chromatin, chromosome, DNA polymerase activity, DNA replication, DNA repair, DNA recombination, and DNA damage response, all exhibited a global downregulation trend in the GSEA (Supplementary Fig. S5). In summary, the loss of Tfdp1A/1B causes a mis-regulation of multiple pathways involved in meiosis, DNA replication and repair, resulting in a failure to undergo key nuclear events during sexual processes.

Another case that causes failure of old MAC fragmentation in P. tetraurelia is PTIWI09 overexpression. When plasmids containing the PTIWI09 gene were strongly injected into the MAC, P. tetraurelia exhibited a similar old MAC phenotype as TFDP1A/1B KD, and could not deform and fragment (Supplementary Fig. S6A). We compared the DEGs in PTIWI09 overexpression and TFDP1A/1B KD cells, and identified 338 DEGs with consistent expression changes (two upregulated and 336 downregulated). Among these genes, 29 were functionally known genes not related to old MAC fragmentation. The remaining 309 genes consisted of 60 classified as Early peak, 197 as Intermediate peak, 16 as Late induction, 31 as Late repression and five as None in the Paramecium database (Supplementary Table S5). Further studies focusing on these genes might reveal the mechanism that regulate the old MAC fragmentation.

Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are dispensable for the production of scnRNAs in the MICs

Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b depletion causes widespread gene dysregulation, with strong enrichment for transcription-related GO terms among downregulated genes (Fig. 6A–C). Combined with its impact on micronuclear (MIC) meiosis (Fig. 4), this prompted us to investigate whether transcriptional activity within the MIC might also be compromised upon Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b loss. During autogamy, the MICs are transcribed to produce a class of small RNAs called scanning RNAs (scnRNAs), which associate with the PIWI proteins Ptiwi01 and Ptiwi09 (Fig. 8A) (Bouhouche et al. 2011; Furrer et al. 2017). These scnRNAs are essential for guiding Ptiwi09 sequentially into the old MAC and then the developing new MACs, ultimately facilitating the recognition of transposable elements (TEs) and TE-derived internally eliminated sequences (IESs) in the new MACs (Fig. 8B). To check if the biogenesis of scnRNAs was affected, we leveraged the established dependency of Ptiwi09 nuclear localization on scnRNA binding: knockdown of the scnRNA-generating ribonucleases Dcl2/3 prevents scnRNA production and consequently traps Ptiwi09 in the cytoplasm, abolishing the nuclear signal (Fig. 8C). Therefore, Ptiwi09 localization serves as a reliable indicator of functional scnRNA production.

We next investigated the effect of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b on Ptiwi09 nuclear localization. Upon knockdown of TFDP1A, TFDP1B, or TFDP1A/1B, Ptiwi09 was still observed within the old MAC despite the occurrence of aberrant nuclear development (including impaired fragmentation of the old MAC and failure to form new MACs) (Fig. 8D–F). This persistent nuclear localization of Ptiwi09 indicates that scnRNA production and their association with Ptiwi09 proceeded normally even in the absence of Tfdp1a and/or Tfdp1b. Thus, transcription and generation of scnRNAs in the MICs are independent of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b.

Discussion

An expanded TFDP gene family in Paramecium tetraurelia

TFDP family proteins are crucial transcriptional regulators that form heterodimers with E2F proteins to orchestrate fundamental processes such as cell cycling, DNA replication, DNA repair, cell proliferation and differentiation (DeGregori and Johnson 2006; Kent and Leone 2019; Ren et al. 2002). While humans and primates each possess three TFDP paralogs (TFDP1, TFDP2, TFDP3), and most mammals (e.g., mice, dogs) retain TFDP1 and TFDP2, invertebrates like Drosophila and C. elegans harbor only a single TFDP (Dynlacht et al. 1994; Huang et al. 2021; Miller et al. 2016). In ciliates, T. thermophila possesses three TFDPs (Zhang et al. 2018), whereas our homology analysis reveals an extraordinary expansion to 16 TFDP homologs in P. tetraurelia (Fig. 1A). This remarkable expansion likely stems from at least three rounds of whole-genome duplication events in the Paramecium lineage (Aury et al. 2006).

The expanded TFDP repertoire in P. tetraurelia displays variations in functional domains, in which six of them harbor both an E2F/DP family winged-helix DNA-binding domain and a heterodimerization domain, while the other ten only have a DNA-binding domain (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the expression profiles of TFDPs in P. tetraurelia exhibit different patterns (Fig. 1B). However, only the knockdowns of TFDP1A and/or TFDP1B have phenotypes and play functions specifically during autogamy. Their deficiency resulted in fragmentation defects of the old MAC and impaired MIC meiosis (Figs. 3, 4). Despite the high sequence similarity between Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b, their functions appear not to be entirely equivalent, as the loss of either gene alone results in abnormal phenotypes. However, the more severe phenotype observed upon simultaneous ablation of both genes compared to single knockdowns indicate partial functional redundancy. Hence, Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b have acquired distinct, essential functions, but retain a degree of functional overlap.

Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b affect the expression of meiosis-related genes

In P. tetraurelia, depletion of Tfdp1a and/or Tfdp1b severely disrupts meiosis, characterized by chromosomes aligning at the metaphase plate but failing to segregate (Fig. 4). Canonically, the E2F/TFDP complex regulates cell cycle progression through transcriptional control of target genes, and our results suggest a similar transcriptional mechanism involving Tdfp1a and Tfdp1b in MIC meiosis. Consistent with the exclusively macronuclear localization of TFDP proteins, we propose that their impact on MIC meiosis is mediated through the transcriptional regulation of effectors within the MAC.

Transcriptome analysis following TFDP1A/1B knockdown revealed widespread downregulation of gene expression (Fig. 5). E2F/TFDP complexes are known to function as context-dependent transcriptional activators or repressors during distinct cell cycle phases (Kent and Leone 2019). In our knockdowns, there was a clear overrepresentation of downregulated genes, suggesting that Tfdp1a/1b in P. tetraurelia primarily act as transcriptional activators at the beginning of autogamy. It is important to note, however, that achieving perfect synchrony during autogamy is challenging in P. tetraurelia. Consequently, the collected samples encompassed cells at various meiotic stages, and the observed phenotypic and transcriptomic results represent population-averaged effects.

Loss of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b caused the suppression of many meiosis-related genes (e.g. Smc1, Ycs4, Cyclin B, Cdc20, Cdh1 and Cks) (Fig. 7A, B, G). Smc1, one of the core components of the cohesin complex, is essential for the formation of sister chromatid cohesion which ensures proper chromosomes segregation and homologous recombination (Ishiguro 2019; Lee and Orr-Weaver 2001; Peters and Nishiyama 2012). Ycs4 is a component of the condensin complex in yeast which is required for chromosome condensation, double-strand break formation and homologous recombination during meiosis (Lu et al. 2019; Sarkar et al. 2021). Cdc20 and Cdh1 are essential activators of APC/C, binding at distinct stages and conferring substrate specificity (Visintin et al. 1997). APCCdc20 targets Securin and Cyclin B (both can inhibit the activity of Seperase) for degradation, which then activates Seperase to cleave cohesin complexes, enabling separation of homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids (Fan et al. 2006; Pesin and Orr-Weaver 2008). APCCdh1 exhibits broader substrate specificity and is crucial for regulating the levels of multiple meiotic cell cycle regulators (Ostapenko and Solomon 2024; Tanno et al. 2020). Cks are regulators of MPF (maturation promoting factor, a complex of CDK1 and Cyclin A/B) activity during meiotic progression, whose deficiency leads to defects in APC/C activation and spindle assembly (Ellederova et al. 2019; Polinko and Strome 2000). All of these proteins are required for the proper progression of meiosis. Loss of Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b cause meiosis of MICs to arrest at metaphase, corresponding to the segregation of homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids. The function of Smc1, Cdc20 and Cks are directly related to this process and their suppressed expression might explain the failure of MIC meiosis. In addition to the genes annotated by KEGG as meiosis-related pathways, many DEGs are annotated as cell cycle-related which might also be involved in meiosis regulation; however, their functions necessitate further exploration.

Genes involved in DNA repair mechanisms are regulated by Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b

DNA damage, arising during both meiosis and mitosis, necessitates timely repair for genome stability. In other organisms, E2F/TFDP complexes have been shown to be involved in this process, and our findings indicate a critical role for Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b in regulating DNA damage repair related genes (Fig. 7D-F). Knockdown of TFDP1A/1B resulted in a significant downregulation of multiple DNA damage repair pathways, specifically non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), mismatch repair (MMR), and base excision repair (BER). These pathways were also enriched in the KEGG analysis of differentially expressed genes. Within NHEJ, the Ku70/Ku80 heterodimer recognizes breaks, after which nucleases, polymerases, and ligases act sequentially to repair the break; in our knockdown, both Ku80 and the gap-filling DNA polymerase λ (DNA Pol λ) exhibited reduced expression (Lieber 2008; Lieber et al. 2010). In MMR, exonuclease 1 (EXO1) excises mismatched segments prior to resynthesis by DNA polymerase δ and ligation by DNA ligase I (Lig1), and both EXO1 and Lig1 were downregulated in the knockdown (Kunkel and Erie 2005; Li 2008). In BER, which processes damaged bases, key downregulated factors included HMGB1 (which enhances AP endonuclease activity by bending DNA), PARP (synthesizing PAR chains to recruit repair factors), and PARG (degrading PAR chains post-repair) (Krokan and Bjørås 2013; Liu et al. 2010; Prasad et al. 2015; Robertson et al. 2009). Furthermore, Pol λ, required for short-patch BER gap synthesis and Lig1, required for long-patch BER ligation, were also downregulated. Our research in P. tetraurelia revealed that numerous DNA repair-related genes were downregulated upon Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b depletion, suggesting that TFDP proteins may also regulate the DNA repair process in this organism, similar to what has been described in other organisms.

Diverse functions of TFDPs in different organisms

TFDP has been reported in a wide range of organisms, where they exhibit various functions. Mammalian TFDP1 and TFDP2 bind with E2Fs to form heterodimers, essential for both high affinity DNA binding and efficient transcriptional regulation of E2Fs (Girling et al. 1993). The heterodimers bind to the consensus E2F DNA recognition site TTT(C/G)GCGC(C/G) on promoters to activate or repress target genes, which depends on the specific E2F members involved (Ginsberg et al. 1994; Humbert et al. 2000; Morkel et al. 1997; Sardet et al. 1995; Wu et al. 2001). Through transcriptional regulation, TFDP1/2-E2Fs are involved in a wide variety of fundamental life processes, such as the transition between G1 to S phases, induction of apoptosis, cell cycle exit, and cell differentiation (Cuitino et al. 2019; Humbert et al. 2000; Sardet et al. 1995; Zhu et al. 1999). Different from TFDP1 and TFDP2, TFDP3 decreases the DNA-binding activity of E2F and inhibits the E2F-mediated transcriptional activity, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation (Huang et al. 2021; Qiao et al. 2007). In the ciliate T. tetrahymena, Dpl2, complexed with E2fl2, regulates the expression of meiosis-related genes, such as genes required for DNA repair and chromosome segregation (Zhang et al. 2018). Consequently, Dpl2 knockout cells arrest meiosis before anaphase. Similar to T. tetrahymena, we found Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b to be needed for meiosis of MICs in P. tetraurelia. Their loss resulted in a failure of chromosome segregation, with MICs arrested in metaphase. Besides meiosis, many genes regulated by Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are required for MAC fragmentation, which is an important process of sexual reproduction in P. tetraurelia.

TFDPs regulate diverse sets of genes in different organisms and govern different biological processes. During the cell cycle, the TFDP-E2F complex regulates genes required for the transition between G1 to S phases and DNA replication in S phase. Similarly, we also observed genes required for cell cycle transition and DNA replication to be downregulated in P. tetraurelia (Fig. 7C), such as Mcm (MiniChromosome Maintenance complex), Orc (origin of replication complex), Lig1 and Dna2 (factors required for lagging-strand synthesis) (Bae et al. 2002; Blair et al. 2022; Fagundes and Teixeira 2021; Sun and Kong 2010). Despite this, MICs can enter the M phase, suggesting the G1, S and G2 phases have completed. After meiosis, one of the gametic nuclei undergoes mitosis in P. tetraurelia. Since the MICs arrest before anaphase when TFDP1A and/or TFDP1B are silenced, DNA replication during mitosis of the gametic nucleus cannot happen. Therefore, even though Tfdp1a and Tfdp1b are required for both meiotic processes and DNA replication in P. tetraurelia, the latter might be an indirect effect caused by a failure in meiosis.
