A Trace–Path Integral Formula over Function Fields
Yan Yau Cheng

Abstract
We show that an arithmetic path integral over the ℓ \ell -torsion of a Jacobian J ⁡ [ ℓ ] J[\ell] is equal to the trace of the Frobenius action on a representation of the Heisenberg group H ⁡ ( J ⁡ [ ℓ ] ) H(J[\ell]) , up to an explicitly determined sign. This is an arithmetic analogue of trace–path integral formulae which arise in quantum field theory, where path integrals over a space of sections of a fibration over a circle can be expressed as the trace of the monodromy action on a Hilbert space.

2.1
The Path Integral formalism

Let the manifold
M
denote the
phase space
in classical mechanics, which encodes the state of a physical system at a given time. This is typically an even-dimensional symplectic manifold consisting of data about the positions and momenta of particles. A
configuration space
is a Lagrangian submanifold
L
of
M
, roughly corresponding to the position coordinates. (There are also other models for the space of wave functions, but this will be the model that we use in this paper.)

In a quantum system, the phase space is replaced with a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, which encodes all the possible states of a quantum system. The process in which the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is obtained from the phase space
M
is called
quantisation
, and we will discuss this process in more detail in
section

2.3
.

One typical example is when the phase space is a cotangent bundle
M=T^{*}X
for some manifold
X
, in which case we could take the configuration space to be
L=X
viewed as the zero-section in
M
, and
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(X)
is the
quantisation
of the symplectic manifold
T^{*}X
.

Setting Planck’s constant to be 1 so we can ignore it, the Schrödinger’s equation can be written as

\frac{d\psi}{dt}=-iH\psi,

Where the
\psi
is a time-dependent wave function
\psi\in\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(L)
, and
H
is a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
called the Hamiltonian, representing energy.

The normal path integral formalism arises in representing time evolution according to Schrödinger’s equation as

[e^{-iHT}\psi](x)=\int K_{T}(x,y)\psi(y)dy

for some kernel function
K_{T}(x,y)
.

The existence of
K_{T}(x,y)
can be rigorously proved in many practical situations where the phase space
M
, a symplectic manifold, is finite-dimensional. Nonetheless, we will not at all be concerned with rigorous physical constructions in this paper.

The path integral formalism interprets
K_{T}(x,y)
as an integral over paths

K_{T}(x,y)=\int_{P(x,y)}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma,

where
A(\gamma)
is the classical action defined on paths
\gamma:[0,T]\to L
, and
P(x,y)
is the set of paths starting at
y
and ending at
x
.

We can interpret the kernel function
K_{T}(x,y)
as a ‘matrix’ with infinite dimensions, and under this viewpoint one has informally

\Tr(e^{-iHT})=\int K_{T}(x,x)dx.

But since we have interpreted
K_{T}(x,y)
as an integral over paths,
K_{T}(x,x)=\int_{P(x,x)}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma
will be an integral over loops based on
x
, leading to

\Tr(e^{-iHT})=\iint_{P(x,x)}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma dx=\int_{\Omega}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma,

the last being an integral over all loops
S^{1}\rightarrow L
.

2.2
Twists and Trace of Monodromy

Note that any map from
S^{1}
to
L
can be viewed as a section of the trivial bundle

S^{1}\times L\to S^{1}.

From a geometric point of view, it is natural to ‘twist’ this situation slightly and integrate over a space of sections of a non-trivial bundle

Y\to S^{1}

In fact, write

Y=([0,T]\times L)/F,

the mapping torus,
where the monodromy map
F:L\to L
is used to glue
T\times L
to
0\times L
.
Then the sections of this fibre bundle can be identified with

c:[0,T]\to L
such that

Fc(T)=c(0).

The diffeomorphism
F
acts on functions in
\mathcal{H}
via

F\psi(x)=\psi(F^{-1}x).

Then we can write

[Fe^{-iHT}\psi](x)=[e^{-iHT}\psi](F^{-1}x)=\int K_{T}(F^{-1}x,y)\psi(y)dy.

That is,
K_{T}(F^{-1}x,y)
is the integral kernel for the operator
Fe^{-iHT}
.
Recalling from earlier that
K_{T}(x,y)=\int_{P(x,y)}e^{iA(q)}dq
, we have:

\Tr(Fe^{-iHT})=\int K_{T}(F^{-1}x,x)dx=\iint_{P(F^{-1}x,x)}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma dx,

Which is an integral over the space of sections of the fibre bundle.

When the theory is
topological
so that the Hamiltonian is zero, we get

\Tr(F)=\int_{\cal\mathcal{F}}e^{iA(\gamma)}d\gamma

where we use
\mathcal{F}
to denote the space of sections of
Y\to S^{1}.

2.3
Geometric Quantisation

As mentioned above, quantisation refers to the process in which a phase space is replaced with a quantum Hilbert space. More precisely, quantisation is a process

(M,\omega)\mapsto\mathcal{H}.

Which takes a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
to a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. This is also accompanied with a process that sends functions
f
on
M
to operators
\hat{f}
on the space
\mathcal{H}
. There are various ways in which quantisation can be performed, and usually one needs more data than just the symplectic manifold
M
. In this section we will briefly sketch the process of
Geometric Quantisation
.

Suppose that
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold. Assume that the symplectic form is in
\omega\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
and lies in the image of the Chern map
c_{1}:H^{1}(M,\mathcal{O}_{M}^{\times})\rightarrow H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
. Then we can construct a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
such that
\omega
is its Chern class

c_{1}(\mathcal{L})=\omega.

Then the
pre-quantisations
of
M
can be viewed as the global sections of this line bundle

\mathcal{H}^{pre}_{k}=H^{0}(M,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k})=\Gamma(M,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}).

The prequantisation is ‘too big’ as a space, in order to obtain the quantisation one often takes a polarisation of
M
and define the quantisation to be the polarised sections of the line bundle instead.

If we additionally suppose
M
is Kähler with complex structure
J
, then the Kähler-polarised sections is simply the space of holomorphic sections of
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\Gamma_{hol}(M,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}).

This means that the sections of the line bundle
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}
are precisely the algebraic sections of the line bundle.

In particular if
M=X_{hol}
is a complex projective variety and
\mathcal{L}
is a line bundle on
X
with Chern class
\omega
, then we can take the quantisation of
M
to be the space of sections
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\Gamma(X,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k})
.

As an example, when
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
, we can take

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},dx)\quad\text{ or }\quad\mathcal{H}=L^{2}_{hol}(\mathbb{C},e^{-|z|^{2}idzd\bar{z}}).

In fact, by the
Stone-von Neumann Theorem
, these two spaces are isomorphic as representations of the Heisenberg algebra.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given two torsion points

and isomorphisms

c_{\beta,f}+c_{\gamma,g}=c_{\beta+\gamma,g\otimes f}.

Proof.

L_{\beta+\gamma}=L_{\beta}\otimes L_{\gamma}
follows from the fact that the group law on the Jacobian is the same as the group law on the Picard group, which is the tensor product of line bundles.

In order to add the two torsors

and

which is a

This sheaf is clearly the same as the torsor

Lemma 3.8

(
[

, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Given classes
\alpha\in H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
and
\beta\in H^{1}(X,\mu_{\ell})
, the Bockstein operators satisfies the following identity:

\delta(\alpha\cup\beta)=\delta^{\prime}\alpha\cup\beta-\alpha\cup\delta\beta

Proof.

Since
X
is a projective variety, it suffices by
[
Mil13
, 10.2]
to verify the above formula is true in Čech cohomology.

Let
\mathcal{U}=(U_{i})_{i\in I}
be an étale covering of
X
. We write
U_{ij}:=U_{i}\times_{C}U_{j}
,
U_{ijk}:=U_{i}\times_{C}U_{j}\times_{C}U_{k}
, etc.

Suppose
\alpha
is represented by the Čech cocycle
(\alpha_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
. In order to compute
\delta^{\prime}\alpha
explicitly, we first pick for every pair
(i,j)
, a lift
\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}
of
\alpha_{ij}
to
\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Then the class of
\delta^{\prime}\alpha
can be represented by the 2-cocycle whose sections are

(\delta^{\prime}\alpha)_{ijk}:=d(\tilde{\alpha})_{ijk}=\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}|_{U_{ijk}}-\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}|_{U_{ijk}}+\tilde{\alpha}_{jk}|_{U_{ijk}}

which takes values in
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}
. We can similarly represent
\delta_{1}\beta
as a Čech cocyle in the same way.

The cup product
\alpha\cup\beta
is represented by the cocycle

(\alpha\cup\beta)_{ijk}=\alpha_{ij}|_{U_{ijk}}\otimes\beta_{jk}|_{U_{ijk}}

which when lifted to
\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mu_{\ell^{2}}
is the cocyle
\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}|_{U_{ijk}}\otimes\tilde{\beta}_{jk}|_{U_{ijk}}
. Applying the isomorphism
\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mu_{\ell^{2}}\simeq\mu_{\ell^{2}}
given by
a\otimes b\mapsto a\cdot b
, the cocycle representing
\delta(\alpha\cup\beta)
will be

\displaystyle(\delta(\alpha\cup\beta))_{ijkl}:=

\displaystyle(\tilde{\alpha}\cup\tilde{\beta})_{jkl}|_{U_{ijkl}}-(\tilde{\alpha}\cup\tilde{\beta})_{ikl}|_{U_{ijkl}}+(\tilde{\alpha}\cup\tilde{\beta})_{ijl}|_{U_{ijkl}}-(\tilde{\alpha}\cup\tilde{\beta})_{ijk}|_{U_{ijkl}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{jk}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}-\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{jl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}-\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{jk}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{jk}-\tilde{\alpha}_{ik})\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot(\tilde{\beta}_{jl}-\tilde{\beta}_{jk})\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{jk}-\tilde{\alpha}_{ik})\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot(\tilde{\beta}_{jl}-\tilde{\beta}_{jk})\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}-\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{jk}-\tilde{\alpha}_{ik}+\tilde{\alpha}_{ij})\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot(\tilde{\beta}_{jl}-\tilde{\beta}_{jk}-\tilde{\beta}_{kl})\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((\delta^{\prime}\alpha)_{ijk}\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{kl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}+\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}\cdot(\delta_{1}\beta)_{jkl}\right)|_{U_{ijkl}}.

Lemma 3.9
.

Upon identifying
H^{1}(X,\mu_{\ell})
with
H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z})
via
\zeta^{*}
, the Abelian Chern-Simons pairing is symmetric

CS(\alpha,\beta)=CS(\beta,\alpha).

Proof.

Consider the pro-sheaf
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1):=\varprojlim_{i}\mu_{\ell^{i}}
, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1)\xrightarrow{\ell}\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1)\rightarrow\mu_{\ell}\rightarrow 0

which induces the long exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})\rightarrow H^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))\rightarrow\cdots.

Since
H^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))=\varprojlim_{i}H^{3}(X,\mu_{\ell^{i}})=\varprojlim_{i}H^{3}(X,\mu_{\ell^{i}})=\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
is torsion free, the boundary map
H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})\rightarrow H^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))
is a map from an
\ell
-torsion group into a torsion-free group, so must be the zero map. This implies that
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})
is surjective.

Since the quotient map
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1)\rightarrow\mu_{\ell}
factors through
\mu_{\ell^{2}}
, the map
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(1))\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})
factors through
H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell^{2}})
, which implies that
H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell^{2}})\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})
is also surjective. Then from the exact sequence

\cdots\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell^{2}})\rightarrow H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})\xrightarrow{\delta_{2}}H^{3}(X,\mu_{\ell})\rightarrow\cdots

it is deduced that
\delta_{2}:H^{2}(X,\mu_{\ell})\to H^{3}(X,\mu_{\ell})
is the zero map.

Thus, by the previous lemma:

\delta^{\prime}\alpha\cup\beta=\alpha\cup\delta\beta

And finally

CS(\alpha,\beta)=\inv(\alpha\cup\delta\beta)=\inv(\delta^{\prime}\alpha\cup\beta)=\inv(\beta\cup\delta^{\prime}\alpha)=\inv(\beta\cup\delta^{\prime}\alpha)=CS(\beta,\alpha).

∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
K=K_{v}
be a non-archimedean local field. Let
G=\pi_{1}(\Spec K_{v})=\Gal(\bar{K_{v}}/K_{v})
and
\Gamma=\pi_{1}(\Spec\mathcal{O}_{K_{v}})=\Gal(K_{v}^{ur}/K_{v})
be the absolute Galois group and maximal unramified Galois group of
K_{v}
respectively.

Suppose we are given a uniformiser
\pi\in H^{0}(K_{v},\mathbb{G}_{m})
and an element
\alpha\in H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{v}},\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z})
, then

\inv(\kappa(\pi)\cup\alpha)=\alpha(\Frob_{v}).

Where
\alpha\in H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{v}},\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z})=\Hom(\Gamma,\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z})
is viewed as a homomorphism from
\Gamma
to
\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{0}
be an
\ell
th root of
\pi
so that
\pi_{0}^{\ell}=\pi
. Then a cocycle representing
\kappa(\pi)\in H^{1}(K_{v},\mu_{\ell})
is
\sigma\mapsto\frac{\sigma(\pi_{0})}{\pi_{0}}
.

Let
\phi
be the cocycle representing
\kappa(\pi)\cup\alpha\in H^{2}(K_{v},\mu_{\ell})
. Since the map
\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}\times\mu_{\ell}\to\mu_{\ell}
is given by
(q,\zeta)\mapsto\zeta^{\ell q}
, it follows that the cocycle
\phi
is given by

\phi(\sigma,\tau)=\left(\frac{\sigma(\pi_{0})}{\pi_{0}}\right)^{\ell\alpha(\tau)}.

Given an element
\sigma\in\Gamma
, let

denote the unique element of
\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
that is congruent to
\ell\alpha(\sigma)
. This definition can also be naturally be extended to
\sigma\in G
via quotienting by the inertia subgroup first.

On the other hand, define the cocycle
\psi:\Gamma^{2}\rightarrow K_{v}^{ur\times}
giving a class

via

\pi&\text{if }n_{\sigma}+n_{\tau}\geq\ell\end{cases}.

We define a cocycle

via

. Then the coboundary of

is

\displaystyle dc(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\sigma c(\tau)-c(\sigma\tau)+c(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma(\pi_{0}^{n_{\tau}})\pi_{0}^{n_{\sigma}}}{\pi_{0}^{n_{\sigma\tau}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sigma(\pi_{0})^{n_{\tau}}}{\pi_{0}^{n_{\tau}}}&\text{if }n_{\sigma}+n_{\tau}<\ell\\[10.0pt]
\pi\dfrac{\sigma(\pi_{0})^{n_{\tau}}}{\pi_{0}^{n_{\tau}}}&\text{if }n_{\sigma}+n_{\tau}\geq\ell\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\phi+\inf\psi.

And thus
[\phi]=-\inf[\psi]
. Thus it suffices to compute

.

Where:

is the map on cohomology induced by the valuation map

.

•

is the connecting homomorphism of the short exact sequence

.

•

is the homomorphism

Thus we wish to compute

, firstly we see that

1&\text{if }n_{\sigma}+n_{\tau}\geq\ell.\end{cases}

Note that the cocycle

is a lift of

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n_{\sigma}+n_{\tau}<\ell\\

\displaystyle=\nu(\psi).

As desired.

3.3
Computation of the Arithmetic Path Integral

Theorem 3.16
.

The arithmetic path integral evaluates to

Proof.

By

and

is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form, so we can write the sum as a Gaussian integral over a finite field of the form

Where

is a vector representing

is the matrix representing

and

. The factor of

Then applying the theorem in

, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})}e^{2\pi iA(\gamma)}

\displaystyle=\ell^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{\det{A}}{\ell}\right)\left(i^{(\ell-1)^{2}/4}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})|}\left(\frac{\det A}{\ell}\right)\left(i^{(\ell-1)^{2}/4}\right)^{\dim_{\ell}J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})}

Noting that

On the other hand, when

Construction 4.6

Proof.

Note that any

-equivariant, i.e. for all
x\in V

g\circ D(x)=\omega(x,g^{-1}(-))=\omega(gx,-)=D\circ g(x).

The inclusion
M\hookrightarrow V
induces a restriction map
r:V^{*}\rightarrow M^{*}
. Composing with
D
we note that the kernel of the map
r\circ D:V\rightarrow M^{*}
is precisely
M
. Thus (as
g
-modules)

M^{\prime}\simeq V/M\simeq M^{*}.

So the fixed points of
M^{\prime}
are the same as the fixed points of
M^{*}
. Note that
\phi\in M^{*}
is a fixed point if and only if for any
m\in M
:
\phi(m)=\phi(g^{-1}(m))
. In other words,
\phi
must factor through the co-invariant module
M/(\id-g^{-1})M=M/(g-\id)M=:M_{g}
, and thus

(M^{*})^{g}\simeq(M_{g})^{*}.

Moreover, from the exact sequence

0\rightarrow M^{g}\rightarrow M\xrightarrow{g-\id}M\rightarrow M_{g}\rightarrow 0,

we can deduce that (since
M
is a finite module)
M
and
M^{\prime}
have the same number of invariant elements

|(M^{\prime})^{g}|=|(M^{*})^{g}|=|(M_{g})^{*}|=|M^{g}|.

Thus we conclude

|(M^{\prime})^{g}|=\sqrt{|(M^{\prime})^{g}||M^{g}|}=\sqrt{|(M^{\prime}\times M)^{g}|}=\sqrt{|V^{g}|}.

Finally we compute
A_{gM^{\circ},M^{\circ}}
. In our setting
I:=M\cap gM=M
and
n_{I}:=\frac{\dim(I^{\perp}/I)}{2}=0
. Moreover the
\omega_{\wedge}
term vanishes since
M/I
is trivial. Thus we have

A_{gM^{\circ},M^{\circ}}=\sigma\left(\frac{o_{M}}{o_{gM}}\right)=\left(\frac{\det(g|M)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right).

And so
\tr(g|C_{M})=\left(\frac{\det(g|M)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\sqrt{|V^{g}|}
as desired.
∎

4.3
Invariant Spaces of a Symplectic Vector Space

We prove some facts about the invariant spaces of a symplectomorphism
g\in\Sp(V)
. Let
\omega
be a symplectic form on a
2g
-dimensional vector space
V
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
\neq 2
. Let
g\in\Sp(V,\omega)
be a symplectomorphism, and suppose
g
is represented by the matrix
S
under the standard symplectic basis.

Proposition 4.9
.

\det(S)=1

Proof.

Viewing
\omega
as an element of
\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
, consider
\Lambda^{g}\omega\in\Lambda^{2g}V^{*}
. The top exterior power is 1 dimensional and the determinant of
S
is equal to its scalar action on the top exterior power
\Lambda^{2g}V^{*}
. But since
S^{*}\omega=\omega
, it follows that
S^{*}(\Lambda^{g}\omega=\Lambda^{g}\omega)
and thus
\det S=1
.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\chi_{S}(t)=\det(tI-S)
denote the characteristic polynomial of
S
. If
\lambda\neq\pm 1
is a root of
\chi_{S}
with multiplicity
d
, then
\lambda^{-1}
is also a root with multiplicity
d
.

Moreover, if
\pm 1
is a root of
f_{S}
, then it will occur with even multiplicity.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be the matrix representing
\omega
, then
S
satisfies
S^{T}\Omega S=\Omega
. Rearranging we get

S^{-1}=\Omega^{-1}S^{T}\Omega

Thus
S^{-1}
and
S
have equal characteristic polynomials. On the other hand:

\chi_{S^{-1}}(t)=\det(tI-S^{-1})=\det(tS-I)\det(S^{-1})=t^{2g}\det(S-\tfrac{1}{t}I)=t^{2g}\chi_{S}(\tfrac{1}{t})

Thus if
\lambda
is a root of
\chi_{S}
with multiplicity
d
, then so is
\lambda^{-1}
.

Since the product of all the roots of
\chi_{S}(t)
must equal
\det(S)=1
, it follows that if
-1
is a root of
\chi_{S}
, it must occur with even multiplicity. Thus
1
must also occur as a root with even multiplicity.
∎

For an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
S
with algebraic multiplicity
d
, let
S_{\lambda}:=\Ker(\lambda I-S)^{d}
denote the
generalised eigenspace
of
V
corresponding to
\lambda
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that the characteristic polynomial of
S
splits completely in
\mathbb{F}
. Given (not neccesarily distinct) eigenvalues
\lambda,\mu
of
S
, then

1.

If
\lambda\mu\neq 1
, then the spaces
S_{\lambda}
and
S_{\mu}
are orthogonal. i.e. for
v\in S_{\lambda},w\in S_{\mu}
,

\omega(v,w)=0.

In particular this implies that when
\lambda\neq\pm 1
,
S_{\lambda}
is a isotropic subspace of
V
.

2.

If
\lambda=\pm 1
, then
S_{\lambda}
is a symplectic subspace of
V
.

Proof.

1) Let
v\in S_{\lambda},w\in S_{\mu}
. We say
v
has rank
r
if it lies in the kernel of
(\lambda I-S)^{r}
but not
(\lambda I-S)^{r-1}
, and similarly for
w
, we will induct on the sum of the ranks of
v
and
w
.

The base case is when
v,w
are both eigenvectors, in that case

\omega(v,w)=\omega(Sv,Sw)=\omega(\lambda v,\mu w)=\lambda\mu\omega(v,w).

Since
\lambda\mu\neq 1
, it follows that
\omega(v,w)=0
.

For the inductive case, suppose
v
has rank
r_{1}
and
w
has rank
r_{2}
. And assume that
\omega(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})=0
whenever the ranks of
v^{\prime},w^{\prime}
sum to less than
r_{1}+r_{2}
.

Then by the definition of rank,
Sv=\lambda v+v^{\prime}
and
Sw=\mu w+w^{\prime}
, where
v^{\prime},w^{\prime}
have ranks
r_{1}-1,r_{2}-1
respectively (If a vector has rank 0 then it is simply the zero vector). Then

\displaystyle\omega(v,w)=\omega(Sv,Sw)=\omega(\lambda v+v^{\prime},\mu w+w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\omega(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})+\omega(\lambda v,w^{\prime})+\omega(v^{\prime},\mu w)+\omega(\lambda v,\mu w)

\displaystyle=\omega(\lambda v,\mu w)=\lambda\mu\omega(v,w).

And once again since
\lambda\mu\neq 1
, it follows that
\omega(v,w)=0
, completing the proof.

2) To show that
S_{\lambda}
is a sympletic subspace of
V
, it suffices to show that
\omega|_{S_{\lambda}}
is a non-degenerate form, since bilinearity and antisymmetry is inherited from
\omega
.

Suppose that
v_{0}\in S_{\lambda}
satisfies
\omega(v_{0},w)=0
for any
w\in S_{\lambda}
. Note that since
\lambda=\pm 1
, for any
\mu\neq\lambda
,
\mu\lambda\neq 1
and thus by the previous part,
\omega(v_{0},w)=0
for all
w\in S_{\mu}
. Since
V
is a direct sum of all its generalised eigenspaces, this implies that
\omega(v_{0},w)=0
for all
w\in V
. Since
\omega
is non-degenerate on
V
, it follows that
v=0
, proving non-degeneracy of
\omega
in
S_{\lambda}
.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Suppose
g\in\Sp(V)
is semisimple, then it is possible to decompose
V
as a direct sum of
g
-invariant symplectic subspaces

V=S_{1}\oplus S_{-1}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}.

Where
S_{\pm 1}
is the
\pm 1
eigenspace of
g
respectively, and
V_{i}
are subspaces such that the restriction
g|_{V_{i}}
of the
g
-action to
V_{i}
has characteristic polynomial
f_{i}
, where
f_{i}
has no repeated roots.

Proof.

For an irreducible monic polynomial
h(t)
, let
h^{-}(t):=t^{\deg h}h(1/t)
denote the irreducible polynomial with roots being the reciprocals of those of
h(t)
.

Since roots of
f
come in pairs of
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
with equal multiplicity, then for each irreducible factor
h(t)
of
f(t)
, either
h^{-}(t)=h(t)
, or
h^{-}(t)
must also be an irreducible factor of
f(t)
with the same multiplicity as
h(t)
.

Thus we can write

f(t)=(t+1)^{a}(t-1)^{b}f_{1}(t)^{k_{1}}f_{2}(t)^{k_{2}}\dots f_{s}(t)^{k_{s}}.

Where each
f_{i}
is either an irreducible factor satisfying
f_{i}(t)=f_{i}^{-}(t)
, or
f_{i}
is a product of two irreducible factors of the form
h(t)h^{-}(t)
. In both cases
f_{i}(t)
has no repeated roots, since finite extensions of
\mathbb{F}
are separable.
Thus we can decompose
V
into
g
-invariant subspaces

V=S_{1}\oplus S_{-1}\oplus W_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{s}

where
W_{i}
is the vector subspace of
V
corresponding to
f_{i}(t)^{k_{i}}
. Then over the algebraic closure
W_{i}\otimes\bar{\mathbb{F}}
is a symplectic subspace of
V\otimes\bar{\mathbb{F}}
since it is a direct sum of
S_{\lambda}\oplus S_{\lambda^{-1}}
for every pair of roots
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
of
f
. And thus
W_{i}
is also a symplectic subspace of
V
.

Thus it suffices to show that each
W_{i}
can be further decomposed into
W_{i1}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{il}
where each
W_{ij}
is a
g
-invariant symplectic subspaces of
W_{i}
.
We know that the the restricted action
g|_{W_{i}}
has characteristic polynomial
f_{i}^{k_{i}}
.
Let
T
be a matrix with characteristic polynomial
f_{i}
that is also symplectic with respect to a symplectic form
\varpi
, then the block diagonal matrix

\begin{pmatrix}T\\
&T\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&T\end{pmatrix}

with
k_{i}
diagonal blocks will have characteristic polynomial
f_{i}^{k_{i}}
. This matrix is also symplectic with respect to the symplectic form
\varpi^{\otimes k_{i}}
.

Applying a suitable change of basis, we can obtain a matrix
U
that is symplectic with respect to the symplectic form on
W_{i}
.

Finally, the summary in
[
Wal15
, p.7]
states that any two semisimple elements in
\Sp(V)
which are conjugate over
\GL(V)
are also conjugate over
\Sp(V)
. Since both
U
and
g|_{W_{i}}
are semisimple with the same characteristic polynomial, this means that under a suitable basis
g|_{W_{i}}
has matrix
U
. It is clear from construction that the matrix
U
decomposes into
g
-invariant symplectic subspaces each with characteristic polynomial
f_{i}
, so we are done.
∎

4.4
Determining the sum

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
g\in\Sp(V)
be a symplectomorphism. Suppose
V
decomposes into a direct sum of
g
-invariant symplectic subspaces

V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}

Let
\mathfrak{H}_{i}=\mathfrak{H}(V_{i})
be the the unique representation of
V_{i}
with identity central character as described in
section

4.1
. Then

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}_{i}).

Proof.

This follows directly from
[
GH09
, Prop 2.14]
which asserts that

\mathfrak{H}(V)=\bigotimes_{i=0}^{r}\mathfrak{H}_{i}

and the fact that the trace on a tensor product of vector spaces is equal to the product of the traces on each individual component.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Suppose that
g\in\Sp(V)
is a semi-simple symplectomorphism which has characteristic polynomial
f(t)
with no repeated roots, then

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right),

where
\dim V=2n
.

Proof.

First we note that since we have assumed that
f(t)
has no repeated roots, this also means that
g
does not have any eigenvalues equal to
\pm 1
. This is because by
proposition

4.10
any eigenvalue of
\pm 1
must occur with even multiplicity.

Fix a standard symplectic basis of
V
and let
S
denote the matrix representing
g
in this basis. Since
f
has no repeated roots, this means that any matrix in
T\in\GL(V)
with the same characteristic polynomial
f
will be similar to
S
(since neither
S
nor
T
will have any Jordan blocks as every eigenvalue has multiplicity 1).

Furthermore, by the summary in
[
Wal15
, p. 7]
, it is shown that the
\GL(V)
conjugacy class of
S
intersects
\Sp(V)
at a unique conjugacy class. This means that any symplectic matrix
T\in\Sp(V)
with the same characteristic polynomial as
g
will be similar to
S
via a symplectomorphism.

Now suppose
g
has characteristic polynomial

f(t)=t^{2n}+a_{1}t^{2n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}t^{n+1}+a_{n}t^{n}+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}t+1

and construct the block matrix

S=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&C\end{pmatrix}

Where
A
and
B
have dimension
n+1\times n
,
C
has dimension
n-1\times n
and they are

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots\\
1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&1&0&\cdots\\
0&0&1&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&-1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-a_{1}\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-a_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-a_{n-1}\\
-a_{1}&-a_{2}&-a_{3}&\cdots&-a_{n}\end{pmatrix},C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&1&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

It can be checked by a straightforward computation that
\chi_{S}(t)=f(t)
and that
S
preserves the standard symplectic form. Thus under suitable symplectic change of basis,
g
is represented by the matrix
S
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
be this standard symplectic basis, then explicitly
g
is the linear map

g:\begin{cases}v_{i}\mapsto v_{i+1}&i\neq n\\
v_{n}\mapsto w_{1}\\
w_{i}\mapsto-a_{i}w_{1}+w_{i+1}&i\neq n\\
w_{n}\mapsto-(v_{1}+a_{1}v_{2}+a_{2}v_{3}+\cdots+a_{n-1}v_{n}+a_{n}w_{1}).\end{cases}

Now that we have a symplectic basis of
g
, we fix the Lagrangian
M
to be the span of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, the complement
M^{\prime}
to be the span of
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
and we apply
lemma

4.7
to calculate the trace of
g
.

We first determine the set
\mathcal{S}=\{x\in M^{\prime}|gx-x\in M+gM\}
. Noting that
M+gM=M+\mathbb{F}_{\ell}w_{1}
, we let
x=\alpha_{1}w_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}w_{n}
and we require that
gx-x
have no
w_{2}\cdots w_{n}
terms. For each
2\leq i\leq n
, the coefficient of
w_{i}
in
x-gx
being zero implies that
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i-1}
. And thus
\mathcal{S}
is the 1 dimensional subspace spanned by
w_{1}+w_{2}+\dots+w_{n}
.

Letting
x=w_{1}+w_{2}+\dots+w_{n}
, we compute that

\begin{aligned} gx-x&=-(v_{1}+a_{1}v_{2}+a_{2}v_{3}+\cdots+a_{n-1}v_{n})-(a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n})w_{1}\\
&=-(v_{1}+a_{1}v_{2}+a_{2}v_{3}+\cdots+a_{n-1}v_{n})-g[(a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n})v_{n}]\end{aligned}.

Thus we can pick
m_{x}=-(v_{1}+a_{1}v_{2}+a_{2}v_{3}+\cdots+a_{n-1}v_{n})
and
n_{x}=-(a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n})v_{n}
. Then we see that

\begin{aligned} \omega(m_{x}+n_{x},x)&=-(a_{n}+2a_{n-1}+2a_{n-2}+\cdots+2a_{1}+2)\\
&=-f(1)\end{aligned}.

Where
f(1)
is the sum of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
g
.

Thus we can compute the sum

\begin{aligned} \sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega(m_{x}+n_{x},x)\right)&=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega(m_{x}+n_{x},x)\right)\\
&=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\psi\left(\frac{-f(1)}{2}k^{2}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)G(1,\ell)\end{aligned}.

Where the last equality is from
[
Lan86
, p.85]

Finally we compute
A_{M^{\circ}\!,gM^{\circ}}
. We fix the orientation

o_{M}=v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{n}

and so

o_{gM}=g(o_{M})=v_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n}\wedge w_{1}=(-1)^{n-1}w_{1}\wedge v_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n}.

Then we can decompose the orientations via
\iota_{M}=\iota_{gM}=v_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n}
, and
o_{M/I}=v_{1}
,
o_{L/I}=(-1)^{m-1}w_{1}
. We note that
n_{I}=1
and so

\begin{aligned} A_{M^{\circ},gM^{\circ}}&=(G(\tfrac{1}{2},\ell)/\ell)^{n_{I}}\left(\frac{(-1)^{\binom{n_{I}}{2}}\frac{\iota_{gM}}{\iota_{M}}\cdot\omega_{\wedge}(o_{gM/I},o_{M/I})}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\\
&=(G(\tfrac{1}{2},\ell)/\ell)\left(\frac{(-1)^{n-1}\omega(w_{1},v_{1})}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\ell}G(\tfrac{1}{2},\ell)\left(\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\ell}G(1,\ell)\left(\frac{(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\end{aligned}.

Thus finally by
lemma

4.7
we have that

\displaystyle\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))=\tr(g|C_{M^{\circ}})

\displaystyle=A_{M^{\circ}\!,gM^{\circ}}\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega(m_{x}+n_{x},x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}G(1,\ell)\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{2}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)G(1,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}G(1,\ell)^{2}\left(\frac{(-1)^{n-1}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-1}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\left(\frac{(-1)^{n-1}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n}f(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right).

Where the second last equality is due to
[
Lan86
, p. 87]
, which asserts that

G(1,\ell)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\ell}&\ell\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
i\sqrt{\ell}&\ell\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

∎

Theorem 4.15
.

Suppose
g\in\Sp(V)
is semisimple with minimal polynomial
f
. Then

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}(-1)^{(\deg\bar{f})/2}\bar{f}(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\sqrt{|V^{g}|}.

Where
n_{-1}
is the dimension of the
-1
-eigenspace of
g
.
And
\bar{f}
satisfies
f(t)=(t+1)^{a}(t-1)^{b}\bar{f}(t)
and
\bar{f}(t)
has no factors of
t+1
or
t-1
.

Proof.

By
lemma

4.12
decompose
V
as a direct sum of
g
-invariant symplectic subspaces

V=S_{1}\oplus S_{-1}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}.

Where
S_{\pm 1}
is the
\pm 1
eigenspace of
g
respectively, and
V_{i}
are subspaces such that the restriction
g|_{V_{i}}
of the
g
-action to
V_{i}
has characteristic polynomial
f_{i}
, where
f_{i}
has no repeated roots.

Then by
lemma

4.13
we can write

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))=\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(S_{1}))\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(S_{-1}))\prod_{i=1}^{r}\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V_{i})).

Since
g
acts as
\Id
and
-\Id
on
S_{1}
and
S_{-1}
respectively, it is easy to compute the traces using
corollary

4.8
by taking any invariant lagrangian

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(S_{1}))=\sqrt{|S_{1}|}=\sqrt{|V^{g}|},

and

\tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(S_{-1}))=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right).

Combining this with
lemma

4.14
we obtain

\begin{aligned} \tr(g|\mathfrak{H}(V))&=\sqrt{|V^{g}|}\cdot\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\frac{(-1)^{(\deg f_{i})/2}f_{i}(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\sqrt{|V^{g}|}\left(\frac{(-1)^{(\deg f_{1}+\cdots\deg f_{r})/2}f_{1}f_{2}\dots f_{r}(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}(-1)^{(\deg\bar{f})/2}\bar{f}(1)}{\mathbb{F}_{\ell}}\right)\sqrt{|V^{g}|}\end{aligned}.

As desired.
∎

Finally we prove the main theorem.

Proof of
theorem

4.2
.

By
lemma

4.5
and the Stone-von Neumann property, we know that
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{H}(J[\ell])
. And thus

\tr(\Fr_{q}|\mathcal{H})=\tr(\Fr_{q}|\mathfrak{H}(J[\ell])).

Then applying
theorem

4.15
and noting that the
\Fr_{q}
fixed points of
J[\ell]
is precisely
J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})
we obtain our desired result.
∎

5
Proving the Main Theorem

Finally, we combine the results of
theorem

3.16
and
theorem

4.2
we obtain our main theorem with all the signs determined.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
J
be the Jacobian of a genus
g
curve
X
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
For primes
\ell
satisfying
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
, supposing that
\Fr_{q}
acts semisimply on the
\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
vector space
J[\ell]
, we have the equality

\tr(\Fr_{q}|\mathcal{H})=\left(\frac{(-1)^{g}\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}}(1)\det(A)}{\ell}\right)\sum_{\gamma\in J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})}e^{iA(\gamma)}.

Where
\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}}
is the largest monic polynomial dividing the characteristic polynomial
\chi_{\Fr_{q}}
of
\Fr_{q}
that does not have any factors of
(t+1)
or
(t-1)
.

Proof.

By combining
theorems

3.16
and
4.2
we have the following equality where both sides are equal to
\sqrt{|J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})|}
:

\left(\frac{(-1)^{n_{-1}/2}(-1)^{(\deg\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}})/2}\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}}(1)}{\ell}\right)\tr(\Fr_{q}|\mathcal{H})=\left(\frac{(-1)^{(\dim_{\ell}J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q}))/2}\det(A)}{\ell}\right)\sum_{\gamma\in J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})}e^{iA(\gamma)}.

Notice that
n_{1}
,
\dim_{\ell}J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
\deg\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}}
are the dimensions of the
-1
eigenspace,
1
eigenspace, and the direct sum of all the non other eigenspaces respecitvely. Thus we have an equality

n_{-1}+\dim_{\ell}J[\ell](\mathbb{F}_{q})+\deg\overline{\chi_{\Fr_{q}}}=\dim_{\ell}\mathbb{F}[\ell]=2g

Thus we can rearrange the Legendre symbols of the above equality to obtain the desired equality.
∎

Further Research

One possible direction of further research would be to find an intrinsic proof to this trace–path integral formula. The current proof evaluates each side of the formula separately and compares them. A more direct proof that shows the trace and the path integral are intrinsically related could be more enlightening.
