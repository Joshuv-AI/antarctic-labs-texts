Multi-omics analysis reveals cell adhesion molecules as key regulators of immune cell infiltration and adverse outcomes and in vitro validation of CLDN16 in pancreatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zewei Zhuo, Jianming Luo, Guanpeng Liang, Jiahao Li, Qi Yang, Lin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is characterized by complex molecular alterations in cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) that influence tumor progression and treatment resistance. Identifying clinically relevant CAM signatures could improve patient stratification and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we integrated bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA and ICGC with single-cell RNA-seq data to identify survival-associated CAM genes and define molecular subtypes using consensus clustering. We identified 22 CAM genes associated with prognosis and established three molecular subgroups with distinct clinical and immune characteristics. An 11-gene CAM-based risk model (CDH3, CLDN16, CLDN3, CLDN15, ITGB1, NCAM1, SDC1, CD99L2, SLITRK2, LRRC4B, CD58) demonstrated strong prognostic performance across cohorts and effectively predicted chemotherapy and immunotherapy responses. High-risk patients exhibited extracellular matrix activation, immune evasion, and poor response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Single-cell analysis revealed distinct expression patterns of CAM genes across malignant and epithelial compartments. Functional validation confirmed CLDN16 as a pro-tumorigenic factor enhancing invasiveness and reducing apoptosis. Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, with a 5-year survival rate below 10% despite decades of research efforts [1]. The global burden of PAAD continues to rise, with an estimated 510,566 new cases and 467,005 deaths annually, positioning it as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [2]. This dismal prognosis stems from multiple factors: late diagnosis due to nonspecific symptoms [3], aggressive tumor biology [4], and remarkable resistance to conventional therapies [5] including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and emerging immunotherapies. Even among patients eligible for surgical resection, the 5-year survival remains only 20–30% [6], highlighting the urgent need for better molecular characterization and therapeutic strategies.

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), a diverse family of proteins mediating cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, have recently emerged as crucial regulators of cancer progression beyond their classical adhesion functions [7]. In PAAD, CAMs participate in virtually all hallmarks of cancer-from sustaining proliferative signaling and activating invasion/metastasis to shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [8]. Particularly noteworthy is their dual role in tumor biology: while some CAMs (e.g., E-cadherin) act as tumor suppressors by maintaining epithelial integrity [9], others (e.g., N-cadherin, selectins) promote aggressive phenotypes through epithelial-mesenchymal transition [10] and stromal interactions [11]. The dysregulated expression of various CAM families - including cadherins [12], integrins [13], immunoglobulin superfamily members [14], and selectins [15]-has been implicated in PAAD progression, yet a systematic evaluation of their prognostic and therapeutic potential remains lacking.

This study represents a significant departure from previous CAM research in PAAD by moving beyond reductionist approaches to a systems-level understanding of CAM networks. By establishing direct links between CAM expression patterns, clinical outcomes, and treatment responses, our work provides both biological insights and immediately actionable clinical tools (Fig. 1A). The timing is particularly opportune given growing interest in targeting tumor-stroma interactions and the extracellular matrix in PAAD therapy. Our findings may inform the development of novel CAM-targeted therapies while providing biomarkers to guide patient selection for existing treatment modalities - crucial steps toward improving outcomes for this devastating disease.

Methods

Data collection and processing

We obtained transcriptomic data from multiple sources to ensure comprehensive analysis. The Cancer Genome Atlas-Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PAAD) dataset (n = 178 PAAD samples) served as our Training cohort, while the International Cancer Genome Consortium-Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma-Australia (ICGC-PAAD-AU) dataset (n = 267 PAAD samples) was used for independent validation cohort. For single-cell resolution analysis, we incorporated the GSE111672 dataset containing single-cell RNA-seq profiles of pancreatic tumor microenvironments. For immunotherapy-related analyses, two datasets were utilized: GSE78220 (n = 26 patients) and IMvigor210 (n = 348 patients). A Phase 2 trial data of radiation therapy combined with combined anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab) and anti-PD1 (nivolumab) antibodies in PAAD patients were obtained from GSE179351 cohorts. All datasets underwent rigorous quality control, including removal of low-quality samples, normalization using DESeq2 for bulk RNA-seq data, and batch effect correction with Harmony to ensure data comparability across platforms. The gene set “Cell Adhesion Molecules” was obtained from the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/entry/hsa04514).

Identification of prognostic CAM genes

To pinpoint survival-related CAM genes, we first performed univariate Cox regression analysis on all genes in PAAD patients. These were intersected with a curated list of 149 known CAM genes, yielding survival-significant CAM genes. We further analyzed these candidates using search tool for recurring instances of neighbouring genes (STRING) database to construct protein-protein interaction networks, identifying key hub genes based on connectivity scores and biological relevance to cancer progression.

Molecular subclassification of patients

Consensus clustering was performed using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus [16] with the maximum number of clusters set to six. The optimal cluster number was determined by evaluating the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and relative change in area under the CDF curve. Hierarchical heatmaps were generated with pheatmap. Genes with a variance greater than 0.1 were retained. When the number of target genes exceeded 1,000, the top 25% with the highest variance were selected for visualization after ranking. We comprehensively characterized these subtypes by examining their association with clinical parameters (e.g. TNM stage, grade).

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

To elucidate biological differences between extreme subtype, we conducted differential expression analysis (|log Fold Change (FC)|>1, False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05) identifying upregulated and downregulated genes in Cluster 1. To gain deeper insights into the oncogenic mechanisms of the target genes, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the associated mRNAs using the ClusterProfiler package in R [17]. Immune cell infiltration across different clusters was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm.

Prognostic model development and validation

Using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-penalized Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation, we refined the survival-related CAM genes to an optimal gene prognostic signature. The risk score formula incorporated weighted gene expression values, with coefficients determined by partial likelihood deviance minimization. Model performance was assessed by Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curve and time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals. The model was independently validated in the ICGC cohort with consistent performance metrics. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between various features (including clinical characteristics, risk scores) and survival in PAAD. A clinical nomogram was constructed using the rms package in R, and ROC curves were used to assess the predictive performance of each factor for PAAD prognosis.

Comprehensive immune profiling

The tumor immune microenvironment was characterized using six complementary algorithms: Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) (for lymphocyte subsets), xCell (64 cell types), Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) (immune estimation), QUANTitative Immune Sequencing Cell Quantification (QUANTISEQ) (immune quantification), Microenvironment Cell Populations-counter (MCP-counter) (stromal and immune populations), and Estimating the Proportions of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC) (immune and cancer fractions). We computed microenvironment scores and analyzed cell-cell interaction networks to identify immune evasion mechanisms. Statistical comparisons between risk groups were performed using Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Therapeutic response prediction

Immunotherapy responsiveness was predicted using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm, incorporating tumor dysfunction and exclusion scores. We further validated predictions in two anti-PD-1/L1 treated cohorts (IMvigor210 bladder cancer and GSE78220 melanoma) and combined immune checkpoint blockade treatment (GSE179351 PAAD). For chemotherapy sensitivity, we calculated half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for 138 compounds from Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [18]. The prediction process was performed using the pRRophetic package in R [19], where the IC50 for each sample was estimated by ridge regression.

Single-cell analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data for PAAD (GSE111672) in H5 format, along with the corresponding annotation files, were downloaded from the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub (TISCH) database. Quality control and downstream analyses were performed using the MAESTRO and Seurat packages in R [20]. Cells were reclustered based on the t-SNE algorithm. Bar plots were generated to illustrate the expression levels of selected genes across different cell types.

Protein validation

Protein-level validation utilized Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) immunohistochemistry images. This involves searching for CAM-related genes or proteins in the HPA database, followed by exploring their expression patterns across different tissues.

Cell culture and SiRNA transfection

Human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (HPDE), MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1, and AsPC-1 cells were cultured in appropriate media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. CLDN16-knockdown PANC-1 cells were established by stable transfection using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RadioImmunoPrecipitation Assay Buffer (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.

Quantitative Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara) on a real-time PCR system. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH.

Cell apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)/Propidium Iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell invasion and migration

Cell migration was evaluated by a wound healing assay at 0 h and 48 h. Cell invasion was assessed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers. After 24 h, invaded cells were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.3. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of Survival-Associated CAM genes in PAAD

Given the critical role of cell adhesion molecules (CAM genes) in PAAD, we first intersected survival-associated genes in PAAD with CAM genes to identify survival-related CAM genes. As shown in Fig. 1B, a total of 2,817 genes were found to be associated with PAAD survival, and after intersecting with 149 CAM genes, 22 survival-associated CAM genes were identified. The protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 1C) highlights key hub genes such as CLDN1, NCAM1, and ITGB1.

22 Survival-associated CAM genes for subclassification of PAAD patients

To obtain molecular subtypes with greater clinical relevance, we first performed univariate Cox regression analysis to identify 22 CAM genes significantly associated with survival, and then conducted consensus clustering based on their expression profiles. This approach retains the key characteristics of the CAM pathway while focusing on the prognostically driven molecular alterations, thereby identifying PAAD subtypes with distinct survival outcomes. In the consensus clustering analysis, the CDF curve (Fig. 1D) showed a marked increase in clustering stability from K = 2 to K = 3, while the change became minimal when K > 3. Consistently, the CDF Delta area curve exhibited a noticeable rise at K = 3 followed by a plateau, indicating that further increasing the cluster number provided little improvement. At K = 3, the consensus clustering heatmap shows good separation among clusters (Fig. 1E). Therefore, K = 3 was determined as the optimal number of clusters. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) analysis further confirmed that when k = 3, PAAD patients were successfully divided into three subgroups (Fig. 1F). The expression levels of the 22 survival-associated CAM genes showed significant differences between the three subgroups (Fig. 1G). Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 1H) indicated that patients in Cluster 3 (n = 8) had the best prognosis, followed by Cluster 2 (n = 78), and Cluster 1 (n = 98) had the poorest prognosis. Regarding clinical information (Fig. 1I), statistical differences were observed between the three subgroups in terms of T stage, N stage, TNM stage, and grade. Cluster 3 patients were mainly concentrated in the early disease stages (e.g., TNM stage I, Grade G1), whereas Cluster 1 patients were primarily in the late stages (e.g., TNM stage IV, Grade G3), suggesting a significant correlation between CAM genes and the progression of PAAD.

Biological function analysis between CAM subgroup 1 and CAM subgroup 3

To explore the molecular differences between the poor-prognosis (subgroup 1) and favorable-prognosis (subgroup 3) clusters, we performed differential gene expression analysis. As shown in the volcano plot (Supplementary Fig. 1A), a total of 3,243 genes were upregulated and 1,861 were downregulated in subgroup 1 compared with subgroup 3 (adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). Red and blue dots represent significantly up- and downregulated genes, respectively, while gray dots denote non-significant genes. These results provided the foundation for subsequent functional enrichment analysis to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the prognostic differences among subgroups. The gene heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 1B) displays the top 50 upregulated and downregulated genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1C) revealed that in the poor-prognosis CAM subgroup 1 patients, the upregulated genes (3,243) were significantly enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, regulation of cell adhesion (e.g., leukocyte migration), and immune-related functions (e.g., cytokine receptor binding). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the activation of ECM-receptor interaction, inflammatory pathways (e.g., TNF signaling), and pro-cancer pathways (e.g., focal adhesion, Hippo pathway). These results suggest that ECM remodeling, chronic inflammation, and immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment may jointly drive tumor aggressiveness and immune evasion, providing potential intervention strategies for targeting ECM degradation (e.g., Matrix Metalloproteinase inhibitors) or immune modulation therapies (e.g., anti-PD-1/IL-6). Furthermore, we assessed the immune cell infiltration levels between the three subgroups using the CIBERSORT algorithm. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1D, poor-prognosis G1 patients exhibited a significant reduction in anti-tumor immune cells (e.g., memory B cells, CD8 + T cells, M1 macrophages), while pro-tumor M0 macrophages and neutrophils were markedly increased. This suggests that the immune microenvironment in this group exhibits a suppressive “cold tumor” phenotype, which may contribute to the poor clinical prognosis. In contrast, patients in the better-prognosis G3 group displayed stronger anti-tumor immune activity, including higher levels of naive B cells, follicular helper T cells, and activated dendritic cells. This immune activation may aid in controlling tumor progression. These findings provide important insights for prognostic prediction and selection of immunotherapy strategies, indicating that G1 patients may benefit from combination therapies targeting myeloid cells to improve the immune microenvironment.

Construction and validation of a PAAD prognostic risk model based on CAM genes

To further construct a prognostic risk model for PAAD, we applied the LASSO algorithm for feature selection and model construction using 22 CAM genes. Based on the minimum lambda value of 0.0389, 11 CAM genes were selected as the prognostic model genes (Fig. 2A-B).

The K-M survival curve (Supplementary Fig. 2B) demonstrates that high-risk patients have significantly worse survival than low-risk patients (P = 1.32e-05). Clinical correlation of the CAM prognostic model with PAAD patients

We analyzed the clinical relevance of the CAM prognostic model with PAAD patients. Univariate regression analysis (Fig. 3A) revealed that Age, T stage, N stage, and Risk score were significantly associated with the survival of PAAD patients. Further multivariate regression analysis (Fig. 3B) showed that only Age, N stage, and Risk score remained significantly correlated with survival. These three factors were selected to construct the clinical nomogram (Fig. 3C). ROC curve analysis showed that the nomogram integrating clinical features and risk scores outperformed any single clinical variable (Age, Sex, Alcohol, Grade, Stage, T stage, N stage, Risk score) in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (Fig. 3D). Notably, for the 5-year survival prediction, both the nomogram and the risk score achieved an AUC of 1, indicating extremely high predictive performance and further demonstrating their utility in clinical survival assessment.

Multi-algorithm integration analysis reveals key immune microenvironment differences between high and Low-Risk groups

We integrated six algorithms—Xcell, TIMER, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, EPIC, and CIBERSORT—to systematically assess the tumor immune microenvironment characteristics. We found significant differences in immune infiltration between the high and low-risk groups (Fig. 4A). In the low-risk group, immune cells with antitumor activity were significantly enriched, particularly Natural Killer (NK) cells (Xcell: p = 5.9e-06; QUANTISEQ: p = 5.5e-06, Fig. 4B) and cytotoxic T cells (CD8 + T cells) (Xcell: p = 0.011; CIBERSORT: p = 0.0036; QUANTISEQ: p = 0.019, Fig. 4C). Additionally, the low-risk group exhibited higher microenvironment activity scores and more favorable immune cell interaction networks. In contrast, the high-risk group displayed clear immune suppression features, characterized by a significant increase in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (TIMER, Fig. 4A), along with enhanced neutrophil infiltration and matrix remodeling. These results suggest that the high infiltration of CD8 + T cells and NK cells is closely associated with a better prognosis, while the immunosuppressive microenvironment in the high-risk group may be a key factor contributing to its poorer clinical outcomes. This provides valuable insights for prognosis assessment and the selection of immune therapeutic strategies.

CAM effectively predicts immunotherapy response in cancer patients

Using the TIDE algorithm, we further analyzed the predictive effect of immunotherapy between high- and low-risk groups. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A, the expression of CD274 (PD1) and Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (MDSC) was significantly higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. Meanwhile, the M2 Tumor-Associated Macrophage (TAM) and Dysfunction scores were significantly lower in the high-risk group, indicating a more pronounced immune suppression phenotype in this group. Based on these findings, we utilized two immunotherapy datasets (GSE78220, with 26 patients, and IMvigor210, with 348 patients) to predict the effects of immunotherapy. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3B, risk scores significantly correlated with immune response rates. In the anti-PD-1 patient cohort, high-risk patients showed significantly worse immune response compared to low-risk patients (Supplementary Fig. 3C), and CAM-based patient stratification revealed significant survival differences (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Similarly, in the anti-PD-L1 cohort, CAM-based stratification also showed distinct differences in immune response and survival (Supplementary Fig. 3E-G). In the study by Parikh et al. [21], we identified a small combined treatment cohort that included pancreatic cancer patients. This study performed RNA sequencing on biopsy samples from 17 patients treated with radiotherapy plus anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab) and anti-PD1 (nivolumab), among which 5 cases were pancreatic cancer. Based on these samples, we found that patients with high CAM risk scores exhibited poor therapeutic responses (all with progressive disease, PD), whereas those with low CAM risk scores showed favorable outcomes, including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), and stable disease (SD) (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4A). Moreover, CAM high-risk patients had significantly worse survival compared with low-risk patients (Supplementary Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the CAM-based risk score may have predictive potential for immunotherapy response in pancreatic cancer.

The CAM gene model demonstrates significant predictive efficacy for chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity in cancer patients

Utilizing the GDSC database, we calculated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of various chemotherapeutic agents across distinct risk subgroups. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 5A, 48 compounds exhibited statistically significant differences in drug sensitivity between the two risk groups. Notably, the top four agents demonstrating higher efficacy in the high-risk group were BIBW2992, PLX4720, bicalutamide, and BI-2536 (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Conversely, PD-173,074, axitinib, MK-2206, and ZM-447,439 showed preferential sensitivity in the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 5C). These findings collectively indicate that the CAM gene model serves as a robust predictor of chemosensitivity in cancer patients.

Integrated analysis of key CAM expression patterns using Bulk-seq and Single-cell omics data

We systematically evaluated the expression profiles of 10 critical CAMs by comparing cancer samples from the TCGA database (G1 group) with adjacent normal tissues and samples collected from 54 disease-free tissue sites across nearly 1,000 individuals (Normal group). As shown in Fig. 5A, CDH3, CLDN15, CLDN16, ITGB1, NCAM1, CD58, CD99L2, LRRC4B, and SDC1 were significantly upregulated in pancreatic tumor tissues, whereas SLITRK2 showed marked downregulation. No statistically significant difference was observed for CLDN3 expression. Through analysis of the PAAD single-cell dataset (PAAD_GSE111672, Fig. 5B), we identified major cellular subpopulations characteristic of pancreatic adenocarcinoma, including acinar cells, ductal cells, CD8 + T cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, malignant cells, mast cells, monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and proliferating T cells. Notably, ductal and malignant cells constituted the two predominant subpopulations, reflecting fundamental pathological features of PAAD. Among these, CDH3, ITGB1, and CD58 are predominantly expressed in Malignant cells, CLDN16, CLDN3, and SDC1 are mainly expressed in Ductal cells, CLDN15 is primarily expressed in T Prolif cells, NCAM1 is mainly expressed in MAST cells, and CD99L2 is predominantly expressed in Endothelial cells (Fig. 5C). These findings suggest that key CAM genes play crucial roles in tumorigenesis and immunomodulation. Protein-level validation through immunohistochemical analysis using the HPA database further corroborated these observations (Fig. 5D). Collectively, our multi-omics approach provides valuable insights into tumor biology and potential therapeutic target development.

Key CAM gene CLDN16 could inhibit cell apoptosis, promote PC cell migration, and invasion

After performing both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, we found that among the risk-associated genes, only CLDN16 was independently associated with prognosis (Fig. 6A). We observed that CCDN16 was positively correlated with CD58, CDH3, SDC1, ITGB1, and CLDN15 in PAAD samples (Fig. 6B). These genes are all associated with cell adhesion and cell–matrix interactions, suggesting that CCDN16 may participate in the regulatory network controlling tumor cell adhesion and migration. To investigate the oncogenic role of CLDN16 in PC, we first analyzed its expression across multiple tumor types using TCGA datasets. CLDN16 was found to be aberrantly upregulated in various cancers, including pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) (Fig. 6C). Further analysis revealed that high CLDN16 expression correlated with advanced tumor grade and worse overall survival in PC patients (Fig. 6D). To validate these findings, we examined CLDN16 expression in pancreatic cell lines. Both mRNA and protein levels of CLDN16 were markedly elevated in pancreatic cancer cell lines (MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1, AsPC-1) compared with the normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line HPDE (Fig. 6E-F). Among them, PANC-1 cells exhibited the highest expression and were selected for loss-of-function assays. Transfection with CLDN16-targeting siRNAs effectively reduced its expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6G-H). Flow cytometric analysis showed that CLDN16 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis in PANC-1 cells (Fig. 6I-J). Functionally, silencing CLDN16 suppressed the migratory and invasive capacities of PANC-1 cells. Wound healing and Matrigel invasion assays demonstrated that CLDN16 depletion reduced cell movement and invasion (Fig. 6K-L). Transwell migration assays further confirmed a significant decrease in the number of migrated cells upon CLDN16 knockdown (Fig. 6M-N). These results suggest that CLDN16 acts as an oncogene in pancreatic cancer by inhibiting apoptosis and promoting cell migration and invasion.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive molecular characterization of CAMs in PAAD, establishing their clinical and biological significance through multi-omics integration and translational validation. Our work advances the field through several key innovations. First, we systematically identified 22 CAM genes significantly associated with overall survival in PAAD, bridging the gap between foundational adhesion biology and clinical oncology. By leveraging these CAM expression profiles, we delineated three robust molecular subtypes with markedly distinct prognoses. Particularly, Cluster 1, which exhibited the worst outcomes, was enriched for advanced TNM stages and poorly differentiated (G3) tumors, underscoring the ability of CAM signatures to reflect tumor aggressiveness and progression. Mechanistically, poor-prognosis clusters were characterized by the enrichment of ECM-receptor interaction, inflammatory cascades (e.g., TNF signaling), and oncogenic pathways (e.g., focal adhesion, Hippo signaling), collectively pointing to CAM-mediated stromal remodeling and tumor–stromal crosstalk as key contributors to immune evasion and therapy resistance [22–24]. This integrated view advances beyond previous studies by establishing CAMs not merely as structural molecules, but as dynamic regulators of both tumor-intrinsic malignancy programs and the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Importantly, our findings challenge the limited efficacy of current stromal-targeting therapies, such as CAF depletion [25], and instead advocate for CAM-focused strategies as a novel avenue to modulate tumor-stroma interactions in a more precise and clinically actionable manner.

Second, our development and validation of an 11-gene CAM prognostic model represents a significant advance in precision oncology. The model’s robust performance across multiple cohorts (AUC 0.836 at 3 years) surpasses existing clinical staging systems, overcoming the diagnosis and the prediction of cancer progression limitations of MSLN that plague current practice [26]. The risk score’s independence from conventional clinical factors in multivariate analysis underscores its unique biological and clinical value. Importantly, single-cell resolution analysis revealed cell-type specific expression patterns, with malignant cells preferentially expressing CDH3/ITGB1/CD58 while ductal cells showed CLDN16/CLDN3/SDC1. These findings reveal that the differential expression of specific CAMs among distinct cell types within the tumor microenvironment (TME), inform future targeted therapies, particularly given emerging nanoparticle delivery systems capable of cell-type-specific targeting [27]. More importantly, we identified a critical role of the key CAM gene CLDN16 in promoting pancreatic cancer cell migration and invasion. As a tight junction protein, CLDN16 has been reported to be highly expressed and function as an oncogenic factor in human breast cancer [28], thyroid cancer [29], and ovarian cancer [30]. However, its role in pancreatic cancer remains unclear. Therefore, our study provides important foundational evidence for further investigation of CLDN16 in pancreatic cancer.

Thirdly, the therapeutic implications of our work are particularly compelling. The differential drug sensitivity patterns (e.g., BIBW2992 sensitivity in high-risk patients) provide immediately actionable insights for personalized treatment selection. This pharmacological vulnerability map complements recent advances in KRAS-G12D targeting [31] by addressing parallel resistance mechanisms. The model’s predictive value for immunotherapy response is especially relevant given PAAD’s notorious resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [32]. Our finding that high-risk patients exhibit both elevated PD-L1 expression and increased myeloid suppression helps reconcile previous paradoxical observations, mirroring the dual immune activation/suppression dynamics seen post-ablation therapy [33], and suggests these patients may benefit from combination strategies targeting both adaptive and innate immune resistance mechanisms. Due to the limited availability of public pancreatic cancer immunotherapy datasets, part of our validation was performed using data from other tumor types. Nevertheless, in a small pancreatic cancer cohort from the study by Parikh et al. (Nature Cancer, 2021), we observed better responses to immune checkpoint blockade in patients with low CAM risk scores, indicating possible applicability of the model in pancreatic cancer. However, given the small sample size, this finding should be interpreted with caution and warrants validation in larger prospective cohorts. Additionally, our chemotherapy response prediction was based on drug sensitivity modeling from the GDSC database. Although this approach has been reported to correlate well with actual patient outcomes [34], its clinical predictive value in pancreatic cancer has not yet been fully validated in prospective studies. Future research integrating experimental and clinical data is needed to further assess the translational potential of CAM features in guiding individualized treatment strategies for pancreatic cancer.

From a translational perspective, this study makes three major contributions: (1) It establishes CAMs as master regulators of PAAD progression through simultaneous modulation of tumor cell behavior, stromal interactions and immune evasion; synthesizing previously fragmented observations about E-cadherin internalization [35], CAF-mediated ECM remodeling [36], and immune exclusion mechanisms [37]; (2) It provides a clinically implementable tool for risk stratification and treatment guidance, outperforming conventional TNM staging and existing published prognostic models [38–41]; (3) It identifies novel therapeutic targets within the CAM family for drug development. The convergence of prognostic value and biological insights positions our CAM signature as a potential biomarker for patient selection in clinical trials of stromal-targeting or immunomodulatory therapies.

Future research should focus on functional validation of key CAM members, particularly exploring whether targeting ITGB1 or CD58 can reverse the aggressive phenotype of high-risk tumors. This could synergize with emerging nanotherapeutic approaches for solid stress modulation [42] or immunostimulatory hydrogel vaccines [33]. The cell-type specific expression patterns also raise intriguing questions about paracrine signaling between malignant and stromal compartments mediated by CAM interactions. The CAF-tumor cell crosstalk mechanisms identified here (e.g., CLDN16 dominance in ductal cells) warrant comparison with lncRNA-mediated CAF regulation [43]. Clinically, prospective validation of our model in randomized trials could establish its utility for guiding therapeutic decisions, potentially integrated with next-generation KRAS inhibitors [44] or enhanced imaging modalities [45].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study redefines CAMs in PAAD as key regulators of tumor progression and therapeutic targets. By integrating multi-omics data with clinical outcomes, we established a precision medicine framework for improved patient management. Notably, we identified CLDN16 as an independent prognostic marker and promoter of cancer cell migration and invasion, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in PAAD.
