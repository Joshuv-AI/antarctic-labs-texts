From CXR to ultrasound: a multi-framework approach to advancing De-implementation in acute care
Authors: Enyo A Ablordeppey, Byron J Powell, Tiffany E Rosenzweig, Rebecca X Lengnick-Hall, Aimee S James, Richard T Griffey, Elvin X Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractEfforts such as Choosing Wisely have raised awareness about low-value care, yet entrenched practices persist, particularly in acute care settings where clinician behavior is shaped by workflow defaults, training norms, and institutional policies. Routine chest radiography (CXR) after central venous catheter (CVC) placement continues despite strong evidence supporting point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) as a safe and efficient alternative. We applied a multi-framework, theory-informed approach to diagnose behavioral drivers sustaining CXR use and to design targeted de-implementation strategies.MethodsWe conducted focus groups and interviews with emergency and critical care clinicians within a single integrated academic health system. Transcripts were deductively coded using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and mapped to the COM-B model (capability, opportunity, motivation). A causal map was developed to represent behavioral drivers. Using the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW), we selected aligned intervention functions and specified mechanisms of action using the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (BCTT). Strategies were organized within an Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM).ResultsThree interrelated themes emerged: (1) variable self-efficacy for POCUS confirmation (capability), (2) entrenched organizational norms and workflow defaults favoring CXR (opportunity), and (3) behavioral inertia reinforced by habit and perceived safety (motivation). These determinants informed seven candidate strategies, including competency-based ultrasound training, audit and feedback with peer comparison, policy supported clinical algorithms, workflow redesign, peer champions, and structured adaptation cycles. Fourteen behavior change techniques were specified to clarify mechanisms of action. The IRLM linked determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and anticipated outcomes, providing a transparent pathway from qualitative diagnosis to strategy specification.ConclusionIntegrating CFIR, COM-B, BCW, and BCTT enabled systematic translation of contextual and behavioral insights into mechanism-based de-implementation strategies. This structured approach enhances transparency in strategy specification and offers a replicable roadmap for reducing low-value care in complex clinical environments. Future work will pilot and evaluate these strategies to support broader de-implementation efforts.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00963-6.Keywords: Deimplementation, Qualitative methods, CFIR, COM-B, POCUS, Ultrasonography; chest xray; central venous catheter, Implementation frameworks

Contribution to the literature• This study complements the prevailing “Choosing Wisely” framework by broadening the conceptualization of low-value care to include complex behavioral, social, and organizational determinants, thereby underscoring the necessity of theory-informed, system-level strategies that are responsive to the complexities of clinical practice.• It advances de-implementation science in acute care by addressing a routine but entrenched practice of chest X-rays (CXR) for central venous catheter (CVC) confirmation, despite the availability of safe, faster, and evidence-based alternatives like point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS).• The study contributes to the limited literature on de-implementation by using a multi-framework, theory-driven approach that integrates COM-B, the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW), and CFIR to systematically identify behavioral drivers and develop targeted strategies for change.• A key innovation is the application of the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (BCTT) to operationalize de-implementation strategies. This allows for clearer specification of mechanisms of action and enhances the replicability and generalizability of the approach.• Embedding strategies within an Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM) helped ensure alignment between behavioral determinants, intervention functions, and expected outcomes, providing a transparent roadmap that can inform similar efforts to reduce low-value care in complex healthcare environments.

Background

Reducing medical overuse and de-implementing low-value practices remain under-addressed yet essential goals for improving healthcare quality and sustainability [1, 2]. Low-value care includes interventions that offer minimal benefit, misalign with patient preferences, or pose unnecessary harm, yet contributes to rising costs and clinician burden [3–5]. While initiatives such as Choosing Wisely and Too Much Medicine have successfully elevated awareness and built cross-specialty consensus around low-value practices, awareness alone is often insufficient to change entrenched behaviors in complex clinical environments [6–9]. In high-acuity settings such as emergency and critical care, clinician behavior is shaped not only by evidence, but also by workflow defaults, training variability, interprofessional expectations, and institutional policy. Rather than relying on passive dissemination or clinician education alone, we apply a theory-driven, multi-level behavioral framework to uncover the mechanisms sustaining low-value care and to guide targeted, context-sensitive deimplementation strategies [10–15].

This paper focuses on the de-implementation of routine chest X-rays (CXR) to confirm central venous catheter (CVC) placement, a long-standing practice that offer minimal benefit compared to its alternative (Fig. 1). Although point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has demonstrated safety, efficiency, and diagnostic reliability for confirmation, CXR remains widely used [16–22]. Over 90% of clinicians use POCUS for CVC insertion guidance, yet fewer than 2% use it for confirmation. This discrepancy reflects more than knowledge gaps, it reflects behavioral, social, and structural influences embedded within acute care systems. With over 5 million central lines placed annually, replacing routine CXR with POCUS offers a safe, evidence-based, and cost-effective opportunity for de-implementation [23–25].

Acute care environments present unique challenges, including malpractice concerns, time pressure, diagnostic uncertainty, and entrenched norms shaped by specialty training and institutional culture [8, 26–30]. Low-value practices persist not only because of these contextual pressures, but also due to their less visible harms and competing clinical demands [3, 28, 29, 31]. In such settings, behavior is shaped by peer influence, team dynamics, and local norms, making top-down directives alone insufficient to drive change [30]. Prior work suggests that sustainable change requires coordinated alignment among motivated individuals, supportive units, and organizational infrastructure [1, 32–37].

To address these complexities, we applied an integrated, multi-level approach to de-implementing a low-value practice, drawing on the following frameworks: Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), COM-B (capability, opportunity, motivation), the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW), and the Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy (BCTT) [34, 36, 38–41]. This combination enabled us to identify contextual determinants, diagnose behavioral drivers, and specify mechanism-informed strategies with transparency and precision. By integrating these frameworks, we offer a replicable model for designing de-implementation strategies in complex clinical environments and strengthen the theoretical and practical foundation for reducing persistent low-value care.

Theoretical approach

Given the complexity of behavior change in acute care settings, we integrated complementary implementation science frameworks to guide a structured strategy development process. We applied a multi-level, multi-framework approach that integrates the CFIR, the COM-B model, the BCW, and the BCTT (Table 1). CFIR was used to identify contextual determinants across individual, team, and organizational levels. These determinants were mapped onto the COM-B determinants to diagnose behavioral drivers in terms of capability, opportunity, and motivation, which served as the behavioral core of our analysis. This step allowed us to move from identifying contextual determinants to understanding how these determinants influence individual and team behaviors. Building on this diagnosis, the BCW informed selection of intervention functions aligned with identified behavioral themes [40]. Finally, the BCTT was used to specify the active components and hypothesized mechanisms of action within each strategy, enhancing precision and reproducibility. Although each framework has been applied independently in implementation research, few studies have integrated CFIR, COM-B, BCW, and BCTT in a coordinated strategy development process for de-implementation. By explicitly linking contextual determinants (CFIR) to behavioral diagnosis (COM-B), structured intervention functions (BCW), and operationalized mechanisms of action (BCTT), this integrated approach provides a transparent rationale for strategy selection and highlights behavioral and organizational drivers beyond policy change [32, 42, 43]. This integrated framework guided the methods described below.

Methods

Our methods took place over three stages–data collection, data analysis, and strategy mapping–with each stage building on the last. First, we collected qualitative data through focus groups and interviews to explore barriers and facilitators to de-implementing CXR in favor of POCUS for CVC confirmation in acute care. Participants were recruited via purposive sampling and included emergency and critical care physicians, as well as emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) nurses, from both academic and community hospitals. All participants were recruited from a single integrated academic health system that includes both academic and community-affiliated hospitals. Five multidisciplinary focus groups (n = 20) were conducted.

Participants included attending physicians and fellows; resident representation was limited, which may have constrained insights into training-stage barriers. Nurses contributed perspectives on workflow integration, documentation processes, and interprofessional expectations influencing imaging confirmation practices. During the interviews, we explored perceived norms, workflow constraints, training gaps, and clinical risks related to POCUS use. The interview guide was iteratively refined to clarify wording and deepen probing of emerging themes; however, core domains remained consistent across groups to ensure comparability of data. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and approved under IRB protocol. The study followed COREQ guidelines (Supplemental File 1) [44, 45]. Additional details, including the full guide and coding tree, are provided in a companion qualitative manuscript [44, 45].

Once data were collected, we moved into analysis, using a multi-framework, theory-driven approach to analyze and interpret qualitative data and develop a corresponding implementation logic model (Fig. 2). Interview transcripts were first deductively coded using CFIR constructs to identify barriers and facilitators, with inductive themes such as time constraints, perceived safety risks, and clinician inertia identified through iterative review [45, 46]. Building on the established CIFR codes, we linked them to COM-B domains, capability, opportunity, and motivation through consensus building [39]. These COM behavioral drivers were used to construct a causal map organized by COM-B constructs (capability → opportunity → motivation). Through additional stakeholder discussions, including ED and ICU leadership, we refined the causal model to reflect real-world workflow challenges and educational gaps (e.g., reduced exposure to POCUS training among recent cohorts) to identify context-sensitive strategies and hypothesized mechanisms of change [47, 48].

Following the mapping stage, we pursued strategy selection. Recognizing that multicomponent interventions are more effective than single strategies, we used COM-B to guide the targeted selection of seven strategies informed by the BCW (Fig. 3): one foundational education-based strategy to build capability, and two additional strategies for each COM-B domain (capability, opportunity, motivation), balancing attention across all key behavioral drivers [9, 15, 49–51]. These strategies were further categorized using the BCTT, which made explicit the mechanisms of action (Table 2) [34, 40]. The BCW was used to identify intervention functions aligned with each COM-B domain, while the BCTT further operationalized these functions into discrete, evidence-based behavior change techniques (BCTs). Together, this integration of frameworks provides a transparent, theory-informed foundation for identifying de-implementation strategies tailored to complex, high-stakes clinical environments.

Finally, we sought to assess feasibility and contextual fit by evaluating each selected strategy using the APEASE criteria from the BCW (affordability, practicability, effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, acceptability, side-effects and safety, and equity) [33, 52, 53]; full evaluations are detailed in Supplemental File 2. This structured, theory-informed process ensured that strategies were directly aligned with identified behavioral drivers and tailored to the complexity of acute care settings. We then integrated the finalized strategies into an Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM), which helped connect each strategy to its proposed corresponding mechanism of action and intended outcomes. In high-stakes environments like ED, logic models provide a valuable tool for clarifying how context and behavior interact and for structuring complex interventions [54, 55]. The IRLM made our decision-making process transparent and replicable, strengthening the theoretical and practical foundation for de-implementation strategy development [56, 57]. Together, our methods followed a structured pathway from qualitative data collection to strategy development.

Results

Causal map and logic model for understanding persistent CXR use

Through our analysis process, we developed a causal map (Fig. 4) to identify key behavioral drivers underlying the persistent use of CXR for confirming CVC placement, despite strong evidence supporting POCUS as a safe and more efficient alternative. Using CFIR-coded qualitative data linked to the COM-B model, we identified three interrelated themes: self-efficacy (capability), organizational norms (opportunity), and behavioral inertia (motivation). These factors operate across both individual and system levels and interact to reinforce continued CXR use in acute care settings. To translate these behavioral insights into actionable strategies, we developed IRLM (Fig. 5). While the causal map visualizes why low-value behavior persists, the IRLM shows how change might be achieved, linking specific behavioral drivers to targeted strategies, their hypothesized mechanisms of action, and intended outcomes. This dual approach provides a structured foundation for designing and testing de-implementation interventions in high-stakes clinical environments.

Contextual assessment and thematic analysis

Using the COM-B model as an organizing framework, we conducted thematic analysis of interview data to identify multilevel barriers and facilitators. Below, we present the three behavioral themes and representative quotes from participants with identified themes in paratheses. For each domain, we describe how the qualitative findings informed the selection of relevant BCW intervention functions using supporting quotes and identified themes, then how it formed the foundation for a targeted de-implementation strategy.

Theme 1: Self-Efficacy (Capability)

The first theme, self-efficacy, is aligned with capability. Capability refers to an individual’s psychological and physical capacity to perform a behavior [34, 36, 52]. This includes both technical skill and confidence in using POCUS for CVC confirmation. Participants frequently cited training variability, limited practice opportunities, and reliance on more familiar tools like CXR as barriers to capability.

My ultrasound skills are not spectacular … I would not trust my ultrasound skills to verify for a pneumothorax. I do trust most of the fellows who get very good ultrasound training and they’re definitely more facile with the ultrasound than I am. (Provider Influences)

If you don’t have a lot of skill in using the confirmation method, I could see that amount of time being much longer than getting the initial read back for your chest X-ray. (Time)

Pretty much anybody that is placing and using central lines—chest X-ray is built in whereas ultrasound may not be. It’s a whole new modality.

(Medical Training)

Although centered on the individual, both physical and psychological capabilities were shaped by institutional support and training infrastructure. These insights informed the selection of education, training, and enablement as aligned BCW intervention functions to strengthen provider capability. While self-efficacy shaped individual readiness, clinicians also described external pressures that reinforced routine CXR use.

Theme 2: Organizational Norms (Opportunity)

The next theme, organizational norms, aligned with opportunity, which encompasses external factors that shape or constrain behavior, including resources, clinical norms, and institutional policies [34, 36, 52]. Participants noted that expectations around CXR use were deeply embedded in workflows and reinforced by policy, interprofessional expectations, and documentation systems.

It’s not just the people putting the lines in. It also has to have buy-in from the nurses”. [and radiologists] (Organizational Influences)

I only order the X-ray for fulfilling what I perceive as a policy … I’m doing it for the next group of physicians, not necessarily for me.

(Legal Influences)

If there was a way to enhance the EMR to have images of ultrasound … I think that would be ideal. (Technology)

Both physical opportunity (e.g., equipment availability, time, space) and social opportunity (e.g., peer norms, institutional culture) played key roles [52]. The BCW functions selected to address opportunity were environmental restructuring, restriction, and enablement, aimed at shifting norms and workflows to support POCUS use [34]. Even when capability and opportunity aligned with POCUS, many clinicians reported still defaulting to CXR out of habit.

Theme 3: Inertia (Motivation)

The final theme, inertia, aligns with the COM-B domain of motivation. Motivation includes both reflective processes (e.g., values, intentions, goals) and automatic processes (e.g., habit, emotional response) [34, 36, 52]. Many clinicians described continuing to order CXRs simply out of routine, even when aware of evidence supporting POCUS.

I mostly get a chest X-ray out of habit. (Inertia)

I feel better as I leave a shift because I’ve done something [POCUS]… it improves my professional satisfaction beyond just what it does for patient care. (Provider Outcomes)

This [change] is for our patients’ safety … that always ends up seeming to win out … is what changes culture. (Patient Outcomes)

Motivational inertia reflected both habit and a lack of reinforcement for alternative practices. This suggested the need for strategies that address both automatic and reflective motivation [52]. Intervention functions from the BCW included persuasion, modeling, incentivization, and enablement. When mapped together, these three themes explained why CXR persisted. We next describe how each theme informed candidate strategies and their associated behavior change techniques.

Linking COM-B constructs to candidate strategies via BCTT

After mapping themes to BCW intervention functions, we selected seven candidate strategies and linked each to specific Behavior Change Techniques (BCTs) using the BCT Taxonomy (Table 2; Fig. 6) [49]. Fourteen BCTs were specified across strategies, clarifying hypothesized mechanisms of action. Importantly, in response to the need for greater operational clarity, we moved beyond high-level strategy labels to define concrete, implementable components that practice leaders and researchers can adapt in other settings. Each strategy is described in terms of who would deliver it, how it would be enacted within existing workflows, and which behavioral mechanism it is designed to influence. This stage therefore operationalized the strategies in a manner that enhances transparency, reproducibility, and transferability across acute care contexts. By explicitly linking each practical component to its corresponding BCT, we make visible not only what should be implemented, but why it is expected to work. This structured specification strengthens the ability of other institutions to replicate, adapt, and evaluate the approach while maintaining theoretical coherence. Discussion

Our findings illustrate not only why routine CXR persists, but also how theory-driven approaches can guide meaningful change. De-implementation of low-value care remains a critical yet underdeveloped area of implementation science, particularly in high-acuity environments like emergency and critical care [73, 74]. Few studies have identified key determinants for low-value care usage and de-implementation in the acute care setting, which are complex and varied [30, 42]. This study contributes to the field by providing both empirical insights into behavioral drivers of persistent CXR use and a structured, theory-informed approach for designing de-implementation strategies. While awareness-focused initiatives like Choosing Wisely play an important role in highlighting key issues, our integrated use of CFIR, COM-B, BCW, and BCTT seeks to build on these efforts by offering a replicable, mechanism-based approach that links qualitative data to actionable behavior change strategies grounded in clinical realities. We hypothesize that beyond identifying strategies, this integrated approach moves from determinant to behavioral mechanisms and active ingredients, enhancing testability.

Through our study, three key takeaways emerged. First, framework integration is necessary for understanding behavior in complex systems. Although replacing routine CXR with POCUS may seem straightforward, sustainable change requires a multi-framework approach that accounts for systemic constraints, hierarchy, and workflow defaults. For example, in acute care, behavior is shaped not only by individual capability but also by supervisory authority and interprofessional norms. In academic environments, attending physicians retain final decision-making authority for imaging confirmation. Even when trainees have greater exposure and acceptability of ultrasound-based techniques, supervisors’ comfort with established practices and perceived risk tolerance influence adoption. Nurses likewise serve as operational gatekeepers, frequently awaiting imaging confirmation before CVC utilization. These supervisory and workflow structures reinforce imaging norms independent of individual clinician preference, underscoring the need for multi-group perspectives and multiframework approach in behavioral diagnosis and strategy design.

Second, acute care demands planned adaptation, continuous evaluation, and clarity regarding mechanisms of action [3, 30, 75, 76]. The causal map and logic model helped translate abstract theory into actionable strategies aligned with actual clinical workflows [53], while use of the BCTT moved beyond identifying determinants to explicitly linking intervention strategies to hypothesized mechanisms. This integration bridges theory and practice by providing a transparent rationale for how strategies, such as audit and feedback or decision support, are expected to influence capability, opportunity, and motivation. Specifying mechanisms at the strategy identification stage sharpens conceptual clarity and facilitates future empirical testing, which is particularly important in high-intensity environments where interventions must be both targeted and feasible.

Third, this study reinforces the importance of using data and theory to drive strategy selection. Our approach mirrors the spirit of original intervention mapping, starting with determinants and working backward to strategy, not through guesswork, but through systematic linkage of empirical data to theory [77, 78]. Importantly, we did not assume what needed to change; we uncovered it through stakeholder-informed qualitative work and then matched strategies accordingly. This ensures that selected interventions directly respond to the barriers clinicians actually face, such as time pressure, lack of confidence, and entrenched norms. Together, these contributions demonstrate that successful de-implementation in acute care requires more than awareness campaigns or guideline dissemination. It requires approaches that are grounded in both behavioral theory and context-specific data approaches that not only explain behavior but can guide meaningful change.

Although our method integrates multiple frameworks, its aim is transparency rather than complexity. To enhance applicability beyond research settings, this structured approach can be streamlined into practical tools for frontline teams. For example, brief COM-B–aligned surveys could help local leaders identify whether capability, opportunity, or motivation barriers predominate, such as skill deficits, workflow constraints, or entrenched norms. Findings could then be paired with pre-specified strategy menus linked to BCW intervention functions and corresponding behavior change techniques. In this format, the causal map and IRLM function as adaptable templates, supporting determinant-driven strategy selection within routine quality improvement cycles while preserving theoretical coherence. Such tools may facilitate adaptation beyond research-intensive contexts and underscore both the practical and conceptual contributions of this work.

Study limitations and next steps

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the integration of multiple frameworks, while offering conceptual depth, relies on interpretive decisions that may limit reproducibility across different teams or contexts. Second, the relatively small number of focus group participants (n = 20) may limit the breadth of perspectives captured, although thematic saturation was achieved within this focused inquiry. Third, the study was conducted within a single integrated academic health system, which may affect transferability to other institutional contexts, particularly non-academic or community-only settings. Forth, while fellows were included, resident representation was limited. In academic environments, hierarchical structures influence decision-making authority, and faculty physicians typically retain final responsibility for procedural confirmation and order entry. Although this reflects real-world practice, broader inclusion of trainees at different stages of training may have provided additional insight into educational barriers and evolving norms around POCUS adoption. Finally, the proposed strategies have not yet been implemented or evaluated. This study represents a theory-informed strategy development phase; future work will focus on piloting, feasibility testing, and evaluation of impact.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a theory-informed, replicable method for linking qualitative insights to mechanism-based de-implementation strategies. Next steps include piloting the strategy bundle to assess feasibility, acceptability, and early impact on CXR use. The IRLM will function as a testable framework to examine whether hypothesized mechanisms drive change, with findings informing iterative refinement of both strategies and the model for broader acute care adaptation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how integrating CFIR, COM-B, BCW, and BCTT can support transparent and systematic specification of mechanism-based de-implementation strategies. CFIR identified contextual and organizational factors, while COM-B distilled behavioral drivers into capability, opportunity, and motivation. BCW and BCTT supported the systematic selection and operationalization of aligned strategies. Our use of a causal map and IRLM adds structure and transparency, strengthening the theoretical foundation for future intervention development. By linking contextual determinants to behavioral drivers and operationalized intervention components, this approach offers a structured roadmap for future testing and refinement in complex acute care settings.
