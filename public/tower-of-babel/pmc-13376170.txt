Deep learning strategies for estimating retinal thickness from fundus images: a comparative study with multi-device data
Authors: Noriyoshi Takahashi, Nikhil Gadiraju, Judy E. Kim, Jui-Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Color fundus photographs (CFPs) are widely used for diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening due to accessibility. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) provides complementary depth-resolved retinal structure information but is less practical for large-scale screening, especially due to its cost. This study presents a deep learning framework based on a foundation model (FM) to estimate OCT-derived total retinal thickness (TRT) maps directly from CFPs, without preprocessing steps such as region registration. RETFound was used as the backbone FM, and the AI-READI dataset with paired CFPs and OCT scans from two devices (Topcon Maestro2 and Triton) was used for training and evaluation. Additional training data came from OCTA-500 and EyePACS. Three decoder training strategies for TRT estimation were compared: Individual, Combination, and Random Selection. Device-derived OCT TRT maps showed a significant inter-device thickness difference, with Maestro2 measuring on average more than 30 µm thicker than Triton. Individual and Combination models achieved low bias (relative difference about 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document}) against device-specific references. The Random Selection model converged toward the average TRT distribution between Maestro2 and Triton, demonstrating robustness to device variability. The proposed FM-based framework bridges CFP and OCT by estimating retinal thickness from CFPs without OCT.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of vision impairment and blindness worldwide1. Without timely detection and treatment, DR can progress to sight-threatening complications such as proliferative retinopathy, intraretinal and vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, and diabetic macular edema (DME)2,3. Early diagnosis and intervention substantially reduce the risk of irreversible vision loss. Color fundus photographs (CFPs) are the most common modality for DR screening because CFPs are convenient, time-tested, relatively portable, and effective for capturing features from the superficial retinal appearance4. However, CFPs lack depth-resolved retinal information, limiting the assessment of structural changes reflected in thickness. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) provides cross-sectional views of the retina and enables quantitative and qualitative assessment of retinal thickness, which is essential for diagnosing and monitoring macular edema4. Total retinal thickness (TRT)5, measured using OCT, is a clinically intuitive metric that provides an objective and quantitative assessment of DME, with accuracy dependent on reliable retinal layer segmentation. Increased retinal thickness is closely associated with fluid accumulation and disease activity, making TRT an important indicator for diagnosis and treatment monitoring6. Longitudinal assessment of TRT could support early detection and timely intervention, which is critical because DME may lead to significant central vision loss despite being asymptomatic in its early stages7. Nevertheless, OCT remains suboptimal for population-level screening and routine, rapid follow-up assessments due to device cost, operational complexity, and the limited image quality of its portable systems. A major unmet need exists for objective methods that bridge CFP and OCT modalities, enabling the estimation of retinal thickness as a proxy for certain retinal structural changes.

Deep learning (DL) foundation models (FMs) have advanced artificial intelligence (AI) in ophthalmology by introducing the concept of training DL encoders and decoders on massive images datasets to compress ophthalmic images into compact representations, also often referred to as feature vectors or latent variables (LVs)8–10, which can be linked to clinical parameters and diagnostic outcomes associated with both ocular and systemic diseases. A promising FM implementation is RETFound11, a vision transformer-based12 model trained on approximately 900,000 CFPs using masked autoencoders as its self-supervised learning mechanism. For diabetic retinopathy diagnosis, RETFound achieved area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) values between 0.82 and 0.94 across APTOS-201913, IDRID14, and MESSIDOR-215. Studies have also shown that RETFound is applicable to a wide range of systemic diseases; the LVs capture critical information from CFPs that is difficult for human experts to detect but can be associated with both ocular and systemic clinical parameters16–18. In addition to the CFP-based model, RETFound includes a counterpart trained on OCT B-scans. The CFP-based model encodes surface-level retinal features, whereas the OCT-based model captures cross-sectional structure. Although these perspectives differ, the retinal surface and structural information is intrinsically connected. However, RETFound does not provide a mechanism to translate retinal information between CFP and OCT representations.

Recognizing the transformative potential of AI in healthcare, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) launched the Bridge2AI Program in 2022 to accelerate innovation through the development of ethically sourced, high-quality, and multimodal datasets19. One of its flagship initiatives is AI-READI (Artificial Intelligence Ready and Equitable Atlas for Diabetes Insights), which focuses on type 2 diabetes mellitus20,21. The AI-READI dataset is a rigorously curated resource that includes multimodal ophthalmic imaging, including CFP and OCT, acquired using devices from multiple manufacturers. Data were collected from three academic medical centers (i.e., University of Alabama at Birmingham, University of California San Diego, and University of Washington) and include standardized image quality assessments, detailed clinical metadata such as age, sex, systemic diabetes status, hemoglobin A1c, blood pressure, and comorbidities. To reduce reidentification risk while still supporting AI model development and evaluation, AI-READI withholds individual demographic details such as sex or race and instead provides predefined training, validation, and test splits with patient-level labels, stratified to balance race and age distributions and promote fairness and generalizability.

Building on recent advances in FMs and the availability of multimodal AI-READI dataset, this study proposed a DL framework to estimate the OCT-derived TRT maps directly from CFPs. Although the depth information of the retina is not directly visible in CFPs, we hypothesize that RETFound encodes CFP features that can support a reasonable approximation of pixel-level TRT maps, which are typically generated from OCT scans using specialized segmentation algorithms. TRT is a key quantitative metric for assessing edema or thinning in diabetic retinopathy and other retinal diseases. Our framework utilizes latent representations (i.e., LVs) extracted by RETFound and was trained on the AI-READI dataset, which contains paired CFP and OCT scans acquired from two devices: the Topcon Maestro2 (spectral-domain OCT, SD-OCT) and the Topcon Triton (swept-source OCT, SS-OCT). Previously, our group developed a related approach to estimate TRT maps from CFPs in cases of optic disc swelling using a U-Net–based architecture22,23. However, the previous methods required explicit image registration to localize the optic nerve head, which is a preprocessing step that substantially limits scalability. The current study removed this tedious registration step by adopting an end-to-end pipeline that operates directly on RETFound’s LVs. Our proposed approach increases applicability and provides a practical solution for estimating TRT in settings where OCT is not available, such as primary care clinics or remote screening environments. Methods

Dataset

This study used data from three publicly available sources. The primary dataset comprised paired CFPs and OCT scans acquired using Topcon Maestro2 and Topcon Triton devices from AI-READI (Version 2.0.0)20,24. To support specific components of the modeling pipeline, two additional datasets were incorporated. First, OCT B-scans from the OCTA-500 dataset25 were used to train an nnU-Net model for total retinal segmentation. Second, CFPs from the EyePACS dataset, obtained through the Kaggle Diabetic Retinopathy Detection competition26, were used to fine-tune the RETFound foundation model. This fine-tuning step was intended to reduce the disparity in RETFound LV’s distributions between the CFPs from the two Topcon devices in the AI-READI dataset. We applied quality control and data pairing steps, as described in the following section, to generate the final dataset used for analysis.

OCT Total Retinal Thickness (TRT) map

Device-derived layer segmentation results are available from both Topcon OCT devices in the AI-READI dataset. The original OCT volumes consist of 360 B-scans (each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$360\times 885$$\end{document} pixels) for the Maestro device and 320 B-scans (each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$320\times 992$$\end{document} pixels) for the Triton device. The TRT map is defined as the A-scan distances for the entire OCT scan between the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and Bruch’s membrane (BM). However, the provided device-derived segmentation results are not always reliable. To improve accuracy, we trained an additional nnU-Net model27 to segment the ILM and BM using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6~\times ~6~\textrm{mm}^2$$\end{document} OCT scans (RTVue-XR, Optovue) from 300 eyes in the publicly available OCTA-500 dataset25. This subset followed the standard OCTA-500 data split: 240 eyes for training, 10 for validation, and 50 for testing. Only the ILM and BM layer annotations, provided as reference standard segmentation in the OCTA-500 dataset, were used during training. Therefore, for each OCT scan, we obtained two TRT maps: one derived from the original OCT device segmentation and the other generated from our nnU-Net model. The color coding scheme used to visualize the TRT maps in this study was designed to resemble the one used in the “3D Macula Reports” produced by the Maestro2 and Triton devices.

Data quality control and selection

The original AI-READI dataset included imaging data from participants scanned with the Maestro2 device, consisting of CFPs from 1,042 participants and OCT scans from 1,043 participants; with the Triton device, both CFPs and OCT scans were available from 1,041 participants.

To evaluate the image quality of the CFPs, a publicly available vessel segmentation tool (AutoMorph28) was used to determine whether each CFP met the “Good Quality” criterion. Based on this assessment, 1,923 good-quality CFPs were identified from Maestro2 and 1,881 from Triton.

For the OCT scans, TRT maps generated by both the original OCT device and the nnU-Net model were reviewed side by side by manual visual inspection, separately for each eye and for each device. The reference standard TRT map was selected according to the following procedure. If one of the two TRT maps (either from the OCT device or nnU-Net) showed clear artifacts, the higher quality map was selected as the reference. If both maps were comparable in quality, the nnU-Net-derived map was chosen. If both maps were of poor quality, the corresponding eye was excluded from further analysis. Following this quality control process, 1,721 eyes imaged with the Maestro2 and 1,714 eyes imaged with the Triton had usable TRT maps.

For inclusion in the final analysis dataset, each eye was required to have a good-quality CFP and complete OCT data available for the TRT maps from both the Maestro2 and Triton devices for that same eye. Applying these criteria yielded a final analysis set of 1,426 eyes from 807 participants (Table 1). Foundation model fine-tuning

The AI-READI dataset includes CFPs acquired from two imaging devices: Maestro2 and Triton. Although the pre-trained RETFound model effectively generated LVs based on these CFP images, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)29 visualization revealed a clear separation between the Maestro2 and Triton, indicating domain-specific bias (Fig. 2A). To reduce this bias, RETFound was fine-tuned using 28,020 CFPs from the EyePACS dataset, selected through AutoMorph-based quality filtering. After fine-tuning, UMAP projections showed improved alignment between the two devices’ representations, illustrating a reduction in device-related distributional differences (Fig. 2B).

Training of the prediction model on the AI-READI dataset

The AI-READI dataset, consisting of 1,426 eyes with paired CFPs and OCT-derived TRT maps (that passed quality control as previously described), was divided into training (1,011 eyes), validation (208 eyes), and test (207 eyes) sets. This partition followed AI-READI’s official patient-level split to ensure consistent evaluation and prevent data leakage across subsets.

During training, data augmentation was applied to CFPs before feature extraction (Fig. 3A). Albumentations library30 (Version 2.0.5) was used to data augmentation, including random horizontal and vertical flips, random cropping, and color adjustments using the ColorJitter function. Specifically, brightness, contrast, and saturation were independently adjusted using random scaling factors sampled from [0.8, 1.2], and hue was shifted by a random value from the range [–0.05, 0.05]. Augmentation was applied only to training data and was not used for validation or testing.

For all experiments, the fine-tuned RETFound model was used as a fixed feature extractor (Fig. 3B). Each CFP was passed through RETFound to generate a feature vector (of 1024 LVs), which was then fed into a CNN decoder trained from scratch. The RETFound weights remained frozen throughout. Decoder weights were randomly initialized and optimized during training. Three distinct training strategies were evaluated in this study, as outlined below: Individual Models (Fig. 3C): Two separate decoders were trained independently. The Maestro2 model was trained exclusively using CFPs acquired from the Maestro2 device and their corresponding reference TRT maps. Similarly, the Triton model was trained using only Triton CFPs and reference TRT maps. No cross-device data was used in either model.Combination Model (Fig. 3D): A single decoder was trained using CFPs from both devices. For each input image, the corresponding device identity was explicitly tracked. The decoder’s output was compared against the reference TRT map generated from the same device as the input CFP. The loss function was computed as the pixel-wise difference between the predicted and reference TRT maps, ensuring device-consistent supervision during training.Random Selection Model (Fig. 3E): A single decoder was trained using CFPs from both devices, but the device identity was not used during training. For each CFP, the loss was computed against a randomly selected reference TRT map (either Maestro2-derived or Triton-derived) from the same eye. This strategy introduced additional variation into the supervision signal to test the model’s robustness to label variation.All three models were trained using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function and optimized with Adam optimizer. Training was conducted for 250 epochs with a batch size of 64 and a fixed learning rate of 0.0001. Both training and validation loss curves were monitored to ensure that the models reached a stable plateau. As the losses showed consistent convergence without significant divergence between the training and validation sets, the model weights from the final epoch were selected for subsequent evaluation. No early stopping or intermediate checkpoint selection criteria were applied. Model evaluations and statistical analysis

Model performance was assessed using the test set from the AI-READI dataset, which included 207 eyes with paired CFPs and OCTs. As shown in Table 4, both the Individual and Combination Models exhibited minimal signed differences when evaluated against their respective device-specific references. Across Maestro2 and Triton inputs, the mean signed errors ranged from approximately −1.0 to 2.4 µm, with standard deviations between 13.8 and 15.2 µm, indicating low prediction bias but mild variability (compared to the inter-device TRT difference in Table 3). In contrast, the Random Selection Model showed substantial signed differences when compared to device-specific references, with a bias of −19.0 µm for Maestro2 inputs and 15.0 µm for Triton inputs.

Meanwhile, we also evaluated all three models against the common “Average” reference map (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y^A$$\end{document}). As expected, because the Individual and Combination Models were heavily biased toward their training devices, they both exhibited significant signed differences against the inter-device Average reference (17.2 µm and 17.4 µm for Maestro2 inputs; −17.9 µm and −14.5 µm for Triton inputs). Conversely, for the Random Selection Model, the bias was largely mitigated when evaluated against the Average reference, yielding signed differences of only −2.1 µm and −1.9 µm for Maestro2 and Triton inputs, respectively. This suggests that the “Average” reference serves as a more appropriate baseline for models not conditioned on device identity, and confirms that the Random Selection approach successfully converges toward a device-agnostic mean.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a DL framework that estimates TRT maps from CFPs by decoding LVs extracted from a foundation model trained on large-scale retinal images. Our models were developed and evaluated using the AI-READI dataset, a publicly available, high-quality resource that includes carefully curated multimodal imaging data from patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, collected across multiple devices. Our previous work demonstrated success in estimating OCT-based thickness from CFPs in the context of optic disc swelling, but the earlier approach required explicit image registration between CFPs and OCTs, which limited scalability22,23. In contrast, the current framework eliminates this need by operating directly on learned CFP latent representations, enabling a fully end-to-end pipeline. This work provides an important step toward linking retinal thickness-related features across imaging modalities in the latent space, providing a platform for future studies that involve other retinal conditions or systemic diseases.

We proposed and evaluated three deep learning strategies for training the decoder to estimate the TRT maps: the Individual models, the Combination model, and the Random Selection model. Both the Individual and Combination models were trained with explicit knowledge of device identity, using device-specific reference TRT maps during supervision. As a result, these two models demonstrated low bias when evaluated against their respective device-specific reference maps and achieved high predictive precision, with relative differences generally around 5% (Table 5).

In contrast, the Random Selection model was trained without device identity, using randomly selected reference maps from either device for each eye, thereby introducing label variability during training. While convergence toward an average representation under such conditions is expected with a mean squared error objective, the model in this study demonstrated this behavior in the context of cross-device TRT map estimation. In particular, the model showed opposing biases when evaluated against device-specific references, but these biases were largely mitigated when compared with the inter-device average reference map. The estimates were slightly closer to Maestro2 than Triton, although the difference was minimal (Table 5). Notably, this averaging behavior was observed at the pixel level while preserving meaningful spatial anatomical patterns.

We observed a consistent baseline discrepancy between the reference TRT maps derived from the Maestro2 and Triton devices. On average, the Maestro2 maps were more than 30 µm thicker than those from Triton (Table 3). To verify this difference, we resegmented all OCT scans using a customized nnU-Net approach and performed manual review of the segmentation results by overlaying them on the original scans (Data Quality Control and Selection section; additional details are provided in the supplementary document). Our data showed that the thickness offset was not segmentation errors from the device software or our segmentation model. These findings highlight the difficulty of defining a consistent “ground truth” across devices and emphasize the importance of developing models that can generalize across device-specific measurement biases, particularly in large-scale and heterogeneous datasets.

However, this inter-device difference contrasts with findings from previous studies. In 2023, Hou et al.31,32 reported no significant differences in TRT and ganglion cell complex thickness measured from macular OCT scans between the Maestro2 and Triton devices in patients with retinopathy and glaucoma. Also, our observation of thicker measurements from the SD-OCT device (Maestro2) compared to the SS-OCT device (Triton) is inconsistent with the results from Wan et al.33, who found the opposite trend. These conflicting findings may reflect differences in diseases, device models, calibration settings, segmentation algorithms, or patient cohorts, and further strengthen the need for AI systems that are robust to such variability. Additional details on the comparison of TRT maps between Maestro2 and Triton are provided in the supplementary document.

To clarify the scope of the proposed method, this study estimated TRT maps as a surrogate for depth-related retinal information derived from OCT. However, thickness changes reflect only one aspect of retinal structural alterations and do not capture the full depth-resolved organization of retinal layers or other morphological features. Accordingly, our proposed approach did not aim to recover the complete structural information available in OCT and was not designed for direct DR or DME diagnosis. Instead, it serves as a modality-bridging framework that provides complementary thickness information for CFPs. With larger paired CFP and OCT datasets, future models that directly estimate OCT volumes from CFPs may enable a more comprehensive characterization of retinal structure and potentially support the detection of DR and DME.

One main limitation of this study was the model’s reduced accuracy in predicting TRT maps in cases of macular edema, particularly in the central foveal region (Figs. 4C and 4D). This limitation is largely attributable to the severe under-representation of such cases in the training dataset with only 19 out of 1,011 eyes identified as having clear macular edema. Due to the small number of these cases, a meaningful quantitative subgroup analysis could not be conducted reliably; therefore, the current evaluation of macular edema remained qualitative. The limited sample size likely prevented the model from learning the diverse fundus characteristics associated with edema. However, this limitation can likely be addressed by expanding future training datasets to include a broader range of diabetic retinopathy severity, particularly cases with macular edema.

Other limitations include potential sensitivity of the model to variations in CFP’s color tone and pigmentation. Differences in illumination and pigmentation may not have been fully captured by data augmentation, and the AI-READI dataset does not include racial or pigmentation-related metadata, which limits the ability to assess or correct for such effects. Additionally, our findings are specific to the two OCT devices used in AI-READI (Maestro2 and Triton) and may not generalize to other CFP systems (e.g., Optos wide-field CFPs). Lastly, the “Average” reference TRT map used for evaluation, while designed to mitigate inter-device bias, may not represent a definitive “ground truth”, and its validity remains open to interpretation.

Despite these limitations, the ability to estimate retinal thickness maps from more accessible CPFs holds potential for enhancing screening and risk assessment in ophthalmology, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Future work will focus on improving model robustness to image variability, enhancing the detection of specific pathological features like edema through training on more diverse and representative datasets, and prospectively validating these approaches across a wider range of devices and diverse patient populations to ascertain their clinical utility. Until such prospective clinical validations are conducted, our current findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Exploring more advanced domain adaptation techniques could also be beneficial in developing truly device-agnostic prediction models.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that TRT maps can be estimated from latent feature vectors extracted from CFPs using a retinal foundation model (RETFound). All three decoder training strategies produced reasonable predictive performance. Among them, the Random Selection model (designed to be device-agnostic) showed particular promise. When evaluated against the inter-device average reference, this model exhibited low overall bias and achieved strong performance in terms of normalized error. These results agree with the ongoing challenge of inter-device variability in ophthalmic AI and support the use of strategies that aim to predict a unified, device-independent representation. Such approaches may enable the development of more robust and generalizable tools for retinal image analysis.
