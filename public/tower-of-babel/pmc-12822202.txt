Disrupted continuity in borderline personality disorder: a systematic assessment of temporal experience
Authors: Anastazja Szuła, Anna Sterna, Giovanni Stanghellini, Anna Rewekant, Marcin Moskalewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhenomenological literature describes borderline personality disorder (BPD) as marked by instantaneous and immediate temporal experience. Meanwhile, qualitative clinical findings suggest that BPD lived experience is dominated by a negatively biased, disorganised past and an overintense present, yet systematic research remains limited.MethodsCross-sectional study, with 52 inpatients diagnosed with BPD compared to a healthy control group (N = 98). To examine temporal experience in individuals with BPD quantitatively, this study uses the Transdiagnostic Assessment of Temporal Experience (TATE), a novel, structured, phenomenological interview tool. Data were analysed using factor analysis to identify underlying temporal constructs; group differences were tested using Mann-Whitney U tests. The interpretative framework is phenomenological psychopathology.ResultsThe most pronounced features in the BPD group included past-negative intrusions, flashbacks, a sense of present paralysis, and anxiety about the future. The BPD group scored overall higher than controls (p < 0.001), and, in particular, significantly higher in the dimensions related to explicit anomalous experiences of the past, present, and future. The temporal disturbances were also felt as more intense, regardless of the actual scores. Crucially, one reliable factor emerged, indicating an underlying structure of disrupted temporal continuity, in line with the existing theory of phenomenological psychopathology.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that temporal fragmentation – especially in the form of past-negative intrusions and flashbacks – may be a core feature of BPD lived experience and relevant for future diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks.Trial registrationNot applicable.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07699-y.

Introduction

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is one of the most common personality disorders in Poland and worldwide. The prevalence is estimated at 0.7 to 2.7% in the general adult population [1].

BPD is characterized by pervasive patterns of instability in mood, self-image, and interpersonal relationships, as well as marked impulsivity and difficulty in adequately regulating emotions, often manifested in impulsive, self-destructive, or self-defeating behavior. The dominant state is a feeling of inner emptinessn [2, 3].

Temporality in borderline personality disorder

Disruptions in lived time are a well-documented feature of various psychiatric conditions. These include mania [4], depression [5, 6], addictions [7, 8], schizophrenia [9, 10], autism [11], and personality disorders [12].

As theory and analyses in phenomenological psychopathology suggest, abnormal temporality functions as a core latent mechanism sustaining – or even generating – symptoms of personality disorders [13]. One of the most thoroughly documented disorders in this regard is BPD [14], consistently described as marked by a temporal experience of immediacy, that is an incapability to narratively mediate between oneself and one’s experience [15] and instantaneity - that is a sense of intense rapidity and swiftness of whatever happens [16]. While empirical research remains limited, existing studies point to a fragmented and discontinuous sense of time [17–19], a dominant and overwhelming present [20], and tendencies toward hedonistic or fatalistic present orientations [21]. Moreover, individuals with BPD tend to have a negatively biased [21] and disorganised view of the past [22–24]. Apart from the general temporal experience pattern, the currently felt sense of temporality in BPD may be related to other life-world structures, including affectivity, spatial or self-experience [25, 26]. It may also bear transitional qualities of momentarily axis I symptoms, such as dissociation, flashbacks, anxiety, or substance-dependent ones.

A recent systematic review [27] concluded that BPD can ultimately be considered a disorder of temporal synthesis. Sterna et al., taking immediacy and instantaneity as a point of departure, incorporated all the preexisting findings on BPD time experience in a phenomenological meta-synthesis [14] and framed BPD as a disorder of temporality. They identify a characteristic temporal structure composed of a negativity-biased, emotionally dense yet disorganised past that is continuously evoked and reinterpreted through the lens of the current affective state; a bidirectional detachment of the present from both past and future, leading to an absorption in an overintense, immediate, and immersive present; and a blurred, disowned future characterised by a truncated temporal horizon and a prevailing sense of passivity. These features contribute to a discontinuous narrative self, alternating between moments of intense affective arousal and a diminished sense of futurity. A person with BPD becomes trapped in repetitive, disturbing cycles between a negatively-biased past and present, which limit self-transcendence and hinder the development of a coherent sense of self. Additionally, the synthesis highlights disruptions in intersubjective temporality, manifesting as a ‘pulsing’ presence, characterised by disturbed linearity and discontinuity, marked by intrusions of flashbacks, ruminations, and dissociative states.

At first sight, this might resemble a fragmented experience of Self characteristic of schizophrenia. This is because both borderline episodic way of being-in-the-world (i.e., discontinuity of Self experience) and schizophrenic fragmentation implies a matrix of profound alteration of temporality. In the first case, however, we find a failure of the active (i.e., narrative) temporal synthesis of individual life episodes (whether traumatic or not). It is the synthesis that presides over the organisation of such episodes in a narrative structure that allows the person/patient to put together the present with the past (i.e., the causes of the present) and with the future (its consequences). In results, borderline individuals do not experience their own Self as continuous over time (i.e, temporal mode called ‘instantaneity’ and kind of existence called ‘episodic’). Also, they have troubles in learning from past experience and therefore they repeat behaviours that lead to traumatic situations, such as abandonment. In the case of schizophrenia, meanwhile, the crisis of so-called passive synthesis (the one that presides over the configuration of the individual details of Self- and World-perception in a unitary framework) leads to the experience of a fragmented and therefore disturbing, unfamiliar and meaningless internal and external reality. Changes in Self- and World-perception, including fragmentation as one of their main features, have been described by classics such as Conrad’s studies on the beginning of schizophrenia.

Transdiagnostic assessment of temporal experience

Despite the importance of temporal experience in psychopathology, there is a lack of readily available tools to assess it in a phenomenologically rigorous manner. Existing research instruments, such as those used in neuropsychology, cognitive science, and psychometrics, focus on objectifying time perception rather than exploring lived time [13]. While some applied phenomenological studies examine temporal experience [14], most remain non-empirical, making it challenging to systematically assess temporality alterations within clinical practice.

To bridge this gap, Stanghellini et al. [28] developed the Transdiagnostic Assessment of Temporal Experiences (TATE), the first fully phenomenological structured interview tool for investigating alterations in temporal experience across psychiatric conditions. Unlike previous research tools, TATE systematically examines various aspects of lived time, and its quantitative nature makes it suitable for large-scale empirical studies. With the recent modification and validation of its Polish version, TATE PL [29], the tool is ready to be used to assess the various dimensions of temporal experience in people with different diagnoses.

TATE consists of 42 items, each corresponding to a different aspect of lived time, which may present as anomalous. The items are separated into seven categories, corresponding to the following dimensions of lived temporality: anomalies of synchrony (items 1.a – 1.c), anomalies of time structure (2.a – 2.c), anomalies of implicit time flow (3.a – 3.f), anomalies of explicit time flow (4.a – 4.c), anomalous experiences of the past (5a – 5.f), the present (6.a – 6.b) and the future (7a – 7.g). Each item consists of one primary interview prompt and several additional prompts. In the Polish version of the TATE interview script, the items are arranged in an alternative, conversational order and grouped into ten sets.

The participants themselves (with help on the part of the interviewer, if needed) evaluate each of the 42 TATE items on three 0–7 scales regarding frequency, intensity, and impairment. A composite scale assesses the overall severity of anomalous temporal experiences by averaging scores from the three scales. The overall TATE score is calculated by summing all 42 severity scores and takes values from 0 to 294.

Aims

This paper presents the results from 52 TATE interviews with people diagnosed with borderline personality disorder in comparison to a healthy control group. The study seeks to map the temporal characteristics that define the BPD experience and identify underlying patterns, and both validate and refine existing phenomenological models of BPD.

Methods

Sample

The sample size for this study was based on the required sample size for a one-tailed Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U test to compare two independent groups. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the required total sample size (for both study and control group) is 106 participants.

Healthy control group

The healthy control group data comes from TATE PL validation [29] on a group of 98 Polish Poznan University of Medical Sciences students who reported having no mental disorder diagnosis. 78 (79.59%) of them were female, and 20 (20.41%) male, aged between 18 and 31 (M = 20.7, SD = 2.0). The data was collected in 2023.

Study group

Fifty-two participants were recruited from inpatients admitted in 2024 to three Polish hospitals based in different regions: the Neurosis and Personality Disorders Ward in Międzyrzecz Psychiatric Hospital, the Neurotic Disorders Treatment Ward in Gniezno Psychiatric Hospital, and the Province Neuropsychiatric Hospital in Kościan. We included participants clinically diagnosed with BPD according to the ICD-10 criteria, which remain in use in Poland. All participants also met the ICD-11 criteria, with symptom severity likely not mild given the fact of hospitalization. The exclusion criteria were immediate suicidal crisis and comorbidities that could affect the lived experience, such as diagnosed neuroatypicality. All included participants had their core characteristics aligned with the BPD pattern, although some still had additional diagnoses that, based on the consultation with the supervising physician, did not affect their experiences of BPD.

All fifty-two participants were Polish natives aged 18 to 63 (M = 32.1, SD = 12.7). Their educational background varied, with the majority holding a high school diploma. 44.2% (N = 23) were employed but on sick leave; others were unemployed - many due to prolonged hospitalisation, which lasted from two days to six months.

BPD study group characteristics

The overall duration of psychological and psychiatric treatment for BPD is difficult to establish due to breaks and past treatment for other diagnoses (such as PTSD, depression, anxiety, and addictions), which often was not continuous either. The mean duration of overall psychological and psychiatric treatment was around 4 years, with the shortest treatments having just started, and the longest lasting for more than twenty years.

Procedure

Each interview lasted between 30 and 60 min and was conducted by the same researcher in person at the hospitals. Participants heard the researcher read each primary interview prompt aloud and saw it displayed on a laptop screen along with the three response scales (frequency, intensity, impairment). They evaluated each item themselves by pointing to their chosen answers on the scales. They could view their selected answers, but did not see any additional prompts. All participants were encouraged to discuss the prompts and share additional information regarding their experience of time so that they could give answers that best aligned with their lived experience.

The researcher conducting the interviews is experienced in qualitative interviewing and attempted to influence the participants’ answers as little as possible, allowing them the freedom to score the items in accordance with their own experience. The researcher’s influence was limited to discussing and suggesting the best possible scores in moments of doubt, solely based on participants’ reports.

We conducted all calculations using SQLite v. 3.38.5 accessed by vscode-sqlite v0.14.1 extension for Visual Studio Code v. 1.95, MS Excel v. 2410, and IBM SPSS Statistics 23. All figures were generated with Plotly graphing library for Python and edited in Inkscape 1.3.2.

Extremes

The extremes across the frequency, intensity, and impairment scales highlight how different ATE impact individuals in varied ways.

Item 5.f – reviving the past – marks the highest difference between intensity and frequency scores, meaning its high severity is mainly caused by how strongly this ATE is experienced, rather than how often it occurs. Item 2.c – déjà vécu – is an ATE that is experienced often but with low impairment, meaning that, although frequent, the experience is not very dysfunctional. The most functionally disruptive experience appears to be 5.a – negative view of the past, which shows the highest combined score for both frequency and impairment. 6.c – multi-possibility of the present – stands out as being intensely felt yet minimally impairing, with a substantial gap between mean intensity (3.635) and impairment (1.692), meaning this experience might be considered positive rather than negative.

Factor analysis

We assessed the suitability of the data for factor analysis using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy. We aimed to identify factors for each of the four scales (frequency, intensity, impairment, and severity). The KMO values for frequency and intensity were below the acceptable threshold of 0.5, whereas impairment and severity yielded values of 0.515 and 0.552, respectively, indicating mediocre suitability [30]. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²=1500.505 and χ²=1560.822, p < 0.001), suggesting sufficient correlations for factor analysis.

Given these results, we focused on impairment and severity scales and conducted an exploratory factor analysis using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation. The initial analysis suggested a 12-factor and an 11-factor solution, respectively, based on eigenvalues > 1. However, further examination and scree plot inspection point to a single dominant factor for both scales, the remaining factors being too weak. Therefore, the results suggest that the underlying construct is largely unidimensional regardless of scale. Figure 4 shows factor loadings for the one, dominant factor of all 42 items.

For both the impairment and severity scales, we assessed factor reliability using the criteria of Guadagnoli and Velicer [31]. The dominant factor for impairment included seven items with loadings above 0.6, while the dominant factor for severity included eleven such items, indicating their reliability. To determine the most meaningful contributors, we applied a cutoff of 0.63, following Comrey & Lee [32], who classify loadings ≥ 0.63 as ‘very good’, as they account for at least 40% of the common variance.

For impairment, the final retained factor comprised five ‘very good’ contributing items, highest to lowest loading: 2.a, 6.h, 2.b, 1.b, 4.c. Similarly, for severity, five items met the criterion: 6.h, 2.a, 1.b, 2.b, and 1.c.

BPD study group vs. healthy control group

The overall TATE scores (all scores for severity, summed) are higher for the BPD group than for the control group (M = 105.590 vs. M = 73.366). Figure 5 shows the distribution of TATE scores among the two groups.

The mean severity is notably higher in the BPD group compared to the control group for all dimensions related to narrative or biographical temporality (5–7), with the most pronounced difference observed in the fifth dimension – anomalous experience of the past – where the BPD study group scored a mean of M = 3.136 versus M = 1.721 in the control group (Fig. 6). Main contributors

Not all results for severity for items assigned to dimensions 5–7 differ significantly between the two groups. In dimension 5 (past), only one out of six does not differ (p > 0.05) − 5.e; in dimension 6 (present), the largest dimension in terms of number of items, three out of fourteen do not differ − 6.d, 6.g, 6.n, and in dimension 7 (future) four out of seven (the majority) do not − 7.a, 7.c, 7.e, 7.g.

Several items primarily contribute to the high differences in mean results described above, whose severity is considerably higher in the BPD group than in the control group (p < 0.001, medium-to-large effect size, |r| > 0.40). A cutoff of p < 0.001 and |r| > 0.40 was chosen to capture items with notably significant differences and at least medium-to-large effects, consistent with Cohen’s [33] conventions and adapted for nonparametric analysis. Discussion

Disruption of Temporal continuity and BPD phenomenology

A number of observations relevant to classical phenomenological concepts regarding BPD may be inferred from the results. Predominantly, our data indicate that individuals diagnosed with BPD experience significant disruptions in narrative or biographical temporality, with intense experience of an intruding, negative past. One reliable factor emerged, encompassing items related to inconsistency of the present, disarticulation, desynchronisation, irruption, and temporal leaps – elements that originate from several distinct dimensions of time experience but collectively point to a multilayered disruption in temporal continuity. This finding relates to previous philosophical contributions concerning the concept of intra festum [34] and instantaneity [35, 36], meaning that people with BPD tend to live in the immediacy of a present not articulated with the past or the future. Research based on the Cottle Circles Test [17] reinforces the image of atomised time: the passage of time is experienced as discontinuous, with clear separations between temporal dimensions. The present experience in BPD thus appears isolated, interrupted only by violent memories of the past or anxious projections into a blurred and undefined future.

Large and significant differences between the BPD and control groups appear in dimensions that more directly target narrative and biographical temporality. They combine into a unified picture of disrupted narrative temporality, thus supporting the existing phenomenological theory but also supplementing it with nuance. What particularly stands out as an addition to previous phenomenological conceptualizations is a negatively biased and reverberating past. Incidentally, psychometric research based on Zimbardo Time Perspectives Inventory complements these insights. It shows that individuals with BPD score higher on the past-negative subscale than controls [21] and individuals with narcissistic personality disorder [12], and lower on the past-positive subscale than individuals with OCD [29]. Meanwhile, the present and the future appear somewhat paralysed – detached and passive, characterised by a lack of novelty or direction, and anticipatory anxiety.

Our study also provides indirect evidence on previous theoretical contributions according to which persons with BPD are trapped in the present moment, unable to project themselves toward the future [12, 15, 16]. This kind of ‘chaotic lethargy’ [17] is experienced as an acute intermittent state, during which the dysphoric mood intensifies, culminating in a painful paralysis of action [16]. The BPD present is experienced as paralysed, laden with a sense of spleen or boredom, rather than sadness or despair [37].

BPD temporal alterations versus melancholic depression and schizophrenia

Borderline, depressive, and schizophrenic alterations of experience can be traced back to disturbed temporality, although of a different nature. Our results can be referred to previous theories and anecdotal findings in phenomenological psychopathology where BPD is often described as constellated by painful mood states. Stanghellini and Rosfort [38] point to dysphoric mood as a recurrent mood state that characterizes depressive experiences in persons with BPD. Dysphoria is experienced both as a deadly stagnation and as a chaotic, wild impulse [39]. In such a state, the past intrudes into the present as sudden, flashback-like episodes marked by resentment and a feeling of impotence. It differentiates BPD from melancholic depression, where past intrusions take a hopeless form. When it comes to schizophrenia, in both cases, the initial disorientation (entailing various degrees of depersonalization and derealization experiences) is followed by an ‘organisational’ framework that takes over and composes episodes and fragments in a ‘great narrative’. Among these ‘great narratives’ are the forms of delusions that characterise each psychopathological condition. Delusions are indeed the new ‘organizational framework’ within which episodes (borderline) and fragments (schizophrenic) find their explanation. But these ‘great narratives’ have different characters in these two conditions. Schizophrenic delusions typically have an ontological nature, meaning that their content concerns the ‘reality of reality’ and other ontological ‘discoveries’ or ‘revelations’ (such as those described by Kępiński [40] related to deep metaphysical issues), for example the imminent end of the world and the artificial nature of reality itself. On the other hand, the delusional themes we find in the delusional ‘outbursts’ of borderline individuals concern ontic issues, i.e. issues that are much more closely linked to personal problems and traumas, such as abandonment by a significant. Other, or the repetition of adverse events attributed to a cruel personal destiny that relentlessly persecutes the borderline individual.

In summary, in both conditions we find a dialectics linking disorganization – i.e., depersonalization and derealization (although of different phenomenological character) – and delusional re-organization (also of different phenomenological character). Disorganization in both conditions is linked to temporal disorganization (disorganization of temporality) but in borderline people it affects the narrative level of temporality, in schizophrenic people the ‘passive’ mode of temporal synthesis. Also, re-organization is profoundly different in the two conditions: in borderline people it has an ontic (‘down-to earth’) character, whereas in schizophrenic people it has an ontological (‘metaphysical’) one.

Limitations

TATE, being a transdiagnostic tool, is not designed to analyse the pattern of strictly BPD experience. It does not contain questions that directly refer to immediacy or instantaneity, the theoretically grounded phenomenological concepts regarding BPD temporality.

Most people with borderline personality disorder have coexisting mental disorders. The common are anxiety disorders (85%), mood disorders (83%), or substance use disorders (78%) (24). We made sure the participants of this study had BPD as their main diagnosis, but there is always a risk that symptoms of the above disorders may have been present and have influenced the answers obtained.

No additional severity measures were included that would allow us to correlate TATE scores with established psychometric variables, meaning that temporality was effectively treated as a stand-alone construct in this study. No standardized instruments were used to assess symptom severity, personality functioning, or other dimensions that might contextualize temporal experience within a broader psychopathological profile. Although severity scores were derived for TATE items, the absence of independent validation measures (e.g., general psychopathology scales, dissociation measures, functional impairment ratings) limits the extent to which the temporal anomalies observed here can be interpreted in relation to other clinical features.

In addition, the control group was not designed to be quota-representative of the study group, which constrains the strength of between-group comparisons.

None of the interviews included a broader, qualitative component; the introductory and concluding questions served a conversational purpose rather than as a data source. Even though the interviews were all one-on-one and included discussions about the items’ content and choice of answers, no qualitative data were collected.

The researcher who conducted the interviews was not blind to the groups (BPD vs. controls). This, however, should not significantly influence the data collected, given the nature of the TATE interview procedure. Participants evaluate the items themselves and are only helped and guided by the interviewer in moments of uncertainty, with guidance based on their own reports.

TATE is a new tool, and this study represents its first application following validation. It still requires further refinement, which will be possible after sufficient data is collected and analyzed. Further research should aim to distinguish between BPD people in therapy and without therapy, and control for psychopathological symptoms as well as psychiatric medications used.

Conclusions

This study set out to map the characteristics of temporality in BPD and identify temporal patterns that shape the lived experience of those affected. The findings reveal a disrupted temporal continuity as an underlying phenomenological structure (identified through factor analysis). Additionally, the data highlight the prominence of a negatively biased and revived past, characterized by high intensity relative to its frequency and impairment. These findings both largely confirm and refine the existing phenomenological model of BPD, which emphasises the instantaneity of experience. Our results suggest a more complex picture: a multilayered narrative discontinuity. Notably, the most pronounced effects appear in the intensity of past-negative intrusions and flashbacks, indicating that these may constitute a core temporal feature of BPD.
