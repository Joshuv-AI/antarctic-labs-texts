Transcriptome analysis reveals a role of FOXO3 in antileukemia/lymphoma properties of panduratin A
Authors: Suttinee Phuagkhaopong, Jiranan Janpattanapichai, Noppavut Sirirak, Phisit Khemawoot, Pornpun Vivithanaporn, Kran Suknuntha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Boesenbergia rotunda, commonly known as fingerroot, is a medicinal and culinary plant native to the Indochina Peninsula. We found that panduratin A (Pan-A), one of the compounds present in the rhizome extract of fingerroot, inhibited cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and promoted cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 phase in multiple hematologic malignant cell lines including leukemia and lymphoma lines. Pan-A inhibited these activities in leukemia and lymphoma cells in a concentration-dependent manner. High-throughput transcriptome analysis indicated that Pan-A is involved in the cell cycle, cellular senescence, apoptosis, and multiple canonical signaling pathways in lymphoma cells. The Forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factor family was identified as a potential target of Pan-A. Western blot showed elevated caspase 7 and cPARP/PARP in the B-cell lymphoma cells after treatment with Pan-A. The inhibitory effects were accompanied by stimulation of Akt signaling and phosphorylation of FOXO3. Immunohistochemistry of tissues from patients with B-cell lymphoma revealed detectable levels of FOXO3 protein specifically in neoplastic B cells. Overall, our results highlight FOXO3 as a player underlying antileukemia/lymphoma effects of Pan-A.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75630-8.

Introduction

Leukemia and lymphoma are cancers that affect the blood and lymphatic system, respectively. Leukemia is a circulating hematologic malignancy that occurs in the bone marrow while lymphoma mainly occurs in the lymphatic system, especially in lymph nodes. One of the hematologic malignancies classified by the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) consists of extremely heterogeneous diseases, ranging from indolent low-grade B-cell lymphoma to aggressive large B-cell lymphoma. The treatment options largely depend on the type and stage of disease. While chemotherapy is the mainstay of treatment, it is associated with toxicity and side effects. In the last decade, immunotherapy such as rituximab, an anti-CD20 antibody, has emerged as one of the most effective treatments for NHL and exhibits broad activity against several types of indolent lymphomas1–3. However, immunotherapy is significantly more expensive than traditional chemotherapy, which is a major limitation to its application. Therefore, alternative agents for the treatment of leukemia and lymphoma are highly desirable.

In recent years, natural products have received increasing interest in the fields of preventive and anticancer medicine4. Several medicinal plants are already being used to treat or prevent development of cancer5,6. Additionally, plants can be easily cultivated locally, and thus scaling-up for industrial purposes is possible.

Boesenbergia rotunda (Zingiberaceae), commonly known as fingerroot, is used as a culinary spice and traditional herb in the Indochina region. The major bioactive compounds in the rhizome extract are flavonoids, including panduratin A (Pan-A) and pinostrobin. Both chemicals exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties (reviewed in 7 and 8). Notably, Pan-A did not show serious toxicity in mice9,10. The standardized rhizome extract contains a significant amount of Pan-A, indicating its feasibility as a source for mass production10,11.

Recent studies have demonstrated that Pan-A exhibits anti-cancer properties. Pan-A inhibits cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in various malignant cell lines including prostate cancer12, lung cancer13, breast cancer, colon cancer9, and leukemia cells14. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that Pan-A triggers apoptosis through the mitochondrial-dependent pathway, the TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand, and upregulation of the FAS death receptor. Pan-A also regulated several cell cycle proteins such as p21WAF1/Cip1, p27Kip1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK6, cyclin D1, and cyclin E12. However, whether Pan-A induces anti-cancer effects in lymphoma has not been clarified.

In this study, we evaluated the anticancer properties and mechanisms of Pan-A in leukemia and lymphoma. We evaluated the effects of Pan-A in leukemia and lymphoma using HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Pfeifer, and Toledo cell lines. Transcriptome analysis was performed to explore the signaling pathways and cellular processes that may be regulated by Pan-A.

Methods

Cell culture and differentiation

The HL-60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Pfeifer, and Toledo hematolymphoid cell lines were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) (Life Technologies, USA), 1X penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco). All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The medium was changed every 5–6 days.

Chemicals

Pan-A (analytical grade) with a purity of more than 98% was purchased from Biosynth Carbosynth (UK). Pinostrobin (analytical grade) with a purity of more than 99% was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Singapore). Pan-A and Pinostrobin were reconstituted in DMSO to make a 50 mM stock solution.

Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50)assays

HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Toledo, and Pfeiffer cells were cultured in 96-well plates in duplicate wells. HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat were plated at 2 × 104 cells/wells while Toledo, and Pfeiffer were plated at 4 × 104 cells/wells. Cells were treated with various concentrations of Pan-A and/or pinostrobin ranging from 0.05 to 100 µM for 24 h. Viable cells were analyzed using CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution (Promega, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. The IC50 values were determined as the concentration at which the proportion of viable cells was 50% compared with control wells. Relative IC50 was determined by fitting a sigmoidal dose-response curve to the cell proliferation data. Data from three independent experiments were used to determine the IC50 values.

Viability/cytotoxicity assay

HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Toledo, and Pfeiffer cells were cultured in 12-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/wells. After a 24 h treatment, cell viability and cytotoxicity were analyzed using the Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay (Biotium, CA, USA) and by flow cytometry. In brief, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove serum esterase activity. Cells were then labelled with Calcein AM and Ethidium homodimer III for 30 min at room temperature prior to flow cytometric analysis using the BD LSRFortessa X-20. Single cells were gated and analyzed by FlowJo software (Becton Dickinson, USA).

Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) assay

HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Toledo, and Pfeiffer cells were cultured in 12-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/wells. After 24 h of treatment, cells were labeled with 10 µM BrdU for 1 h. Treated and control cells were collected, fixed, and permeabilized using the BrdU FITC kit (BD Bioscience, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. In brief, the cells were incubated with 4 N HCl + 0.5% Triton X for 45 min at room temperature. The samples were neutralized with 0.1 M sodium borate pH 8.5 for 10 min prior to labeling with anti-BrdU FITC antibody for 1 h. Flow cytometric analysis was performed as described above.

Apoptosis assay

HL60, K562, THP1, Jurkat, Toledo, and Pfeiffer cells were cultured in 12-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/wells. Following treatment with 10 µM Pan-A for 24 h, cells were washed twice with Annexin V binding buffer. Cells were then stained with Annexin-V–FITC and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Bioscience) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Isotype-matched controls were used to set the threshold for non-specific background staining. Flow cytometric analysis was performed as described above.

Transcriptome analysis

Pfeiffer and Toledo cells were cultured in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/wells. Cells were treated with 10 µM Pan-A for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated from control or treated cells using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit with on-column DNase treatment (New England Biolabs, USA). Equal amounts of RNA were pooled from four independent experiments. The quality of total RNA was confirmed by capillary electrophoresis on the Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The transcriptome analysis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA by BMKGENE - Multi-omics Sequencing Services. In brief, the mRNA was isolated by Oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads to generate the cDNA library. The qualified library was pooled on the basis of pre-designed target data volume and then sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform. The raw sequencing reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38_release95.genome.fa) using HISAT2 15. StringTie16 was applied to assemble the mapped reads.

Quantitative real time PCR (qPCR)

Pfeiffer and Toledo cells were cultured in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/wells. Following treatment of cells with 10 µM Pan-A for 24 h, total RNA was extracted using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit with on-column DNase treatment (New England Biolabs). cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA and the iScrip cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR (Bio-Rad) and self-designed primers specific for FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, FOXO6, GADD45, SOD2, CDKN2B, TP5, and GAPDH genes (Supplementary Table 1). Reactions were carried out on a Bio-rad CFX96 real-time PCR machine. The expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method relative to the reference expression of GAPDH in each sample17. For the comparative quantitation across all samples, untreated Pfeiffer was used as the calibrator18.

Western blot

Pfeiffer and Toledo were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL in 12-well plates and incubated overnight. After serum starvation for 24 h, cells were treated with 10 µM Pan-A in complete medium for 6 h. The protein concentration of the cell lysate was determined using the Bio-Rad DC Protein assay reagents (Bio-Rad). Protein samples (20 µg) were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was cut and blocked in 5% skimmed milk for 2 h prior to hybridization with primary antibodies against pFOXO3, FOXO3, pAkt, Akt, cPARP, PARP, Caspase 7 or β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, CA, USA; 1:1000 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology; 1:3000 dilution) at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and analyzed using Image Studio software version 5.2 (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). The original blot images are included in the supplementary Fig. 2.

Immunohistochemistry

Patients ≥ 18 years old who had biopsy-proven B-cell lymphoma from December 2022 to December 2023 at the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Thailand were evaluated in this study. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the Mahidol University (MURA2022/783). Biospecimen were retrieved from The Biospecimen Trust Oversight Committee (Mahidol University, RABB 2022/006). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Specimens were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin wax prior to processing. BOND Epitope Retrieval Solution 1 (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA) was used for heat-induced epitope retrieval. Tissue sections were stained with FOXO3 (D19A7, Cell Signaling, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight and secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody (ab205718, Abcam, 1:500) at room temperature for 2 h.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from multiple experiments were reported as mean ± SD. Significance levels were determined by two-tailed Student t test or one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test as appropriate. p < 0.05, two-tailed, is considered significance. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Pan-A decreased cell viability and induced apoptosis in leukemia and lymphoma cells in vitro

We first examined Pan-A and pinostrobin, another bioactive compound found in the rhizome extract, in cytotoxicity assays. The inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) values were calculated from sigmoidal dose-response curves in the end-point CellTiter 96 assays measuring metabolic activity (Table 1). The IC50 was 3–5 µM for Pan-A, while pinostrobin showed no inhibitory effect (Supplementary Fig. 1). We next examined the combination of pinostrobin and Pan-A and found that the addition of pinostrobin had little impact on the IC50. Therefore, pinostrobin was excluded from subsequent experiments. We next performed flow cytometric analysis to simultaneously detect both viable and dead cells using Calcein AM and ethidium homodimer III (Fig. 1A). We found that Pan-A at 5 µM showed modest inhibitory effects on the cell viability while 10 µM was more remarkable. Thus, Pan-A at 5 and 10 µM was used for experiments. Cells in early (Annexin V+/7AAD−) and late apoptosis (Annexin V+/7AAD+) were analyzed using Annexin V/7AAD. After treatment with Pan-A for 24 h, the early and late apoptotic cell populations were significantly increased (p < 0.05) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B).

Pan-A induced G0/G1 arrest

To examine whether Pan-A reduced cell viability through alteration of the cell cycle, we next performed BrdU assays. The relative percentages of cells in the G0/G1 phase were increased by Pan-A in a dose-dependent manner in all cell lines except for HL60 cells (Fig. 2). The G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest was accompanied by a decrease in the percentage of cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.

Transcriptome analysis and identification of FOXO

We next explored the mechanism underlying the effects of Pan-A and focused on B-cell lymphoma. RNA-sequencing was performed in Pfeiffer and Toledo cell lines treated with 10 µM Pan-A for 24 h, and gene expression differences between the Pan-A and control groups were examined. A total of 2,459 and 1,838 genes were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Pfeiffer and Toledo cells, respectively (Fig. 3A&B). The DEGs were annotated by their functions using Cluster of Orthologous Groups (KOG databases for eukaryotes) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis19–21. KOG analysis indicated that posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones, and signal transduction mechanism were affected in both cell lines (Fig. 3C). Further analysis using KEGG enrichment analysis indicated Pan-A may be related to apoptosis, cell cycle, and major intracellular signaling pathways in both cell lines (Fig. 3D). The notable DEGs found in both lines included CDKN2B, FBXO32, GABARAPL1, CDK2, DDIT3, DUSP8, CXCL8, FZD7, FLNC, TNFAIP3, SKP2, and SYK. These genes are involved in the FOXO, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways. FOXO proteins mediate quiescence and enhanced survival in the hematopoietic stem cells22 while Akt involves in their posttranslational modification.

Pan-A treatment resulted in increased FOXO mRNA

We evaluated FOXO and the related genes, including FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, FOXO6, GADD45, SOD2, CDKN2B, and TP53, in the leukemia and lymphoma cell lines by quantitative PCR. Jurkat and Toledo cells tended to express higher levels of FOXO1 and FOXO3 compared with other cell lines (Fig. 4A). The mRNA levels of FOXO6, CDKN2B, and TP53 were too low for detection or not consistently detected in some cell lines (results not shown). After Pan-A treatment, all cell lines demonstrated elevated FOXO mRNAs, and Pfeiffer cells showed the highest fold change in FOXO3 (Fig. 4B). SOD2 and GADD45A did not show any significant changes in response to Pan-A treatment.

Pan-A induced stimulated Akt and increased phosphorylated FOXO3 during apoptosis

Among FOXO family proteins, FOXO3 has been demonstrated in variety of cancers23–26. Western blot analysis demonstrated elevation of apoptosis markers (caspase 7, PARP, and cleaved PARP) in Pfeiffer and Toledo cells treated with Pan-A (Fig. 5A&B), consistent with the Pan-A-mediated apoptosis demonstrated in Annexin V assays. While total FOXO3 remained unchanged upon Pan-A treatment, phosphorylated FOXO3 (pFOXO3) was significantly increased (Fig. 5A&B). The transcriptional activity of FOXO proteins is regulated through the insulin-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase - Akt/PKB signaling pathway (reviewed in27). We found that pAkt was significantly upregulated by 1.5–2 fold in cells treated with Pan-A compared with controls (Fig. 5A&B).

Immunohistochemistry

We evaluated the expression of FOXO3 protein in clinical specimens of B-cell lymphoma. Specimens with different diagnosis of B-cell lymphoma including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (n = 2), primary central nervous system lymphoma (n = 2), mantle cell lymphoma (n = 1), and Burkitt lymphoma (n = 1) were included into our pilot evaluation. Although the signal intensity was low, FOXO3 was heterogeneously detected in neoplastic B cells in all specimens except Burkitt lymphoma specimens (Fig. 6). FOXO3 was detected in the nucleus and/or cytoplasm.

Discussion

Naturally derived compounds have drawn researcher attention toward their anticancer properties as they are generally considered less toxic than standard chemotherapy. Several plants are already being used to treat or prevent development of cancer28. In this study, we demonstrated that Pan-A had significant inhibitory effect in vitro against several cell lines representing human chronic myeloid leukemia in the blastic phase, acute myeloid leukemia, monocytic leukemia, T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, and large B-cell lymphoma. Cyclophosphamide, a commonly used chemotherapy, reaches the maximal plasma concentration of 37.5–439 µM after the first dose29. In contrast, the IC50 for Pan-A was 3–5 µM across the tested hematologic cell lines. At these concentrations, Pan-A demonstrated no or minimal cytotoxicity in other cell types, including a human liver cancer cell line (HepG2), immortalized hepatocyte-like cell line (imHC), human normal kidney (HK-2), human neuroblastoma cell line (SH-SY5Y), and human colon cancer cell line (Caco-2)30.

Our results showed that Pan-A mediated cellular cytotoxicity through the induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in a dose-dependent manner. Pan-A induced a G0/G1 phase arrest, accompanied by a decrease in the percentage of cells in the S phase. While Pan-A demonstrated potent effects on apoptosis induction and viability inhibition across all tested lines, the effect on the cell cycle was pronounced in the THP1, Jurkat, Pfeiffer, and Toledo cells. This variation in response might be explained by the different cell types of each line. Jurkat, Pfeiffer, and Toledo cells are derived from a lymphoid lineage, THP1 cells are derived from a monocytic lineage, and HL60 and K562 are derived from a granulocytic lineage.

Anticancer activities of Pan-A have been reported in breast cancer, colon adenocarcinoma, and prostate cancer9,12–14; however, the precise mechanism of action remains unknown. We investigated the mechanism of Pan-A action in lymphoma cells using transcriptome analysis. The analysis of DEGs identified in Pan-A-treated B-cell lymphoma cells indicated the potential involvement of several canonical pathways involving cellular processes and intracellular signaling. The genes related to Forkhead box transcription factors (FOXO) were among the identified DEGs and selected for further evaluation. FOXO proteins play a role in various stress-related transcriptional programs including the antioxidant response, gluconeogenesis, cell cycle control, apoptosis, and autophagy31–38. Four FOXO proteins, FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4 and FOXO6, have been identified in mammals. While FOXO6 is largely specific to neurons, the other three factors are widely distributed in most tissues. FOXO3 has been implicated as a tumor suppressor in several cancers23,24,26,39,40. At the cellular level, FOXO3 is primarily regulated by posttranslational modification. Phosphorylation leads to the exclusion of FOXO3 from the nucleus and inhibits its activity41. This could explain why we did not detect any significant changes in total FOXO3 protein after Pan-A treatment. Similarly, SOD2 and GADD45A, well characterized FOXO target genes38,42,43 did not show any significant changes in transcript levels in response to Pan-A treatment.

In the absence of growth factors, FOXO3 controls cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis. When growth factors are present, Akt phosphorylates FOXO3, resulting in its inactivation and promoting degradation34. In our study, we found that Pan-A induces pAkt, which in turn phosphorylates FOXO3 during apoptosis. This cascade of signaling likely allows lymphoma to survive during Pan-A treatment. We speculate that FOXO3 may be suppressed during lymphomagenesis and drug resistance. We further examined FOXO3 protein expression in tumors from naïve B-cell lymphoma patients. Among the evaluated lymphomas, Burkitt lymphoma is well-known for its extremely high proliferation rate, as indicated by a Ki-67 (approximately 100%)44. We found that Burkitt lymphoma was negative for FOXO3, while other cases showed weak and heterogenous FOXO3 expression in the nucleus of neoplastic B cells. In one case, FOXO3 exhibited cytoplasmic expression, suggesting either constitutive nuclear export or prevention of nuclear import. Deregulation of FOXO3 is associated with a variety of malignancies. In many cancerous cell lines, c-Myc suppresses p27 mRNA expression by blocking FOXO3-mediated p27 transcription, resulting in uncontrolled proliferation and aggressive phenotype45. Therefore, our results suggest that suppression of FOXO3 is part of a survival program in lymphoma following exposure to noxious stimuli, and loss of FOXO3 expression in clinical specimens may indicate aggressive behavior and an unfavorable prognosis.

Typically, transcriptome analysis reveals multiple differentially expressed genes (DEGs), suggesting that more than one pathway is involved. In this study, we focused exclusively on FOXO3, which is a limitation of our work. Additionally, our pilot evaluation was limited to six newly diagnosed patients. Further evaluation with a larger population of clinical samples, including a variety of lymphomas, is required before considering clinical implications. Since Pan-A was used to screen for antileukemia/lymphoma activity, FOXO3 expression in clinical specimens might not affect its clinical use.

Overall, our results demonstrated that Pan-A inhibits cell proliferation, induces G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, and increases apoptosis of leukemia and lymphoma cells, in part, by suppression of FOXO3 via activation of Akt. Future studies should focus on mechanistic study of Pan-A, its clinical pharmacokinetics to determine the appropriate dose for therapeutic use, and a correlation of FOXO3 in clinical specimens of lymphoma and its impact.
