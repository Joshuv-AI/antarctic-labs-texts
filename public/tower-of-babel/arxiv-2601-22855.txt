The two-nest ants process on triangle-series-parallel graphs
Cécile Mailler, Zoé Varin

Abstract
The ants process is a stochastic process introduced by Kious, Mailler and Schapira as a model for the phenomenon of ants finding shortest paths between their nest and a source of food (seen as two marked nodes in a finite graph), with no other means of communications besides the pheromones they lay behind them as they explore their environment. The ants process relies on a reinforcement learning mechanism. In this paper, we modify the ants process by having more than one ants nest (and still one source of food). For technical reasons, we restrict ourselves to the case when there are two nests, and when the graph is a triangle between the two nests and the source of food, whose edges have been replaced by series-parallel graphs. In this setting, using stochastic approximation techniques, comparison with Pólya urns, and combinatorial arguments, we are able to prove that the ants process converges and to describe its limit.



1
Introduction

The ants process, originally introduced by Kious, Mailler and Schapira
12
;
13
,
is a stochastic process of successive random walks on a graph, designed to model the well-known phenomenon of a colony of ants being able to find shortest paths between the nest and a source of food.
It is widely believed that this biological phenomenon can be modelled using reinforcement learning, and this has inspired a whole branch of computer science called “ant colony optimisation” (see for example the book of Dorigo and Stützle
4
for an introduction to this topic).

Another striking biological phenomenon that is believed to be well-modelled by reinforcement learning is the phenomenon of a slime mould called “physarum” being capable of finding “optimal transport networks”. See, for example, Tero et al.
17
in which the authors make an experiment that shows that “
the slime mold Physarum polycephalum forms networks with comparable efficiency, fault tolerance, and cost to those of real-world infrastructure networks—in this case, the Tokyo rail system
”, and introduce a mathematical model for this phenomenon.
See also Bonifaci, Mehlhorn and Varma
3
, in which the authors prove that, in the model of Tero et al.
17
, physarum indeed can find shortest paths between two points in a graph.

Our aim in this paper is to generalise the ants process of Kious, Mailler, and Schapira
12
;
13

and see if this could be a model for the phenomenon of
physarum finding “optimal” transport networks
(we write “optimal” in quotes to acknowledge the fact
that there is no clear mathematical definition of that concept; this will thus be discussed later on).
Although we are able to prove that our new ants process converges, we fail to interpret the limit as an “optimal transport network”, suggesting that this generalisation is
not
a good model for physarum.

1.1
The multi-nest ants process

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph and let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
,
\mathcal{N}_{2}
and
\mathcal{F}
be three vertices in
V
.
Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
(this models the proportion of ants living in the first nest).
The ants process is a process
({\bf W}(n))_{n\geq 0}
such that, for all
n\geq 0
,
{\bf W}(n)=(W_{e}(n))_{e\in E}
is a sequence of non negative integers. For all
n\geq 0
and
e\in E
, we call
W_{e}(n)
the weight of edge
e
at time
n
and interpret it as the amount of pheromones laid on that edge up to time
n
.

We let
W_{e}(0)=1
for all
e\in E
.
Furthermore, for all
n\geq 0
, given
{\bf W}(n)
:

(a)

We set
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{0}=\mathcal{N}_{1}
with probability
\alpha
, and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{0}=\mathcal{N}_{2}
with probability
1-\alpha
(independently from everything).

(b)

We let
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be the random walk on
V
started at
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{0}
, stopped when first hitting
\mathcal{F}
, and whose transition probabilities are given by the edge-weights
{\bf W}(n)
. In other words, for all
i\geq 0
, for all
u\in V\setminus\{\mathcal{F}\},v\in V
, the probability that
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i+1}=v
given that
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i}=u
is proportional to
W_{\{u,v\}}(n)
(if
X_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=\mathcal{F}
, then
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i+1}=\mathcal{F}
with probability 1). This is interpreted as the trajectory of the
(n+1)
-th ant.

(c)

We then define
\gamma_{n+1}
as the set of edges crossed by the
(n+1)
-th ant on its way back to the nest, and we assume that it goes back to its nest following its trajectory backwards in time, erasing loops. Equivalently, every time it sits at a vertex that was visited several times on the way forward, the ant choose to cross the edge that was crossed first on the way forward.
Mathematically,
we define
i_{0}\coloneqq\min\{i:X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i}=\mathcal{F}\}
,
and while
j
is such that
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i_{j}}\neq X_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}
,
we define
i_{j+1}\coloneqq\min\{i-1:X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i}=X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i_{j}}\}
.
Then we set
\gamma_{n+1}=\{\{X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i_{j+1}},X^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}_{i_{j}}\},0\leq j<J\}
,
where
J
is the largest
j
for which
i_{j}
has been defined.
(See Figure
1
for an illustration.)

Finally, for all
e\in E
, we let

W_{e}(n+1)=W_{e}(n)+{\bf 1}_{e\in\gamma_{n+1}}.

Remark 1.1
.

If
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
, then one of the two nests is never used and we recover the model of Kious, Mailler and Schapira
12
;
13
.
In their papers, Kious, Mailler and Schapira study three variants of the ants process, which only differ by the definition of
\gamma_{n+1}
as a function of
(X_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)})_{i\geq 0}
.
In
13
, they show that the “trace-reinforced” version of the model (in which
\gamma_{n+1}
is the set of all edges crossed at least once by the
(n+1)
-th ant) does not find shortest paths in general, hence why we choose to not consider this version here.
In
12
, they consider two variants of the model, the “loop-erased” version and the “geodesic” one, which they conjecture both find shortest paths on any graph.
We choose to consider here the “loop-erased” version of the model because of technical reasons and because our analysis partly relies on applying the results obtained by
12
in the loop-erased model.

Theorem 1.5
.

We assume that
G
is a triangle-series-parallel graph as in Definition
1.4

\alpha\in(0,1)
. We let
\ell_{i}=h(G_{i})

\ell_{i}\geq 1
, for all

.
Finally, we let
({\bf W}(n))_{n\geq 0}
be the (loop-erased) multi-nest ant process on

Then, almost surely as

\frac{W_{e}(n)}{n}\to\chi_{e},

1-\alpha&\text{ if }i=3.\end{cases}

Furthermore, for all

(resp.

to
\mathcal{F}
inside

(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}
to
\mathcal{N}_{1}
inside

).

(ii)
If
\ell_{2}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
and
\ell_{3}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, then, almost surely as

,

\frac{N_{i}(n)}{n}\to\begin{cases}\beta_{1}&\text{ if }i=1\\
1-\beta_{1}&\text{ if }i=2\\
\beta_{3}&\text{ if }i=3.\end{cases}

where

\begin{cases}\beta_{1}&=\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}\left(\ell_{3}+\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}\right)}{\ell_{1}\ell_{3}+(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})\left((1-\alpha)(\ell_{3}-\ell_{2})+\alpha\ell_{1}\right)},\\[7.0pt]

Moreover, for

\forall e\in E_{i}
(respectively

\chi_{e}\neq 0

if and only if

belongs to at least one of the shortest paths from
\mathcal{N}_{i}
to
\mathcal{F}
inside
G_{i}
(respectively from
\mathcal{N}_{1}
to
\mathcal{N}_{2}
inside
G_{3}
).

(iii)
Finally, if
\ell_{3}\geq\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, then almost surely as

,

\frac{N_{i}(n)}{n}\to\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }i=1\\
1-\alpha&\text{ if }i=2\\
0&\text{ if }i=3.\end{cases}

(Consequently,
\forall e\in E_{3}
,
\chi_{e}=0
.)
Moreover, for all

, for all
e\in E_{i}
,
\chi_{e}\neq 0
if and only if

belongs to at least
one of the geodesics from
\mathcal{N}_{i}
to
\mathcal{F}
inside
G_{i}
.

We call an SP-triangle graph the

Ideas of the proof:
The idea of the proof of Theorem

Intuitively, it is clear that the two-nest ants process restricted to
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
behaves like a single nest ant process on these series-parallel graphs, and thus, by

and
G_{2}
.
Because, when they explore
G_{3}
, the ants can go from
\mathcal{N}_{1}
to
\mathcal{N}_{2}
as well as from
\mathcal{N}_{2}
to
\mathcal{N}_{1}
, the fact that the two-nest ant process restricted to

is equal in distribution to a single-nest ants process is less straightforward,
but true, implying that the ants also find shortest paths within
G_{3}
.

Since, inside each of
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and
G_{3}
, our process finds shortest paths,
it is enough to discuss our result in the particular case when

is an
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
-triangle.
We start this discussion with very simple examples of triangle graphs and show on these simple examples that an interpretation in terms of an optimal transport network does not seem possible.

Example 1.7
.

Let

In this case, Theorem

applies and shows that, if

, almost surely as

This indeed corresponds to the intuition that, on

, one should build one road of “width”

and

(i.e. a road big enough to support a proportion

Example 1.8
.

Let

be the

In this case, Theorem

In this example, one would expect all the ants from

Similarly between

.
The ants process seems to suggest one also builds a road of width

and

We failed at interpreting its weight of

Example 1.9
.

Let
G
be the
(2,2,3)
-triangle.
In this case, Theorem
1.5
applies and shows that, almost surely as

\frac{W_{e}(n)}{n}\to\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }e=\{\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{F}\}\\

We now discuss our result by applying it to a general
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
-triangle graph:

If
\ell_{2}>\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, our main result implies that, asymptotically, almost all ants (i.e. a proportion converging to 1) starting from
\mathcal{N}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}
) go to
\mathcal{F}
following edges in
G_{1}
(resp.
G_{3}\cup G_{1}
), i.e. edges on the shortest path between
\mathcal{N}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}
) and
\mathcal{F}
. In other words, in this case, the ants find the shortest path between their nest and the source of food.

When
\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, an interesting phenomenon occurs:
indeed, in this case, there are two equally long paths to go from
\mathcal{N}_{2}
to
\mathcal{F}
,
but, asymptotically, almost all ants that start at
\mathcal{N}_{2}
go from
\mathcal{N}_{2}
to
\mathcal{F}
through
G_{3}\cup G_{1}
.
It shows that there is some interaction between the ants that start from
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and those starting from
\mathcal{N}_{2}
.

When
\ell_{3}\geq\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, then the shortest path from
\mathcal{N}_{1}

(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}
) to

is through
G_{1}
(resp.
G_{2}
), and, asymptotically, almost no ants goes through

.
In other words, in this case as well, the ants find the shortest paths between their nest and the source of food.

Finally, the case
\ell_{2}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}

.

1.3
Discussion

Open problems:

Our main result leaves many questions about the multi-nest process unanswered. In particular, our main result only holds in the case when the underlying graph is a triangle-series-parallel graph, but we conjecture that the multi-nest ant process converges on any underlying graph, although it is not clear what the limit should be.
Before looking at the fully general case, one may want to look at the
k
-cycle (or at the
k
-complete graph), with
k-1
of the nodes being nests, and the last node being the source of food, and with each edge then replaced by a series-parallel graph.
In this case, one would have to analyse a
(k-1)
-dimensional (resp.
\binom{k}{2}
-dimensional, for the
k
-complete graph) stochastic approximation.

With the aim of modelling physarum being able to find optimal transport networks,
one could modify the model as follows: instead of having
(k-1)
nests and one source of food, one could imagine having
k
distinguished nodes representing
k
cities in the graph.
Each ant would then pick at random one of the cities where it would start its walk (its nest), and another city where it would stop its walk (its source of food).
Could this multi-city ants process be a better model for finding optimal transport networks?

Other ant walks:

Several other probabilistic models of path formation are inspired by ants depositing pheromones behind them when exploring a graph by random walks.
For example, Le Goff and Raimond
14
consider the case of one self-avoiding vertex reinforced random walk and show that on the complete graph, and with super-linear reinforcement, the walk eventually localises on a finite number of vertices.
Similarly, Erhard, Franco, and Reis
6
consider a directed-edge-reinforced random walk and show that, on any finite graph, and on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(
d\geq 2
), the random walk almost surely localises on a cycle.
More recently, Erhard and Reis
7
generalise the model of
6
to the case of
N
walkers interacting with each other by reinforcing the same directed-edge-weights and give conditions under which the
N
walkers all localise on the same cycle.

Other path-formation models:
The ants process can be seen as a path-formation model: indeed, the set of edges whose weights grow linearly in
n
, which can be seen as the output of the process, is a subgraph of the original graph, and in some cases, a simple path.
Just like the phenomenon of ants finding shortest paths, it is believed that the development of the brain can be modelled using reinforcement learning.
A probabilistic model for this phenomenon, which can also be seen as a path-formation model was proposed by van der Hofstad, Holmes, Kuznetsov and Ruszel
18
, and later studied by
Hirsch, Holmes and Kleptsyn
8
;
9
, Holmes and Kleptsyn
11
, Couzinié and Hirsch
11
.

2
Strategy and preliminary results

The proof is done in two main steps:
We first prove that
(N_{1}(n),N_{2}(n),N_{3}(n))/n
converges almost surely when
n\to\infty
; this is done using stochastic approximation techniques.
We then use this convergence, together with Theorem 1.3 of

1.5

To prove convergence of

G

2.1
Random walks on weighted graphs: the method of conductances

(

The effective conductance of a series-parallel graph

•

C_{G}=w_{e}

Generalised Pólya urn processes

In our proofs, we will use the following results, which are
stated and proved by Kious, Mailler and Schapira

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a function from
[0,1]
to

.
The integer-valued process

is a

-urn if, almost surely,

\hat{X}_{n}={X_{n}}/{n}
(the results below are true for any normalization

(

13
)
.

Let
\left(X_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}

G
-urn process, with

-function. Then almost surely
\left(X_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}

converges towards a stable fixed point of

(Corollary 2.2 in
13
)
.

Let

, such that almost surely for all

2.3
The ODE method on stochastic approximations

In this section, we define stochastic approximations and state results from “the ODE method” that are useful in our proofs.
For more information on stochastic approximation, we refer the reader to the book of Duflo
5
, the book of Benaïm
2
, and the survey of Pemantle
16
.

Note that there are different definitions for stochastic approximation: we choose the one that serves best our purpose:

Definition 2.4
.

A stochastic approximation is a process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, adapted to some filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, with values in a convex compact subset
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
), and such that, for all
n\geq 0
,

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\frac{F(X_{n})+\xi_{n+1}+r_{n}}{n+1},\text{ for all }n\geq 0,

(2)

where the vector field
F:\mathcal{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is some Lipschitz function, the noise
\xi_{n+1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
-measurable and satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=0
, for all
n\geq 0
, and the remainder term
r_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable and satisfies almost
surely
\sum_{n}n^{-1}\|r(n)\|<\infty
.

Our next proposition states that
({\bf N}(n)=(N_{1}(n),N_{2}(n),N_{3}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is a stochastic approximation. We need the following definitions first:
for all
c=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})\in[0,\infty)^{3}
, we let

P_{1}(c)=1-P_{2}(c)=\alpha\frac{c_{1}}{c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}c_{3}}{c_{2}+c_{3}}}+(1-\alpha)\frac{\frac{c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}+c_{3}}}{\frac{c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}+c_{3}}+c_{2}}=\frac{\alpha c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}c_{3}+c_{2}c_{3}}.

(3)

We also set

P_{3}(c)=\alpha\frac{\frac{c_{2}c_{3}}{c_{2}+c_{3}}}{c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}c_{3}}{c_{2}+c_{3}}}+(1-\alpha)\frac{\frac{c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}+c_{3}}}{\frac{c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}+c_{3}}+c_{2}}=\frac{\alpha c_{2}c_{3}+(1-\alpha)c_{1}c_{3}}{c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}c_{3}+c_{2}c_{3}}.

(4)

Finally, for all
w\in[0,\infty)^{3}
, we set

p(w)=P\bigg(\frac{w_{1}}{\ell_{1}},\frac{w_{2}}{\ell_{2}},\frac{w_{3}}{\ell_{3}}\bigg).

(5)

The following lemma is useful to prove that the ants process is a stochastic approximation:

Lemma 2.5
.

Almost surely,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{N_{1}(n)+N_{3}(n)}{n+2}\geq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{N_{2}(n)+N_{3}(n)}{n+2}\geq 1-\alpha.

This implies that, almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\frac{N_{1}(n)+N_{3}(n)}{n+2}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{N_{2}(n)+N_{3}(n)}{n+2}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{2}.

(6)

Proof.

For all
n\geq 1
, we let
A(n)
be the event that the
n

.
If

occurs then either

or

N_{1}(n)+N_{3}(n)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{A(i)}.

Similarly,

N_{2}(n)+N_{3}(n)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(1-{\bf 1}_{A(i)}\big).

By definition,
({\bf 1}_{A(i)})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables, whose distribution is the Bernoulli distribution of parameter
\alpha
.
Thus, the conclusion follows by the strong law of large numbers, which implies that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{A(i)}\to\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(1-{\bf 1}_{A(i)}\big)\to 1-\alpha,

almost surely as

.
∎

We finally define

\mathcal{E}=\big\{(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})\in[0,1]^{3}\colon w_{2}=1-w_{1}\big\}.

3.2
Flow of the ODE

Lemma 3.3
.

For all

, we let

be the solution of
\dot{y}=F(y)
such that

For all

exists.

Proof.

The idea of this proof is to first use the Bendixson–Dulac theorem (see for example
(
10
, Section 4.1)
) to show that the ODE has no non-constant periodic solution (which we call orbit).
And then use the Poincaré-Bendixson theorem (see, for example
(
10
, Theorem 4.1.1)
) to conclude the proof.

The Bendixson-Dulac theorem states that, if there exists a continuously-differentiable function
g:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
w\in[0,1]^{2}
(except, possibly, in a set of measure 0),

\frac{\partial(gF_{1})}{\partial w_{1}}(w)+\frac{\partial(gF_{2})}{\partial w_{2}}(w)>0,

then the ODE
\dot{y}=F(y)
admits no orbit.

We let

g(w)=-\frac{w_{3}(\ell_{1}+w_{1}(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}))+\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})}{w_{1}w_{3}(1-w_{1})}.

for all
w\in(0,1)\times(0,1]
, and
g(w)=0
otherwise.
By (
23
) and (
25
),

\displaystyle-(gF_{1})(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{3}(\alpha-w_{1})}{w_{3}}+\ell_{2}-\ell_{1},\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle-(gF_{3})(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{2}(1-\alpha)-(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})w_{3}}{1-w_{1}}+\frac{\ell_{1}\alpha}{w_{1}}-\ell_{3}-\frac{\ell_{1}w_{3}}{w_{1}(1-w_{1})},

which imply

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(gF_{1})}{\partial w_{1}}(w)=\frac{\ell_{3}}{w_{3}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial(gF_{3})}{\partial w_{3}}(w)=\frac{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}}{1-w_{1}}+\frac{\ell_{1}}{w_{1}(1-w_{1})}.

Thus, for all
w\in(0,1)\times(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(gF_{1})}{\partial w_{1}}(w)+\frac{\partial(gF_{3})}{\partial w_{3}}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{3}}{w_{3}}+\frac{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}}{1-w_{1}}+\frac{\ell_{1}}{w_{1}(1-w_{1})}=-g(w)>0.

Therefore, the Bendixson-Dulac theorem (see, e.g.,
(
10
, Section 4.1)
) applies and gives that every solution of
\dot{y}=F(y)
starting in
(0,1)\times(0,1]
has no orbit.

The Poincaré-Bendixson theorem (see, e.g.,
(
10
, Theorem 4.1.1)
) states that, because
y_{w}
takes values in
[0,1]^{2}
, the limit set of
y_{w}
is either a zero of
F
or an orbit of
F
. Because there are no orbits, this concludes the proof.
∎

We thus have the following corollary, which states that the normalised process
(\hat{\bf N}(n))_{n\geq 0}
converges almost surely as
n
tends to infinity:

Corollary 3.4
.

Almost surely,
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\hat{\bf N}}(n)
exists and

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\hat{\bf N}}(n)\in\{(1,1-\alpha),(0,\alpha),(\alpha,0),(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\}.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
,

(\hat{N}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is a stochastic approximation with vector field
F
.
By Lemma
3.3
, any solution to the ODE
\dot{y}=F(y)
started on
[0,1]^{2}
converges.
Thus, Proposition
2.8
to
(\hat{N}(n))_{n\geq 0}
and gives that, almost surely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\bf N}(n)\in\{w:{F}(w)={0}\}=\{(1,1-\alpha),(0,\alpha),(\alpha,0),(0,0),(1,0),(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\},

by Lemma
3.1
.
Lemma
2.5
gives that, almost surely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\bf N}(n)\notin\{(0,0),(1,0)\}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Unstable zeros

From Corollary
3.4
, we have four possible values for
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\bf N}(n)
.
In this section, we show almost sure lower and upper bounds for
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\bf N}(n)
,
which then allow us to discard three out of the four possible limits and thus prove Theorem
1.5
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Almost surely,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{1}(n)\geq\alpha
.

Proof.

Recall that, by Lemma
2.14
, almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\frac{1-\Delta_{n}}{1+\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{1}(\hat{\bf N}(n))\leq\mathbb{P}\big(N_{1}(n+1)=N_{1}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\leq\frac{1+\Delta_{n}}{1-\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{1}(\hat{\bf N}(n)),

where
\Delta_{n}=\sup_{i}\{\ell_{i}K_{i}n^{(\alpha_{i}-1)\varepsilon_{i}}\}
,
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
are almost surely finite random variables,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
are three positive constants, and
\alpha_{i}=\ell_{i}/(\ell_{i}+1)
for all
1\leq i\leq 3
.
This implies, in particular, that, for all
\varepsilon>0
,
almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\big(N_{1}(n+1)=N_{1}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\geq\frac{1-\Delta_{n}}{1+\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{1}(\hat{\bf N}(n))\geq(1-\varepsilon)g(\hat{N}_{1}(n)),

(28)

where we have set, for all
w_{1}\in[0,1]
,

g(w_{1})=\inf_{0\leq w_{3}\leq 1}p_{1}(w_{1},1-w_{1},w_{3}).

Our aim is to apply Corollary
2.3
to the case
G=(1-\varepsilon)g
.
To do so, we need to lower bound
g
by a linear function of slope at least 1, in a sufficiently large neighbourhood of
0
.
Recall that, by definition (see (
3
) and (
5
)),

p_{1}(w)=\frac{\alpha\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}w_{3}}{\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}w_{3}+\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})w_{3}}.

This implies

\frac{\partial p_{1}}{\partial w_{3}}(w)=\frac{\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})((1-\alpha)\ell_{2}w_{1}-\alpha\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})))}{(\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}w_{3}+\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})w_{3})^{2}}.

Thus,
w_{3}\mapsto p_{1}(w)
is a non-decreasing function if and only if

w_{1}\geq\frac{\ell_{1}\alpha}{\ell_{2}(1-\alpha)+\ell_{1}\alpha}\eqqcolon c_{1}.

We thus get that

g(w_{1})=\begin{cases}p_{1}(w_{1},1-w_{1},0)=\alpha&\text{if }w_{1}\geq c_{1},\\
p_{1}(w_{1},1-w_{1},1)=\frac{w_{1}\left(\alpha\ell_{3}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}\right)}{\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}+\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
g
is continuous since
w_{3}\mapsto p_{1}(c_{1},1-c_{1},w_{3})
is constant and thus
p_{1}(c_{1},1-c_{1},1)=p_{1}(c_{1},1-c_{1},0)=\alpha
.
Also, because, by assumption,
\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{1}
, we get that
\alpha\geq c_{1}
.

We now show that, on
[0,\alpha]
, the function
g
only cross the diagonal at
w_{1}=0
and
w_{1}=\alpha
(only if
c_{1}=\alpha
). Indeed, for
w_{1}<c_{1}
,
g(w_{1})=w_{1}
if and only if
w_{1}=0
or

\frac{\alpha\ell_{3}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}}{\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}+\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})}=1.

(29)

Note that (
29
) is equivalent to
(\alpha-w_{1})\ell_{3}=\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}
, which admits no solution on
[0,\alpha)
because
\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}
, by assumption.
Thus, as claimed, on
[0,\alpha]
, the function
g
only crosses the diagonal at
w_{1}=0
and
w_{1}=\alpha
.
In fact,
g
is (strictly) above the diagonal on
(0,\alpha)
, since its derivative at
0
is
(\alpha\ell_{3}+\ell_{2})/\ell_{1}>1
(since
\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{1}
and
\alpha\ell_{3}>0
).
Therefore, for any
\eta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
small enough such that, for all
w_{1}\in(0,\alpha-\eta)
,
(1-\varepsilon)g(w_{1})>(1+\delta)w_{1}
.
By Corollary
2.3
, this implies

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{1}(n)\geq\alpha-\eta,

which concludes the proof, because this holds for all
\eta>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\ell_{3}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, then
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{3}(n)>0
almost surely.

Proof.

We fix
\delta>0
small enough so that

\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-\alpha-\delta)+(\alpha-\delta)(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}(\alpha+\delta)(1-\alpha+\delta)}>1.

This is possible because

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-\alpha-\delta)+(\alpha-\delta)(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}(\alpha+\delta)(1-\alpha+\delta)}=\frac{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}}>1,

by assumption.
We then fix
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that

\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-\alpha-\delta)+(\alpha-\delta)(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}(\alpha+\delta)(1-\alpha+\delta)}>\frac{1+\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}.

(30)

Recall that, by Lemma
2.14
, almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\frac{1-\Delta_{n}}{1+\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{1}(\hat{\bf N}(n))\leq\mathbb{P}\big(N_{1}(n+1)=N_{1}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\leq\frac{1+\Delta_{n}}{1-\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{1}(\hat{\bf N}(n)),

where
\Delta_{n}=\sup_{i}\{\ell_{i}K_{i}n^{(\alpha_{i}-1)\varepsilon_{i}}\}
,
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
are almost surely finite random variables,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
are three positive constants, and
\alpha_{i}=\ell_{i}/(\ell_{i}+1)
for all
1\leq i\leq 3
.
This implies, in particular, that,
almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\big(N_{3}(n+1)=N_{3}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\geq\frac{1-\Delta_{n}}{1+\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{3}(\hat{\bf N}(n))\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot p_{3}(\hat{\bf N}(n)).

(31)

By Lemmas
2.5
and
3.5
, almost surely,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{1}(n)\geq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\hat{N}_{1}(n)-\hat{N}_{3}(n))\leq\alpha.

Almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\hat{\bf N}(n)\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, where

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}=\{w\in\mathcal{E}\colon w_{1}\geq\alpha-\delta\text{ and }w_{1}-w_{3}\leq\alpha+\delta\}.

By (
31
), almost surely for all
n\geq n_{\delta}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(N_{3}(n+1)=N_{3}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\geq h(\hat{N}_{3}(n)),

where
h(w_{3}):=(1-\varepsilon)\inf\{p_{3}(w)\colon w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\}
.
By (
4
) and (
5
), we have, for all
w\in\mathcal{E}
(see (
7
)),

p_{3}(w)=w_{3}\cdot\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-w_{1})+(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}w_{1}}{\ell_{3}w_{1}(1-w_{1})+\ell_{2}w_{1}w_{3}+\ell_{1}w_{3}(1-w_{1})}.

Now note that, for all
w\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
,
\alpha-\delta\leq w_{1}\leq\alpha+\delta+w_{3}
, and thus

p_{3}(w)\geq w_{3}\cdot\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-\alpha-\delta-w_{3})+(\alpha-\delta)(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}(\alpha+\delta+w_{3})(1-\alpha+\delta)+(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})w_{3}}.

There exists
\kappa>0
small enough so that, for all
w\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
satisfying
w_{3}\leq\kappa
,

p_{3}(w)\geq(1-\varepsilon)w_{3}\cdot\frac{\alpha\ell_{1}(1-\alpha-\delta)+(\alpha-\delta)(1-\alpha)\ell_{2}}{\ell_{3}(\alpha+\delta)(1-\alpha+\delta)}.

By (
30
), we get that, for all
w\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
satisfying
w_{3}\leq\kappa
,

p_{3}(w)\geq(1+\varepsilon)w_{3}/(1-\varepsilon)
.
This implies that, for all
w_{3}\leq\kappa
,

h(w_{3})\geq(1+\varepsilon)w_{3}
.
Thus, by Corollary
2.3
,

\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}\hat{N}_{3}(n)\geq\kappa>0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

If
\ell_{2}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, then
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{1}(n)<1
almost surely.

Proof.

First note that, by definition of the model, almost surely for all
n\geq 0
,
\hat{N}_{1}(n)=1-\hat{N}_{2}(n)
. It is thus enough to prove that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{2}(n)>0
almost surely.
By Lemma
2.14
, almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\frac{1-\Delta_{n}}{1+\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{2}(\hat{\bf N}(n))\leq\mathbb{P}\big(N_{2}(n+1)=N_{2}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\leq\frac{1+\Delta_{n}}{1-\Delta_{n}}\cdot p_{2}(\hat{\bf N}(n)),

where
\Delta_{n}=\sup_{i}\{\ell_{i}K_{i}n^{(\alpha_{i}-1)\varepsilon_{i}}\}
,
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
are almost surely finite random variables,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
are three positive constants, and
\alpha_{i}=\ell_{i}/(\ell_{i}+1)
for all
1\leq i\leq 3
.
For all
\varepsilon>0
,
almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\big(N_{2}(n+1)=N_{2}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\geq(1-\varepsilon)p_{2}(\hat{\bf N}(n)).

(32)

We choose
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\delta<1-\alpha-\delta
,
\delta<1-\beta_{1}-\delta
, and

\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\delta)\ell_{3}+(1-\alpha-\delta)\ell_{1}}{\ell_{3}\delta+\ell_{1}\delta(1-\alpha+\delta)+\ell_{2}(1-\alpha+\delta)}>\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.

(33)

This is possible since

\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\delta)\ell_{3}+(1-\alpha-\delta)\ell_{1}}{\ell_{3}\delta+\ell_{1}\delta(1-\alpha+\delta)+\ell_{2}(1-\alpha+\delta)}\to\frac{\ell_{3}+\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}>1,

as
\delta\downarrow 0
,
and because
\alpha<1
and
\beta_{1}<1
(see Remark
3.2
).
Now, by Corollary
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
, almost surely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\hat{\bf N}}(n)\in\{(1,1-\alpha),(\alpha,0),(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\}.

Thus, almost surely for all
n
large enough,
\hat{\bf N}(n)\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
is the set of all points at distance at most
\delta
of
\{(1,1-\alpha),(\alpha,0),(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\}
(for the
L^{\infty}
-norm).
Thus, by (
32
), almost surely for all
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\big(N_{2}(n+1)=N_{2}(n)+1|\hat{\bf N}(n)\big)\geq g(\hat{N}_{2}(n)),

(34)

where

g(w_{2})=(1-\varepsilon)\inf_{w_{3}\colon(1-w_{2},w_{3})\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}}p_{2}(1-w_{2},w_{3})

if
w_{2}
is at distance at most
\delta
of
\{0,1-\alpha,1-\beta_{1}\}
, and
g(w_{2})=0
otherwise.
Now, assume that
w_{2}<\delta
and
(1-w_{2},w_{3})\in\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
.
Because
\delta<1-\alpha-\delta
and
\delta<1-\beta_{1}-\delta
, we get that

(1-w_{2},w_{3})
is at distance larger than
\delta
of both
(\alpha,0)
and
(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})
.
Thus, it must be at distance at most
\delta
of
(1,1-\alpha)
, which implies

w_{3}\in(1-\alpha-\delta,1-\alpha+\delta)
.
Recall that

p_{2}(1-w_{2},w_{3})=\frac{(1-\alpha)\ell_{3}(1-w_{2})+\ell_{1}w_{3}}{\ell_{3}w_{2}(1-w_{2})+\ell_{1}w_{2}w_{3}+\ell_{2}(1-w_{2})w_{3}}\cdot w_{2}.

Thus, because
w_{2}<\delta
and
1-\alpha-\delta<w_{3}<1-\alpha+\delta
, we get

p_{2}(1-w_{2},w_{3})>\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\delta)\ell_{3}+(1-\alpha-\delta)\ell_{1}}{\ell_{3}\delta+\ell_{1}\delta(1-\alpha+\delta)+\ell_{2}(1-\alpha+\delta)}\cdot w_{2}>\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot w_{2},

by (
33
).
Therefore, for all
w_{2}<\delta
,
g(w_{2})>(1+\varepsilon)w_{2}
.
Thus, by (
34
) and Corollary
2.3
,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\hat{N}_{2}(n)\geq\delta>0
, concludes the proof.
∎

3.4
Conclusion

We can now conclude the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We treat the three cases of Theorem
1.5
separately:

\bullet
We first assume that
\ell_{3}\geq\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, which implies
\ell_{2}\leq\ell_{3}-\ell_{1}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
(because
\ell_{1}\geq 1
, by assumption).
In this case,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\notin[0,1]^{2}
(except if
\ell_{3}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, but then
(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})=(\alpha,0)
).
Lemma
3.7
applies in this case and gives that, almost surely,
\hat{\bf N}(n)
does not converge to
(1,1-\alpha)
. Also, Lemma
3.5
implies that, almost surely,
\hat{\bf N}(n)
does not converge to
(0,\alpha)
.
Thus, by Corollary
3.4
, we get that, almost surely as
n
tends to infinity,

{\hat{\bf N}}(n)\to(\alpha,0),

as desired.

\bullet
We now assume that
\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, which implies
\ell_{3}\leq\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}+\ell_{1}
.
In this case again,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})\notin[0,1]^{2}
(except if
\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, but then
(\beta_{1},\beta_{3})=(1,1-\alpha)
).
By Lemma
3.6
, we get that, almost surely,
\hat{\bf N}(n)
does not converge to
(\alpha,0)
. Also, by Lemma
3.5
, almost surely,
\hat{\bf N}(n)
does not converge to
(0,\alpha)
.
Thus, by Corollary
3.4
, we get that, almost surely as
n
tends to infinity,

{\hat{\bf N}}(n)\to(1,1-\alpha),

as claimed.

\bullet
Finally, if
\ell_{2}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
and
\ell_{3}<\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, the same reasoning combining Lemmas
3.5
,
3.6
,
3.7
and Corollary
3.4
implies that, almost surely as
n
tends to infinity,

{\hat{\bf N}}(n)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}(\beta_{1},\beta_{3}).

Finally, to conclude about the edge-weights convergence, recall that, by Lemma
2.10
, the processes induced by the two-nest process on each of
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
are three single-nest ants processes.
Thus, convergence of the edge-weights follows from
(
12
, Theorem 1.3)
, which concludes the proof.
∎
