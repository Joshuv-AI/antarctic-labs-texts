Proprioceptive judgements made with the hand and the jaw: two distinct but functionally related body parts
Authors: Lucy S. Robertson, Annie A. Butler, Georgia Fisher, Simon C. Gandevia, Martin E. Héroux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We compared proprioceptive judgements made within a single frame of reference (e.g., grasp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} grasp; i.e., low-level) and those made between frames of reference (e.g., grasp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} vision; i.e., high-level) at the hand and jaw in 30 healthy participants. Participants judged the size of grasped or bitten objects of different sizes. Compared to high-level judgements, low-level judgements were more accurate (hand: mean difference in mean absolute error: 0.20 mm [95% CI 0.12 to 0.27]; jaw: (0.15 mm [0.09 to 0.20]) and precise (hand: mean difference in RMSE: 3.94 [2.73 to 5.15]; jaw: 1.67 [1.19 to 2.14]). A regression line was fit to a participant’s high-level proprioception responses to reflect the central transformation used to make these judgements. Comparing the regression lines for the hand and jaw conditions indicates the brain does not appear to apply a similar calibration to make high-level proprioceptive judgements with these distinct yet functionally related body parts.

Introduction

Proprioception, our awareness of the mechanical and spatial state of the body and its musculoskeletal parts, is essential for normal movement and to interact with our surroundings1,2. Proprioception is generated by inputs from muscle spindles, cutaneous and joint receptors, as well as central motor commands1,3. Proprioceptive ability can be measured as accuracy, which refers to the absolute error between the actual proprioceptive stimuli (e.g. position, movement, weight) and a participant’s judgements, and precision, which refers to the inter-trial variability of a participant’s judgements. Recently, we proposed and provided experimental evidence of two distinct types of proprioceptive judgements: low-level and high-level3,4.

Low-level judgements are those made within a single proprioceptive frame of reference that require a person to detect, discriminate or match proprioceptive signals or central representations generated from proprioceptive signals5,6. For example, a person may be asked to detect a passively imposed movement, discriminate between two objects of different thicknesses, or match joint angles between the right and left sides of their body. People tend to be accurate and precise when making these judgements, with little person-to-person variability in performance.

High-level proprioceptive judgements are those made across different frames of reference, where one reference frame is proprioceptive6–9. For example, a person may be asked to report the location of their hand hidden under a table by selecting from a set of 2D coordinates projected on the table10. Here, the brain must transform coordinates of the body’s configuration from a proprioceptive frame of reference to coordinates of the body’s location in a visual – external world – frame of reference. Compared to low-level proprioception tasks, accuracy and precision tend to be worse, sometimes dramatically so, when making these judgements, with greater person-to-person variability in performance3,4,10,11. These performance differences likely reflect the need for cross-modal transformations and additional neural computations with high-level proprioceptive judgements.

In a recent study, participants grasped hidden objects between their thumb and index finger. To report the perceived width of these objects, participants a) selected from a series of hidden objects of different widths that could be grasped (low-level proprioception) or b) selected from a series of visible lines of different lengths (high-level proprioception)4. A range of object widths were tested: 3 cm to 9 cm (5 trials each) in the first experiment and 2 cm to 10 cm (8 trials each) in the second experiment. This range of widths avoids the extremes, which allows a person’s judgements, both low-level and high-level, to be modelled with a simple linear equation12,13. For high-level judgements, this linear equation models the central calibration that governs cross-modal transformations. For each experimental condition, mean absolute error (cm) was used to measure the accuracy of participant responses, and the variance explained by the line fit to participant responses was used as an indirect measure of precision (R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}, range 0 to 1). The slope and intercept of this linear fit were also extracted as an estimate of the underlying central calibration. As expected, low-level proprioceptive judgements were accurate and precise. In contrast, high-level proprioceptive judgements were less accurate (low-level = 0.27 cm [0.20 to 0.34], mean [95% CI]; high-level = 1.40 cm [1.16 to 1.63]; mean difference = 1.07 cm [0.81 to 1.33]). Correspondingly, the range of mean absolute errors was larger for high-level proprioceptive judgements (low-level = 0.03 to 0.71 cm; high-level = 0.60 to 3.14 cm). Thus, on average, participants made systematic errors in high-level proprioceptive judgements. Interestingly, these errors and the outcomes associated with the linear fit (i.e. intercept, slope, R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}) were consistent across blocks of trials completed with the same hand, across blocks of trials completed with the left and right hands, and across blocks of trials completed with the same hand, one week apart. Thus, at least for the hands, humans appear to have unique and stable (but possibly inaccurate) central transformations that governs how they make perceptual judgements across proprioceptive and visual frames of reference.

Stable transformations make sense. They favour consistent perceptual judgements and stable central predictions for motor planning and execution14,15. For similar reasons, it would make sense if we used a similar central calibration for high-level proprioceptive judgements made with different but functionally linked body parts – this is the primary hypothesis we tackle in the present study. From early childhood, humans bring items of food to the mouth multiple times per day. Visual information is used to guide grasping movements of the hand, and the mouth opens sufficiently wide to either bite or ingest the item of food. Like our hands, the mouth and face are densely innervated and have disproportionately large central sensory representations16–18. Similar to the hands, the jaw makes accurate low-level proprioceptive judgements. For example, humans can discriminate between two bitten objects that differ by only 0.5 mm in width19,20. Given these similarities and the numerous daily interactions between our hands and our mouth, we focused on the hand and jaw to elucidate the possible link between proprioceptive judgements made with distinct yet functionally related body parts. Of particular interest were the similarities and differences in the central transformations that govern high-level proprioceptive judgements.

In the present study, we compared low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements that required participants to either grasp or bite a series of objects of different size. There were four experimental conditions: hand low-level proprioception (grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}grasp), jaw low-level proprioception (bite\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}bite), hand high-level proprioception (grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}vision) and jaw high-level proprioception (bite\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}vision). For each condition, simple linear equations (slope and intercept) were computed to estimate the central calibrations of participants and the model fit (R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}), while accuracy (mean absolute error, MAE) and precision (root-mean squared error, RMSE) of participants judgements were computed to estimate performance. For the hand and the jaw, we compared central calibrations and measures of performance between high-level and low-level proprioceptive conditions. Similarly, for low-level and high-level proprioception conditions, we compared these outcomes between the hand and jaw. Finally, we determined the linear relationship between accuracy and precision within each condition (low-level, high-level), and between outcome measures (i.e. intercept, slope, model fit, accuracy and precision) of the hand and jaw.

Methods

Participants

We recruited thirty participants to take part in the study (17 females, three left handed, mean age 33.6 years (SD 8.0); (range 21-52)). Participants were included in the study if they were 18 to 65 years of age and had no major musculoskeletal or neurological conditions that impacted sensory or motor function of their upper limb and hand or mouth and jaw. Participants were informed they would grasp and bite objects and make judgements about their size, but they were not informed of the experimental hypotheses. All participants provided written informed consent. The experimental procedures were approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HC190729), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), except for registration in a database.

Experimental conditions

The study consisted of four experimental conditions: (1) low-level hand proprioception (grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}grasp), (2) high-level hand proprioception (grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}vision), (3) low-level jaw proprioception (bite\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}bite) and (4) high-level jaw proprioception (bite\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}vision). Each trial had a stimulus object (grasp or bite) and either a hidden response object (grasp or bite) or a visible response line (vision) that the participant could adjust to match the width of the stimulus object. Condition order was randomised between body parts (hand, jaw) and then proprioceptive tests (low-level, high-level).

There was no time limit to complete the conditions. However, the participant was encouraged to not labour over their judgements. Judgements were self paced and took \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 5 s for each high-level proprioceptive trial and 8-15 s for each low-level proprioceptive trial. Participant response time was not was not measured quantitatively, but was based on experimenter observation. The additional time for low-level trials reflects the time required for the participant to adjust the width of the response object and move between the stimulus and response objects (see below for details). In general, participants were consistent in the time it took them to complete trials for a given experimental condition.

At the hand, the grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}grasp condition avoided the possible influence of (a) inter-hemispheric transfer of information required in bilateral matching proprioceptive tasks21, and (b) the side-to-side bias often present in bilateral proprioceptive judgements22. However, both low-level proprioceptive conditions (grasp\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}grasp and bite\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}bite) required the participant to briefly remember perceived widths as they moved between the stimulus and response object. To limit the impact of memory, we designed our experimental set-up in a way that, once familiar with the task, a participant could switch between objects in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 1 s23.

Hand proprioception

Experimental set-up

The participant sat at a height-adjustable table with their right forearm resting on a support surface in front of them (Fig. 1A). The participant’s left hand rested on a keypad with up/down arrows. The participant’s right hand was positioned in front of the stimulus and response objects (Fig. 1B). Each object comprised two flat rectangular metal plates (width: 3 mm, height: 15 mm, depth: 30 mm), with the left plate mounted to a linear actuator (stroke length: 140 mm, gear ratio: 35:1, displacement velocity: 32 mm/s; L16-P, Actuonix). The linear actuator was attached such that the plates were separated horizontally and the distance between the outer aspect of the two plates represented the width of the stimulus and response objects. The linear actuators were connected to control boards (Actuonix) controlled by an Arduino Uno microcontroller (www.arduino.cc). This microcontroller interfaced via serial communication with data acquisition software written in the Python programming language (v3.12). The response object was located 60 mm above the stimulus object. A thin, rigid horizontal rectangle was located between the two objects and extended 10 mm past the end of the plates. An opaque drape extended from the participant’s shoulders to a shoulder-height shelf located in front of the participant to hide from the view the participant’s upper limbs and the stimulus and response objects. A monitor (height: 134 mm, width: 295 mm) was located 50 cm in front of the participant at eye level and displayed instructions and, in the case of high-level proprioceptive judgements, the response line.

Experimental protocol

Prior to the start of the low-level and high-level proprioception conditions, the participant watched an instructional video that described the experimental procedures. Next, the participant performed a minimum of two practice trials to confirm they understood the task.

In both the low-level and high-level proprioception conditions, five stimulus widths (30 mm, 40 mm, 50 mm, 60 mm, 70 mm) were each presented five times, for a total of 25 trials. This range of widths, which spanned 20-30% to 70-80% of the maximal aperture between the thumb and index finger (when the pads of these digits are in full contact with the grasped object), avoided the non-linearity in perceptual judgements that tends to occur when very small or very large widths are included4. Stimulus widths were presented in a pseudo-randomised order to ensure the same stimulus width was not presented twice in a row. Each experimental condition (i.e. low-level proprioception, high-level proprioception) took on average of 15 min to complete.

The participant was required to perform a light pinch with their right thumb and index finger before each trial and, for the low-level proprioception condition, every time they moved their hand between the stimulus and response objects. The pinch at the start of each trial controlled for the possible influence of muscle thixotropy on perceptual judgements by standardising the mechanical state of muscle spindles located in the flexor muscles of the thumb and index finger1,24. The pinch performed when moving between the stimulus and response objects ensured the participant did not use their unaltered grasp as simple measurement calipers25.Fig. 1Experimental set-up. Low-level proprioception

As described above, each trial started with a pinch of the right thumb and index finger. Next, the participant grasped the stimulus object for 2-3 s, released it, and moved their hand upwards towards the response object. The rigid horizontal separator located between the stimulus and response objects (Fig. 1A) ensured the participant retracted their hand slightly when moving between objects. This small backwards movement ensured each grasp was initiated with a forward movement of the arm and hand. It also served as a cue for the participant to perform a pinch between their thumb and index finger every time they moved between objects. After the participant grasped the response object, they used their left hand on the keypad to adjust the width of the response object to match the width of the stimulus object; each press of the keypad increased or decreased the width of the response object by 5 mm. The participant was able to move their hand back and forth between the stimulus and response objects and, if desired, re-adjust the width of the response object. However, a light pinch was always required when the hand moved between objects. Once satisfied that the width of the response object was the same as the width of the stimulus object, the participant verbally confirmed their choice, released the object, and moved their hand back to the support surface ready for the next trial. Between each trial, the linear actuators performed a series of 2-3 back and forth movements of random amplitude; these movements ensured the participant could not use the sound of the actuators to inform future judgements. The stimulus object then moved to the test width for the next trial and the response object moved to a randomly selected width within the range of test widths (30-70 mm, resolution: 5 mm). The starting width of the response object was never the same as the width of the stimulus object. Between trials, as the actuators moved, instructions about the required judgement were presented on the monitor. The participant started the next trial when the instructions were removed from the monitor.

High-level proprioception

Each trial started with the participant performing a pinch between right thumb and index finger. Next, the participant moved their right hand forward and grasped the stimulus object with their right thumb and index finger. At the same time, a horizontal line was presented on the monitor. The initial length of the line was randomly set within the range of test widths (30-70 mm; resolution: 5 mm). The participant then used their left hand on the keypad to adjust the length of the horizontal line to match the width of the stimulus object; each press of the keypad increased or decreased the length of the response line by 5 mm. When the participant was satisfied that the length of the visible line was the same as the width of the stimulus object, the participant verbally confirmed their choice and removed their hand from the stimulus object, ready for the next trial. Between each trial, the linear actuator attached to the stimulus object performed a series of 2-3 back and forth movements of random amplitude before finishing at the test width for the next trial. As the actuators moved between trials, the line from the previous trial was removed and instructions about the required judgement were presented on the monitor. The participant started the next trial when the instructions were removed from the monitor.

Jaw proprioception

Experimental set-up

The participant sat at the same height-adjustable table. The participant’s left hand rested on a keypad with up/down arrows. The stimulus and response objects were mounted on a shelf attached to the table (Fig. 1D). The stimulus object was located immediately in front of the participant at mouth height while the response object was located at the same height but \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$45^{\circ }$$\end{document} to the right. Stimulus and response objects were made with the same metal plates as those used to test hand proprioception, with the lower plate mounted to a linear actuator (stroke length: 50 mm, gear ratio: 63:1, displacement velocity: 20 mm/s; L16-P, Actuonix). The linear actuator was attached such that the plates were separated vertically and the distance between the outer aspect of the two plates represented the height of the stimulus and response objects. The participant wore a pair of glasses with no lenses, but fitted with an opaque surface that obscured vision below eye-level (Fig. 1D). The same monitor used to test hand proprioception was used.

Experimental protocol The experimental protocol was similar to the one for hand proprioception, with slight differences. First, the stimulus widths were smaller (16 mm, 21 mm, 26 mm, 31 mm, 36 mm). This range of widths represents 20-30% to 70-80% of maximal jaw aperture (vertical distance between the upper and lower front teeth). To keep terminology consistent between hand and jaw experiments and results, we always use the term width to refer to the size of the stimulus and response objects, despite the vertical orientation of the stimulus and response objects when testing jaw proprioception. Second, rather than perform a light pinch between trials and when moving between stimulus and response objects, the participant performed a light bite. Third, the participant was instructed to bite the object with the upper and lower front teeth, without the lips or tongue touching the metal plates. Finally, given the smaller range of widths used for the jaw, each press of the keypad increased or decreased the width of the response object or the response line by 2.5 mm, and the starting position of the response object or line was randomly set within the range of test widths (i.e. 16-36 mm) with a resolution of 2.5 mm. The experimenter was seated next to the participant throughout the experiment and ensured the lips or tongue did not come into contact with the stimulus or response object. Verbal reminders to avoid contact with the lips and tongue were also provided at regular intervals (every 4-5 trials) throughout this part of the experiment.

Low-level proprioception

Low-level proprioception of the jaw was tested in a similar way as low-level proprioception of the hand. However, rather than move the hand up and down between the stimulus and response objects, the participant brought their head slightly back and rotated their body \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$45^\circ$$\end{document} by turning their swivel chair to be in line with either the stimulus or response object (Fig. 1C).

High-level proprioception

High-level proprioception of the jaw was tested in a similar way as high-level proprioception of the hand. However, for the jaw, the response line was oriented vertically on the monitor.

As expected, participant responses scaled linearly across the range of tested widths. Thus, for each experimental condition for each participant, a linear regression line was fit and the intercept and slope, R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} (model fit) of each line were retained for analysis.

A primary aim of our study was to compare the central calibrations that govern proprioceptive judgements that involve the hand to those that involve the jaw. However, considering intercept and slope values independent of one another does not fully capture a participant’s performance. For example, a participant may have the same intercept value for high-level proprioceptive judgements made with their jaw and hand, but with very different slope values. A participant’s performance and the underlying central calibration that governs this performance is best represented by a combination of the slope and intercept (i.e. the regression line). Therefore, to directly compare the central calibrations used to make proprioceptive judgements at the hand and jaw, we compared their regression lines. However, because the range of stimulus widths differed between the hand and the jaw, it was not possible to directly compare regression lines computed on data in original units (i.e. millimetres). Thus, stimulus and response widths were normalised to the range of test widths for each condition:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} normalised~width = \frac{width - minimum~stimulus~width}{maximum~stimulus~width - minimum~ stimulus~width}~\times ~100 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The following formula was used to normalise data from the hand conditions:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} normalised~width_{ hand} = \frac{width - 30}{70 - 30}~\times ~100 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The following formula was used to normalise data from the jaw conditions:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} normalised~width_{ jaw} = \frac{width - 16}{36 - 16}~\times ~100 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Once normalised, the range of test widths spanned 0 to 100 for both the hand and the jaw. Regression lines were fitted to these normalised data, and the difference between hand and jaw regression lines was computed for both low-level and high-level proprioception. If the regression lines for the hand and the jaw were identical, the resultant line would be a flat horizontal line starting at (0, 0) (x,y) and ending at (0, 100). If the regression lines for the hand and the jaw had the same slope but different intercepts, the resultant would be a flat horizontal line, offset vertically by the difference in intercepts between the two regression lines. If the regression lines for the hand and the jaw had the same intercept but different slopes, the resultant would be a line starting at (0, 0) with a slope equal to the difference in slope between the two regression lines. Finally, if the regression lines for the hand and the jaw had different intercepts and slopes, the resultant would be a line starting at the difference in intercept between the two regressions lines, with a slope equal to the difference in slope between the two regression lines.

Statistical analysis

An estimation-based approach was used for the statistical analyses26–29. For each condition, means and 95% confidence intervals of the intercept, slope, R2, accuracy (MAE), and precision (RMSE) were calculated across participants. Also, mean [95% CI] differences were computed for pre-planned contrasts. Overall proprioceptive performance

Discussion

The present study compared low-level proprioceptive judgements to high-level proprioceptive judgements made with the hand and the jaw. For both body parts, low-level proprioceptive judgements were more accurate compared to high-level proprioceptive judgements. However, there was slightly greater variability in judgements made with the jaw in both the low-level and high-level tasks. This study builds on our previous findings4, and reveals that across different body parts performance in low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements differ. Importantly, the central calibrations used to transform coordinates from a proprioceptive frame of reference to a visual one are not identical between the hand and the jaw – two distinct, but functionally linked body parts.

Low-level versus high-level proprioceptive judgements

The present study replicated our previous finding at the hand4, showing that low-level proprioceptive judgements (grasp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} grasp) are more accurate than high-level judgements (grasp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} vision). Importantly, we extend this work by demonstrating, for the first time, that the same pattern is observed at the jaw: low-level judgements (bite \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} bite) are more accurate than high-level judgements (bite \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} vision). The results reveal that low-level proprioceptive judgements are more precise at the hand compared to the jaw (i.e. lower RMSE). This points to differences in the cortical processes that underpin low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements. As Jastrow eloquently explained over a century ago "When the same sense acts as the receiving and expressing sense, the error is small (and the process is easy)"7. Indeed, in our low-level tasks, the brain had to compare like with like. In contrast, high-level proprioceptive judgements are more complex4,7,10,11,30. They require the brain to integrate and convert sensory information from two sensory systems (e.g. visual and proprioceptive)7,8,10,11,30). Here, Jastrow explains that "When the expressing sense differs from the receiving sense, heterogeneous sensations must be compared ... a difficult operation with a larger error." In line with this view and results from previous studies, participants made greater errors when judgements required the brain to transform spatial coordinates across reference frames31–34. Also in line with previous results, high-level proprioceptive judgements showed greater person-to-person variability4. In terms of methodology, a key difference between the present study and our recent study4 is that here, our high-level proprioception task required the participant to adjust the length of the visually presented line until they perceived it matched the width of the grasped object. Previously participants selected from a series of visually presented lines of different length to report the perceived width of the grasped object4,9,35,36. Nevertheless, despite this difference, results for the hand high-level proprioception condition of the present study mirror those of our recent study4. This is reassuring, and indicates that the tasks of adjusting the length of a single line – with no other visual reference present – is largely similar to selecting a line of given length when other lines of different length are present and can be compared. As noted previously4, the grasp-based low-level proprioceptive task used in the present study avoided the possible influence of inter-hemispheric transfer of information required in bilateral matching proprioceptive tasks21 and the side-to-side bias that may be present in bilateral proprioceptive tasks22. However, it does have a slight memory component (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 1-2 s), when participants transfer between the stimulus and response objects. In contrast, with high-level proprioceptive judgements, participants are simultaneously grasping (or biting) the test object when they make their judgement. This slight difference in the experimental protocol is unlikely to have a meaningful effect on our results and their interpretation.

The differences observed between low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements in the present study are, for the most part, consistent with those we recently reported for grasp-based judgements4. In that earlier study, the differences between low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements were: intercept = 17.8 mm, slope = 0.167, R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} = 0.06, and MAE = 11.3 mm. The corresponding values for the grasp-based judgements in the current study were: intercept = 13.1 mm, slope = 0.40, R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} = 0.06, and MAE = 10.0 mm. In our view, these differences are meaningful. For instance, given that the tested widths ranged from 30 to 70 mm, a 10 mm difference in MAE between grasp-based low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements represents a substantial disparity in perceptual accuracy.

For both the hand and the jaw, there was a strong negative relationship between accuracy (i.e. mean absolute error) and precision (i.e. response variability) for low-level proprioceptive judgements. That is, people who were less accurate tended to also be more variable in their judgements. In contrast, the strength of the the association between accuracy and precision decreased by more than half for high-level proprioceptive judgements made with the hand, and there was little to no correlation for such judgements made with the jaw. These differences between low-level and high-level proprioception are in line with results from our recent study4. As mentioned in the Introduction, people have individual calibrations for the central transformations that govern high-level proprioceptive judgements4,9. Although these personalised calibrations may yield inaccurate judgements, if these calibrations are stable, which our recent study indicates they are, they will result in precise judgements. For example, in the present study, a participant consistently perceived the 6 cm grasped object to be 12 cm and the 7 cm object to be 14 cm wide. These are large errors. Does this imply their proprioception is impaired? Likely not. This participant was consistent in their judgements, reflected by an RMSE of 7.9 (group range: 3.9 to 17.3). More broadly, it appears that consistent but potentially inaccurate calibrations are used centrally to transform sensory information from one frame of reference to another, in this case, between a proprioception-based model of the body and a vision-based internal model of the external world9.

Proprioceptive judgements of the jaw versus the hand

Overall, proprioceptive judgements made with the hand – both low-level and high-level – were more precise than those made with the jaw. Why might this be? Vision aside, the hand is the principal tool through which humans perceive objects in the environment37. Everyday, we grasp dozens of objects with our hands before we bring them to our mouth. Rarely do we bite onto an object without first holding it in our hands. Therefore, we usually rely on proprioceptive feedback from our hands about the size, weight and shape of the object to determine how much (or how little) we should open our mouth for it to fit between our teeth.

Depending on ones culture and the type of food eaten and the type of utensils used, the dominant or right hand may preferentially be used to bring food to the mouth, which could result in a stronger pairing in judgements made with one of the hands and the jaw. In the present study, the right hand was tested, and 27 of 30 participants were right handed. Although the sample of left handers was too small for a formal dominant/non-dominant analysis, results from our recent study found no difference in high-level proprioceptive grasp-based outcomes (slope, intercept, R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}, MAE) between the dominant and non-dominant hands4. Nevertheless, the question of how repeated linked exposure (i.e. hand and jaw) influence central transformations warrants further investigation.

Anatomical differences between the hand and the jaw may also have contributed to the greater precision seen with the hand when making both low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements. When we grasp or bite an object in the absence of vision, the brain estimates the size of the object based on signals from cutaneous receptors, muscle spindles, joint receptors and, for the jaw, periodontal mechanoreceptors38–40. However, a key difference is that for the hand, contact with the object is made with the pad of the thumb and index finger, while for the jaw, contact is made with the most distal part of the teeth. The pads of our digits are highly innervated and cutaneous receptors signal contact with the object, and its surface properties (e.g. temperature, compliance, texture), information that is critical to sensing the configuration of our digits17,38,41–44. Although periodontal mechanoreceptors are highly sensitive, they likely do not provide the same richness of sensory signals45,46. Moreover, no part of the exposed enamel – the part of the tooth that contacts the object – is innervated19,40.

Muscle spindles are also important in perceiving the aperture of the hand and the jaw1,47,48. However, here too, there are differences that could have contributed to the greater precision at the hand. For example, the abductor pollicis brevis muscle that abducts the thumb contains 29 spindles per gram of muscle, whereas the massester muscle that elevates the jaw contains only 11 spindles per gram47. Like fingers, the lips are densely innervated. Thus, participants may have been more precise if we had asked them to contact the objects with their teeth and lips. Indeed, previous research revealed that proprioceptive accuracy in a width discrimination task was more accurate at the lips, compared to the jaw49. However, we opted to not allow contact with the lips to focus on true jaw aperture and the spacing between the front teeth, as this is a key metric when inserting food into the mouth. At present, it is not known whether low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements of jaw aperture are more accurate and precise when they involve contacting an object with only the teeth, only the lips, or a combination of both the lips and teeth. Similarly, we did not allow participants to contact the objects with their tongue; whether this would impact jaw-based proprioceptive judgements remains unclear. Although answering these questions was beyond the scope of the present study, they are worthy of future investigation.

Despite these differences at the hand and the jaw, there was a moderate positive relationship between jaw and hand intercept values and jaw and hand slope values for both low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements, as well as a moderate positive relationship between jaw and hand accuracy values for low-level proprioceptive judgements. This coupling between the hand and the jaw may in part reflect, at least for low-level proprioception, a genetic predisposition. Low-level proprioceptive abilities are, to a large extent, genetically determined, with heritability estimates as high as 77% for a low-level position sense matching task at the elbow50. Existence of a genetic contribution to proprioceptive abilities may also explain some of the between-participant variability we observed. However, determining whether high-level proprioceptive abilities are heritable will require appropriately designed twin or family studies that can dissociate genetic and environmental influences, an approach that was beyond the scope of the present study.

Our results are in contrast to previous studies that reported no relationship between proprioceptive accuracy across different body parts (i.e. the ankle and shoulder)51,52. While this may reflect a difference in the proprioceptive tests used, it may also reflect that, in our study, we assessed functionally related body parts that often come into contact with the same objects53. This functional as well as physical proximity may in part explain these results.

Interestingly, there was no relationship between the accuracy of proprioceptive judgements made with the hand and jaw in our high-level task. That is, individuals that were accurate in their proprioceptive judgements in the grasp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} vision task were not necessarily accurate in the bite \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} vision task and vice versa. In line with this finding, there was, on average, a systematic difference in the regression lines computed to estimate the central calibrations that governed the high-level proprioceptive judgements of the hand and jaw (Fig. 5). Also, there was considerable person-to-person variability in this comparison of hand and jaw regressions lines, reflecting individual differences in the central calibrations of the hand and jaw. These differences were not fully apparent when the related intercepts and slopes were considered on their own. Overall, this provides evidence that, for high-level proprioceptive judgements like those used in the present study, the central calibrations and associated central transformations are not identical within a given individual, even for functionally related body parts. Why might these central transformations be different within and between individuals? This fundamental question, unfortunately, cannot be answered by the results of the present study.

This study is the first to assess jaw and hand proprioception within the same experiment. While grasp-based proprioception is relatively straightforward to test, assessing jaw-based proprioception presented additional challenges. Contact with the lips and tongue needed to be carefully controlled for, and our usual approach to high-level proprioceptive testing had to be modified. Previously, we had participants grasp an object and verbally indicate its perceived width by selecting from visually presented lines of varying lengths. Because verbal responses are less practical during jaw-based testing, we adapted the setup to have participants adjust the line length using a keypad. Although jaw-based proprioceptive testing is less common, all participants understood the task after clear instructions and brief a practice, completing the jaw-based judgements in a similar amount of time as the hand-based ones.

Conclusion

Proprioception is integral for normal movement and to interact with the world around us. The present study provides additional evidence of the fundamental difference that exists between low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements. Moreover, it demonstrates that, across different but functionally related body parts, low-level and high-level proprioceptive judgements are related but also distinct. For cross-modal perceptual judgements, it appears the brain does not apply the same calibration to transform coordinates from a proprioceptive frame of reference to a visual frame of reference across different body parts.
