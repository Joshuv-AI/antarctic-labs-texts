Secondary metabolites of Minutisphaera thailandensis and Hongkongmyces kokensis revealed by LC–QTOF–MS and their antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities
Authors: Sarunpron Khruengsai, Teerapong Sripahco, Prasat Kittakoop, Patcharee Pripdeevech
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The mycelial extracts of two novel fungal species, Minutisphaera thailandensis and Hongkongmyces kokensis, were investigated for their antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and secondary metabolite profiles to assess their potential as sources of bioactive natural products. LC–QTOF–MS profiling revealed that the mycelial extracts contained a greater diversity of metabolites compared to the culture supernatants. M. thailandensis demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against Bacillus subtilis (MIC = 62.25 µg/mL; inhibition zone = 20.12 ± 0.21 mm), Staphylococcus epidermidis (125.00 µg/mL; 19.32 ± 0.15 mm), and Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus (125.00 µg/mL; 19.08 ± 0.12 mm). H. kokensis was active against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (16.45 ± 0.28 mm) and Escherichia coli (16.87 ± 0.36 mm). Both fungi also exhibited antifungal activity against Candida albicans (zones = 13.38–17.29 mm). Fifteen metabolites were identified in the mycelial extract of M. thailandensis while 16 compounds were found in those obtained from H. kokensis. These included alkaloids (norharman, indole-3-carboxylic acid), fatty acid amides (oleamide, stearamide), sphingolipids (phytosphingosine, 3-ketosphinganine), polyketides (chaetochromin), and unsaturated fatty acids (linoleic acid), among others. Both fungi shared 4-hydroxycoumarin, carapin-8(9)-ene, and phytosphingosine, while chaetochromin and 13-epi-12-oxo-phytodienoic acid were unique to H. kokensis. Antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities may be attributed to the presence of bioactive compounds including phytosphingosine, 4-hydroxycoumarin, chaetochromin, linoleic acid, and oleamide, all of which possess reported pharmacological effects. M. thailandensis exhibited stronger cytotoxicity against SW480 (IC50 = 25.11 µg/mL) and A549 (IC50 = 32.68 µg/mL) cell lines than H. kokensis (IC50 = 58.88–63.41 µg/mL), underscoring its therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25050-z.

Introduction

Fungi are extraordinary biosynthetic chemists of nature, capable of producing an extensive repertoire of structurally diverse secondary metabolites with wide-ranging biological activities1. These natural products have played critical roles in shaping modern medicine, exemplified by the discovery of penicillin, cephalosporins, statins, and immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine2,3. Their relevance extends beyond pharmaceuticals to agriculture, food preservation, and cosmetics. Notably, several fungal-derived metabolites have demonstrated cytotoxic or anticancer potential, including podophyllotoxin from Phialocephala fortinii, camptothecin from Entrophospora infrequens, phomoxanthone A and B from Phomopsis sp., and ergoflavin from Claviceps purpurea4,5. Therefore, fungal-derived compounds continue to represent a promising source of new chemotherapeutic scaffolds. Despite these successes, a vast majority of fungal biodiversity, particularly from aquatic, endophytic, and lignicolous niches, remains underexplored from a chemical standpoint4. This knowledge gap represents a valuable opportunity to uncover novel scaffolds that may address the urgent global challenges of antibiotic resistance, emerging pathogens, and cancer drug resistance.

In the pursuit of structurally novel and bioactive fungal metabolites, recent taxonomic and ecological studies have drawn attention to fungi inhabiting wood-decaying environments. These niches impose intense microbial competition and environmental stress, promoting the evolution of unique chemical defense mechanisms4. Among these ecologically specialized fungi are two recently described species, Minutisphaera thailandensis and Hongkongmyces kokensis, isolated from submerged decaying wood in riparian habitats of northern Thailand6. Belonging to the class Sordariomycetes, both species are lignicolous ascomycetes, a group historically known to produce a wide array of antimicrobial and cytotoxic metabolites such as cytochalasins, sordarins, and depsipeptides7,8. However, no studies have yet reported the cytotoxic potential or secondary-metabolite composition of M. thailandensis or H. kokensis. These two fungal species were selected for investigation because they are newly reported taxa from biodiverse habitats in Thailand, where competition among microorganisms is known to promote unique chemical defences. Their taxonomic novelty, ecological adaptation to lignicolous substrates, and phylogenetic proximity to metabolite-rich genera suggest a high potential for producing structurally distinct secondary metabolites. Exploring these species therefore offers an opportunity to uncover new antimicrobial and cytotoxic compounds while expanding knowledge of chemical diversity within Sordariomycetes.

Given their unique phylogenetic placement, ecological origin, and lack of prior investigation, these fungi offer compelling candidates for natural product discovery. Advances in hyphenated analytical techniques have revolutionized the ability to profile fungal metabolites. Liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC–QTOF–MS) enables untargeted, high-resolution profiling of complex extracts, allowing for rapid dereplication and putative identification of known and novel compounds9,10. This approach, when integrated with bioassay-guided screening, supports the identification of promising leads for pharmaceutical or biotechnological development. Importantly, combining metabolomics with functional assays allows for correlation of specific metabolite classes with observed biological activities offering mechanistic insights and guiding future isolation strategies11,12.

In this study, we present the first comprehensive metabolomic and bioactivity analysis of M. thailandensis and H. kokensis. Using LC–QTOF–MS, we identified a diverse array of secondary metabolites from mycelial extracts. These include sphingolipids (e.g., phytosphingosine, 3-ketosphinganine), alkaloids (e.g., norharman), polyketides (e.g., chaetochromin), and fatty acid derivatives (e.g., linoleic acid, oleamide), several of which have known antimicrobial and cytotoxic properties. We further evaluated the biological potential of these extracts through antimicrobial assays against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and the pathogenic yeast Candida albicans, as well as cytotoxicity assays against human colorectal (SW480), lung (A549) carcinoma cell lines, together with normal fibroblast (3T3-L1) cells.

Our findings demonstrate that M. thailandensis exhibits notably higher cytotoxic activity than H. kokensis, correlating with the presence of bioactive metabolites such as phytosphingosine and linoleic acid. Both fungal extracts showed antibacterial and antifungal effects, supporting the ecological hypothesis that competitive environments select for metabolically rich fungal taxa. This integrative study highlights the importance of investigating novel fungal lineages from biodiverse tropical habitats and contributes to the growing recognition of local fungi as a reservoir of pharmaceutically relevant secondary metabolites.

Materials and methods

Fungal specimen and culture preparation

The fungal strains M. thailandensis (MFLUCC 20–0072) and H. kokensis (MFLUCC 21–0081) were provided by the Center of Excellence in Fungal Research, Mae Fah Luang University, Chiang Rai, Thailand. Both isolates were recovered from decaying wood. These habitats are known to foster intense microbial competition, potentially stimulating the production of bioactive secondary metabolites. Taxonomic identification was performed based on morphological and molecular analyses. Internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and large subunit (LSU) rDNA sequences were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers MZ493355 (ITS) and MZ493343 (LSU) for M. thailandensis, and MZ538507 (ITS) and MZ538541 (LSU) for H. kokensis. Detailed descriptions of their morphology and phylogeny are available in the previous literature6.

For primary culturing, fungal strains were grown on yeast malt glucose (YMG) agar composed of 10 g/ L malt extract, 4 g/ L yeast extract, 4 g/L D-glucose, and 20 g/L agar in deionized water. The medium pH was adjusted to 6.3 before autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min. This nutrient-rich medium supports optimal mycelial development in filamentous fungi and was used following the modified protocol of Pripdeevech et al.13 Cultures were incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 7 days to achieve full colony growth. Mycelial plugs (8 mm diameter), excised using a sterile cork borer, were aseptically transferred to 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 250 mL of sterilized liquid Zucker–Malz (ZM) medium.

The ZM broth was formulated to induce secondary metabolite biosynthesis by mimicking carbon- and nitrogen-limited conditions. It contained 5 g/L molasses, 5 g/L oatmeal, 4 g/L saccharose, 4 g/L mannitol, 1.5 g/L D-glucose, 1.5 g/L CaCO3, 0.5 g/L enzymatic digest of casein, and 0.5 g/L (NH4)2SO4. The final volume was brought up with deionized water and adjusted to pH 7.2 before sterilization. Cultures were incubated on a rotary shaker at 140 rpm and 23 °C in static-dark conditions for 14 days to maximize metabolite production. Each fungal strain was cultured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. After incubation, the culture broth was filtered using sterile gauze followed by Whatman No. 1 filter paper to separate the mycelial biomass from the supernatant. The supernatant was extracted three times with ethyl acetate in a 1:2 (v/v) ratio, and the organic phases were pooled and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 to eliminate residual water. The mycelial biomass was separately extracted to isolate intracellular metabolites. It was immersed in 500 mL of analytical-grade acetone and subjected to ultrasonication at 40 °C for 30 min using a bath sonicator (frequency: 40 kHz; power: 120 W) following the optimized protocol described by Pripdeevech et al.13 Acetone was chosen for its intermediate polarity, high cell-wall permeability, and compatibility with LC–QTOF–MS analysis, which together enable efficient recovery of semi-polar to non-polar secondary metabolites associated with antimicrobial and cytotoxic activity. The acetone extract was filtered, and the solvent was recovered using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure at 40 °C. Ethyl acetate extracts (extracellular metabolites) were similarly concentrated. All crude extracts were weighed, sealed in amber glass vials, and stored at − 20 °C until further chemical and biological analyses. Yields of both extracts were recorded and normalized to biomass (mg extract/g dry weight) to facilitate comparative bioactivity assessment.

Antimicrobial assay

The antimicrobial activity of fungal extracts was evaluated against reference microbial strains, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and a pathogenic yeast, following the standardized microdilution and agar diffusion protocols of Pripdeevech et al.13, with modifications. The bacterial panel comprised Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus Rosenbach (ATCC 25923), Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCC 12228), Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 9372), Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), and Klebsiella aerogenes (ATCC 13048). The yeast Candida albicans (ATCC 10231) was included for antifungal screening.

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined by the broth-microdilution technique in sterile 96-well microplates (flat-bottom, 200 µL total volume per well) following CLSI guidelines (M07-A10 for bacteria; M27-A3 for yeasts). Crude extracts were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and serially twofold-diluted in Mueller–Hinton broth (for bacteria) or Sabouraud dextrose broth (for C. albicans) to yield final concentrations ranging from 7.81 to 1,000 µg/ mL. In each well, 90 µL of extract solution was mixed with 10 µL of microbial inoculum adjusted to ~ 1 × 106 CFU/mL for bacteria or ~ 1 × 104 CFU/mL for C. albicans, resulting in a total volume of 100 µL. Negative controls contained medium with ≤ 1% (v/v) DMSO, and sterility controls contained medium only. Positive-control wells contained chloramphenicol (30 µg/mL) for bacteria and amphotericin B (5 µg/mL) for C. albicans. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for bacteria and 30 °C for C. albicans for 24 h. MIC values were defined as the lowest extract concentration showing no visible growth, confirmed spectrophotometrically at 600 nm (BioTek Epoch 2, USA).

Agar diffusion tests were performed to determine inhibition-zone diameters, ZOI (mm). Sterile Mueller–Hinton agar (for bacteria) and Sabouraud dextrose agar (for C. albicans) plates were seeded with 100 µL of standardized inoculum (106 CFU/mL for bacteria; 104 CFU/mL for yeast) and spread evenly. Wells (6 mm diameter) were cut aseptically into the agar, and 50 µL of each fungal extract (1,000 µg/mL) was loaded. DMSO served as the negative control and the same antibiotics as positive controls. Plates were incubated under the same conditions as above, and the diameter of the clear inhibition zone was measured (mm) using a digital caliper. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SD.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effects of fungal extracts were assessed using the colorimetric MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide), which measures mitochondrial activity as an indicator of cell viability. Two human cancer cell lines were employed: colorectal carcinoma (SW480) and lung adenocarcinoma (A549) and one normal cell line, mouse fibroblast (3T3-L1), obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For the assay, cells were seeded into 96-well flat-bottom microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Fungal extracts were dissolved in DMSO and serially twofold diluted in culture medium to yield final concentrations ranging from 7.81 to 1,000 µg/mL. The final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.5% (v/v) in any treatment. Cells were exposed to each concentration of extract in triplicate and incubated for 72 h. Untreated cells served as negative controls, and doxorubicin (10 µg/mL) was used as a positive control. Additional control wells containing 0.5% DMSO were included to confirm that the solvent itself did not influence cell viability.

After incubation, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) was added to each well and incubated for a further 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO, and absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Epoch 2, USA). Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls, and half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) were obtained by nonlinear regression analysis of dose–response curves in GraphPad Prism v9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± SD.

LC–QTOF–MS profiling analysis

The non-volatile secondary metabolites present in the fungal extracts of M. thailandensis and H. kokensis were analyzed using LC–QTOF–MS. Analyses were performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system equipped with a Poroshell EC-C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at 35 °C and connected to an Agilent 6545 QTOF-MS detector. The mobile phase consisted of water (Solvent A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (Solvent B). The gradient program began with 5% B in 1 min, increased linearly to 17% B from 1 to 10 min, held for 3 min, then returned to 5% B over 7 min, followed by a 5-min equilibration. The flow rate was maintained at 0.2 mL/min, with an injection volume of 1 µL per sample. Prior to analysis, all fungal extracts were filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filters and diluted with HPLC-grade methanol as required to ensure compatibility with the mobile phase and avoid column overloading. Mass spectrometry was conducted in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. However, only results from the positive ion mode were emphasized due to higher metabolite yield and signal intensity. The QTOF-MS was operated with the following parameters: capillary voltage at 3.5 kV, nebulizer pressure at 35 psi, drying gas flow of 10 L/min at 300 °C, and a fragmention voltage of 175 V. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over a mass range of m/z 50–1100 at a scan rate of 3 spectra/second.

Data acquisition and processing were performed using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software (version 10.0). Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was conducted in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode to obtain fragmentation spectra of selected precursor ions. Nitrogen served as the collision gas, and collision energies were automatically adjusted based on precursor m/z values to generate informative fragment ion profiles. Tentative metabolite identification was achieved by comparing the accurate mass and MS/MS fragmentation spectra of each feature against entries in the METLIN, MassBank of North America (MoNA), and the NIST 20 Mass Spectral Library. A strict mass error tolerance of ± 10 ppm was applied to minimize false positives, and only those compounds with a matching score exceeding 85% and consistent fragmentation patterns were reported as putatively identified. Positive ESI mode was selected due to its enhanced sensitivity toward basic nitrogen-containing and neutral lipid compounds—common classes in fungal extracts. Annotation also considered common protonated adducts ([M + H]⁺) to improve mass accuracy. All analyses were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and analytical reliability.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among treatment groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test to assess pairwise differences. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Both extracts exhibited measurable antimicrobial effects, with M. thailandensis generally showing greater potency.

Among Gram-positive bacteria, M. thailandensis inhibited B. subtilis with a MIC of 62.25 µg/mL and a zone of inhibition of 20.12 ± 0.21 mm, whereas H. kokensis showed similar MIC (62.25 µg/mL) but a smaller zone (18.04 ± 0.14 mm). For S. aureus, both species had MICs of 125.00 µg/mL, with M. thailandensis producing a larger inhibition zone (19.08 ± 0.12 mm) compared to H. kokensis (13.29 ± 0.67 mm). Against S. epidermidis, H. kokensis demonstrated a lower MIC (62.25 µg/mL) than M. thailandensis (125.00 µg/mL), although inhibition zones were similar (17.54–19.32 mm). For Gram-negative bacteria, M. thailandensis was most active against P. aeruginosa (MIC = 31.12 µg/mL; ZOI = 28.24 ± 0.31 mm), while H. kokensis showed moderate inhibition (MIC = 125.00 µg/mL; ZOI = 12.16 ± 0.43 mm). Both extracts exhibited comparable effects on E. coli and K. aerogenes (MICs = 62.25 µg/mL; ZOI = 9.34–18.35 mm).

In antifungal testing, both extracts inhibited C. albicans, with MICs ranging between 62.25 and 125.00 µg/mL. The extract from M. thailandensis produced a larger inhibition zone (17.29 ± 0.63 mm) than H. kokensis (13.48 ± 0.39 mm). The positive-control antibiotic, chloramphenicol (30 µg/mL) was used only as reference standards for inhibition-zone comparison in agar diffusion tests. Their MICs were not determined in this study, which explains why these standard drugs were not included in the MIC table.

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxic potential of the mycelial extracts from M. thailandensis and H. kokensis was evaluated against three human cancer cell lines (SW480, A549) and one normal cell line (3T3-L1). The results are summarized in Table 2. All tests were conducted in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± SD. Both fungal extracts inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner with M. thailandensis demonstrating stronger cytotoxic effects. Against SW480 and A549 cells, M. thailandensis exhibited IC50 values of 25.11 µg/mL and 32.68 µg/mL, respectively, whereas H. kokensis showed higher IC50 values (63.41 µg/mL and 58.88 µg/mL). In contrast, both extracts were much less toxic to normal 3T3-L1 fibroblasts (IC50 = 563.11–701.35 µg/mL), indicating good selectivity. The positive control, doxorubicin, exhibited potent cytotoxicity, with IC50 values below 7.81 µg/mL for all cancer cell lines. Because the lowest tested concentration (7.81 µg/mL) already produced more than 50% inhibition, the IC50 was reported as < 7.81 µg/mL. Future assays will include lower concentrations (0.78–7.81 µg/mL) to determine the exact IC50. These results confirm the assay validity and demonstrate that M. thailandensis possesses stronger and more selective cytotoxic compounds than H. kokensis.

LC–QTOF–MS -based secondary metabolite profiling

High-resolution LC–QTOF–MS in positive ion mode enabled detection of 15 and 16 non-volatile compounds in the mycelial extracts of M. thailandensis and H. kokensis, respectively (Tables 3, 4). Compound annotation was based on accurate mass, MS/MS spectral matching, and database formula confirmation. In M. thailandensis, key metabolites included hypoxanthine, thymine, trans-zeatin, 5′-methylthioadenosine, oxyquinoline, norharman, 4-hydroxycoumarin, linoleic acid, phytosphingosine, 3-ketosphinganine, sphinganine, carapin-8(9)-ene, oleamide, stearamide, 13E-docosenamide, and 11-ketorockogenin acetate. These compounds span diverse biosynthetic classes such as purines, alkaloids, fatty acid amides, sphingolipids, and triterpenes. H. kokensis yielded a partially overlapping but chemically distinct profile. Notable constituents included pyroglutamic acid, larixinic acid, herbacetin, phenylalanine, chaetochromin, 13-epi-12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, linoleic acid, vaccenic acid, oleamide, and stearamide. Some metabolites, phytosphingosine, 3-ketosphinganine, 4-hydroxycoumarin, linoleic acid, oleamide, carapin-8(9)-ene, and 11-ketorockogenin acetate, were shared between both fungi.

Discussion

The present study represents the first comparative metabolomic and bioactivity profiling of M. thailandensis and H. kokensis, two novel fungi. Both species, isolated from submerged wood in a tropical ecosystem, exhibit distinct yet overlapping secondary metabolite profiles that correlate with their observed antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities. These findings underscore the chemical and pharmacological potential of fungi, a niche that remains vastly underexplored14,15. The LC-QTOF-MS data revealed that both fungi produce structurally diverse compounds including sphingolipids, alkaloids, phenolic derivatives, and fatty acid amides.

The metabolomic analysis was performed using high-resolution LC-QTOF-MS operated in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI⁺). This mode was selected for its superior sensitivity toward key fungal metabolite classes including alkaloids, sphingolipids, amides, and polyphenols9,10. ESI⁺ generates protonated adducts that facilitate clear and reproducible fragmentation patterns in MS/MS spectra, allowing reliable tentative identification through database matching. The success of this approach underscores the utility of ESI⁺ in profiling complex secondary metabolomes from fungal extracts and justifies its application in future dereplication workflows. In M. thailandensis, 15 compounds were identified, with notable constituents including phytosphingosine, norharman, linoleic acid, oleamide, and 4-hydroxycoumarin. These metabolites are consistent with those previously reported from ascomycetous fungi and plants, many of which are associated with antibacterial and anticancer effects16–18.

The antimicrobial potency of M. thailandensis, especially against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus subtilis, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, can be linked to its chemical constituents. Some major bioactive compounds found among both fungal extracts are shown in Fig. 1. Phytosphingosine is a well-characterized antimicrobial sphingoid base that disrupts membrane integrity and inhibits bacterial growth19. Norharman, a β-carboline alkaloid, has DNA-intercalating properties that contribute to antimicrobial and cytostatic effects20. Moreover, oleamide, a fatty acid amide found in both extracts, is known to interfere with quorum sensing and biofilm formation in bacteria21. These mechanisms may explain the enhanced efficacy of M. thailandensis against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. H. kokensis, in contrast, produced 16 metabolites, including chaetochromin, 13-epi-12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, herbacetin, and vaccenic acid. The presence of chaetochromin, a polyketide known for strong cytotoxicity and antifungal properties, suggests a role in the moderate but broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity observed in this extract22. Additionally, the detection of 13-epi-12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, a plant-like oxylipin derivative, suggests a possible case of convergent biosynthesis or metabolic interaction with plant-derived substrates, rather than direct metabolite exchange23. Such compounds are known to modulate antimicrobial defense responses, supporting the hypothesis that H. kokensis adapts to environments through chemical defense mechanisms involving oxylipin- or polyphenol-like metabolites24.

In terms of cytotoxicity, M. thailandensis exhibited stronger inhibition of colorectal (SW480) and lung (A549) carcinoma cell lines than H. kokensis. This correlates with its unique detection of norharman and 13E-docosenamide, both compounds well-documented anticancer activities25,26. Norharman induces apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in cancer cells, while long-chain fatty acid amides such as docosenamide modulate membrane signaling and inhibit proliferation. In addition, linoleic acid, although ubiquitous, contributes to pro-oxidative stress and has been linked to apoptosis induction under certain cellular conditions.

Interestingly, despite having fewer total metabolites, H. kokensis shares key molecules with M. thailandensis, including phytosphingosine, 4-hydroxycoumarin, and carapin-8(9)-ene. These shared metabolites may underpin their comparable, albeit slightly reduced, antifungal and anticancer effects. The distinct presence of chaetochromin in H. kokensis suggests its cytotoxic mechanism may differ mechanistically, possibly through mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation, as reported in similar polyketides27. The broader implication of this study lies in its ecological and bioprospecting significance. The fungi, such as M. thailandensis and H. kokensis, are adapted to submerged woody habitats characterized by microbial competition and limited nutrients28,29. These stressors likely drive secondary metabolite diversity, leading to the evolution of structurally varied and pharmacologically active compounds30,31. Their demonstrated ability to produce antimicrobial and cytotoxic agents from simple liquid cultures positions them as promising candidates for drug discovery pipelines.

Future studies should focus on isolating and structurally confirming key metabolites using NMR spectroscopy combined with bioassay-guided fractionation. Additionally, genomic and transcriptomic analyses of biosynthetic gene clusters could clarify the regulatory mechanisms responsible for species-specific metabolite production. Overall, this study provides foundational evidence for the biotechnological potential of these newly described fungal species and underscores the ecological importance of freshwater habitats in fungal natural product discovery.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive investigation of two novel fungal species, M. thailandensis and H. kokensis, integrating antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and metabolomic analyses. The marked differences in bioactivity and secondary metabolite profiles between the two species underscore their distinct biosynthetic capacities and ecological adaptations. The ability of M. thailandensis to produce a diverse array of bioactive compounds with strong antibacterial and cytotoxic effects suggests a rich chemical repertoire with promising therapeutic applications. Meanwhile, H. kokensis also demonstrated notable bioactivity and harbored a unique set of metabolites, reinforcing the value of local fungi as reservoirs of pharmacologically relevant scaffolds. Our findings expand the known chemical diversity of aquatic Ascomycota and support the utility of LC-QTOF-MS-based profiling in untargeted natural product discovery. The detection of structurally diverse metabolite classes, such as sphingolipids, fatty acid amides, alkaloids, and polyketides, validates the biotechnological promise of these species. Moreover, the identification of shared and species-specific compounds suggests ecological specialization and warrants further genomic and biosynthetic pathway studies. Future work should focus on the bioassay-guided isolation of active metabolites, mode-of-action studies, and in vivo efficacy models to fully realize the pharmacological potential of these fungi. As global efforts in biodiversity-driven drug discovery intensify, this study reinforces the importance of underexplored ecosystems as fertile grounds for new natural products.
