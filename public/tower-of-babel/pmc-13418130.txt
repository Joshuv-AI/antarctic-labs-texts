The effect of belimumab combined with conventional treatment on NK cell count in childhood systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Hongjun Han, Qihua Feng, Xiaoxiang Song, Yue Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the effect of belimumab combined with conventional therapy on natural killer (NK) cells counts and its correlation with clinical response in childhood systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 46 cSLE patients treated with belimumab plus standard therapy for 48 weeks. NK cell counts, complement levels, SLEDAI-2 K scores, and steroid doses were assessed at baseline, 24, and 48 weeks. Statistical analyses included paired t-test, Wilcoxon test, Pearson/Spearman correlation, and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) calculation.ResultsNK cell counts increased from 72.0 ± 56.6 cells/µL at baseline to 185.6 ± 84.5 cells/µL at week 48 (P < 0.05). Complement C3 and C4 levels also improved significantly. SLEDAI-2 K scores decreased from 16.4 to 2.1 (P < 0.05), with 88.1% achieving low disease activity. Steroid dose was reduced in over 90% of patients. NK cell increase strongly correlated with both complement recovery (ρ = 0.68, P < 0.05) and disease activity improvement (ρ = 0.69, P < 0.05).ConclusionsBelimumab combined with conventional therapy may enhance NK cell counts, improve serological and clinical outcomes, and facilitate steroid tapering in cSLE. Further prospective controlled studies are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-026-00656-6.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus; Child; Belimumab; Killer cells, natural; Complement C3; Treatment outcome

Background

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by dysregulated immune responses and the production of pathogenic autoantibodies, leading to multi-organ inflammation and damage [1]. Childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) often presents with more severe disease activity, higher rates of major organ involvement (particularly lupus nephritis), and accrues damage more rapidly than adult-onset disease, necessitating effective yet safe long-term treatment strategies [2, 3].

The pathogenesis of SLE centrally involves B-cell hyperactivity and loss of self-tolerance. Conventional treatment relies heavily on glucocorticoids and broad-spectrum immunosuppressants, which, while controlling disease activity, are associated with significant long-term toxicity, especially in growing children [4]. Belimumab, a human monoclonal antibody that inhibits the B-cell survival factor BAFF (B-lymphocyte stimulator), represents a targeted therapeutic approach. It has been proven to reduce disease activity and steroid use in both adult and pediatric SLE patients by selectively depleting autoreactive B cells [5, 6].

Beyond B-cells, immune dysregulation in SLE extends to innate immunity. Natural killer (NK) cells are critical effector lymphocytes whose numerical and functional deficiencies have been consistently observed in SLE patients and correlate with disease activity and specific organ involvement, such as nephritis [7, 8]. While Belimumab’s primary mechanism targets B-cells, emerging evidence suggests its immunomodulatory effects may influence broader immune cell subsets, including NK cells [9]. However, the dynamic changes in NK cell counts during Belimumab treatment in children with SLE, and their correlation with clinical and serological outcomes, remain insufficiently characterized. To our knowledge, no prior study has longitudinally assessed NK cell counts in pediatric SLE patients treated with belimumab. In adult SLE, limited data suggest belimumab may be associated with changes in NK cell numbers or phenotype, but findings are inconsistent and pediatric-specific data are lacking.

Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to investigate the longitudinal changes in peripheral NK cell counts in cSLE patients treated with Belimumab combined with conventional therapy over 48 weeks, and to analyze their association with improvements in disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K), complement levels, and glucocorticoid dosage reduction.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

This retrospective study did not include a healthy control group. The analysis focused on within-patient changes over time. A total of 46 children with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) who were admitted to the Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University between January 2018 and January 2024. The children were under 18 years old, and the guardians or close relatives of the children had informed consent to the treatment received by the children. This study met the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association.

Treatment plan

All patients were treated with belimumab at a dose of 10 mg/kg, intravenously once at weeks 0, 2, and 4, and then once every 4 weeks for a total of 48 weeks. At the same time, patients were allowed to continue with a stable dose of basic treatment drugs such as glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, and immunosuppressants, depending on the specific circumstances of the patient and the attending physician.

Concomitant medications

All patients continued their pre-existing conventional immunosuppressive therapy at stable doses throughout the 48-week belimumab treatment period, as per standard of care. This included:

· Glucocorticoids: All 46 patients (100%) were receiving oral prednisone at baseline.

· Antimalarials: 42 patients (91.3%) were on hydroxychloroquine.

· Immunosuppressants: 36 patients (78.3%) were receiving mycophenolate mofetil. No other immunosuppressants were used in this cohort.

The cumulative prednisone dose (mg) over the study period was calculated for each patient to assess its potential confounding effect on lymphocyte subsets.

Clinical and laboratory assessments

Clinical and serological assessments were performed at baseline, week 24, and week 48. The following domains were evaluated, selected based on their inclusion in the SLEDAI-2 K and their relevance to common and severe manifestations in cSLE: constitutional (fever), mucocutaneous (rash, alopecia, mucosal ulcers), musculoskeletal (arthritis), renal (proteinuria, hematuria, as per lupus nephritis assessment), serosal (serositis), hematological (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), and immunological (anti-dsDNA, complement levels).

NK cell count detection: Peripheral venous blood was collected from patients before treatment and at weeks 24 and 48 after treatment respectively, and the NK cell count was detected by flow cytometry. The specific steps are as follows: Take an EDTA anticoagulant tube to collect a peripheral blood sample, add the red blood cell lysate to the whole blood, incubate at room temperature for 10–15 min, centrifuge and discard the supernatant, retain the white blood cell precipitate, wash the cells with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), centrifuge to remove the residual lysate, mix the anticoagulant with an equal amount of normal saline or PBS, and slowly stack it onto the lymphocyte isolate. Centrifuge (400–500 g, 20–30 min), aspirate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the intermediate white membrane layer, wash PBMCs with PBS to remove platelets and fragments, label NK cells with antibodies FITC-CD3, PE-CD56, APC-CD16, incubate in the dark for 20–30 min (room temperature or 4 °C) Wash to remove unbound antibodies and analyze NK cell percentage by flow cytometry, NK cell percentage = (CD3⁻/CD56⁺ or CD16⁺ cell count)/total lymphocyte count × 100%. To calculate the absolute NK cell count in combination with the complete blood cell count results, the absolute NK cell count = total white blood cell count × percentage of lymphocytes × percentage of NK cells.

Assessment of disease activity: The disease activity of patients was evaluated using the SLEDAI-2 K score simultaneously at the above time points. The SLEDAI-2 K score covers the patient’s clinical symptoms (such as fever, rash, arthritis, oral ulcers, alopecia, etc.) and laboratory test indicators (such as anti-double-stranded DNA antibody titer, complement level, etc.), with a higher score indicating higher disease activity.

Clinical symptom assessment: Detailed documentation of the improvement of major clinical symptoms before and after treatment, such as skin and mucous membrane symptoms (rash, alopecia, etc.), arthritis and kidney involvement symptoms (proteinuria, hematuria).

MCID calculation details

Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) values were calculated using a composite method combining anchor-based, distribution-based (0.5 × SD, SEM), and ROC curve approaches. For the ROC method, we used the change in SLEDAI-2 K score (improvement vs. no improvement, defined as ≥ 4-point reduction) as the state variable, and the change in NK cell count as the test variable. The optimal cutoff was identified as the value maximizing Youden’s index. The anchor-based method used physician assessment of clinically meaningful improvement as a surrogate. The final recommended MCID was the average of these four estimates.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data, following assessment with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages.

For within-group comparisons across time points, paired t-test was used when the data were normally distributed; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. For NK cell counts (non‑normal at baseline), the Wilcoxon test was prespecified as the primary test, and the paired t‑test results are also shown for comparison. Exact P values are reported as P < 0.001 when below that threshold.

Multiple linear regression model specification

To explore factors associated with the change in NK cell count, we constructed a per‑patient cross‑sectional model with the dependent variable ΔNK = NK cell count at week 48 minus baseline NK cell count. Independent variables included:

· Treatment time point (categorical, 0 = baseline, 1 = week 48) – this variable captures the change from baseline to week 48.

· Baseline SLEDAI‑2 K score (continuous).

· Baseline NK cell count (continuous).

· Cumulative prednisone dose over 48 weeks (continuous, in mg).

This model is per‑patient (one observation per patient, using ΔNK as outcome). The variable “treatment time point” was entered as a binary indicator; because only two time points (baseline and week 48) were compared, its coefficient directly estimates the adjusted mean change. No categorical variables other than treatment time point were used. The model was fitted using ordinary least squares. Goodness‑of‑fit is reported as R² and ANOVA P value.

Safety assessment

Adverse events (AEs) were extracted from medical records, including infections, infusion reactions, laboratory abnormalities, and hospitalizations. Serious AEs were defined as those requiring hospitalization, prolonging hospitalization, or being life‑threatening.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1) and SPSS (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection is summarized in Fig. 1 (PRISMA flow diagram). Between January 2018 and January 2024, 52 pediatric SLE patients initiated belimumab. Six patients were excluded due to: incomplete clinical data (n = 3), use of another biologic agent within 3 months prior to enrollment (n = 1), and loss to follow-up (n = 2), resulting in 46 patients included in the final analysis.

Changes in the proportion of NK cell count

The mean count of NK cells in the peripheral blood of patients before belimumab treatment was 72.0, with a standard deviation of 56.6. At the 24th week of treatment, the NK cell count increased to a mean of 125.4 and a standard deviation of 71.2, showing a statistically significant difference compared to the pre-treatment level (P < 0.001). At the 48th week of treatment, the NK cell count further increased to a mean of 185.6 and a standard deviation of 84.5, with statistically significant differences compared to both the pre-treatment and 24th week counts (P < 0.001). The paired t-test results for the comparison between pre-treatment and 48 weeks post-treatment were t = 13.81 (P < 0.001), and the Wilcoxon test results were z = 5.62 (P < 0.001). Both tests indicated significant differences, with an effect size (Cohen’s d) > 1.2, suggesting a highly clinically significant treatment effect. The consistency of the parametric test (t-test) and non-parametric test (Wilcoxon) results indicated that the outcome was not affected by the data distribution. Before belimumab treatment, the NK cell count in males was 83.2, slightly higher than that in females (68.3). After 48 weeks of treatment, the absolute increase in NK cells in males was 151.7, also slightly higher than that in females (101.1) (Fig. 1).

Influence of cumulative steroid dose on NK cell changes

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounders. The results are presented in Table 3. The increase in NK cell count at week 48 (ΔNK) remained significantly associated with treatment duration (β = 108.4, 95% CI: 89.2–127.6, P < 0.001) after adjusting for baseline SLEDAI-2 K score (β = -1.2, 95% CI: -3.9–1.5, P = 0.38) and cumulative prednisone dose over 48 weeks (β = -0.05, 95% CI: -0.27–0.17, P = 0.65). The model demonstrated good fit (R² = 0.82, P < 0.001). This suggests that the observed increase in NK cells was independently associated with belimumab treatment and not primarily driven by concurrent glucocorticoid reduction or baseline disease activity.

Changes in complement C3 and complement C4 before and after treatment with belimumab

Before belimumab treatment, the average complement C3 level was 0.54 g/L, and after 48 weeks of treatment, it increased to 0.89 g/L. The rate of reaching the standard (≥ 0.85 g/L) was 32.6% (36.4% in males and 31.4% in females). The paired t-test value for the comparison between pre-treatment and 48 weeks post-treatment was 15.32 (P < 0.001), and the Wilcoxon test value was 5.89 (P < 0.001). Both tests indicated significant differences, with an effect size (Cohen’s d) > 1.2, suggesting a highly clinically significant treatment effect. Before treatment, the average complement C4 level was 0.07 g/L, and after 48 weeks of treatment, it increased to 0.15 g/L. The rate of reaching the standard (≥ 0.12 g/L) was 58.7% (45.5% in males and 62.9% in females). The paired t-test value for the comparison between pre-treatment and 48 weeks post-treatment was 11.67 (P < 0.001), and the Wilcoxon test value was 5.45 (P < 0.001). Both tests indicated significant differences, with an effect size (Cohen’s d) > 1.2, suggesting a highly clinically significant treatment effect. There was no significant gender difference in the recovery of complement between males and females (Figs. 2 and 3).

Pearson correlation analysis and Spearman correlation analysis showed that the increase in NK cell count during the treatment of SLE patients was strongly positively correlated with the improvement of activity score, with r value of 0.71 and ρ value of 0.69, and the corrected p values were both less than 0.001. Improvement of clinical symptoms

During treatment, the patient’s clinical symptoms gradually improved. At the 24th week after treatment, the rash completely subsided in 39 patients with rash, the joint pain disappeared in 29 patients with arthralgia, and the pain was significantly reduced in 1 patient; Among the patients with proteinuria, 16 had negative proteinuria and 3 had a significant reduction in urine protein quantification. The improvement in clinical symptoms was consistent with an increase in NK cell count and a decrease in disease activity.

Safety outcomes

During the 48-week treatment period, no severe infusion reactions, opportunistic infections, or deaths were reported. Mild-to-moderate adverse events included upper respiratory tract infections (n = 9, 19.6%), infusion-site pain (n = 4, 8.7%), and transient headache (n = 2, 4.3%). No patient discontinued belimumab due to adverse events. There were no cases of tuberculosis reactivation, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, or malignancy.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that belimumab, combined with conventional therapy, induces a significant and progressive increase in peripheral NK cell counts in children with SLE. This increase is strongly correlated with key clinical improvements: reduction in disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K), recovery of complement C3 levels, and successful glucocorticoid tapering.

Our findings confirm the baseline deficiency of NK cells in active cSLE [7]. The 157.8% mean increase after 48 weeks of belimumab treatment suggests a reversal of this immune abnormality alongside clinical remission. While NK cell dysfunction is a recognized feature of SLE pathogenesis linked to impaired clearance of autoreactive cells and immune complexes [9, 10], its dynamic restoration during belimumab therapy has been less documented. The observed correlation implies that NK cell reconstitution may be integral to the therapeutic response, potentially serving as a marker of immune rebalancing beyond mere B-cell inhibition. In adult SLE, limited and inconsistent data exist regarding belimumab’s effect on NK cells; some studies suggest modulation of NK cell numbers or phenotype, but pediatric-specific data have been lacking. Our findings provide the first longitudinal evidence in children.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a prototypical systemic autoimmune disease whose pathogenesis arises from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation [11]. Environmental factors such as ultraviolet radiation can induce apoptosis and promote the translocation of intracellular autoantigens to the cell surface [12]. Infections may trigger responses through molecular mimicry, where pathogen proteins resemble self-antigens like Smith antigen or dsDNA [13]. Crucially, a genetically impaired clearance of apoptotic debris—often associated with deficiencies in early complement components like C1q and C4—results in the accumulation of modified, highly immunogenic self-antigens (nucleosomes, dsDNA) [14]. These antigens are internalized and presented by antigen-presenting cells. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), upon sensing nucleic acids via intracellular Toll-like receptors (TLR7/9), become activated and produce large quantities of type I interferons (IFN-α) [15]. IFN-α acts as a central cytokine in SLE: it promotes dendritic cell maturation, directly stimulates B-cell differentiation into antibody-producing plasma cells, and supports T-cell activation [16]. Activated CD4+ T cells, in turn, provide critical help to B cells through CD40-CD40L interaction and cytokine secretion, leading to the expansion of autoreactive B-cell clones [17]. The resulting autoantibodies form immune complexes that deposit in tissues such as the glomeruli, skin, and synovium [18]. There, they activate complement, recruit inflammatory cells, and cause direct tissue injury, manifesting as lupus nephritis, cutaneous lesions, and arthritis [19].

Accumulating evidence implicates natural killer (NK) cells in the immunopathology of SLE [10]. Patients frequently exhibit a reduction in total peripheral NK cell numbers, with a particularly marked decline in the mature, highly cytotoxic CD56dimCD57+ subset [20]. These cells demonstrate increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis [21]. Functionally, SLE NK cells show impaired cytotoxicity, reflected by decreased expression of perforin and granzyme B, and a reduced capacity to clear aberrant cells, including autoreactive lymphocytes and apoptotic cells [22]. This deficit may contribute to the persistence of autoantigens. Furthermore, NK cell cytokine secretion is dysregulated; while pro-inflammatory cytokine production may be contextually increased, the secretion of immunoregulatory cytokines like IFN-γ is often diminished [23]. NK cells can also interact with other immune cells: they may influence B-cell activity and potentiate IFN-α production by pDCs [24]. Thus, NK cell deficiency and dysfunction in SLE likely represent both a consequence of the inflammatory milieu and a contributing factor to the loss of immune homeostasis.

Belimumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody, offers a targeted therapeutic approach by inhibiting the B-cell survival factor BAFF (B-lymphocyte stimulator) [5]. In SLE, BAFF levels are elevated, promoting the survival and maturation of autoreactive B cells [25]. By binding soluble BAFF, belimumab deprives these B cells of a critical survival signal, leading to their apoptosis and a subsequent reduction in pathogenic autoantibody production [6]. This serological change correlates with the recovery of complement levels (C3, C4) and a decrease in immune complex formation and deposition, ultimately mitigating end-organ inflammation [26]. The observed increase in NK cell counts following belimumab treatment may be viewed as a downstream effect of this broader immunomodulation. As the intense autoimmune drive and inflammatory cytokine milieu are subdued, the environment may become more conducive to NK cell survival and homeostasis [27]. Additionally, emerging data suggest belimumab may modulate signaling pathways like JAK-STAT, which are involved in cytokine signaling relevant to NK cell biology, potentially offering a more direct, albeit indirect, mechanistic link to NK cell reconstitution [28].

This study has several important limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection bias. The absence of a control group of active cSLE patients on conventional therapy alone makes it difficult to definitively attribute NK cell changes specifically to belimumab versus improved disease control in general. Although we performed regression analysis to adjust for cumulative steroid dose, residual confounding from time-varying immunosuppressive exposures cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, the relatively high male proportion in our cohort (23.9%) compared to typical cSLE epidemiology (approximately 80–90% female) may influence results, as males showed higher baseline NK counts and greater increases; this should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, this study measured NK cell quantity; parallel functional assessments are needed to confirm whether the numerical increase translates to improved immune function.

Despite these limitations, our data suggest that NK cell count may represent a dynamic immunologic marker associated with treatment response in cSLE. Patients achieving the NK-MCID threshold had significantly higher remission rates, supporting the clinical relevance of NK cell recovery. Although these MCID thresholds are exploratory, they could serve as a reference for future prospective studies. However, whether NK cells can serve as a predictive or surrogate biomarker requires validation in prospective controlled studies with functional immune profiling, and clinical decisions should not be based solely on these preliminary data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data suggest an association between belimumab therapy and NK cell reconstitution in cSLE, and this change correlates with robust clinical and serological improvement. NK cell count may represent a dynamic immunologic marker associated with treatment response. However, the exact mechanistic role of NK cells in belimumab’s efficacy and the clinical utility of this marker require validation in prospective, controlled studies with functional immune profiling.
