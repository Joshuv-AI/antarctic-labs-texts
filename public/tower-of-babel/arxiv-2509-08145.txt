Estimating Peer Effects Using Partial Network Data
Vincent Boucher

Abstract




We study the estimation of peer effects through social networks when researchers do not observe the entire network structure. Special cases include sampled networks, censored networks, and misclassified links. We assume that researchers can obtain a consistent estimator of the distribution of the network. We show that this assumption is sufficient for estimating peer effects using a linear-in-means model. We provide an empirical application to the study of peer effects on students’ academic achievement using the widely used Add Health database, and show that network data errors have a large downward bias on estimated peer effects.

JEL Codes: C31, C36, C51

Keywords: Social networks, Peer effects, Missing variables, Measurement errors

Introduction

There is a large and growing literature on the impact of peer effects in social networks.
1
1

1

For recent reviews, see
Bramoullé et al. 2020
, and
De Paula 2017
.
However, since eliciting network data is expensive
(
Breza et al. 2020
)
, relatively few data sets contain comprehensive network information, and existing ones are prone to data errors. Despite some recent contributions, existing methodologies for the estimation of peer effects with incomplete or erroneous network data either focus on a specific kind of sampling or error, or they are highly computationally demanding.

In this paper, we propose a unifying framework that allows for the estimation of peer effects under the widely used linear-in-means model (e.g.
Manski 1993
;
Bramoullé et al. 2009
) when the researcher does not observe the entire network structure (but still observes
some
information about the network). Our methodology is computationally attractive and sufficiently flexible to cover cases where, for example, network data are sampled
(
Chandrasekhar and
Lewis 2011
;
Liu 2013
;
Lewbel
et al. 2023
)
, censored
(
Griffith 2022
)
, or misclassified
(
Hardy
et al. 2024
)
. Our central assumption is that the researcher is able to estimate a network formation model using some partial information about the network structure. Leveraging recent contributions on the estimation of network formation models and focusing primarily on models with conditional link independence, we show that this assumption is sufficient to identify and estimate peer effects.

Assuming that the network is exogenous, we propose two estimators. First, we present a computationally attractive estimator based on a simulated generalized method of moments (SGMM). The moments are built using draws from the (estimated) network formation model. We study the finite sample properties of our SGMM estimator via Monte Carlo simulations. We show that the estimator performs very well, even when a large fraction of the links are missing or misclassified. Second, we present a flexible likelihood-based (Bayesian) estimator allowing us to exploit the entire structure of the data-generating process. The Bayesian approach is flexible as it allows to cover cases for which the asymptotic framework of our SGMM fails. Although the computational cost is higher than that of the SGMM, we exploit recent computational advances in the literature, e.g.
Mele 2017
;
Hsieh
et al. 2019
, and show that the estimator can be successfully implemented on common-sized data sets. In particular, we apply our estimator to study peer effects on academic achievement using the widely used Add Health database. We find that data errors have a large downward bias on the estimated endogenous effect.

Our SGMM estimator is built as a bias-corrected version of the instrumental strategy proposed by
Bramoullé et al. 2009
. Using a network formation model, we obtain a consistent estimator of the distribution of the true network.
2
2

2

This generally requires some form of conditionally random sampling of the true network.
We then use this estimated distribution to obtain different draws from the distribution of the network. We show that our moment conditions are asymptotically valid and that the estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, even with a finite number of draws from the estimated distribution of the network. This property significantly reduces the computational cost of the method compared to methods that rely on integrating the moment conditions
(
Chandrasekhar and
Lewis 2011
, e.g.,)
.

Importantly, our SGMM strategy requires only the (partial) observation of a
single
cross-section, unlike, for example, the approach of
Zhang 2024
. The presence of this feature is because of two main properties of the model. First, we can consistently estimate the distribution of the mismeasured variable (i.e., the network) using a single (partial) observation of the variable. Second, in the absence of measurement error, valid instruments for the endogenous peer variable are available
(
Bramoullé et al. 2009
)
.

Our Bayesian estimator is based on the likelihood function and therefore uses more information about the structure of the model, leading to more precise estimates. In the context of this estimator, the estimated distribution for the network acts as a prior distribution, and the inferred network structure is updated through a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. Our approach relies on data augmentation
(
Tanner and
Wong 1987
)
, which treats the network as an additional set of parameters to be estimated. In particular, our MCMC builds on recent developments from the empirical literature on network formation
(
Mele 2017
;
Hsieh
et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. We show that the computational cost of our estimator is reasonable and that it can easily be applied to standard data sets.

We study the impact of errors in adolescents’ friendship network data for the estimation of peer effects in education
(
Calvó-Armengol et al. 2009
)
. We show that the widely used Add Health database features many missing links—around 45% of the within-school friendship nominations are coded with error—and that these data errors strongly bias the estimated peer effects. Specifically, we estimate a model of peer effects on students’ academic achievement. We probabilistically reconstruct the missing links, accounting for the potential censoring, and we obtain a consistent estimator of peer effects using both our estimators. The bias due to data errors is qualitatively important, even assuming that the network is exogenous. Our estimated endogenous peer effect coefficient is 1.5 times larger than that obtained by assuming the data contains no errors.

This paper contributes to the recent literature on the estimation of peer effects when the network is either not entirely observed or observed with noise. In particular, our framework is valid when network data are either sampled, censored, or misclassified.

Sampled networks and censoring:

Relatedly,

Griffith and

present a characterization of the analytic bias of censoring for the reduced-form parameters in the linear-in-means and linear-in-sums models under an Expectational Equivalence assumption.

We contribute to this literature by proposing two simple and flexible estimators for the estimation of peer effects based on a linear-in-means model. Our estimators do not require many observations of the sampled network. Similar to
Griffith 2022
and

Kim 2023

Misclassification:

Hardy
et al. 2024

Lewbel

Our model allows for the misclassification of all links with positive probability, and we do not impose restrictions on the rate of misclassification. As in
Hardy

2.1
The Linear-in-Means Model

In this section, we present the linear-in-means model
(
Manski 1993
;
Bramoullé et al. 2009
)
, arguably the most widely used model for studying peer effects in networks
(see
Bramoullé et al. 2020
, for a recent review)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}=f(\mathbf{A}_{m})
, the
\displaystyle N_{m}\times N_{m}

interaction matrix
for some function
\displaystyle f
. Unless otherwise stated, we assume that
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
is a row-normalization of the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}
.
10
10

10

In such a case,
\displaystyle g_{ij,m}=a_{ij,m}/n_{i,m}
whenever
\displaystyle n_{i,m}>0

\displaystyle g_{ij,m}=0
otherwise.
Most of our results hold for any function
\displaystyle f

We focus on the following model:

\mathbf{y}_{m}=c\mathbf{1}_{m}+\mathbf{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\alpha\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{y}_{m}+{\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m},

(1)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{m}
is a
\displaystyle N_{m}-
dimensional vector of
\displaystyle 1

The parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
therefore captures the impact of the average outcome of one’s peers on their behavior (the endogenous peer effect). The parameter
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}
captures the impact of one’s characteristics on their behavior (the individual effects). The parameter
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}
captures the impact of the average characteristics of one’s peers on their behavior (the contextual peer effects). For simplicity, we assume that the constant
\displaystyle c
does not vary across
\displaystyle m
. However, our results can be adapted to include group-level fixed effects.

We impose the following assumptions.

Assumption 3
.

\displaystyle|\alpha|<1/\|\mathbf{G}_{m}\|
for some submultiplicative norm
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
, and all
\displaystyle m=1,...,M
.

Assumption 4
.

Exogeneity:
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m}|\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{A}_{m},\mathcal{A}_{m}]=\mathbf{0}
for all
\displaystyle m=1,...,M
.

Assumption
3
ensures that the model is coherent and that there exists a unique vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{m}
compatible with (
1

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
is row-normalized,
\displaystyle|\alpha|<1
is sufficient.
Finally, Assumption

While the exogeneity of the network is a strong assumption, we consider it as a benchmark and focus on the case in which the network is not perfectly observed. 2.2
The Network Formation Model

In this paper, we relax the costly assumption that the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}
is observed. We assume instead that sufficient information about the network (i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}
) is observed so that a network formation model can be estimated. The discussion below formalizes our assumptions about the relationship between
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}
. We start by describing the data-generating process for
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}
.

We assume that for any group
\displaystyle m
,
\displaystyle P(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})=\Pi_{ij}P(a_{ij,m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})
, where

P(a_{ij,m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})=\frac{\exp\{a_{ij,m}Q(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{ij,m})\}}{1+\exp\{Q(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{ij,m})\}},

(2)

and where
\displaystyle Q
is some known function that is twice continuously differentiable in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{ij,m}=\mathbf{w}_{ij,m}(\mathbf{X}_{m})
is a vector of observed characteristics for the pair
\displaystyle ij
in group
\displaystyle m
.
11
11

11

Throughout,
\displaystyle P
refers to the probability notation. Note that by construction, links are only defined between individuals of the same group so the probability that individuals from different groups are linked is zero.

We focus on network formation models that are conditionally independent across links:
\displaystyle P(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})=\Pi_{ij}P(a_{ij,m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})
. This notably includes models such as the ones in
Graham 2017
and
Breza et al. 2020
, but excludes many models of strategic network formation such as the ones in
Mele 2017
and
De Paula
et al. 2018
.

This is mainly done for simplicity (and clarity) of the analysis in the main text. We however note that our methodology can be adapted to more general network formation models. In Online Appendix B, we further discuss how this can be done for a few specific network formation processes, such as the one in
Boucher and
Mourifié 2017
, as well as exponential random graph models (ERGM) featuring only reciprocity.

Using Partial Information

In this section, we discuss how partial network information can be used in order to estimate the structural parameters
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
from the network formation process (
2

Assumption 5

Given

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{M}

\displaystyle\sqrt{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{M}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\rightarrow_{d}N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{V}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})

Assumption

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}
is sufficient to
identify

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}

12

the dependence between

When Assumption

Definition 1

A consistent estimator of the distribution of the true network for some function

\displaystyle\hat{P}(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\mathbf{X}_{m},\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{m,\mathbf{A}_{m}}\|\hat{P}(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\mathbf{X}_{m},\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}))-{P}(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\mathbf{X}_{m},\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}))\|\rightarrow_{p}0

\displaystyle M\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}

Thus, the structural model is only used to obtain the probability of links that are not observed for individuals whose links are potentially censored, i.e.,
\displaystyle\hat{P}(a_{ij,m}=1|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\mathbf{X}_{m},a_{ij,m}^{obs}=0)={\exp\{\mathbf{w}_{ij,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\}}/({1+\exp\{\mathbf{w}_{ij,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\}})
for all
\displaystyle ij
, such that
\displaystyle n_{i}=T
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}
can be represented by an
\displaystyle N_{m}\times N_{m}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}^{mis}

The (consistent) estimation

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(a_{ij,m}=1|a_{ij,m}^{mis}=0,\mathbf{X}_{m})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{2}P_{ij,m}^{1}}{\rho_{2}P_{ij,m}^{1}+(1-\rho_{1})(1-P_{ij,m}^{1})}

3
Simulated Generalized Method of Moment Estimators

In this section, we present an estimator based on a Simulated Generalized Method of Moments (SGMM). Our SGMM is constructed as a de-biased simulated version of the widely used linear GMM in
Bramoullé et al. 2009
.

Before presenting the estimator, we start with an informal discussion of how the moment function is built. A formal treatment is presented in Appendix
A
. Recall first the linear-in-means model presented in the previous section:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{m}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{m}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\alpha\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{y}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m},

where we defined
\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{m}=[\mathbf{1}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}]
, and
\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[c,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}]^{\prime}
. A valid set of instruments is:
\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{m}=[\mathbf{1}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{G}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{G}_{m}^{2}\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{G}_{m}^{3}\mathbf{X}_{m},...]

(
Bramoullé et al. 2009
)
. This defines the following moment function:
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{Z}_{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m}
, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\alpha,c,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}]^{\prime}
, and one can easily show that
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\mathbf{A}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{m}]=\mathbf{0}
for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
is identified under the usual rank condition.

14

14

As standard, we use the subscript
\displaystyle 0
to denote the true value of the parameter. See e.g.,
Bramoullé et al. 2009

Lee
et al. 2010

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
is observed.

Unfortunately, this approach is not feasible when
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}=f(\mathbf{A}_{m})

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{m}
(see Definition

To simplify the notation, we denote
\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{G}}_{m}=f(\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{m})

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{m}=f(\ddot{\mathbf{A}}_{m})

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{m}

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{A}}_{m}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{m}

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{m}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\dot{\mathbf{m}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}),\mathbf{X}_{m})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}_{m}[(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\alpha\dot{\mathbf{G}}_{m})\mathbf{y}_{m}-\dot{\mathbf{V}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]|\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}),\mathbf{X}_{m})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}_{m}\dot{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{m}|\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}),\mathbf{X}_{m}),

\displaystyle N_{m}

There are two issues with the previous moment function. First, the instruments and the explanatory variables are generated using the same network draw

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{m}

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{m}

This leads to:
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\ddot{\mathbf{m}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}),\mathbf{X}_{m}\right)=\mathbb{E}(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}_{m}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{m}|\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m}),\mathbf{X}_{m})

\displaystyle\ddot{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{m}=(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\alpha\ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{m})\mathbf{y}_{m}-\ddot{\mathbf{V}}_{m}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{m}

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\alpha_{0}\ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{m})(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\alpha_{0}\mathbf{G}_{m})^{-1}

Here, a few remarks regarding the consistency and asymptotic normality are in order. Note that the simulated moment function is based on network draws that depend on an

First, it implies that our SGMM estimator is a two-stage estimator and therefore that the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix for

5
Application

In this section, we assume that the econometrician has access to network data but that the data may contain errors due to both
sampling
(links coded with errors) and
censoring
. To show how our method can be used to address these issues, we consider a simple example where we are interested in estimating peer effects on adolescents’ academic achievements.

We use the widely used AddHealth database and show that network data errors have a large impact on the estimated peer effects. Specifically, we focus on a subset of schools from the Wave I “In School” sample that have less than 200 students (33 schools). Table G.1 in the Online Appendix G.3 presents the summary statistics.

Most papers estimating peer effects that use this particular database have taken the network structure as given. One notable exception is
Griffith 2022
, looking at censoring: students can only report up to five male and five female friends. We also allow for censoring, but show that censoring is not the most important issue with the Add Health data. To understand why, we discuss the organization of the data.

Each adolescent is assigned a unique identifier. The data includes ten variables for the ten potential friendships (a maximum of five male and five female friends). These variables can contain missing values (no friendship was reported), an error code (the named friend could not be found in the database), or an identifier for the reported friends. These data are then used to generate the network’s adjacency matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
.

Of course, error codes cannot be matched to any particular adolescent. Moreover, even in the case where the friendship variable refers to a valid identifier, the referred adolescent may still be absent from the database. A prime example is when the referred adolescent has been removed from the database by the researcher, perhaps because of other missing variables for these particular individuals. These missing links are quantitatively important as they account for roughly 45% of the total number of links (7,830 missing for 10,163 observed links). Figure
2
displays the distribution of the number of ‘‘unmatched named friends.’’

32

32

We focus on within-school friendships; thus, nominations outside of school are not treated as “unmatched friends.” Note also that these data errors could be viewed as a special case of censoring
(
Griffith 2022
)
in which researchers know exactly how many links are censored. The attenuation bias is thus expected.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose two estimators for which peer effects can be estimated without observing the entire network structure. We find, perhaps surprisingly, that even very partial information on network structure is sufficient. By specifying a network formation model, researchers can probabilistically reconstruct the true network and base the estimation of peer effects on this reconstructed network. Importantly, we provide computationally tractable and flexible estimators to do so, all of which are available in our R package
PartialNetwork
. We apply our methodology to the widely used Add Health data and find that missing links due to noise in the data have large effects on the estimated peer effect coefficient.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}=\{\{a_{ij,m}\}_{i,j\in\hat{N}^{2}_{m}},\bar{a}_{m}\}

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{m}=\mathbf{A}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}

Other ERGM

Exponential Random Graph models (ERGM) are such that:

B.1.4
ERGM: Reciprocal links

This simplest possible ERGM is such that:

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exp\{w_{ji}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\}

B.1.5
ERGM: Transitive triads

A typical feature of the data that is hard to replicate using the model in Equation (
2

\displaystyle i
is linked to
\displaystyle j
and
\displaystyle j
is linked to
\displaystyle k
, then the probability that
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle k
are linked is higher than what would be predicted by a model with conditionally independent links. Consider the following ERGM:

P(\mathbf{A}_{m}|\mathbf{X}_{m})\propto\exp\{\sum_{ij}(a_{ij}w_{ij}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\rho_{1}a_{ij}a_{ji}+\rho_{2}\sum_{k}a_{ij}a_{jk}a_{ki})\},

\displaystyle\rho_{2}>0

\displaystyle i
is linked to
\displaystyle j

\displaystyle j
is linked to
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k
is linked to
\displaystyle i
are more likely (everything else equal).

Here, the sufficient statistics are: the fraction of links (given a set of observed pair characteristics
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{ij}

requires
sampling triads of individuals, which substantially complicates the sampling design. We are not aware of any such application. We also note that even with the consistent estimation of sufficient statistics, the estimation of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}

B.1.6
Aggregated relational data

Aggregated relational data (ARD) are obtained from survey questions such as, “How many friends with trait ‘X’ do you have?” Here,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
can be represented by an
\displaystyle N\times K
matrix of integer values, where
\displaystyle K

Building on
McCormick and
Zheng 2015

Breza et al. 2020

P(a_{ij,m}=1)=\frac{\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\}}{1+\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\}}.

\displaystyle\nu_{i}
and
\displaystyle\nu_{j}

\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j

Hoff
et al. 2002

First, ARD does not provide information on any specific links;

B.2
Implications for survey design

As seen throughout the paper, the researchers’ ability to obtain a consistent estimator of the network formation process (see Definition
1
and Assumption
5
) depends on the available information about the network (i.e.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
) as well as on the flexibility of the network formation process. Highly structured network formation processes are powerful and require less information. More flexible network formation processes (such as the ones discussed above) require more data. In particular, the type of network formation model chosen will in general affect the data collection. Here, we would like to mention some general messages:

1.

Asking individuals about the number of links they have allows estimating much more flexible models and should be considered. In particular, it is necessary to the estimation of models in
Graham 2017
and
Boucher and
Mourifié 2017
, and strongly improves the estimation of the model in
Breza et al. 2020
.

2.

Whenever possible, surveys should try obtain
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{Gy}
from the respondents. This could be done by asking questions such as “Which fraction of your friends are X”. When
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{Gy}
are (at least partly) observed, the estimation of the linear in means is less reliant on the simulated draws.
41
41

41

See Proposition
1
of the Online Appendix
D
.
Moreover, coupled with information about the number of links individuals have, these variables act as ARD data.

3.

When researchers only require a random sample of pairs,
Conley and

propose the following sampling strategy: For each respondent, generate a random sample of names from the within-group population and ask the respondent about their linking status with each of the individuals in the sample.

Estimating the asymptotic variance of

Taking the first derivative of the objective function at the second stage (for finite

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\rho}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}

By applying the mean value theorem to

\sqrt{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=-\left[\mathbf{H}_{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\prime}\mathbf{W}_{M}\mathbf{H}_{M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\ast})\right]^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\prime}\mathbf{W}_{M}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}),

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\dfrac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{m}}_{M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\ast}
is some point between
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Let
\displaystyle\textstyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{M}=\Var(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}))

Assumption 12
.

\displaystyle\plim\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{M}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}
and
\displaystyle\plim\mathbf{H}_{M}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})=\mathbf{H}_{0}

Under this assumption, the asymptotic variance of
\displaystyle\sqrt{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})

\mathbb{V}_{0}(\sqrt{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}))=(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0})^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}\mathbf{W}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\prime}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0})^{-1}.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{m}

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{M}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{0}
can be estimated by
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{M}
. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
be the set of simulated networks from the network distribution and the true network. To estimate
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{M}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{M}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbb{V}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})|\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathcal{S}\right)\right\}+\mathbb{V}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})|\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathcal{S}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{m}=\mathbb{V}\left(\mathbf{m}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})|\mathbf{X}_{m},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\kappa(\mathcal{A}_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{V}_{m}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Delta}/M
otherwise. Consider
\displaystyle\|\mathbf{RK}\|_{F}=\sqrt{trace(\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K})}=\sqrt{trace(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\mathbf{R})}
. We have

(1/M^{2})\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=[\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{W}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}}]^{-1}\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}}[\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{W}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}}]^{-1}.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}
and let
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
. We have

(1/M^{2})\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}.

Now, define
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}-(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})^{-1}
. We have

(1/M^{2})\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}+(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{B})^{-1}=\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}+(\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}})^{-1}.

Therefore, we have

(1/M^{2})\|\mathbf{R}\|_{F}=\sqrt{trace(\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}+(\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}})^{-1})}=\sqrt{trace(\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\mathbf{J})+trace((\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}})^{-1})}.

When
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}
, we have that
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{0}
and the Frobenius norm of
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}
is given by
\displaystyle M^{2}\sqrt{trace((\ddot{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\dot{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathbf{S}})^{-1})}
.

F.1
Posterior Distributions for Algorithm
1
.

To compute the posterior distributions, we set prior distributions on
\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, and
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}
, where
\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\log(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha})
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=[\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]
. In Algorithm
1
, we therefore sample
\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}
and compute
\displaystyle\alpha
, such that
\displaystyle\alpha=\dfrac{\exp(\tilde{\alpha})}{1+\exp(\tilde{\alpha})}
. Using this functional form for computing
\displaystyle\alpha
ensures that
\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1)
. The prior distributions are set as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\tilde{\alpha}},\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{\alpha}}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}|\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim IG(\dfrac{a}{2},\dfrac{b}{2}).

For the simulations and estimations in this paper, we set
\displaystyle\mu_{\tilde{\alpha}}=-1
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}_{\tilde{\alpha}}=2
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{100}\boldsymbol{I}_{K}
,
\displaystyle a=4
, and
\displaystyle b=4
, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{K}
is the identity matrix of dimension K and
\displaystyle K=\dim(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
.

Following Algorithm
1
,
\displaystyle\alpha
is updated at each iteration
\displaystyle t
of the MCMC by drawing
\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}^{*}
from the proposal
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\alpha}_{t-1},\xi_{t})
, where the jumping scale
\displaystyle\xi_{t}
is also updated at each
\displaystyle t
following
Atchadé and
Rosenthal 2005
for an acceptance rate of
\displaystyle a^{*}
targeted at 0.44. As the proposal is symmetrical,
\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\dfrac{\exp(\tilde{\alpha}^{*})}{1+\exp(\tilde{\alpha}^{*})}
is accepted with the probability

\min\left\{1,\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{A}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t-1},\alpha^{*})P(\tilde{\alpha}^{*})}{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{A}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})P(\tilde{\alpha_{t}})}\right\}.

The parameters
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}]
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t}
are drawn from their posterior conditional distributions, given as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{A}_{t},\alpha_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}},\sigma^{2}_{t-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{A}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim IG\left(\dfrac{\hat{a}_{t}}{2},\dfrac{\hat{b}_{t}}{2}\right),

where,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{-1},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}\left(\mathbf{V}_{t}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y}-\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{y})+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{a}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a+N,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{b}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=b+(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})+(\boldsymbol{y}-\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{V}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t})^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y}-\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{V}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{t}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{1},\penalty\ \boldsymbol{X},\penalty\ \boldsymbol{G}_{t}\boldsymbol{X}].

F.2
Network Sampling

This section explains how we sample the network in Algorithm
1
using Gibbs sampling. As discussed above, a natural solution is to update only one entry of the adjacency matrix at every step
\displaystyle t
of the MCMC. The entry
\displaystyle(i,j)
is updated according to its conditional posterior distribution. For each entry, however, we need to compute
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}|0,\mathbf{A}_{-ij})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}|1,\mathbf{A}_{-ij})
, which are the respective likelihoods of replacing
\displaystyle a_{ij}
by 0 or by 1. The likelihood computation requires the determinant of
\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G})
, which has a complexity
\displaystyle O(N^{3})
where N is the dimension of
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}
. This implies that we must compute
\displaystyle 2N(N-1)
times
\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G})
to update the adjacency matrix at each step of the MCMC. As
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}
is row-normalized, alternating any off-diagonal entry
\displaystyle(i,j)
in
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}
between
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle 1
perturbs all off-diagonal entries of the row
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G})
. We show that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}
and
\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G})
can be updated by computing a determinant of an auxiliary matrix that requires only updating two entries.

Assume that we want to update the entry
\displaystyle(i,j)
. Let
\displaystyle h
be the function defined in
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle\forall\penalty\ x\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\displaystyle h(x)=x
, and
\displaystyle h(0)=1
. Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}
be an N
\displaystyle\times
N diagonal matrix, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{ii}=h(n_{i})
, and
\displaystyle n_{i}
stands for the degree of
\displaystyle i
, while
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{kk}=1
for all
\displaystyle k\neq i
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}
is the matrix
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}
where the row
\displaystyle i
of
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}
is replaced by the row
\displaystyle i
of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}
. Then, as the determinant is linear in each row, we can obtain
\displaystyle\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G}
by dividing the row
\displaystyle i
of
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W}
by
\displaystyle h(n_{i})
. We get:

\text{det}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G})=\dfrac{1}{h(n_{i})}\text{det}(\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W}).

When
\displaystyle a_{ij}
changes (from
\displaystyle 0
to
\displaystyle 1
, or
\displaystyle 1
to
\displaystyle 0
), note that only the entries
\displaystyle(i,i)
and
\displaystyle(i,j)
change in
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W}
. Two cases can be distinguished.

•

If
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
before the update, then the new degree of
\displaystyle i
will be
\displaystyle n_{i}+1
. Thus, the entry
\displaystyle(i,i)
in
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W}
will change from
\displaystyle h(n_{i})
to
\displaystyle h(n_{i}+1)
(as the diagonal of
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}
equals
\displaystyle 0
), and the entry
\displaystyle(i,j)
will change from
\displaystyle 0
to
\displaystyle-\alpha
. The new determinant is therefore given by

\text{det}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G}^{*})=\dfrac{1}{h(n_{i}+1)}\text{det}(\mathbf{L}^{*}-\alpha\mathbf{W}^{*}),

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{*}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{*}
, and
\displaystyle\alpha\mathbf{W}^{*}
are the new matrices once
\displaystyle a_{ij}
has been updated.

•

If
\displaystyle a_{ij}=1
before the update, then the new degree of
\displaystyle k
will be
\displaystyle n_{i}-1
. Thus, the entry
\displaystyle(i,i)
in
\displaystyle\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W}
will change from
\displaystyle h(n_{i})
to
\displaystyle h(n_{i}-1)
, and the entry
\displaystyle(i,j)
will change from
\displaystyle-\alpha
to
\displaystyle 0
. The new determinant is therefore given by

\text{det}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{G}^{*})=\dfrac{1}{h(n_{i}-1)}\text{det}(\mathbf{L}^{*}-\alpha\mathbf{W}^{*}).

Then, to update
\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathbf{L}-\alpha\mathbf{W})
when only the entries
\displaystyle(i,i)
and
\displaystyle(i,j)
change, we adapt the Lemma 1 in
Hsieh
et al. 2019
as follows:

Proposition 3
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i}
be the
\displaystyle i
’th unit basis vector in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}
denote an N
\displaystyle\times
N matrix and
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{ij}(\mathbf{Q},\epsilon)
an N
\displaystyle\times
N matrix as a function of an N
\displaystyle\times
N matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}
and a real value
\displaystyle\epsilon
, such that

\mathbf{B}_{ij}(\mathbf{Q},\epsilon)=\dfrac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{Q}}{1+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}_{i}}.

(18)

Adding a perturbation
\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}
in the (i, i)th position and a perturbation
\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}
in the (i, j)th position to the matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{M}
can be written as
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{M}+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\prime}+\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}
.

1.

The inverse of the perturbed matrix can be written as

\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{B}_{ii}(\mathbf{M}^{-1},\epsilon_{1})-\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{B}_{ij}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{B}_{ii}(\mathbf{M}^{-1},\epsilon_{1}),\epsilon_{2}\right).

2.

The determinant of the perturbed matrix can be written as

\text{det}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}\right)=\left(1+\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{B}_{ii}(\mathbf{M}^{-1},\epsilon_{1})\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)\right)(1+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i})\text{det}\left(\mathbf{M}\right).

Proof.

1.

By the Sherman–Morrison formula
(
Mele 2017
)
, we have

\left(\mathbf{M}+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\epsilon\dfrac{\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{-1}}{1+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\epsilon\mathbf{B}_{ij}(\mathbf{M},\epsilon).

Thus,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left((\mathbf{M}+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\prime})+\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}-\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{B}_{ij}((\mathbf{M}+\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\prime})^{-1},\epsilon_{2}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}

F.3
How to build prior distributions

The two following examples discuss how to construct prior distributions depending on whether the first stage is estimated by a classical or Bayesian estimator.

Example 4

(Priors from the Asymptotic Distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
)
.

In a classical setting, and under the usual assumptions, the estimation of (
2
) produces an estimator
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
and an estimator of the asymptotic variance of
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{V}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
. In this case, we define the prior density
\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\rho})
as the density of a multivariate normal distribution with mean
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and variance–covariance matrix
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{V}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
.

Example 5

(Priors from the Posterior Distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
)
.

In a Bayesian setting, the estimation of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
from the network formation model (
2
) results in draws from the posterior distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
. It is therefore natural to use such a posterior distribution as the prior distribution of
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}
for the estimation based on (
5
). Performing such a sequential Bayesian updating approach comes with a well-known numerical issue.
50
50

50

See
Thijssen and
Wessels 2020
for a recent discussion.

Indeed, the evaluation of the acceptance ratio in Step 1 of Algorithm
1
below requires the evaluation of the density of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
at different values. Ideally, one would use the draws from the posterior distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
from the first step (network formation model) and perform a nonparametric kernel density estimation of the posterior distribution. However, when the dimension of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
is large, the kernel density estimation may be infeasible in practice.

This is especially true for very flexible network formation models, such as that proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
for which the number of parameters to estimate is
\displaystyle O(N_{m})
. In such a case, it might be more reasonable to use a more parametric approach or to impose additional restrictions on the dependence structure of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
across dimensions.
51
51

51

For example, if we assume that the posterior distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
is jointly normal, the estimation of the mean and variance-covariance matrix is straightforward, even in a high-dimensional setting. Simulations suggest that this approach performs well in practice. See the Vignette accompanying our R package.

Appendix G
Application

G.1
Error codes only

Each student nominates their best friends up to 5 males and 5 females. Because we know the sex of nominated friends, even when the identifier is coded with errors, we associate each missing link to a male or female student. We then have two sets of network data for each student
\displaystyle i
: the set of data from
\displaystyle i
to their male schoolmates and the set of data from
\displaystyle i
to their female schoolmates. A set is considered fully observed if it has no missing values. We estimate the network formation only using the fully observed sets. The sets with partial or no observed data are inferred (even the data we do not doubt in those sets are inferred).

This approach raises a selection problem that we address by weighting each selected set, following
Manski and
Lerman 1977
. The intuition of the weights lies in the fact that the sets with many links have lower probabilities to be selected (because error codes are more likely). The weight is the inverse of the selection probability. For a selected set
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
(of network data from
\displaystyle i
to schoolmates of sex
\displaystyle s
), the selection probability can be estimated as the proportion of
sets without missing data among the sets of network data to schoolmates of sex
\displaystyle s
having the same number of links than
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
.

For the Bayesian estimator, we jointly estimate the peer effect model and the network formation model (i.e., using Step 1’ on Page 28). Thus, in the MCMC,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
and the sets with partial or no network data are inferred using information from the weighted sets and the peer effect model.

G.2
Error codes and top coding

We consider the same selected sets as in the case of missing data only. However, we doubt the exactitude of a link
\displaystyle a_{ij}\in\mathcal{S}_{is}
if
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
and the number of links in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
is five. Therefore, if the number of links in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
is five, we adjust the weight associated with each
\displaystyle a_{ij}
. For
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
, we multiply the weight obtained in the case of missing data only by
\displaystyle(\lvert\mathcal{S}_{is}\rvert-\ell(\mathcal{S}_{is}))/(\lvert\mathcal{S}_{is}\rvert-\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{is}))
, and for
\displaystyle a_{ij}=1
, we multiply the weight by
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{is})/\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{is})
, where
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{is})
is the estimate of the true number of links from
\displaystyle i
to their schoolmates of sex
\displaystyle s
,
\displaystyle\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{is})
if the number of links declared in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
, and
\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{S}_{is}\rvert
is the number of data in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{is}
(number of students having the sex
\displaystyle s
in the school minus one).

We denote
\displaystyle s=m
for males and
\displaystyle s=f
for females. Fours scenarios are possible:
\displaystyle\{\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{im})<5,\penalty\ \hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{if})<5\}
,
\displaystyle\{\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{im})=5,\penalty\ \hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{if})<5\}
,
\displaystyle\{\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{im})<5,\penalty\ \hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{if})=5\}
, and
\displaystyle\{\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{im})=5,\penalty\ \hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{if})=5\}
. In the last three cases,
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})+\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})
is left-censored and we know the lower bound. Assuming that the number of links
\displaystyle i
follows a Poisson distribution of mean
\displaystyle n_{i}^{e}
, we estimate
\displaystyle n_{i}^{e}
using a censored Poisson regression on the declared number of links. We assume that
\displaystyle n_{i}^{e}
is an exponential linear function of
\displaystyle i
’s characteristics (age, sex, …), and we also include school-fixed effects to control for school size.

The estimate of
\displaystyle n_{i}^{e}
is
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})+\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})
, and it allows us to compute
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})
and
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})
. For the case
\displaystyle\{\hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{im})=5,\penalty\ \hat{\ell}(\mathcal{S}_{if})=5\}
, we assume that
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})=\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})=0.5(\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})+\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if}))
. In the other cases, as either
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})
or
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})
is known, the second member of
\displaystyle\ell(\mathcal{S}_{im})+\ell(\mathcal{S}_{if})
can be computed.

For the Bayesian estimator, and contrary to the case with error codes only, it is more challenging to jointly estimate the peer effect model and the network formation model in a single step. We therefore first estimate the network formation model and then the Bayesian estimator (i.e., using Step 1 in Algorithm
1
).
Thus, for the MCMC, the estimated distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
from the network formation model is used as a prior distribution. We then infer
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
and the network data
\displaystyle a_{ij}=0
that we are doubtful about, using information from the peer effect model and the prior distribution of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}
.

G.4
Key Player

Figure
G.1
shows a scatter plot of the vector of centralities in the observed and reconstructed networks. The figure illustrates the effects of missing network data. First, because the reconstructed network has more links, centrality is higher on average. This is essentially the social multiplier effect. Not accounting for missing links leads to an underestimation of spillover effects.
Second, some individuals, in particular those having very few links in the observed network, are in reality highly central. Therefore, targeting a policy at individuals having a high centrality in the observed network would be inefficient. In particular, Figure
G.2
shows that even isolated individuals and individuals interacting in isolated pairs in the observed network (having centralities of
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle 1.35
respectively) can be, in reality, highly central. Thus, a policy based on the evaluation of an observed network, coupled with the associated endogenous peer effect coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha
, would not only underestimate the social multiplier but would also target the wrong individuals.

Appendix H
Technical Appendix: Aggregated Relational Data

This section provides details about ARD simulation and model estimation using a MCMC method. We simulate the network for a population of 5000 individuals divided into
\displaystyle m=20
groups of
\displaystyle n=250
individuals. Within each group, the probability of a link is

P(a_{ij}=1)\propto\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\}.

(20)

As there is no connection between the groups, the networks are simulated and estimated independently. We first present how we simulate the data following the model (
22
).

H.1
ARD Simulation

The parameters are defined as follows:
\displaystyle\zeta=1.5
,
\displaystyle\penalty\ \nu_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(-1.25,0.37)
, and the
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}
are distributed uniformly according to a von Mises–Fisher distribution. We use a hypersphere of dimension 3. We set the same values for the parameter for the 20 groups. We generate the probabilities of links in each network following
Breza et al. 2020
.

P(a_{ij}=1|\nu_{i},\nu_{j},\zeta,\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})=\frac{\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}}{\sum_{ij}\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\}},

(21)

where
\displaystyle d_{i}
is the degree defined by
\displaystyle d_{i}\approx\frac{C_{p}(0)}{C_{p}(\zeta)}\exp{(\nu_{i})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\nu_{i})
, and the function
\displaystyle C_{p}(.)
is the normalization constant in the von Mises–Fisher distribution density function. After computing the probability of a link for any pair in the population, we sample the entries of the adjacency matrix using a Bernoulli distribution with probability (
21
).

To generate the ARD, we require the “traits” (e.g., cities) for each individual. We set
\displaystyle K=12
traits on the hypersphere. Their location
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
is distributed uniformly according to the von Mises-Fisher distribution. The individuals having the trait
\displaystyle k
are assumed to be generated by a von Mises–Fisher distribution with the location parameter
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
and the intensity parameter
\displaystyle\eta_{k}\sim|\mathcal{N}(4,1)|
,
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,12
.

We attribute traits to individuals given their spherical coordinates. We first define
\displaystyle N_{k}
, the number of individuals having the trait
\displaystyle k
:

N_{k}=\Bigg\lfloor r_{k}\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{k},\eta_{k})}{\max_{i}f_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{k},\eta_{k})}\Bigg\rfloor,

where
\displaystyle\lfloor x\rfloor
represents the greatest integer less than or equal to
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle r_{k}
is a random number uniformly distributed over
\displaystyle(0.8;0.95)
, and
\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{k},\eta_{k})
is the von Mises–Fisher distribution density function evaluated at
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}
with the location parameter
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
and the intensity parameter
\displaystyle\eta_{k}
.

The intuition behind this definition for
\displaystyle N_{k}
is that when many
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}
are close to
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
, many individuals should have the trait
\displaystyle k
.

We can finally attribute trait
\displaystyle k
to individual
\displaystyle i
by sampling a Bernoulli distribution with the probability
\displaystyle f_{ik}
given by

f_{ik}=N_{k}\dfrac{f_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{k},\eta_{k})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{k},\eta_{k})}.

The probability of having a trait depends on the proximity of the individuals to the trait’s location on the hypersphere.

H.2
Model Estimation

In practice, we only have the ARD and the traits of each individual.
McCormick and
Zheng 2015
propose an MCMC approach to infer the parameters in the model (
20
).

However, the spherical coordinates and the degrees in this model are not identified. The authors solve this issue by fixing some
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
and use the fixed positions to rotate the latent surface back to a common orientation at each iteration of the MCMC using a Procrustes transformation. In addition, the total size of a subset
\displaystyle b_{k}
is constrained in the MCMC.

As discussed by
McCormick and
Zheng 2015
, the number of
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}
and
\displaystyle b_{k}
to be set as fixed depends on the dimensions of the hypersphere. In our simulations,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}
, …,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{5}
are set as fixed to rotate back the latent space. When simulating the data, we let
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}=(0,1,0)
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{3}=(0,0,1)
. This ensures that the fixed positions on the hypersphere are spaced, as suggested by the authors, to use as much of the space as possible, maximizing the distance between the estimated positions. We also constrain
\displaystyle b_{3}
to its true value. The results do not change when we constrain a larger set of
\displaystyle b_{k}

Following
Breza et al. 2020
, we estimate the link probabilities using the parameters’ posterior distributions. The gregariousness parameters are computed from the degrees
\displaystyle d_{i}
and the parameter
\displaystyle\zeta
using the following equation:

\nu_{i}=\log(d_{i})-\log\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(\frac{C_{p}(\zeta)}{C_{p}(0)}\Big).

H.3
Finite Sample Performance Using ARD

In this section, we study the small sample performance of the estimator presented in Section
3
when the researcher only has access to ARD. First, we simulate network data using the model proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
and simulate outcomes using the linear-in-means model (
1
) conditional on the simulated networks. Second, we estimate the network formation model using the Bayesian estimator proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
(yielding
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{B}
) and using the classical estimator proposed by
Alidaee
et al. 2020
(yielding
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}
). Third, we estimate the linear-in-means model using the estimators presented in Proposition
2
and Theorem
1
based on
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{B}
.

Recall that

P(a_{ij}=1)\propto\exp\{\nu_{i}+\nu_{j}+\zeta\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{z}_{j}\},

(22)

where
\displaystyle\nu_{i}
,
\displaystyle\nu_{j}
,
\displaystyle\zeta
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{j}
are not observed by the econometrician but follow parametric distributions. We refer the interested reader to
McCormick and
Zheng 2015
,
Breza et al. 2020
, and
Breza et al. 2023
for a formal discussion of the model, including its identification and consistent estimation.

To study the finite sample performance of our instrumental strategy in this context, we simulate 20 groups, each having 250 individuals. Within each subpopulation, we simulate the ARD responses and a series of observable characteristics. The details of the Monte Carlo simulations can be found below in the Online Appendix
H
.

Importantly, the model in (
22
) is based on a single population framework. Thus, the network formation model must be estimated separately for each of the 20 groups. With only 250 individuals in each group, we therefore expect significant small-sample bias.

We contrast the estimator proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
with that of
Alidaee
et al. 2020
. Whereas
Breza et al. 2020
present a parametric Bayesian estimator,
Alidaee
et al. 2020
propose a (nonparametric) penalized regression based on a low-rank assumption. One main advantage of the estimator proposed in
Alidaee
et al. 2020
is that it allows for a wider class of model and ensures that the estimation is fast and easily implementable.
52
52

52

The authors developed user-friendly packages in R and Python. See
Alidaee
et al. 2020
for links and details.
Note, however, that their method only yields a consistent estimator of
\displaystyle\hat{P}(\mathbf{A})
if the true network is effectively low rank.

Very intuitively, the low-rank assumption implies that linking probabilities were generated from a small number of parameters. Importantly, the model (
22
) is not necessarily low rank; for example, if the individuals’ latent positions (i.e., the
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i}
’s) are uniformly distributed, then the model may not be low rank and the method proposed by
Alidaee
et al. 2020
would perform poorly. If, however, individuals’ latent positions are located around a few focal points, then the model might be low-rank because knowledge of these focal points may have high predictive power.

We compare the performance of both estimators as we vary the concentration parameter (that is,
\displaystyle\kappa
; see below in the Online Appendix
H
for details). This has the effect of changing the
effective rank
of the linking probabilities: increasing
\displaystyle\kappa
decreases the effective rank.
53
53

53

We refer the interested reader to
Alidaee
et al. 2020
for a formal discussion of the effective rank and its importance for their estimator.
We therefore expect the estimator proposed by
Alidaee
et al. 2020
to perform better for larger values of
\displaystyle\kappa
.

The results are presented in Tables
H.1
and
H.2
. Table
H.1
presents the results for the special case where
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
are observed in the data. The table displays the performance of our simulated GMM (see Corollary
1
) when the network formation model is estimated by
Breza et al. 2020
and
Alidaee
et al. 2020
.

When
\displaystyle\kappa=0
, the network formation is not low rank. This disproportionately affects the estimator of
Alidaee
et al. 2020
. When
\displaystyle\kappa=15
, the estimators proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
and
Alidaee
et al. 2020
perform similarly.

We now turn to the more general case where
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
are not observed. Table
H.2
presents the performance of our SGMM estimator (Theorem
1
) when the network formation process is estimated using the estimators proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
and
Alidaee
et al. 2020
and when we assume that the researcher knows the true distribution of the network.

We see that the performance of our estimator is strongly affected by the quality of the first-stage network formation estimator. When based on either the estimator proposed by
Breza et al. 2020
or
Alidaee
et al. 2020
, for
\displaystyle\kappa=0
or
\displaystyle\kappa=15
, our SGMM estimator performs poorly.

The poor performance of our SGMM estimator in a context where both
\displaystyle\mathbf{Gy}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
are unobserved was anticipated. This occurs for two main reasons.
First
, the consistency of the network formation estimator in
Breza et al. 2023
holds as the size of each subpopulation goes to infinity, whereas the consistency of our estimator holds as the number of (bounded) subpopulations goes to infinity. This should affect the performance of our estimator, when based on
estimated
network formation models but not when based on the true distribution of the network.

Second
, as discussed in
B.1.6
, ARD provides very little information about the realized network structure in the data (as opposed to censoring issues, for example; see Example
2
). Then, if the true distribution is vague in the sense that most predicted probabilities are away from
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
, we expect the estimation to be imprecise. This is what happens when
\displaystyle\kappa=15
, where our estimation based on the true distribution of the network is very imprecise in a context where the network affects the outcome through both
\displaystyle\mathbf{Gy}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{GX}
.

In the next section, we present a likelihood-based estimator, which uses more information on the data-generating process of the outcome to improve the precision of the estimation.
