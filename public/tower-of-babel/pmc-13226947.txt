Protective effect of Chlorogenic Acid complex against intestinal oxidative stress in broilers and screening of its key isomer
Authors: Hong Yao, Shourong Shi, Ruqian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress triggers physiological and pathological damage in animals. As a natural polyphenol, chlorogenic acid (CGA) is a potential candidate for alleviating such damage, while discrepancies in antioxidant activity among its isomers and underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to clarify the protective effects of the CGA complex on broiler intestinal oxidative stress and identify the key active isomers by integrating in vivo animal models, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro antioxidant assays. In vivo, the CGA complex effectively reversed DSS-induced reductions in broiler body weight, slaughter performance, and alterations in jejunal glutathione peroxidase (GSH-px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities (P < 0.05). Network pharmacology analysis identified 4,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid (4,5-CQA) as a key isomer (Betweenness 1549.69), and molecular docking results showed that it can stably bind to the core targets CASP3, SRC and MMP9. 4-CQA exhibited stronger free radical scavenging activity in ABTS/DPPH/FRAP assays (P < 0.05); 4,5-CQA displayed superior cytoprotection, enhanced Nrf2, HO-1 protein expression (P < 0.05) and inhibited Caspase-3 protein expression (P < 0.05) in tBHP-stimulated intestinal epithelial cells. In conclusion, the CGA effectively mitigates DSS-induced intestinal oxidative stress in broilers via regulating multiple signaling pathways, and 4,5-CQA serves as the pivotal bioactive isomer in complex physiological processes.Keywords: Chlorogenic acid isomers; Oxidative stress; Network pharmacology; 4,5-CQA

Introduction

As the primary barrier against exogenous stimuli, the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal tract is pivotal for the overall health and production efficiency of broilers. Intestinal stress is frequently accompanied by excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which initiates oxidative stress cascades (Ngo and Duennwald, 2022). This process not only impairs intestinal barrier integrity, disrupts gut microbiota homeostasis, and reduces nutrient absorption efficiency, but also further induces intestinal inflammation, inhibits growth performance, and elevates mortality rates (Liu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). Therefore, alleviating intestinal oxidative stress has become a key focus in the research of broiler nutrition and health research.

In recent years, polyphenolic compounds derived from natural plants have attracted significant attention in antioxidant research due to their high free radical scavenging capacity (Rana et al., 2022). A large number of studies have found that supplementing chlorogenic acid can effectively prevent and alleviate brain damage, kidney damage, and liver damage caused by oxidative stress (Jiao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2022). Chlorogenic acid is widely distributed in higher dicotyledonous plants and ferns, with relatively high contents detected in honeysuckle (Xiong et al., 2014), Eucommia ulmoides leaves (Li et al., 2019), sunflower seeds (Scharlack et al., 2017) and other plant materials. Chlorogenic acid and its isomers exert anti-inflammatory effects by scavenging intracellular ROS, inhibiting p38 cascade phosphorylation, and upregulating the NF-κB signaling pathway (Liang and Kitts, 2018; Shin et al., 2017). Up to now, six main isomers have been identified, including 3-caffeoylquinic acid, 4-caffeoylquinic acid, 5-caffeoylquinic acid, and three dicaffeoylquinic acid derivatives (Meinhart et al., 2017; Naveed et al., 2018). In broiler chicken farming, chlorogenic acid has been found to alleviate oxidative stress, inhibit inflammatory factors, and mitigate intestinal damage, among other effects (Song et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020). However, due to differences in molecular spatial structure, these isomers exhibit distinct variations in bioactivity, bioavailability, and mechanism of action (Liang et al., 2019).

Notably, most existing studies have focused on CGA total extracts, lacking systematic identification and validation of the key active isomers responsible for antioxidant and intestinal stress-alleviating effects. The ambiguity regarding which specific isomers play core roles not only hinders the elucidation of structure-activity relationships but also limits the development of high-efficiency, targeted feed additives. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the protective effect of the chlorogenic acid complex on DSS-induced intestinal oxidative stress in broilers, identify the key active isomers via network pharmacology and molecular docking, and verify the antioxidant capacity of core isomers using in vitro assays.

Materials and methods

Birds and experimental design

A total of 288 healthy one-day-old Arbor Acres (AA) broiler chicks with similar initial body weights were purchased from Jinghai Poultry Industry Co., Ltd., (Jiangsu, Chia). All chicks were randomly allocated into three groups with 8 replicates per group and 12 birds per replicate. The feeding trial lasted from day 1 to day 42. The control group (CON) and DSS model group were fed a basal diet, while the DSS+CGA group received the basal diet supplemented with 350 g/t stevia chlorogenic acid complex (CGA). The CGA was provided by Zhucheng Haotian Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., (Shandong, China) containing 21% total chlorogenic acid, including 2.13% 3-CQA, 4.07% 4-CQA, 14.44% 5-CQA, 0.12% 3,5-CQA, 0.07% 3,4-CQA, and 0.17% 4,5-CQA.

For the DSS challenge procedure: starting at 9:00 on day 14, the CON group was supplied with regular drinking water, whereas the DSS and DSS+CGA groups were given drinking water containing 0.35 mg/mL DSS (MP Bio, Cat. No. 0216011080) for 7 consecutive days. From 9:00 on day 21, all three groups were provided with regular drinking water for another 7 days. A second DSS challenge was performed starting at 9:00 on day 28 with the same treatment protocol as day 14 for 7 consecutive days. All groups were returned to regular drinking water from 9:00 on day 35 until the end of the trial on day 42.

The broilers were reared on floor litter throughout the experiment. All management indicators during the trial were performed in accordance with the Hygienic Requirements for Broiler Production (GB14925-1994). Temperature and humidity were adjusted dynamically according to the actual growth status of the broilers. The chickens were vaccinated following routine procedures, and other management practices were consistent with standard rearing protocols. Ad libitum access to feed and water was provided during the entire experimental period. The composition and nutritional levels of the diets are presented in Table 1. The diets were formulated and pelleted with reference to the standards of NRC (1994) and the Industry Standard of the People's Republic of China (NY/T 33-2004).

Slaughter performance and meat quality

On day 42 of the experiment, broilers in each group were weighed. For meat production performance, indices including carcass weight, dressing percentage, semi-eviscerated weight, eviscerated weight, leg muscle percentage and breast muscle percentage were determined in accordance with the national standard NY/T 823-2020. Breast muscles were dissected separately for the determination of meat quality traits (meat color, drip loss, shear force and pH value). The remaining tissues and organs were weighed to calculate the organ indices.

Antioxidant indices

At the end of the dietary treatment, the wing vein blood samples (approximately 2 mL) from 24 birds (one bird from each replicate, eight birds per group) were placed into tubes containing EDTA-Na2 (an anticoagulant). Blood samples were collected from the wing vein and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain plasma. Jejunal tissue samples (1 g segments from the mid-jejunum) were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (1:9, w/v) and centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 10 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. A kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) was used to measure the levels of antioxidant indices in plasma and jejunal tissues, including malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015-2-1), superoxide dismutase activity (SOD, A001-3), glutathione peroxidase activity (GSH-Px, A005-1), and catalase activity (CAT, A007-1-1).

Network pharmacology analysis and molecular docking

The Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology database (TCMSP) was used for the determination of all active ingredients contained in CQA (Ru et al., 2014). The data of existing components of CQA were screened out from TCMSP. The SMILES notations of the main components were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and then submitted to the Swiss Target Prediction (www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) platform to predict the potential targets of active components (Daina et al., 2019; Gfeller et al., 2013). The obtained targets were de-duplicated and then used in the following data processing step.

Collection of Potential Target Genes for Oxidative Stress

With “oxidative stress” as the keywords, the oxidative stress related targets were predicted in the disease databases DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org), Gene Card (https://www.genecards.org). Entries with a score ≥ 5 in the Gene Cards search results were retained. Combine databases to obtain all targets related to oxidative stress.

Target Organization of Chlorogenic Acid in Alleviating Oxidative Stress

Upload the obtained CQA target set and oxidative stress related target gene set to the bioinformatics mapping platform (Bardou et al., 2014) (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). Venn diagrams were drawn using the two gene sets to obtain the intersecting genes, namely the target set of CQA for alleviating oxidative stress. Construct a Network file containing the genes targeted by each of the major components of CQA. Additionally, create a Type file containing the target genes associated with CQA alleviation of oxidative stress. Import the file into Cytoscape 3.10 to build an interaction network.

Adjust the correlation strength based on the centrality measures and use the CytoNCA plugin to calculate the centrality of the major component nodes. Four centrality indicators were used to assess network nodes: degree for direct interaction strength, betweenness for transit hub and pathway crosstalk, closeness for global signal transmission efficiency, and eigenvector for high-level core interactions. In this component-target network analysis, their screening priority was defined as Betweenness > Eigenvector > Degree > Closeness, to accurately identify hub nodes and key functional molecules underlying the bioactive effects.

Construction of protein-protein interaction network

All the common targets were retrieved in the STRING (https://string-db.org/) database to obtain the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, with the species set as “Gallus gallus” and the “minimum required interaction score” set at 0.4. The visualization of the PPI network and topology analysis were performed via Cytoscape 3.10.

GO and KEGG pathway analyses

Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database (https://fsabcl-david01p.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) (Aggarwal et al., 2024). The gene set associated with CQA-mediated alleviation of oxidative stress was uploaded, and the species was specified as “Gallus gallus” for analysis. Additionally, the KEGG pathway file was retrieved from the pathway analysis results. The downloaded files were then processed and filtered for further visualization.

Molecular docking verification

The chemical structures of key CQA isomers were obtained from the PubChem database, while the PDB structures of core target proteins were retrieved from the UniProt database. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina, and the resulting docking conformations were visualized and optimized using PyMOL.

Evaluation of in vitro antioxidant capacity

DPPH method

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity was determined by the method described by Locatelli et al. (2009) with slight modifications. For the DPPH radical assay, 0.5 mL sample extract or standard (ascorbic acid) with varying concentrations (5, 15, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150 μg/mL, Haotian Pharm CO., LTD, Zhucheng, DF191104-10, China) were added to the same volume of DPPH radical methanolic solution (60 μM, Aladdin, D141336, Shanghai, China). Mixtures were shaken vigorously and left to incubate for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (infinite M200 Pro, TECAN, Switzerland). ABTS method

ABTS radical scavenging activity was determined according to the method described by Li et al. (2008) with some modifications. The ABTS + stock solution was produced by reacting ABTST aqueous (7 mM, Aladdin, A109612, Shanghai, China) with 2.45 mM aqueous solution of potassium persulfate in equal quantities and allowed them to react for 16 h at room temperature in the dark. Then, 990 μL of ABTS radical + solution was mixed with 10 μL of the aqueous extract or standard (ascorbic acid) at different concentrations (5 - 150 μg/mL). The mixture was then incubated at room temperature for exactly 5 min in the dark. The control was prepared by mixing 990 μL of ABTS radical + solution with 10 μL double distilled water. The absorbance measured for the control solution was in the range of 0.7±0.02. The percentage results of scavenging activity were calculated as % inhibition using above Eq. (1).

FRAP method

The FRAP (Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power) solution (Sigma, T1253, America) was prepared by mixing three components as follows: 1) Solution A: 300 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 3.6); 2) Solution B: 10 mM TPTZ (2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine) dissolved in 40 mM hydrochloric acid; 3) Solution C: 20 mM FeCl₃ prepared in ultrapure water. The FRAP working solution was freshly prepared by combining Solutions A, B, and C at a volume ratio of 10:1:1, respectively. The mixture was incubated at 37°C prior to use. For the assay, a reaction mixture was prepared by mixing 3.1 mL of ultrapure water, 1.8 mL of the FRAP working solution, and 0.1 mL of the test sample. The absorbance was measured at 593 nm. A standard curve was constructed using ascorbic acid solutions at concentrations of 5, 15, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 150 μg/mL. The reducing capacity of the samples was calculated based on the linear regression equation derived from the standard curve.

Cell culture

Chicken embryos at embryonic day 18 (E18) were used for the isolation of primary intestinal epithelial cells. The jejunum was dissected and placed in pre-cooled DHank’s solution. Intestinal contents were gently removed using forceps until the intestinal segments appeared translucent. The tissue suspension was centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 8 min, and the pellet was collected and incubated with 20 mL of type I collagenase (1 mg/mL) for enzymatic digestion at 37°C for 20 min using a thermostatic magnetic stirrer. The digested suspension was then filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer. The isolated cells were counted and seeded onto cell culture dishes pre-coated with type I rat tail collagen. Cells were plated in 6-well or 96-well plates, with or without CQA (3-CQA, 4-CQA, 5-CQA, 3,5-CQA, 3,4-CQA, 4,5-CQA, 200 μM) pre-treatment for 12 h, and stimulated for 1 h with tert‑butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP, 180 nM, Sigma, America).

Cell viability and antioxidant capacity

Then the relative cell viabilities were detected by the cell counting kit-8 assay (CCK8, Abbkine, Wuhan, China). For detecting ROS by fluorescence-based methods, a fluorescence-based DCFH-DA (10 μM, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) detecting probe is incubated with the cells for 30 min, flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta, America) measurement provides dynamic the fluorescence of the reaction product is detected to determine the relative amounts of ROS. Collect 6-well plate cells and add 400 μL of PBS to each well. After ultrasonic disruption, measure the levels of T-AOC, SOD, MDA, GSH-px, and total protein in the cell lysate were quantified using kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using lysis buffer, separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody and development to complete protein detection. Primary antibody against β-actin (bs-0061R) was purchased from Bioss (Beijing, China). Primary antibodies against α-Tubulin (11224), HO-1 (10701), SOD (10269), NrF2 (16396) and Caspase 3 (19677) were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inv., Chicago, IL), employing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons between the control group and various treatment groups. LSD test was used for post hoc comparisons. All data are presented as the means with pooled SEM values. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Antioxidant properties

Compared with the CON group, DSS treatment significantly decreased catalase (CAT) activity in the jejunum (Fig. 1F, P < 0.05). Conversely, both DSS and DSS+CGA groups exhibited significantly elevated GSH-Px activity in the jejunum (Fig. 1G, P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected in MDA content in either plasma or jejunum following DSS treatment (Fig. 1C and H, P > 0.05). DSS treatment also led to a marked reduction in SOD activity in the jejunum (Fig. 1I, P < 0.05), whereas SOD activity in both plasma and jejunum did not differ significantly between the DSS+CGA group and CON group (Fig. 1I and D, P > 0.05).

Potential targets of compound therapy for oxidative stress

The retrieved results were integrated to obtain the oxidative stress associated targets from DisGeNET, and Gene Cards, which are 1,078 targets. We used the potential targets and oxidative stress related targets to construct a Venn diagram and obtained 66 common targets (Fig. 2A). A topological analysis diagram was drawn based on the betweenness centrality, where the node size is proportional to the betweenness centrality of the components (Fig. 2B). The components with the highest intermediate centrality in the intersection network between chlorogenic acid and the target core were 4,5-CQA and 3-CQA (Table 5). We then obtained the PPI network of the targets obtained using the STRING database and Cytoscape 3.10 (Fig. 2C-E). PPI analysis showed apoptosis-related cysteine aspartic acid protease (CASP3), nonreceptor tyrosine kinase (SRC), matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) occupy the core position in the PPI network and are considered hub genes of chlorogenic acid against oxidative stress.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of active components of CQA intervention oxidative stress related targets was carried out with DAVID, limiting the species to Gallus gallus, and the results were visualized. We obtained 258 GO function enrichment entries, including130 BP, 30 CC, and 98 MF terms in total. The top 10 terms in BP, CC and MF were selected and plotted in the column chart (Fig. 2G). BP mainly involve protein phosphorylation and proteolysis. In the CC category, the target proteins were classified into cytoplasm, cytosol, and extracellular space. In the MF category, the target proteins were mainly involved in ATP binding, cysteine-type endopeptidase activity.

KEGG enrichment analysis of the target gene set indicated that these genes were mainly enriched in the C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, apoptosis pathway and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the relevant targets of chlorogenic acid against oxidative stress and KEGG enrichment data were integrated and imported into Cytoscape software, to construct a visualized regulatory network model of chlorogenic acid‑oxidative stress target‑signaling pathway (Fig. 2F).

Core gene cluster

Using the MCODE algorithm in Cytoscape, we identified two major gene clusters and constructed their interaction networks. Functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct biological roles for each cluster, cluster 1 (Fig. 2D) was significantly enriched in processes including protein phosphorylation, positive regulation of cell migration, and cell migration (Table 6). Cluster 2 (Fig. 2E) primarily participated in regulation of cell communication, ERBB signaling pathway, and ERBB2-EGFR signaling pathway (Table 6).

Molecular docking analysis

The top three core targets (CASP3 PDB ID:1RHQ, SRC PDB ID: 3QLF, MMP9 PDB ID: 5TH6) were selected and docked with the top one core component (4,5-CQA). It is generally believed that a binding free energy < −5.0 kcal/mol indicates good binding activity with the target (Hsin et al., 2013). Visualization of molecular docking results is shown in Fig. 3A-D, displaying the binding mode of 4,5-CQA to the receptor. The docking results showed that the binding energy of 4,5-CQA and CASP3 protein (−9.2) was the lowest (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the amino acid residues of the target protein mainly form hydrogen bonds with the phenolic hydroxyl group at the C1 position of the A ring in 4,5-CQA.

Antioxidant capacity of chlorogenic acid

The DPPH radical scavenging capacities of different chlorogenic acid isomers increased with rising concentration and reached saturation at 75 μg/mL. Within the concentration range of 5-25 μg/mL, 4-CQA exhibited significantly higher DPPH scavenging activity than the other five isomers (Fig. 4A-H, P < 0.05). The ABTS radical scavenging ability of all isomers showed a concentration-dependent increasing trend, and 4-CQA maintained a markedly stronger effect throughout 5-150 μg/mL (Fig. 4I-P, P < 0.05). Similarly, the FRAP ferric ion reducing capacity increased in a dose-dependent manner. In the range of 15-150 μg/mL, 4-CQA displayed significantly superior ferric reduction activity compared with other chlorogenic acid isomers (Fig. 4Q-W, P < 0.05).

Effect of chlorogenic acid on cellular oxidative damage

Following tBHP-induced oxidative stress, cell viability was markedly reduced. All six chlorogenic acid isomers effectively alleviated cell damage, with 4,5-CQA exhibiting the most prominent protective effect (Fig. 5A, P < 0.05). 5-CQA, 3,5-CQA and 3,4-CQA significantly inhibited the excessive accumulation of intracellular ROS triggered by tBHP (Fig. 5B-C, P < 0.05). Meanwhile, 4,5-CQA distinctly increased the T-AOC level in intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 5D, P < 0.05).

Discussions

Intestinal oxidative stress induced by DSS disrupts the balance of antioxidant defense systems in broilers, leading to intestinal oxidative damage and reduced slaughter performance. Previous studies have shown that dietary chlorogenic acid (CGA) supplementation promotes muscle growth and improves meat quality in finishing pigs (Wang et al., 2021), while in broilers, it enhances antioxidant capacity by upregulating antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby effectively improving growth performance and meat quality (Chen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Consistent with the findings of Wang et al. (2021) in finishing pigs, the CGA complex in the present study improved broiler slaughter performance by increasing leg muscle percentage, which is likely associated with its ability to protect against muscle oxidative damage and promote muscle growth. Additionally, as an important immune organ involved in the body’s defense against oxidative stress and inflammation, the significant increase in spleen weight following CGA complex treatment suggests that it may enhance immune function by promoting spleen development, which is conducive to alleviating DSS-induced intestinal oxidative damage.

DSS has been validated to induce intestinal inflammation in chickens, and excessive inflammation triggers massive production of inflammatory mediators and ROS (Dal Pont et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). In this study, DSS challenge significantly decreased jejunal CAT and SOD activities, which are key enzymes involved in ROS scavenging, while increasing GSH-Px activity as a compensatory response. Dietary CGA complex effectively restored CAT and SOD activities, suggesting its capacity to strengthen antioxidant defense and relieve oxidative damage, which supports previous reports that CGA elevates antioxidant enzyme activities in broilers (Zha et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2018) demonstrated that 1,000 mg/kg chlorogenic acid significantly reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the jejunal and duodenal mucosa of weaned piglets, upregulated CAT and GSH-Px activities, and consequently improved intestinal antioxidant function and barrier integrity.

Network pharmacology is an effective approach to screen bioactive components and reveal the underlying mechanisms of natural products. Topological analysis identified 4,5-CQA as the core isomer with the highest betweenness centrality, indicating its pivotal role in regulating the oxidative stress-related target network. Previous research demonstrated that dicaffeoylquinic acid isomers exert stronger antioxidant potential than monocaffeoylquinic acid isomers via direct free radical scavenging (Liang et al., 2019) . PPI network analysis screened CASP3, SRC, MMP9 and EGFR as hub genes closely involved in oxidative stress and apoptosis. CASP3 acts as a core executive protease in apoptotic progression; its activation initiates downstream apoptotic cascades (Beroske et al., 2021) . Activated Caspase-3 further mediates EndoG nuclear translocation, promotes SRC/STAT3 phosphorylation, and drives malignant transformation (Zhu et al., 2024) . In addition, inhibition of the SRC-mediated NF-κB p65 and MAPK pathways can attenuate renal inflammation and apoptosis(Ren et al., 2020).

GO and KEGG enrichment showed that target genes were predominantly enriched in C-type lectin receptor, apoptosis, and MAPK signaling pathways, all critical for modulating oxidative stress and inflammation. The C-type lectin receptor pathway regulates immune and inflammatory responses; its activation promotes inflammatory factor secretion, excessive ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation, and aggravates oxidative damage (Han et al., 2025) . This pathway also maintains oxidative homeostasis by modulating Nrf2-related antioxidant signaling (Srivastava et al., 2024). Oxidative stress initiates apoptosis via ATP depletion, impaired apoptotic cell clearance, and activation of pro-apoptotic Caspase-3 (Anderson et al., 2002) . Excessive ROS upregulates Bax and downregulates Bcl-2 to accelerate apoptosis, while dysregulated apoptosis further exacerbates oxidative injury (Abbas et al., 2022) . The ROS-sensitive MAPK pathway, including ERK, p38 and JNK subfamilies, modulates cell proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis (Huang et al., 2024) . Notably, chlorogenic acid can alleviate intestinal oxidative stress through suppressing the MAPK signaling pathway (Gao et al., 2019).

In vitro DPPH, ABTS and FRAP assays showed that the antioxidant capacity of CGA isomers was concentration-dependent, and 4-CQA exhibited the strongest activity among six isomers. Structure–activity relationship analysis indicated that free radical scavenging ability depends on the number and spatial exposure of phenolic hydroxyl groups. 3-CQA has a fully exposed catechol structure with strong hydrogen- and electron-donating ability, conferring a high radical scavenging rate. Although 5-CQA presents slightly lower scavenging activity than 3-CQA, it possesses better structural stability, with an IC₅₀ of 110 μM and a rate constant of 7.73 × 10⁹ M⁻¹·s⁻¹ (Zang et al., 2003). 4-CQA showed superior ABTS radical scavenging capacity compared with 3-CQA and 5-CQA, and could scavenge free radicals via direct hydrogen donation to alleviate oxidative stress-induced cell damage (Liang et al., 2019).

The intestinal epithelial oxidative stress model revealed that 4,5-CQA exerted the optimal effects on improving cell viability and T-AOC level among all isomers, with the strongest ability to upregulate Nrf2 and inhibit Caspase3 activation, which was highly consistent with network pharmacology prediction. DPPH, ABTS and FRAP are cell-free, metabolism-independent chemical systems that only reflect instantaneous radical scavenging and metal reduction capacity. As a monocaffeoylquinic acid, 4-CQA has lower steric hindrance and more exposed phenolic hydroxyl groups, enabling rapid radical capture and reducing reaction in simple in vitro buffer systems (Liang et al., 2019) . By comparison, dicaffeoyl isomers contain dual catechol structures that provide more hydrogen atoms and electrons, thus possessing stronger radical scavenging capacity than monocaffeoyl isomers. Among them, 4,5-CQA shows optimal conformational flexibility and phenolic hydroxyl synergism, contributing to superior overall antioxidant activity (Iwai et al., 2004) .

Collectively, 4-CQA dominates in chemical antioxidant assays due to low steric hindrance and rapid direct radical scavenging. In contrast, the cellular model involves complex physiological processes including cell uptake, signal transduction and anti-apoptosis. As a dicaffeoylquinic acid, 4,5-CQA has higher membrane permeability and intracellular bioavailability, and effectively activates Nrf2 antioxidant signaling while suppressing Caspase3-mediated apoptosis, thereby exerting stronger protective effects against intestinal epithelial oxidative damage at the cellular level. ROS activates the MAPK pathway to suppress Nrf2/HO-1 and activate Caspase‑3 ‑mediated apoptosis; CGA inhibits excessive MAPK signaling, restores Nrf2/HO-1 activity, and reduces Caspase‑3 activation to mitigate oxidative injury (Chen et al., 2024).

In conclusion, the CGA effectively alleviates DSS-induced oxidative stress in broilers by improving weight loss, poor slaughter performance and reduced jejunal antioxidant capacity. Network pharmacology and molecular docking identified 4,5-CQA as the core component, with CASP3, SRC, MMP9 as targets, involving C-type lectin and MAPK signaling pathways. In vitro tests confirmed 4-CQA’s strong direct antioxidant capacity and 4,5-CQA’s optimal protective effect on intestinal cells.
