Comparative Analysis of Dynamic Versus Static Navigation in Implant Placement Accuracy
Authors: Pratesh Nitin Dholabhai, Vijay Parmar, Jaiganesh Ramamurthy, Noor Addeen Abo Arsheed, Adyasha Mohanty, Sameer Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Precision in implant placement is a critical determinant of clinical success in implant dentistry. Computer-aided navigation systems, including static and dynamic modalities, have been developed to enhance accuracy. However, comparative data evaluating their effectiveness remain limited.Objective:This study aimed to compare the accuracy of implant placement using dynamic navigation versus static guided systems in terms of angular deviation, global entry deviation, and apical deviation.Materials and Methods:A total of 40 implants were placed in 20 partially edentulous patients. Each patient received two implants: one using static navigation and the other with dynamic navigation. Postoperative cone beam computed tomography scans were analyzed using specialized implant planning software to calculate deviations in implant position relative to preoperative planning. Mean deviations were compared using paired t-tests.Results:The mean angular deviation was significantly lower in the dynamic navigation group (2.15° ±0.68°) compared to the static group (3.74° ±1.12°; P < 0.001). Entry point deviation averaged 0.78 ± 0.25 mm for dynamic navigation and 1.14 ± 0.38 mm for static guidance (P = 0.003). Apical deviation was 1.12 ± 0.31 mm for dynamic navigation versus 1.55 ± 0.49 mm for static navigation (P = 0.001).Conclusion:Dynamic navigation demonstrated superior accuracy compared to static guidance in implant placement. These findings suggest that dynamic systems may offer clinicians enhanced control and precision, especially in anatomically complex cases.

INTRODUCTION

The success of dental implant therapy relies significantly on the precise positioning of implants in three-dimensional space.[1] Inaccurate placement can result in biomechanical overload, esthetic failure, or damage to anatomical structures.[2] With the evolution of digital dentistry, computer-aided implant navigation has emerged as a valuable adjunct to conventional surgical protocols.[3]

Navigation systems for implant placement are broadly categorized into static and dynamic types. Static navigation involves preoperative planning and the fabrication of a surgical guide that restricts the position and angulation of drilling during surgery.[4] In contrast, dynamic navigation provides real-time feedback and intraoperative flexibility, allowing the clinician to adjust the implant trajectory during the procedure.[5]

Recent studies have demonstrated the accuracy of both methods, but there remains debate over which modality provides superior results. While static guides are widely used due to their simplicity and presurgical control, dynamic systems offer adaptability and the potential for improved precision in challenging anatomical situations.[67]

Despite growing adoption, comparative clinical evidence between dynamic and static navigation systems in implant dentistry is sparse. Most available data are derived from in vitro or pilot studies with limited sample sizes. Therefore, there exists a research gap regarding the in vivo comparative accuracy of these two modalities.

OBJECTIVE

This study aims to perform a comparative evaluation of dynamic versus static navigation systems in implant placement accuracy in terms of angular deviation, coronal entry point deviation, and apical deviation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 20 partially edentulous patients (11 males, 9 females; age range: 35–65 years) requiring bilateral implant placement in the posterior mandible were recruited. Inclusion criteria were good systemic health, minimum bone width of 6 mm, and adequate keratinized tissue. Patients with systemic disorders, parafunctional habits, or previous grafting at the implant site were excluded.

Each patient received two implants—one using dynamic navigation and the other with static guidance—assigned randomly using a computer-generated list. A total of 40 implants were placed (20 per group).

Surgical procedure

For both groups, preoperative cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were obtained, and implant positions were planned using the respective software. In the static group, surgical guides were fabricated using stereolithography and verified for passive fit. In the dynamic group, the navigation system was calibrated preoperatively, and registration was performed using fiducial markers and intraoral landmarks.

Implants were placed under local anesthesia by the same experienced surgeon. Postoperative CBCT was performed within 72 h, and pre- and postoperative data were superimposed using coDiagnostiX™ software to measure deviations.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired t-tests were used to compare mean deviations between groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

Accurate implant positioning is essential for long-term prosthetic success and to avoid complications.[8] Our study demonstrates that dynamic navigation provides significantly higher accuracy than static guides, corroborating earlier findings.[910]

The significantly reduced angular deviation with dynamic systems supports previous in vitro analyses where real-time visual feedback minimized operator error.[11] Moreover, real-time adaptation during surgery offers better intraoperative control, which may explain the reduced coronal and apical deviations.

However, while static navigation still yielded clinically acceptable results, its rigidity limits intraoperative flexibility, especially in cases of mucosal movement, minor anatomical variation, or limited mouth opening.[12]

Despite these benefits, dynamic navigation has limitations, including steep learning curves, increased setup time, and higher costs.[13] Additionally, the requirement for calibration and stable tracking can be challenging in cases with poor intraoral visibility or limited interocclusal space.

Strengths

This study’s strengths include a randomized paired design, a single-surgeon protocol to reduce variability, and the use of high-resolution CBCT with validated superimposition software.

Limitations

Limitations include the relatively small sample size and focus on the posterior mandible. Long-term clinical outcomes such as prosthesis survival and patient-reported satisfaction were not assessed.

Further research on cost–benefit analysis, operator experience effects, and long-term follow-up is warranted to validate the broader clinical utility of dynamic navigation systems.

CONCLUSION

Within the limits of this clinical study, dynamic navigation demonstrated superior accuracy over static guided surgery in implant placement. The reduced angular, coronal, and apical deviations may contribute to improved clinical outcomes, especially in complex cases requiring enhanced precision.
