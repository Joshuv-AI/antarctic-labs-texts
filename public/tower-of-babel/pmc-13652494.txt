A case report of Mirizzi syndrome type II in a patient with situs inversus totalis treated with laparoscopic cholecystectomy
Authors: Amy Lo, Trista Reid, Daryhl Johnson, Steven Satterly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Situs inversus totalis (SIT) is a rare congenital condition characterized by complete mirror-image transposition of the thoracic and abdominal organs. Mirizzi syndrome is an uncommon complication of cholelithiasis that can substantially increase the technical difficulty of cholecystectomy.Importance:The coexistence of SIT and Mirizzi syndrome is exceedingly rare and presents unique challenges related to altered anatomy, operative orientation, and surgical ergonomics.Case presentation:A 30-year-old man with SIT, Kartagener syndrome, Hirschsprung disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pre-hypertension, and primary biliary dyskinesia presented with worsening abdominal pain despite prior biliary stent placement. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated cholelithiasis with an impacted gallstone at the gallbladder neck, consistent with Mirizzi syndrome, gallbladder distention, biliary ductal dilation, and a low medial insertion of the cystic duct. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed using mirrored port placement, with the surgeon positioned on the right side of the patient. Intraoperatively, an aberrant left hepatic artery was found in close association with the cystic duct and gallbladder, preventing safe attainment of the critical view of safety. A dome-down approach was therefore used to complete the operation safely. The patient recovered without complications.Clinical discussion:Published reports and a meta-analysis of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with SIT demonstrate that minimally invasive management is feasible and safe despite the challenges posed by mirrored anatomy, altered ergonomics, and anatomic variation.Conclusion:Preoperative imaging, mirrored port placement, and alternative dissection strategies, such as the dome-down approach, may facilitate safe laparoscopic management of Mirizzi syndrome in patients with SIT.

Introduction

Situs inversus totalis (SIT) is a rare congenital condition characterized by a mirror-image transposition of both the abdominal and thoracic organs[1]. The flipped orientation of organs makes diagnosis and surgery more difficult because many physicians have limited experience treating affected patients. Mirizzi syndrome is a condition in which the common bile duct (CBD) or common hepatic duct (CHD) is obstructed by a gallstone, either from multiple impacted gallstones or from a single large impacted gallstone in Hartmann’s pouch[2]. There are five types of Mirizzi syndrome (Fig. 1) with different gallstone positions in relation to the CBD. In Type I, the gallbladder infundibulum is directly impacted by a stone and compresses the CHD but does not cause a fistula. In Type II, the stone causes obstruction that is less than one-third of the CHD diameter. In Type III, the stone causes obstruction of one-third to two-thirds of the CHD diameter. In Type IV, the stone causes complete obstruction of the CHD. In Type V, there is a cholecystoenteric fistula with or without gallstone ileus[3]. The incidence of Mirizzi syndrome is less than 1% in developed Western countries, and the global incidence of SIT ranges from 1:6500 to 1:25000[1,4]. The co-occurrence of both conditions is even rarer. There have only been two reported cases of surgical management of Mirizzi syndrome in a patient with SIT, both of which were performed in India. The current standard of care for Mirizzi syndrome is cholecystectomy[2]. However, the procedure can be complicated by inflammation or adhesions, obscuring the critical view of safety and increasing the risk of porta hepatis or bile duct injury[2]. We obtained verbal consent from the patient to publish this case report and procured the necessary information from their electronic medical record. This work has been reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria[5]. Artificial intelligence was not used in the writing of this report.

Case

A 30-year-old man presented to the emergency department with worsening abdominal pain and loss of appetite. He had presented to the emergency department 5 days earlier with similar symptoms and was known to the trauma surgery faculty, as he was scheduled for an elective robotic cholecystectomy in 1 month and had previously received two CBD stents. His past medical history is notable for SIT, Kartagener syndrome, Hirschsprung disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pre-hypertension, primary biliary dyskinesia, choledocholithiasis, Mirizzi Syndrome seen on ERCP several months prior, and biliary colic.

On physical examination, the patient appeared to be in pain, had a positive Murphy’s sign, was afebrile, and was hemodynamically stable. Laboratory results showed a total bilirubin of 0.9 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase of 50/82, and a white blood cell count of 10.5 × 109/L. Abdominal ultrasound showed cholelithiasis with mild dilatation of the CBD measuring up to 0.7 cm, no pericholecystic fluid, and no gallbladder wall thickening. During his previous emergency department presentation 5 days prior, his computed tomography scan showed moderate steatosis and mild hepatomegaly, no significant abnormalities in the gallbladder, and a CBD measuring up to 1 cm.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) demonstrated cholelithiasis with a gallstone lodged at the gallbladder neck and associated mild gallbladder distention. Additional findings included pneumobilia from prior endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), mild central intrahepatic biliary ectasia, and dilation of the CHD up to 9 mm, as well as the CBD measuring up to 7 mm with distal tapering near the ampulla (Fig. 2). Several filling defects measuring up to 7 mm were identified within the cystic duct, and a low medial insertion of the cystic duct was noted (Fig. 2). The patient was scheduled for a robotic cholecystectomy due to the complexity of the case. Unfortunately, the patient’s symptoms progressed, and a laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed by two trauma surgeons with more than 10 years of experience each. The patient was placed in the supine position with arms outstretched. The abdomen was entered using the Hasson technique, then a 5 mm port was placed in the epigastrium and two 5-mm ports in the left upper quadrant, mirroring those that normally would be placed on the opposite side in a patient without situs inversus. The operating surgeon was positioned on the right side of the patient to accommodate the reversed location of the traditional port placement. There were multiple pertinent surgical findings during the case. Careful dissection revealed an aberrant hepatic artery coursing along the medial aspect of the gallbladder wall and into the left hepatic lobe. This vessel was carefully dissected due to its proximity to the cystic structures until its relationship to the cystic artery was clarified. The cystic artery demonstrated an abnormally short takeoff of approximately 2–3 mm before branching into the cystic duct branch and anterior cystic artery directly from the left hepatic artery. Because of this close association, the gallbladder was meticulously dissected away from the left hepatic artery down to the confluence. The aberrant anatomy, in addition to the situs inversus, contributed to the complexity of the case.

The anterior cystic artery was isolated, and two clips were placed proximally, with one on the specimen side, followed by sharp division. This provided improved anatomic visualization, and the middle third of the cystic plate was cleared to allow posterior mobilization of the gallbladder. Due to the inability to safely separate the left hepatic artery from the cystic duct and the concern for potential injury, the decision was made to convert to a dome-down technique. This was carried inferiorly with electrocautery to the level of the hepatic artery takeoff, during which purulent bile spilled from the gallbladder.

Given the thickened, large cystic duct and the proximity to the hepatic artery, the 5-mm epigastric port was upsized to 15 mm, and a 60-mm black load electric laparoscopic stapler was used to divide the cystic duct. The staple line was intact without evidence of leakage or bleeding. Given the length of the procedure and risk of biloma, a temporary 19 Blake abdominal drain was placed.

The patient’s postoperative course was unremarkable, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 2. Pain was managed with oxycodone 5 mg and acetaminophen 1000 mg. The patient complained of right eye pain shortly after the operation; he was started on erythromycin 5 mg/gram (0.5% ophthalmic ointment and ketorolac 0.5% ophthalmic solution) out of concern for corneal abrasion. Bile duct stents were removed by gastroenterologists approximately 1 month after the operation. Six months after his operation, the patient has not experienced any surgical complications and has adhered appropriately to postoperative instructions on limited weight-bearing for 4 weeks and drain care.

Discussion

In summary, this case report describes a 30-year-old male with a past medical history notable for SIT, Kartagener syndrome, Hirschsprung disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pre-hypertension, primary biliary dyskinesia, choledocholithiasis, and biliary colic. He presented with a positive Murphy’s sign, mildly elevated liver enzymes, and imaging consistent with cholecystitis. MRCP demonstrated cholelithiasis with a gallstone lodged at the gallbladder neck, with associated mild gallbladder distension, consistent with Mirizzi syndrome Type II. A laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed, employing mirrored port placement and surgeon positioning. Aberrant anatomy and mirrored orientation of organs made the surgery especially challenging, necessitating additional senior-surgeon involvement and a dome-down approach.

The case reports by Bose and Badipati et al (Table 1) describe rare cases of Mirizzi syndrome in patients with SIT managed successfully with laparoscopic techniques, highlighting the technical challenges of mirrored anatomy[3,6]. The Badipati et al case involves a complex Type III Mirizzi syndrome with significant comorbidities, requiring staged management with ERCP followed by delayed laparoscopic cholecystectomy and bile duct exploration, while Bose et al report a Type I case treated with laparoscopic cholecystectomy, choledocholithotomy, and T-tube drainage after failed ERCP. Both emphasize that although SIT creates challenges in orientation, port placement, and ergonomics, careful preoperative planning and modified surgical techniques allow for safe and effective minimally invasive management. However, both cases were managed by surgeons in India, who have different training and operate within a different healthcare system. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with SIT was analyzed by Enicu et al in a meta-analysis that evaluated case reports of 121 patients[7]. The authors found that left-hand-dominant surgeons and preoperative imaging were associated with statistically significantly shorter operative times. 77.68% of cases used the mirrored American port placement, 10% used the mirrored French port placement, 7.5% used a single port, and the remaining 6 patients were operated on using novel port placements to accommodate the challenging anatomy. Overall, the analysis outlined the different approaches and associations with clinically relevant outcomes of laparoscopic cholecystectomies in patients with SIT and concluded that the operation is clearly technically challenging. However, seasoned surgeons have meticulously planned their operations to be safe, resulting in good outcomes for the patients. Therefore, laparoscopic cholecystectomy remains the gold standard for gallbladder inflammation despite changes in anatomy, port placement, positioning, and awkward handedness.

Traditionally, surgeons rely on the critical view of safety to minimize the risk of bile duct injury during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Achieving this view requires meticulous dissection of the hepatocystic triangle, exposure of at least the lower one-third of the cystic plate, and clear identification of only two tubular structures, the cystic duct and cystic artery, entering the gallbladder8. In patients with SIT, however, this process becomes more challenging. The surgeon’s perspective of Calot’s triangle is mirrored, resulting in a lateralized and reversed orientation of the usual anatomic landmarks. Additionally, the cystic artery typically arises from the right hepatic artery rather than the left like in this patient, further disrupting familiar spatial relationships. In the case above, the variation in which the left hepatic artery was closely associated with the cystic duct and gallbladder made the case challenging and highlighted the importance of complete dissection and increased vigilance during the operation. Finally, anatomic variations of the gallbladder and biliary tree, which may be present in any patient (Fig. 3), can compound these challenges and make dissection and visualization of Calot’s triangle even more complex in the setting of SIT. There are several strengths in this report. It presents an exceptionally rare case of Mirizzi syndrome Type II in a patient with SIT and clearly outlines the diagnostic workup, intraoperative findings, and surgical modifications required to safely manage the altered anatomy. The discussion of mirrored port placement, aberrant vascular anatomy, and the use of a dome-down approach provides valuable technical insights for surgeons and validates the challenges involved. Additionally, the manuscript includes appropriate follow-up, demonstrating a successful postoperative outcome.Further reports and research into patients with non-typical anatomy is needed to establish standards of care.

Conclusion

Future laparoscopic cholecystectomies in patients with Mirizzi syndrome and SIT should use mirrored port placement, obtain imaging prior to operating, use the dome-down approach to dissect the challenging anatomy, and consider having more than one experienced surgeon operating in anticipation of poor ergonomics and aberrant anatomy complicating the operation.
