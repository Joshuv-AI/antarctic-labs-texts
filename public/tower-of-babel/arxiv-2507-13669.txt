A Note on Helicoidal Singular Minimal Surfaces
Rafael López

Abstract
. Let α ∈ ℝ \alpha\in\mathbb{R} and let v → ∈ ℝ 3 \vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} be a unit vector. A singular minimal surface Σ \Sigma in Euclidean space is a surface Σ \Sigma whose mean curvature H H satisfies H = α ​ ⟨ N , v → ⟩ ⟨ p , v → ⟩ H=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle}{\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle} , where N N is the unit normal vector of Σ \Sigma . 1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the Euclidean
3
-dimensional space and let
\langle,\rangle
be the Euclidean metric. Fix
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a unit vector and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. An orientable surface
\Sigma
immersed in the halfspace
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle>0\}
is called a
\alpha
-singular minimal surface if its the mean curvature
H
satisfies

(1)

H(p)=\alpha\frac{\langle N(p),\vec{v}\rangle}{\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle},\quad p\in\Sigma,

where
N
is the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
. The convention for
H
is to be the sum of the principal curvatures. When
\alpha=1
, singular minimal surfaces generalize in dimension two, the notion of catenary because a singular minimal surface has the property of having the lowest center of gravity among all surfaces with the same boundary curve and surface area
[
1
,
2
,
8
]
. An interesting case is
\alpha=-2
. If we take
\vec{v}=(0,0,1)
, then
\Sigma
is a singular minimal surface for
\alpha=-2
if and only if
\Sigma
is a minimal surface in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
when this space is viewed in the upper half-space model
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
. The case
\alpha=0
corresponds to classical minimal surfaces. From now on, we will discard the value
\alpha=0
.

In the search of examples of singular minimal surfaces, it is natural to assume some type of symmetry of the surface. If the surface is invariant by translations (cylindrical surface), then the vector
\vec{v}
is parallel to the direction of the rulings of the surface. The classification was done by the author in
[
13
]
. If the surface
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution, then the vector
\vec{v}
is parallel to the rotation axis or the rotation axis lies contained in the vector plane
\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle=0\}
(
[
13
]
). The first type of rotational singular minimal surfaces was studied in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
13
]
.

In Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, there are three types of one-parameter groups of rigid motions. Besides translations and rotations, the third one-parameter group of rigid motions is formed by helicoidal motions. A helicoidal motion is a rotation about some line, called the twist axis, followed by a translation parallel to that same line. Without loss of generality, we will assume that the twist axis is the
z
-axis.
For
h\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the one-parameter group of helicoidal motions of
\mathbf{R}^{3}
where
\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y,z)&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t&0\\
\sin t&\cos t&0\\
0&0&1)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
ht\end{pmatrix}\\
&=(x\cos t-y\sin t,x\sin t+y\cos t,z+ht),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

A helicoidal surface
\Sigma
with axis the
z
-axis and pitch
h
is a surface invariant under
\mathcal{H}_{t}
for all
t
, that is,
\mathcal{H}_{t}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
. If
h=0
, the surface is a surface of revolution. The limit case
h\rightarrow\infty
corresponds with surfaces invariant under translations along the
z
-axis.

In this paper, we are interesting to classify the helicoidal singular minimal surfaces. This type of classification has been done for a variety of classes of surfaces under some hypothesis on the curvature of the surface. Without to include all types of curvatures and ambient spaces, we mention some simple examples: surfaces with constant mean curvature
[
3
,
14
,
15
]
, surfaces with constant ratio of principal curvatures (
[
12
]
), surfaces with constant anisotropic mean curvature (
[
11
]
) and self-similar solutions of the mean curvature flow (
[
10
]
).

In this paper, we determine all helicoidal singular minimal surfaces. As in the case of cylindrical surfaces and rotational surfaces, this amounts to solving a second order ordinary differential equation. Although it may seem like a simple exercise, we point out that with other types of assumptions on the curvature of the surface, the corresponding ODE may turn out be difficult to manage. Taking into account what happens for rotational singular minimal surfaces, in principle there is no a relation between the vector
\vec{v}
of the definition of singular minimal surface in Eq. (
1
) and the twist axis of the helicoidal surface. In fact, the natural choice that the twist axis is parallel to the vector
\vec{v}
cannot occur unless that the surface is a surface of revolution (
h=0
). This will be proved in Prop.
2.1
. This is because in Eq. (
1
), the left hand-side does not depend on the parameter
t
of the helicoidal motion
\mathcal{H}_{t}
but the right hand-side does. Notice that the parameter
t
appears, in general, in the denominator
\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle
in (
1
). A similar situation occurs in the classification of the ruled singular minimal surfaces, where the only possible case is that the surface is cylindrical (
[
9
]
).

Definitively, we have to assume fully generality between the twist axis and the vector
\vec{v}
. In this note, the classification of the helicoidal helicoidal singular minimal surfaces is obtained in the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface invariant by the helicoidal group
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
with pitch
h\not=0
. If
\Sigma
is a singular minimal surface with respect to
\vec{v}
, then the twist axis is contained in the vector plane
\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle=0\}
(in particular,
\vec{v}
is orthogonal to the twist axis),
\alpha=-1
and
\Sigma
is a circular cylinder about the
z
-axis.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

A helicoidal surface
\Sigma
invariant by the helicoidal group
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
can be obtained by moving a regular planar curve parametrized by arc-length

\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\gamma(s)=(x(s),0,z(s)),

under the helicoidal motions
\mathcal{H}_{t}
. A parametrization of
\Sigma
is
\Psi\colon I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
where

(2)

\Psi(s,t)=\mathcal{H}_{t}(\gamma(s))=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)+ht).

We compute all terms of Eq. (
1
). From now on, we will drop the variable of differentiation if it is understood in the context. The unit normal vector
N
of
\Sigma
is

(3)

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+h^{2}x^{\prime 2}}}\left(hx^{\prime}\sin t-xz^{\prime}\cos t,-hx^{\prime}\cos t-xz^{\prime}\sin t,xx^{\prime}\right).

We now compute the mean curvature
H
of
\Sigma
. The coefficients of the first fundamental form of
\Sigma
are

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{s}\rangle=1,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{\theta}\rangle=hz^{\prime},

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\theta},\Psi_{\theta}\rangle=x^{2}+h^{2}

and its coefficients of the second fundamental form are

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{ss},N\rangle=\frac{x\left(z^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime}-z^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}\right)}{\sqrt{h^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s\theta},N\rangle=-\frac{hx^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{h^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}},

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\theta\theta},N\rangle=\frac{x^{2}z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{h^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}}.

Regularity of the surface is equivalent to
EG-F^{2}>0
, that is,

x^{2}+h^{2}-h^{2}z^{\prime 2}=x^{2}+h^{2}x^{\prime 2}>0

because
x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}=1
. Therefore, the mean curvature of a helicoidal surface
\Sigma
is given by

(4)

H=\frac{eG-2fF+gE}{EG-F^{2}}=\frac{(x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime})(x^{3}+h^{2}x)+z^{\prime}(2h^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})}{(h^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

In order to simplify all expressions, we use that
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length. Since
x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}=1
, there is a smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
such that

(5)

\begin{split}x^{\prime}(s)&=\cos\theta(s),\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\theta(s).\end{split}

Moreover, the curvature of the curve
\gamma
coincides with
\theta^{\prime}(s)
. We now have from (
4
) and (
5
) that the mean curvature is

(6)

H=\frac{x(x^{2}+h^{2})\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta\left(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right)}{(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3/2}}.

Similarly, the unit normal vector
N
on (
3
) is now

(7)

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}\left(h\cos\theta\sin t-x\sin\theta\cos t,-h\cos\theta\cos t-x\sin\theta\sin t,x\cos\theta\right).

Notice that

(8)

EG-F^{2}=x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta>0.

We now prove that the natural choice when the twist axis coincides with the vector
\vec{v}
is not possible unless that the helicoidal motions are pure rotation motions (
h=0
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface invariant by the helicoidal group
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
with pitch
h
. If
\Sigma
is a singular minimal surface with
\vec{v}=(0,0,1)
, then
h=0
.

Proof.

From (
2
) and (
7
) we have

\begin{split}\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle&=\frac{x\cos\theta}{\sqrt{x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}},\\
\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle&=\langle\Psi(s,t),\vec{v}\rangle=htz.\end{split}

By using the expression of the mean curvature
H
in (
6
), equation (
1
) writes as a polynomial on
t
of degree
1
, namely,

A_{0}(s)+A_{1}(s)t=0.

Then the coefficients
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
must be zero. A computation of these coefficients gives

\begin{split}A_{0}&=z\left(x(x^{2}+h^{2})\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right))-\alpha x\cos\theta\left(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right),\\
A_{1}&=h\left(x(x^{2}+h^{2})\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta(x^{2}+2h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)\right).\end{split}

The linear combination
hA_{0}-zA_{1}=0
becomes

\alpha hx\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)=0.

Since
x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\not=0
by (
8
), we deduce
h=0
, or
x=0
identically or
\cos\theta=0
identically. If
h=0
, then the result is proved. Assume now that
h\not=0
and we see that the cases
x=0
or
\cos\theta=0
identically cannot occur.

If
x=0
identically, then
x^{\prime}=\cos\theta=0
hence
x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta=0
, which it is not possible. If
x^{\prime}=\cos\theta=0
identically, then
x=x(s)
is a constant function,
x(s)=x_{0}
with the extra condition that
x_{0}\not=0
by regularity (
8
). In particular, from (
5
) we deduce
z(s)=\pm s+z_{0}
. Computing again the coefficients
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
, we simply have

A_{0}(s)=x_{0}(\pm s+z_{0})
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
A_{1}(s)=hx_{0}
. Then
A_{0}=0
gives a contradiction. This completes the proof of Prop.
2.1
.
∎

We prove Thm.
1.1
assuming that
\vec{v}
is an arbitrary unit vector of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let write the vector
\vec{v}
in Cartesian coordinates,

\vec{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}).

From (
2
) and (
7
), we have

\begin{split}\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle&=\frac{\cos\theta(v_{3}x+hv_{1}\sin t-hv_{2}\cos t)-x\sin\theta(v_{1}\cos t+v_{2}\sin t)}{\sqrt{x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)}},\\
\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle&=\langle\Psi(s,t),\vec{v}\rangle=(ht+z)v_{3}+x(v_{1}\cos t+v_{2}\sin t).\end{split}

In contrast to the proof of Prop.
2.1
, equation (
1
) now is an equation of type

A_{0}(s)+A_{1}(s)t+A_{2}(s)\sin t+A_{3}(s)\cos t=0.

Since the functions
\{1,t,\sin t,\cos t\}
are linearly independent, the coefficients
A_{i}(s)
must be zero. The computation of these coefficients yields

\begin{split}A_{0}&=-v_{3}\left(\alpha x\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)-xz\theta^{\prime}(h^{2}+x^{2})-x^{2}z\sin\theta-2h^{2}z\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta\right),\\
A_{1}&=hv_{3}\left(x\theta^{\prime}(h^{2}+x^{2})+x^{2}\sin\theta+2h^{2}\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta\right),\\
A_{2}&=\left(\alpha x\sin\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)+x^{2}\theta^{\prime}(x^{2}+h^{2})+2h^{2}x\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta+x^{3}\sin\theta\right)v_{2}\\
&-\alpha h\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)v_{1},\\
A_{3}&=\left(\alpha x\sin\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)+x^{2}\theta^{\prime}(x^{2}+h^{2})+2h^{2}x\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta+x^{3}\sin\theta\right)v_{1}\\
&+\alpha h\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)v_{2}.\\
\end{split}

The linear combination
hA_{0}-zA_{1}=0
writes simply as

\alpha hv_{3}x\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)=0.

By regularity (
8
) and because
h\not=0
, we deduce

v_{3}x\cos\theta=0.

We distinguish three cases.

(1)

Case
x=0
identically. Then
\cos\theta=0
and regularity (
8
) is lost. This case cannot occur.

(2)

Case
\cos\theta=0
identically. Then
x(s)=x_{0}\not=0
is a constant function, being
z(s)=\pm s+z_{0}
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\gamma
is a vertical line. In this case,

\Psi(s,t)=(x_{0}\cos t,x_{0}\sin t,ht\pm s+z_{0}).

This surface is a circular cylinder of radius
|x_{0}|
. Now we have

\begin{split}A_{0}&=x_{0}^{2}zv_{3},\\
A_{1}&=x_{0}^{2}hv_{3},\\
A_{2}&=x_{0}^{3}v_{2}(1+\alpha),\\
A_{3}&=x_{0}^{3}v_{1}(1+\alpha)\\
\end{split}

Then
A_{1}=0
implies
v_{3}=0
. This means that the
z
-axis, which it is the twist axis of the helicoidal surface, lies contained in the vector plane
\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{v}\rangle=0\}
. Since
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
cannot be simultaneously
0
, from
A_{2}=0
or
A_{3}=0
we deduce
\alpha=-1
.

(3)

Case
v_{3}=0
. The linear combination
v_{2}A_{3}-v_{1}A_{2}=0
becomes

h\alpha\cos\theta(x^{2}+h^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)=0.

Since
h\not=0
, this equation implies
\cos\theta=0
identically. This case has been considered in the previous item. This completes the proof of Thm.
1.1
.
