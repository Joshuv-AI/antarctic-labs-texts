Does stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) added to continued systemic therapy improve time to treatment failure compared with physician’s choice of systemic therapy in oligoprogressive ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer? Study protocol for a multicentre, randomised, open-label phase II trial in Australian tertiary cancer centres (AVATAR-II)
Authors: Emma Connolly, Michelle White, Shankar Siva, Mathias Bressel, Adrienne See, Kate Hunter, Jennifer Tan, Vanessa Panettieri, Daphne Day, Keelan Byrne, Amelia McCartney, Kate Webber, Katrina Woodford, Steven David
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOligoprogressive disease (OPD) is a clinically significant pattern of progression observed in patients with oestrogen receptor (ER)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative advanced breast cancer treated with endocrine therapy (ET) and cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitors. Stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) is an emerging strategy that may ablate resistant subclones and prolong the benefit of systemic therapy. The AVATAR-II trial investigates whether the addition of SABR to continued systemic therapy can delay the need to change treatment strategy.Methods and analysisAVATAR-II is a multicentre, randomised, open-label, phase II randomised controlled trial enrolling 74 patients with histologically confirmed ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer and 1–5 sites of extracranial OPD. Eligible patients must have demonstrated clinical benefit (stable disease or partial response) from ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors for at least 6 months prior to randomisation. Participants will be randomised 1:1 to either SABR to all OPD sites with continuation of current systemic therapy (Arm A) or physician’s choice of systemic therapy (Arm B). The primary endpoint is time to treatment failure, defined as progression not amenable to SABR, cessation of systemic therapy or death. The secondary endpoints include progression-free survival (PFS), PFS2, overall survival, treatment-related adverse events and patient-reported quality of life using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Breast score.Ethics and disseminationThis study received ethical approval from the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), under approval number 25/45 and HREC reference HREC/105165/PMCC. Results of the study will be disseminated via peer-reviewed presentation at scientific conferences and open-access publication.Trial registration numberNCT06882499.

Background

Stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR), an advanced form of radiation therapy, holds promise for treating metastatic breast cancer when it is delivered to limited metastatic sites in specific patient cohorts. Breast cancer is a complex disease featuring various subtypes, each characterised by distinct molecular and cellular pathologies. The AVATAR trial1–3 demonstrated that oestrogen receptor (ER)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative patients who underwent established first-line systemic therapy for metastatic disease benefited from SABR as second-line therapy along with continuation of their original systemic treatment. In this trial, modified progression-free survival (mPFS) was defined as disease progression not amenable to SABR treatment or death from any cause. When SABR was added as a second-line treatment, AVATAR demonstrated an improved mPFS of 9.9 months. This gave patients almost an additional year on their standardised first-line systemic therapy. Given the lack of data for a well-defined second-line treatment, it is reasonable to consider SABR in this setting.

The oligoprogressive state

42% of patients undergoing treatment for metastatic ER-positive breast cancer develop ‘oligo-progression’.4 Oligoprogressive disease (OPD), a recent term, describes a state where a tumour progresses at a few sites (usually 1–5) while other sites remain stable or respond to systemic therapy.5

These new lesions may represent subclones of disease that develop resistance to both cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitors and hormone therapy through various pathways. Patients progressing in this manner suggest a need for further research into localised ablative techniques as second-line treatments.

The management of OPD can be challenging. However, different treatment approaches are emerging, including (a) switching systemic therapy or (b) if there is low-burden disease progression, continuing the same line of systemic therapy +/− considering local therapy, such as ablative radiotherapy.6 Recently, there has been increasing interest in the use of ablative radiotherapy in the management of OPD. Indeed, the Consolidative Use of Radiotherapy to Block (CURB) study, which focused on breast and lung cancer, sought to determine whether early treatment of OPD with localised ablative therapies could prolong patient progression-free survival (PFS). They demonstrated that non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) could be treated with this approach; however, in the breast cancer cohort (mostly triple-negative pathology), no benefit was observed.7 Several other retrospective studies of patients with epidermal growth factor receptor and/or anaplastic lymphoma kinase-mutated NSCLC treated with local ablative therapy and continued treatment with targeted therapy reported improved PFS.8–11 Similarly, a prospective trial of SABR combined with erlotinib in patients with metastatic NSCLC reported median PFS and overall survival (OS) rates of 14.7 and 20.4 months, respectively.12

Systemic therapy

The standard first-line therapy for most ER-positive HER2-negative advanced breast cancer patients involves combining endocrine treatment (ET) with a CDK4/6 inhibitor. The ET typically prescribed is an aromatase inhibitor (AI). Notably, randomised controlled trials in this area (MONALEESA-2, MONALEESA-7, PALOMA-2 and MONARCH-3)13–16 have demonstrated improved PFS with this combined approach of approximately 24 months. Trials such as PALOMA-3, MONARCH-2, MONALEESA-3 and DAWNA-117–20 confirmed the prolonged and consistent benefit of a CDK4/6 inhibitor combined with fulvestrant. Fulvestrant can be a preferred choice over an AI in those who progress while receiving or shortly after the completion of adjuvant ET.21 22

There is no standard of care for second-line treatment. Owing to notable limitations in current systemic therapies used as second-line agents, many patients with hormone-sensitive breast cancer are on chemotherapy within their first year following their first progression.23

Toxicity

Early stages of metastatic breast cancer can progress with minimal symptoms, and the impact on quality of life (QoL) often stems from treatment effects rather than the disease itself.24 SABR is a treatment modality known for its favourable efficacy with notably low toxicity.25 It is, therefore, rational to use SABR to ablate the progressing disease sites in OPD to eliminate the resistant subclones and enable the continued efficacy of systemic therapy—in this case, an ET plus a CDK4/6 inhibitor.

Novel primary endpoints

Traditional primary endpoints (OS, PFS) are not useful in trials where the treatment is not designed to alter them. This trial seeks to assess SABR as a method to delay a change in systemic therapy, which is a popular strategy for both patients and clinicians, by maintaining the current line of endocrine therapy (ET) in combination with a CDK4/6 inhibitor, which is a low-toxicity, well-tolerated treatment. Metastatic breast cancer is a systemic disease. The risk of distant metastases depends more on tumour biology than its laterality, anatomical site or corresponding lymphatic drainage.26–28 SABR, hypothetically, kills subclones of early mutating disease, potentially offering patients the ability to remain on convenient, well-tolerated medication.

In this way, the contribution of SABR to overall disease control is often under-reported. There is a lack of specific endpoints that examine the single contribution of ablative therapies in driving the disease course through repeated radical treatment to site(s) of progression.29 Assessing this impact is necessary to quantify the effects of these multiple treatments and capture the overall effects that ablative therapies have on a patient’s outcome. SABR to oligoprogressive lesions to extend the effect of ongoing systemic therapy is not unique to breast cancer.30 It is also relevant to those malignancies that respond to checkpoint inhibitors, with reports of lasting disease control31 32 and even a potential survival benefit.33

In this study, we propose time to treatment failure (TTF) as our primary endpoint to reflect the real-world effect of SABR in this setting. TTF is defined as time to change in treatment strategy and is intended to capture the potential advantages of a SABR-based approach on patient outcomes, including prolongation of response to first-line ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors. It was selected as it reflects a pragmatic, treatment strategy-based approach that mirrors real-world clinical decision-making, capturing the durability of a treatment strategy, including repeated SABR to manage subsequent oligoprogression, rather than treating each new lesion as an isolated progression event, as is the case with traditional PFS.

Methods/design

Study design

AVATAR-II is a prospective, multicentre, randomised phase II clinical trial evaluating the impact of SABR on treatment outcomes in patients with ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer presenting with extracranial OPD. Accrual will open from August 2026 for 2 years. Eligible patients will have demonstrated clinical benefit (stable disease or partial response) for at least 6 months on ET and a CDK4/6 inhibitor, followed by progression at 1–5 extracranial metastatic sites. All OPD lesions must be amenable to SABR.

Seventy-four patients will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the following:

Arm A (experimental): SABR to all OPD sites with continuation of current systemic therapy.

Intervention

Arm A: stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy with maintenance of systemic therapy (experimental arm)

Participants in Arm A will continue their current ET and CDK4/6 inhibitor regimen. All radiologically defined sites of oligoprogression (1–5 lesions) will be treated with SABR. SABR must be initiated within 28 days of randomisation, and all planned sites must be treated within a 21-day treatment window (figure 2).

Arm A schema. CDK, cyclin-dependent kinase; ET, endocrine therapy; OPD, oligoprogressive disease; PFS, progression-free survival; PFS2, Progression-free survival 2; SABR, stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy; TF, treatment failure.

Simulation and planning

All patients will undergo CT simulation for treatment planning. For thoracic or upper abdominal lesions, four-dimensional (4D)-CT or deep inspiration breath-hold techniques are required to assess motion. Intravenous contrast is recommended unless contraindicated. Co-registration with diagnostic MRI or positron emission tomography (PET)/CT is permitted and encouraged to assist in target delineation.

The gross tumour volume (GTV) is defined as the visible tumour on CT and/or fused imaging.

The clinical target volume (CTV) is equal to the GTV (no expansion). For bone lesions, CTV of 3–5 mm will be allowed. For vertebral lesions, the approach as per the International Spinal consortium guidelines should be used.

The clinical target volume (CTV) is equal to the GTV (no expansion). For bone lesions, CTV of 3–5 mm will be allowed. For vertebral lesions, the approach as per the International Spinal consortium guidelines should be used.

The planning target volume (PTV) is generated by adding a margin of 2–5 mm to account for setup variability and motion, which is based on institutional practice.

SABR must be delivered using advanced techniques such as volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or stereotactic-specific platforms with tissue heterogeneity correction and high-resolution dose calculation. Daily image guidance (eg, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) or stereoscopic X-ray) is mandatory.

Arm B: physician’s choice of systemic therapy (control arm)

Participants in Arm B will either transition from their current ET/CDK4/6 regimen to second-line systemic treatment or continue their current regimen, at the discretion of the treating medical oncologist. This may include alternate ET, a switch in CDK4/6 inhibitors, targeted therapy (eg, everolimus), chemotherapy or other agents consistent with the standard of care for ER-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer (figure 3).

Arm B schema. PFS, progression-free survival; PFS2, progression-free survival 2.

The physician’s choice control arm is an intentional and pragmatic design decision, because there is currently an absence of standard of care regimens for second-line treatment in ER-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer. Acceptable therapies should follow current international guidelines and are expected to reflect real-world clinical practice on the basis of individual patient factors and institutional practice. The timing and nature of systemic therapy change will be recorded at each decision point, enabling exploratory analyses of treatment heterogeneity across the control arm.

The use of SABR in Arm B was not permitted during the study period. Radiotherapy may be used for symptomatic palliation if deemed necessary.

Management of disease progression

Definition of oligoprogression amenable to stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy

Oligoprogression for consideration of subsequent SABR in Arm A will be defined on the basis of RECIST 1.1 (clinician interpreted) criteria as follows (on a per-lesion basis, between 1 and 5 metastases, including the primary) via CT:

At least a 20% increase in the diameter of a lesion, taking as reference the smallest diameter on a previous study with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm.

At least a 20% increase in the diameter of a lesion, taking as reference the smallest diameter on a previous study with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm.

Appearance of a new lesion(s) via a consistent imaging modality.

Appearance of a new lesion(s) via a consistent imaging modality.

Repeat stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy

There is no maximum number of SABR courses permitted in Arm A. Prior radiotherapy is permitted provided the composite plan (prior+planned dose) meets all protocol OAR constraints. Substantial overlap with a currently or previously irradiated volume that cannot be made to meet composite constraints is excluded. Prior doses will be converted to biologically effective dose and summed against the tolerance limits in online supplemental appendix 2. A time-based recovery adjustment may be discussed with and approved by the study PI. All cases involving overlap must be reviewed and approved by the local and study PI prior to treatment.

Repeat SABR within the assigned treatment strategy does not itself constitute a treatment failure event; TTF is defined from randomisation to the first failure of the overall treatment strategy, not individual treatment episodes. PFS is measured from randomisation to first radiographic or clinical progression or death, regardless of subsequent SABR courses delivered within the assigned strategy.

The use of SABR in Arm B for management of further progression is discouraged but not prohibited. SABR can be considered in select cases of OPD after discussion with the AVATAR-II TMC. It will be recorded as a treatment failure event.

Brain metastases on follow-up

The appearance of brain metastases during follow-up should be managed at the discretion of clinicians. Continuation of the current line of therapy in Arm A is permitted as long as all new or progressing brain metastases are managed with locally ablative therapy, which includes stereotactic radiosurgery, surgical resection or both. The radiosurgical dose fractionation schedules and surgical techniques used will be as per local institutional guidelines.

Disease progression not amenable to stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy

In both arms, palliative systemic therapy is recommended as indicated, where the choice of systemic agent is at the discretion of the treating team. Any further palliative systemic therapy or palliative radiation therapy after progression is at the discretion of the treating physicians.

Statistical considerations

Patient randomisation

Eligible patients will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either Arm A (SABR with continuation of first-line ET and CDK4/6 inhibitor) or Arm B (physician’s choice of systemic therapy) via a secure, centralised electronic data capture (EDC) system. Block randomisation with random block sizes will be used to ensure allocation concealment, and treatment assignment will not be known prior to randomisation. Patients will be stratified by metastatic burden (oligometastatic: 1–5 metastases vs polymetastatic: >5 metastases). As a small phase 2 randomised controlled trial (RCT) with n=74, we elected to use only one stratification factor. Metastatic burden was selected as the stratification factor on the basis that it is the variable believed most likely to influence TTF and is consistent with stratification used in similar trials in this setting. Randomisation can proceed only after informed consent is obtained, eligibility is confirmed and all screening assessments are completed within the protocol-defined timeframes. Following randomisation, SABR in Arm A must commence within 28 days.

Statistical methods

Patient demographics, baseline characteristics and treatment details will be described via descriptive statistics such as the minimum, maximum, median, IQR, mean and SD for quantitative variables. Qualitative variables are described in tabular form as counts and percentages.

PROs will be analysed using linear mixed models (LMMs) with time (as factor), arm and interaction of time and arm included as a fixed effect and patient included as a random effect. The outcome variable will be the PRO score, and patient’s baseline score will be included as covariate in the model. Means and 95% CIs will be estimated from the LMM contrasts. The model will be built including all time points, and appropriate contrasts will be used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC). No imputation of missing data is intended; however, LMM appropriately handles missing data under the assumption of missing at random.

The worst grades of toxicity will be tabulated as frequencies by grade for each TRAE type. The incidence of any TRAE of any grade will be compared between arms via Fisher’s exact test.

Time to event endpoints will be described by arm via Kaplan‒Meier methods. The stratified Cox proportional hazard model will be used to compare the time-to-event outcomes between the arms, with oligometastatic versus polymetastatic as a stratification factor. A swimmers plot will also be provided. Details of censoring event for time-to-event endpoints will be described in the statistical analysis plan (SAP).

Sample size calculation

The assumed control arm, median TTF of 6 months is informed by real-world data on time to next systemic therapy following first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor progression (23), reflecting the typical duration of benefit before systemic therapy escalation is required. The assumed improvement to 11 months in the experimental arm is consistent with modified PFS benefit observed in the original AVATAR trial. With 74 patients randomised 1:1 between the arms, the study will have 80% power to reject the null hypothesis that the TTFs in both arms are the same if the true median TTF in the SABR arm is 11 months. This calculation was performed with a 5% alpha, one-sided test, assuming 3 years of accrual, 3 years of follow-up and a 5% dropout rate. A one-sided test is appropriate as the trial is specifically designed to detect whether SABR is superior to physician’s choice of systemic therapy.

Statistical analysis plan

The trial is planned to open to accrual in August 2026 and is anticipated to complete enrolment by August 2028. Two analyses are planned for this study. Neither analysis constitutes a formal interim efficacy analysis; both will occur after completion of accrual. The first analysis will occur after the last patient has completed 12 months of follow-up, and the second analysis will occur at the end of the study, 3 years after the last patient has been randomised. This is not an adaptive trial, there are no stopping rules for the analysis with 12 months follow-up, no alpha-spending or multiplicity adjustment, the rationale for two analyses is because some endpoints can be analysed once the last patient completed 1 year of follow-up while others require longer follow-up for mature data, which is common in many oncology trials.

All participating centres must complete site credentialing prior to activation. All cases must undergo peer review through a dedicated stereotactic chart round or by a radiation oncologist with SABR expertise. Ethics and dissemination

Ethics

This study received ethical approval from the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), under approval number 25/45 and HREC reference HREC/105165/PMCC. The coordinating PI is A/Prof Steven David. All participants will provide written informed consent using the approved PICF prior to enrolment. Each participating site will obtain local institutional research governance authorisation before commencing study activities.

Results of the study will be disseminated via peer-reviewed presentation at scientific conferences and open-access publication, in accordance with Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines, regardless of outcome. Authorship will follow International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) criteria, and participating investigators and contributors will be appropriately acknowledged.

Participant withdrawal and treatment discontinuation

Participants may discontinue trial treatment at any time, either by personal choice or at the discretion of the investigator due to clinical concerns, non-compliance, adverse events or disease progression. All reasons for discontinuation must be documented in the medical record and recorded in the eCRF. Patients who discontinue treatment will remain in the follow-up phase unless they formally withdraw consent. If consent is withdrawn, patients will be asked to specify whether they are withdrawing from further treatment, follow-up or both. Reasonable efforts, including multiple contact attempts, will be made to follow-up patients who miss visits; those who cannot be reached will be considered lost to follow-up. The trial sponsor may discontinue individual sites or the entire study at any time for reasons including safety concerns, insufficient accrual or non-compliance with protocol or Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not formally involved in the design, conduct, analysis or dissemination planning of this protocol or trial. The research question and primary endpoint were developed by the multidisciplinary investigator team based on clinical experience and evidence from the prior AVATAR trial, with input from site-specific multidisciplinary tumour boards involving patient case discussions. Results will be disseminated via open-access peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations accessible to patients and clinicians.

Discussion

OPD represents an increasingly recognised pattern of metastatic progression, particularly in patients with ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer receiving ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors. As systemic therapy effectively suppresses most disease sites, resistant subclones may emerge in isolated lesions, offering a potential opportunity for local intervention.

The AVATAR-II trial addresses a critical gap in the management of OPD. While retrospective data and single-arm studies, such as the original AVATAR trial, suggest a potential benefit from local ablative strategies such as SABR, prospective evidence in the breast cancer setting is lacking. Moreover, the use of SABR in this context has been more thoroughly explored in other malignancies, such as NSCLC where prospective trials have demonstrated improved PFS with local therapy to sites of OPD.

The AVATAR-II study builds on these findings by directly comparing SABR plus maintenance of first-line systemic therapy with physician’s choice of systemic therapy. This approach reflects real-world decision-making, where clinicians and patients often prefer to delay transitioning to more toxic second-line treatments if a less intensive, localised approach can maintain disease control.

The trial’s primary endpoint, TTF, is novel and reflects a pragmatic, patient-centred outcome. It encompasses both radiographic progression not amenable to SABR and systemic therapy changes, acknowledging the clinical nuance in managing metastatic disease. By focusing on TTF rather than traditional endpoints such as OS or time to next treatment, this study aims to evaluate the true benefit of delaying systemic therapy escalation while maintaining QoL.

The incorporation of quality-of-life assessments (FACT-B) and rigorous evaluation of TRAEs will also provide valuable insight into the tolerability and patient experience associated with SABR. This is particularly important in a population where long-term disease control is possible, and treatment decisions should balance efficacy with toxicity.

This trial was designed with pragmatism as a guiding principle, prioritising real-world applicability and reflecting clinical practice. The physician’s choice control arm reflects the general absence of standard of care in this setting and is intentional in its design. We acknowledge this limitation in methodology as it introduces treatment heterogeneity, and that TTF, as a novel endpoint, lacks the established surrogacy data of traditional measures. The open-label design is similarly inherent to the pragmatic framework. Crossover contamination is possible but is captured and recorded as a protocol event and will be reported fully at the time of results publication. The evolving systemic therapy landscape and the restriction of the trial to ER-positive, HER2-negative disease are acknowledged as factors that may affect generalisability and will be addressed fully in the reporting of trial results.

If successful, AVATAR-II may support a paradigm shift toward the integration of local ablative therapy in the management of OPD, not as a curative intervention but as a strategy to prolong the duration of response to well-tolerated systemic therapies. The results of this study will inform the design of future phase III trials and contribute to evolving standards of care in patients with metastatic breast cancer.
