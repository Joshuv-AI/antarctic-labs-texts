Abstract
. We study the compactness of Willmore surfaces without assuming the convergence of the induced complex structures. In particular, we compute the energy loss in the neck in terms of the residue and we prove that the limit of the image of the Gauss map is a geodesic in the Grassmannian G ⁡ ( 2 , n ) G(2,n) whose length can also be computed in terms of the residue. Moreover, we provide a family of explicit Willmore surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} that illustrate the denegeration phenomenon involved in the above results.

Introduction

In the previous paper
[
LY24
]
, the authors used the three-circle theorem to study the blow-up of Willmore surfaces. As an application, they provided new proofs to the removable singularity theorem by Kuwert-Schätzle and Rivière
[
KS04
,
KS07
,
Riv08
]
, as well as the energy identity by Bernard-Rivière
[
BR14
]
when the induced complex structures converge.

The convergence of the induced complex structure is very important for the above mentioned papers, because it implies the vanishing of some residue, which is the key to the proofs in
[
LY24
,
BR14
,
KS07
]
. In spite of the difficulty, Laurain and Rivière
[
LR18a
]
was able to show the validity of the energy identity under the assumption that the product of this residue with the length of the neck approaches zero. Very recently, Martino proved that energy identity is true when
n=3
and the index is bounded
[
Mar23
]
.

In this paper, we continue to investigate the neck part with possible non-zero residue. It is by now well known that the problem can be reduced to studying a sequence of Willmore immersions satisfying the following assumptions. (For the convenience of the readers, we include a brief outline of the reduction in the appendix.)

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

f_{k}:[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a conformal and Willmore immersion with
g_{k}=f_{k}^{*}(g_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})=e^{2u_{k}}(dt^{2}+d\theta^{2})
, which satisfies

A1)

u_{k}=-mt+v_{k}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, with

\|v_{k}(0,\theta)\|_{L^{\infty}(S^{1})}\leq 1,\,\,\,\,\|\nabla v_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T_{k}]\times S^{1})}\rightarrow 0,

A2)

\Theta_{k}=\sup_{t\in[0,T_{k}-1]}\int_{[t,t+1]\times S^{1}}|A_{k}|^{2}{d}V_{g_{k}}\rightarrow 0,

A3)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{k}(T_{k}-1,\theta)d\theta=0
.

Under assumptions A1), A2) and A3), we would like to study the following questions:

Q1)

What is the limit

\lim_{m\rightarrow+\infty}\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}W(f_{k},[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1})?

Q2)

What is the limit of the imagine of Gauss map?

Before we state our main theorems, we need to define the residue mentioned above.
Given
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n},S\in\mathfrak{so}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(the set of
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices) and a conformal and Willmore immersion
f:[0,T]\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(f,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(-2\int_{\{t\}\times S^{1}}\partial_{t}Hd\theta-4\int_{\{t\}\times S^{1}}(H\cdot A_{ti})g^{ij}(\partial_{j}f)d\theta+\int_{\{t\}\times S^{1}}|H|^{2}\partial_{t}fd\theta\big)\cdot c

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(f,S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\left\{t\right\}\times S^{1}}(H\cdot\partial_{t}(Sf)-\partial_{t}H\cdot Sf)d\theta-4\int_{\left\{t\right\}\times S^{1}}(H\cdot A_{ti})g^{ij}(\partial_{j}f\cdot Sf)d\theta

\displaystyle+\int_{\left\{t\right\}\times S^{1}}\left|H\right|^{2}\partial_{t}f\cdot Sfd\theta.

\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
are independent of
t
(see
[
LY24
]
). Please note that
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
are two of a series of quantities that are known either as residues, or conservation laws. They have long been known to be important in the study of Willmore surfaces
[
KS04
,
BR14
,
Ber16
,
BR13
]

For our purpose, we need to measure the size of
\tau_{2}
. Indeed,
\tau_{2}
can be considered as a linear function over

\mathfrak{so}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We take
\mathfrak{so}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as a subspace of
M(n)
, the linear space consisting of

n\times n
matrices, with the given inner product

\langle A,B\rangle=tr(AB^{T}),

and put

\|\tau_{2}(f,\cdot)\|=\sup_{\langle S,S\rangle=1}\tau_{2}(f,S).

Now, we can state our main theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f_{k}
be a conformal and Willmore immersion which satisfies A1)-A3). Then

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}W(f_{k},[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1})=\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}T_{k}.

Moreover, the limit of the image of the Gauss map is a geodesic in
G(2,n)

of length

\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\pi}\cdot\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|T_{k}.

Remark 1.2
.

In
[
LR18a
]
, Laurain and Rivière have shown that if
\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|T_{k}=0
, then there is no energy loss. In
[
Mar23
]
, Martino proved that the the image of the conformal Gauss map converges to a geodesic when
n=3
.

Remark 1.3
.

In the field of harmonic maps, the corresponding results can be found in
[
CT99
,
CLW12
,
Zhu10
]
.

Our main tool is the three-circle theorem suggested in the title of this paper. There are several versions of it, and in this paper, we will apply it several times to the mean curvature
H
, the second fundamental form
A
and the immersion
f
. The basic idea is to prove decay for certain quantities of an almost harmonic functions.

For some constant
L>0
to be determined in later proofs, we assume that there is
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

T_{k}=m_{k}L.

We define

Q_{i}=[(i-1)L,iL]\times S^{1}.

In
[
LY24
]
, the authors have verified that when
u
is a harmonic function defined on the cylinder, and if the Fourier expansion of

u
does not include the
e^{mt}
terms, then
\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2mt}u^{2}dtd\theta

satisfies the inequality that we call the three-circle property. In
[
LY24
]
,
\tau_{1}=0
and
\tau_{2}=0
so that the terms involving
e^{mt}
in the Fourier expansion of
H_{k}
are almost absent.
In this paper, we do not have
\tau_{2}=0
and hence to capture the obstruction to the three circle theorem, we make the following definitions for a general function
u(t,\theta)

(1.1)

\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}(\int_{0}^{2\pi}u\cos m\theta d\theta,\int_{0}^{2\pi}u\sin m\theta d\theta),

(1.2)

g^{+}(u)=\varphi^{\prime}+m\varphi,\,\,\,\,g^{-}(u)=m\varphi-\varphi^{\prime},

(1.3)

\mathcal{F}_{i}(u)=\frac{g^{+}({(2i-1)L/2})}{2m}e^{mt-m(2i-1)L/2}\cdot(\cos m\theta,\sin m\theta),

and

(1.4)

\mathcal{G}_{i}(u)=\frac{g^{-}({(2i-1)L/2})}{2m}e^{-mt+m(2i-1)L/2}\cdot(\cos m\theta,\sin m\theta).

When
u
is harmonic, we have the following expansion

(1.5)

u=at+b+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{e^{kt}(a_{k}\cos k\theta+b_{k}\sin k\theta)}_{=:u_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{e^{-kt}(a_{k}^{\prime}\cos k\theta+b_{k}^{\prime}\sin k\theta)}_{=:u_{-k}}.

It is clear that

\varphi(t)=(a_{m},b_{m})e^{mt}+(a_{m}^{\prime},b_{m}^{\prime})e^{-mt}=:\varphi_{m}+\varphi_{-m},

g^{-}(u)=2m(a_{m}^{\prime},b_{m}^{\prime})e^{-mt},g^{+}(u)=2m(a_{m},b_{m})e^{mt},

and hence

\mathcal{F}_{i}(u)=e^{mt}(a_{m}\cos m\theta+b_{m}\sin m\theta)=u_{m},

\mathcal{G}_{i}(u)=e^{-mt}(a_{m}^{\prime}\cos m\theta+b_{m}^{\prime}\sin m\theta)=u_{-m}.

So, the operators
\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}
are designed to be the projections to the eigenspaces when restricted to harmonic functions.

When
u
is only almost harmonic functions,
\mathcal{F}_{i}(u)
is then the obstruction of having the three circle lemma for
\int_{Q_{i}}\left|u\right|^{2}e^{-2mt}dtd\theta
. In other words, as long as
\mathcal{F}_{i}(u)
is small compared with
\int_{Q_{i}}\left|u\right|^{2}e^{-2mt}dtd\theta
, the three circle argument still works.

Following the above discussion, we define

\lambda_{i}(H_{k})=\sqrt{\frac{\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2mt}|\mathcal{F}_{i}(H_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta}{\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2mt}|H_{k}|^{2}dtd\theta}}.

When
\lambda_{i}
is smaller than some universal constant, we will be able to use the 3-circle for the equation of mean curvature (see Theorem

2.8
). We also define

\mu_{i}(f_{k})=\frac{W(f_{k},Q_{i})}{\int_{Q_{i}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}},

such that when
\mu_{i}
is smaller than another universal constant, the 3-circle works for the equation of the Gauss map (see Lemma
3.2
). We will also need

\nu_{i}(H_{k})=\frac{\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2mt}|H_{k}-\mathcal{F}_{i}(H_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta}{\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2mt}|\mathcal{F}_{1}(H_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta}.

When
\lambda_{i}
is bounded away from zero and
\nu_{i}
is small, we may use 3-circle for the equation of
H
again to see the decay of
\int_{Q_{i}}\left|H_{k}-\mathcal{F}_{i}(H_{k})\right|^{2}e^{-2mt}dtd\theta
(see Theorem
1.10

2.
3-circle lemma for elliptic equation

In this section, we first define what we mean by 3-circle lemma and then prove various versions of it.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q_{i}=[(i-1)L,iL]\times S^{1}
and
q>0
.
For each
Q_{i}
, we attach some quantity
\Phi_{i}\geq 0
. We say
\Phi_{i}
satisfies the 3-circle
lemma on
Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}\cup Q_{3}
for
(q,L)
, if

(2.1)

\Phi_{2}\leq e^{-qL}(\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{3}).

Throughout this paper,
L
depends on
q
. Hence, we may assume
e^{qL}>2
. Once
q
and
L
are fixed in some context, we set

(2.2)

q^{\prime}=q-\frac{\log 2}{L}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
e^{qL}>2
and
q^{\prime}
be given in (
2.2
).
Assume
\Phi_{i}
satisifes 3-circle lemma on
Q_{i-1}\cup Q_{i}\cup Q_{i+1}
for
(q,L)
.
Then

1)

either
\Phi_{i}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i-1}
or
\Phi_{i}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i+1}
;

2)

when
\Phi_{i-1}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i}
, there holds
\Phi_{i}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i+1}
;

3)

when
\Phi_{i+1}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i}
, there holds
\Phi_{i}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i-1}
;

Proof.

For the proof of 1), if

\Phi_{i}>e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{i}>e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i+1},

then

\Phi_{i}>\frac{1}{2}e^{-q^{\prime}L}(\Phi_{i-1}+\Phi_{i+1})=e^{-qL}(\Phi_{i-1}+\Phi_{i+1}),

which contradicts (
2.1
).

For the proof of 2), if
\Phi_{i-1}\leq e^{-q^{\prime}L}\Phi_{i}
, then (
2.1
) implies that

(1-e^{-qL}e^{-q^{\prime}L})\Phi_{i}\leq e^{-qL}\Phi_{i+1}.

The definition of
q^{\prime}
and the assumption
e^{qL}\geq 2
together imply that

\mu_{2}(f_{k})\leq 2e^{qL}\min\{\mu_{1}(f_{k}),\mu_{2}(f_{k}),\mu_{3}(f_{k})\}\to 0.

Setting
\phi_{k}=\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{k}}{\sqrt{\int_{Q_{2}}|\nabla\mathfrak{n}_{k}|^{2}dtd\theta}}=:\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{k}}{c_{k}}
, we have

(3.9)

\Delta_{g_{k}}\phi_{k}-A_{G(2,n)}(d\mathfrak{n}_{k},d\phi_{k})=\nabla_{1}^{\bot}\frac{H_{k}}{c_{k}}\wedge\partial_{2}f_{k}-\nabla_{2}^{\bot}\frac{H_{k}}{c_{k}}\wedge\partial_{1}f_{k},

\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{2})}=1\text{ and }\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\sqrt{1+e^{qL}}\leq C.

In order to control the right hand side above, we use the equation of
H_{k}
, which by Lemma
A.6
takes the form

\Delta H_{k}=\alpha_{k}H_{k}+\beta_{k}\nabla H_{k},

with
\|\alpha_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\beta_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0
. Here to verfiy the assumptions of Lemma
A.6
, we have used A1), A2) and the assumption that
\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C
.
Now, the elliptic estimate implies that for any
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{\bot}H_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}((\eta,3L-\eta)\times S^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\eta)\|H_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\leq 5C(\eta)\|H_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{2})}

\displaystyle=5C(\eta)\sqrt{\mu_{2}(f_{k})}\|\nabla\mathfrak{n}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{2})}=5C(\eta)c_{k}\sqrt{\mu_{2}(f_{k})}.

Combining this with
|\partial_{j}f_{k}|=e^{u_{k}}\to e^{u}
, we know

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}^{\bot}\frac{H_{k}}{c_{k}}\wedge\partial_{2}f_{k}-\nabla_{2}^{\bot}\frac{H_{k}}{c_{k}}\wedge\partial_{1}f_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}((\eta,3L-\eta)\times S^{1})}\leq C\sqrt{\mu_{2}(f_{k})}\to 0.

Moreover, we also have

\|A_{G(2,n)}(d\mathfrak{n}_{k},d\phi_{k})\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq C\|\nabla\mathfrak{n}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\to 0

thanks to A2) and the
\epsilon
-regularity.
In summary, we have proved that the
L^{2}
norm of
\Delta\phi_{k}
on
[\eta,3L-\eta]\times S^{1}
converges to zero.
Hence,

\|\phi_{k}-\bar{\phi}_{k}\|_{W^{2,2}[2\eta,3L-2\eta]}\leq C(\eta),\quad\forall\eta>0.

Thus
\phi_{k}-\bar{\phi}_{k}
converges in
W^{1,s}
to some harmonic function
\phi
for any
s<\infty
.
By (
3.2
), we know

\displaystyle|\int_{Q_{2}}(|\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}|^{2}-|\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial\theta}|^{2})dtd\theta|\leq C\frac{\|A_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{2},dV_{g_{k}})}\|H_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{2},dV_{g_{k}})}}{c^{2}_{k}}=C\sqrt{\mu_{2}(f_{k})}\to 0.

The convergence is strong enough so that

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{2}}|\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}|^{2}dtd\theta=\int_{Q_{2}}|\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}|^{2}dtd\theta.

The three-circle for harmonic function (Lemma
3.1
) implies that

\int_{Q_{2}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dtd\theta<e^{-qL}(\int_{Q_{1}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dtd\theta+\int_{Q_{3}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dtd\theta).

This contradicts (
3.8
). Therefore, (
3.6
) does not hold, that is,
\int_{Q_{i}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}
satisfies (
2.1
).

It remains to show that (
3.7
) is not true. For this purpose, we set
\tilde{q}=\frac{q+2}{2}
and by the first half that has been proved above, there exist
\delta_{3}
and
\epsilon_{2}
such that
\int_{Q_{i}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}
satisfies (
2.1
) for

.

By (
3.4
), if one of

vanishes, so do the other two. In this case,

for

and there is nothing to prove. Hence, we may assume all

(hence
\int_{Q_{i}}\left|A_{k}\right|^{2}dV_{g}
) are positive. It follows from the following computation that (

\frac{\frac{1}{\mu_{2}(f_{k})}}{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}(f_{k})}+\frac{1}{\mu_{3}(f_{k})}}\leq 2\frac{\int_{Q_{2}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}}{\int_{Q_{1}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}+\int_{Q_{3}}|A_{k}|^{2}dV_{g_{k}}}\leq 2e^{-\tilde{q}L}\leq e^{-qL}.

The proof of the lemma is complete.

Easy case.

We first discuss Theorem
1.1
in an almost trivial case, namely,

(5.1)

\limsup_{k\to\infty}(\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{\prime}-\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{\prime})=\tilde{T}<\infty.

In such cases, due to Part 1) of Theorem
1.9
, we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\|A_{k}\|^{p}_{L^{2}(Q_{i})}=0.

This implies that both the Willmore energy on the neck and the total length of the curve converge to zero. The energy part is trivial and the claim on the length follows from the formula

\displaystyle|\alpha_{k}(t_{1})-\alpha_{k}(t_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\nabla\hat{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}|(t,\theta)d\theta dt

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{i})},

for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,m_{k}L]
.
Hence, the image of
\alpha_{k}
converges (in the Hausdorff sense) to a single point.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
is done in this case by Part 5) of Theorem
1.7
, which implies that the limit of
\left\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\right\|
is zero.

5.2.
General case: energy and length

In what follows, we assume

\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{T}_{k}<\infty.

Similar to the discussion above, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}W(f_{k},[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1})=\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}W(\hat{f}_{k},[-\tilde{T}_{k}+l,\tilde{T}_{k}-l])

and

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\text{Length}(\alpha_{k})=\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\text{Length}(\alpha_{k},[-\tilde{T}_{k}+l,\tilde{T}_{k}-l]).

The first claim in Theorem
1.1
follows from (
5.3
) and Part 5) of Theorem
1.7
.

Remark 5.1
.

It is worth noting that Theorem
1.7
implies

\lim_{l\rightarrow+\infty}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\max_{\mathfrak{a}_{k}+l<i<\mathfrak{b}_{k}-l}\left|\frac{\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|}{\sqrt{W(f_{k},Q_{i})}}-2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L}}\right|=0.

In fact, if the above limit is not true, then we may find a subsequence
i_{k}
contradicting Part 5) of Theorem
1.7
.

For the length of
\alpha_{k}
, we derive from Par 2) and 5) of Theorem
1.7
that for any sequence
t_{k}
satisfying
t_{k}+\tilde{T}_{k}\to\infty
and
\tilde{T}_{k}-t_{k}\to\infty
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})|}{\sqrt{W(f_{k},Q_{[t_{k}]+\bar{i}_{k}})}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi L}},

where
[t_{k}]
is the unique
j
satisfying
t_{k}\in[(j-1)L,jL)
.

Hence,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\text{Length}(\alpha_{k})=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\pi}\cdot\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|m_{k}L.

5.3.
General case: being geodesic

To conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to show that
\alpha_{k}
in its arc-length parametrization converges in some sense to a geodesic in
G(2,n)
. For that purpose, we use
s
for the arc-length parameter, denote the parameter transformation by
t=\xi_{k}(s)
. Assume
\xi_{k}(0)=0
and for fixed
l
, set

S_{1k}:=\xi_{k}^{-1}(-\tilde{T}_{k}+l);\quad S_{2k}:=\xi_{k}^{-1}(\tilde{T}_{k}-l).

By taking subsequence, we assume

S_{1}=\lim_{k\to\infty}S_{1k};\quad S_{2}=\lim_{k\to\infty}S_{2k}.

Note that it is possible that the above limit is infinity.

We need the following notion of weak geodesic.

Definition 5.2
.

Assume that
M
is an embedded submaniold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\gamma:(a,b)\to M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{1,\alpha}
curve (parametrized by arc-length). It is said to be a weak geodesic if for any tangent vector field
V
of
M
such that
V\circ\gamma
has compact support, we have

\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d}{ds}\gamma(s)\cdot\frac{d}{ds}V(\gamma(s))ds=0.

Obviously, a smooth geodesic is a weak geodesic. Moreover, it is known that a weak geodesic is smooth and hence a classical one. In our case, we will regard
G(2,n)
as a submanifold of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

\{\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is
C^{2}
uniformly bounded on any compact subset of
I:=(S_{1},S_{2})
and converges to a geodesic
\alpha:I\to G(2,n)
with

\text{Length}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\pi}\cdot\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\tau_{2}(f_{k},\cdot)\|m_{k}L.

Proof.

In what follows, we use
\dot{\alpha}_{k}
for the
t
-derivative of
\alpha_{k}
.
By direct calculation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}}{ds}=\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))}{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))|},

and

(5.6)

\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}}{ds^{2}}=\frac{\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))}{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))|^{2}}-\langle\frac{\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))}{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))|^{2}},\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))}{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))|}\rangle\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))}{|\dot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))|}.

By the definition of arc-length paramtrization,
\left|d\alpha_{k}/ds\right|=1
. For the
C^{2}
bound, it suffices to estimate
\left|d^{2}\alpha_{k}/ds^{2}\right|
. By (
5.5
), this amounts to

(5.7)

\left|\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(\xi_{k}(s))\right|\leq CW(f_{k},Q_{[\xi_{k}(s)]}),

which we prove now.

For any fixed
s_{0}
, let
t_{k}=\xi_{k}(s_{0})
and
\tilde{i}_{k}=\bar{i}_{k}+[t_{k}]
and set (using (
1.10
))

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{W(f_{k},Q_{\tilde{i}_{k}})}

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{k}

\displaystyle=f_{k}^{\tilde{i}_{k}}(=(\hat{f}_{k})^{[t_{k}]})

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{n}(\tilde{f}_{k}).

Since translation and scaling do not change the Gauss map, if we define
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}
by

\tilde{\alpha}_{k}(t)=\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}^{*}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}(t,\theta)d\theta,\quad\text{for}\quad t\in[-\tilde{T}_{k}-[t_{k}]L,\tilde{T}_{k}-[t_{k}]L],

we have

\tilde{\alpha}_{k}(t)=\alpha_{k}(t+[t_{k}]L).

Using (
B.2
), we have

\displaystyle\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ddot{\tilde{\alpha}}_{k}(t_{k}-[t_{k}]L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}A_{G(2,n)}(d\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k},d\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k})+e^{-2\tilde{u}_{k}}(\nabla^{\perp}_{t}\tilde{H}_{k}\wedge\partial_{\theta}\tilde{f}_{k}-\nabla^{\perp}_{\theta}\tilde{H}_{k}\wedge\partial_{t}\tilde{f}_{k})d\theta\right)(t_{k}-[t_{k}]L).

Note that for any
t_{k}
, we always have

0\leq t_{k}-[t_{k}]L\leq L.

By Theorem
1.7
and the Part 1) of Lemma
4.1
(with
\tilde{f}_{k}
as
\hat{f}_{k}
there), we know that

\left|\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})\right|\leq C\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2},

from which (
5.7
) follows.
It also follows from Theorem
1.7
that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}}=-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}\wedge v_{2}-e_{2}\wedge v_{1})\in T_{-e_{1}\wedge e_{2}}G(2,n).

Together with (
5.7
), we obtained that
\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}
is bounded. Hence, we may assume that by taking subsequence,
\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}
converges in
C^{1,\alpha}
topology to a curve
\alpha(s)
.

To show that
\alpha(s)
is a weak geodesic, it suffices to show

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{S_{1}}^{S_{2}}\frac{d}{ds}\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s)\cdot\frac{d}{ds}V(\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s))ds=0

for any smooth tangent vector field
V
as in Definition
5.2
. Since
\alpha_{k}
is smooth, by integration by parts, the above is equivalent to

(5.8)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{S_{1}}^{S_{2}}\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s)\cdot V(\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s))ds=0.

Due to the Dominated Convergence Theorem, it suffices to show for each
s_{0}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\alpha_{k}\circ\xi_{k}(s_{0})\quad\text{is a normal vector of $G(2,n)$ at $\alpha(s_{0})$.}

Let
t_{k}
be as before.

\alpha(s_{0})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}(t_{k})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k}-[t_{k}]L)=-e_{1}\wedge e_{2},

where
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are those in Theorem
1.7
when we apply it to
\tilde{f}_{k}
. By (
5.6
), it remains to show

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{k})^{2}}\quad\text{is a normal vector of $G(2,n)$ at $-e_{1}\wedge e_{2}$.}

To see this, we use the equation

\frac{\ddot{\alpha}_{k}(t_{k})}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{k})^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}A_{G(2,n)}(\frac{d\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}},\frac{d\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}})+e^{-2\tilde{u}_{k}}\frac{1}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{k})^{2}}(\nabla^{\perp}_{t}\tilde{H}_{k}\wedge\partial_{\theta}\tilde{f}_{k}-\nabla^{\perp}_{\theta}\tilde{H}_{k}\wedge\partial_{t}\tilde{f}_{k})d\theta\right)(t_{k}-[t_{k}]L).

For the first term, the limit is a normal vector at
-e_{1}\wedge e_{2}
because
A_{G(2,n)}
is continuous and that
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}(t_{k}-[t_{k}]L,\theta)
converges uniformly to
-e_{1}\wedge e_{2}
. For the second term, we use the Part 2) of Lemma
4.1
.

The claim about the length has been proved in the previous subsection and the proof of Lemma
5.3
is done.

6.
An example of Willmore surface

In this section, we present a family of Willmore surfaces which is model case of what we have proved in this paper.

6.1.
A Willmore surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

The surface is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and we use the cylinder coordinate
(t,\theta)
where
t=\log\left|x\right|
. For some parameter
l>0
, define a skew-symmetric matrix

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&-l\\
0&0&0\\
l&0&0\end{array}\right).

The surface is parametrized by

(6.1)

f(t,\theta)=\exp(tA)(e^{-t}\cos\theta,e^{-t}\sin\theta,0)

for
(t,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}
. With the simple choice of
A
, we obtain

f(t,\theta)=(e^{-t}\cos(lt)\cos\theta,e^{-t}\sin\theta,e^{-t}\sin(lt)\cos\theta).

Note that
\exp(tA)
is a one parameter family of rotations in the direction of
A
and
l
is the speed of rotation.

The parametrization is not a conformal one, but we can compute the first fundamental form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f_{t}\cdot f_{t}&f_{t}\cdot f_{\theta}\\
f_{\theta}\cdot f_{t}&f_{\theta}\cdot f_{\theta}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-2t}(1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)&0\\
0&e^{-2t}\end{array}\right).

The normal vector is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{t}\times f_{\theta}}{\left|f_{t}\times f_{\theta}\right|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{l\cos(lt)\cos^{2}\theta-\sin(lt)}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}},-\frac{l\cos\theta\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}},-\frac{\cos(lt)+l\sin(lt)\cos^{2}\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}\right).

The second fundamental form is

\displaystyle A=-\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f_{t}\cdot\mathfrak{n}_{t}&f_{t}\cdot\mathfrak{n}_{\theta}\\
f_{\theta}\cdot\mathfrak{n}_{t}&f_{\theta}\cdot\mathfrak{n}_{\theta}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-t}l\cos\theta\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}&\frac{e^{-t}l\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}\\
\frac{e^{-t}l\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}&\frac{e^{-t}l\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}\end{array}\right)

The mean curvature function is

H=\frac{2e^{t}l\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+l^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}}.

We may also verify by direct computation that

\Delta H+\left|\mathring{A}\right|^{2}H=0.

and hence it is indeed a Willmore surface.

6.2.
The model case of the neck with nonvanishing residue

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{k}
be the map given by (
6.1
) with
l=\frac{1}{k}
. Obviously,
f_{k}
is a sequence of Willmore surfaces defined on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}
satisfying A1)-A3).

We can find the expansion of the some quantities in terms of the power series of
l=k^{-1}
.

•

The parametrization of the surface

f_{k}(t,\theta)=e^{-t}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)+te^{-t}(0,0,\cos\theta)k^{-1}+O(k^{-2}).

•

The (scalar) mean curvature

H_{k}=(2e^{t}\cos\theta)k^{-1}+O(k^{-3}).

•

The normal vector

\mathfrak{n}_{k}=(0,0,-1)-(-t+\cos^{2}\theta,\sin\theta\cos\theta,0)k^{-1}+O(k^{-2}).

•

The mean curvature vector

\vec{H}_{k}=(0,0,-2e^{t}\cos\theta)k^{-1}-2e^{t}\left(-t\cos\theta+\cos^{3}\theta,\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta,0\right)k^{-2}+O(k^{-3}).

•

The normalizing constant
\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\int_{[0,L]\times S^{1}}\left|H_{k}\right|^{2}dV_{g}=4\pi Lk^{-2}+O(k^{-3}),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=2k^{-1}+O(k^{-2}).

•

\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{k}-\bar{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}}{\epsilon_{k}}=\frac{1}{2}t(1,0,0)-\frac{1}{4}(\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta,0)+O(k^{-1}).

•

The Second fundamental form

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{tt},\hat{A}_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-t}\cos\theta\cdot k^{-1}+O(k^{-3})

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{t\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-t}\sin\theta\cdot k^{-1}+O(k^{-3}).

By taking
v_{1}=(0,0,-1)
and
v_{2}=(0,0,0)
, we verify that the above results agree with Theorem
1.7
and Theorem
1.10
.

6.3.
Generalizations

In dimension three, we may also consider some more general matrix
A
. For example, we may introduce one more parameter
\beta\in S^{1}
as follows

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&l\cos\beta\\
0&0&l\sin\beta\\
-l\cos\beta&-l\sin\beta&0\end{array}\right).

It is not difficult to see that (
6.1
) still gives the same surface up to a rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Moreover, we may consider a covering of the above surface. For example, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the surface

\tilde{f}(t,\theta)=f(mt,m\theta)

is also Willmore. They also arise as model case in the neck analysis.

We may also consider similar construction in dimension four. In this case,
\dim G(2,4)=4
. The infinitesimal rotation (including the speed) is a vector space of dimension four. Let

f(t,\theta):=\exp(tA)\left(e^{-mt}\cos m\theta,e^{-mt}\sin m\theta,0,0\right)

where

(6.3)

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&a&b\\
0&0&c&d\\
-a&-c&0&0\\
-b&-d&0&0\end{array}\right).

It is not easy to verify that this is indeed a Willmore surface in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. The problem is the expression of
\exp(tA)
is too difficult to be computed by hand. However, we did manage to verify the Willmore equation with the help of the computer software: Mathematica and the symmetry of surface. It is worth noting that the software produces symbolic computation, not numerical simulation. Therefore the result is rigorous.

Remark 6.1
.

We definitely want to have the upper-left corner to be zero. If otherwise, the first fundamental form is no longer diagonal. It is a rotation in the plane itself, not interesting.

Remark 6.2
.

When the upper-left corner is not zero, obviously, our computation fails. But we have no proof of the claim that ”they are not Willmore”. When the lower-right corner is not zero, the formulas used in this computation works, but we haven’t run the computer program for it (because it takes too long). Again, we can’t say they are not Willmore.
Our guess is that when they are not zero, they will not give essentially new surfaces.

Appendix A
The compactness of Willmore surfaces

In the first subsection below, we briefly explain why we may assume A1) and A2) in the study of energy idenity problem of Willmore surfaces. In the second subsection, we give consequences of the assumption A3), that will be used in the proof of the main theorem. In the final subsection, we prove a basic lemma about the structure of the curvature of the mean curvature and state without proof some formulas of the residues
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
under various transformations.

A.1.
Estimate on the conformal factor

A natural setting for the compactness problem is to consider a sequence of closed surfaces
\Sigma_{k}
with bounded
\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}}
. Simon’s monotonicity inequality
[
Sim93
]
ensures the existence of
\Lambda
such that the following holds for each
f_{k}
uniformly

(A.1)

\frac{\mu(f^{-1}(B_{r}^{n}(y)))}{\pi r^{2}}<\Lambda,\,\,\,\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\,\,\,r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

The upper bound (
A.1
) allows us to obtain uniform upper bound for the gradient of the conformal factor. Here we present a proof using the blowup technique as in
[
LWZ20
]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f
be a conformal and Willmore immersion from
D
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume (
A.1
) holds. Then there exist constants
C
and
\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}
, such that if
\int_{D}|A|^{2}d\mu<\epsilon_{0}^{\prime}
, then

\|\nabla u\|_{C^{0}(D_{\frac{1}{2}})}<C.

Proof.

Assume the Lemma is not true. Then we can find
f_{k}
such that
\int_{D}|A_{k}|^{2}d\mu_{k}\rightarrow 0
and

\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{C^{0}(D_{\frac{1}{2}})}\rightarrow+\infty.

Set

\rho_{k}=\sup_{x\in D_{2/3}}(2/3-|x|)|\nabla u_{k}|(x).

Then we can find
x_{k}\in D_{\frac{2}{3}}
, such that
\rho_{k}=(\frac{2}{3}-|x_{k}|)|\nabla u_{k}|(x_{k})
. Define

r_{k}=1/|\nabla u_{k}(x_{k})|
.

Since

\rho_{k}\geq(2/3-1/2)\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{C^{0}(D_{\frac{1}{2}})}\rightarrow+\infty,

we have

\frac{r_{k}}{2/3-|x_{k}|}=\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\rightarrow 0,

which implies that
D_{Rr_{k}}(x_{k})\subset D_{\frac{2}{3}}
for any fixed
R
, when
k

is sufficiently large.

Now consider a scaling and translation of
f_{k}
by

\tilde{f}_{k}(x)=e^{-u_{k}(x_{k})-\log r_{k}}(f_{k}(x_{k}+r_{k}x)-f_{k}(x_{k}))

and
v_{k}
is defined by
g_{F_{k}}=e^{2v_{k}}g_{euc}
, i.e.

v_{k}(x)=u_{k}(r_{k}x+x_{k})-u_{k}(x_{k}).

\tilde{f}_{k}
and
v_{k}
are defined on any
D_{R}
as long as
k
is large. We claim that for large
k

\sup_{x\in D_{R}}\left|\nabla v_{k}\right|(x)\leq 2.

In fact, for large
k
and any
x\in D_{R}
,
x_{k}+r_{k}x\in D_{\frac{2}{3}}
, therefore

(\frac{2}{3}-|x_{k}|)|\nabla u_{k}|(x_{k})\geq(2/3-|x_{k}+r_{k}x|)|\nabla u_{k}|(x_{k}+r_{k}x),

which yields that

\displaystyle\left|\nabla v_{k}\right|(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{k}|\nabla u_{k}(x_{k}+r_{k}x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{2}{3}-|x_{k}|}{2/3-|x_{k}+r_{k}x|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{2}{3}-|x_{k}|}{\frac{2}{3}-|x_{k}|-r_{k}R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\frac{r_{k}}{2/3-|x_{k}|}R}<2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2.

when
k
is sufficiently large.

Applying the
\epsilon
-regularity, we may assume
\tilde{f}_{k}
converges in
C^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to a conformal and Willmore immersion
\tilde{f}
from
\mathbb{C}
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
g_{\tilde{f}}=e^{2\tilde{v}}g_{euc}
. It is easy to check that
A_{\tilde{f}}=0
and
|\nabla\tilde{v}|<2
,
|\nabla\tilde{v}|(0)=1
. By Gauss curvature equation,
\tilde{v}
is harmonic and therefore
\tilde{v}(x)=ax^{1}+bx^{2}
with
a^{2}+b^{2}=1
. Choosing new coordinates, we may assume
\tilde{f}=e^{z}
which is a map from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We can choose
R
, such that

\frac{\mu_{f_{k}}(f_{k}(D_{Rr_{k}}(x_{k}))\cap B_{Rr_{k}}^{n}(x_{k}))}{\pi(Rr_{k})^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{\tilde{f}_{k}}(\tilde{f}_{k}(D_{R})\cap B_{R}^{n}(0))}{\pi R^{2}}\rightarrow\frac{\mu_{\tilde{f}}(\tilde{f}(D_{R})\cap B_{R}^{n}(0))}{\pi R^{2}}>2\Lambda,

which leads to a contradiction to (
A.1
).

\hfill\Box

, where
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is a finite set.

2)

After tanslation and rescaling,
\Sigma_{k}^{i}
converges to a plane in
C^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{i})
for
i=j_{0}+1
,
\cdots
,
j_{1}
. (We usually call them ‘ghost bubbles’).

3)

For
j>j_{1}
,
\Sigma_{k}^{j}
is a image of a conformal map
f_{kj}
from
[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1}
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that
f_{kj}
has no bubbles. (We usually call them the ‘neck’ part).

As a consequence of the above construction, to prove the energy identity, it is suffice to study a conformal and Willmore immersion
f_{k}
from
[0,T_{k}]\times S^{1}
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the assumption that
f_{k}
has no bubble. The assumption A2) is a consequence of this no more bubble assumption.

For a sequence of
f_{k}
satisfying A2), similar to Lemma 5.2 of
[
LY24
]
, by shrinking the length of the cylinder by a finite amount, we may assume

\left\|\nabla v_{k}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T_{k}]\times S^{1})}\to 0.

By a scaling of
f_{k}
if necessary, we have A1).

A.2.
Consequences of the assumption A3)

For any sequence
f_{k}
satisfying A1) and A2), we may always assume the extra A3) by a translation of
f_{k}
, because all the results in the main theorems are invariant under translations. Nonetheless, A3) is necessary if we want to obtain good control over
f_{k}
.

Lemma A.4
.

Assume
f_{k}
is a sequence of conformal and Willmore immersions from
[0,m_{k}L]\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying A1) and A2). Let

u_{k}^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u_{k}(t,\theta)d\theta,\,\,\,\,and\,\,\,\,f^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{k}(t,\theta)d\theta.

Then

(1) for any
t\in[0,m_{k}L]
,

u_{k}(t,\theta)\leq-\frac{mt}{2}+2.

In particular

\text{Vol}(f_{k},[0,m_{k}L]\times S^{1})\leq\frac{2\pi e^{4}}{m}.

(2) for any
1<t<T\leq m_{k}L-1
, we have

(A.2)

e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t)}|f_{k}^{*}(t)-f_{k}^{*}(T)|\leq C[\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}+(T-t)e^{-\frac{m}{2}(T-t)}],

Proof.

(1) By A1),
(u^{*}_{k})^{\prime}<-\frac{m}{2}
when
k
is sufficiently large, which implies that

(A.3)

u_{k}^{*}(s)-u_{k}^{*}(t)\leq-\frac{m}{2}(s-t),\,\,\,\,\forall t<s.

Also by A1), we have

\displaystyle u_{k}(s,\theta)\leq u_{k}^{*}(s)+\text{osc}_{\{s\}\times S^{1}}v_{k}\leq-\frac{ms}{2}+u_{k}^{*}(0)+2\pi\|\nabla v_{k}\|\leq-\frac{ms}{2}+2,

and hence

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(f_{k},[0,m_{k}L]\times S^{1})=\int_{[0,m_{k}L]\times S^{1}}e^{2u_{k}}dtd\theta\leq\frac{2\pi e^{4}}{m}.

(2)
By the
\epsilon
-regularity of Willmore surface, we have
|e^{u_{k}}H_{k}|(t,\theta)<C\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}
for
t\in[1,m_{k}L-1]
, which implies that for
1\leq t\leq T\leq m_{k}L-1
,

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(t)-\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(T)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Delta fd\theta ds|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{2u_{k}}H_{k}d\theta ds|\leq C\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}\int_{t}^{T}e^{u_{k}^{*}(s)}ds.

Here in the last line above, we have used the fact that
\left|\partial_{\theta}u_{k}\right|\leq C
.
By (
A.3
),

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{u_{k}^{*}(t)}\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\frac{m}{2}(s-t)}ds+\left|\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(T)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{u_{k}^{*}(t)}\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}+\left|\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(T)\right|.

Using the definition of
f_{k}^{*}
, we estimate the second term above as

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}f_{k}^{*}(T)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\partial_{t}f_{k}\right|(T,\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cu_{k}^{*}(T).

Integrating over
[t,T]
, we obtain

|f_{k}^{*}(t)-f_{k}^{*}(T)|\leq C\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}\int_{t}^{T}e^{u_{k}^{*}(s)}ds+Cu_{k}^{*}(T)(T-t).

Multiplying both sides by
e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t)}
and using (
A.3
) again, we have

\displaystyle e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t)}|f_{k}^{*}(t)-f_{k}^{*}(T)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}\int_{t}^{T}e^{u_{k}^{*}(s)-u_{k}^{*}(t)}ds+Ce^{u_{k}^{*}(T)-u_{k}^{*}(t)}(T-t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sqrt{\Theta_{k}}\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\frac{m}{2}(s-t)}ds+C(T-t)e^{-\frac{m}{2}(T-t)},

which yields the lemma.

\hfill\Box

Corollary A.5
.

Assume
f_{k}
is a sequence of conformal and Willmore immersions from

[0,m_{k}L]\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying A1)-A3).
Then, we have

(A.4)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\max_{1\leq t\leq(m_{k}-1)L}e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t)}|f_{k}^{*}(t)|=0,

and

(A.5)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{2\leq i\leq m_{k}-2}\int_{Q_{i}}e^{-2u_{k}}|f_{k}-\mathcal{G}_{i}(f_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta=0.

Proof.

We first argue by contradiction to show (
A.4
). If (
A.4
) is not true, then there exists
t_{k}\in[1,(m_{k}-1)L)
such that

e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}|f_{k}^{*}(t_{k})|\to\eta>0.

If
m_{k}L-t_{k}\to\infty
, then (
A.4
) follows from A3) and Lemma
A.4
by taking
t=t_{k}
and
T=m_{k}L-1
. Hence, we may assume that
\sup_{k}(m_{k}L-1)-t_{k}<+\infty
. Without loss of generality, we assume
s_{k}:=(m_{k}L-1)-t_{k}\to s_{\infty}
. Then by A1)-A3) and the
\epsilon
-regularity of Willmore immersions, we know that

e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}f_{k}(t_{k}+t,\theta)\to cRf_{\infty}+v,\text{ on }[-1,s_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}]\times S^{1}

for some
c>0,R\in SO(n),v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
f_{\infty}(t,\theta)=\frac{1}{m}e^{-mt}(e_{1}\cos m\theta+e_{2}\sin m\theta)
. Noticing that

\lim_{k\to\infty}|e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}\partial_{t}f_{k}(t_{k},\theta)|=e^{u_{k}(t_{k},\theta)-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}=1,

we find that
c=1
. Moreover, by A3) and the fact that
t_{k}+s_{k}=m_{k}L-1
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}f^{*}_{k}(t_{k}+s_{k})=v=0.

This is a contradiction, because

0<\eta=\lim_{k\to\infty}e^{-u_{k}^{*}(t_{k})}|f_{k}^{*}(t_{k})|=|Rf_{\infty}^{*}(0)|=0.

Thus, we have proved (
A.4
)

For the proof of (
A.5
), it suffices to show that for any sequence
i_{k}\in[2,m_{k}-2]
, there holds

(A.6)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{Q_{i_{k}}}e^{-2u_{k}}|f_{k}-\mathcal{G}_{i_{k}}(f_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta=0.

For this purpose, we consider the same scaling as above. Namely, by setting
t_{k}=(i_{k}-1)L
, we consider

\tilde{f}_{k}(t,\theta)=e^{-u^{*}_{k}(t_{k})}f_{k}(t+t_{k},\theta).

The difference is that we now have (
A.4
), with the help of which we may derive from A1) and A2) that

\tilde{f}_{k}\to cRf_{\infty}\quad\text{on}\quad[-L,L],

for
c,R
and
f_{\infty}
as before. For the same reason as above, we have
c=1
.
On the other hand,

\int_{Q_{i_{k}}}e^{-2u_{k}}|f_{k}-\mathcal{G}_{i_{k}}(f_{k})|^{2}dtd\theta=\int_{Q_{1}}e^{-2\tilde{u}_{k}}\left|\tilde{f}_{k}-\mathcal{G}_{1}(\tilde{f}_{k})\right|^{2}dtd\theta.

Since
f_{\infty}-\mathcal{G}_{1}(f_{\infty})=0
, the limit of the right hand side vanishes. Hence (
A.6
) (and therefore (
A.5
)) is proved.

\hfill\Box

A.3.
The equation of mean curvature

The following lemma shows how the equation for the mean curvature of a Willmore surface is of the form of the nonlinear equation discussed in Section
2.2
.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
f
be a conformal and Willmore immersion from
[a,b]\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u
be defined by
df\otimes df=e^{2u}(dt^{2}+d\theta^{2})
. Assume that

u\leq C,\,\,\,\,\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}([a,b]\times S^{1})}\leq C\,\,\,\,\text{ and }\int_{[a,b]\times S^{1}}|A|^{2}dV_{g}\leq\epsilon

for some
C>0
and
\epsilon
sufficiently small.
Then

|\Delta H|\leq\alpha\cdot(|H|+|\nabla H|)

with

\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}([a+\delta,b-\delta]\times S^{1})}<C(\delta)\|A\|_{L^{2}([a,b]\times S^{1})}

for small
\delta>0
.

Proof.

Recall that the Willmore equation in conformal coordinates is (see Section 2 of
[
LY24
]
)

\displaystyle\Delta H=-2\partial_{q}(H\cdot A_{pq}e^{-2u}f_{p})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{q}(|H|^{2}f_{q}).

The right hand side of the above equation is

\displaystyle rhs:=-2\partial_{q}(H\cdot A_{pq}e^{-2u})f_{p}-2H\cdot A_{pq}e^{-2u}f_{pq}+H\cdot H_{q}f_{q}+\frac{1}{2}|H|^{2}He^{2u},

where we have used
\Delta f=He^{2u}
.

For
\epsilon
small, we can use the
\epsilon
-regularity to bound

\sup_{[a+\delta,b-\delta]\times S^{1}}\left|H\right|+\left|A_{pq}\right|+\left|\partial H\right|+\left|\partial A_{pq}\right|\leq C(\delta)\left\|A\right\|_{L^{2}([a,b]\times S^{1})}.

Due to the upper bound of
u
, the first derivatives of
f
are bounded. For the second order derivatives of
f
, we note that

f_{pq}=A_{pq}+\langle f_{pq},f_{l}\rangle e^{-2u}f_{l}.

Taking derivative of the equations
\langle f_{1},f_{1}\rangle=e^{2u}
, we can bound

\langle f_{11},f_{1}\rangle,\quad\langle f_{12},f_{1}\rangle.

Moreover, due to
\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=0
, we have
\langle f_{11},f_{2}\rangle=-\langle f_{1},f_{12}\rangle
. Switching
1
and
2
in the subscript, we are able to bound all
\langle f_{pq},f_{l}\rangle
, hence
f_{pq}
.
The lemma follows easily from the explicit expression of
rhs
and the above bounds on
H
,
A
,
u
,
f
and their derivatives.

\hfill\Box

Lemma A.7
.

For any

and

Appendix B
Gauss maps and their tension fields

In this appendix, we collect some formulas and properties on the Grassmannian
G(2,n)
, among which is the explicit expression for Ruh and Vilms’ formula
[
RV70
]
in an isothermal coordinate system.

Let
\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the
p
-th wedge product of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. With the standard inner product of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can define one for
\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by asking

e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{p}},\qquad i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}

to be an orthonormal basis of
\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if
(e_{i})
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, the inner product of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given by the formula

\langle v_{1}\wedge w_{1},v_{2}\wedge w_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle-\langle v_{1},w_{2}\rangle\langle v_{2},w_{1}\rangle.

Given
P\in G(2,n)
, an oriented plane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Let
(e_{1},e_{2})
be an orthonormal basis of
P
that agrees with the orientation of
P
. The map

P\rightarrow e_{1}\wedge e_{2}

gives an isometric embedding from
G(2,n)
into
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}
. Throughout the paper, we often use
e_{1}\wedge e_{2}
to represent a point in
G(2,n)
.

For
P=e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\in G(2,n)
, assume that
e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}
is a choice of orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the tangent space
T_{P}G(2,n)
is spanned by

\{e_{i}\wedge e_{\alpha}|\,i=1,2,\,\,\,\,\alpha=3,\cdots,n\}.

To see this, we know the dimension
\dim G(2,n)=2(n-2)
on one hand, while on the other hand,
\gamma(t)=e_{1}\wedge(e_{2}\cos t+e_{\alpha}\sin t)
is a curve in
G(2,n)
with

\gamma(0)=e_{1}\wedge e_{2},\,\,\,\,\dot{\gamma}(0)=e_{1}\wedge e_{\alpha},

which implies that
e_{1}\wedge e_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha=3,\cdots,n
is in the tangent space.
Using the formula of inner product, we derive that the normal space at
P
is spanned by

\{e_{1}\wedge e_{2},e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{\beta}|\,\alpha,\beta=3,\cdots,n\}.

Now, we let
f:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a conformal map with
g=e^{2u}g_{euc}
, and set
\Sigma=f(D)
. For each
x\in D
, let
e_{1}=e^{-u}f_{1}
,
e_{2}=e^{-u}f_{2}
and choose
\left\{e_{3},\cdots,e_{n}\right\}
such that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that depends on
x
smoothly. In what follows, we use the Latin letters for
1
or
2
and Greek letters for
3,\cdots,n
.
The Gauss map
\mathfrak{n}
of
f
and the second fundamental form are given by

\mathfrak{n}=e_{1}\wedge e_{2},\,\,\,\,A_{ij}=(f_{ij})^{\bot}=\langle f_{ij},e_{\alpha}\rangle e_{\alpha}=-\langle f_{i},\frac{\partial e_{\alpha}}{\partial x^{j}}\rangle e_{\alpha}.

Moreover, we have

f_{ij}=e^{-2u}\langle f_{ij},f_{k}\rangle f_{k}+A_{ij},\,\,\,\,\frac{\partial e_{\alpha}}{\partial x^{j}}=B_{j\alpha}^{\beta}e_{\beta}-e^{-2u}A_{jk}^{\alpha}f_{k},

where
B_{i\alpha}^{\beta}
are the coefficients for the induced connection in the normal bundle. Then

(B.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathfrak{n}}{\partial x^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial e_{1}}{\partial x^{i}}\wedge e_{2}+e_{1}\wedge\frac{\partial e_{2}}{\partial x^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\frac{\partial e_{1}}{\partial x^{i}},e_{\alpha}\rangle e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{2}+e_{1}\wedge\langle\frac{\partial e_{2}}{\partial x^{i}},e_{\alpha}\rangle e_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(f_{i},e_{1})\wedge e_{2}+e_{1}\wedge A(f_{i},e_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-2u}(A_{i1}\wedge f_{2}+f_{1}\wedge A_{i2}).

Let
J
is the complex structure on
\Sigma
(induced from that on
D
), i.e.

J(f_{1})=f_{2},\,\,\,\,J(f_{2})=-f_{1}.

It follows from (
B.1
) that

\frac{\partial\mathfrak{n}}{\partial x^{i}}=A_{ip}\wedge J(f_{q})g^{pg}

Next, we take a normal frame of
T\Sigma
at
x
, denoted by
(\tilde{e}_{1},\tilde{e}_{2})
. Namely, they are orthonormal at
T_{x}\Sigma
and
\nabla_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{e}_{j}(x)=0
. We write
\tilde{A}_{ij}
for
A(\tilde{e}_{i},\tilde{e}_{j})
. Then the above equation becomes

\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathfrak{n})=\tilde{A}_{ip}\wedge J(\tilde{e}_{p}).

Using
\nabla_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{e}_{j}(x)=0
, we have, at
x
,

\Delta_{g}\mathfrak{n}(x)=\tilde{e}_{i}(\tilde{A}_{ip}\wedge J(\tilde{e}_{p})).

Here we have taken
\tilde{A}_{ip}
,
J(\tilde{e}_{p})
as
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued functions defined on
\Sigma
and
\tilde{e}_{i}
acts on them by taking directional derivatives. We use
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
to denote the projections to the tangent and the normal directions of the surface (at a fixed point), then for any vector
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V=\pi_{1}(V)+\pi_{2}(V)
. For our purpose, we are interested in the projection of
\Delta_{g}\mathfrak{n}(x)
onto the tangent space of
G(2,n)
at
\mathfrak{n}(x)
. Keeping in mind that for any
V,W\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\pi_{2}(V)\wedge\pi_{2}(W)
and
\pi_{1}(V)\wedge\pi_{1}(W)
are in the normal space, we have

\displaystyle\text{the tangent projection of}\,\Delta_{g}\mathfrak{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\tilde{e}_{i}}^{\perp}\tilde{A}_{ip}\wedge J(\tilde{e}_{p})+\tilde{A}_{ip}\wedge\nabla_{\tilde{e}_{i}}(J\tilde{e}_{p}).

Here
\nabla^{\perp}
and
\nabla
are the induced connections of the normal and the tangent bundle of the surface
\Sigma
respectively.
Using the Codazzi-Mainardi equation
\nabla^{\perp}_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{A}_{ip}(x)=\nabla^{\perp}_{\tilde{e}_{p}}H(x)
, the fact that
J
is parallel and
\nabla_{\tilde{e}_{i}}\tilde{e_{p}}(x)=0
, we derive the formula for the tension field of
\mathfrak{n}

\tau(\mathfrak{n})(x)=\text{the tangent projection of}\,\,\,\,\Delta_{g}\mathfrak{n}(x)=\nabla^{\perp}_{\tilde{e}_{p}}H\wedge J(\tilde{e}_{p}).

Or equivalently, using the second fundamental form of
G(2,n)
in
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(B.2)

\tau(\mathfrak{n})=\Delta\mathfrak{n}-A_{G(2,n)}(d\mathfrak{n},d\mathfrak{n})=e^{-2u}\left(\nabla^{\bot}_{1}H\wedge f_{2}-\nabla^{\bot}_{2}H\wedge f_{1}\right).
