Innovative vision rehabilitation method for epiretinal membrane: Enhancing visual functions through Biofeedback Training
Authors: Mariana Akemi Matsura Misawa, Samuel N. Markowitz, Monica Daibert-Nido
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report a case with epiretinal membrane (ERM) treated with audio-luminous Biofeedback training (BT) which resulted in improvement of monocular and binocular visual functions.ObservationsWe report a case of ERM with distance Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 in the right dominant eye, and 20/20 in the left eye. The main symptom was binocular blurry vision, and the patient did not desire to pursue posterior vitrectomy due to its risks. Using a 10-2 microperimetry test with the MAIA Microperimeter, it was possible to identify an inferior paracentral relative scotoma in the right eye with splitting fixation. The right eye was trained with five 20-minute sessions with Biofeedback training towards a trained retinal locus (TRL) with better retinal sensitivity and 1.46° superior and temporal to the current preferred retinal locus (PRL). After training, the patient reported total improvement of symptoms, and BCVA was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye.Conclusions and importanceBiofeedback training is a valuable treatment with no known adverse effects that can be performed as an alternative to patients with ERM with no surgical indication and debilitating symptoms, or as a post-surgery adjuvant treatment.

Introduction

Epiretinal membrane (ERM) is a common retinal disease characterized by the proliferation of abnormal cells that form a preretinal membrane, which can cause distortion of the inner and outer retina due to contraction.1 The primary symptoms include decreased visual acuity and metamorphopsia, while diplopia and aniseikonia can result from image disparity.1,2 Studies show that ERM prevalence varies from 2 % to 34 % depending on the sensitivity of the test used for detection, and most cases are considered to be idiopathic (iERM).3,4 Secondary causes include previous cataract surgery, retinal detachment, diabetic retinopathy, and uveitis 1,2. Posterior vitrectomy surgery with peeling of the ERM and internal limiting membrane (ILM) is currently the only treatment available.2,5 However, the optimal time for surgery is still uncertain, and treatment decisions are made case by case based on visual acuity and subjective symptoms.4,6,7

We present herein a case treated with an innovative therapy called audio-luminous biofeedback training (BT) for eccentric viewing. This therapy has previously demonstrated success in treating other conditions, such as age-related macular degeneration, hemianopsia, and nystagmus.8, 9, 10 The objective of BT is to enhance fixation stability and relocate the preferred retinal locus (PRL) to an area of the retina with greater retinal sensitivity, which is identified by a microperimetry test. Training the fixation stability and improving oculomotor functions, leads to an optimized new PRL and better functional binocular vision.

Case report

Here, we report the case of a 71-year-old Caucasian woman diagnosed with ERM in the right eye after cataract surgery in 2019. A detailed fundoscopic examination or optical coherence tomography (OCT) was not possible before the procedure due to media opacity. The most probable etiology was idiopathic epiretinal membrane, since the symptoms began right after surgery and the diagnosis was made only a few days after cataract extraction. Her main complaint was binocular blurry vision with a ghost image and decreased visual acuity in the right eye. She experienced relief in her symptoms when she closed her right eye, which was her dominant eye. To evaluate the dominance, the patient was instructed to form a triangle with her fingers and to look at a distant object to verify which eye was her dominant.

At presentation, 3 years after ERM diagnosis, the best corrected visual acuity was 20/40−2 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye, as tested with the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart. The continuous binocular reading ability test was 20/20 using the Colenbrander Reading Card. Contrast sensitivity in the right eye was tested with the Vistech Vision Contrast Test System 6500 chart, and it was within normal limits (1.54 (0.5 cy/d), 1.93 (1.0 cy/d), 1.85 (2.0 cy/d), 1.94 (4.0 cy/d), and 1.6 (6.0 cy/d) log units). Objective binocular function tests were not performed before the training session. Nevertheless, the patient reported observing a "ghost" image from the right eye during binocular vision. This subjective complaint hinted an impairment in binocularity. Intraocular pressure and anterior segment examination were within normal limits with posterior chamber intraocular lenses in both eyes. Dilated fundoscopic examination showed foveal blunting and wrinkling of the posterior pole with vascular tortuosity and posterior vitreous detachment in the right eye. Fundoscopy in the left eye was within normal limits.

Spectral Domain OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA) in the right eye (Fig. 1) showed a hyper-reflective line above the ILM, retinal wrinkling, and absence of the foveal pit. No ellipsoid or cone outer segment defects were detected. According to the ETDRS macular grid, the central foveal thickness was 404 μm, with increased thickness of the inner macular ring and the inferior and temporal quadrants of the outer rings (Fig. 2). Based on the classification system proposed by Govetto et al.,11 the patient had stage 3 ERM. At the time of the diagnosis in 2019, the patient was advised to undergo pars plana vitrectomy, but she declined due to surgical risk. We proposed audio-luminous BT as a non-invasive alternative treatment. We programmed the training to relocate fixation in the patient's right eye to a 1.5° superior area with better retinal sensitivity. By doing so, monocular and binocular visual functions would improve and her symptoms would be alleviated as the non-dominant eye (left eye) would be able to use the corresponding superior area.

To assess the safety and feasibility of the method for this patient, we placed 3 prism diopters with base up at 90° degrees on top of the distance prescription in both eyes. Considering 1° is equivalent to 1.75 prism diopters, 3 prism diopters could relocate the fixation 1.7°. The patient reported an immediate and complete improvement in her visual symptoms. With the prisms, her distance BCVA was 20/32 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye. For near, her symptoms improved with 2 prism diopters with base up in both eyes.

The patient underwent five weekly sessions of 20 minutes of audio-luminous BT in the MAIA Microperimeter (total of 100 minutes). During the training, a trained retinal locus (TRL) was set 1.46° superior to the patient's current preferred retinal locus (PRL) in the right eye. After the training, the patient reported significant symptom improvement, and did not require prism glasses or occlusion of the right eye to see better. Visual function tests were repeated 1 month after the end of the treatment, and distance BCVA was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye on the ETDRS charts. Her near vision improved to 20/16 with both eyes, and her contrast sensitivity in the right eye increased from 1.85 log to 2.1 log on the 2.0 cycles/degree (Table 1). Frisby stereotest was administered, revealing that stereopsis was at a level of 600″, thus indicating that the epiretinal membrane (ERM) was indeed affecting and compromising binocular vision. The PRL in the trained eye relocated effectively 0.51° superiorly, and the average retinal sensitivity in the right eye improved from 18.5 to 20.0 dB (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Discussion

Our patient presented with an iERM, which deeply affected her functional vision and quality of life as her right eye was the dominant one. Considering she did not desire PPV, we pioneered BT as an alternative treatment to improve visual functions in this ERM case. The results were very positive, and the patient's subjective symptoms completely improved.

ERM is a common retinal disease, and age is one of the most consistent risk factors, along with posterior vitreous detachment (PVD).1,12 Most cases are idiopathic, but up to 32.2 % may have secondary causes, with previous cataract surgery being the most common (∼77 %).1 Our patient was diagnosed with an iERM since the symptoms started immediately after the cataract extraction, when media opacity was resolved.

Idiopathic ERM progresses anatomically in 17–39 % of cases over a 24-month period,4,11,13 meaning more than half will remain stable or improve spontaneously. The anatomical factors that predict progression are no PVD at baseline, global attachment at baseline, early stage of iERM according to the Govetto classification, and a decrease of disk-fovea-vascular distance during follow-up.4,11 Our patient presented a partial attachment, stage 3, with PVD. Her visual functions were stable since diagnosis, and her ERM would probably remain stable or progress slowly over time.

Epiretinal membrane (ERM) is a condition that often causes mild or no symptoms, and most patients with idiopathic ERM have visual acuity of 20/30 or better.2,4 The standard practice for these patients is to monitor their visual functions and macular anatomy periodically with OCT scans. However, some patients may experience severe symptoms that affect their quality of life and daily activities, such as blurry vision, metamorphopsia, aniseikonia, and decreased stereopsis. For patients with moderate to severe symptoms, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with ERM peeling is a highly effective treatment that can improve vision by two lines on average.2,14 Over the last decades, technology has rapidly evolved, leading to significantly safer and more precise surgical procedures such as microincision vitrectomy, among other features.15 Nonetheless, the procedure is the only current therapy available for ERM cases and carries some risks and complications, such as retinal breaks and detachment, accidental intraoperative lens damage, endophthalmitis, and phototoxicity.2,16 Furthermore, up to 70 % of the patients evolve with nuclear cataract postoperatively.2

Given the potential risks associated with PPV, the doctor-patient decision in borderline cases is not easy. Even if patients are experiencing debilitating symptoms, some may choose to delay surgery or may not have a clear indication for it yet. Because patients with ERM may develop relative central vision loss and relocate to a new PRL with poorer fixation stability and retinal sensitivity, we propose the use of BT as an alternative therapy to improve visual functions. BT can also be considered as an adjuvant treatment in postoperative cases. BT is a safe and well-established technique in Low Vision Rehabilitation that aims to relocate the patient's preferred retinal locus (PRL) to an area with better retinal sensitivity, called the trained retinal locus (TRL).

BT is a technique that has been studied for many years within the Ophthalmology field, especially for diseases with central vision loss,17 such as AMD.18 Amore et al.19 conducted a systematic review of 25 peer-reviewed studies focusing on BT. Their findings indicated that eight of the studies showed improvement in most of the visual functions, i.e., visual acuity for distance and near, reading speed, and macular sensitivity. Additionally, other studies revealed improvements in a subset of these variables. The review highlighted that BT can effectively enhance oculomotor control and visual functions. Nevertheless, the diverse range of BT techniques employed across the studies, the variability of diseases under investigation, and the lack of control group in the majority of cases makes it difficult to comprehensively assess the overall effectiveness of BT.

In this particular case, the patient demonstrated improvement in monocular visual acuity for distance in the right eye, binocular visual acuity for near vision, fixation stability in the right eye, and reported a great subjective symptom improvement. Furthermore, the fixation point had a superior relocation, aligning with the predetermined TRL point for training. The selection of the TRL typically involves choosing the area with best retinal sensitivity points, ideally in the superior region of the retina, as close as possible to the foveola (as the farther from the fovea, the worse visual acuity is), and with a broader retinal span. In this case, the selected point was located adjacent to the superior parafoveal point with the highest retinal sensitivity. To justify this choice, trial with prisms prior to the training was performed to ensure that the fellow eye could functionally adjust to the TRL and that the symptoms would improve without causing diplopia. Given the patient's high level of contentment with prisms base up, the training point was consequently determined. Considering 1° of relocation is equivalent to 1.75 prism diopters, a relocation according to the TRL set for training can be simulated with prisms. The final PRL relocation really used by the patients on their visual activities rarely reaches the same amount of relocation as trained. However, the functional level achieved compensates for the visual loss and improves significantly the visual functions, as seen in this case.

Conclusion

The gold standard treatment for ERM is PPV, nevertheless, for some patients who wish to delay or avoid surgery or who do not have a definite surgical indication, BT can serve as a valuable functional addressing alternative. BT can potentially improve visual functions in patients with ERM even after surgery. This treatment could be particularly advantageous for individuals with unstable fixation and relative or absolute central vision loss.
