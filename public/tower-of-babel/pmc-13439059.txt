The Compilation of Ocean Economic Input-Output Tables of China from 2002-2020
Authors: Jianyue Ji, Yue Huang, Zhihui Yang, Shaopeng Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ocean economic input-output tables serve as a fundamental data source for analyzing internal industrial linkages within the ocean economy and its connections to the land-based economy. However, existing ocean economic input-output studies in China predominantly consist of cross-sectional tables for specific years, lacking consistent long-term time series data. This constraint limits deeper inquiry into temporal structural change in the ocean economy. In response, we assemble a longitudinal set of China’s ocean economic input-output tables covering 19 marine sectors and 42 land-based sectors. This was achieved using the stripping coefficient method and an improved generalized RAS approach, based on China’s official input-output tables and ocean economy statistics from 2002 to 2020. This dataset demonstrates high reliability and validity based on multidimensional technical validation, which covers internal consistency, time-series smoothness, and external data comparisons. This dataset underpins advanced studies on China’s marine economy supply chain resilience, structural dynamics, and land-sea interdependencies, and informs sustainability-oriented ocean development.

Background & Summary

The ocean economy constitutes a vital component of the national economy1–3. Within the global economic landscape, it is emerging as a key growth engine for expanding blue space and driving sustainable development4, with its strategic value gaining widespread international recognition5. From 2010 to 2030, the marine economy is expected to outpace worldwide growth, heralding an unprecedented era of blue growth6. As a maritime power with vast maritime territories and abundant resources7, China is a major global participant in marine product production, consumption, and trade8,9, with its ocean economic development drawing particular attention10. Since Reform and Opening-up, China’s marine economy has expanded substantially, with its strategic importance continuously rising. In 2024, China’s ocean GDP surpassed 10 trillion yuan for the first time, accounting for 7.8%11 of the national GDP, highlighting its status and resilience as a vital pillar of the national economy.

Although the official ocean economic accounting system provides macro-level aggregate indicators, such as the gross value added of the ocean economy and its core industries12, it is difficult to use these data to fully present the complex interdependencies among sectors within the ocean economy and its connections with the broader terrestrial economy. Input-output analysis is a powerful tool for achieving this objective. Ocean economy input-output tables can quantify the complex economic relationships among marine sectors and their connections with the overall national economy13,14. These tables document inter-industry transactions in goods and services within the marine economy, enabling comprehensive analyses of the economic structure of marine-related industries, land-sea economic linkages, and economic impacts13,15,16. The existing literature has attempted to compile ocean economic input-output tables for countries including Brazil3, the United Kingdom15,17, China1,18,19, the United States20, Ireland14, Australia21, and South Korea22. Among the attempts for China, Wang and Wang1 produced Ocean Economic Input-Output Tables for 2002, 2007, and 2012, and used these accounts to evaluate the contribution of marine industries to the national economy. Zheng, et al.18 subsequently extended the tables to 2007, 2012, and 2017, employed structural decomposition analysis (SDA) to quantify intra- and inter-industry linkages and investigated drivers of marine industry growth. Liang, et al.19 compiled an ocean economic input-output table for China’s coastal provinces in 2017, forming a provincial data framework compatible with the official IO system and standardizing the characterization of intermediate inputs and final uses. Their dataset provides a key provincial-level foundation for research on land-sea integration, industrial structure evolution, and supply chain resilience, and fills a critical data gap in provincial-level ocean economic input-output tables.

Although these pioneering efforts have laid an important foundation for input-output analysis of China’s ocean economy, existing studies generally have limited temporal coverage, which constrains their value for longitudinal analysis of structural change and for research on long-term trends. To fill this data gap, we compiled a China ocean economic time series input-output dataset covering 2002 to 2020. The compilation follows a two-step pathway in which baseline OEIO tables are first constructed and then updated and extrapolated over time. Given data limitations, we applied the stripping coefficient method to the national benchmark IO tables to derive baseline-year Ocean Economic Input-Output Tables. Building on these, we employed an improved generalized RAS (IGRAS) procedure to produce the 2002–2020 time series OEIO tables. This approach helps control aggregation bias at the source and reduces uncertainty during extrapolation23. The resulting time-series OEIO tables preserve sectoral structural detail while ensuring year-to-year consistency in economic and accounting logic. The complete series provides a critical data foundation for in-depth analysis of supply-chain dynamics in the ocean economy24 and associated carbon emissions15,25, for understanding the structural effects of the ocean economy within the national economy26, for quantifying land-sea linkage effects27, and for informing decision-making on the sustainable development of the blue economy28.

Methods

Overall compilation framework

The core strategy for constructing this dataset is first to compile a detail-rich baseline ocean economic input-output (OEIO) table, and then generate a coherent time series that covers the entire period on this basis. This pathway fully leverages the rich sectoral detail embedded in the national input-output tables, builds a comprehensive industrial system for the ocean economy, and effectively controls aggregation bias at the source while reducing uncertainty during time-series extrapolation23. The resulting series of IO tables preserves sectoral specificity and ensures consistency across all years in both economic meaning and accounting logic.

The National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) of China released nine IO tables, including extended tables, between 2002 and 2020. However, there are substantial differences in sector classifications and statistical conventions across years. To achieve temporal comparability, the IO tables for different years need to be harmonized in terms of sector classification. We defined the input-output tables released in 200229, 200730, 201231, 201732, 201833, and 202034 as benchmark tables, with their respective years designated as benchmark years35, while other years are classified as non-benchmark years. After stripping ocean-related activities from the benchmark-year IO tables, the remaining terrestrial economy sectors are consolidated into a unified set of 42 sectors. This reclassification is guided by the Industrial Classification for National Economic Activities (GB/T 4754-2017) and the structure of the 2017 Provincial Input-Output Tables, thereby providing a consistent sectoral framework for the entire time series.

Definition of the ocean economy industries and their correspondence with input-output table sectors

The ocean economy refers to the sum of industrial activities that develop, utilize, and protect the ocean, as well as activities associated with these functions. Ocean industries are the production and service activities conducted for the development, utilization, and protection of the ocean6. Building on the “three tiers and five categories” classification of ocean industries, we delimit the scope of the ocean economy industries. The three tiers are the core, support, and peripheral tiers of the ocean economy, and the five categories consist of major ocean industry groups within the core tier; marine scientific research and education within the support tier; marine public management and services within the support tier; and upstream related industries and downstream related industries within the peripheral tier36. As shown in Fig. 2, the core tier of the ocean economy includes 15 major ocean industry groups, 59 medium groups, and 176 minor groups, covering the production and service activities conducted for ocean development, utilization, and protection. The support tier includes marine scientific research and education (2 major groups, 8 medium groups, 28 minor groups) and marine public management and services (6 major groups, 21 medium groups, 66 minor groups), and mainly encompasses activities such as research, education, administration, and services that occur in the processes of developing, utilizing, and protecting the ocean. The peripheral tier comprises upstream related industries (2 major groups, 17 medium groups, 60 minor groups) and downstream related industries (3 major groups, 16 medium groups, 32 minor groups), which are upstream and downstream industries that maintain techno-economic linkages with the principal ocean industries through various input-output connections.Fig. 2Classification framework of ocean industries and related activities used to define the industrial scope of the ocean economy in the input-output tables. The framework distinguishes the core, support and peripheral layers of the ocean economy and shows how specific ocean-related activities are mapped into the sectoral scope adopted in the compiled ocean economic input-output tables.

Taking into account the above definition of ocean economy, the delineation of ocean industry scope, and limitations in data availability, we define the scope of ocean economy industries to include 19 industries in total, composed of 15 core ocean industries, 2 support industries, and 2 peripheral industries. Among these 19 industries, 15 belong to the major groups in the core tier of the ocean economy. In addition, two industries are included from the support tier, namely marine scientific research and education, and marine public management and services; and two industries are included from the peripheral tier, namely upstream related industries and downstream related industries. The inclusion of the two supportive industries, marine scientific research and education and marine public services, strengthens the intellectual support and institutional safeguards for ocean economy development. The addition of upstream and downstream related industries improves the structure of the ocean supply chain. This industry scheme provides economy-wide coverage of the ocean economy and enables comprehensive accounting of sectoral size. By incorporating support and peripheral industries, it establishes a complete industrial chain from input supply to core production to product application, and it provides a basis for analyzing the interaction between the ocean economy and the terrestrial economy.

A rigorous mapping from ocean economy sectors to the industries used in input-output accounts is foundational for building the ocean economic input-output tables; it conditions the estimation of intersectoral flows and the representation of technological economic connections. The National Industrial Classification for Ocean Industries and Their Related Activities (GB/T 20794-2021) provides an appendix mapping the ocean industries to the Industrial Classification for National Economic Activities (GB/T 4754-2017) and offers brief guidance on how to isolate or define ocean-related activities within the broader economy. Building on this mapping and integrating the Industrial Classification for National Economic Activities (GB/T 4754-2017) and industry categories of the input-output table, we have systematically developed a mapping system presented in supplementary information, linking ocean economy sectors to their corresponding input-output table industries. To simplify the compilation process using programming, this study reordered the input-output table sectors in strict numerical order, which differs from the original table’s industry coding system. This approach not only avoids the ambiguity that can arise from direct one-to-one matches but also ensures that ocean economic activities are represented with greater precision in the input-output framework, thereby providing a robust data foundation for analyzing the interactions between the ocean economy and the national economy. Moreover, it offers a methodological framework for other coastal nations seeking to undertake similar analyses①.1

Using the above scope definition for ocean economy industries and the year-specific concordance with input-output sectors, we specify the basic layout of the comprehensive ocean economic input-output tables. As shown in Fig. 3, the basic format contains 61 industries in the rows and columns of the basic format, including 19 ocean economy industries and 42 land-based economy industries. The 61 × 61 matrix includes the core, support, and peripheral ocean economy industries, and documents technical and economic links with the land economy industries. Preparation for non-benchmark year compilation

Total output and value added

Value-added data for most ocean industries are directly available from the China Ocean Statistical Yearbook and the China Ocean Economy Statistical Yearbook. For industries with missing time-series observations, we supplemented the estimation using the multiple approaches described in the previous subsection, “Stripping and consolidation of ocean industries,” with the detailed procedures provided in the Supplementary Information.

For the total output of ocean industries, we use the value-added rates of each sector from the adjacent benchmark-year IO tables as the value-added rates for non-benchmark years, and thereby compute sectoral total output. This assumption holds that changes in production technology and price structure between adjacent years are gradual. Therefore, the most recent known data point is used as the best estimate. This approach balances stability and realism in time series extrapolation. For other non-ocean industries, following the National Bureau of Statistics method for extended IO tables and existing studies37, we determine total output directly from the China Statistical Yearbook or other statistical sources where such indicators are available, or after harmonizing accounting bases if required. For industries without reported total output, we use proxy indicators provided in statistical sources, such as sales value, operating revenue, or total wages, to infer total output. The detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Quadrant II (final use) matrix data

For urban and rural household consumption required to compile the Quadrant II time-series IO tables, the consumption data for 2002–2020 were obtained from the China Yearbook of Rural Household Survey, the China Urban Life and Price Yearbook, and the China Yearbook of Household Survey. These data report per-capita consumption for eight broad categories② and are therefore used only as a structural reference, since the classification differs from the sectoral detail required here. Specifically, we first adjust urban and rural consumption for prices using the benchmark year IO table and treat the adjusted series as the initial structure for the compilation year. We then calibrate this structure with the category-level composition in that year to obtain the distribution of urban and rural consumption across sectors. Lastly, we reconcile the data to the expenditure-based GDP by using the aggregates for urban and rural household consumption as controls, thereby fixing the respective totals.2

For government consumption, we use public budget expenditure as a reference indicator. We first aggregate the benchmark year OEIO table’s government procurement by sector into six broad categories③. Following the procedure used for industrial total output, we harmonize the categories of public budget expenditure to obtain an initial structure. We then project government consumption by sector with reference to the benchmark year multi-sector structure.3

The treatment of gross fixed capital formation follows the same approach used for government consumption. Given data limitations, we harmonize the accounting basis using the three-industry classification and proceed accordingly④.4

Because the statistical foundation for changes in inventories is relatively weak, these data are difficult to obtain. Following Zhang, et al.37, we collect inventory data for industrial enterprises above a designated size and compute inventory increases as a first-step estimate for total industrial inventories. We took inventory growth for enterprises above designated size as the change in cumulative inventories from t-1 to t. For agriculture and selected services, due to limited source data, we make preliminary estimates based on the reference-year IO tables and the inventory-increase aggregate reported in expenditure-side GDP.

For imports and exports, the statistical base is relatively robust and only requires harmonization of classifications. Using the China Statistical Yearbook, we map commodity import and export data to the IO sector classification and convert values using the average annual exchange rate. For services trade, we take the relevant Balance of Payments (BOP) service items as the basis for compiling and mapping to the IO sectors. For a small number of sectors, we apply interpolation using adjacent IO tables.

Quadrants II and III consistency adjustment

GDP measured by the expenditure approach and GDP measured by the production approach should be identical in theory, but discrepancies often arise in practice due to differences in accounting coverage and data sources. In an input-output table, they corresponded to Quadrant II and Quadrant III, respectively. According to the row and column balancing identity of input-output tables, the total final demand in Quadrant II should equal the total value added in Quadrant III, thereby ensuring consistency between expenditure approach GDP and income or production approach GDP. To ensure that this balancing relationship held and that Quadrant II and Quadrant III were coherent at the aggregate level, after obtaining initial sectoral estimates for each final demand category, we further constructed a mathematical programming model to perform a consistency adjustment. The adjustment aimed to equalize the totals of Quadrant II and Quadrant III and to jointly constrain them to the production approach GDP series published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. Compiling time-series ocean economic input-output tables

We compile the time-series OEIO tables using the improved generalized RAS (IGRAS) method. The IGRAS extends the RAS and GRAS frameworks as a matrix-balancing approach suited to IO matrices with negative entries and structural zeros. Its main advantages include solving a convex optimization problem, sign and zero preservation, unbiased and bounded estimates, and a closed-form multiplicative scaling structure35. In the time-series compilation, IGRAS enables robust calibration of intermediate flows and primary inputs under annual row and column controls, mitigating cross-year structural jumps. Data Records

The dataset is available at figshare39. The 2002–2020 China Ocean Economic Time Series input-output database comprises nineteen Excel workbooks, one for each year. Each workbook contains 61 sectors, including 19 ocean sectors and 42 non-ocean sectors, and follows the standard input-output structure (see Fig. 3), comprising three quadrants. The upper-left Quadrant I presents the intermediate-use flow matrix (61×61), describing inter-sectoral input-output linkages. The upper-right Quadrant II presents the final-use flow matrix (61×8), covering rural household consumption, urban household consumption, government consumption, gross fixed capital formation, changes in inventories, exports, imports, and a total final use column for the 61 sectors. The lower-left Quadrant III reports value added, representing the value added of each sector. All data are expressed at current prices, with monetary values in ten thousand yuan (RMB).

Technical Validation

To comprehensively evaluate the reliability of this dataset and its applicability boundaries, we conducted a systematic validation across four core dimensions of data quality, as shown in Fig. 4. At the foundational level, we carried out feasibility, balancing, and statistical error reasonableness checks to confirm that the tables met the necessary conditions for input-output analysis, including non-negativity of the intermediate transaction matrix, computability of the Leontief inverse matrix, closure of accounting identities, and acceptable magnitudes of error terms. These checks provided the prerequisites for all subsequent analyses. At the methodological level, we performed robustness validation of the stripping coefficient method as a key assumption. By comparing gross output structure, intermediate input ratios, and interindustry linkage coefficients with independent studies, we assessed whether the ocean sector input-output structure derived under the value added ratio assumption exhibited systematic bias. We also used benchmark year backcasting tests40 to identify the boundary between algorithm induced smoothing and actual economic evolution, and to clarify the capacity of the updating framework to capture structural changes over different time spans, together with its potential dampening effects. At the structural level, we characterized the plausibility of time series evolution using multidimensional smoothness diagnostics. We examined indicators such as the magnitude of changes in input structure shares, Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, supply concentration, and core supplier stability, to determine whether the annual series preserved intertemporal comparability while allowing phase specific adjustments consistent with economic reality, and to avoid misinterpreting mechanical interpolation as economic continuity. At the robustness level, external data comparisons, error term sensitivity assessment, and Monte Carlo uncertainty testing were further conducted to examine the external consistency of the tables, their response characteristics to error perturbations, and the stability bounds under reasonable perturbation ranges. These four layers supported each other and together formed a complete validation chain from data compilation to practical application. The framework identified the dataset’s best achievable positioning under current conditions while clearly delineating its applicability boundaries and potential limitations, and it provided a transparent and traceable basis for quality assessment in subsequent research. To improve readability and coherence, all equations used in the data validation section were presented in the supplementary information. Methodological level

Key methodological assumptions and robustness validation

Input-output analysis is commonly based on the homogeneity assumption, also referred to as the pure sector assumption, under which representative input structures and value added ratios at the sector level are used to characterize the average technoeconomic relationships of heterogeneous activities within a sector. In the absence of independent and comparable statistics on gross output and intermediate input structures for ocean industries, this study applied the stripping coefficient method under the constraints of the bridge matrix to separate ocean activities, and it constructed stripping coefficients using the representative value added ratios of aggregated sectors to maintain accounting closure and intertemporal comparability. Given that mappings between ocean sectors and national economy sectors were often not one-to-one, this study implemented necessary temporary aggregation of the relevant sectors in an accounting sense and estimated stripping coefficients accordingly to maintain account consistency.

The main sources of error in this method can be grouped into four categories. First, errors from departures from the homogeneity assumption. When within sector heterogeneity is substantial, the embedded ocean industries may deviate systematically from the sector average in input intensity, factor input structure, and value added ratio, which can bias stripping coefficients constructed from representative sector level value added ratios. Second, errors from mapping and temporary aggregation. Because ocean sectors and national economy sectors are not one to one, although this study carried out detailed matching based on the National Industrial Classification for Ocean Industries and Their Related Activities and the Industrial Classification for National Economic Activities, constraints related to accounting coverage boundaries, attribution of cross cutting activities, and multi product structures within sectors can still lead to under coverage or over coverage. As a result, the aggregated sector bundles may exhibit structural bias in approximating the true structure of ocean industries. Third, errors from proxy variables and gap filling. In the absence of direct observations, stripping coefficient estimation inevitably relies on information sources such as value added shares, output proxies, or trend extrapolation. Differences in statistical definitions, measurement errors, and extrapolation assumptions can further transmit into the interannual variation of stripping coefficients. Fourth, errors from intertemporal updating and balancing. In constructing the time series, subsequent updating and balancing procedures adjust transaction structures under control total constraints. Their minimum change property may interact with the errors above, weakening or amplifying coefficient changes for certain sectors.

We compared our results with those reported by Zheng, et al.18. A key difference is that Zheng, et al.18 specified stripping coefficients separately for value added, gross output, and the intermediate transaction matrix, and these coefficients were directly obtained from statistical data. By contrast, under unified bridge mapping and accounting closure constraints, this study derived annual stripping coefficients based on a traceable industry value added structure, and it backcast gross output for ocean industries using the value added ratio assumption, thereby establishing a reproducible time series estimation framework.

The benchmarking results are shown in Figs. 6, 7. From the perspective of gross output structure shares reported in Fig. 6, the two studies exhibited a high level of consistency across three periods for the comparable set of ocean industries. The overall curves closely overlapped for most industries, and the dominant pattern and relative ranking of high share industries remained stable across years. This indicated that the annual stripping coefficients derived from a traceable value added structure produced an industry weight distribution similar to that of the existing study, providing a comparable basis for subsequent network and structural analyses. From the perspective of intermediate input ratios reported in Fig. 7, most industries showed input ratio levels within similar ranges across the two studies, and they displayed broadly consistent interannual trends. Given that intermediate input ratios and value added ratios are complementary in accounting, this finding corroborated the consistency in gross output structure shares. It suggested that the backcasting mechanism used in this study was comparable to the existing study not only in output structure, but also in the degree of intermediate input dependence that reflected technology and cost structures. Deviations observed for a small number of industries in specific years may have arisen from differences in stripping scope, processing of statistical coefficients, and sector aggregation schemes. Because the framework in this study emphasized intertemporal comparability and reproducibility, these deviations did not alter the overall conclusion of consistency. Validation for identifying algorithm induced smoothing

Smoothness had two meanings in this study. One referred to moderate coefficient changes associated with the gradual evolution of economic structure over a medium term horizon. The other referred to volatility suppression that may arise because the updating algorithm, under reference matrix constraints, tended to minimize the magnitude of adjustments. The former helped improve intertemporal comparability and reduce noise, whereas the latter could mask genuine structural breaks.

To avoid misinterpreting algorithm induced smoothing as economic continuity, this study supplemented a benchmark year backcasting validation. Within periods where benchmark input-output tables were available, the updating method was used to backcast another benchmark year and compare it with the observed benchmark table, while differences in smoothness were also compared. This allowed the economic interpretation and applicability boundaries of the smoothness diagnostics to be clarified. Because no observed intermediate transaction matrix was available for each within segment year as an external reference, we could not conduct equally intensive external backcasting validation for every year. Therefore, bidirectional backcasting at the benchmark year boundaries served as the strongest identifiable validation of the updating framework, and internal consistency indicators were considered jointly to assess the plausibility of within segment results. Specifically, the starting benchmark matrix was used as a prior, and IGRAS generated a backcast matrix under the row and column control total constraints of the target year, which was then compared item by item with the observed benchmark matrix. Because the updated terminal year result deviated from the observed terminal year, when ET was large, the gap between TP and TT reflected not only smoothing but also measurement shifts induced by terminal year deviation. Overall, the fact that TT and ET were of the same order and moved in the same direction indicated that the updating deviation converged or expanded with changes in the true structure. This suggested that the updating results reflected the intensity of genuine evolution rather than purely mechanical interpolation. The systematic differences between TT and TP suggested a smoothing tendency in long span intervals, and part of the early stage differences were influenced by the overlay of ET. In short span intervals such as 2017–2020, TT, TP, and ET were jointly low and TP was closer to TT. This supported that the adjacent year iteration strategy adopted in this study reduced the impact of over smoothing to a low level and provided robust evidence for structural interpretation of the time series tables.

Structural level

Structural smoothness

To assess the structural stability and the smoothness of its evolution in the input-output time series, this section employs a set of internal statistical indicators. The method first normalizes the intermediate use matrix by columns, converting it into the input structure share matrix for each sector. Subsequently, the change in input structure between adjacent years is evaluated using a multidimensional indicator system. These indicators include the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) and the Average Euclidean Distance (AED) to measure structural differences, Cosine Similarity (COS) to assess structural similarity43–45, the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) for supply concentration46, and Top-k Jaccard Similarity to evaluate the stability of main suppliers47. All indicators are calculated for each sector (column) and then aggregated for overall assessment.

The three indicators presented above characterize structural changes from different perspectives, as shown in supplementary information. MAD directly quantifies the average magnitude of change in input shares; AED measures the geometric distance between the input structure vectors for two years in Euclidean space; COS assesses the directional consistency of the input structure vectors for the two years, with values closer to 1 indicating greater structural similarity. To further complement the overall analysis, it is necessary to examine the evolution of the dominant patterns in the input structure by introducing two additional indicators: the HHI and the Top-k Jaccard Similarity. The HHI measures the concentration of input sources for specific sectors through the sum of squared shares46. Its calculation formula is provided in supplementary information.

The Jaccard coefficient is a classic metric for measuring the similarity between two sets, proposed by Paul Jaccard in 190149. In this study, we build the Top-k coefficient based on the Jaccard coefficient to measure the stability of key suppliers. Error term sensitivity assessment

The above tests show that error terms account for only a small share at the aggregate level, yet it remains necessary to further assess their propagation sensitivity within the input-output accounting framework in order to examine the stability of the dataset. To this end, this section treats the error term as an independent perturbation component in final demand and incorporates it into the Leontief quantity model to assess its sensitivity from two perspectives. First, we compare the differences in induced output between the cases with and without the error term, so as to characterize the relative sensitivity of error-term perturbations and their sectoral distribution. Second, we clarify the transmission path through which the error term enters the accounting results as a final-demand perturbation, thereby identifying, from a theoretical perspective, how it is transmitted into the relevant accounting outcomes.

First, by comparing the differences in induced output between the cases with and without the error term, we examine the relative sensitivity of error-term perturbations after they are transmitted across sectors through the multiplier mechanism. The detailed calculation methods are provided in the Supplementary Information. The above assessment mainly characterizes the relative magnitude of error-term perturbations at the results level. To further clarify the theoretical basis of this sensitivity assessment, it is necessary to briefly explain how the error term enters the demand-driven framework. Because the error term is treated as part of final demand in the consistency adjustment, its potential effect is essentially the transmission of a final-demand perturbation through the Leontief multiplier system. To avoid interference from price factors in identifying the effect of the error term itself, this study does not examine the numerical results under the absence of a strict constant-price system, but instead explains its possible transmission path within the demand-driven framework from the perspective of error-term sensitivity alone. Detailed formula derivations and the expressions under different two-factor decomposition forms are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Taken together, the error term sensitivity assessment indicates that the transmission effects of error-term perturbations are limited in most years and sectors, and do not materially interfere with the overall stability of the tables. Although benchmark years and a small number of sectors exhibit relatively higher sensitivity, the scope and magnitude of these effects remain overall controllable. These results suggest that the time-series ocean economic input-output tables constructed in this study maintain good overall robustness under error-term perturbations.

For the magnitude settings, we used the observed smoothness scale of the time series tables as a reference, so that perturbations spanned a range from mild to stress while avoiding extreme noise that was clearly inconsistent with intertemporal structural features.
