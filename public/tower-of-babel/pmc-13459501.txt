Primary Burkitt lymphoma of the breast in a young north African woman: diagnostic challenge and benefit of early intensive chemotherapy
Authors: Abouchabaka Ibtihal, Nouama Bouanani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurkitt lymphoma (BL) is a highly aggressive B-cell malignancy that rarely presents primarily in the breast. Its rapid progression and inflammatory features can mimic breast cancer or mastitis, leading to a delay in diagnosis. A 26-year-old nulliparous North African woman presented with a painful, rapidly growing breast mass that had appeared within the last four weeks. Clinical examination revealed an 8 cm mass accompanied by orange-peel skin. Imaging suggested a likely benign lesion (BI-RADS 3). However, histology showed a classic ‘starry sky’ pattern, a Ki-67 rate of 100% and positivity for CD20/CD10/BCL6 with negativity for BCL2/CD5/EBER. PET-CT revealed a solitary hypermetabolic lesion (SUV 32) without systemic involvement (stage IE, BL-IPI 1). Treatment with R-COPADM was initiated within 48 hours, resulting in complete remission. This case highlights that an early biopsy is essential in the presence of any atypical inflammatory breast mass, even when imaging appears reassuring.Keywords: Burkitt lymphoma, breast, young adult, immunohistochemistry, early diagnosis

Introduction

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a high-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), characterised by rapid proliferation and a high proliferation index. There are three main epidemiological forms of the disease: the endemic form, which is observed in sub-Saharan Africa and is frequently associated with the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV); the sporadic form, which is predominant in non-endemic countries and most often presents with abdominal involvement; and the form associated with immunodeficiency [1].

Primary breast involvement by BL is an exceptional occurrence. While primary breast lymphomas account for less than 0.5% of breast tumours, the BL form is an even rarer entity, with a very limited number of cases reported in the literature [2].

The clinical presentation of breast BL poses a genuine diagnostic challenge. Rapid progression, breast enlargement and inflammatory signs, particularly an orange-peel appearance, may be indicative of inflammatory breast cancer. However, these symptoms can also be indicative of infectious mastitis or other benign inflammatory conditions. It should be noted that this presentation may be accompanied by a clinical-radiological discrepancy. This is due to the fact that imaging is sometimes non-specific or deceptively reassuring, which can delay diagnosis [2].

We present a case of a 26-year-old nulliparous North African woman who presented with a rapidly progressing painful breast mass associated with inflammatory signs. The absence of immunosuppression, the isolated extranodal location and the non-endemic geographical origin suggest a sporadic form of BL. This case demonstrates the significance of early biopsy and comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis in situations where there is a discrepancy between clinical and radiological findings. This approach enables a rapid diagnosis and the initiation of appropriate, potentially curative treatment.

Case report

A 26-year-old woman from North Africa, with no previous pregnancies and regular menstrual cycles, was admitted to our department because of a painful lump in the left breast that had increased in size over the course of four weeks. A physical examination was conducted, resulting in the determination of an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 1. The left breast exhibited signs of a mass, including swelling, erythema, and an ``orange peel'' appearance. It is important to note that no nipple discharge was observed (see Fig. 1). The remainder of the clinical examination revealed no significant findings, with no palpable lymph nodes or abnormalities detected in the right breast or any other part of the patient's body (see Fig. 1).

The left breast mass shows signs of swelling and erythema, with an ``orange peel'' appearance.

The results of the blood test indicated the presence of inflammatory microcytic hypochromic anaemia, with a haemoglobin level of 10 g/dL, lymphopenia at 900 cells/mm3, and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 60 mg/L (reference range: < 5 mg/l). Additionally, the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level was recorded as 1400 U/l (reference range: 120 U/l and 246 U/l). The patient's leukocyte count was 7200 cells/μl (reference range: 4000-10 000 cells/μl), and their platelet count was 245 000 cells/μl (reference range: 150000-400 000 cells/μl). Liver and renal function tests were normal. EBV serology was IgG anti-EBNA positive and PCR negative. HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B serologies were negative. In light of the established epidemiological link between malaria and endemic Burkitt lymphoma, malaria was also investigated as part of the etiological work-up, with both negative thick and thin blood smears being obtained.

Imaging studies, including bilateral mammography and bilateral breast ultrasound, were requested to further evaluate breast changes. The patient underwent a mammogram, which revealed heterogeneously dense involution in the breasts, a thin, regular skin, no suspicious nodular opacities, no microcalcification foci, no architectural disorganisation, and a right axillary lymph node opacity with a fatty hilum. Overall, the mammography classified the right breast as BI-RADS 2 and the left breast as BI-RADS 3 (see Fig. 2).

Bilateral mammography (mediolateral views). (A) Right breast showing no suspicious abnormalities. (B) the left breast is classified as BI-RADS 3 and shows a subtle focal density (indicated by white circle).

Breast ultrasound revealed a mammary gland with heterogeneous echotexture of micronodular changes, predominantly on the left, and a homogeneous seat of a retromammary mass with a heterogeneous tissue echotexture and no suspicious microvascularity. The mass showed large transverse orientation and discrete posterior acoustic enhancement, measuring 49 × 23 mm. Microcystic changes were evident in the inner quadrants, and the skin was thin and regular. No axillary lymphadenopathy was detected (see Fig. 3).

Breast ultrasound showing an oval, well-defined hypoechoic mass (green arrow) with posterior acoustic enhancement.

An ultrasound-guided percutaneous (tru-cut) biopsy was performed, revealing a malignant tumour proliferation that had completely occupied the mammary parenchyma. A histopathological examination revealed diffuse sheets of monomorphic, medium-sized lymphoid cells with a characteristic `starry-sky' appearance, caused by the presence of numerous tingible-body macrophages. The nuclei appeared irregular, with prominent nucleoli. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a characteristic Burkitt lymphoma immunophenotype. The tumour cells showed positive expression of pan-B-cell markers (CD19, CD20 and CD79a), as well as germinal centre markers (CD10 and BCL6). The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 100%. The neoplastic cells were negative for BCL2, CD5, TdT and EBER. This immunophenotypic profile, together with the characteristic starry-sky morphology, supported the diagnosis of Burkitt lymphoma. In addition, the results indicated negative pan-T-cell markers (see Fig. 4).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical features of primary breast BL. (A and B) haematoxylin and eosin staining showing diffuse sheets of monomorphic, medium-sized lymphoid cells with numerous tingible-body macrophages (indicated by a white circle), creating a characteristic `starry-sky' appearance. C: Ki-67 immunostaining demonstrating a proliferation index of almost 100%. D: CD20 immunostaining confirming the B-cell lineage of the tumour cells.

Myelocytomatosis oncogene (MYC) protein expression was negative by immunohistochemistry. Unfortunately, cytogenetic analysis for MYC rearrangement by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) (t(8;14) (q24;q32) or variants) could not be performed due to unavailability of this molecular technique in our institution and limited resources in our healthcare setting. The diagnosis of BL was therefore established based on characteristic morphological features (starry sky pattern), consistent immunophenotype (CD20+, CD10+, BCL6+, Ki-67 ~ 100%, BCL2-, MYC IHC negative), and appropriate clinical context, consistent with World Health Organization (WHO) 2022 classification criteria. Further immunohistochemical analysis (TdT, CD36, EBER) yielded negative results, excluding other high-grade B-cell lymphomas, such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) or B-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (B-LL). Additionally, the presence of CD23 negativity ruled out chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/small lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL/SLL). Bone marrow assessment and cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed no lymphomatous involvement. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) scan revealed a large, highly hypermetabolic mass in the left breast, with a standardized uptake value (SUV) of 32. According to the Ann Arbor staging classification, the patient was classified as stage IE (single nodal or extranodal site), with an International Prognostic Index (IPI) of 1.

The treatment was administered in accordance with institutional protocols, which were based on international guidelines for BL. Although the disease was localised (Ann Arbor stage IE), treatment decisions were based on the biological aggressiveness intrinsic to BL, as well as established risk-adapted therapeutic strategies, rather than stage alone. The treatment plan involved an initial cytoreduction phase with COP (Cyclophosphamide, Vincristine, Prednisone), followed by an induction phase commencing on day 8 with two courses of R-COPADM (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, methotrexate, prednisone, and vincristine). A total of four doses of rituximab were administered, two in the COPADM-1 cycle and two in the COPADM-2 cycle. The second cycle of COPADM-1 (COPADM-2) was initiated on day 18, subsequent to the confirmation that the patient's WBC count exceeded 2000 cells/μl, and their platelet count surpassed 100 000 cells/μl. The rituximab was administered at a dose of 375 mg/m2 per injection. The consolidation phase was initiated on day 21 of COPADM-2, with two cycles of CYM (methotrexate, folinic acid, cytarabine). The end-of-treatment cycle commenced on day 21 of the second CYM cycle. Central nervous system (CNS) prophylaxis was administered with intrathecal methotrexate 15 mg during each COPADM cycle (total 2 doses) (see Table 1).

Following the completion of chemotherapy, the patient showed significant clinical improvement (see Fig. 5). The post-treatment course was characterised by persistent mild anaemia, which was effectively managed with supportive care and remained stable. The treatment response was assessed using FDG-PET/CT according to the standard criteria for lymphoma response. End-of-treatment imaging showed no residual hypermetabolic lesions, which is consistent with a complete metabolic response (Deauville score 1–2) and was associated with normalisation of serum LDH levels. Post-treatment surveillance included regular clinical evaluations, serial imaging and biochemical monitoring. Six months after treatment completion, the patient remains in sustained complete remission, with no evidence of disease recurrence. At the most recent follow-up, almost two years after completing treatment, she continues to demonstrate excellent clinical status with no signs of relapse, and she continues to undergo regular outpatient surveillance.

Discussion

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a high-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) first described by Denis Burkitt in African children during the mid-20th century, associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) and malaria endemicity. BL accounts for 1%-5% of all NHLs and demonstrates distinct epidemiological patterns across geographical regions. While it was historically considered endemic to equatorial Africa, sporadic forms predominate in Western populations, with notable variations in anatomical presentation. The endemic variant usually affects children, with involvement of the jaw and facial bones. By contrast, sporadic BL primarily manifests in the abdomen and bone marrow. The geographical and clinical heterogeneity of this malignancy highlights its biological plasticity [1]. The molecular landscape of BL is characterised by MYC oncogene dysregulation, representing the paradigm of translocation-driven lymphomagenesis. The t(8;14) (q24;q32) translocation, present in 70%-80% of cases, juxtaposes the c-MYC gene with immunoglobulin heavy chain enhancers, driving uncontrollable cellular proliferation. In EBV-associated endemic BL, viral proteins such as EBNA1 and EBER contribute to B-cell immortalization by inhibiting apoptosis and inducing genomic instability. This molecular signature explains the characteristic starry sky histology and the near 100% Ki-67 proliferation index observed in our patient [3].

Primary breast involvement in BL is exceptionally rare, representing an unusual extranodal presentation that challenges established clinical paradigms. Most reported cases in the literature occur in specific contexts, such as pregnancy, lactation or the postpartum period, and are characterised by bilateral or diffuse breast involvement rather than a localised lesion [4]. By contrast, our patient was a young, nulliparous, immunocompetent North African woman who presented with a rapidly progressive, unilateral breast mass, with no pregnancy-related or immunodeficiency context. This clinical and epidemiological profile differs from that of most previously reported cases, thus broadening the known spectrum of presentations of breast BL. Another distinctive feature of our case was the significant discrepancy between clinical and radiological findings. Despite the presence of aggressive clinical findings, including rapid enlargement and inflammatory skin changes that were suggestive of inflammatory breast carcinoma, the initial imaging classification was BI-RADS 3, indicating probable benignity. Such misleading radiological presentations have rarely been emphasised in previous reports, as most cases were described as presenting with more overtly suspicious imaging features. This discrepancy highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall and underscores the importance of maintaining a high level of suspicion and performing an early tissue biopsy in young patients presenting with rapidly evolving breast masses, even when radiological findings appear reassuring [2].

When making a differential diagnosis of a rapidly growing inflammatory breast mass in young women, several entities should be considered. Inflammatory breast carcinoma, the most critical mimic, typically presents with dermal lymphatic tumour emboli causing orange peel, but differs radiologically with frequent microcalcifications, architectural distortion, and suspicious axillary adenopathy [5]. Granulomatous mastitis, particularly in its idiopathic form, can present as painful masses and skin changes, mimicking lymphoma. However, it typically exhibits neutrophilic infiltrates and lacks the distinctive ``starry sky'' histology characteristic of lymphoma. Primary breast diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), the most common breast lymphoma subtype, differs in terms of its immunophenotype, with BCL2 positivity, a lower Ki-67 proliferation index (typically 70%-90% vs. 100%), and the absence of MYC rearrangement [2, 3]. Secondary breast involvement from systemic lymphoma should be excluded by staging evaluation. Infectious mastitis (bacterial or TB) may present with inflammatory signs but responds to antibiotics and lacks malignant cytology. Metastatic disease to the breast from extramammary primaries (melanoma, lung, ovarian) remains rare but should be considered in appropriate clinical contexts. Overall, these differential diagnoses show that a diagnosis of breast Burkitt lymphoma cannot be based solely on clinical presentation or imaging results. Instead, a comprehensive assessment is required that considers the tumour's rapid progression, histopathological morphology, immunophenotypic profile, and staging results. This evidence-based approach is particularly important in cases of rare extranodal manifestations, as a delay in performing a biopsy can result in the patient receiving inappropriate surgical or anti-infective treatment [2].

The management of BL requires prompt intervention, ideally within 48 hours of diagnosis, in view of the aggressive doubling time of the tumour [6]. A number of intensive treatment plans have shown that they are effective, including CODOX-M/IVAC with or without rituximab [7], R-hyper-CVAD [8] and dose-adjusted EPOCH-R [9]. However, it should be noted that these protocols frequently entail significant toxicity profiles [6]. The COPADM regimen is derived from French LMB (French Lymphome Malin B) protocols and offers a balanced approach combining efficacy with tolerability. Retrospective data demonstrate superior 3-year overall survival (83.5%) and event-free survival (73.2%) compared to conventional therapies (47.4% and 42.1% respectively), with particular benefit observed in patients ≤ 40 years following rituximab addition [10]. Our patient's rapid achievement of complete metabolic remission following R-COPADM therapy validates this approach in atypical presentations.

This case highlights several important clinical implications. Firstly, rapidly enlarging inflammatory breast masses in young women should prompt an immediate tissue diagnosis, regardless of any seemingly reassuring imaging findings. Secondly, BL must be considered in the differential diagnosis of such cases, even when typical epidemiological factors such as pregnancy or immunodeficiency are absent. Thirdly, comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis is essential for an accurate diagnosis and to distinguish BL from similar conditions.
