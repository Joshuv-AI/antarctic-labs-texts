On Computing the Shapley Value in Bankruptcy Games — Illustrated by Rectified Linear Function Games —
Shunta Yamazaki, Tomomi Matsui

Abstract
In this research, we discuss a problem of calculating the Shapley value in bankruptcy games. We show that the decision problem of computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games is NP-complete. We also investigate the relationship between the Shapley value of bankruptcy games and the Shapley–Shubik index in weighted voting games. The relation naturally implies a dynamic programming technique for calculating the Shapley value. We also present two recursive algorithms for computing the Shapley value: the first is the recursive completion method originally proposed by O’Neill, and the second is our novel contribution based on the dual game formulation. These recursive approaches offer conceptual clarity and computational efficiency, especially when combined with memoisation technique. Finally, we propose a Fully Polynomial-Time Randomized Approximation Scheme (FPRAS) based on Monte Carlo sampling, providing an efficient approximation method for large-scale instances.

Introduction

A bankruptcy problem is a situation
in which the estate of a debtor must be divided
among several claimants,
while the estate is insufficient to satisfy all claims.
The central question is how to allocate
the estate fairly among the claimants.
A typical example occurs
when a firm goes bankrupt and its liquidation value
must be shared among creditors,
each holding a specific claim.

Research on bankruptcy problems has been connected
with cooperative game theory.
This connection was formally established
in pioneering studies
by O’Neill
o1982problem

and by Aumann and Maschler
aumann1985game
.
The cooperative games arising from bankruptcy problems
are commonly referred
to as
bankruptcy games
.
For more details about bankruptcy games,
see
driessen1988cooperative
;
thomson2015axiomatic
.

Several applications of bankruptcy games
to real-world problems have been studied.
For instance,
situations resembling bankruptcy arise
in the case of corporate insolvency,
such as the bankruptcy
of Pacific Gas and Electric Company (PG&E),
where the division of assets among creditors
can be studied
as a bankruptcy problem
borm2005constrained
.
Similar ideas appear
in the allocation of scarce natural resources,
such as water distribution under scarcity,
where bankruptcy rules provide equitable
and efficient
allocation principles
zheng2022water
.
Another example can be found
in the field of education management,
where extended bankruptcy models
have been applied to the allocation
of university budgets across faculties,
balancing subjective claims
with objective
entitlements
pulido2002game
.

In this paper, we focus
on the computational aspects
of the Shapley value
in bankruptcy games
and make the following contributions:

1.

We show that a decision problem of computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games is NP-complete (Section
3
).

2.

We investigate the relationship
between the Shapley value in bankruptcy games
and the Shapley–Shubik index in weighted voting games.
This connection naturally leads to an algorithm for computing the Shapley value,
based on the dynamic programming technique
used for evaluating
the Shapley–Shubik index.
Our result yields a refinement in the time complexity
of a known algorithm. (see Section
4
).

3.

We present two recursive algorithms
for computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games.
We first describe the recursive completion method,
originally proposed by O’Neill,
which builds values from smaller games obtained by restriction.
We also discuss the time complexity of the method.
The second is a novel algorithm proposed by the authors,
based on the dual game formulation. (Section
5
).

4.

We propose
a Fully Polynomial-Time Randomized Approximation Scheme
(FPRAS) based on Monte Carlo sampling,
providing an efficient approximation method
for large-scale instances. (Section
6
).

2
Preliminaries

This section describes notations, definitions, and
some known properties.

Bankruptcy Game

Definition 2.1

(Bankruptcy Games)
.

Let
N=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be a set of players (claimants),
and a positive rational number
w_{i}
denotes
the claim of player
i\in N
.
Let an estate be a positive rational number
E

which satisfies
0<E<\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}
.
The bankruptcy game,
denoted by
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}]
,
is the cooperative game
(N,v)
with a characteristic function

v:S\mapsto\max\left\{0,\,E-\sum_{i\in N\setminus S}w_{i}\right\}\quad(\forall S\subseteq N).

Throughout this paper,
we denote the total weight

\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}
by
W.

For each subset
S\subseteq N
,
we define
w(S)=\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}.

The supermodularity of the above characteristic function
v

is established in
aumann1985game
;
curiel1987bankruptcy
.
Thus, every bankruptcy game is a convex game
and has a non-empty core including the Shapley value (defined below).

The characteristic function
v
of a bankruptcy game
(N,v)
as given in Definition
2.1
can also be expressed in the following form:

v(S)=\max\left\{0,\,E-\sum_{i\in N\setminus S}w_{i}\right\}=\max\left\{0,\,-w_{0}+\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}\right\}\mbox{ where }w_{0}:=W-E.

We refer to a game
(N,v)
as a
Rectified Linear Function Game
, denoted by

\mbox{\rm ReL}[w_{0};w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}]
,
when its characteristic function
is expressed as

v:S\mapsto\max\left\{0,\,-w_{0}+\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}\right\}\quad(\forall S\subseteq N),

(1)

where the inequalities
0<w_{0}<W
are satisfied.
Given a vector of positive rational numbers

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
and a pair of positive rational numbers
\{E,w_{0}\}
,
we say that the bankruptcy game
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
is equivalent to
the rectified linear function game
\mbox{\rm ReL}[w_{0};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
if and only if
E+w_{0}=W.

Rectified linear function games can also be applied to model various real-world situations.
For example, consider a set of players
N
cooperating to host an event.
Each player
i\in N
knows their expected revenue
w_{i}
from participating in the event.
Additionally, there is a cost (e.g., venue fee)
w_{0}
required to host the event.
A coalition
S\subseteq N
can host the event if and only if
the total revenue
\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}
is greater than or equal to
w_{0}
.
Therefore,
when coalition
S
hosts the event,
the benefit obtained by the coalition is the total revenue of players in
S
minus
w_{0}
,
i.e.,
\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}-w_{0}\;(\geq 0)
.
On the other hand, when coalition
S
cannot host the event
(
\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}<w_{0}
),
the benefit obtained by the coalition is equal to zero.
If we describe the above situation as a characteristic function form game,
we obtain a rectified linear function game
in a straightforward manner. In this paper, we shall exploit this equivalence and present some proofs and technical arguments in terms of rectified linear function games, since their formulation is more convenient for analysis.

Shapley Value

The Shapley value, formally defined below, is a payoff vector introduced by Shapley
shapley1953value
.

Definition 2.2

(Shapley value)
.

A permutation
\pi
of players in
N

is a bijection

\pi:\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\rightarrow N
,
and we say that

\pi(j)
denotes a player at the position
j
in the permutation
\pi
.
Let
\Pi_{N}
be the set of all permutations defined on
N
.
The Shapley value of characteristic function form game
(N,v)
is a payoff vector

\phi=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{n})
defined by

\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}\delta_{v}(\pi,i)\;\;(\forall i\in N)

where
\delta_{v}(\pi,i)=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))
,
called the marginal contribution of player
i
in permutation
\pi
, where

\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)
is the set of players in
N
which precede
i
in the permutation
\pi
.

The following lemma captures a fundamental property of the Shapley value in bankruptcy games.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(N,v)
and
(N,v^{\prime})
be a pair of bankruptcy games defined by

\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
and
\mbox{\rm BG}[E^{\prime};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
satisfying
E\leq E^{\prime}
.
Then, the inequality
\delta_{v}(\pi,i)\leq\delta_{v^{\prime}}(\pi,i)
holds for each

(\pi,i)\in\Pi_{N}\times N
.
The Shapley values
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
defined by

(N,v)
and
(N,v^{\prime})
satisfy that

\phi_{i}\leq\phi^{\prime}_{i}

(\forall i\in N)
.

Proof.
.

In the following proof, we assume that
(N,v)
and
(N,v^{\prime})
be a pair of rectified linear function games defined by

\mbox{\rm ReL}[w_{0};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
and
\mbox{\rm ReL}[w^{\prime}_{0};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]

satisfying
w_{0}+E=w^{\prime}_{0}+E^{\prime}=W
and thus
w_{0}\geq w^{\prime}_{0}
.
Obviously, we have that
\delta_{v}(\pi,i),\delta_{v^{\prime}}(\pi,i)\in[0,w_{i}]
.
If
w^{\prime}_{0}\leq w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))
,
then the inequality

\begin{array}[]{r@{}l}\delta_{v^{\prime}}(\pi,i)&=v^{\prime}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))\\
&=(-w^{\prime}_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}))-(-w^{\prime}_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)))=w_{i}\geq\delta_{v}(\pi,i)\end{array}

holds.
If
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})\leq w_{0}
,
then we have the following inequality

\delta_{v}(\pi,i)=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=0-0=0\leq\delta_{v^{\prime}}(\pi,i).

Lastly, we consider the remained case that

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))<w^{\prime}_{0}\leq w_{0}<w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})
.
It is easy to see that

\begin{array}[]{r@{}l}\delta_{v^{\prime}}(\pi,i)&=v^{\prime}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=(-w^{\prime}_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}))-0\\
&\geq(-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}))-0=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=\delta_{v}(\pi,i).\end{array}

∎

Previous studies have extensively examined various solution concepts in the context of bankruptcy games.
The pioneering work of O’Neill
o1982problem
introduced
the random arrival rule,
which is equivalent to the Shapley value in bankruptcy games.
Aziz
aziz2013computation
showed that
the problem of computing the Shapley value is #P-complete.
The celebrated paper by Aumann and Maschler
aumann1985game

proved that the solution concept discussed in the Babylonian Talmud
coincides with the nucleolus.
They also described a polynomial time algorithm
for computing the nucleolus of the bankruptcy game.
The pre-kernel and pre-nucleolus for bankruptcy games
are discussed in
funaki2006prekernel
.
For a detailed account of the properties of solution concepts in bankruptcy games, see
thomson2015axiomatic
;
driessen1988cooperative
.
A dual game representation of bankruptcy games is studied in
driessen1998greedy
.

3
Hardness Result

In this section, we prove that the problem of computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games is NP-complete.
The next lemma presents a special instance
in which the Shapley value admits
a closed-form expression.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any vector of positive rational numbers

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
,
the Shapley value
of
\mbox{\rm BG}[\frac{W}{2};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
is equal to

(w_{1}/2,w_{2}/2,\ldots,w_{n}/2),

where
W=\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}
.

Proof.
.

In this proof, we discuss the game
(N,v_{\frac{1}{2}})

defined by
\mbox{\rm ReL}[\frac{W}{2};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
,
which is equal to
\mbox{\rm BG}[\frac{W}{2};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}].

We define
\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)=v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))

(\forall(\pi,i)\in\Pi_{N}\times N)

and

w_{0}=\frac{1}{2}W.

For any permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{N}
,
we denote its reverse permutation by
\pi^{\textup{R}}
.

In the following, we show that

\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)=w_{i}\quad(\forall i\in N).

by considering the following three cases;

Case 1:

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})<w_{0},

Case 2:

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})<w_{0},

Case 3:

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})\geq w_{0}
and

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})\geq w_{0}.

Case 1
: Let consider the case that
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})<w_{0}
.
Then, the marginal contribution satisfies

\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)=v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=0-0=0.

The reverse permutation
\pi^{\textup{R}}
satisfies that

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))&=-w_{0}+w(N\setminus(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}))=-w_{0}+w(N)-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})\\
&>-w_{0}+W-w_{0}=W-2w_{0}=W-W=0\end{array}

and thus,

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)&=v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})-v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))\\
&=-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})-(-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)))\\
&=w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))+w_{i}-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))=w_{i}.\end{array}

We obtain that
\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)=w_{i}
.

Case 2
: When
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})<w_{0}
,
we can prove the desired equality similarly to Case 1 by swapping
\pi
and

\pi^{\textup{R}}
.

Case 3
:
Consider the remained case that both

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})\geq w_{0}
and

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})\geq w_{0}
hold.
The assumption directly implies

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})=-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})=-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))+w_{i}\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})&=-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})=-w_{0}+w(N\setminus\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))\\
&=-w_{0}+W-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)).\end{array}

We also obtain that

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=w(N\setminus(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})=W-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})\leq W-w_{0}=W-W/2=w_{0}\end{array}

and thus

v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=\max\{0,-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))=0.

Similarly, we have that

v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))=0.

The above properties result in the following

\begin{array}[]{r@{ }l}\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)&=v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))+v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)\cup\{i\})-v_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))\\
&=(-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))+w_{i})-0+(-w_{0}+W-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)))-0\\
&=-2w_{0}+W+w_{i}=w_{i}.\end{array}

The Shapley value
\phi_{i}
of player
i
satisfies

\displaystyle\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)=\frac{1}{2\cdot n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}(\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i))=\frac{1}{2\cdot n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}w_{i}=\frac{1}{2}w_{i}.

∎

The above proof relies on the following fundamental property.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
(\pi,i)\in\Pi_{N}\times N
,
the equality

\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,i)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},i)=w_{i}

holds.

We next consider the problem SHP,
which concerns the analysis of hardness.

Problem SHP

Instance:

A positive integer vector
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})

satisfying that

w_{i}
is an even integer for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, and

w_{n}=1.

Question:

Let
(N,v)
be a characteristic function form game defined by

\mbox{\rm BG}[\lfloor\frac{W}{2}\rfloor;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]

(which is equivalent to

\mbox{\rm ReL}[\left\lceil\frac{W}{2}\right\rceil;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
),
where
W=\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}
.
Does the Shapley value

\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}\delta_{v}(\pi,n)

satisfies
\phi_{n}<\frac{1}{2}
?

Theorem 3.3
.

Problem SHP is NP-complete.

Proof.
.

(1) Problem SHP belongs to the class NP.

We show that
\phi_{n}<\frac{1}{2}
if and only if

\exists\pi\in\Pi_{N}
satisfying
\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)<1
.
Lemma
2.3
and Corollary
3.2
imply that

\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)\leq\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi,n)+\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)=w_{n}=1.

Thus, we have that

\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}\delta_{v}(\pi,n)=\frac{1}{2\cdot n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}(\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n))\leq\frac{1}{2\cdot n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}1=\frac{1}{2}.

The above properties directly imply that

\phi_{n}=(1/2)

if and only if

\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)=1

holds for any
\pi\in\Pi_{N}
.
Now we have a desired result and
a permutation
\pi
satisfying

\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)<1

is a polynomial size certificate when problem SHP has YES answer.

(2) We show that the known NP-complete problem PARTITION can be reduced to problem SHP in polynomial time.

PARTITION Problem

Instance:

A positive integer vector

(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
.

Question:

Does there exist a subset
S\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
?

We construct an instance of problem SHP from that of PARTITION as follows:
we set
n=m+1
,

w_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2a_{i}&(i\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}),\\
1&(i=n=m+1),\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }w_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}+1=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}+1.

Now, we prove that the PARTITION problem instance has YES answer if and only if the answer to problem SHP instance is Yes.

[PARTITION has Yes answer
\Rightarrow
SHP has Yes answer]

As problem PARTITION has Yes answer,
there exists
S\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}

satisfying
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
.
We construct a permutation
\pi_{*}\in\Pi_{N}

satisfying
\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*},n)=S
.
Then, it is easy to see that

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*},n)\cup\{n\})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}+1=w_{0}.

Thus, we have that

\delta_{v}(\pi_{*},n)=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*},n)\cup\{n\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*},n)))=0-0=0.

Similarly, the equalities

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*}^{\textup{R}},n)\cup\{n\})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}+1=w_{0}

imply that

\delta_{v}(\pi_{*}^{\textup{R}},n)=(v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*}^{\textup{R}},n)\cup\{n\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi_{*}^{\textup{R}},n)))=0-0=0.

As shown in (1), the inequality

\delta_{v}(\pi_{*},n)+\delta_{v}(\pi_{*}^{\textup{R}},n)=0+0=0<1

implies
\phi_{n}<\frac{1}{2}
.

[SHP has Yes answer
\Rightarrow
PARTITION has Yes answer]

In the following, we show the contraposition.
Assume that PARTITION has NO answer, i.e.,

\forall S\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots n-1\},

w(S)\neq(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}
.
We only need to show that
\forall\pi\in\Pi_{N}
,

\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)=1
.

For any permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{N}
, we have the following.
The assumption implies that
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))\neq(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}
.
Consider the case that
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))<(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}
.
As
\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1}\}
is a set of even integers,
the inequality
w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))\leq(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}-1
holds.
Thus, we have that

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))\leq w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n)\cup\{n\})=w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))+1\leq(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}=w_{0},

which leads to the equality

\delta_{v}(\pi,n)=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n)\cup\{n\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))=0-0=0.

Similarly, we have that

\begin{array}[]{r@{}l}w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)\cup\{n\})&\geq w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n))=(W-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n)\cup\{n\}))\\
&\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}+1-(w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))+w_{n})\\
&\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}+1-((1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}-1)-w_{n}=(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}+1=w_{0},\end{array}

which leads to the equality

\begin{array}[]{r@{}l}\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)&=v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)\cup\{n\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n))\\
&=(-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)\cup\{n\}))-(-w_{0}+w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)))\\
&=w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n))+w_{n}-w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n))=1.\end{array}

From the above, the equality
\delta_{v}(\pi,n)+\delta_{v}(\pi^{\textup{R}},n)=1
holds.
In a similar way, we can deal with the remaining case that

w(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,n))>(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}
.
∎

4
Relation to Weighted Voting Games

Lemma
2.3
demonstrates that
the Shapley value is non-decreasing
in the estate
E
.
In this section, we present a detailed parametric analysis
of the Shapley value as a function of the estate
E
.
Our result reveals a connection between
the Shapley value of bankruptcy games
and the Shapley-Shubik index in weighted voting games.
Throughout this section, we assume that

E,w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
are positive integers.

Definition 4.1

(Weighted Voting Game)
.

Let
N=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a set of players,

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
be a vector of positive integers,
and
q
be a positive integer satisfying

0<q\leq w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}
.
Here,
w_{i}
denotes the number of votes held by player
i
,
and
q
denotes the quota,
i.e., the minimum number of votes required for a coalition to win.
A weighted voting game,
denoted by
\mbox{\rm WVG}[q;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
,
is a characteristic function form game

(N,v^{\langle q\rangle})
,
whose characteristic function is defined as

v^{\langle q\rangle}(S)=\begin{cases}1&(\mbox{if }S\neq\emptyset\mbox{ and }\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}\geq q),\\
0&(\mbox{otherwise}).\end{cases}

The Shapley value of a weighted voting game
is called the

Shapley–Shubik index
(see
ShapleyShubik1954
).
Given a quota
q
,
define
\varphi^{\langle q\rangle}
as
the Shapley–Shubik index
(Shapley value) of
\mbox{\rm WVG}[q;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}].

For a detailed account of algorithms for calculating power indices
in weighted voting games,
see
Mann1962RM3158
;
matsui2000survey
;
uno2012efficient
;
chalkiadakis2011computational
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that

E,w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
are positive integers
satisfyig
E<\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}
.
The Shapley value
\phi
of
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]

satisfies

\phi=\sum_{q=1}^{E}\varphi^{\langle q\rangle},

where
\varphi^{\langle q\rangle}
is the
Shapley–Shubik index
of
\mbox{\rm WVG}[q;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
.

Proof.
.

Let
(N,v)
be a game defined by
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
.
The dual game of
(N,v)
is the game

(N,v^{*})
defined by

v^{*}(S)=v(N)-v(N\setminus S)

for every
S\subseteq N
.
It is obvious that

v^{*}(S)=E-\max\left\{0,E-\sum_{i\in N\setminus(N\setminus S)}w_{i}\right\}=\min\left\{E,\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}\right\}=\sum_{q=1}^{E}v^{\langle q\rangle}(S)\;\;\;(\forall S\subseteq N).

The additivity (law of aggregation
shapley1953value
)
of the Shapley value implies that

\phi^{*}=\sum_{q=1}^{E}\varphi^{\langle q\rangle}

where
\phi^{*}
is the Shapley value of
(N,v^{*})
.
The self-duality of the Shapley value
(see, e.g.,
peleg2007introduction
)
implies
\phi^{*}=\phi

and thus we obtain the desired result.
∎

The above theorem implies that
the marginal contribution
to the Shapley value
resulting from an increase
in the estate from
E-1
to
E

coincides with the Shapley–Shubik index
of the weighted voting game
\mbox{\rm WVG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
.

By employing Theorem
4.2
,
one can compute the Shapley value of a bankruptcy game
by executing an algorithm for computing
the Shapley–Shubik index of a weighted voting game

E
times.
Furthermore, by modifying the algorithm for computing
the Shapley–Shubik index,
it may be possible to construct a more efficient method than simply running it
E
times.

In the following, we describe an algorithm
based on the dynamic programming technique,
whose time complexity is bounded by
O(n^{2}E)
for each player.
The definition of weighted voting game

(N,v^{\langle q\rangle})
implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{i}^{\langle q\rangle}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}(v^{\langle q\rangle}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\langle q\rangle}(S))\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\sum_{w=q-w_{i}}^{q-1}\frac{t!(n-t-1)!}{n!}c_{i}(w,t),\end{array}

where

c_{i}(w,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\#\left\{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}\left|\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}=w\mbox{ and }|S|=t\right.\right\}&(\mbox{ if }1\leq w\leq W\mbox{ and }1\leq t\leq n-1),\\
1&(\mbox{ if }w=t=0),\\
0&(\mbox{otherwise}).\end{array}\right.

Theorem
4.2
implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi_{i}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{E}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\sum_{w=q-w_{i}}^{q-1}\frac{t!(n-t-1)!}{n!}c_{i}(w,t)\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{t!(n-t-1)!}{n!}\sum_{w=0}^{E-1}\left(\min\{E-w,w_{i}\}c_{i}(w,t)\right)\right),\end{array}

which allows us to compute the Shapley value
\phi_{i}

in
O(nE)
time using
the matrix
C_{i}=(c_{i}(w,t))

defined by components satisfying

0\leq w\leq E-1
and
0\leq t\leq n-1
.
An ordinary dynamic programming technique gives
an algorithm for calculating the matrix
C_{i}

in
O(n^{2}E)
time
(see, e.g.,
matsui2000survey
;
chalkiadakis2011computational
).
Thus, there exists an algorithm for calculating
the Shapley value of a bankruptcy game
whose time complexity is bounded by
O(n^{2}E)
for each player.

According to Theorem
4.2
, one can easily construct an algorithm for computing the Shapley–Shubik index by applying either of the recursive algorithms (Algorithm
1
or Algorithm
2
in the next section) to the games with estates
q
and
q-1
respectively, and taking the difference between the resulting Shapley values.

5
Recursive Algorithms

In this section,
we describe recursive algorithms.
Throughout this section, we assume that

E,w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}

are positive rational numbers
satisfying
E+w_{0}=W=\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}.

For each pair
(S^{\prime},j)
satisfying

j\in S^{\prime}\subseteq N
,
we define

\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}

to be the set of permutations on
S^{\prime}

whose last component is player
j
.

5.1
Recursive Completion Method

In the following,
we describe a recursive completion method
proposed in
o1982problem
,
which is based on the following property.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(N,v)
be a characteristic function form game
defined by
\mbox{\rm ReL}[w_{0};\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
,
and let
\phi
denote
its Shapley value.
Given a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq N

with
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2
and

\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>w_{0},

the restricted game
(S^{\prime},v^{[S^{\prime}]})

is defined by

v^{[S^{\prime}]}(S)=\max\{0,-w_{0}+\sum_{k\in S}w_{k}\}

(\forall S\subseteq S^{\prime}).

For every non-empty subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq N,

we define a vector
\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}

indexed by
S^{\prime}
as follows:

\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}=\begin{cases}\mbox{the Shapley value of }(S^{\prime},v^{[S^{\prime}]})&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|\geq 2\mbox{ and }\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>w_{0}),\\
\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|\geq 2\mbox{ and }\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}\leq w_{0}),\\
v(\{k\})&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|=1\mbox{ and }S^{\prime}=\{k\}).\\
\end{cases}

Then, whenever
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>w_{0}
,
the components of
\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}
satisfy
the recursive relation

\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}_{i}=\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(\min\{w_{i},v(S^{\prime})\}+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\phi^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}\right)\quad(\forall i\in S^{\prime}).

Proof.
.

It is obvious that
for every subset

S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N
such that

|S^{\prime\prime}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime\prime}}w_{k}>w_{0}
,
we have

v^{[S^{\prime\prime}]}(S)=\max\left\{0,-w_{0}+\sum_{k\in S}w_{k}\right\}=v(S)\;\;(\forall S\subseteq S^{\prime\prime}).

If
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>w_{0},

then the Shapley value
\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}

satisfies that

\displaystyle\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}}\delta_{v^{[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)=\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|!}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*i}}\delta_{v^{[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\delta_{v^{[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(\min\{w_{i},v(S^{\prime})\}+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{[S^{\prime}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{[S^{\prime}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(\min\{w_{i},v(S^{\prime})\}+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}\right).

We now turn to the final term introduced above
for each
j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}.

(1) In case that
|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|\geq 2
and

\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}w_{k}>w_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{\delta_{v^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}}(\pi,i)}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}}\delta_{v^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}}(\pi,i)=\phi^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

(2) Consider the case that

|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}w_{k}\leq w_{0}.

As
j\neq i
, every permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}
satisfies that

\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\subseteq\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}\subseteq S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}

and thus

0\leq v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))\leq v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})\leq v(S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\})=0
.
From the above, we obtain

\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{0-0}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=0=\phi^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

(3) Finally, we consider the case
where
|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|=1.

It implies that
S^{\prime}=\{i,j\}
and

\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}
contains the unique permutation
(\pi(1),\pi(2))=(i,j)
,
from which it follows that

\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=\frac{v(\{i\})-v(\emptyset)}{1!}=v(\{i\})=\phi^{[\{i\}]}_{i}=\phi^{[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

∎

The recursive formula
in Theorem
5.1
,
leads to Algorithm
1
.
In the implementation of Algorithm
1
,
the memoisation technique is employed,
which stores
previously computed results so as to prevent
redundant computations.
The use of this technique allows
the time complexity to be bounded
by
O(n^{2}{\cal L})
,
assuming sufficient memory resources,
where
{\cal L}=\#\{S^{\prime}\subseteq N\mid|S^{\prime}|\geq 2,v(S^{\prime})>0\}=\#\{S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N\mid|S^{\prime\prime}|\leq n-2,\sum_{k\in S^{\prime\prime}}w_{k}<E\}
.

5.2
A Recursive Algorithm via the Dual Game

In this subsection,
we propose a recursive algorithm
based on the dual game
(N,v^{*})

of
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]
,
which is defined by

v^{*}(S)=\min\{E,\sum_{k\in S}w_{k}\}

(\forall S\subseteq N).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(N,v^{*})
be the dual game of
\mbox{\rm BG}[E;\mbox{\boldmath$w$}]

and denote its Shapley value by
\phi^{*}
.
Given a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq N

satisfying
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2

and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>E
,
the restricted game

(S^{\prime},v^{*[S^{\prime}]})

is defined by

v^{*[S^{\prime}]}(S)=\min\{E,\sum_{k\in S}w_{k}\}

(\forall S\subseteq S^{\prime}).

For every non-empty subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq N,

we define the vector
\phi^{*[S^{\prime}]}

indexed by
S^{\prime}
as follows:

\phi^{*[S^{\prime}]}=\begin{cases}\mbox{the Shapley value of }(S^{\prime},v^{*[S^{\prime}]})&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|\geq 2\mbox{ and }\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>E),\\
(w_{i})_{i\in S^{\prime}}&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|\geq 2\mbox{ and }\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}\leq E),\\
v^{*}(\{k\})&(\mbox{if }|S^{\prime}|=1\mbox{ and }S^{\prime}=\{k\}).\\
\end{cases}

Then, for any
S^{\prime}\subseteq N

satisfying
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2

and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>E
,
the vector
\phi^{[S^{\prime}]}
satisfies the recursive relation

\phi^{*[S^{\prime}]}_{i}=\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(v^{*}(S^{\prime})-v^{*}(S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\})+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\phi^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}\right)\qquad(\forall i\in S^{\prime}).

Proof.
.

Whenever

S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N
satisfies

|S^{\prime\prime}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime\prime}}w_{k}>E
,
we have

v^{*[S^{\prime\prime}]}(S)=\min\left\{E,\sum_{k\in S}w_{k}\right\}=v^{*}(S)\;\;(\forall S\subseteq S^{\prime\prime}).

If
|S^{\prime}|\geq 2
and
\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}}w_{k}>E
,
then the Shapley value
\phi^{*[S^{\prime}]}
satisfies that

\displaystyle\phi^{*[S^{\prime}]}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}}\delta_{v^{*[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)=\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|!}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*i}}\delta_{v^{*[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\delta_{v^{*[S^{\prime}]}}(\pi,i)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle v^{*[S^{\prime}]}(S^{\prime})-v^{*[S^{\prime}]}(S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\})\\
\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*[S^{\prime}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*[S^{\prime}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle v^{*}(S^{\prime})-v^{*}(S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\})\\
\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}\end{array}\right)

We now turn to the final term introduced above
for each
j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}.

(1) In case that
|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|\geq 2
and

\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}w_{k}>E
,
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{\delta_{v^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}}(\pi,i)}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}}\delta_{v^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}}(\pi,i)=\phi^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

(2) Consider the case that

|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|\geq 2
and

\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}w_{k}\leq E
.
As
j\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}
,
every permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}
satisfies that

\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\subseteq\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}\subseteq S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}

and thus

\sum_{k\in\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)}w_{k}\leq\sum_{k\in\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}}w_{k}\leq\sum_{k\in S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}}w_{k}\leq E.

From the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\}}w_{k}-\sum_{k\in\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)}w_{k}\right)=\frac{\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}w_{i}}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=w_{i}=\phi^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

(3) Finally, we consider the case
where
|S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|=1.

It implies that
S^{\prime}=\{i,j\}
and

\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}
contains the unique permutation
(\pi(1),\pi(2))=(i,j)
,
from which it follows that

\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{S^{\prime}}^{*j}}\frac{v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i)\cup\{i\})-v^{*}(\mbox{\rm Prc}(\pi,i))}{(|S^{\prime}|-1)!}=\frac{v^{*}(\{i\})-v^{*}(\emptyset)}{1!}=v^{*}(\{i\})=\phi^{*[\{i\}]}_{i}=\phi^{*[S^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}]}_{i}.

∎

The recursive formula
in Theorem
5.2
,
leads to Algorithm

.

6
Monte Carlo Method

In this section, we introduce a Monte Carlo method for computing the Shapley value of bankruptcy games,
which constitutes
a fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme
(FPRAS).
Throughout this section, we assume that

E,w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}

are positive rational numbers
satisfying
E<W=\sum_{i\in N}w_{i}.

First, we introduce a simple preprocedure.
If a player
i\in N
satisfies
E<w_{i}
,
then
w_{i}
is replaced by
\min\{w_{i},E\}
,
under which the characteristic function
v
remains unchanged.
This transformation reduces
the input size of the game,
and preserves (or potentially enhances)
computational efficiency.
In this section, we assume the following.

Assumption 6.1
.

The claim
w_{i}
of each player
i\in N
satisfies

0<w_{i}\leq E.

We now describe a Monte Carlo method,
which corresponds to the most simple form
of the algorithms
for calculating the Shapley-Shubik index
proposed by Mann and Shapley in their seminal work
Mann1960RM2651
.
For an overview of Monte Carlo methods
in Shapley value (and/or Shapley-Shubik index)
computation,
see
bachrach2010approximating
;
Liben-Nowell2012Computing
;
Mann1960RM2651
;
ushioda2022monte
for example.

\phi^{\prime}_{i}\leftarrow\phi^{\prime}_{i}+\delta_{v}(\pi,i)

(\forall i\in N)
and
m\leftarrow m+1
.

end

while

Output
\phi^{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\phi^{\prime}_{i}
for each
i\in N
.

The time complexity of Algorithm
3
is
O(Mn)
.

We denote the vector (of random variables)
obtained by executing Algorithm
3

by
(\phi^{A}_{1},\phi^{A}_{2},\ldots,\phi^{A}_{n})
.
The following results are obtained straightforwardly.

Theorem 6.2
.

For each player
i\in N
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]=\phi_{i}
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\delta<1
,
we have the following.

(1)

If we set

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{n^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}
,
then each player
i\in N
satisfies that

\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\frac{|\phi^{A}_{i}-\phi_{i}|}{\phi_{i}}<\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(2)

If we set
\displaystyle M\geq\frac{n^{2}\ln(2n/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}
,
then

\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\forall i\in N,\frac{|\phi^{A}_{i}-\phi_{i}|}{\phi_{i}}<\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.
.

For each
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
and
i\in N
,
define the random variable

\Delta^{(m)}_{i}=\delta_{v}(\pi,i)
,
where
\pi\in\Pi_{N}
is
the permutation sampled
in the
m

3
.
As Algorithm
3
chooses a permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{N}
randomly,
it is obvious that for each player
i\in N
,

\{\Delta^{(1)}_{i},\Delta^{(2)}_{i},\ldots,\Delta^{(M)}_{i}\}

is a Bernoulli process satisfying

\phi^{A}_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Delta^{(m)}_{i}/M
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta^{(m)}_{i}\right]=\phi_{i}

(\forall m\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\})
.

The definition of Algorithm

directly implies that

\Delta^{(m)}_{i}\in[0,w_{i}]

(\forall i\in N).

In addition,

6.1
ensures that

\delta_{v}(\pi,i)=w_{i}
holds
for any

such that
\pi(n)=i.

Thus, we obtain a lower bound for
the Shapley value, satisfying

\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}}\delta_{v}(\pi,i)\geq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{N}:\pi(n)=i}\delta_{v}(\pi,i)=\frac{(n-1)!}{n!}w_{i}=\frac{w_{i}}{n}(>0)\;\;\;(\forall i\in N).

Hoeffding’s inequality
hoeffding1963probability

implies that
each player
i\in N
satisfies

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{2M^{2}t^{2}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}({w_{i}}-0)^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(\frac{-2Mt^{2}}{{w_{i}}^{2}}\right).

(1) If we set

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{n^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}
,
then

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\frac{|\phi^{A}_{i}-\phi_{i}|}{\phi_{i}}<\varepsilon\right]=\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|<\varepsilon\phi_{i}\right]\geq\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|<\varepsilon\left(\frac{{w_{i}}}{n}\right)\right]\\
\displaystyle=1-\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|\geq\left(\frac{\varepsilon{w_{i}}}{n}\right)\right]\geq 1-2\exp\left(\frac{-2M(\varepsilon{w_{i}}/n)^{2}}{{w_{i}}^{2}}\right)\\
\displaystyle=1-2\exp\left(-M\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)=1-\delta.\end{array}

(2) If we set

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{n^{2}\ln(2n/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}
,
then we have that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\forall i\in N,\frac{\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\varphi_{i}\right|}{\phi_{i}}<\varepsilon\right]=1-\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\exists i\in N,\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\phi_{i}\right]\\
\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i\in N}\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\phi_{i}\right]\geq 1-\sum_{i\in N}\mbox{\rm Pr}\left[\left|\phi^{A}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{A}_{i}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\left(\frac{{w_{i}}}{n}\right)\right]\\

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\exp\left(-\ln\left(\frac{2n}{\delta}\right)\right)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\delta}{n}=1-\delta.\end{array}

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we focus on the computational aspects of the Shapley value in bankruptcy games and make the following three contributions:

1.

We show that a decision problem
of computing the Shapley value
in bankruptcy games is NP-complete.
This finding suggests that
it is difficult to construct
a polynomial-time algorithm
for the exact computation of the Shapley value.

2.

We investigate the relationship
between the Shapley value of bankruptcy games
and the Shapley-Shubik index
in weighted voting games.
Our result naturally leads to a dynamic programming technique
for calculating the Shapley value of a bankruptcy game
whose time complexity is bounded by
O(n^{2}E)
for each player,
assuming that
E,w_{1},\ldots w_{n}
are positive integers.
Our result improves the time complexity
of the algorithm proposed in
aziz2013computation
.

3.

We present two recursive algorithms
for computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games.
Unlike the dynamic programming approach,
these algorithms do not assume that

E
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
are integers.
We first describe the recursive completion method,
originally proposed by O’Neill.
We also introduce our own recursive algorithm
based on the dual game formulation.
We show that both approaches benefit from memoisation,
improving computational efficiency.
Their time complexities are bounded
by
O(n^{2}{\cal L})
and
O(n^{2}{\cal W})
,
where

{\cal L}=\#\{S^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N\mid|S^{\prime\prime}|\leq n-2,\sum_{k\in S^{\prime\prime}}w_{k}<E\}
and

{\cal W}=\#\{S^{\prime}\subseteq N\mid|S^{\prime}|\geq 2,\sum_{i\in S^{\prime}}w_{i}>E\}
, respectively.

4.

We describe a Fully Polynomial-Time Randomized Approximation Scheme
(FPRAS) for computing the Shapley value.
We have also shown that when
E\geq\tfrac{1}{2}W
,
the required number of samples
grows sublinearly with respect to
n

(number of players).

Future Work

An interesting direction for future work is to develop an FPRAS for computing the Shapley value in bankruptcy games that also satisfies monotonicity,
i.e., ensuring that
w_{i}\leq w_{j}
implies
\phi_{i}^{A}\leq\phi_{j}^{A}
, which the Monte Carlo method described in Section
6
does not always guarantee.
This line of research is motivated by the construction
of an FPRAS for the Shapley-Shubik index in weighted voting games by Ushioda et al.
ushioda2022monte
.
