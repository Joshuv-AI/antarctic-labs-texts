Effects of Parameter Combinations in Cervical Rotation-Traction Manipulation on Intervertebral Foramen Opening and Biomechanical Responses: A Finite Element Study
Authors: Hanze Mao, Xinyu Wang, Guangqi Lu, Shuaiqi Zhou, Minghui Zhuang, Xin Xiu, Jing Li, Xinyue Sun, Yakun Liu, Mingming Ma, Jiaming Hu, Jie Yu, Liguo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate, using a finite element model, the effects of different combinations of rotation, flexion, and re-rotation parameters in cervical rotation-traction manipulation (CRTM) on intervertebral foramen opening and biomechanical distribution, and to provide a basis for segment-specific parameter optimization.MethodsA validated C2–T1 finite element model, derived from CT images of a healthy adult male volunteer, simulated CRTM by sequentially applying rotation, flexion, re-rotation, and upward traction. Rotation angles: 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°; flexion: 20°, 30°, 40°; re-rotation: 5°, 10°, 15°, with rotation+re-rotation sum ≤80°, yielding 27 combinations. Vertical diameter changes at C3/4–C7/T1 were measured, and stresses in vertebrae, discs, and facet cartilage were analyzed via stress cloud maps.ResultsForaminal opening showed segment-specific characteristics; increased flexion promoted opening, with flexion 40°being most effective. Optimal combinations were: C3/4 with axial rotation of 70°, re-rotation of 10°, and flexion of 40° (AR70-10_FL40); C4/5 with AR65-15_FL40; C5/6 with AR60-15_FL40; and C6/7 and C7/T1 with AR60-10_FL40. Peak vertebral stress generally corresponded to the segments showing maximal foraminal opening. Stress trends in the vertebral bodies, discs, and facet articular cartilage were consistent, following the order AR60-10_FL40 < AR65-15_FL40 < AR60-15_FL40 < AR70-10_FL40. High-stress regions were mainly located in the upper and middle cervical spine, with differences reflected primarily in stress magnitude rather than stress location.ConclusionCRTM effects appear parameter-dependent and segment-specific. Flexion may be key in promoting opening, with segment-specific responses to rotation/re-rotation. Parameter adjustments affect both opening and stress distribution, suggesting segmental controllability. However, as these findings derive solely from a finite element model, they are preliminary biomechanical evidence, not directly applicable clinically. Further clinical and cadaveric studies are warranted.

Introduction

Cervical spondylotic radiculopathy (CSR) is a common degenerative cervical spine disorder in clinical practice. Its occurrence is mainly associated with intervertebral disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, and narrowing of the intervertebral foramen, and it is primarily manifested by neck and shoulder pain, as well as radiating pain and numbness in the upper limbs.1 Clinically, cervical degeneration and nerve root compression most commonly occur in the lower cervical segments, particularly at C5/6 and C6/7.2 Therefore, improving the mechanical environment of the lower cervical segments and alleviating nerve root compression are of great importance.

Various conservative treatments, including physical therapy and exercise training, have been employed for managing CSR.3,4 Cervical rotation-traction manipulation (CRTM) is a commonly used treatment for CSR, and its mechanical effects are generated through combined movements including rotation, flexion, and re-rotation.5 Previous studies have shown that the therapeutic effects of manipulative treatment are closely related to vertebral displacement, changes in intervertebral disc stress, and alterations in intervertebral foramen morphology, among which the degree of foraminal opening is an important indicator for evaluating the potential for nerve root decompression.6–8 A previous study found that the principal stress induced by CRTM was mainly concentrated at the C4/5 segment,9 suggesting that its mechanical advantage may not yet be fully exerted in the lower cervical segments. Bu et al6 further demonstrated that, under different preconditioning conditions, the effects of CRTM on facet articular cartilage stress varied among segments. These studies suggest that the biomechanical effects of CRTM may be segment-dependent. However, most existing studies have adopted single-parameter or fixed-parameter settings, and have not systematically investigated the interactive effects of combined adjustments of rotation angle, flexion angle, and re-rotation angle on intervertebral foramen opening. Moreover, there is a lack of comparative understanding regarding the optimal parameter combinations for different cervical segments.

Finite element analysis can quantitatively reveal the mechanical responses of the cervical spine under complex loading conditions in a noninvasive manner, reflecting the stress distribution, displacement changes, and deformation characteristics of the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and related structures under different conditions.10,11 Its advantage lies in the ability to simulate multiple parameter combinations within a unified model and to comparatively analyze the mechanical effects at different segments.12 Therefore, it provides an effective approach for investigating the segmental action characteristics and biomechanical mechanisms of CRTM.

Based on this, the present study used a finite element cervical spine model to set different combinations of rotation, flexion, and re-rotation parameters, and to analyze changes in the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramina at different segments under CRTM, along with the stress distribution in the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs. We hypothesized that the optimal combinations of rotation and re-rotation would differ across cervical segments, particularly for the lower segments, which may require specific parameter sets to achieve maximal foraminal opening while maintaining mechanical safety. By testing this hypothesis, we aim to explore the variation patterns of segmental responses under different parameter conditions and to provide a biomechanical basis for optimizing CRTM parameters according to the affected segment.

Methods

Model Construction

The finite element model used in the present study was developed following a previously established and validated protocol13 (Figure 1). The C2–T1 finite element model included cortical bone, cancellous bone, annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus, ligaments, spinal cord, and nerve roots. It was derived from CT images of a healthy adult male volunteer (age 28 years, height 172 cm, body mass 72 kg) with no history of cervical disease. CT scanning was performed using a multi-slice spiral CT scanner (SIEMENS, Germany) with the volunteer in a supine position and the neck relaxed. The scanning range covered the skull base, cervical spine, and upper thoracic segments, with a slice thickness of 0.45 mm and a resolution of 512×512 pixels, yielding a total of 610 axial slices. The DICOM data were imported into Mimics (version 10.1, Materialise Inc., Belgium) for segmentation and three-dimensional reconstruction of the bony structures, using a CT threshold range of 226–2311 Hounsfield units. Vertebral geometries were extracted, and intervertebral discs were reconstructed in Rapidform (INUS Technology Inc., South Korea) by sweeping curves along the endplate margins, with the annulus fibrosus accounting for 53% and the nucleus pulposus 47% of the disc volume. Because articular cartilage could not be directly segmented from CT images, it was modeled by creating a 1-mm gap at each facet joint and generating a 0.5-mm-thick layer.14,15 Ligaments and major cervical muscles were modeled based on anatomical origins and insertions, with cross-sectional areas and material properties taken from the literature.16,17Figure 1Completed finite element model of the cervical spine (C2–T1 segment). The reconstructed model was finally imported into Abaqus (version 2021, Dassault Inc., USA) for solid model partitioning and mesh generation. The final model consisted of 251,996 elements and 186,361 nodes. Among them, the dura mater and cortical bone were modeled using triangular shell elements; the cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter, white matter, nerve roots, and cancellous bone were modeled using tetrahedral elements; the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus ground substance were modeled using linear hexahedral elements; and the annulus fibrosus fibers and ligaments were modeled using linear truss elements. All components were assigned linear elastic, isotropic material properties based on previously published literature.13 Regarding boundary conditions, the inferior surface of the T1 vertebra was fully constrained in all six degrees of freedom to simulate the fixed thoracic cage, while the superior surface of C2 served as the loading site. The facet joints were modeled as surface-to-surface contact with a friction coefficient of 0.1; the interfaces between the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus, and between the endplates and adjacent vertebral bodies, were defined as bonded (tied) contacts. All ligaments were modeled as tension-only truss elements that do not resist compression. The finite element model was validated by comparing its predicted segmental range of motion under physiological loading (1.8 Nm moment) with previously published in vitro experimental data,18,19 confirming that the biomechanical responses of the model were within acceptable limits and suitable for subsequent parametric simulations.

Loading Application and Grouping

Based on the kinematic characteristics of traditional cervical rotation-traction manipulation (Figure 2), rotational, flexion, and re-rotational motion loads were sequentially applied to the C2–T1 cervical spine model. After completion of rotation, flexion, and re-rotation, an upward vertical traction force of 150 N was applied to the superior end of the model to simulate the traction effect in CRTM. This force was applied as a dynamic load, simulating the transient, high-velocity upward traction thrust characteristic of the manipulation. This force magnitude is consistent with that used in previous biomechanical studies of cervical manipulation, in which 150 N was one of the representative loading conditions.6,9 Previous literature has shown that the maximum range of lateral rotation of the human cervical spine is approximately 80°.20 According to the composite motion characteristics of CRTM, the rotation angles were set at 60°, 65°, 70°, and 75°; the flexion angles at 20°, 30°, and 40°; and the re-rotation angles at 5°, 10°, and 15°. The sum of the rotation and re-rotation angles was constrained to not exceed 80°. Under this principle, rotation angles of 60° and 65° could each be combined with re-rotation angles of 5°, 10°, and 15°; a rotation angle of 70° could be combined with re-rotation angles of 5° and 10°; and a rotation angle of 75° could only be combined with a re-rotation angle of 5°. This yielded 9 valid “rotation–re-rotation” combinations. Combined with the 3 flexion angles, a total of 27 loading groups were finally obtained for simulation analysis.Figure 2Procedure of CRTM. Because the model used in this study was a C2–T1 cervical spine model, conversion calculations were performed based on the in vitro experimental data reported by Moroney and Panjabi18,21 to determine the ratios of the rotational angles of the C2–T1 segments to those of the C0–T1 segments in the three anatomical planes. Specifically, in the flexion direction, the motion angle of the C2–T1 segments was approximately 42.1% of that of the C0–T1 segments; in the axial rotation direction, the motion angle of the C2–T1 segments was approximately 49.4% of that of the C0–T1 segments.

Outcome Measures and Post-Processing Analysis

Under the 27 loading conditions, the model was analyzed using the Abaqus post-processing module. Measurements of the intervertebral foramen usually include the vertical and transverse diameters. Because CRTM is primarily characterized by vertically directed lifting traction, its effect on the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen is more pronounced; therefore, the vertical diameter of the cervical intervertebral foramen was used as the primary evaluation index in this study.8 Taking the initial model state as the reference, changes in the vertical diameter of the contralateral intervertebral foramen at C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, C6/7, and C7/T1 were measured to evaluate the effects of different CRTM parameter combinations on foraminal opening. During measurement, the linear measurement tool in the software was used, and the line connecting the midpoints of the adjacent upper and lower pedicle margins was defined as the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen (Figure 3). In addition, peak stresses in the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and facet articular cartilage under each condition were extracted. For stress cloud map visualization, the parameter combinations that yielded the maximal foraminal opening at each segment were selected, and the corresponding stress distribution patterns were analyzed. This comparison was designed to determine whether the segment of peak stress concentration would shift in accordance with the segment showing maximal foraminal opening, thereby assessing whether parameter adjustments could alter both foraminal opening and stress localization patterns.Figure 3Measurement of the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen. Characteristics of Vertebral Stress Distribution at Different Segments

As shown in Figures 4–7, the peak stresses of the C4–C7 vertebral bodies differed markedly under different representative conditions, and the optimal condition corresponding to peak stress was not the same for each vertebra. Specifically, the peak stress of C4 was greatest under AR70-10_FL40, that of C5 was greatest under AR65-15_FL40, whereas those of both C6 and C7 were greatest under AR60-10_FL40. This pattern was generally consistent with the optimal conditions corresponding to the maximum opening of the intervertebral foramen. In terms of spatial distribution, the locations of the high-stress regions varied among vertebral bodies. The high-stress regions of C4 and C5 were mainly concentrated at the anterosuperolateral margin of the vertebral body on the rotation side, adjacent to the upper endplate. The high-stress region of C6 was mainly located at the posteroinferolateral margin of the vertebral body on the contralateral side of rotation, adjacent to the lower endplate. The high-stress region of C7 was mainly concentrated in the transitional region from the middle posterior margin to the posterolateral aspect of the vertebral body. Stress Distribution Characteristics of the Intervertebral Discs

The stress cloud maps of the intervertebral discs in the whole cervical spine (Figure 8) showed that the order of maximum stress under different conditions was AR60-10_FL40 < AR65-15_FL40 < AR60-15_FL40 < AR70-10_FL40, with corresponding values of 4.23, 4.27, 4.43, and 4.47, respectively. In terms of spatial distribution, disc stress at each segment generally showed lower values in the central region and higher values in the peripheral annulus fibrosus, with high-stress regions mainly concentrated at the posterolateral margins. Stress Distribution Characteristics of Facet Articular Cartilage

The variation trend of peak stress in the facet articular cartilage was consistent with the overall stress results of the vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs (Figure 10), following the same order: AR60-10_FL40 < AR65-15_FL40 < AR60-15_FL40 < AR70-10_FL40, with corresponding peak values of 4.83 MPa, 4.96 MPa, 5.37 MPa, and 5.48 MPa, respectively. The high-stress regions were mainly concentrated on the contact surface of the facet articular cartilage on the contralateral side of rotation, showing a marked asymmetric distribution, and were mostly located at the local marginal areas of the contact surface, indicating localized stress concentration. Discussion

This study performed a finite element analysis to investigate the effects of different parameter combinations of CRTM on cervical intervertebral foramen opening and overall mechanical distribution. Rather than attributing the effects of CRTM simply to a change in a single angle, the present study suggests that its biomechanical action may be essentially a composite regulatory process involving initial rotation, re-rotation, and flexion, manifested as an overall alteration in segmental posture, facet contact relationships, and load transmission pathways under conditions of multi-parameter coupling.

From a biomechanical perspective, the observed changes in the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen, although seemingly modest in absolute terms, are meaningful when evaluated against established anatomical reference data. The cervical nerve root diameter ranges from approximately 0.25 cm to 0.46 cm,22 and the maximum increment observed in the present study is comparable to or even exceeds the diameter of most cervical nerve roots, suggesting that such dimensional changes may translate into a measurable reduction in nerve root compression. Therefore, while these findings are derived from a computational model and cannot be directly applied to clinical decision-making, they provide biomechanically grounded evidence supporting the potential of parameter-optimized CRTM to achieve meaningful foraminal decompression.

From a mechanistic perspective, the significance of flexion in the manipulation lies not only in changing the position of the cervical spine, but also in reshaping the transmission pattern of the traction effect.23 Under a greater flexion position, the tension state of the posterior structures, the relative relationship of the intervertebral space, and the contact pattern of the facet joints may all be altered, allowing the traction load to be more readily converted into a segmental longitudinal separation effect, thereby promoting intervertebral foramen opening.24,25 Therefore, flexion may constitute an important mechanical prerequisite for the foraminal opening effect of CRTM. In manipulative treatment, flexion should be regarded as a key basic variable influencing decompression potential; for manipulations primarily aimed at nerve root decompression, appropriate control of the flexion angle may be of greater practical significance than simply increasing the rotation angle.

Notably, different segments responded differently to the parameter combinations, indicating that CRTM has evident segment-specific characteristics. Differences exist between the upper cervical spine and the middle-lower cervical spine in terms of anatomical morphology, motion characteristics, facet orientation, and constraints imposed by the surrounding soft tissues. In addition, the upper and lower cervical spine exhibit different motion patterns during axial rotation, with the upper cervical spine contributing the major portion of head axial rotation.26,27 Therefore, the same manipulation parameters may not produce identical mechanical effects at all segments. The present study found that the upper cervical segments were more sensitive to larger rotation angles, whereas the middle-lower cervical segments were more likely to achieve favorable opening effects under relatively smaller rotation combined with appropriate re-rotation. These findings suggest that CRTM can generate differential mechanical effects at different segments through parameter adjustment, and that more refined parameter matching based on the target segment, lesion location, and individual anatomical characteristics of the patient may help improve the specificity of its effect. However, these observations are derived from a computational model and should be interpreted as biomechanical hypotheses that require further validation through clinical studies before they can be translated into specific treatment recommendations.

On the other hand, analysis of vertebral stress and stress cloud maps suggests that the improvement in intervertebral foramen opening does not occur in isolation, but is accompanied by an overall mechanical redistribution among multiple cervical structures. The stress variation trends of the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and facet articular cartilage showed good consistency, indicating that parameter adjustment in CRTM does not merely cause a localized change in the stress state of a single tissue, but rather elicits a coordinated response of the anterior column, posterior column, and their connecting structures within the same mechanical system. The underlying mechanism may lie in the fact that manipulation parameters alter the overall postural coupling relationship and load transmission pathway of the cervical spine, thereby producing a multi-structural biomechanical effect.24,28,29 Therefore, evaluation of the effectiveness of CRTM should not focus solely on whether the intervertebral foramen is enlarged, but should also take into account whether structures such as the intervertebral discs, vertebral endplates, and facet joints are under relatively reasonable stress conditions.

However, when interpreting the stress results, the relationship between foraminal opening and stress elevation must be considered with caution in a clinical context. A larger opening is generally favorable for neural decompression, but higher stress concentration may raise safety concerns, particularly in patients with pre-existing degeneration, osteoporosis, or facet joint disease. Therefore, it is necessary to distinguish between “optimal opening” and “mechanically safer” conditions. In the present study, the segment with maximal opening under a given parameter combination also tended to show higher peak stress, indicating that decompression benefit and mechanical risk are coupled to some extent. This is quantitatively supported by the stress ranking among the four optimal combinations: AR60-10_FL40 < AR65-15_FL40 < AR60-15_FL40 < AR70-10_FL40. Notably, the C6/7 and C7/T1 segments achieved optimal opening with the lowest stress, suggesting a favorable efficacy-safety balance, whereas the C3/4 segment achieved maximal opening at the cost of the highest stress, implying a greater mechanical cost. These comparisons suggest that parameter selection for clinical application should consider multi-structural stress responses, rather than relying solely on foraminal opening. Furthermore, this balance may vary across segments, reinforcing the need for segment-specific optimization that accounts for individual anatomical and pathological variability.

Although parameter optimization can enhance the opening effect at specific segments and, to a certain extent, redistribute the dominant stress-bearing segments, this adjustment is mainly manifested as reinforcement of local advantageous segments and has not altered the overall pattern in which high stress remains primarily concentrated in the upper and middle cervical spine. Therefore, adjustment of CRTM parameters may be more suitable for improving the targeted efficiency of action at the intended segment, rather than being interpreted as a means of completely reshaping the overall stress pattern of the cervical spine. For CSR, these computational findings raise the hypothesis that optimizing CRTM parameters according to the responsible segment might help improve the specificity of local decompression and targeted intervention. However, this hypothesis requires further validation through clinical and cadaveric studies before any direct therapeutic recommendations can be made.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the finite element model allowed intuitive characterization of the mechanical variation patterns under different parameter combinations, some soft-tissue structures and their material properties were simplified during model construction. As a result, the model could not fully represent the heterogeneity, anisotropy, and individual variability of the human cervical spine, and therefore still differs from the complex in vivo biomechanical environment. More importantly, the model was reconstructed from a single healthy adult male volunteer rather than from patients with cervical spondylotic radiculopathy, and thus does not account for the degenerative pathological features characteristic of the target clinical population, including disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, reduced disc height, and pre-existing foraminal narrowing. These structural alterations may substantially affect the biomechanical responses to manipulation, so the present findings may not be directly generalizable to patients with established CSR. In addition, the model did not incorporate several clinically relevant factors, such as muscle activation, pain-related reflex responses, patient relaxation, operator technique variability, and tissue viscoelasticity or tolerance limits—all of which are inherently dynamic in the clinical setting and may influence the magnitude and distribution of mechanical effects during actual CRTM. Accordingly, the findings require further validation through cadaveric experiments and clinical studies. Second, the CRTM parameter combinations selected in this study were discrete rather than continuous. Thus, the results mainly reflect the variation patterns within the preset conditions, and the optimal parameter range may not have been fully covered, making some degree of selection bias unavoidable. Finally, this study mainly used changes in the vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen and stress distribution as evaluation indices. Although these indices can adequately reflect the foraminal opening effect and stress characteristics of CRTM, they remain limited in fully characterizing its biomechanical effects. Future studies should incorporate additional evaluation indices to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the biomechanical effects of CRTM on the cervical spine under different parameter combinations based on a three-dimensional finite element model. The results showed that CRTM exhibited evident segment-specific characteristics in promoting intervertebral foramen opening. The vertical diameter of the intervertebral foramen generally increased with increasing flexion angle in all segments, and maximal opening was observed under the FL40 condition. With respect to rotation angle, the upper cervical segments tended to respond more favorably to larger rotation angles, whereas the middle-lower cervical segments showed more pronounced opening under relatively smaller rotation angles combined with appropriate re-rotation. Parameter adjustment not only altered the degree of intervertebral foramen opening but also synchronously affected the stress distribution of the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and facet articular cartilage, suggesting that CRTM may have a certain degree of segment-specific controllability from a biomechanical modeling perspective. However, this should not be interpreted as established clinical controllability. These findings provide preliminary finite element biomechanical evidence for optimizing CRTM parameters according to the affected segment, but further validation through cadaveric experiments, in vivo imaging studies, and clinical trials is required before these results can be translated into clinical practice.
