Numerical evidence of contrasting hydrodynamic responses of concave and convex breakwaters
Authors: Shaik Firoj, Mohammad Saud Afzal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study numerically investigates the interaction of solitary waves with arc-shaped breakwaters using the non-hydrostatic shallow water equation model REEF3D::SFLOW. Large-domain simulations are conducted to compare the hydrodynamic responses of concave arc-shaped breakwater (CC-ASBW) and convex arc-shaped breakwater (CX-ASBW) with those of a straight vertical-wall breakwater (VBW). Model validation was carried out using analytical solutions, laboratory measurements, and previously published numerical data, confirming its predictive accuracy. The simulations resolve free-surface evolution, diffraction patterns, and vortex dynamics, supported by detailed analyses of circulation, core vorticity, vortex radius, and trajectory. The results show that the concave geometry concentrates energy toward the central region, generating strong wave focusing, higher normalized free-surface elevation, and pronounced reflection along the centerline. Conversely, the convex geometry disperses energy laterally, lowering reflection near the center and providing improved sheltering behind the structure. The VBW exhibits nearly uniform reflection along its face but limited energy dissipation in the lee. Quantitative analysis confirms that geometric curvature critically governs energy redistribution and vortex behavior, with concave curvature intensifying local reflection and coherent vortices, while convex curvature weakens circulation and reduces transmission. These findings establish the contrasting hydrodynamic behavior of concave and convex geometries and provide practical guidance for curvature-aware breakwater design.

Introduction

Coastal regions, harbours, and ports across the globe face increasing threats from extreme wave events such as tsunamis, storm surges, and other weather-induced phenomena. These events often lead to flooding, erosion, and severe structural damage, endangering lives, economies, and the long-term stability of critical infrastructure. Breakwaters play a pivotal role in mitigating these impacts by absorbing and deflecting wave energy, thereby reducing the force of waves reaching vulnerable shorelines, port facilities, and navigation channels. Traditionally, straight and linear breakwaters have dominated practice due to their relative simplicity and proven effectiveness under many wave conditions. However, it is well established that conventional straight or vertical breakwaters exhibit important limitations. Experimental studies on semi-infinite and rigid breakwaters show that straight configurations are particularly sensitive to wave directionality, with diffraction effects leading to reduced shelter performance under multi-directional or focusing wave fields1,2. Analytical diffraction solutions for rigid straight breakwaters further demonstrate limited adaptability of linear layouts when subjected to oblique and directional wave attack3, while investigations considering random wind-generated waves confirm that significant wave energy can persist in the lee of straight or detached breakwaters due to directional spreading4. Moreover, rigid straight breakwaters typically generate high wave reflection, which increases wave-induced forces acting on the structure5 and promotes localized scour at the structure toe, as demonstrated in laboratory experiments on vertical impermeable breakwaters6. Such reflection-induced scour processes contribute to increased maintenance demands and long-term stability concerns7.

Over time, diverse breakwater types have been developed to address site-specific requirements and hydrodynamic conditions. Rubble mound breakwaters remain among the most widely used, providing durability and adaptability across a variety of coastal settings. Massive vertical breakwaters, including block, caisson, and cellular types, offer stability in deeper waters and heavily trafficked ports. Composite breakwaters combine rubble mound foundations with vertical superstructures, while pile-supported and other flexible designs are used where seabed or environmental constraints demand adaptability. Beyond these standard forms, more specialized concepts have been proposed, including semi-infinite8, rectangular1, cylindrical9, and detached breakwaters10. Multilayered porous breakwaters under regular and irregular waves, demonstrating that appropriate structural layering and frictional properties significantly influence reflection and transmission behaviour11. Spectral analyses further indicate that porous configurations can enhance wave energy dissipation depending on sea state and material properties12. Each approach demonstrates compromises between construction cost, structural resilience, and wave attenuation.

More recently, attention has shifted toward curved breakwaters, including concentric and arc-shaped breakwaters (ASBWs). Liu and Lin13 applied a semi-analytical scaled boundary finite element method to concentric cylinders, including porous arc-shaped walls, observing that arc curvature could substantially modify hydrodynamic loading. Zhai et al.14–16 developed eigenfunction expansion models for cnoidal and short-crested wave interaction by arc-shaped porous breakwaters, finding that dual-arc arrangements offered stronger attenuation than single arcs. Han et al.17 conducted FLOW-3D simulations of oblique wave interaction with ASBWs, noting that curvature exerted limited influence on sectional load distribution but strongly affected lee-side diffraction patterns. Negi et al.18 investigated flexural–gravity waves interacting with porous dual-arc breakwaters shielding floating plates, highlighting their potential for offshore energy protection. Despite these advances, knowledge remains fragmented, with many studies confined to idealized analytical models. Taken together, these studies suggest that ASBWs can produce distinctive hydrodynamic responses compared with straight designs. However, systematic evaluation of their performance under extreme wave events are still scarce, leaving unresolved questions about their reliability, energy dissipation capacity, and practical effectiveness in coastal protection.

The study of wave diffraction has long been central in understanding breakwater hydrodynamics. Early theoretical investigations by Penny and Price19, Johnson20, and Wiegel8 established foundational insights into wave behavior near semi-infinite straight barriers and gaps between long breakwaters. Later, Briggs1 employed large-scale physical modelling to quantify three-dimensional diffraction around breakwater heads, extending predictive capability to zones within several wavelengths from structural tips. Isaacson and Qu21 studied partially reflecting harbour boundaries, while Williams and Crull22 explored offshore vertical-screen systems. Further, Abul-Azam23 analyzed twin flexible breakwaters, and Yu24 developed a porous breakwater diffraction model rooted in linear potential theory. Subsequent works expanded scope to porous25 and detached structures26, multidirectional random waves27, and novel geometries such as two-legged floating breakwaters28. More recently, Zhou29 modelled partially reflecting breakwaters with varying reflection coefficients and layouts. Scattering studies involving porous breakwaters and floating docks confirm that geometry and porosity strongly influence reflection, transmission, and wave forces under varying sea states30. Collectively, these contributions highlight diffraction as a key determinant of breakwater performance.

However, much of this literature has been dominated by linear wave theory. While valuable for small-amplitude, harmonic waves, linear assumptions break down under highly nonlinear conditions such as solitary waves, which feature strong amplitude dependence, steep fronts, and pronounced energy concentration. Recognizing these limitations, researchers began probing nonlinear effects. Liu31 derived a Fourier-integral solution for a solitary wave incident on a semi-infinite thin barrier, showing partial alignment with experiments but systematic underestimation of arrival times and overestimation of amplitudes. Power and Chwang32 studied solitary wave reflection at vertical walls, while Wang33 employed generalized Boussinesq equations to extend diffraction modelling into the weakly nonlinear regime, achieving closer agreement with experimental data than linear counterparts. Grilli34 further examined solitary wave breaking over breakwaters, highlighting the diverse behaviors that emerge from nonlinear interactions. Wave-current interaction studies on oscillating water column systems demonstrate that current-induced frequency shifts can significantly alter resonance and hydrodynamic loading35,36.

Advances in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) have significantly broadened the scope for studying wave-structure interactions beyond analytical and laboratory constraints. Three-dimensional Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes solvers remain central for resolving free-surface evolution, wave breaking, and pressure fields around coastal structures, and recent investigations have applied them to diverse configurations, including porous foundations, slotted barriers, and interlocked armour systems37–39. In parallel, physical modelling supported by CFD has clarified reflection behaviour, trapped-wave resonance, and flow separation in structures equipped with horizontal and vertical slotted barriers40,41. Coupled FDEM-CFD frameworks have further enhanced the ability to simulate contact forces, interlocking behaviour, and the dynamic response of armour units under wave loading42, while semi-resolved non-hydrostatic CFD-DEM approaches have proven effective for modelling polydisperse permeable breakwaters43. Alongside full three-dimensional CFD, depth-integrated and non-hydrostatic formulations have emerged as efficient alternatives for large-domain coastal simulations, offering a practical balance between accuracy and computational cost. Notable advances include nonlinear shallow-water-based non-hydrostatic solvers for surf-zone waves44, hydrostatic front approximations suitable for coarse vertical grids45, finite-element non-hydrostatic models capable of simulating breaking and run-up46, and hybrid approaches incorporating dispersion effects47. Additional developments including depth-integrated finite-volume solvers48, depth-averaged non-hydrostatic extensions equivalent to Boussinesq-type equations49–51, and explicit non-hydrostatic shallow-water formulations52 have strengthened the modelling framework for coastal hydrodynamics. Recent numerical investigations have further demonstrated the influence of seabed geometry and structural configuration on wave transformation and hydrodynamic response in nearshore systems53–55. Collectively, these studies illustrate a clear progression toward high-fidelity numerical strategies capable of resolving nonlinear wave motion, vortex generation, and wave-structure interactions across a range of coastal settings.

Despite these advances, previous studies on arc-shaped and curved breakwaters have largely relied on analytical approaches that cannot fully capture large-scale diffraction and associated flow structures. The detailed vortex dynamics governing energy dissipation and wake development around concave and convex geometries remain insufficiently understood. Large-scale numerical modelling provides an effective approach to address these limitations by resolving complex flow behavior and energy transfer at realistic scales. The present study conducts large-domain simulations using REEF3D::SFLOW56 to compare the hydrodynamic performance of CC-ASBW and CX-ASBW with that of a VBW. The objective is to elucidate the dominant hydrodynamic and vortex processes that control wave attenuation and flow behavior around curved breakwaters, thereby informing the design of more efficient coastal structures.

Unlike conventional shallow water solvers based solely on hydrostatic assumptions, SFLOW incorporates a quadratic pressure profile following Jeschke et al.51, allowing the representation of depth-averaged vertical velocity. The extended formulation refines the representation of wave dispersion and strengthens the model’s capacity to reproduce nonlinear wave–structure interactions. Numerical schemes

The governing system is discretized using the finite difference method on a regular staggered grid. Convective terms are treated with a fifth-order Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory scheme58, ensuring robustness in the presence of strong gradients. Time integration is performed using a third-order Total Variation Diminishing Runge–Kutta algorithm59, providing accuracy and stability for transient wave fields. Non-hydrostatic pressure is computed through the solution of a Poisson equation, where the dynamic pressure field is updated at each step to enforce divergence-free velocity. Computational efficiency is achieved through integration of the Hypre library60 employing a BiCGStab solver accelerated by PFMG preconditioning. Adaptive time stepping, constrained by the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition, is used to balance computational efficiency with accuracy. A sponge layer is applied near the domain boundaries to suppress wave reflection. This framework makes REEF3D::SFLOW particularly well suited for large-scale simulations of curved breakwaters, where capturing diffraction, vortex generation, and nonlinear interactions requires both computational efficiency and physical accuracy.

Figure 12 illustrates the temporal development of the vorticity field for the CC-ASBW. At the initial stage, a counter clockwise vortex emerges behind the lower end of the structure (Fig. 12a), accompanied by flow separation and concentrated swirling motion. As time progresses, the vortex rotates and drifts downstream, deforming continuously under its self-induced rotation (Fig. 12b). Subsequently, another counter clockwise vortex forms close to the end, while the earlier one shifts backward (Fig. 12c). A larger clockwise vortex then develops near the same region due to the transmitted flow arriving from the other side (Fig. 12d). This dominant clockwise vortex occurs because the concave curvature directs the incoming flow inward toward the center of the breakwater. As the flows entering from both ends collide, a pressure gradient develops that redirects the flow toward the opposite end, consolidating the energy into a larger clockwise vortex. Thus, the inward-facing curvature promotes flow convergence and recirculation, which naturally produces clockwise-dominated vorticity at later stages. These vortices interact, eventually merging into a stronger, coherent structure with intensified rotational motion. With further evolution, the clockwise and counter clockwise vortices exhibit mutual rotation, gradually weakening as viscous dissipation increases (Fig. 12e). Toward the end of the sequence (Fig. 12f–h), the vortex cores lose strength, and their filaments shed downstream, producing smaller secondary vortices with reduced intensity. These small eddies mark the gradual decay of energy and the transition toward a more stable flow field.

The vorticity development around the CX-ASBW is displayed in Fig. 13. Initially, a large counter clockwise vortex forms near the lower end (Fig. 13a), noticeably larger than that observed in the concave case, reflecting a broader flow separation region. In contrast to the concave case, the convex face directs the incoming flow outward along the outer curvature. The flow follows the exterior arc instead of being drawn toward the center. Because no inward convergence occurs, the counter clockwise vortex generated by flow separation retains its strength and remains the dominant feature of the wake. The outward curvature therefore encourages lateral spreading of the flow, allowing the counter clockwise rotation to persist throughout the evolution. The velocity vectors reveal intense swirling motion with strong rotational gradients. As the flow evolves, the vortex takes a more defined circular form (Fig. 13b), and small clockwise vortices appear in its vicinity (Fig. 13c). These secondary vortices orbit around the primary vortex and interact dynamically. Over time, an additional counter clockwise vortex emerges nearby, further enriching the rotational pattern (Fig. 13d). Subsequently, a small clockwise vortex forms as a result of transverse flow from the opposite end. The large counter clockwise vortex slowly migrates away from the breakwater, accompanied by the gradual shedding of minor vortices (Fig. 13e–h). During this phase, both clockwise and counter clockwise vortices lose rotational strength, and their vorticity magnitudes diminish due to energy dissipation.

The vorticity evolution around the VBW is depicted in Fig. 14. The wave interacts with the edges of the breakwater earlier than in the other two cases, leading to the prompt formation of a well-defined counter clockwise vortex (Fig. 14a). Shortly afterward, smaller clockwise vortices appear and orbit around it (Fig. 14b). The interaction between the dominant and secondary vortices results in visible shedding from the breakwater end (Fig. 14c,d). Eventually, the clockwise and counter clockwise vortices drift backward, continuing their mutual rotation while gradually losing coherence and strength as the flow stabilizes (Fig. 14g–h).

Figure 15 summarises how geometry affects anticlockwise vortex properties. The time history of core vorticity (Fig. 15a) shows that CX-ASBW maintains the largest peak core vorticity throughout the evolution, followed by VBW and then CC-ASBW. The characteristic early dip and subsequent rapid rise in core vorticity correspond to the formative stages of separation followed by the consolidation of a concentrated vortex core. After formation, the core vorticity for each case decays as viscous diffusion and vortex interaction remove concentrated rotation, with CC-ASBW showing the most pronounced gradual decline. The vortex radius (Fig. 15b) begins smallest for CC-ASBW but undergoes a sudden increase when successive anticlockwise eddies shed from the tip merge; this merging produces a larger effective radius for CC-ASBW that, for a period, exceeds the radii observed for VBW and CX-ASBW. The radius then oscillates as shedding and readjustment continue before settling into a slowly varying trend. Circulation (Fig. 15c) follows the radius behaviour for CC-ASBW but remains lower in magnitude than for CX-ASBW and VBW; in other words, CC-ASBW produces a broader, less intense anticlockwise footprint whereas VBW concentrates more vorticity into stronger circulation. Taken together, these metrics indicate that the integrated rotational strength of the anticlockwise vortex is highest for VBW, intermediate for CX-ASBW, and lowest for CC-ASBW. This behaviour is consistent with the different flow mechanisms around the two curved geometries. For the CX-ASBW, the outward-facing curvature pushes the incoming flow away from the structure and creates a broad, stable separation zone. This promotes a strong and persistent anticlockwise vortex that maintains high circulation throughout the evolution. In contrast, the concave curvature of the CC-ASBW draws the incoming flow inward, producing smaller anticlockwise eddies that interact and merge but do not retain concentrated rotational strength. As a result, even when the vortex radius temporarily increases due to merging, the overall anticlockwise circulation remains comparatively weak.

Figure 16 reports the corresponding quantities for the clockwise vortices. Core vorticity magnitudes for the clockwise eddies are broadly similar across the three geometries (Fig. 16a) but decay most rapidly for CC-ASBW, consistent with faster dissipation in its broader leeside flow. The initial vortex radius is larger for CC-ASBW (Fig. 16b), then contracts during vortex re-organization and becomes smaller than VBW while remaining larger than CX-ASBW. Over time the radius for VBW and CX-ASBW tends to grow as those vortices translate and spread, whereas CC-ASBW shows a decreasing radius trend. Circulation for the clockwise vortices exhibits transient oscillations during the early formation and merging phases (Fig. 16c) but then decays steadily. Overall the clockwise circulation ranks as CC-ASBW (largest), then VBW, and finally CX-ASBW. This indicates that, although the convex geometry produces extended vortex footprints, the concave geometry concentrates more integrated rotational energy in the clockwise eddy generated by transmitted flow. These circulation patterns arise because the two curved geometries generate clockwise vortices through entirely different mechanisms. In the concave case (CC-ASBW), the inward-facing curvature pulls the transmitted flows from both ends toward the centre, where they collide and form a strong clockwise recirculation cell. This produces a larger footprint but also enhances dissipation, leading to a steady decline in circulation. For the CX-ASBW, the clockwise vortex forms later and is generated mainly by transverse flow produced after diffracted waves interact near the symmetry line. Because much of the wave energy has been damped by the outer and inner convex faces before reaching the vortex-formation region, the resulting clockwise eddy is weaker but more concentrated, which results in a consistently smaller circulation for the CX-ASBW compared with the CC-ASBW.

The evolution of the free surface elevation (η /H) contours for the CC-ASBW, CX-ASBW, and VBW is shown in Figs. 18, 19 and 20, respectively. For the CC-ASBW, the η /H initially increases near the ends during the incident wave interaction (Fig. 18a). As the wave converges along the concave surface, the local η /H rises sharply, reaching a maximum value of 2.69 (Fig. 18b). During reflection, the wave exhibits a concave pattern with the highest η /H concentrated at the center of the breakwater (Fig. 18c). Subsequently, the reflected wave evolves into an oval-shaped region with a peak η /H of 2.37, and the free surface gradually transforms into a convex pattern as the wave propagates seaward, accompanied by a steady decrease in η /H (Fig. 18e–h). The observed behavior indicates strong wave focusing caused by the concave curvature, which amplifies the local free surface elevation during convergence and reflection.

In the case of the CX-ASBW, the η /H increases during the initial interaction with the incident wave, attaining a maximum of 2.17 (Fig. 19a). This behavior is consistent with analytical predictions reported for arc-shaped and V-shaped breakwaters under solitary and short-crested wave conditions16,61. As the wave diverges along the convex arc, the η /H intensifies near the lateral ends, reaching approximately 1.85 (Fig. 19b). Thereafter, the reflected wave propagates outward with a gradual reduction in η /H as it moves seaward (Fig. 19b–d). The overall pattern demonstrates that the convex geometry disperses wave energy laterally, thereby reducing the peak elevation and minimizing wave focusing compared to the CC-ASBW.

For the VBW, the η /H increases uniformly along the vertical wall due to the larger contact area between the incoming wave and the structure, as shown in Fig. 20a. This trend is consistent with analytical results reported for vertical breakwaters61. Notably, the η /H of the reflected wave exhibits a slight increase as it travels seaward (Fig. 20a–d). During diffraction at the breakwater ends, the η /H is highest for the VBW, followed by the CC-ASBW and CX-ASBW, as observed in Figs. 20b,c, 18b,c, and 19c,d. However, when the diffracted waves from both ends propagate into the sheltered region and collide along the symmetry line, the maximum η /H occurs for the CC-ASBW, followed by the VBW and CX-ASBW (Figs. 18f, 20e, and 19f). This collision is not predicted or captured in existing analytical studies16,61. It is important to note that the wave collision occurs just behind the breakwater for the CC-ASBW and VBW, whereas for the CX-ASBW, the collision point is farther leeward due to stronger wave dispersion. Comparison of hydrodynamic responses for CC-ASBW, CX-ASBW, and VBW.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive numerical investigation of the hydrodynamic performance of CC-ASBW, CX-ASBW, and VBW using the non-hydrostatic model REEF3D::SFLOW. The model was validated against analytical, experimental, and numerical benchmarks and shown to reproduce free-surface and velocity responses with satisfactory accuracy. The results demonstrate that breakwater curvature plays a decisive role in wave transformation and flow dynamics. Relative to VBW, CC-ASBW intensifies wave-structure interaction due to wave convergence and energy focusing toward the centerline, resulting in a 28.9% increase in reflected velocity, a 42.6% increase in the reflection coefficient, a 43.1% increase in transmitted velocity, a 7.65% increase in the transmission coefficient, and a 17.6% increase in maximum wave run-up, accompanied by stronger local agitation and secondary wave collisions in the lee region. In contrast, CX-ASBW promotes lateral wave dispersion along the arc, leading to a 47.5% reduction in reflected velocity, a 51.6% decrease in the reflection coefficient, a 52.4% reduction in transmitted velocity, a 3.82% reduction in the transmission coefficient, and a 4.8% reduction in maximum wave run-up, thereby reducing central reflection and providing more effective sheltering behind the structure. VBW exhibits nearly uniform reflection along its face and intermediate performance, with lower energy dissipation than the curved geometries. Vortex analysis further confirms the curvature-controlled flow mechanisms, with CC-ASBW generating stronger and more concentrated vortices with higher circulation near the center, while CX-ASBW produces weaker, more diffuse vortical structures that dissipate more rapidly downstream, contributing to enhanced energy decay and improved near-bed flow stability. From a design perspective, CC-ASBW is suitable where strong wave blocking is required but may induce higher local loads and increased scour potential, whereas CX-ASBW offers superior lee-side protection and calmer sheltered conditions. Overall, the quantitative comparisons of velocity attenuation, wave coefficients, vortex behavior, and wave run-up presented in this study provide a clear, curvature-aware basis for selecting arc-shaped breakwaters. The present study is limited to two-dimensional non-hydrostatic simulations under solitary wave conditions and considers impermeable rigid breakwaters over an idealized bathymetry. While this framework efficiently captures large-scale free-surface evolution and vortex dynamics, it does not resolve three-dimensional flow effects, wave irregularity, structural porosity, or sediment transport processes. Future work should extend this approach to more complex wave conditions, realistic bathymetries, porous or rubble-mound structures, and coupled sediment transport to further evaluate practical implications.
