Avoided recurrences and costs with adjuvant atezolizumab for patients with early non-small cell lung cancer in Europe
Authors: Alastair Greystoke, Nick Jovanoski, Pavel Napalkov, Melina Arnold
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study estimated avoided recurrences and treatment costs in patients with stage II-IIIA non-small cell lung cancer treated with adjuvant atezolizumab in five European countries over 10 years (2024–2034).Materials & MethodsRecurrences and deaths were projected in the presence and absence of adjuvant atezolizumab. Inputs were from the literature with extrapolation of disease-free survival from the IMpower010 trial. Country-level direct costs of treating recurrences were estimated.ResultsAdjuvant atezolizumab prevented an estimated 4952 recurrences (2199 locoregional, 2753 metastatic) over 10 years (2024–2034), associated with €264 million savings in treatment costs across the five countries.ConclusionAdjuvant atezolizumab may provide a meaningful reduction in recurrences and associated treatment costs among patients with early non-small cell lung cancer in Europe.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death in men and women across Europe, [1] with approximately 477,000 new lung cancer cases and 384,000 lung cancer-related deaths in Europe in 2020 [2]. Among the most populous countries, including France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the United Kingdom (UK), the estimated age-standardized mortality rate for lung cancer ranges from 9.63/100,000 person-years (Spain) to 16.20/100,000 (UK) [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, [3] with approximately one-third diagnosed in resectable or “early-stage” NSCLC (eNSCLC; AJCC v7 stage I, II or IIIA) [4]. Historically, surgical resection was the preferred primary treatment option for this patient group, with or without neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy for eligible patients [5]. Despite the availability of adjuvant chemotherapy regimens, disease recurrence remains high, with >50% of patients with stage II-IIIA NSCLC experiencing a recurrence within 5 years [6–8].

Recurrence rates for patients with eNSCLC have improved with the relatively recent availability of immunotherapy and targeted treatments [9–11]. The anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) immunotherapy atezolizumab showed a significant disease-free survival (DFS) benefit compared with best supportive care (BSC) among stage II-IIIA (AJCC v7) resected patients with NSCLC receiving adjuvant chemotherapy in the randomized, open-label, Phase III IMpower010 clinical trial (NCT02486718) [9,10,12]. Atezolizumab is approved for use in Europe as monotherapy for adjuvant treatment following complete resection and platinum-based chemotherapy for adult patients with NSCLC with a high risk of recurrence whose tumors have PD-L1 expression on ≥50% of tumor cells (PD-L1 TC ≥ 50%) and who do not have EGFR mutant or ALK-positive NSCLC [13]. Atezolizumab received marketing authorization approval in the UK (January 2022) and was recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) for use within the Cancer Drugs Fund as adjuvant treatment after complete resection for adults with stage II-IIIA (UICC/AJCC 7th edition) NSCLC whose tumors have PD-L1 expression on ≥50% of tumor cells and whose disease has not progressed following platinum-based adjuvant chemotherapy [14].

In this context, it is important to quantify and characterize the potential impact of adjuvant atezolizumab on population-level patient benefits, particularly its potential to reduce recurrences for adults with eNSCLCs. Such estimates are essential to evaluating the clinical and economic value of adjuvant atezolizumab for patients with eNSCLC in order to support evidence-based access and reimbursement decisions in Europe. We developed a treatment impact model to estimate the effect of adjuvant atezolizumab on recurrences avoided and associated treatment cost savings in patients with eNSCLC in five European countries.

Materials & methods

Model overview

We developed a treatment impact model (Supplementary Figure S1) to estimate the potential of adjuvant atezolizumab to reduce locoregional and metastatic recurrences and associated direct treatment costs among patients with eNSCLC in France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the UK between 2024 and 2034. We estimated the size of the target population in each country from 2024 to 2034, including the number of patients eligible for and treated with adjuvant atezolizumab or BSC, treatment patterns in scenarios with and without adjuvant atezolizumab, and the probability of locoregional and metastatic recurrence or death based on time spent disease-free (disease-free survival, DFS). Consultation was provided by three clinical experts in the UK. The model was developed in Microsoft® Excel® 2016 (www.microsoft.com)

Patients with NSCLC and adjuvant treatment assumptions

The target population comprised adults with resected stage II-IIIA NSCLC who have PD-L1 TC ≥ 50%, EGFR- and ALK-, and are eligible to receive adjuvant treatment. The number of patients with newly diagnosed lung cancer was estimated by histology, stage and country based on country-specific cancer registry data and the literature (Table 1; additional detail in Supplementary Table S1) [15–34]. Approximately one-quarter (24%) of all patients were assumed to be PD-L1 TC ≥ 50%, [15] 10% and 4% of whom were assumed to be EGFR+ and ALK+, respectively [16]. Across countries, 74% of stage I-III patients were assumed to be eligible for adjuvant treatment after surgery [15]. Since the actual uptake of adjuvant atezolizumab, specifically, could not be known or derived from existing sources, the base case analysis applied an assumption that 75% of eligible patients would receive adjuvant atezolizumab from 2024 to 2034, based on clinical expert consultation (25% BSC). Scenario analyses explored lower (50%) and higher (100%) estimates of this assumed adjuvant atezolizumab uptake.

Future projection of NSCLC incidence was done using the following steps. First, compound annual growth rate was calculated for each country for historical age-standardized incidence rates of NSCLC obtained from local cancer registries (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S2) [17–34]. Then, projection factors were applied to historical age-, sex-, and country-specific incidence rates to predict future trends in incidence rates. These incidence rates were then applied to the age-, gender-, and country-specific demographic projections from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2019 report [26].

Extrapolation of DFS

Number of recurrences and deaths were estimated for the adjuvant atezolizumab and BSC treatment scenarios: (1) where adjuvant atezolizumab is present (with usage determined by market shares) but some patients still receive BSC only, and (2) where adjuvant atezolizumab is absent and all patients receive BSC.

DFS was based on the IMpower010 clinical trial (NCT02486718), which had a 32-month follow-up period (clinical cutoff date, January 2021) [9,10,35]. As the epidemiological model can follow patients for up to 10 years after initiation of adjuvant treatment, DFS beyond the timeframe of the IMpower010 trial was extrapolated. Different parametrical distributions were tested and found to provide good fit to the observed DFS according to Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria (AIC, BIC). The extrapolated DFS curves for each treatment arm are provided in Supplementary Figure S3. The model assumes that recurrences and disease-related deaths would cease at 5 years after initiation of adjuvant treatment (or 5 years from entering the model). This assumption was determined to be plausible by expert clinical consultation based on the small number of cancer-related deaths expected with adjuvant chemotherapy after year 5 [6]. Clinical expert consultation suggested that 40% to 50% of patients receiving BSC might be expected to remain disease-free at year 5, which was reflected in the Log-logistic, Gompertz, and Generalized gamma distributions. However, the Gompertz and Generalized gamma distributions showed fewer patients receiving adjuvant atezolizumab vs BSC would be disease-free at year 10 which was not considered to be clinically plausible. The Log-logistic model appeared to better match observed DFS (AIC, 281.5; BIC, 286.8) compared with the Log-normal model (AIC, 281.8; BIC, 287.2). Therefore, the Log-logistic distribution provided the most appropriate and relatively conservative estimate of patients likely to be disease-free and was used for the model. From a statistical point of view, not including the 5-year restriction could lead to the model overestimating recurrences since the extrapolation of DFS was based on the results of a parametric survival analysis from a time period when most recurrences occur (within 3 years of surgery). Scenario analyses explored the impact of using other distributions to extrapolate the DFS model inputs and of assuming that recurrences and disease-related deaths could occur up to 10 years.

Among patients who had disease recurrence, 44% and 56% were assumed to have a locoregional or metastatic recurrence, respectively, based on the IMpower010 trial [10]. After locoregional or metastatic recurrence, 81% [36] and 82% [37] of patients were assumed to experience further disease progression (only considered to estimate costs).

Costs of treating recurrence

This model only considered drug acquisition and administration costs from an international pharmaceutical pricing database and from country-specific sources (data.navlin.com). The country-level costs of treating recurrences were estimated separately for patients who received adjuvant atezolizumab and can re-challenge with cancer immunotherapy (recurrence ≥12 months after initiation of adjuvant treatment) and those who cannot re-challenge with cancer immunotherapy (recurrence <12 months after initiation of adjuvant treatment). Scenario analyses

Scenario analyses were conducted to explore alternative assumptions to those used in the base case model. Scenario analysis 1 varied the proportion of eligible patients assumed to receive atezolizumab to low (50%) and high (100%) values relative to the base case assumption (75%).

Scenario analysis 2 assumed that recurrences and disease-related deaths would cease at year 10 rather than the base case assumption of year 5. Scenario analysis 3 explored the use of alternate statistical distributions for the extrapolation of DFS, including the Exponential, Weibull, Log-normal, and Gamma distributions. Generalized Gamma and Gompertz distributions were tested but resulted in clinically implausible projections of DFS (Supplementary Figure S3), and therefore were not included in the scenario analyses.

Results

Recurrences avoided with adjuvant atezolizumab

Across the five countries, the base case model estimated a total of 6967 and 9165 patients experiencing locoregional recurrences with and without adjuvant atezolizumab over 10 years (2024–2034), respectively, resulting in 2199 avoided locoregional recurrences with adjuvant atezolizumab. According to the model, metastatic recurrence was predicted in 8724 and 11,477 patients with and without adjuvant atezolizumab, respectively, resulting in 2753 avoided metastatic recurrences with adjuvant atezolizumab over 10 years (2024–2034) (Figure 1, Supplementary Figure S4). In total, 4952 locoregional or metastatic recurrences are thus potentially avoidable over the next decade (2024–2034). The country-level estimates of avoided recurrences are presented in Figure 1. Costs of treating recurrences

Following the estimated reductions in recurrences, the base case analysis projected that treating recurrences would cost €264 million less across the five countries when adjuvant atezolizumab was available (atezolizumab, €612 million; BSC, €876 million). The cost savings associated with treating recurrences with vs without adjuvant atezolizumab ranged from €46 million (France and the UK) to €62 million (Italy) (Figure 2). Scenario analyses

Scenario analysis 1 exploring levels of uptake for adjuvant atezolizumab treatment showed lower (50%) or higher (100%) use of atezolizumab treatment to be associated with fewer or more avoided recurrences, respectively (Supplementary Figure S5), and higher or lower associated cost savings (Supplementary Figure S6) compared with the base case analysis. Scenario analysis 2, varying the assumption that recurrences and disease-related deaths cease only after 10 years, showed fewer avoided recurrences (Supplementary Figure S7) and smaller cost savings (Supplementary Figure S8) with vs without atezolizumab compared with the base case analysis. Scenario analysis 3 exploring alternative distributions for DFS extrapolation, where events were assumed to cease at year 5, supported the use of the log-logistic distribution in the base case analysis (Supplementary Figures S9 and S10, respectively).

Discussion

This study showed a substantial population-level reduction in recurrences and associated direct treatment costs from avoided recurrences when adjuvant atezolizumab was available for eligible patients with eNSCLC in France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the United Kingdom. In the base case analysis assuming 75% of eligible patients would receive adjuvant atezolizumab, the treatment impact model estimated 4952 avoided recurrences across the included countries over the next decade (2024–2034), ranging from 800 in Spain to 1231 in Germany. The direct treatment costs of treating recurrences over 10 years (2024–2034) were €264 million lower with adjuvant atezolizumab treatment. These costs ranged from –€46 million in France and the UK to –€62 million in Italy, reflecting differences in second-line treatment use and subsequent costs between countries. Scenario analyses showed more avoided recurrences and greater reductions in associated direct treatment costs when all eligible patients received adjuvant atezolizumab. This treatment impact model suggests that adjuvant atezolizumab would reduce locoregional and metastatic recurrences among eligible patients and would substantially reduce the direct costs associated with treating recurrences across these five countries.

The cost-effectiveness of adjuvant atezolizumab has been demonstrated previously in several countries. Yip et al (2023) reported an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of £20,392 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) vs BSC for patients with resected stage II-IIIA NSCLC and PD-L1 TC ≥ 50% over a lifetime time horizon from an NHS perspective [38]. Similar studies supported the cost-effectiveness of adjuvant atezolizumab in Spain [39] and Finland [40]. Atezolizumab has also been shown to be cost-effective vs BSC among patients with eNSCLC and PD-L1 ≥ 1% from a US payer perspective, where Das et al. (2022) reported an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $46,859 per QALY, well below the US willingness-to-pay threshold of $150,000/QALY [41]. These models also extrapolated the proportions of patients in DFS beyond those reported in the IMpower010 trial, with sensitivity and scenario analyses supporting the robustness of the primary findings [38,41].

Findings from this study should be interpreted in the context of certain strengths and limitations. This treatment impact model focused specifically on the European perspective, using inputs from European registries and published epidemiological and clinical sources. The model assumed that 74% of patients would be eligible for adjuvant treatment based on the 2018 National Lung Cancer Audit report in the UK. Kehl et al. (2022) reported 57% of patients with stage IB-IIIA NSCLC received adjuvant treatment in the US ALCHEMIST real-world study, but comparable real-world data from Europe were not available for this study [42]. While extrapolation of DFS data comes with inherent uncertainty around clinical benefit beyond the timeframe of the IMpower010 trial, the log-logistic distribution was a conservative, appropriate approach based on clinical consultation, and scenario analyses explored the impact of other DFS distributions on estimated recurrences with vs without adjuvant atezolizumab. It should be noted that differences in the characteristics of patients in IMpower010 and real-world clinical practice could limit the generalizability of the modeled results. However, use of real-world data for this model would have raised issues related to the non-randomized nature of adjuvant atezolizumab or BSC use in the real-world (where DFS could be biased by patient differences even after statistical adjustments) and the short follow-up time that would be available in real-world data sources given the regulatory approval of adjuvant atezolizumab in 2022. The model did not allow the split in the type of DFS event (recurrence or death) to change over time, which may not reflect real-world clinical outcomes.

The objective of this model was to estimate the number of patients who would avoid relapse after adjuvant atezolizumab and the translation of this population-level outcome to a decrease in treatment costs (only drug acquisition and administration costs) related to avoided relapses. This model was not designed to account for the larger societal burden of recurrences, such as health utilities and indirect costs, and did not account for surrounding medical costs such as for management of adverse events associated with either adjuvant atezolizumab or treatment of avoided relapses. Treatment costs were estimated using a simple approach that assumed patients could receive a limited number of treatment options for each type of recurrence and progression event, with time spent on treatment based on evidence from clinical trials. Thus, the estimated costs may differ from those that would be incurred in real-world practice. It may also be noted that the treatment landscape for this patient population continues to evolve, which may impact future interpretation of these estimates. Changes in lung screening practices over the next 10 years may also impact these estimates, as would the potential increase in use of neoadjuvant and perioperative therapy. Future work may explore the societal perspective for specific countries where robust cost-effectiveness studies have not already been conducted. Expanding the scope of health economic burden associated with recurrences would likely improve the estimation of benefits of avoided recurrences and, in turn, of adjuvant atezolizumab in this setting.

Conclusion

This modeling study suggests that adjuvant atezolizumab following chemotherapy may lead to a substantial reduction in recurrences among patients with PD-L1 TC ≥ 50% stage II-IIIA NSCLC, and a meaningful reduction in the direct costs of treating recurrences in Europe.
