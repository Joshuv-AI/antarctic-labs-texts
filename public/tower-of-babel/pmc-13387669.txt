Is theoretical physics, large language models and artificial intelligence the power accelerator needed for the development of the discipline of theoretical medicine
Authors: Sunil K. Raina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTheoretical physics shaped much of what eventually strengthened the domain of physics. Will large language models (LLMs), and artificial intelligence (AI) emerge as the primary accelerators for the development of theoretical medicine. The window of opportunity for this to happen is now.

Background

In the very recent past, France was at the center of the artificial intelligence (AI) debate, with the official conduct of the third edition of the AI Action Summit in its capital, Paris, India’s Prime Minister acting as the co-chair of the Summit.[1] The gathering was large as large language models have made AI friendlier than it ever has been reflecting how large language models have made AI friendlier than ever before, as well as the elevated curiosity surrounding the low-cost Chinese foundational model DeepSeek, which has shaken up the industry. Stated to achieve three major objectives; providing access to independent, safe and reliable AI to a wide range of users; developing AI that is more environmentally friendly; and ensuring global governance of artificial intelligence that is both effective and inclusive. The summit emphasized the growing role of AI in contemporary life.[1] One area where Large Language models and artificial intelligence could be domain shifting; is in the development of a functioning theoretical medicine discipline on lines similar to theoretical physics with an aim to achieve in medicine and public health, what theoretical physics could in physics. Of all the technological innovations available to mankind at current times, large language models (LLMs) and AI will have a significant role in shaping the future of theoretical medicine. Critically, however, they should be understood as functioning like a powerful accelerator rather than as the absolute origin of that development, since the evolution of theoretical medicine has been a process attempting to gain ground for centuries. This evolution has been grounded in disciplines like biophysics, systems biology, and computational medicine, yet has not reached a degree of sophistication commensurate with its ambitions. AI and LLMs are now bringing unprecedented capabilities that could decisively shift that trajectory.

Why Theoretical Medicine

Medicine is more dynamic than what one may perceive it to be as it must continuously keep evolving in response to scientific discoveries, technological advancements, emerging diseases, and societal needs. Its epistemology is a complex interplay of empirical evidence, scientific rigour, clinical expertise, and evolving knowledge. While medicine strives for objectivity and despite claims of it being truly evidence based, it remains a largely probabilistic discipline, constantly refining its understanding of health and disease.

Research in medicine and public health faces several limitations, including but not limited to funding constraints, bureaucratic and regulatory barriers, data access and privacy issues, public distrust, inequitable global research focus, publication bias and industry influences, and rapidly changing threats of emerging and re-emerging diseases. Theoretical medicine could be a game-changer in this context. Beyond health being defined in social-ecological systems, theoretical medicine has the capacity to build in the Complex adaptive capabilities needed to make healthcare systems more resilient and adaptive.

In a world where for example the discovery and development of a drug becomes a long, costly, and high-risk process that can take over 10–15 years with an average cost of over $1–2 billion for each new drug to be approved for clinical use, the stakes for conduct of research are too high.[2] The resources required are far beyond the means of many institutions, let alone individual researchers. The stakes are compounded by the fact that nine out of ten drug candidates entering clinical studies fail during Phase I, II, or III trials. And this 90% failure rate is for the drug candidates that are already advanced to phase I clinical trial and does not include the drug candidates in the preclinical stages. If drug candidates in the preclinical stage are also counted, the failure rate of drug discovery/development is even higher than 90%. These failures are primarily attributable to lack of clinical efficacy (40–50%), unmanageable toxicity (30%), poor drug-like properties (10–15%), and lack of commercial need or poor strategic planning (10%). Theoretical medicine offers a framework that could fundamentally reduce these failure rates by improving target selection and mechanistic understanding before any resource-intensive empirical trials begin.

What Makes Theoretical Medicine Relevant

From science to philosophy, attempts at understanding the universe, through “quantum theory” focusing on the behavior of matter at the subatomic level, or “general theory of relativity” describing gravity and the structure of space-time on large scales or plain philosophical “physicalism” claiming that everything is physical, human endeavors seem to have consistently stayed theoretical in principle for past many centuries now. Therefore, the elevated emphasis in medical scientific epistemology on empirical evidence gathered through observation and experimentation, or through a systematic approach to inquiry alone seems contradictory to these human endeavors. The idea, of course, is to remain objective. But this perception of objectivity is itself rooted in an acceptance of tentativeness as central to evaluation where conclusions can be revised based on new evidence, all the while remaining open to falsification. This openness to falsification makes the practice of science in medicine both fascinating and selectively cautious. In doing so, compared to other domains of science, medicine tends to become less of an art of science and more of an art of practice. But practice without theory is logically incomplete. While we keep basking in the glory of the development of theoretical physics, a long and fascinating journey, shaped by the evolution of ideas and discoveries that have expanded our understanding of the universe, we find little similar in the field of medicine.

Finding the Common Ground

Ancient civilizations like the Greeks, Egyptians and Indians laid down the groundwork for theoretical physics through their observations and theories about nature, proposing ideas about motion, the elements, and the nature of the cosmos; Though many of these ideas were more philosophical than scientific by contemporary standards, they represented unified thinking about the laws governing life and the universe. But while theoretical physics progressed from classical mechanics and thermodynamics to statistical mechanics and ultimately quantum theory, the understanding of medicine began to become more and more restricted to specific domains. At the turn of the 20th century, physics experienced a dramatic shift with contributions from Max Planck (1900) and Albert Einstein (1905).

While theoretical medicine did make efforts to evolve at that time, these efforts were set back by a failure to demonstrate the consistency that had become the expected standard of medical science. The most widely cited reason for the rejection of Semmelweis insights; what we might retroactively identify as a proto-theoretical contribution was not the idea itself but the failure to present consistent mechanistic data. When Joseph Skoda lectured to the Imperial Academy of Sciences on 18 October 1849, he mentioned only cadavers as the causative source, omitting the broader pattern. The rejection of Semmelweis is therefore not merely a story about institutional resistance to a good idea; it is a story about what happens when a discipline lacks a theoretical framework capable of absorbing and contextualizing anomalous evidence. Had theoretical medicine existed as a coherent discipline, it might have provided exactly such a framework.

The Window of Opportunity

The advent of quantum computing which is pushing the boundaries of computational power with the capacity to revolutionize fields like materials science combined with LLMs and AI, could open the decisive window for theoretical medicine. The discipline needs to pick up the threads from theoretical physics and develop a deeper understanding of the laws governing everything from the sub-cellular level to environmental influences. But the three components of this enabling triad play distinct and complementary roles, and understanding the mechanism of their interaction is essential to appreciating why the combination is qualitatively more powerful than any of the three alone.

The contribution of theoretical physics: Language, abstraction, and epistemic discipline

Theoretical physics contributes the most important thing that theoretical medicine currently lacks; a culture and toolkit of abstraction. Physicists are trained to strip a system down to its essential variables and write governing equations that are predictive, falsifiable, and scalable. Several tools are already migrating from physics into medicine. Statistical mechanics for modeling cell populations and epidemic dynamics, information theory for neural coding and genomic compression, renormalization group theory for understanding multi-scale biological organization, non-equilibrium thermodynamics for metabolism and morphogenesis, and network topology for protein interaction webs and connectomics are already finding space in medicine. Physicists have also imported phase-transition thinking into biology; the idea that healthy-to-diseased states may be analogous to physical phase transitions, which yields predictive leverage over tipping points and critical thresholds in disease progression. This kind of thinking could transform how we understand and anticipate disease, offering something that purely empirical medicine cannot; a predictive framework built on mechanism rather than pattern alone.

The Higgs boson analogy, frequently cited in discussions of theoretical prediction, deserves deeper unpacking in this context. The deeper point is not simply that theoretical physics predicted something before it was confirmed. It is that theoretical physics had created a falsifiable predictive framework, the Standard Model capable of absorbing that confirmation meaningfully and directing the next generation of experimental questions. Theoretical medicine’s analogous goal should be to create frameworks that are simultaneously falsifiable at multiple scales, sub-cellular, organismal, and epidemiological. The theoretical physicist’s instinct to work across scales simultaneously is precisely what systems biology aspires to, and what AI now makes computationally tractable.

The contribution of large language models: Knowledge integration and hypothesis generation at scale

LLMs are, counter intuitively, not merely text engines. They function as pattern-compression machines trained on the accumulated record of human scientific knowledge. For theoretical medicine, their specific value lies in several capacities that address real bottlenecks in the discipline.

First, biomedical literature is so vast and fragmented that no human researcher can hold it all. LLMs can identify non-obvious connections across immunology, genomics, pharmacology, and clinical data, connections that might otherwise require decades of cross-disciplinary collaboration to surface. Second, one of the central bottlenecks of theoretical medicine is translating biological intuitions into formal mathematical structures. LLMs can assist in proposing candidate equations, identifying analogous frameworks from physics or engineering, and stress-testing the logical consistency of theoretical models. Third, LLMs combined with structured data; genomic, proteomic, clinical can construct richer intermediate representations that feed into formal theoretical models, serving as a translation layer between empirical complexity and theoretical elegance. This synthesis role is genuinely novel. Theoretical medicine requires a scientist who is simultaneously a biologist, a mathematician, a physicist, a clinician, and an epidemiologist. No such person reliably exists. LLMs, imperfect as they are, begin to approximate a collaborator who can hold these domains in simultaneous view.

The contribution of broader AI: Empirical discovery and multi-scale modeling

Broader AI particularly deep learning, graph neural networks, reinforcement learning, and symbolic AI contributes at a different level than LLMs. This is where the empirical discovery engine for theoretical medicine lives. Alpha Fold essentially solved protein structure prediction, which had been an open problem for fifty years. That has been a paradigm-shifting empirical result that now allows theoretical frameworks around protein function, misfolding diseases, and drug binding to be constructed on solid structural ground. Reinforcement learning is being applied to discover optimal treatment protocols. Graph neural networks model biological interaction systems at scales previously intractable. These represent step changes in what is computable about living systems. Perhaps most consequential for theoretical medicine is the maturation of causal AI. Theoretical medicine ultimately requires causal models, not merely correlational ones. The distinction matters enormously; knowing that two variables are correlated tells you nothing about what will happen if you intervene on one of them. Causal inference integrated with machine learning is now reaching a level of sophistication that allows causal structures to be inferred from observational data at biological scales. This represents the most direct path toward the kind of mechanistic, interventional knowledge that theoretical medicine needs.

The synthesis: Why the triad constitutes a qualitatively new capability

The reason these three components together constitute something qualitatively new rather than merely additive is that they solve different bottlenecks simultaneously and in a mutually reinforcing loop. Theoretical physics provides the language and the epistemological discipline; how to think formally about complex systems, how to frame falsifiable predictions, and how to reason across scales. LLMs provide knowledge integration and hypothesis generation at a scale no individual researcher can achieve. Broader AI provides empirical discovery and model validation against real biological complexity. Alone, each has serious limitations. Physics-style theorizing in medicine has historically been too reductive, forcing elegant mathematics onto systems that resist it. LLMs hallucinate and lack ground truth. AI without theoretical structure produces black boxes that are clinically uninterruptable and scientifically unsatisfying. Together, they form a productive loop: theory constrains AI modeling, AI surfaces patterns that refine theory and LLMs help translate and synthesize across both. The core of theoretical medicine will be formed by systems biology modeling complex biological systems using mathematical equations, networks, and simulations describing interactions between cells, genes, proteins, and tissues. But the extension of systems biology to social and environmental situations is an essential element, and this is where the triad becomes indispensable.

The frontier: What theoretical medicine can achieve

Theoretical medicine will be better positioned to understand and explain the complexity of health and disease because it will not require simultaneous proof of existence at the microscopic level and validation of large-scale phenomena such as a pandemic at the same moment. It can generate entirely new ideas and models that explain existing data or predict new phenomena, much as theoretical work predicted the existence of the Higgs boson before it was experimentally confirmed in 2012.

Critically, theoretical medicine will provide the framework and hypotheses that guide experimental research, making its conduct easier, simpler, and less time- and resource-intensive. Without theoretical predictions, experiments lack direction a reality made apparent by the inability to complete all the clinical trials initiated and the difficulty of interpreting results when over-reliance on frequentist research design prevails. Theoretical medicine can also help identify novel targets for intervention by uncovering previously overlooked interactions between genes, proteins, and cells that play crucial roles in disease.

At the population level, models can simulate the spread of infectious diseases, predict the impact of public health policies, and optimise hospital resource allocation. In regenerative medicine, theoretical approaches can guide understanding of stem cell behaviour, tissue engineering, and mechanisms of regeneration potentially accelerating therapies for spinal cord injury, heart failure, and other conditions where regenerative approaches hold significant promise. Making predictions and framing policies that incorporate uncertainty as central rather than peripheral to the policy framework will be perhaps the most distinctive and important contribution of theoretical medicine. Uncertainty is not noise; in living systems, it is signal.

Honest confrontation: Institutional and epistemological challenges

The caveat to describing this triad as a ‘power accelerator’ is that it resolves neither the fundamental ontological complexity of living systems nor the institutional and cultural barriers that have historically constrained theoretical medicine. Biology evolved; it was not designed. It is full of historical contingency, redundancy, and context-dependence that resists the kind of elegant unification theoretical physics achieved. There is a real possibility, perhaps even a probability that theoretical medicine will never have its equivalent of Maxwell’s equations. The discipline may converge on a messier, more pluralistic kind of theory than physicists are accustomed to; reliable predictive pluralism rather than unified theory. Naming this distinction honestly is not a concession of defeat; it is a clarification of what success actually looks like.

Beyond the intellectual challenge, the institutional one is equally formidable. Medicine’s regulatory frameworks are built entirely around empirical trial design. Its peer review culture rewards incremental empirical findings over bold theoretical synthesis. Its training pipelines produce clinicians and bench scientists, not theoreticians. No amount of computational power resolves these structural barriers without deliberate disciplinary and institutional reform running in parallel. The triad is a powerful accelerant; but it is accelerating a vehicle that must also be steered by a community willing to reconceive what medical science is for.

Conclusions

By leveraging computational power, causal modeling, multi-scale simulation, and cross-domain knowledge synthesis, theoretical medicine could reshape healthcare, making it more efficient, predictive, and scientifically robust, not as an abstract aspiration, but as a programme of work that can begin now. What is needed alongside this computational infrastructure is an equally serious investment in the institutional, educational, and epistemological reforms that would allow a community of theoretical medicine practitioners to form, be trained, and be rewarded for the kind of work this discipline demands. The accelerant is real. The vehicle must now be built to receive it.
