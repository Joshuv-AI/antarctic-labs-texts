Evaluation of operator experience and nickel–titanium retreatment file systems in curved root canals
Authors: İdil Özden, Arzu Çil, Beste Cilli, Merve Gökyar, Tuba Ayhan, Hesna Sazak Öveçoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to evaluate the influence of operator experience and four different nickel–titanium (NiTi) retreatment systems in curved canals with respect to procedural time, complications, and file distortions during root canal filling removal.Materials and MethodsRoot canal treatment was performed on 80 acrylic blocks with a 30° curvature. The blocks were assigned to either an endodontist or an endodontic resident (n = 40 each) and further divided into four subgroups (n = 10) according to the retreatment system used: ProTaper Universal Retreatment, ROGIN Retreatment Files, FANTA AF Retreatment Files, and EndoArt Retreatment Gold. Procedural time, complications (perforation, ledge formation, and apical transportation), and file distortions (unwinding, tip deformation, and instrument separation) were recorded.ResultsThe median procedural time for the endodontist (120 [95–142]) was significantly shorter than that for the resident (145 [117–162]) (p = 0.007). No statistically significant differences were observed between operators with regard to complications. The ProTaper Universal Retreatment system exhibited the lowest overall complication rates numerically. Additionally, both the FANTA AF Retreatment Files and the ProTaper Universal Retreatment system demonstrated a lower incidence of ledge formation when used by the resident; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionThe endodontist completed the procedures faster than the endodontic resident. The ProTaper Universal Retreatment system showed the lowest overall number of complications across both operators. The FANTA AF Retreatment Files demonstrated the lowest incidence of ledge formation, whereas EndoArt Retreatment Gold exhibited the highest incidence.Keywords: root canal therapy, retreatment, clinical competence, intraoperative complications

Introduction

Various techniques are available for removing root canal filling materials during non-surgical retreatment, including manual instruments used with heat or solvents, ultrasonic devices, lasers, and motorized rotary systems (1, 2). Nevertheless, complete cleanliness of the canal walls remains difficult to achieve, regardless of the method employed (3, 4, 5). To improve cleaning efficiency, nickel–titanium (NiTi) rotary instruments have been developed, and their efficacy, safety, and cleaning capacity have been extensively investigated (6, 7, 8, 9). Retreatment of curved or narrow canals increases the risk of file separation by compromising the mechanical strength of NiTi instruments (3). To address this issue, manufacturers have introduced thermal treatment processes during production to modify the crystalline structure of these instruments, resulting in improved stability and increased resistance to fatigue and deformation (10).

ProTaper Universal Retreatment (PTU-R) files (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) are conventional, non–heat-treated instruments designed for the removal of root canal filling materials and are available in various sizes, tapers, and tip diameters. These files have a convex triangular cross-section similar to that of ProTaper Universal shaping files, and their efficacy has been well documented in straight root canals (11, 12, 13, 14). In contrast, EndoArt Retreatment Gold (EAR-G) files (İnci Dental, Istanbul, Turkey) are manufactured from thermally treated controlled memory (CM) wire. Thermal processing increases the austenite–martensite transition temperature to approximately 50 °C, allowing the instruments to remain predominantly in the martensitic phase, thereby enhancing flexibility. EAR-G files also feature a convex triangular cross-section.

ROGIN Retreatment Files (R-RF) (Shenzhen, China) and FANTA AF Retreatment Files (FAF-R) (Shanghai, China) are cost-effective alternatives that are frequently preferred in regions with budgetary constraints. R-RF files are produced from a high-strength, elastic NiTi alloy and have a triangular cross-section. FAF-R files are manufactured using AF-R wire technology, which improves flexibility and fatigue resistance, thereby enhancing elasticity and reducing fracture risk. These instruments feature a diamond-shaped cross-section.

Rotary NiTi retreatment systems have been shown to be effective in the removal of obturation materials (11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18). However, despite their advantages, instrument fracture remains a concern, particularly among inexperienced operators (19, 20, 21). The risk of fracture is influenced by several factors, including operator experience, instrument design, manufacturing process, retreatment technique, and root canal anatomy (22, 23, 24). Although previous studies have evaluated residual filling material, procedural time, and complication rates independently, limited data are available regarding the interaction between operator experience and the physical properties of retreatment instruments. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize four NiTi retreatment systems, assess procedural time, and evaluate complication rates in curved canals with respect to operator experience. Three null hypotheses were tested: there is no difference in procedural time with respect to operator experience or the retreatment system used; there is no difference in the incidence of complications with respect to operator experience or the retreatment system used; and there is no difference in file distortion with respect to operator experience or the retreatment system used.

Materials and Methods

Sample size determination

A quota sampling method was used to determine the sample size in the study, taking into account sample sizes from other studies in the literature (10, 25)

Sample preparation and root canal treatment

Eighty acrylic blocks with a 5 mm radius and a 30° apical curvature were used in this study (EndoPrep, EduDent, Turkey). After establishing a 16 mm working length, canals were prepared with Ni-Ti rotary files up to size 35/0.4 (Smart Gold, EndoArt, Inci Dental, Turkiye) per manufacturer instructions by the same operator (IO, endodontist). Irrigation was performed with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) using a total of 5 mL per canal, delivered with a 30-gauge side-vented needle (NaviTip, Ultradent, USA) positioned 1–2 mm short of the working length. Canals were minimally dried with paper points (PearlEndo, PearlDent, Vietnam) to preserve moisture for sealer setting. Obturation was completed using a bioceramic sealer (DiaRoot Biosealer, DiaDent, South Korea) with the single-cone technique, utilizing a 35/0.04 gutta-percha cone. Fillings were verified radiographically, with voids recondensed. Access cavities were sealed with cotton pellets and temporary material (Cavit, Espe, Germany). Samples were stored at 37°C and 100% humidity for 14 days to ensure complete sealer setting.

Study groups

To blind operators to canal direction, blocks were covered with dark tape by an independent researcher (IO), and were randomly divided into two groups (n=40) among an experienced endodontist (HSO) with 10 years of clinical practice, and a first-year postgraduate endodontic resident (AC) with approximately 50 prior endodontic procedures and the groups were further divided into four subgroups (n=10) based on retreatment systems: ProTaper Universal Retreatment (PTU-R), EndoArt Retreatment Gold (EAR-G), ROGIN Retreatment Files (R-RF) and FANTA AF Retreatment Files (FAF-R).

Retreatment protocols and procedure duration

Procedures were conducted simultaneously under identical environmental conditions. Each file was used only once (20 per system) following manufacturer-recommended settings (PTU-R: 500 rpm, 3 N·cm; EAR-G: 400 rpm, 2.5 N·cm; RR-F: 350 rpm, 2.5 N·cm; FAF-R: 300 rpm, 2 N·cm). Root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 5.25% NaOCl (Wizard, Rehber Kimya, Istanbul, Turkiye) following each file. Procedure time was defined as the duration required solely for gutta-percha removal using each retreatment system. The timing started with the insertion of the first file into the obturated canal and ended when the removal of the root canal filling was accomplished by the last file (26).

Classification of complications

Complications were documented with photographs (Canon EOS 600D, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) by a researcher (MG) and subsequently assessed through visual inspection by three independent, blinded evaluators (IO, TA, HSO) (Figure 1). Coronal third defects were classified as perforations, errors along canal curvature as ledges, and apical region deviations as apical transportation. In cases where the working length could not be reached due to complications or instrument separation, these events were documented. The affected blocks were then replaced with new samples, and the procedure was repeated using a new instrument. In these cases. The duration was recorded based on the replacement sample.

Complications observed during removal of root canal filling: A) Intact Block; B) Perforation; C) Ledge formation; D) Apical transportation.

Surface examination

Following the procedures, all instruments were sterilized and examined under a dental operating microscope (OPMI Pico, Zeiss, Germany) at ×1.6 and ×2.5 magnification to assess file distortions (Figure 2). File distortions including unwinding (defined as the loosening or straightening of the instrument's helical flutes under torsional stress), tip deformation (defined as the bending or distortion of the file tip) and instrument separation (defined as the fracture of the file during clinical use) were examined. To minimize bias, complication and file distortion assessments were performed by an independent researcher (BC) blinded to group allocation and not involved in the experimental procedures.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive data were presented as median (25th–75th percentiles) and frequency (percentage). Normality was assessed using histograms, Q-Q plots, the Shapiro-Wilk test, and skewness, revealing a non-normal distribution. Therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for continuous variables, while Pearson's Chi-square or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical comparisons. Confidence interval was set to 95% and p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 80 blocks were included in the study, including 40 additional blocks that were used to replace those in which patency could not be achieved due to complications or instrument separation. The median procedural time was 145 seconds for the endodontist and 120 seconds for the resident. Overall, 45 (24 by the resident; 21 by the endodontist) complications and 98 (58 the resident; 40 the endodontist) file distortions were recorded.

In the RR-F group, experienced endodontists also had significantly shorter procedure times (p < 0.001). For the PTU-R, FAF-R, and EAR-G systems, procedure times tended to be shorter for the endodontist, but the differences were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Complication rates, including perforation, ledge formation and apical transportation showed no consistent operator-dependent pattern across the systems (p = 0.390, p = 0.338, and p = 0.626, respectively).

Discussion

This study evaluated the procedure time, complications and file distortions with four Ni-Ti retreatment systems in curved canals based on operator experience. Although several studies have examined the clinical performance of retreatment systems (11, 15, 16), the influence of operator experience and instrument design has not been sufficiently researched. The curvature evaluated in this study represents a clinically meaningful scenario, providing a standardized yet challenging model for assessing instrument performance and the risk of complications (27).

Acrylic blocks were selected to ensure standardization. In cases where patency could not be achieved due to complications or instrument separation, the affected block was replaced with a new one. The procedure was then repeated using a new block, and the procedure time was recorded based on the replacement sample to prevent any loss of data for time measurement. Mechanical properties of Ni-Ti instruments, such as size, taper, and cross-sectional geometry, are known to affect flexibility and torsional resistance (28). For this reason, systems with different cross-sectional designs were selected. Additionally, each instrument, intended for a specific canal third, was evaluated individually.

The single-cone technique was preferred over lateral or warm vertical compaction because it is simpler, highly reproducible, and minimizes operator-related variability in a standardized experimental setting. A bioceramic sealer was selected instead of resin-based sealers, as it has gained popularity owing to its biocompatibility (29), low resorption rates (30) and suitability for use with the single-cone technique (31).

In this study, different evaluation methods were employed for complications and file distortions. Complications such as perforation, ledge formation, and apical transportation were assessed through standardized photographic documentation to enable blinded evaluation by multiple independent examiners. In contrast, file distortions were examined directly under a dental operating microscope, as this approach provides higher magnification and allows a more precise detection of subtle changes such as unwinding or tip deformation. The use of two different methods was therefore based on the need to ensure both reproducibility and accuracy for each parameter.

The first null hypothesis "There is no difference in procedural time with respect to either operator experience or the type of system used" was partly rejected as the endodontist demonstrated significantly shorter procedure times, likely reflecting their greater clinical experience whereas there was no statistically significant difference between the Ni-Ti retreatment systems.

On the other hand, the second null hypothesis stating that "There is no difference in the incidence of complications with respect to either operator experience or the type of system used"could not be rejected, as no statistically significant differences were observed between groups. Nevertheless, numerically lower complication rates were recorded in the endodontist group, with the PTU-R system exhibiting the lowest overall incidence. Moreover, both the FAF-R and PTU-R systems showed a lower frequency of ledge formation when used by the resident; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

The superior performance of PTU-R may be attributed to the convex triangular design, which reduces wall contact and friction, facilitating smoother canal navigation and lowering perforation risk (32). Furthermore, the active tip of the PTU-R D1 file enhances filling material removal, while the passive tips of PTU-R D2 and PTU-R D3 files allow controlled advancement, improving adaptation to canal anatomy and reducing canal deviations.

In contrast with our results, some studies reported higher error rates with PTU-R instruments (33, 34, 35). For instance, Çelik Ünal et al. (34) reported two perforations and five instrument fractures (3 D3, 2 D2) using PTU-R in curved canals, contradicting the present study, where no fractures and the fewest procedural errors were observed with PTU-R. These discrepancies may be due to methodological differences, including the lack of canal curvature standardization with natural teeth, the use of resin-based sealers, and chemical solvents such as eucalyptol in previous studies. Solvent use and continuous wave obturation have been shown to increase procedural errors, whereas the single-cone technique used here offers a simpler, more controlled approach (36, 37, 38, 39, 40). Notably, the procedure times in this study were shorter than those reported in similar studies on curved canals (34, 41), which may be attributed to the higher rotational speed used, facilitating gutta-percha plasticization and easier material removal (42). According to the manufacturer's instructions, PTU-R files can be operated at speeds between 300–700 rpm. In the present study, a speed of 500 rpm was employed, whereas Çelik et al. (34) used 300 rpm, which may also explain the differences in outcomes.

One noteworthy finding of this study was that the frequency of ledge formation ranged from 30% to 40% across the different systems. Although the difference between operators was not statistically significant, the FAF-R and PTU-R files resulted in numerically fewer ledges when used by the resident. Pertinent literature indicates that this complication is frequently encountered in curved root canals and is influenced by both the operator's level of experience (43) and the techniques employed (44). Among the file systems evaluated, the lowest incidence of ledge formation in both operator groups was observed with the FAF-R system, which may be associated with its cross-sectional design. While the other systems examined in this study featured triangular or convex triangular cross-sections, the FAF-R system had a diamond-shaped cross-section. Although diamond-shaped designs are generally considered more rigid, the present study demonstrated lower ledge formation with this geometry compared to triangular and convex triangular designs. This outcome may be explained by the improved centering ability and the balanced distribution of cutting forces provided by the four cutting edges of the diamond design, which could reduce canal deviation and prevent excessive dentin removal from one side of the canal wall. Additionally, the larger surface contact area may help decrease the screw-in effect, thereby minimizing procedural errors such as ledge formation. In the literature, the tip design of instruments has been reported to influence ledge formation, with active cutting tips posing a higher risk compared to non-cutting modified tips (45). In the present study, the PTU-R, EAR-G, and FAF-R file systems included instruments with active tips for coronal use, whereas the instruments intended for the middle and apical thirds featured non-cutting tip designs (information regarding RR-F is not available). However, since procedural complications were assessed only after completion of the retreatment procedure, it was not possible to determine which specific instrument was responsible for ledge formation. Therefore, the potential impact of tip design was not considered in this study. Furthermore, previous studies have emphasized that ledge formation is significantly more common among inexperienced operators (43, 46). In the present study, although the numerical incidence of ledge formation was higher for the resident, the difference did not reach statistical significance.

The results of the current study have failed to reject the third null hypothesis "there is no difference in file distortion with respect to either operator experience or the type of system used" because unwinding, tip deformation, and fracture, were rare and differ significantly between neither operators nor Ni-Ti retreatment systems. Tip deformation rates were higher in number for instruments used by the resident, particularly in third instruments working in the apical region. Unwinding occurred with similar frequency in both groups but was more pronounced in the resident's third instruments. These findings likely reflect the resident's limited clinical experience, as maintaining the correct canal angulation and applying controlled force are advanced skills that require practice and precision. Excessive force, prolonged instrumentation, and misjudgment of canal curvature, especially in narrow and curved canals, increase deformation risk. Instrument fracture occurred in only one case (resident, EAR-G D3), likely related to the combination of large taper and small canal curvature radius, both recognized fracture risk factors (47, 48). Although instruments with greater taper are thicker, they exhibit reduced flexibility and generate higher stress concentrations in curved canals, thereby predisposing them to fracture (23). Notably, the one and the only fracture occurred exclusively in the third instrument (D3) of the EAR-G system. Considering that all third instruments shared the same taper, the fracture observed exclusively in the D3 of the EAR-G system cannot be solely attributed to geometrical factors. Given that EAR-G instruments are manufactured from CM-wire, which is known for its superior fatigue resistance and enhanced flexibility in curved canals, the observed failure may instead reflect isolated factors such as localized structural defects, overuse, or operator-induced stress concentration rather than intrinsic alloy or manufacturing limitations. This finding underscores the need for heightened caution when employing EAR-G instruments in anatomically challenging canals, particularly those with pronounced curvature or limited diameter.

Differences in elasticity and thermal conductivity between resin and natural dentin could also have influenced file performance. Although acrylic blocks offer standardization, they do not fully replicate the structural properties of dentin. Despite these limitations, strict adherence to standardized protocols and the use of new instruments for each procedure enhanced reproducibility. Additionally, no formal calibration was performed among the evaluators, which should be considered as a limitation of this study. Due to the limitations of this study, future research using larger sample sizes, natural teeth, and clinical settings is recommended to validate these findings and clarify the role of operator experience in retreatment outcomes.

Conclusion

Among the systems evaluated in this study, PTU-R was found to be generally safer, while FAF-R instruments demonstrated the lowest incidence of ledge formation and EAR-G instruments exhibited the highest. Clinically, these findings emphasize that retreatment of curved canals obturated with gutta-percha and a bioceramic sealer using a single-cone technique can be performed safely and predictably through appropriate file system selection and adequate operator training.
