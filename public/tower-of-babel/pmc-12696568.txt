Autonomy versus paternalism: what is the physician’s responsibility in a life-threatening situation?
Authors: Sunil Kumar Garg, Ahmed Altigany Fadlallah
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

The most appropriate therapeutic approach to a life-threatening illness can be challenging to implement as well as raising ethical concerns when encountering the resistance of substitute decision-makers. An elderly man presented to our hospital with a massive pulmonary embolism. In view of absolute contraindications to systemic thrombolysis, endovascular thrombectomy was recommended. However, this treatment plan was refused by the patient’s son even after repeated explanation. In a desperate attempt to save his life during rapid deterioration when his blood pressure reached below 50 mm Hg, a low dose of thrombolytics given. Although complications and death due to thrombolytics was a high likelihood, the patient survived without complications. Since systemic thrombolysis was not the preferred therapy in view of the absolute contraindications and was only given as a life-saving measure, this situation created ethical concerns. It raised the question of what is more important in a life-threatening situation like this, a paternalistic approach by physicians, or the autonomy of patients/relatives.

Autonomy is an ethical principle emphasizing that patients are the final authority to decide what is done with their bodies and they should be actively involved while various therapeutic options are discussed and finalized. Essentially, without the consent of patients, physicians cannot implement any intervention or treatment. [1]. Paternalism means intervention done or treatment carried out by physicians without patients’ prior approval but keeping in mind the best interest of patients. It plays a fundamental role in ethical decision-making specifically in life-threatening situations. It serves as a balancing factor alongside core ethical principles such as beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, and justice. Paternalism also reflects the physician’s ethical duty to provide guidance and maintain professional responsibility, rather than withholding expertise or adopting a passive stance and accepting patients’ wishes even when they are detrimental [2,3].

In our case, the optimal treatment—endovascular thrombectomy—was declined by relatives due to lack of familiarity with this advanced treatment, despite thorough explanations and expert guidance. We could not adopt a paternalistic approach and instead resorted to judicious and individualized use of systemic thrombolytics at a very low dose when patient rapidly deteriorated. We administered thrombolytic therapy not because of the lack of consent for endovascular embolectomy, but as a life-saving measure.

A paternalistic approach plays a critical role in patient care. Physicians are ethically bound to prioritize beneficence, ensuring actions are taken in the best interest of the patient. In life-threatening situations, where the patient lacks decision-making capacity (as in our case), paternalism becomes essential to uphold the principle of beneficence. During delivery of healthcare, each ethical principle serves as a prima facie obligation that must be upheld unless it conflicts with another principle in a specific situation. When such conflicts arise, the physician must carefully assess competing obligations, weighing their importance within the given context. [2]. In our case, the principle of beneficence, means do what is best for patient should have taken precedence over autonomy through the paternalistic approach of the physician, as the patient’s life was in danger because of massive pulmonary embolism and the presence of absolute contraindications to systemic thrombolytics, and urgent action was required to ensure his survival, but we could not adopt this approach.

Moreover, the physician’s knowledge, honed through years of education and experience, encompasses complexities and nuances that cannot be fully conveyed to patients or relatives in a dynamic and rapidly worsening situation. Knowledge-based value judgements are integral to ensuring high-quality medical care. As such, paternalism is inherently embedded in the physician’s role and is a critical element in the decision-making process, particularly in situations requiring complex clinical judgements in a life-threatening situation where time is very limited and urgent decisions and interventions are required to save a life [2,3].

Additionally, autonomy and informed consent are also not without their own shortcomings, including inherent concerns regarding the informedness gap, cognitive capacity, and underestimation or overestimation of treatment risks [4]. It is possible that patients are unable to understand nuances of medical details given to them during discussions by their physicians. This is especially true when discussions are not in their language, do not take consideration of their level of understanding and discuss complicated medical treatments & interventions. During such situations, patients may feel inundated by the given details and not able to make truly informed decisions [4]. The limited interaction time between patients and physicians is an important underlying reason for the informedness gap reported in approximately 1 of every 3 patients [5]. Another important consideration that limits the utility of autonomy and informed consent is overestimation or underestimation of inherent risks in treatment . This is regarding success or complications of a treatment or procedure that patient may not understand exactly hence, underestimate the risks of adverse outcomes, complications or failures. Contrary to this, patients may overestimate the treatment effects due to anxiety and fear [4]. Anxiety or fear may cause increased perception of risk hence, overestimating the treatment effects that may lead to refusal of beneficial treatments contrary to objective medical evidence [4]. Thus, autonomy and informed consent have their own limitations. Even if patients are fully aware and knowledgeable with regards to their health, it is not necessary that they will be correct with their medical decisions . In a real sense, these limitations may have detrimental effects when patients have the power of autonomy to make medical choices, especially in life-threatening situations [4].

Balancing the principles of patient autonomy and beneficence can be challenging ethically, and needs mindful deliberation of likely harms and benefits of differing available options. Shared decision making is one such balancing act in which the patient and physician reach to a unanimous medical decision together. While patients contribute to their own healthcare goals and their personal values, the physicians provide medical knowledge, training, experience and judgement. Based on these parameters, each therapeutic option is evaluated.

We believe there are three clear choices: autonomy, shared decision-making, and paternalism. When we move between stable patients in an outpatient setting to sick patients on general floor/ward and then to critically ill patients in an intensive care unit or emergency department, to some extent, we move from autonomy to shared decision-making to paternalism. If healthcare seeks to achieve positive health outcomes, patient education and effective communication are ongoing needs to address the shortcomings of autonomy.

Thus, when physicians encounter a life-threatening situation, they should respond more paternalistically, bearing the professional responsibility to act in the patient’s best interest.
