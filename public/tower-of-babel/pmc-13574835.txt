The posterior ulna medial approach to the elbow: a cadaveric anatomical study of exposure of the coronoid and posteromedial collateral ligament
Authors: Feras Qawasmi, Mustafa Yassin, Adam C Watts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to describe the posterior ulna medial approach (PUMA) that permits exposure and repair of the anteromedial coronoid facet and posterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament (pMCL) in posteromedial rotatory instability (PMRI). Varus PMRI of the elbow disrupts key stabilizers, the anteromedial coronoid facet, the lateral ligament complex, and the posterior band of the medial collateral ligament, often requiring surgical stabilization. Although the pMCL is pivotal for stability, a reproducible posterior approach allowing for its direct repair has not been well described.MethodsSix freshly unfrozen upper limbs were dissected using the PUMA. We assessed the exposure, involvement with the ulnar nerve, and feasibility of pMCL repair. Surgeons completed online pre- and post-cadaveric questionnaires assessing the approach’s utility.ResultsThe PUMA consistently exposed the pMCL without detaching the primary origin of the flexor pronator, violating the anterior MCL, or requiring transposition of the ulnar nerve. It enabled simulated repair of the pMCL and, when required, the medial collateral ligament, and fixation of the anteromedial coronoid. Neurovascular structures remained protected throughout the procedure.ConclusionsThe PUMA offers anatomical access to the pMCL and adjacent medial elbow structures in a cadaveric setting, demonstrating feasibility for repair in selected PMRI patterns.Level of evidenceAnatomical study.

INTRODUCTION

Elbow dislocations present a surgical challenge, particularly when they involve complex instability, such as varus posteromedial rotatory instability (PMRI). This pattern is classified as a type A injury in the Wrightington classification [1], and typically includes fractures of the anteromedial facet coronoid and disruption of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament. Cadaveric studies have also highlighted the role of the posterolateral capsule (Osborne–Cotterill lesions) in elbow stability, which is relevant when considering combined medial-lateral injuries [2]. Recent biomechanical and clinical data further suggest that the posterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament (pMCL) plays a crucial role in this type of instability, particularly in resisting varus posteromedial rotational forces [1,3]. However, multiple biomechanical studies have demonstrated that the anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament (aMCL) is the primary restraint to valgus stress across most functional positions of the elbow, with the pMCL playing a secondary, position-dependent role [4]. Direct exposure and repair of the pMCL may therefore be advantageous in selected cases, such as those with confirmed posteromedial capsuloligamentous disruption or large posteromedial coronoid fragments that require posterior access for fixation, or when posterior capsular reconstruction is indicated [5]. Despite its biomechanical importance, the pMCL is not routinely addressed in surgical practice due to the difficulty of accessing it through existing surgical approaches. Anteromedial coronoid fixation may be accessed via a number of medial approaches [6]. Smith et al. [7] described a medial approach to muscle-splitting to access the aMCL and ulnar collateral ligament complex without detaching the common flexor origin. More recently, the extended medial elbow approach has been described as a modification that enhances anterior visualization of the anterior coronoid by extending access both proximally and anteriorly [8]. However, no comparable posterior extension to reach the pMCL and posteromedial capsule has been reported. This may result in persistent PMRI and overload the anteromedial facet of the coronoid. The aim of this study is to describe the posterior ulna medial approach (PUMA) to the elbow and assess its utility as reported by orthopedic surgeons using it for the first time in cadaveric elbows. This technique builds upon the Smith medial approach, extending both posteriorly and proximally, avoiding injury to the ulnar nerve, and enhancing visualization of the pMCL.

METHODS

This cadaveric anatomical study was conducted at Wrightington Hospital, United Kingdom, under Human Tissue Authority (HTA) anatomy licence no. 12487, using donated cadaveric upper limbs in accordance with the institution's policies on anatomical specimens. All specimens were obtained via an anatomical donation program and a commercial supplier operating with appropriate ethical approval for anatomical donation and research use, and were handled in accordance with the Human Tissue Act 2004 and institutional HTA governance. According to the institutional policy governing the use of donated cadaveric specimens for anatomical research and education, formal ethics committee approval was not required for this study.

Specimen Preparation

Six freshly unfrozen cadaveric upper limbs were used for anatomical dissection. All specimens were obtained via an anatomical donation program and a commercial supplier operating with appropriate ethical approval. Specimens with prior trauma, deformity, or surgical scars around the elbow were excluded. Each elbow was thawed for 24 hours at room temperature. Standard anatomical landmarks were identified and marked, including the medial epicondyle, olecranon, radial styloid, and ulnar nerve course. Elbows were positioned at approximately 90° of flexion with the forearm in supination.

Surgical Technique: Posterior Ulna Medial Approach

A curvilinear skin incision was made approximately 4 cm proximal and posterior to the medial epicondyle, extending distally for approximately 10 cm and curving gently anteriorly and laterally toward the radial styloid (Fig. 1). Skin and subcutaneous flaps were elevated anteriorly and posteriorly, with care taken to identify and preserve the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve, which is typically encountered distal to the medial epicondyle (Fig. 2).

Step 1: ulnar nerve identification and protection

The ulnar nerve was identified proximally, just medial to the triceps fascia, and traced distally into the cubital tunnel. The Osborne ligament was either preserved, to maintain stability of the ulnar nerve, or released depending on exposure needs. The fascia overlying the flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) was incised, the nerve was dissected between the two heads of the FCU, and the deep fascia over the FCU (Osborne's fascia) was divided (Fig. 3). The ulnar nerve was protected in situ and not routinely transposed. Although posterior extension of the approach was also performed, routine anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve was not required, as adequate posterior exposure was achieved without excessive tension or traction on the nerve. In clinical practice, anterior transposition may be considered on a case-by-case basis, particularly in the presence of nerve tethering or symptomatic ulnar neuritis.

Step 2: Smith interval access

The interval between the FCU and palmaris longus was developed using muscle-splitting and preserving the origin of the flexor pronator. Distally, fascial condensation marked this internervous plane between the ulnar and median nerves (Figs. 4 and 5). Palpation of the sublime tubercle guided deeper exposure.

Step 3: joint exposure

A blade was placed on the flat surface of the ulna, just anterior to the sublime tubercle, allowing access to the anteromedial facet while preserving the aMCL (Fig. 6). If coronoid fixation was necessary, partial release of the brachialis was performed to allow placement of a buttress plate.

Step 4: posterior extension and pMCL exposure

The FCU was released from its humeral origin to permit posterior extension of the approach and expose the pMCL (Fig. 7). The ulnar nerve was left in place, and careful sharp dissection was performed to access the ligament without the need for nerve transposition (Fig. 8). In acute trauma this would expose the pMCL pathology as, in the senior author’s experience, the majority of pMCL injuries are avulsions from the humerus. In this cadaveric study, simulated injuries were created by sharply releasing the pMCL from its humeral origin. This represents a simplified injury model and may not fully reproduce the full spectrum of clinical pMCL injury patterns. Ligament repair was performed using a transosseous suture, but suture anchors can also be used (Fig. 9).

Clinical Indications for PUMA Approach

The PUMA approach is indicated for isolated Z-shaped tears of the MCL, characterized by detachment of the anterior band from the sublime tubercle and the posterior band from the humerus (Fig. 10). This pattern may be encountered in individuals whose sporting activities require a highly stable medial elbow (e.g., baseball pitchers and overhead throwers as well as judoka) and trauma settings. The PUMA provides access to the sublime tubercle for repair of the aMCL and to the inferior aspect of the medial epicondyle for repair of the pMCL.

The PUMA may also be indicated in patients with acute Wrightington type A injuries involving an anteromedial facet coronoid fracture (a posteromedial elbow fracture-dislocation) associated with disruption of soft tissue leading to joint subluxation. In these cases, the aMCL may remain intact, whereas a sleeve avulsion of the lateral ligament complex and the pMCL from the humerus is often present. The PUMA allows access for fixation of the anteromedial coronoid fracture and repair of the pMCL to the humerus. A separate lateral approach is typically required for repair of the lateral ligament complex.

This approach is generally not indicated for isolated injuries of the aMCL without posteromedial involvement, for which standard medial approaches are typically sufficient. As this study is based on anatomical observations of cadavers, these indications should be interpreted as suggested guidance rather than definitive clinical recommendations, and further clinical studies are required to validate their clinical applicability.

Surgeon Survey

Surgeons participating in a cadaveric training course were asked to complete an online survey using Google Forms, reporting their experience with medial approaches to the elbow and fixing the pMCL. The survey evaluated five domains: quality of exposure, reproducibility, ulnar nerve protection, ease of pMCL fixation, and overall utility of the approach. Each domain was scored using a 5-point Likert scale (1=very poor, 2=poor, 3=neither poor nor good, 4=good, 5=very good). The responses were analyzed descriptively and reported as counts and percentages.

RESULTS

Participants and Survey Response

Survey responses were collected before and after the cadaveric course. Fourteen surgeons, consisting primarily of early-career surgeons, including trainees (8/14, 57.1%), fellows (4/14, 28.6%), and early consultants (2/14, 14.3%), participated. Before the course, 71.4% (10/14) expressed concerns or uncertainty regarding use of a medial approach.

Technical Feedback

All participants (14/14, 100%) rated coronoid access as “good” or “very good”. Reproducibility of the approach was rated as “good” or “very good” by 12 of 14 participants (86%). Visualization of the pMCL was rated as “very good” by 8 of 14 (57%) and as “good” by 4 of 14 (29%). Ulnar nerve protection was rated as “good” or “very good” by 12 of 14 participants (86%). Ease of coronoid fixation was rated as “good” by 12 of 14 (86%), and fixation of the pMCL was rated as “good” or “very good” by 12 of 14 (86%). When compared with other medial approaches for PMRI, the PUMA was rated as “very good” by 12/14 participants (86%) and as “good” by 2 of 14 (14%). All ratings are subjective surgeon assessments based on a 5-point Likert scale, as defined in the Methods section.

DISCUSSION

PMRI of the elbow is a challenging injury pattern characterized by anteromedial coronoid fractures, disruption of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament, and injury to the MCL. Prior biomechanical and clinical studies emphasize that the aMCL is the primary stabilizer against valgus stress in many functional elbow positions [4]. However, the pMCL and the posteromedial capsule may contribute additional stability in specific injury patterns, particularly in PMRI. Several biomechanical studies have demonstrated the importance of pMCL in resisting varus and posteromedial forces [1,3]. To date, no standard surgical approach has been described that reliably provides exposure to fix or repair all the components of the PMRI, the coronoid fracture, aMCL, and direct posterior access to the pMCL for repair.

In this anatomical feasibility study, we describe the PUMA to the elbow that enables feasible and direct anatomical exposure of the aMCL, pMCL, posteromedial capsule, and anteromedial coronoid, in cadavers, without detaching the major elements of the common origin of the flexor. This builds upon the classical Smith medial interval [7], and extends posteriorly in a manner conceptually similar to the extended medial elbow approach [8]. In contrast to existing medial approaches, PUMA is an extensile approach that specifically provides access to posteromedial structures, which may be relevant in selected instability patterns.

The pMCL contributes to elbow stability by limiting posterior rotation of the olecranon away from the trochlea, particularly while the arm is pronated [9]. However, several biomechanical studies have demonstrated that the aMCL functions as the primary restraint to valgus stress throughout most functional positions of the elbow, whereas the pMCL plays a secondary, position-dependent role [4,9]. Although this structure is not routinely addressed in standard elbow instability surgery, it may become clinically relevant in selected cases of PMRI, particularly when instability persists despite appropriate management of the bony and major ligamentous components of the injury.

The PUMA approach may be particularly useful in complex elbow dislocations in which coronoid involvement is accompanied by medial and lateral ligamentous injury, such as seen in a Wrightington type A, or PMRI as described by O’Driscoll. Accordingly, we propose using the PUMA primarily as an extensile posteromedial exposure in selected clinical scenarios rather than as a routine approach for isolated aMCL injuries in which standard medial approaches are usually sufficient. This extensile approach enables the surgeon to address the anterior and posterior components of the MCL and simultaneously fix associated coronoid fractures through the same exposure.

The described approach permits direct visualization and repair of both the pMCL and aMCL using standard instrumentation and suture techniques, while also allowing fixation of the coronoid fracture when indicated, using either screws alone or screws with a plate. The PUMA approach represents a complementary option to existing medial and lateral approaches rather than a replacement. It may address a previously limited surgical corridor to injuries of the posteromedial elbow, particularly in light of emerging evidence supporting the role of pMCL repair in PMRI.

Although the technique aims to preserve the flexor-pronator mass, limited release of the FCU from its humeral origin is required to achieve posterior visualization. This maneuver increases surgical exposure but may involve a more invasive dissection compared with standard medial approaches. At present, no clinical or biomechanical data are available to determine whether this affects postoperative pain, rehabilitation, or functional recovery.

The post-course survey results suggest that the PUMA is both teachable and well accepted by early-career surgeons. Participants reported a marked increase in confidence among such physicians of successful medial elbow exposure following the course, even among those with limited prior experience. However, these findings are based on subjective assessments using an unvalidated survey instrument and should be interpreted with caution.

Specific anatomical objectives such as visualization of the pMCL and protection of the ulnar nerve were generally achieved to a high degree of confidence. More than 85% of participants rated coronoid and posterior MCL fixation as “good” or “very good.” While these findings support the technical feasibility of the approach, they do not establish clinical efficacy or superiority over existing techniques.

Limitations

The sample size was small and limited to cadaveric specimens, and outcome measures were primarily subjective. Because this study used cadavers, the clinical consequences of the partial FCU release, including its potential effect on rehabilitation progression or postoperative function, are unknown. In addition, the findings are limited to anatomical relationships, and we did not determine the true clinical incidence of ulnar nerve injury or postoperative neuritis associated with the PUMA approach. Further biomechanical and clinical studies are required to evaluate the stability, complication rates, and functional outcomes associated with this approach.

CONCLUSIONS

The PUMA allows feasible, direct anatomical exposure of the pMCL, posteromedial capsule, and coronoid process in a cadaveric setting. This technique may serve as an effective surgical option for selected cases of PMRI and coronoid fractures; however, further clinical validation is required to determine its clinical utility.
