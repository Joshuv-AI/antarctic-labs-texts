Metabolomic profiling of HepG2/C3A liver-on-chip model exposed to oleic acid, palmitic acid and oleic/palmitic acids mixture
Authors: Lisa Morisseau, Françoise Gilard, Bertrand Gakière, Cécile Legallais, Yasuyuki Sakai, Eric Leclerc, Rachid Jellali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HepG2/C3a were cultured in microfluidic biochip and exposed to free fatty acids.•Cells response to fatty acids exposure was investigated by metabolomics analysis.•Oleate treatment induced moderate perturbation, with change in lipid secretion.•Exposure to palmitate led to modulation of TCA cycle and oxidative stress metabolism.•Nitrogen metabolism perturbation was a common signature of FAs and their mixture.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has become a global health concern due to its correlation with the obesity pandemic and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM, Targher et al., 2024). Currently, MASLD affects more than one-third of the world general population, with an alarming prevalence of 56% among people with T2DM and approximately 80% among obese individuals (Wong et al., 2023). This disease encompasses a range of conditions, starting with benign steatosis characterized by triglyceride (TG) accumulation in liver cells, and can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH, old terminology: non-alcoholic steatohepatitis NASH), leading to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC, Sandireddy et al., 2024). Additionally, MASLD is linked to extra-hepatic complications like cardiovascular diseases, which are the leading cause of mortality in MASH and fibrotic patients (Drew, 2017; Sandireddy et al., 2024).

The progression of MASLD is a complex process involving several stages (Benedict and Zhang, 2017; Drew, 2017). It begins with the accumulation of triglycerides in hepatocytes, known as steatosis or lipotoxicity, resulting from disrupted systemic energy balance in obesity. Hepatocytes with accumulated triglycerides become more vulnerable to secondary insults like oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to apoptosis or necrosis. The accumulation of free fatty acids (FFAs) from dietary products is one of the potential sources of the onset of the steatosis. FFAs have a significant impact on human health, including obesity and MASLD (Kien et al., 2014; Palomer et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2020). Despite increasing efforts to study MASLD, the mechanisms involved in MASLD development and progression are still only partially understood, and there is only a limited numbers of available treatments for the disease. Among them, only late-stage treatments, for MASH and fibrosis patients, with resmetirom (a thyroid hormone β-receptor agonist) and semaglutide (a glucagon-like-peptide-1 receptor agonist) are now approved by the Food and Drug Administration for patients with MASH (Abreu et al., 2026). Therefore, the development of MASLD relevant models is necessary for disease understanding and treatment discovery.

In recent years, liver-on-chip technology has emerged as one of the most promising in vitro approaches, faithfully mimicking the architecture and physiological microenvironment of the human liver and providing a highly relevant and scalable model for studying disease mechanisms and drug responses (Lee et al., 2021; Messelmani et al., 2022a). Indeed, by recreating the liver's cellular complexity and interactions with other tissues, liver-on-chip devices offer a more accurate representation of human liver physiology, enabling the investigation of cell-cell crosstalk, nutrient transport, and metabolic regulation under dynamic flow conditions (Ramos et al., 2021; Messelmani et al., 2022a). Moreover, these microsystems can be engineered to incorporate various cell types, such as hepatocytes, stellate cells, Kupffer cells, and endothelial cells, which collectively contribute to understand the intricate pathogenesis of MASLD (Kostrzewski et al., 2019; Freag et al., 2020). Furthermore, recent liver on chip demonstrated the ability of the technology to recapitulate MASLD features similarly in biochip and mice model (He et al., 2026). In this frame of idea, our team has made significant advancements in liver-on-chip technology, enabling us to gain valuable insights into liver metabolism (Jellali et al., 2016a), identify in vitro biomarkers, and predict toxicology outcomes (Choucha Snouber et al., 2013; Jellali et al., 2018; 2021).

Notably, we have previously validated the ability to recapitulate steatosis using HepG2/C3A (Morisseau et al., 2026) in a liver-on-chip models. This study investigates the differential and synergistic lipotoxic effects of oleic acid (OA), palmitic acid (PA), and their mixture on 3D HepG2/C3A liver cells using organ-on-chip technology. Oil Red O staining revealed significant intracellular lipid accumulation in OA-treated cells, which was absent in PA-treated cells and reduced in OA/PA co-exposure, indicating OA’s role in potential promoting of steatosis. ROS production increased under OA, PA, and OA/PA treatments, with OA and OA/PA inducing stronger oxidative stress than PA alone, suggesting both triglyceride (TG)-dependent (OA) and TG-independent (PA) mechanisms. Furthermore, PCR data showed that PA downregulated key lipid and glucose metabolism genes (GLUT2, INSRA, FASN, SREBP1) after 7 days, while OA/PA co-treatment partially restored these levels, highlighting OA’s mitigating effect on PA-induced metabolic perturbations. Additionally, OA supported long-term cell proliferation, whereas PA and OA/PA did not, underscoring distinct cellular responses to these fatty acids. Overall, the study emphasizes the complex interplay between fatty acids, oxidative stress, lipid accumulation, and gene expression in MASLD progression. To go beyond this analysis, we complete our investigation in the present study with a metabolomic profiling.

In fact, in addition to MASLD relevant models, it is imperative to identify and develop powerful analytical tools to characterize and knowledge the disease, and explore the potential biomarkers for diagnosis and risk identification. In the last years, omics technologies have emerged as a promising tool to investigate and elucidate the mechanisms and pathways involved in chemical-induced toxicity and pathologies development, including MASLD and MASH (Canzler et al., 2020; Perakakis et al., 2020; Bourganou et al., 2025). Among omics, metabolomics is a cutting-edge field that focuses on the comprehensive study of small molecules (< 1500 Da), known as metabolites, within biological systems (Canzler et al., 2020). Using analytical techniques such as mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, metabolomics can be employed to identify and quantify a wide range of metabolites present in biological samples, including blood, urine, cell culture medium and tissues (Beyoğlu and Idle, 2020). It can reveal metabolic perturbations associated with diseases, providing valuable insights about their mechanisms and progression, but also potential therapeutic targets (Qiu et al., 2023). Metabolomics has been used in various studies to understand the pathways and mechanisms associated with MASLD/MASH, as well as to identify the diseases biomarkers (Beyoğlu & Idle; Perakakis et al., 2020; Bourganou et al., 2025).

Previously, we demonstrated the potential of coupling organ-on-chip technology with metabolomics to identify the effects of various molecules on HepG2/C3A (Choucha Snouber et al., 2013; Messelmani et al., 2023), rat hepatocytes (Jellali et al., 2018) and rat islets of Langerhans (Essaouiba et al., 2022). In this study, we aim to investigate the perturbation of the metabolomic profile due to the effects of FFAs, using our HepG2/C3A-based liver-on-chip. For that purpose, we have selected the oleic acid (OA), palmitic acid (PA), and a mixture of OA and PA (OA/PA) following our previous analysis (Morisseau et al., 2026). Notably, it has been observed that OA co-exposure partially counterbalances PA toxicity in various hepatic cells, including HepG2/C3A in 2D Petri cultures (Ricchi et al., 2009; Zeng et al., 2020). We previously confirmed that those FFAs exposures led to specific hepatic perturbation with ROS production in OA, PA and OA/PA treatments, but only an important lipid accumulation in OA treated condition (Morisseau et al., 2026). We aim now to characterize the metabolic perturbations resulting from those FFAs exposures in order to extract the subsequent pathways perturbations and potential in vitro FFA exposure biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Cells and reagents

HepG2/C3A cells, derived from hepatocellular carcinoma, were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-10741). The cells were routinely cultured in 75 cm² flask (Falcon) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The culture medium was renewed every 2 days and the cells were passaged weekly when reaching 80–90% confluence. Minimal essential Medium (MEM) with phenol red (Life Technologies) was used for HepG2/C3A culture. It was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies), 1 mM hydroxy‑ethylpiperazine-N-2-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, Gibco), 2 mM l-glutamine (Pan Biotech), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (Pan Biotech), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Pan Biotech), and 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Pan Biotech).

Oleic acid (OA), palmitic acid (PA) sodium salt and fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA-FAF) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. The solutions stock of OA (500 mM) and PA (100 mM) were prepared in absolute ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and in a mixture of absolute ethanol and milli-Q water (50:50), respectively. The BSA-FFA stock solution (10%) was prepared in sterile milli-Q water.

Microfluidic biochip and perfusion platform

The biochips used for liver cell cultures were made from polydimethylsiloxane using a replica molding process. The detailed designs (Fig. S1A) and manufacturing process have been reported in our previous works (Jellali et al., 2016b; Messelmani et al., 2022b). These biochips consisted of two layers of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184 kit, Dow Corning), which were permanently sealed together using reactive air plasma treatment (1 min at 30 W, Harrick Scientific).

To ensure culture medium perfusion, we used our Integrated Dynamic Cell Cultures in Microsystems (IDCCM) device (Fig. S1B, Jellali et al., 2016b). This platform, made of polycarbonate, ensured precise flow control across 12 biochips in parallel, each operating within its own closed loop. The biochips were interconnected between two wells (with a volume of 2 mL per well) at the bottom of the IDCCM device through a series of connectors. For perfusion, a peristaltic pump was connected to the cover of the IDCCM device via silicone and Teflon tubing. All components (biochip, IDDCM platform and tubing) were sterilized by autoclave prior to use.

Culture in biochip and FFAs treatment

After detachment from the T75 flask using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco), HepG2/C3A cells were seeded (75,000 cells/cm2) into the biochips previously coated with collagen (300 µg/mL rat tail type 1 collagen, Corning). The seeded biochips were incubated in static condition overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% of CO2. After adhesion step, the biochips were connected to the IDCCM platform and the culture medium was added (2 mL per well, 4 mL per biochip). The IDCCM was then connected to a peristaltic pump through the silicone/Teflon tubing and the perfusion started at 15 µL/min. The entire system was kept in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. As shown in Fig. 1A, after 4 days of dynamic culture, the FFAs treatments were initiated. The culture medium was prepared with different concentrations of FFAs, using the previously prepared stock solutions: 0.66 mM OA (OA group), 0.33 mM PA (PA group), or combination of 0.66 mM OA/0.33 mM PA (OA/PA group). To improve FFAs solubility, BSA-FFA was added to the mixture in a ratio of 1 (FFAs stock solution): 20 (BSA-FFA stock solution). The control group (CT group) correspond to cells treated with the same amount of lipid solvent (BSA and ethanol) used in the OA, PA and OA + PA group. Metabolomics analysis

The metabolomics analyses were performed on the culture media collected along the experiments for the CT, OA, PA and OA/PA treated conditions. These culture media were stored at −80 °C until use. For each culture condition, 6 samples from 3 independent experiments were used for 2 days of exposures, 5 samples from 3 independent experiments were used for 4 days of exposures and 5 samples from 3 independent experiments were used for 7 days of exposures. All steps of metabolomics analysis were adapted from the original protocol described by Fiehn et al. (2006). A volume of 400 µL of culture medium was mixed with 800 µL of frozen (−20 °C) water: acetonitrile: isopropanol (2:3:3) solution containing 4 mg/L of ribitol and 2.75 mg/L of α-aminobutyric acid (αABA). The mixture was extracted for 10 min at 4 °C with shaking at 1500 rpm in an Eppendorf thermomixer. Insoluble materials were removed through two centrifugation steps at 14,000 rpm for 15 min. Then, 100 µL were collected and 10 μL of myristic acid d27 (30 pg/mL) was added as an internal standard for retention time locking. The samples were then dried for 4 h at 35 °C in a speed-vac system and stored at −80 °C.

Before injection into the gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer (GC–MS), the samples were taken out of −80 °C, warmed 15 min and speed-vac dried again for 2 h at 35 °C. Then, 10 µL of a methoxyamine solution in pyridine (20 mg/mL) were added. After 90 min at 30 °C under continuous shaking, 90 µL of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyl-trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA, Thermo Scientific) were added and the reaction continued for 30 min at 37 °C. After cooling, 100 µL of the resulting solution was transferred to an Agilent vial for injection.

The GC–MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled to an Agilent 5977A quadrupole mass spectrometer (injection of 1 μl of sample splitless mode). The column used was a DB-5 ms capillary column (30 m, 0.25 mm i.d., and 0.25 μm thickness, Agilent Technologies). For quantification of saturated compounds, a split mode with a ratio of 1:30 was used for injection. The oven temperature ramp ranged from 60 °C for 1 min, then increased at a rate of 10 °C/min to 325 °C for 10 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas with a constant flow of 1.1 mL/min. The temperatures for the injector, transfer line, source, and quadrupole were set at 250 °C, 290 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. The mass spectrometer was turned on after a solvent delay of 5.90 min, and data was collected in the range of 50–600 u. External retention index calibration was performed using a fatty acid methyl ester mix (C8, C9, C10, C12, C14, C16, C18, C20, C22, C24, C26, C28, C30).

Raw data files obtained from Agilent were analyzed using AMDIS software (www.amdis.net). Metabolite identifications were conducted using the Agilent Fiehn GC/MS Metabolomics RTL Library (version June 2008). Peak areas were determined using Agilent Masshunter Quantitative Analysis in both splitless and split modes. To ensure accuracy, automated peak integration was verified manually for each compound, and peak areas were normalized to ribitol. Metabolite contents were expressed in arbitrary units, representing semi-quantitative determinations. Finally, the metabolites content in the culture medium samples were normalised by the metabolite’s content detected in the blank culture medium (medium not exposed to the cells).

Metabolomics statistical analysis

The metabolomic multivariate data analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (Pang et al., 2024). The data were auto-scaled (mean-centered and divided by the standard deviation of each metabolite). Supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA, were applied to get the maximum separation between control and treated groups, and to explore the variables that contributed to this separation. The quality of PLS-DA model was evaluated by the R2Y (fitting degree) and Q2 (prediction parameter) values. To determine the best discriminating metabolites, the variable importance in the projection value (importance of each variable to the whole model, VIP > 1), the adjusted p_value < 0.05 (t-test for comparison of 2 groups or ANOVA for comparison of more than two groups) and fold change (FC > 1.2) were used. Heatmap and hierarchical clustering were performed using the normalized data (auto-scale features, Euclidean distance measure, and ward clustering method on the metabolites identified). Finally, pathway enrichment analysis was performed with MetaboAnalyst using the selected significant metabolites.

At day 2, only four metabolites were differentially expressed (Fig. 3B). Indeed, we noticed a consumption of oleic acid for both OA and OA/PA conditions when compared to CT condition. Then, 13 differentially expressed metabolites were extracted after 4 days of exposure. Furthermore, we noticed the start of a switch in the metabolic signature (Fig. 3D). The CT condition was characterized by the production of proline, trans-4‑hydroxy-l-proline, tyrosine and malic acid, and the consumption of phosphoric acid. The OA condition also showed a production of tyrosine but, unlike the CT, we observed a consumption of proline, trans-4‑hydroxy-l-proline and malic acid and a production of phosphoric acid. OA/PA and PA conditions demonstrated a similar metabolic pattern: higher consumption of amino acids such as leucine, threonine and aspartic acid, as well as higher production of proline and uracil, when compared to CT condition.

After 7 days of treatment, we extracted 14 differentially expressed metabolites (Fig. 3F). We still observed similar features between OA/PA and PA conditions. Indeed, we noticed a higher production of proline, beta-alanine, palmitoleic acid, and a higher consumption of threonine and tryptophan for both OA/PA and PA conditions when compared to CT. However, we observed, for PA condition only, a production of oleic acid, a higher production of cholesterol (when compared to CT and OA/PA conditions) and a lower consumption of nicotinamide when compared to CT. Concerning the OA condition, we detected a production of tyrosine, a consumption of palmitic acid, trans-4‑hydroxy-l-proline, sarcosine, cholesterol, and a high consumption of oleic acid (in comparison to CT). Unlike PA condition, we observed a high consumption of nicotinamide for OA condition.

Important common and specific metabolites of the different treatments

To identify the common and specific in vitro FFA exposure biomarkers of each treatment, two Venn’s diagrams were plotted using the discriminating metabolites identified at day 4 and 7. As shown in Fig. 7A, at day 4, there were no metabolites common to the three treatments. OA, PA and OA/PA treatments presented three, seven and six specific metabolites, respectively. However, we highlighted that the OA/PA mixture treatment shared seven metabolites in common with the PA treatment, while there were no metabolites in common with the OA treatment. Interestingly, at day 7, we extracted two metabolites that were common to the three treatments: oxalic acid and nicotinamide (Fig. 7B). Nicotinamide was produced by all treated cells at day 4 and 7, while oxalic acid was consumed by OA-treated cells and highly produced by cells treated with PA and the mixture. As on day 4, OA/PA condition had more metabolites in common with the PA condition than the OA condition. Indeed, the OA/PA condition shared the consumption of palmitic acid, threonine and serine with the PA treatment, whereas it only shared the consumption of oleic acid with the OA treatment. In addition, cells treated with OA/PA for 7 days showed the greatest metabolic change, with 10 specific metabolites differentially expressed including amino acids and lipids (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

In this study, metabolomic profiling was used to investigate the effect of FFAs on HepG2/C3A cells cultured in microfluidic biochip. The organ-on-chip technology allows cells culture in controlled dynamic microenvironment and the maintain of cell viability and functionality for long time (Messelmani et al., 2022a). Due to the microenvironment and microstructures in our biochip, we observed a multilayer cell proliferation that led to 3D-like tissue organization.

A general feature of our metabolome analysis concerned the lipid and fatty acid metabolisms perturbation due to the OA, PA and OA/PA. The perturbation of lipid metabolism is a hallmark of fatty acid exposure on liver cells. Thus, oleic acid is reported to modify the lipidic secretion in hepatocytes (intracellular lipidomic showed the increases of diglycerides, triglycerides and acyl carnitine; the reduction of ceramide, cholesterol ester, lysophosphaticylcholine, phosphatidylglycerol; Xu et al., 2020). Although, we did not detect directly those compounds in our extracellular metabolomic analysis, we found in our previous study a lipid accumulation inside of the cells of our OA cultures consistently with the triglycerides lipidomic accumulations (Morisseau et al., 2026). Furthermore, while we cannot exclude some contributions from cell density differences, we identified common and specific signatures that were FFA dependent in our HepG2/C3a model that we will now discuss.

A common signature reveals attrition of nitrogen metabolism and nicotinamide production

After 7 days of exposure, we found that the OA, PA, and OA/PA commonly increased the production of nicotinamide. Nicotinamide is natural molecule that acts as a coenzyme in energy transfer inside the cell (El-Kady et al., 2022). It was involved in the improvement of liver regeneration and liver function (Wan et al., 2019). Furthermore, it is known as a protection against liver steatosis and metabolic imbalances in NAFLD (El-Kady et al., 2022). Nicotinamide derivatives are also reported to improve glucose tolerance in mice plasma with elevated FFAs (Nahle et al., 2021) and correct the glycolysis and fatty acid beta-oxidation in citrin-KO mice (Yau et al., 2023). In addition, serum levels of nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT) are associated with hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL) in a sex-dependent manner in NAFLD’ patients.

When compared to their controls, OA led to a lower consumption of glutamine (at day 7); PA and OA/PA led to ornithine and proline productions at day 4; PA led to a lower production of serine and trans-4‑hydroxy-l-proline at day 7 and OA/PA to high proline production at day 7. Those metabolites perturbations showed that an important feature of our data set was the perturbation of the biological processes related to the nitrogen metabolisms (in treated samples, when compared to control, after 4 days and after 7 days of exposure). This interpretation was reinforced by the pathway enrichment analysis. Although the urea was detected in our medium, no particular modulation of this metabolite was measured when we compared the CT and all the FFAs treatments at each time points. However, it is recently reported that NAFLD contribute to downregulate nitrogen genes involved in nitrogen conversion (Eriksen et al., 2019). Furthermore, urea cycle dysregulation leads to ammonia accumulation in the blood of NAFLD’ patients. In addition, the disruption of glutamine/glutamate metabolism is a pathway reported to lead to ammonia accumulation in cirrhotic patients after NAFLD (Thomsen et al., 2023).

The specific signatures extracted the key features of each fatty acid treatment

Regarding the specificity of each fatty acid, overall, the OA exposure appeared to induce only a mild modification of the metabolome as far as only few metabolites discriminated the treated conditions and the controls. OA is reported to promote larger lipid droplet accumulation when compared to PA in HepG2 cells and to lead to weaker mitochondrial oxidative metabolism (Eynaudi et al., 2021). Furthermore, in the early stage of steatosis, the lipotoxicity is characterized by an intense ROS production via the increase of the beta-oxidation of free fatty acid accumulated in the cells (Mooli and Ramakrishnan, 2022). In our culture model, however, we found that OA treated cells displayed an important ROS generation in lipid accumulation dependent manner (reported in Morisseau et al., 2026). Interestingly, after 4 days of exposure, we detected a clear production of l-pyroglutamate in OA treated conditions. The 5-oxoproline/pyroglutamate is reported as a typical ROS biomarker (Gall et al., 2010).

Literature report metabolome perturbation of the phenylalanine, tyrosine, fatty acid metabolism and bile acids when HepG2 are exposed to 400 μM of palmitic acid (Aggarwal et al., 2023). Furthermore, tryptophan, kinerusine and carnitine were discriminant to separate NAFLD, NASH to cirrhosis subjects (Aggarwal et al., 2023). After 4 days of exposure, we detected a switch from a production in control cells to a consumption of tyrosine in PA treated cells. We also found a switch from a consumption to a production of tryptophan in PA treated cells. Furthermore, after 4 days and 7 days of PA treatment, we detected a modification of the metabolism of TCA intermediates and substrates (such as a lower consumption of pyruvic acid, succinic acid and citramalic acid; a switch from a consumption to a production of beta-alanine at day 7 when compared to control condition). PA is reported to enhance mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in HepG2 (Eynaudi et al., 2021) but to reduce it in some other work (García-Ruiz et al., 2015). Thus, our signature reflected a modification of the energy and respiration state in PA treated conditions when compared to control, in which we may suspect a lower TCA activation (because of lower consumption of the metabolites) and thus a lower oxidative phosphorylation. Furthermore, it appeared consistent with our previous data in which we observed a lower ROS production in PA conditions (Morisseau et al., 2026). Nevertheless, additional experiments are necessary to confirm this hypothesis.

Then, we found a specific glycine/serine pathway signature in OA/PA treatments illustrated by the low production of glycine and serine. In fact, it is reported that serine, via liver folate metabolism, regulate hepatic lipogenesis and NADPH (Zhang and Zhang, 2022). In parallel, important serine deprivation alters glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids metabolisms and potential mechanism for serine‑derived lipids in mitochondrial processes are reported (Gao et al., 2018). Furthermore, low serine and glycine are also more and more reported as a disease hallmark (in neuropathy (Handzlik et al., 2023); in alcoholic liver disease (Shi et al., 2020)). Serine is also an important metabolite for nucleotide and redox metabolism functions via folate metabolism as well (Gao et al., 2018). More particularly, serine is a precursor of glycine and cysteine for glutathione synthesis (glutathione being an important anti-oxidant involved in ROS detoxification). Furthermore, we detected a switch from a production to consumption of sarcosine (related compound in the serine/glycine/folate pathways), when compared to control. Interestingly, sarcosine is reported to increase, in human and rat bloods, in the case of dietary restriction and to reduce with aging (Walters et al., 2018).

An important feature of the mixture of OA/PA at day 7 is the switch from production of lipids toward their consumption (oleic, palmitic, eicosapenteanic acids) and the stop of palmitoleic acid production (when compared PA and OA/PA) in our model. A larger lipidomic analysis on HepG2/C3A spheroids in the literature has confirmed complex lipidic metabolism switch in OA/PA co-exposure (Nahle et al., 2021). Furthermore, we noticed that the presence of palmitic acid in the PA exposures contributed to increase the production of palmitoleic acid and to stop the PA production when compared to controls. Palmitoleic acid is synthesized from palmitic acid via SCD-1 in the endoplasmic reticulum (Bermúdez et al., 2022). Although palmitoleic acid effect in NAFLD/NASH is still contradictory (Bermúdez et al., 2022), palmitoleic acid was reported to reduce the lipid accumulation, to increase the insulin secretion and to improve the glucose homeostasis (Yang et al., 2011) and to prevent lipo-apoptosis coupled with anti-inflammatory effects (Akazawa et al., 2010; Souza et al., 2014; 2020). Then, we found a lower production of cholesterol in OA/PA treated condition after 7 days of culture. Interestingly, too high cholesterol level is a pro-steatosis signaling, leading to lipotoxicity including endoplasmic stress and a pro-apoptosis in hepatocytes (Tirosh, 2018).

Then, it is largely reported that OA co-exposure with PA can reduce and attenuate the PA toxicity. Regarding the OA/PA and PA signatures after 7 days of exposure, OA co-exposure to PA seems to reduce the productions of palmitoleic acid and cholesterol when compared to controls, restore the levels of TCA substrates and beta-alanine (when compared to PA condition, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). As cholesterol, palmitoleic acid, TCA, and beta-alanine may have different role in response to fatty acids’ overloads, additional investigation would be required to confirm if there is a potential bridge with the serine metabolism. Furthermore, although palmitic acid and oleic acid have been extensively used in in vitro studies aiming to mimic MASLD, other fatty acids relevant to this pathology could be investigated, such as lauric, vaccenic, and palmitoleic acids (Puri et al., 2009; Miyake et al., 2022).

HepG2/C3A model limitations

Finally, HepG2 cells exhibit impaired mitochondrial function and reduced oxidative capacity for substrates like long-chain fatty acids and pyruvate (Prip-Buus et al., 1992; Igoudjil et al., 2008; Peyta et al., 2016; Maseko et al., 2024). Consequently, more advanced cells such as primary human hepatocytes or HepaRG PHHs, hiPSCs, or HepaRG cells are generally preferred for modeling MASLD, as they better capture the early-stage oxidative capacity increases (Sunny et al., 2016; Kotlyarova et al., 2026). While HepG2 cells are derived from hepatocellular carcinoma, their response to free fatty acids (FFAs) may also reflect a specific metabolic phenotype observed in certain NAFLD-related liver cancers, where altered lipid metabolism and oxidative stress are hallmark features. This does not imply that HepG2 cells fully replicate MASLD but suggests that they may model a particular subset of metabolic dysfunctions relevant to both NAFLD and its progression to malignancy. Furthermore, our approach was by design isolating specific metabolic responses to fatty acids without the confounding influence of other cell types (e.g., stellate or Kupffer cells), which are critical in later stages of MASLD but beyond the scope of this study. As a result, our data may reflect an FA lipotoxic response rather than a full MASLD model recapitulation. However, when compared to clinical data, our metabolomic analysis of the OA/PA mixture at days 4 and 7 revealed enriched pathways (glycine/serine and arginine metabolism) consistent with recent studies (Miyake et al., 2022; Fotakis et al., 2023). Additionally, nitrogen-related pathways (urea cycle, ammonia recycling; our data set and Miyake et al., 2022) and glutathione/methionine (our data set and Fotakis et al., 2023) metabolisms were commonly highlighted. However, unlike these references, carbohydrate metabolism was not significantly enriched in our model, potentially due to the specific metabolic limitations of HepG2 cells previously mentioned. Furthermore, additional target metabolic flux analysis or additional assays such ATP measurement would be required to confirm our observation. Nevertheless, these clinical comparisons validate several of our findings while underscoring the context-dependent nature of metabolic responses to FFAs.

Conclusion

We have performed the metabolomic analysis of HepG2/C3A cells cultured in microfluidic biochip and exposed to oleic acid, palmitic acid and oleic/palmitic acid mixture. The three fatty acids treatments led in a dependent manner to switch metabolic processes associated with the fatty acid beta-oxidation and the lipogenesis. Furthermore, they commonly led to increase the production of nicotinamide which could appeared as a potential in vitro biomarker of FFAs exposures in our model. Even if they may appear as mechanistic candidate, it would now request further validation in preclinical and clinical settings. All treatments led to modify the nitrogen metabolism processes. Specific signatures, illustrating the cell response to each exposure were extracted. The oleic presented a moderate metabolome perturbation in which we detected the production of pyroglutamate as potential ROS detoxification marker. The palmitic acid particularly contributed to modify the TCA intermediates and substrates illustrating a potential cellular respiration adaptation. Finally, the palmitic/oleic acids mixture were characterized by lower production of serine and glycine and consumption of sarcosine, highlighting a perturbation of the serine, glycine, folate related pathways. This perturbation was coupled with lower cholesterol synthesis and a more global reduction of the fatty acid and lipid metabolisms. Overall, our data demonstrate the sensibility of our strategy, using organ-on-chip technology coupled with metabolomic profiling, to investigate in vitro FFA response and potentially to pro-steatosis-like cellular response.
