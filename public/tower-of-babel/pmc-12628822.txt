Prediction of the Ki-67 proliferation index in lung adenocarcinoma using an interpretable CT-based deep learning radiomics model: a two-center study
Authors: Cuiling Zhu, Yanli Liang, Yexing Li, Cheng Li, Zhifeng Xu, Yunjun Yang, Tingsong Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop an interpretable computed tomography (CT)-based deep learning radiomics (DLR) model that integrates deep learning (DL) and radiomics features to predict the preoperative Ki-67 proliferation index in lung adenocarcinoma.MethodsA retrospective investigation involved 136 patients with lung adenocarcinoma who had undergone surgical resection between 2020 and 2024. Ki-67 expression was categorized as negative when ≤ 5%, and positive when > 5%. The training set comprised 91 patients, and the external test set contained 45 patients. Preoperative CT images were analyzed to derive 794 radiomics features, alongside 120 DL features obtained from the ResNet50 model. Model development was carried out by integrating DLR features through the application of ten distinct machine learning classifiers. The model’s diagnostic performance was evaluated based on the area under the curve (AUC). The interpretability was further examined using the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) technique.ResultsAmong the classifiers evaluated, the logistic regression (LR) model that integrates both DL and radiomics features exhibited the superior predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.904 in the training set, and the external test set AUC was 0.865, respectively. This result surpassed both traditional radiomics-based models (AUCs of 0.847 and 0.821 were observed for the training set and external test set, respectively) and DL models alone (AUCs of 0.802 and 0.733 were observed for the training set and external test set, respectively). Additionally, SHAP analysis demonstrated the two radiomics features with the greatest influence on model predictions.ConclusionThe CT-based DLR model demonstrates strong predictive capabilities for distinguishing Ki-67 expression status in lung adenocarcinoma, indicating its capacity as an interpretable and robust tool for preoperative evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03983-5.Keywords: Computed tomography, Ki-67, Lung cancer, Radiomics, Deep learning

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a widely prevalent cancer and one of the foremost contributors to cancer mortality across the world [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma is the most commonly observed histological subtype among those of NSCLC. Unfortunately, numerous patients are confirmed at later stages, which increases their risk of recurrence and results in a poor prognosis [2, 3]. The characteristics and speed of tumor cell proliferation are closely connected to the cancer’s aggressive behavior and its clinical outcomes. Ki-67 is the most frequently employed biomarker for evaluating cellular proliferation [4]. As a nuclear antigen expressed during the cell cycle stages involved in proliferation, Ki-67 is a well-established marker for monitoring proliferative activity, offering valuable prognostic insights in lung cancer [5]. According to the literature, cutoff values of 5% and 40% are frequently employed to differentiate between low and high Ki-67 expression levels [6]. Meta-analysis findings reveal a strong correlation between high Ki-67 expression and adverse prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma compared to lung squamous cell carcinoma within the context of NSCLC [7]. Therefore, accurately predicting the Ki-67 index can offer valuable insights into tumor biology and facilitate personalized management strategies for patients.

Currently, the Ki-67 index is assessed using invasive tissue sampling techniques such as needle biopsy or surgical resection, both of which are prone to variability and sampling errors [8]. Traditional immunohistochemical methods are limited to analyzing small biopsy specimens, which may not fully capture tumor heterogeneity. Moreover, these invasive procedures are not always feasible for patients who are ineligible for surgery or biopsy. Therefore, an immediate need exists for a non-invasive and reliable method to predict the Ki-67 index.

Computed tomography (CT) serves as the primary technique for lung cancer detection and screening [9]; however, visual interpretation alone is insufficient for accurately assessing tumor heterogeneity. Radiomics, a burgeoning field, enables the quantitative analysis of tumor characteristics in a non-invasive manner [10]. This approach has been widely applied in lung cancer studies, demonstrating significant potential for forecasting the invasiveness of lung cancer or identifying benign versus malignant lung nodules [11–13]. Several studies have explored machine learning model utilizing radiomics features for estimating the status of Ki-67 expression in NSCLC [13–16]. However, the clinical deployment of these models is restricted because of factors such as the lack of independent validation, inconsistent model performance, and insufficient interpretability. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method provides a unified framework to quantify feature importance and elucidate their contributions to model predictions. When combined with radiomics, SHAP enhances both the interpretability and clinical applicability of the predictive model [17]. In contrast, deep learning (DL) has established itself as a critical tool in medical imaging [18]. DL enables the quantification of high-dimensional radiological phenotypes beyond the limits of human perception and offers a non-invasive approach for constructing specialized predictive models across diverse clinical applications. For instance, deep learning–based imaging features extracted using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been utilized in the construction of predictive models. However, the application of deep learning to predict Ki-67 expression in lung adenocarcinoma remains largely underexplored [19]. The combination of deep learning radiomics (DLR) has shown encouraging results in other cancers, highlighting its potential for enhancing lung cancer prognosis prediction [20, 21]. The fusion strategy involves integrating multiple extracted radiomics and DL features through various approaches for subsequent analysis. At present, there is limited evidence supporting the feasibility of combining radiomics and deep learning techniques to predict Ki-67 expression levels in clinical cases of lung adenocarcinoma. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that an interpretable DLR model may accurately assess the level of Ki-67 expression in lung adenocarcinoma.

Therefore, this study’s goal was to establish an interpretable, CT-based DLR model for the non-invasive preoperative evaluation of the expression levels of Ki-67 in lung adenocarcinoma patients, utilizing a two-center patient dataset.

Materials and methods

Patients

Approval for conducting this retrospective study was granted from our institutional review board, with informed consent being waived. Data were obtained from consecutive patients diagnosed with lung cancer who underwent surgical intervention during the period from January 2020 to November 2024. Data from laboratory, imaging, clinical, and pathological sources were acquired and assessed including factors such as gender, age, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, tumor density, and size. Based on the data sources, two groups were formed from the patients: the training set followed by the external test set, respectively. 91 patients from the first hospital were included in the training set (January 2020–September 2024), while a total of 45 patients from the second hospital were involved in the external test set (July 2020–November 2024). The criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) a histopathological diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma with Ki-67 proliferation index assessment; (2) a preoperative lung CT scan; and (3) no prior anti-tumor treatment before CT imaging. Exclusion was based on the following criteria: (1) absence of Ki-67 immunohistochemical analysis or CT imaging; (2) the presence of other primary malignant tumors; and (3) poor-quality imaging or a CT slice thickness of ≥ 5 mm. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, 136 patients with lung adenocarcinoma were ultimately included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the patient selection

CT image acquisition

All patients enrolled in the study underwent chest spiral computed tomography (CT) scans. Imaging was carried out with the use of Philips CT scanners, utilizing the Philips Brilliance iCT 256-slice CT scanner and the Philips Ingenuity 64-slice CT scanner (Philips Medical Systems). The scanning parameters for these devices had the following settings: a field of view (FOV) of 350 × 350 mm, slice thickness of 1 mm, matrix size of 512 × 512, and tube voltage of 120 kV. For patients at Center 2, imaging was conducted with the GE 256 Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare), utilizing identical parameters: FOV of 350 × 350 mm, slice thickness of 1.25 mm, tube voltage of 120 kV, and matrix size of 512 × 512. Both standard and high-resolution algorithms were applied for the reconstruction of all images.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Ki-67 protein expression in tumor cells was evaluated through immunohistochemical staining using a specific Ki-67 antibody. For further analysis, three areas showing the highest positivity in stained cells were identified. Using an optical microscope at high magnification (×400), five representative fields were selected to quantify the count of tumor cells positive for Ki-67. The Ki-67 expression level in each field was calculated by determining the percentage of cells exhibiting positive staining, which was derived by dividing the count of positive cells by the total tumor cell count in the field, then multiplying by 100%. As demonstrated by prior studies [13, 22], a Ki-67 index ≤ 5% was categorized as negative, while a value greater than 5% was considered positive.

Region of interest segmentation and feature extraction

An overview of the research methodology is depicted in the workflow diagram in Fig. 2. Tumor segmentation and the definition of the three-dimensional region of interest (ROI) was manually performed by Radiologist A using ITK-SNAP version 3.6.0 on CT images from the lung window (1500HU and − 600HU) to delineate tumor boundaries. In order to analyze the consistency of radiological features, Radiologist B randomly selected 30 CT images for ROI segmentation and used them to calculate the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Senior Radiologist C assessed the quality of the ROI and performed necessary checks and adjustments. During the ROI delineation, they were blinded to the pathological results. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions. An ICC exceeding 0.75 was viewed as reflecting good concordance both within and between the observers. Using Pyradiomics (version 3.0.1), radiomics features from the region of interest (ROI) were extracted, and all images were resampled to have a voxel spacing of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. To enhance feature recognition, preprocessing included the application of wavelet filters, including high-pass and low-pass, together with a Laplacian Gaussian filter, each applied with different λ parameters. Each tumor yielded 794 radiomic features, comprising neighboring grey tone difference matrix (ngtdm) features (n = 100), grey-level run-length matrix (glrlm) features (n = 320), first-order features (n = 360), and shape-based features (n = 14), based on both original and pre-processed images. A comprehensive list of all radiomic features can be accessed online (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/features.html). The training set consisted of patients from Center 1. Meanwhile, patients from Center 2 were designated for a separate external test set. Radiomics features were initially screened using the Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.05). All radiomics features were normalized using z-scores and lastly screened using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO).

The overall process diagram for this study

For the transfer learning models employed in this study, we utilized ResNet-50, which was pretrained on the ImageNet dataset to initialize the model’s weights [23]. The training procedure involved both forward propagation and backpropagation. Prior to training, the initial images were adjusted to a size of 224 × 224 pixels. Training was conducted with a stochastic gradient descent optimizer, starting with a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴, a cosine annealing schedule with a 22-fold decay, and a batch size of 256. Specifically, DL features were obtained from the outputs of the model’s penultimate layers of the trained convolutional neural network (CNN). ResNet-50 initially extracted 2048 features from each patient’s ROI, which were then compressed into 120 deep features for further analysis [24].

Feature selection and DLR model construction and verification

To minimize inconsistencies in radiomics features resulting from inter-center variations in imaging equipment and protocols, the combat compensation method was applied. Subsequently, Z-score normalization was performed on all features prior to the feature selection process. The same processing steps were applied independently to radiomics, DL, and combined radiomics-DL features. To begin with, the high-dimensional data was reduced in dimensionality. The Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for analyzing all features, and Pearson correlation tests were conducted to explore feature-category associations. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered an indicator of potentially predictive features and were kept for subsequent analysis. The LASSO was utilized to select key features. Following this, a joint prediction model was developed by integrating both radiomic and DL features. To facilitate feature selection, LASSO regression was applied with a regularization term governed by the parameter λ, which penalizes less informative features by shrinking their coefficients to zero. The optimal λ value was determined through cross-validation by minimizing the corresponding validation error. Additionally, due to the initial extraction of 2048 deep learning feature dimensions, principal component analysis was employed to reduce and balance the feature dimensionality to 120 features. This dimensionality reduction enhanced the model’s generalization ability and reduced the risk of overfitting. The DL signature demonstrating the highest classification performance was selected from three potential DL signatures and combined with nine radiomics signatures to form the deep learning-radiomics (DLR) signature. The DLR features were constructed using a stack fusion approach, combining radiomic and DL features into a unified “DLR feature” set.

To construct the machine learning models, ten classifiers were employed: Naive Bayes, LightGBM (gradient boosting machines), Logistic Regression (LR), Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and AdaBoost. The training dataset was subjected to a 10-fold cross-validation process for classifier training, and an external test set was employed for model validation. To determine the model’s effectiveness in predicting Ki-67 expression levels, the key indicators were accuracy and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The top-performing machine learning model was selected according to these metrics.

Interpretability and visualization of DLR model

To explain the decision-making process of the model and evaluate its interpretability, we employed gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) to focus on the regions that most impacted the model’s predictions [25]. Through Grad-CAM and feature maps, the deep learning model’s decision-related information, including its main distribution and site, is illustrated. By combining gradient information from the last convolutional layer of the convolutional neural network (CNN) using a weighted fusion technique, we generated class-activation maps that highlight significant areas of the target image relevant to classification. These activation maps pinpoint key areas associated with tumor Ki-67 grading.

Additionally, we employed SHAP to further improve the interpretability of the model. SHAP provides a way for clinicians to comprehend machine learning models through both global and local interpretability [26]. SHAP value plots reveal the positive and negative contributions of features to the model’s predictions. Features contributing more significantly to the model are ranked higher. An aggregated SHAP plot was used to highlight the most influential features in the optimized model. Our SHAP analysis examined the influence of both DL and radiomics features on Ki-67 grading prediction in lung adenocarcinoma.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the clinical and CT features, with continuous variables expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare continuous variables between groups, we used either independent-sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as needed. To analyze categorical variables, either Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used. Feature selection was performed in a multi-step process, incorporating the Mann-Whitney U test for initial filtering, Pearson correlation analysis to eliminate redundant features, and the LASSO for optimal feature selection. Model performance was evaluated using key metrics, including the AUC, specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, and both negative and positive predictive values (PPV, NPV). Python version 3.7.14 (https://www.python.org) was used to conduct all statistical analyses. P-values below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

This process reduced the potential radiomics predictors from 794 to 9, while the amount of DL features from ResNet50 was decreased from 120 to 3 (Table S1, Supplementary Information). A prediction model based on DLR features was then developed using 10 machine learning algorithms. The performance of this joint feature prediction model for Ki-67 classification based on DLR is summarized in Table 2. Of the models evaluated, the LR classifier with DLR features demonstrated the superior predictive performance, with the training set yielding an AUC of 0.904 and the external test set yielding 0.865 (Fig. 4). In contrast, when only DL features were used, the LR classifier yielded an AUC of 0.802 for the training set and 0.733 for the external test set. Similarly, using only radiomics features, the LR classifier demonstrated an AUC of 0.847 for the training set and 0.821 for the external test set. The DLR model exhibited greater performance overall, outperforming both standalone DL and radiomics models.

Discussion

In this study, we focus on creating a machine learning model with interpretability to distinguish Ki-67 expression levels in lung adenocarcinoma by integrating CT image-derived radiomics and DL features. The DLR model, which was built using ten distinct machine learning techniques, such as Random Forest, SVM, and LR, among others, demonstrated that LR provided the best performance. The optimal LR classifier enabled the DLR model to outperform both unimodal radiomics and DL models, with an AUC of 0.904 for the training set and 0.865 for the external test set. Furthermore, through Grad-CAM visualization and SHAP analysis, the model exhibited strong interpretability, offering insights into the factors influencing its predictions. These results suggest that the fusion of radiomics and DL features has substantial potential to predict Ki-67 expression in patients with lung adenocarcinoma.

A non-invasive strategy for determining Ki-67 expression levels in lung adenocarcinoma was developed in this study, utilizing a CT-based DLR model. Since the advent of radiomics, it has become widely utilized in the classification of lung nodules as benign or malignant and the evaluation of lung disease invasiveness, and has shown promising diagnostic outcomes [27]. Increased Ki-67 expression is typically a marker of aggressive tumor growth and adverse prognosis, making non-invasive radiomic approaches for Ki-67 detection highly valuable. Several studies have demonstrated the capacity of radiomics to estimate Ki-67 expression levels in lung cancer [15, 28]. For instance, a study involving 110 lung cancer patients revealed significant associations between Ki-67 status and 12 radiomics features. The Ki-67 proliferation index was independently predicted by negative variance, short axis, and elongation, as identified through multivariate logistic regression analysis, with an AUC of 0.77 in differentiating between high and low Ki-67 status [29]. Among the six machine learning based classifiers, the Random Forest-based radiomics model outperformed the others, which attained an AUC of 0.776 for predicting Ki-67 expression levels [14]. Another multicenter study explored the effectiveness of a CT image-derived radiomics model for estimating Ki-67 expression in pure-solid NSCLC, reporting strong predictive power with AUC values of 0.81 were achieved in both the diagnostic training set and internal validation set, while the external validation set yielded an AUC of 0.78. By incorporating both clinical and radiomics features, the model’s diagnostic performance improved, yielding AUCs of 0.83, 0.83, and 0.81, respectively [30]. A novel nomogram for predicting Ki-67 in NSCLC was developed and validated by Dong et al., with an AUC of 0.828 was reported in the validation set for the clinical-radiomics model [16]. Our radiomics-only results are consistent with previous studies, yielding AUC values of 0.847 and 0.821 in the training and external test sets, respectively, indicating moderate diagnostic performance. In contrast, the proposed DLR model, particularly when combined with the optimal LR classifier, demonstrated superior classification performance. The DLR model achieved AUCs of 0.904 in the training set and 0.865 in the external test set for predicting Ki-67 expression levels, outperforming models based solely on radiomics features. Moreover, the performance of our DLR fusion model exceeds that of traditional radiomics models reported in previous literature. Notably, earlier studies have indicated that clinical models alone often exhibit limited predictive capability compared to radiomics-based approaches. A clinical model for predicting Ki-67 status, for instance, achieved AUCs of 0.75, 0.73, and 0.72 in the internal validation, training, and external validation sets, respectively [30]. In another study, the test group showed an AUC of 0.756 for the clinical model [15]. In our study, although variations in lesion density types and lesion diameters (long and short axes) were observed among clinical and imaging parameters, univariate and multivariate analyses revealed no significant differences in clinical features between the groups. This may be partly due to the limited sample size. Previous studies have reported conflicting findings: for instance, short-axis diameter has been identified as a risk factor for Ki-67 expression in lung cancer [30], whereas other research found no significant association between lesion density type and Ki-67 levels [16]. Additionally, inconsistencies in the cut-off values used to stratify Ki-67 expression—such as 5%, 10%, 40%, and 50% in different studies—may also contribute to these divergent results [28]. These discrepancies underscore the need for future studies with larger sample sizes, ideally using multicenter or prospective designs, to more thoroughly investigate the clinical factors influencing Ki-67 expression. These findings suggest that a combined model using both deep learning and imaging features is superior to individual models and can significantly improve the division of Ki-67 expression levels into low and high categories in lung cancer.

DL is an emerging technique in image analysis, and numerous studies have demonstrated that DL algorithms are capable of identifying complex patterns in medical images, achieving accuracy that matches or even surpasses human performance [31]. The ResNet50 architecture, which enables deeper feature representation, has resulted in marked performance improvements across various tasks, such as image classification [32]. In particular, ResNet50 has proven valuable in tumor diagnosis and management, notably in predicting the prognostic outcomes of early-stage oral and oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma [33]. However, there have been relatively few studies applying deep learning to investigate Ki-67 expression and its significance in lung cancer. One study explored a DL-based classification model utilizing FDG-PET/CT images to determine Ki-67 expression and forecast prognosis in NSCLC. The study, which employed Densenet201, demonstrated the greatest predictive power for Ki-67 prediction, achieving an AUC of 0.891 when using PET, CT, and combined PET/CT images [19]. Building on these insights, we hypothesized that integration conventional radiomics and DL features would result in a more robust predictive model. Our innovative approach integrated DL features with radiomics features to estimate the Ki-67 expression and grade in lung adenocarcinoma. The integrated model, consisting of 9 radiomics features and 3 DL features, achieved AUC values of 0.904 for the training set and 0.865 for the external test set, outperforming individual feature sets. Additionally, comparative studies have assessed models utilizing Various DL models such as VGG 19, DenseNet 121, MobileNet V3, ShuffleNet V2, ResNet 50, and Inception V3 in conjunction with radiomics features, with one study highlighting that MobileNet V3 outperformed other models in predictive performance [23]. Future research integrating various deep learning models with radiomics features may further enhance the performance in evaluating Ki-67 expression levels in lung cancer.

According to our findings, the LR classifier, based on DLR features, yields the best classification performance among the machine learning models evaluated. Efficient and trustworthy machine learning techniques are key to successful clinical applications. Therefore, comparing different classifiers is essential, as selecting the optimal method is critical. Previous studies have frequently relied on single classifiers for predicting Ki-67 expression in lung cancer, we selected ten different algorithms to enhance the robustness of our analysis, including SVM, LR, LightGBM, MLP, Naïve Bayes, Random Forest, GradientBoosting, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, and AdaBoost. Our results indicate that the LR classifier provided the best AUC, which aligns with Prior studies that incorporated radiomics-based nomograms to forecast the Ki-67 index in advanced NSCLC, as observed in dual-phase enhanced CT [15]. However, other studies have explored the predictive value of radiomics in differentiating high and low Ki-67 expression levels in lung adenocarcinomas presenting as ground-glass opacity (GGO) nodules. These studies found that SVM-based models outperformed those based on LR [34]. This highlights the variability in results when using different machine learning algorithms. While LR is commonly used, the inconsistency in outcomes across various algorithms emphasizes the need for further validation in future studies, particularly those with larger sample sizes. Although this study primarily focuses on model development and validation, the integration of such tools into routine radiology workflows is essential for clinical translation. Future work should assess practical factors, including time efficiency, and cost-effectiveness, to support implementation in real-world settings. Moreover, prospective studies and close collaboration with clinicians will be vital for evaluating the model’s clinical impact.

Using advanced Grad-CAM and SHAP analysis techniques, we visualized the tumor regions that contributed most to the model’s predictions and identified the two most influential radiological features, demonstrating their utility as interpretative tools to support clinical decision-making. The activation maps generated by Grad-CAM can reveal crucial areas associated with lung cancer grading. In comparison to lung adenocarcinoma cases with low Ki-67 expression, our Grad-CAM analysis showed more extensive activation regions in cases with high Ki-67 expression. These regions were larger, more intensely red, and predominantly concentrated in the central part of the tumor. This observation underscores the inherent tumor heterogeneity in lung cancers, evidenced by differential activation patterns across large tumor regions, which may reflect variations in tumor cell proliferation. Our findings are consistent with previous studies [23, 35], which similarly observed significant regional differences in tumor activation. Additionally, although prior research has demonstrated the promising potential of machine learning models utilizing radiomics to forecast Ki-67 expression in lung cancer prior to surgery, several challenges persist. First, the ability to generalize results is often restricted by single-center and small sample sizes studies. Second, the lack of interpretability analysis affects the real-world applicability of these models in clinical practice. In response to these limitations, this study examines the interpretability of the models. SHAP analysis offers a clinician-friendly visualization technique, such as summary plots, to depict the contribution of individual features to the model’s predictions. We innovatively employed SHAP analysis to identify the two most influential radiomic features (wavelet_LHL_glszm_SmallAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis and wavelet_HLH_glszm_LargeAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis) that contributed to the model’s predictions, thus improving the transparency and interpretability of our approach. Furthermore, wavelet-based radiomic features have proven to be particularly insightful in relation to Ki-67, offering more detailed information compared to the original imaging features [15, 34]. The top two wavelet radiomics features exhibited the strongest correlation with Ki-67 grading, capturing texture heterogeneity and the spatial distribution of high-intensity regions across multiple frequency scales. These features likely reflect variations in tumor cell density and proliferative activity indicated by Ki-67 expression. Prior studies have associated such texture characteristics with tumor invasiveness and prognosis, underscoring their biological significance. Furthermore, radiomics feature extraction may be influenced by patient-specific factors, including body weight, smoking status, and tumor volume. Consequently, future prospective studies are necessary to validate and expand upon these findings. Through the combined use of Grad-CAM visualization and SHAP interpretability analysis, our findings suggest that the DLR model provides effective predictive facility for differentiating between distinct Ki-67 expression levels in lung adenocarcinoma patients.

This study is subject to several limitations that should be considered. First, given that this is a retrospective tow-center study, it is susceptible to selection bias and other inherent biases. To address these concerns, future prospective studies are crucial to mitigate these limitations and provide more robust evidence. Second, the limited sample size, being relatively small, may potentially diminish the statistical power of the analysis. Although the sample size (n = 136) may be considered small for deep learning research, it is similar to previous radiomics studies where cohorts typically range from 100 to 200 patients. In contrast to earlier research on NSCLC, our study specifically included patients with lung adenocarcinoma and follow strict inclusion criteria. Larger sample sizes and prospective studies are crucial for confirming the broader applicability of our model. Third, manual tumor segmentation was employed in this study, which introduced variability between observers. The implementation of automated segmentation methods in future research would help minimize this variability and streamline the segmentation process. Finally, this study utilized a limited set of baseline clinical data, with features found to be non-significant in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Future studies should aim to develop more comprehensive predictive models by incorporating a wider range of multidimensional data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the CT-based DLR model demonstrated strong efficacy in predicting Ki-67 expression levels in lung adenocarcinoma patients. Its interpretability further emphasizes its potential in supporting the creation of personalized treatment approaches for these patients.
