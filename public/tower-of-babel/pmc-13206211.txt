Solvent‐Controlled Chemoselectivity in Brønsted Acidic Deep Eutectic Mixtures: A Metal‐Free One‐Pot Cascade Meyer‐Schuster/C–P Bond Formation
Authors: Gloria Morís‐Menéndez, Nicolás Ríos‐Lombardía, Marina Ramos‐Martín, Alejandro Presa Soto, Joaquín García‐Álvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne‐pot tandem methodologies offer powerful opportunities to enhance step economy while reducing waste, energy input, and operational complexity, thereby directly aligning with the Principles of Green Chemistry. Thus, herein, we report a one‐pot cascade, metal‐free Meyer‐Schuster/C‐P (and C‐Cl) bond‐forming protocol enabled by Brønsted Acidic Deep Eutectic Solvents (BADESs), which act simultaneously as solvent, promoter and chemoselectivity controller. This eutectic medium enables the in situ generation of α,β‐unsaturated aldehydes from propargylic alcohols and their interception by secondary phosphine oxides, allowing both orthogonal transformations to operate: i) simultaneously from the beginning of the reaction in a single and operationally simple step; and ii) under mild, air‐ and moisture‐compatible conditions. Remarkably, the modulation of the HBA/HBD ratio within the same BADES system allows predictable switching between the selective formation of α‐hydroxy or α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides, without changing reagents or reaction conditions. Additionally, the protocol features: i) broad substrate scope; ii) high chemoselectivity and functional‐group tolerance; and iii) enables product isolation by simple precipitation/filtration, thereby avoiding VOC‐intensive chromatographic purification. Moreover, the eutectic medium can be recycled, the reaction is readily scalable to gram scale, and the process displays a favorable environmental footprint, with an E‐factor as low as 11.Keywords: Acidic Deep Eutectic Solvents, C‐P and C‐Cl formation, medium‐controlled chemoselectivity, one‐pot cascade, organophosphorus compounds

Introduction

One‐pot tandem methodologies represent powerful synthetic platforms that enhance step and atom economy while significantly reducing waste, reaction time, and operational complexity [1, 2], thereby directly aligning with the Principles of Green Chemistry [3, 4, 5, 6]. In this sense and during the last decade, there has been a notable increase of interest within the synthetic chemistry community toward the development of multistep one‐pot tandem protocols, since the implementation of these methodologies avoids the need for halfway isolation/purification of reaction intermediates that are typically required in tedious and traditional step‐by‐step procedures [7, 8]. While these conventional tandem strategies have demonstrated remarkable success in accessing a broad range of natural products and complex molecular architectures, they typically rely on protecting atmospheres (Ar or N2) and carcinogenic/nonrenewable VOC solvents, often in combination with expensive, toxic, and scarce transition‐metal catalysts [9]. Furthermore, these processes frequently require final purification of the desired products by column chromatography, which increases time, cost, and solvent consumption, thus leading to poorer values of key green chemistry metrics, such as the E‐factor [10, 11]. Moreover, in most reported cases, these tandem protocols: i) cannot be recycled or readily scaled up (thereby limiting their practical applicability and diminishing their overall sustainability); and ii) operate in a sequential regime (i.e., one‐pot/two‐step fashion) where the different synthetic tools cannot operate together from the beginning of the reaction and must instead be applied in a stepwise procedure [12]. In contrast, the implementation of new recyclable tandem and one‐pot cascade regime sequences capable of proceeding under mild, open‐air conditions (thus compatible with moisture and oxygen) and operating without the need of transition metals, VOCs or chromatographic purifications remains still comparatively limited.

Within this context, Acidic Deep Eutectic Solvents (ADESs), derived from either Lewis (LADESs) or Brønsted (BADESs) acidic counterparts, have emerged as versatile reaction media capable of simultaneously acting as promoters and solvents, thereby enabling cascade bond‐forming events under mild and operationally simple conditions [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In general, the inherent low toxicity, negligible vapor pressure and straightforward preparation of these eutectic mixtures (starting from inexpensive and readily available components) has positioned DESs as attractive alternatives to volatile organic solvents (VOCs) [18, 19], thus being considered promising and sustainable media in the fine chemical and pharmaceutical industries [20]. In this regard, our research group has previously demonstrated the effectiveness of both Lewis‐ and Brønsted‐Acidic DESs (ADESs) in the development of efficient and highly selective one‐pot tandem protocols operating under mild, greener, and aerobic conditions [21, 22, 23]. These methodologies are based on the following reaction combinations: i) the hydration/oxidation of internal alkynes to produce the corresponding 1,2‐diketones [promoted by the eutectic mixture FeCl3 · 6H2O/Gly (3:1; Gly = glycerol)] (Figure 1a) [21]; ii) the regio‐ and stereoselective hydroiodination/sulfenylation of nonsymmetric internal alkynes into the corresponding (Z)‐vinyl sulfides [promoted by the eutectic mixture ChI/p‐TSA·H2O (1:1; ChI = choline iodide; p‐TSA = p‐toluenesulfonic acid)] (Figure 1b) [22]; and iii) the tricomponent hydration/aldolic condensation/1,4‐nucleophilic addition between terminal alkynes, aromatic aldehydes and different heteroatom‐based nucleophiles (phosphine oxides or sodium sulfinates) [promoted by the eutectic mixture ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2)] (Figure 1c) [23]. Furthermore, we have previously shown that the aforementioned Lewis ADESs FeCl3 · 6H2O/Gly (3:1) is the system of choice to promote the efficient and selective Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement of propargylic alcohols, leading to α,β‐unsaturated carbonyl compounds under greener, mild, and air‐ and moisture‐compatible conditions (Figure 1d) [24].

Previous examples of efficient and selective ADES‐promoted one‐pot tandem transformations and Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement under mild, greener and aerobic conditions.

Building on these precedents, and considering the intrinsic reactivity of α,β‐unsaturated carbonyl compounds as activated Michael acceptors [25], we envisioned the design of a one‐pot cascade protocol in which α,β‐unsaturated enals (generated in situ via ADESs promoted Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement of propargylic alcohols) could be intercepted by a regioselective 1,2‐addition of secondary phosphine oxides. Thus, in our integrated reaction protocol, both orthogonal processes operate together from the onset of the reaction, enabling direct C‐P (and C‐Cl) bond formation without intermediate accumulation or isolation and ultimately delivering the desired family of highly functionalized organophosphorus compounds in a single, streamlined and sustainable operational step (see Figure 2). Moreover, the composition of the eutectic mixture was found to play a decisive role, enabling highly selective access to either α‐hydroxy or α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides. This tunable behavior arises from the dual role of the eutectic medium as solvent, chemical promoter, and chemoselectivity controller, by providing the selective formation of either product depending on its formulation. Notably, the α‐chloro derivatives obtained under optimized conditions constitute versatile synthetic platforms for other structurally diverse targets through reaction with the n‐Bu3SnH/AIBN system, sodium azide or EtOH, under mild and sustainable conditions. Beyond its synthetic applicability, our protocol exhibits good sustainability credentials as: i) the eutectic mixture can be efficiently recycled (up to 4 consecutive runs); ii) the process is readily scaled‐up to gram level without loss of efficiency; iii) the target products can be isolated by simple filtration (thus avoiding the use of VOC‐based chromatographic purification techniques); and iv) presents a calculated Sheldon's environmental factor (E‐factor; total waste mass/product mass) value as low as 11.3.

Results and Discussion

Parametrization Studies for the ADESs‐Promoted Selective and Efficient One‐Pot Cascade Synthesis of α‐Hydroxy (3aa) or α‐Chloro Allyl Phosphine Oxides (4aa)

For the initial parametrization studies, we focused on the envisioned one‐pot cascade reaction between propargylic alcohol 1a and diphenylphosphine oxide 2a under aerobic, metal‐free, moisture‐compatible, mild, and greener conditions, as summarized in Table 1. Building on our previous success in the Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement of propargylic alcohols promoted by Fe(III)‐DESs [24], we began our investigation using the eutectic mixture FeCl3 · 6H2O/Gly (3:1) as both reaction medium and promoter for the desired one‐pot Meyer‐Schuster/C–P bond‐forming cascade (entry 1, Table 1). Thus, employing stoichiometric amounts (1 mmol) of both reagents 1a and 2a at 60°C under bench‐type conditions, this Lewis acidic eutectic mixture proved to be unable to promote the formation of the desired allyl phosphine oxides, as only traces (3%) of 4aa were detected by 1H NMR. Considering the variety of LADESs reported in the literature [14, 15, 17], we next examined alternative systems containing different metal halides acting either as hydrogen‐bond acceptors (HBA) or hydrogen‐bond donors (HBD). Thus, replacement of FeCl3 · 6H2O by ZnCl2 while maintaining glycerol as hydrogen‐bond donor [ZnCl2/Gly (1:4); entry 2, Table 1] did not improve the reaction outcome. Similarly, the corresponding Zn‐based choline chloride (ChCl) eutectic mixture [ChCl/Zn(NO3)2 · 6H2O (1:1); entry 3, Table 1] was also not capable of promoting the desired tandem process. Finally, the use of a mixed Brønsted/Lewis acidic eutectic system [e.g., FeCl3 · 6H2O/MSA (1:2), MSA = methanesulfonic acid, entry 4, Table 1] led only to a minor improvement, affording again low amounts (6%) of the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa after 16 h of reaction. Consequently, these experimental results seem to indicate that LADESs are not well suited to promote the desired tandem cascade protocol.

In contrast, when Brønsted acid‐based eutectic mixtures (BADESs) were employed as both reaction media and promoters (entries 5–12, Table 1), a clearly improved reactivity profile was observed. Thus, eutectic mixtures derived from choline chloride (ChCl) and relatively weak organic acids, such as citric acid, showed only minor activity (entry 5, Table 1). However, a progressive enhancement of our tandem process was detected as the acidity of the hydrogen‐bond donor increased. Thus, moving to mixtures containing oxalic (entry 6), trichloroacetic (TCA; entry 7) or methanesulfonic acid (MSA; entry 8) led to a gradual improvement in reaction efficiency, although different mixtures of both α‐hydroxy (3aa) or α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides (4aa) were obtained. However, the use of eutectic mixtures based on choline chloride (ChCl) and p‐toluenesulfonic acid (p‐TSA·H2O) clearly outperformed all other systems in promoting an efficient and selective Meyer‐Schuster/C–P bond‐forming cascade protocol (see entries 9–12). Remarkably, the ratio between the hydrogen‐bond acceptor (ChCl) and the hydrogen‐bond donor (p‐TSA·H2O) in the eutectic mixture provides an effective synthetic tool to control the chemoselectivity of the tandem protocol (being thus a clear example of a medium‐controlled chemoselectivity protocol). Thus, we found experimentally that by increasing the proportion of p‐TSA·H2O [therefore changing both the Brønsted acidity and the rest of physicochemical properties of the eutectic medium (such as hydrogen‐bonding networks, local microenvironment organization and specific solvation effects)] [16], we can switch completely the selectivity from the α‐hydroxy product (3aa) towards the α‐chloro derivative (4aa; compare entries 9–12), a transformation that proceeds via the concomitant and selective formation of both the C‐P and C‐Cl bonds, enabling direct access to the desired α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide (4aa) in a one‐pot cascade regime, therefore highlighting the chemoselective nature of our process. This experimentally observed change in the chemoselective product outcome can be rationalized by taking into account the aforementioned higher acid content of this eutectic mixture, which favors the protonation of the hydroxyl group present in 3aa and its concomitant elimination as water, followed by a subsequent capture of the resulting allylic cationic intermediate by the chloride anion. Nevertheless, the contribution of the aforementioned physicochemical properties of the DES to the observed chemoselectivity should also not be neglected. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first example in the literature of a proportion‐directed synthesis of different products by modulation of the ratio HBA:HBD while keeping the same components in an acidic eutectic mixture. Overall, this behavior highlights the key role of Brønsted acidity, together with the nucleophilic components of the eutectic medium, in governing both the efficiency and the selectivity of the tandem process.

Further optimization of the reaction parameters showed that fine tuning of substrate concentration, reaction time, and temperature was crucial to fine the optimal conditions for the selective synthesis of either 3aa or 4aa (entries 13–17, Table 1). Thus, variations in these parameters within the ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O system allowed us to maximize both efficiency and chemoselectivity. For instance, at a substrate concentration of 0.5 M and 75°C, ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) mixture selectively afforded the α‐hydroxy phosphine oxide 3aa in high yield (83%; entry 13), whereas under otherwise similar conditions but using the more acidic ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2), the corresponding α‐chloro derivative 4aa was obtained as the only product, with the best result being achieved at 75°C after 16 h of reaction (95%; entry 17). These experimental data seem to indicate that not only eutectic composition but also concentration, temperature, and reaction time are important factors in finding the optimal conditions for each selective outcome. Further control experiments demonstrated that the unique performance of the eutectic systems cannot be reproduced by their isolated individual components (entries 18 and 19, Table 1). Thus, the use of an external source of p‐TSA·H2O in: i) a neutral eutectic mixture such as ChCl:Gly (1:2; entry 18); or ii) a conventional volatile organic solvent such as toluene (entry 19) led to much poorer results and in no case matched the efficiency or selectivity previously observed with the ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O eutectic mixtures. This observation suggests a synergistic effect between the hydrogen‐bond donor (p‐TSA·H2O) and the hydrogen‐bond acceptor (ChCl) within the eutectic structure, which creates a unique reactive environment that cannot be replicated by the individual components acting separately. Finally, we also examined the influence of the halide nature of the choline ammonium salt in the eutectic mixture (see Supporting Information for details). Thus, under the optimized conditions using ChX/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2), a clear halide dependence was observed. In this sense, chloride (coming from ChCl) proved to be the most effective nucleophile, affording the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa in almost quantitative yield (95%). In contrast, replacement of choline chloride by its bromide counterpart (ChBr) led to a marked decrease in efficiency, giving rise to the corresponding α‐bromo derivative 5 in only 25% yield, while the choline iodide (ChI)‐based system completely suppressed product formation. This experimental trend can be explained by a combination of steric effects, as we proposed that the smaller size of chloride facilitates its approach to the possible highly congested allylic cationic intermediate generated after protonation and dehydration of the α‐hydroxy intermediate 3aa, whereas the larger bromide and iodide anions suffer greater steric hindrance in their approximation into the crowded allylic cation.

Scope of the Chemoselective Synthesis of α‐Hydroxy Allyl Phosphine Oxides 3 via One‐Pot Cascade Meyer‐Schuster/C–P Bond Formation Promoted by ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1)

After optimizing the reaction conditions, the versatility of this tandem protocol towards the chemoselective synthesis of a wide variety of α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxides (3) was evaluated by studying the substrate scope of both the propargylic alcohols precursors (1a‐i) and the phosphine oxide partners (2a‐e), using the eutectic mixture ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) both as reaction media and promoter of the formation of new C–C and C‐P bonds (Scheme 1). Firstly, we decided to maintain constant the nature of the phosphine oxide 2a while changing the electronic and steric properties of the employed propargylic alcohols. In this sense, we found experimentally that a range of aryl‐substituted alkynes bearing either electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing groups at the para‐position, such as Me (3ba, 99%), F (3ca, 90%), and Cl (3da, 94%), were efficiently converted into the corresponding α‐hydroxyphosphine oxides in good to excellent yields, indicating that the electronic nature of the aromatic ring has only a limited influence on the overall efficiency of the process. At this point, it is important to mention that these almost quantitative isolated yields were achieved by simple filtration of the reaction mixture, as the obtained α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxides (3aa‐3da) precipitated directly from the reaction medium upon addition of water. Notably, this simple experimental setup enabled straightforward isolation without the need for VOC‐intensive and tedious chromatographic purification techniques, thus further enhancing the operational simplicity and sustainability of the protocol. For the more sterically demanding meta‐ and ortho‐substituted alkynols 1e‐f, the more acidic DES ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2) was required. Under these optimized conditions, meta‐ and ortho‐substituted α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxides were obtained, albeit with slightly lower yield for the ortho‐substituted substrate (3ea in 72%; 3fa in 62%), consistent with a moderate steric effect. The presence of strongly electron‐withdrawing groups like CF3 (3ga) is also tolerated by our system, further highlighting the robustness and applicability of our method, taking into account the well‐established importance of trifluoromethyl substituents in medicinal chemistry for modulating lipophilicity, metabolic stability and bioavailability [26]. In contrast, preliminary experiments with strongly electron‐rich aryl‐substituted propargylic alcohols (e.g., OMe derivatives) resulted in lower chemoselectivity and complex product mixtures under the optimized BADES conditions. Notably, the system is effective with secondary propargylic alcohols (1h), delivering the desired cinnamyl‐based phosphine oxide 3ha exclusively as the E‐isomer, with complete stereoselectivity and in the absence of any chromatographic isolation/purification step. Finally, and regarding purely aliphatic propargylic alcohols containing CH‐groups at β‐position (which can undergo the more challenging Rupe rearrangement) [27], our protocol led to the corresponding methyl ketone intermediate 6 that preferentially undergoes a conjugate 1,4‐phospha‐Michael addition giving rise to the corresponding γ‐keto phosphine oxide 7 although formed in a lower yield.

For completeness of our study, we extended our investigations to a family of structurally diverse phosphine oxides 2b‐e. In this sense, a range of aryl phosphine oxides bearing both electron‐donating [Me (2b) or MeO (2c)] and electron‐withdrawing substituents [CF3 (2d)] were tolerated by our protocol, giving rise to the desired products in good to excellent yields (70‐88%). Notably, these α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxides 3ab‐3ad were isolated from the reaction media by simple filtration (without the need for chromatographic purification), further highlighting the operational simplicity and sustainability of our protocol. Finally, it is important to mention that aliphatic phosphine oxides were also competent reaction partners, delivering the corresponding α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide 3ae in good yield (68%). Overall, all the aforementioned results demonstrate the broad applicability and modularity of our method, which tolerates diverse electronic and steric environments and enables the chemoselective synthesis of highly functionalized α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxides under milder and greener conditions.

Scope of the Chemoselective Synthesis of α‐Chloro Allyl Phosphine Oxides 4 via One‐Pot Cascade Meyer‐Schuster/C‐P/C–Cl Bond Formation Promoted by ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2)

As a demonstration of the versatility of our system, it is important to mention that a modification in the molar ratio of the components of the eutectic medium [from ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) to the more acidic ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2)] is enough to completely redirect the reaction pathway, thus constituting an illustrative example of a medium‐controlled chemoselectivity. Therefore, the eutectic mixture plays an active role beyond being an innocent solvent, as it enables a switch in the reaction outcome by favoring the protonation of the α‐hydroxy group present in allyl phosphine oxides 3 and its concomitant elimination as water, followed by a subsequent capture of the resulting allylic cationic intermediate by the chloride anion (thus enabling the concomitant and highly selective formation of both C‐P and C‐Cl bonds), leading exclusively to α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides 4. This small yet strategic change in the eutectic environment ultimately dictates the identity of the final product, allowing precise control over chemoselectivity without changing the starting materials or the overall reaction conditions, as we are still working in the presence of air and moisture.

Under these conditions, we explored the substrate scope of the propargylic alcohols (1a‐i), keeping constant the phosphine oxide partner (2a) and employing the acidic eutectic system ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2), which acts simultaneously as reaction medium and promoter of sequential C‐P and C–Cl bond formation (Scheme 2). Thus, by varying the electronic and steric properties of the aryl‐substituted propargylic alcohols, we observed experimentally that alkynols bearing either electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing substituents at the para‐position, such as Me (4ba, 81%), F (4ca, 87%), and Cl (4da, 94%), were efficiently converted into the corresponding α‐chloro phosphine oxides in good to excellent yields, indicating that electronic effects on the aromatic ring have only a minor impact on the efficiency of the transformation. At this stage, it is worth noting that the good to excellent isolated yields were obtained by simple filtration, as the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides (4aa‐4da) precipitated directly from the reaction medium upon addition of water [28]. This straightforward workup eliminates the need for VOC‐intensive and time‐consuming chromatographic purification, thereby further enhancing the operational simplicity and sustainability of the protocol. Regarding meta‐ and ortho‐substituted propargylic alcohols, our protocol remains effective for meta‐substituted substrates (4ea), whereas ortho‐substitution leads to a marked decrease in efficiency, consistent with increased steric congestion and a pronounced steric effect (4fa). Notably, a substrate bearing a strongly electron‐withdrawing group is also tolerated (like CF3 in 4ga), underscoring the robustness of the methodology and its relevance for the synthesis of motifs of high interest in medicinal chemistry. Secondary propargylic alcohols (1h) were also competent substrates, delivering the corresponding cinnamyl‐type α‐chloro phosphine oxide (4ha) with complete regio‐ and stereoselectivity. In contrast, purely aliphatic propargylic alcohols prone to Rupe rearrangement (e.g., 1i) led preferentially to the corresponding ketone intermediate 6, which subsequently underwent 1,4‐conjugate phospha‐Michael addition to afford the aforementioned γ‐keto phosphine oxide 7 in better yield than that observed in Scheme 1. Finally, the scope of the protocol was further expanded by varying the nature of the phosphine oxide partner. In this sense, aryl phosphine oxides bearing both electron‐donating [Me (2b); OMe (2c)] or electron‐withdrawing [CF3 (2d)] substituents, as well as aliphatic phosphine oxides (2e), were all successfully engaged, providing the desired products (4ab‐4ae) in almost quantitative yields (up to 99%), even in the absence of any VOC‐based chromatographic purification. Overall, these results highlight again the broad applicability of our tandem process, which accommodates diverse electronic and steric environments and enables straightforward and selective access to highly functionalized α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides 4 under mild, greener and operationally simple acidic DES conditions.

Control Experiments and Mechanistic Proposal for the Medium‐Controlled Formation of α‐Hydroxy (3aa) and α‐Chloro Allyl Phosphine Oxides (4aa). Downstream Functionalization Studies for 4aa

To try to gain deeper insight into the possible reaction mechanism involved in our one‐pot cascade Meyer‐Schuster/C‐P (and C‐Cl) bond formation and also to rationalize the divergent formation of α‐hydroxy (3aa) vs α‐chloro phosphine oxide (4aa), a series of control experiments were performed (Scheme 3). In this sense, the first test (Scheme 3a) revealed that the Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement of the propargylic alcohol 1a in the absence of the phosphine oxide 2a proceeds with very low yield (38%) when using ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) as promoter under the previously optimized reaction conditions (16 h, 60 ºC under air/moisture). This observation seems to indicate that the global tandem transformation is not promoted by the Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement but instead relies on the rapid interception of the transient α,β‐unsaturated aldehyde by the phosphorus nucleophile. In this regard, the high nucleophilicity of the phosphine oxide 2a effectively pulls the equilibrium forward, enabling an efficient one‐pot cascade process rather than a stepwise sequential pathway. This experimental observation is in good agreement with the poor performance observed in the case of the in situ generated α,β‐unsaturated methyl ketone 6 (formed via the Rupe rearrangement, see Scheme 1) which displays reduced electrophilicity, thus disfavoring 1,2‐nucleophilic addition and instead leading to the corresponding 1,4‐addition product 7 in lower yields.

Control experiments designed to gain mechanistic insight into the one‐pot tandem Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement/C‐P (and C‐Cl) bond‐forming process and to rationalize the divergent formation of the α‐hydroxy (3aa) vs α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide (4aa) products by using either ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) or ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2) as promoters.

To further elucidate the origin of the observed chemoselectivity between α‐hydroxy (3aa) and α‐chloro (4aa) products, the reactivity of the isolated key enal intermediate 8 (generated in situ after the Meyer‐Schuster isomerization of the starting propargylic alcohol 1a) was examined under the two different acidic DES conditions. Firstly, when enal 8 and phosphine oxide 2a were exposed to the less acidic eutectic mixture [ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1)], we experimentally found exclusive formation of the α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide (3aa in Scheme 3b), consistent with the results described in Scheme 1. In contrast, when the enal 8 and phosphine oxide 2a were treated with the strongly acidic eutectic mixture [ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (1:2)], it resulted in the selective formation of the α‐chloro derivative (4aa in Scheme 3c), as observed previously in Scheme 2. Finally, and closing a possible mechanistic cycle, we further demonstrated that isolated α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide 3aa evolves into α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa in the presence of the more acidic DES (Scheme 3d). The chemical behavior observed in these four control experiments (Scheme 3a‐d) is consistent with our proposed mechanism (Scheme 4) and highlights the key role of the reaction medium. Thus, the tandem process is initiated by a Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement of the propargylic alcohol 1a to give the corresponding enal 8, which is subsequently trapped by the phosphine oxide 2a. By increasing the ratio of the Brønsted acidic in the eutectic medium, the protonation of the hydroxyl group in 3aa takes place, therefore triggering the water elimination and concomitant generation of a stabilized cationic intermediate 9. Thus, these experimental facts suggest that the observed chemoselectivity is possible governed by the Brønsted acidity of the BADES medium together with chloride availability. Although these factors appear to play a major role in the observed reactivity and chemoselectivity, other intrinsic physicochemical features of the DES media, such as hydrogen‐bonding networks, local microenvironment organization and specific solvation effects, should also be taken into consideration [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In the same line, variations in the HBA/HBD molar ratio may also induce changes in the physicochemical properties and supramolecular organization of p‐TSA‐based DES systems, which can directly influence their reactivity and ability to promote organic transformations [29]. Finally, nucleophilic capture of this vinyl cation 9 by the chloride anion (present in the eutectic medium) selectively furnishes the α‐chloro derivative 4aa, thereby rationalizing the medium‐controlled OH/Cl chemoselectivity observed in this tandem transformation. Overall, these observations seem to indicate that our tandem transformation operates under a one‐pot cascade regime. Nevertheless, the possibility of a rapid sequential Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement/phospha‐Michael process can be also considered.

Proposed reaction mechanism for the one‐pot tandem Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement/C‐P/C–Cl bond‐forming process, rationalizing the divergent formation of the α‐hydroxy (3aa) and α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide (4aa) products under medium‐controlled acidic DES conditions.

Taking into account that the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa contains an activated C–Cl bond in the vinylic position, and thus it can undergo substitution reactions with a variety of nucleophiles via allylic pathway [30], we decided to select this derivative for further downstream functionalizations (see Scheme 5). In this sense, we were pleased to find that compound 4aa readily participated in a range of derivatization processes, including: i) radical‐mediated dechlorination under n‐Bu3SnH/AIBN conditions [31] to afford the reduced product 10; as well as ii) substitution with different heteroatom‐based anionic nucleophiles (like azide and ethoxide) to deliver the corresponding allyl azide 11 and ether derivative 12 in good to excellent yields (Scheme 5). These transformations highlight the versatility of the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides 4 as modular intermediates for the synthesis of highly elaborated organic scaffolds and further validate the strategic value of the medium‐controlled chemoselective chloride installation from both mechanistic and synthetic perspectives.

Downstream diversification of α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa into reduced (10), azido (11) and alkoxy (12) allyl phosphine oxide derivatives.

Scalability, Recyclability, and Green Metrics Evaluation of the Acidic DES‐Promoted One‐Pot Tandem Meyer‐Schuster Rearrangement/C–P Bond Formation Protocol

To assess the sustainability and practical applicability of our chemoselective ADES‐promoted Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement/C–P bond formation protocol, we decided to study its: i) gram‐scale synthesis; ii) recyclability; and iii) green metrics calculations. In this sense, we first investigated the scalability of our protocol up to a gram‐level synthesis of α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide 4aa. Thus, following the previous optimized conditions (75°C, under air, Scheme 6) but increasing the scale up to 6 mmol (1.25 g of 1a and 1.21 g of 2a), we were able to produce the desired α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide 4aa in 92% isolated yield (2.4 g), without the need for VOC‐based chromatographic purification, as product isolation was achieved by simple aqueous work‐up and filtration. This result demonstrates the scalability and operational robustness of our protocol, providing an indication of its potential applicability in larger‐scale and industrially relevant syntheses, where: i) straightforward product isolation, ii) VOC‐solvent minimization; and iii) tolerance towards moisture/air are key sustainability drivers.

ADES‐promoted gram‐scale synthesis of α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxide 4aa under bench‐type reaction conditions.

Next, the sustainability of our ADES‐promoted tandem protocol was further examined through recyclability studies using, in this case, the synthesis of α‐hydroxy phosphine oxide 3ac as a representative example (see Scheme 7). In this sense, we were pleased to find that the ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1) eutectic mixture could be recovered and reused over four consecutive cycles with a gradual decrease in efficiency (even working in the presence of air and moisture), thus highlighting the robustness and reuse potential of our ADES to promote several consecutive transformations. Additional recycling studies beyond the fourth cycle are not reported because, from the fifth recycling experiment onward, the reaction yield drops below 50%. Thus, and from a practical perspective, we consider that isolated yields below 50% would likely compromise the operational attractiveness and sustainability of the protocol, particularly because additional purification steps (i.e., chromatographic techniques) would require VOC‐solvent consumption. Nevertheless, within the four recycling cycles evaluated in this work, the reaction still proceeds with synthetically useful efficiencies, complete chemoselectivity, and straightforward product isolation by simple precipitation/filtration, without detectable formation of significant by‐products or the need for VOC‐intensive chromatographic purification. Importantly, the gradual decrease in catalytic activity observed upon BADES recycling is also consistent with our previous studies employing the same DES medium in related one‐pot tandem organophosphorus transformations [23], where a similar loss of efficiency was mainly attributed to cumulative DES volume losses occurring during the recycling procedures.

Recycling studies on ADES‐promoted synthesis of α‐hydroxy allyl phosphine oxide 3ac under bench‐type reaction conditions.

Finally, and to evaluate the environmental impact of our ChCl/p‐TSA·H2O (3:1)‐mediated synthesis of 3ac, we calculated the Sheldon environmental factor (E‐factor) [11, 12]. The E‐factor calculation was performed on the basis of the work‐up and recycling experiments conducted over four consecutive cycles using 1a and 2c as model substrates (see Supporting Information for full details), giving rise to a value as low as 11.3. This good E‐factor value reflects: i) the use of stoichiometric amounts of reagents (in the absence of cocatalysts or cosolvents); ii) the beneficial impact of simple product isolation (no VOC‐based chromatographic techniques are required); and iii) the ADES recovery and recyclability on waste minimization. Notably, our calculated E‐factor falls within the expected range for fine chemical synthesis (5–50) and compares favorably with related i) ADES‐mediated transformations that employ nonrecyclable solvents and chromatographic purification [13, 14, 15, 16]; and ii) conventional VOC‐solution‐based protocols that rely on the use of Brønsted acidic promoters [32], reflecting the combined benefits of medium recyclability and simplified purification protocols. All together, these results emphasize the viability of our acidic DES systems as more sustainable platforms for the scalable synthesis of added‐value organophosphorus scaffolds. In contrast to many homogeneous precious‐metal catalytic systems commonly employed for Meyer‐Schuster rearrangements in conventional VOC media [33], our BADES platform can be readily recovered and reused over several cycles without requiring additional catalyst separation procedures or inert‐atmosphere techniques.

Finally, and from the perspective of a potential industrial application and a possible economic viability of our methodology, we would like to mention that the BADESs employed are prepared from inexpensive and commercially available bulk components (e.g., choline chloride and p‐toluenesulfonic acid monohydrate, typically ranging from ca. 40–100 USD per 500 g), without requiring additional purification or inert‐atmosphere techniques. In addition, the operational simplicity of our protocol, together with: i) the recyclability of the employed eutectic medium; ii) the absence of expensive transition‐metal catalysts; and iii) the circumvention of VOC‐intensive chromatographic purification, further reinforces the potential applicability of these BADESs systems in fine chemical synthesis. In the same line, it is also important to note that the obtained families of allyl phosphine oxides 3 and 4 constitute highly functionalized organophosphorus scaffolds that are commonly regarded as added‐value intermediates in fine chemical synthesis, ligand, and pharmaceutical synthesis [34, 35, 36], further supporting the possible practical relevance of our methodology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed a one‐pot cascade protocol that merges a Meyer‐Schuster rearrangement with direct and chemoselective C‐P (and C‐Cl) bond formation under metal‐free and operationally simple conditions (in the presence of air and moisture and without the need of cocatalyst or cosolvents). Thus, the use of Brønsted Acidic Deep Eutectic Solvents (BADESs; which act simultaneously as reaction medium, promoter, and chemoselectivity controller), enables both transformations to operate together from the onset of the reaction. Notably, and by changing the HBA/HBD ratio within the same eutectic system, the reaction outcome can be predictably switched between α‐hydroxy and α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides with total selectivity, thus providing a good example of medium‐controlled chemoselectivity without changing starting reagents or reaction conditions. Moreover, mechanistic studies reveal the key role of the acidic eutectic medium in controlling reaction concurrence and OH/Cl chemoselectivity, with the α‐chloro derivatives emerging as readily functionalizable intermediates for further diversification.

From a sustainability standpoint, it is important to mention that our methodology fulfills several key Principles of Green Chemistry, since it: i) operates efficiently under air and moisture conditions (no inert atmosphere is required); ii) avoids the use of expensive, toxic and scarce transition metals; and iii) does not require traditional VOC‐intensive chromatographic purification techniques, as product isolation is achieved by simple filtration. In the same line, it should be highlighted that the eutectic medium can be efficiently recycled over four consecutive cycles and that the protocol is readily scalable to gram level without erosion of yield or selectivity. These features translate into a positive environmental footprint, as reflected by a low E‐factor (11.3), which compares favorably with both conventional VOC‐based processes and related tandem methodologies requiring nonrecyclable media or extensive purification steps, while also reinforcing the potential practical applicability and economic attractiveness of the methodology. Moreover, the use of inexpensive and recyclable BADES media under solvent‐free and air‐compatible conditions further enhances the practical and sustainability profile of the methodology compared to conventional homogeneous VOC‐based systems.

Finally, and beyond its environmental credentials, the organic synthetic value of our approach is highlighted by its: i) broad substrate scope; ii) functional‐group tolerance; and iii) downstream diversification of the α‐chloro allyl phosphine oxides into structurally diverse organophosphorus scaffolds. Taken together, this work highlights BADESs as powerful, versatile, and selective reaction environments for the design of one‐pot cascade processes, offering a practical and sustainable platform for the scalable synthesis of added‐value organophosphorus compounds with potential relevance in fine chemical and pharmaceutical applications.
