INTRODUCTION

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a systemic necrotizing vasculitis of small- and medium-sized vessels that evolves through prodromal allergic, eosinophilic, and vasculitic phases[1]. Approximately one-third of patients are anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-positive, predominantly with myeloperoxidase (MPO) specificity, and exhibit more pronounced vasculitic manifestations including glomerulonephritis and peripheral neuropathy, with less prominent asthma[1].

Diagnostic delay in EGPA is the longest among ANCA-associated vasculitides (AAV), with a median of 455 days[2]. Cardiac disease, the leading cause of EGPA-related mortality, is largely preventable with early immunosuppression[1]. Despite expert consensus recommending ANCA testing in unexplained eosinophilia with organ involvement[3], such testing frequently goes unordered.

No gold standard diagnostic test exists; the diagnosis is clinical. The 2022 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria, designed for research, require a prior clinical diagnosis of small- or medium-vessel vasculitis and can be applied without biopsy when clinical and serologic data are concordant[4]. Separately, the 2021 ACR/Vasculitis Foundation guidelines support treatment initiation without biopsy in the setting of positive ANCA serology and compatible clinical features[5].

We present a patient with 20 years of documented eosinophilia diagnosed only after progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and severe cardiomyopathy, illustrating diagnostic delay and a cognitive pattern we term “diabetic attribution bias”.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 53-year-old woman, a never-smoker, with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and no documented asthma, sinusitis, or nasal polyps was admitted in April 2026 with shortness of breath and chest pain. Peripheral neuropathy had been attributed to diabetes without nerve conduction studies (NCS) or electromyography (EMG) (Fig. 1).

Phase 1: prodromal phase (2006–2023)

The earliest documented eosinophilia dates to March 2006 (absolute eosinophil count [AEC] 1.73 ×103/μl) (Table 1). The patient had no documented comorbidities, establishing that eosinophilia predated all conditions later used to explain her organ damage. Socioeconomic barriers (limited English proficiency, no insurance, no primary care physician) were documented.

Eosinophilia persisted in March 2013 (AEC 2.1 ×103/μl) with normal renal function (creatinine 0.6 mg/dl) and abnormal urine sediment (protein 2+, blood 3+, red blood cells [RBC] 3–5/HPF, white blood cells [WBC] 0–3/high-power field [HPF]), with proteinuria and haematuria suggesting early glomerular involvement. By December 2023, creatinine had risen to 3.5 mg/dl (glomerular filtration rate [GFR] 15.2) with persistent eosinophilia (AEC up to 2.44 ×103/μl), nephrotic-range proteinuria (protein-to-creatinine ratio 10.1 g/g), and a glomerulonephritic sediment (>50 RBC/HPF). Nephrology attributed the decline to diabetic nephropathy despite these atypical features; ANCA testing was not performed (Fig. 1).

Phase 2: eosinophilic phase (2024)

In March 2024, the patient was admitted with cough, dyspnoea, and wheezing. Computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest showed bilateral lower lobe infiltrates with nodular components (Fig. 2A). Bone marrow biopsy showed a hypercellular marrow with a reactive eosinophilic process, excluding clonal eosinophilic neoplasms (e.g., FIP1L1-PDGFRA–associated disease). Echocardiography showed ejection fraction (EF) 55–60%; stress testing showed an EF of 70% (Fig. 3).

By May 2024, the patient had progressed to ESRD requiring peritoneal dialysis (creatinine 10.7 mg/dl). C-reactive protein (CRP) was markedly elevated at 280 mg/l (reference <10 mg/l), consistent with severe systemic inflammation. CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated large bilateral pleural and pericardial effusions (Fig. 2B). Methylprednisolone 40 mg intravenously (IV) every 12 hours suppressed peripheral eosinophils to undetectable levels within 48 hours. Thoracentesis yielded an exudative effusion with neutrophilic predominance (72% polymorphonuclear neutrophils); the exudative character was inconsistent with the clinical attribution to volume overload. The absence of eosinophils in the fluid likely reflected concurrent steroid-induced suppression. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) was negative. ANCA testing was not ordered.

After steroid discontinuation, eosinophilia rebounded to its highest level (AEC 2.7×103/μl) by August 2024, with active glomerulonephritic sediment (protein 3+, blood 3+, >50 RBC/HPF, 10–20 WBC/HPF). The rising urinary WBC count: from 0–3/HPF (December 2023) to 10–20/HPF, suggested increasing glomerular inflammation.

Phase 3: vasculitic phase (2025–2026)

Progressive cardiac deterioration ensued. High-sensitivity troponin I peaked at 91.12 ng/l (April 2025), then declined to 36.5–38.9 ng/l (August 2025), consistent with acute eosinophilic myocarditis transitioning to chronic fibrotic cardiomyopathy[6]. The EF fell from 55–60% at rest (March 2024) to 25% (April 2025). Left heart catheterization revealed only minimal coronary disease. An electrocardiogram (EKG) showed prolonged QTc (477 ms). B-type natriuretic peptide rose from 351 pg/ml (May 2024) to >5,000 pg/ml (March 2026). CT imaging demonstrated migratory pulmonary infiltrates (Fig. 2C), with right middle lobe consolidation replacing the prior bilateral lower lobe disease and complete resolution of pleural and pericardial effusions. Serial peritoneal fluid analyses showed rising eosinophilia from 8 to 22% over four steroid-free months, inconsistent with idiopathic peritoneal dialysis (PD)-associated eosinophilic peritonitis. Eosinophilic involvement of all three serous cavities (pleural, pericardial, peritoneal) was never linked to a unifying diagnosis.

On the April 2026 admission, urinalysis showed the most severe findings in the series (protein 4+, blood 4+, >50 RBC/HPF, 10–20 WBC/HPF), documenting progressive worsening across all three phases.

Echocardiography confirmed an EFof 20–24% with diffuse hypokinesis, elevated filling pressures (E/e’ 24), and decreasing wall thickness (IVS 1.4 → 1.2 cm)

ANCA testing was first ordered on April 30, 2026; the first time in the patient’s documented medical history[3]. Results showed ANCA IIF positivity at 1:160 with MPO-ANCA >8.0 units (reference 0.0–0.9). PR3-ANCA was negative. ANA was negative. Total IgE was 935 IU/ml. The clinical diagnosis of EGPA was established: MPO-ANCA positivity combined with glomerulonephritis, non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, migratory pulmonary infiltrates, eosinophilic serositis, and peripheral neuropathy. ACR/EULAR weighted scoring yielded ≥6 points (Table 2) (+5 for AEC ≥1 ×109/l, +2 for extravascular eosinophilic-predominant inflammation [based on peritoneal fluid eosinophilia up to 22% during steroid-free intervals], −1 for haematuria), meeting the threshold without asthma or nasal polyps[4]; the +1 for mononeuritis multiplex was not scored as no NCS/EMG had been performed. Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) was excluded by MPO-ANCA >8× ULN (essentially absent in primary HES), bone marrow excluding clonal disorder, and active glomerulonephritic sediment characteristic of pauci-immune glomerulonephritis[1]. Drug-induced vasculitis (hydralazine) was excluded by negative ANA, single-specificity MPO-ANCA, and eosinophilia predating hydralazine by 20 years.

The revised (2011) Five-Factor Score (FFS) was 3 (cardiac involvement +1, renal insufficiency +1, absence of ear, nose and throat manifestations +1), indicating poor prognosis. Using the original (1996) FFS, a score ≥2 was associated with approximately 40% five-year mortality[5,7].

Remission induction with glucocorticoids plus cyclophosphamide or rituximab was planned per ACR/Vasculitis Foundation guidelines[5]. Anti-IL-5 agents (mepolizumab, benralizumab) may serve as adjunctive or maintenance therapy[5,8].

Approximately four weeks after ANCA results were reported, the patient was admitted to a different facility within the same hospital system for symptomatic anaemia (haemoglobin 7.2 g/dl) with persistent gross haematuria. Peripheral eosinophilia persisted at 14.1%. No immunosuppressive therapy had been initiated.

DISCUSSION

This case documents a 20-year prodromal-to-vasculitic phase transition (Table 1), quantifying irreversible organ damage: creatinine 0.6 mg/dl and EF 60% to ESRD and EF 20–24% (Fig. 3).

Notably, renal involvement is associated with shorter time to AAV diagnosis (HR 1.65, p = 0.004)[2], yet here it was present for over a decade without prompting ANCA testing, likely because diabetic attribution bias overrode the diagnostic signal renal disease typically provides.

We propose the term “diabetic attribution bias” to describe the reflexive attribution of organ damage to diabetes when clinical features are atypical. This operated at two levels. First, renal decline was attributed to diabetic nephropathy despite a glomerulonephritic sediment more consistent with pauci-immune glomerulonephritis; the progressive rise in urinary WBCs further supports active inflammatory glomerulonephritis rather than the relatively acellular sediment of diabetic nephropathy. The serial HbA1c trajectory (7.9% → 6.0% → 5.6%) likely reflects a reduced erythrocyte lifespan in ESRD; regardless, the sediment pattern was inconsistent with diabetic glomerulosclerosis (Fig. 3). Second, peripheral neuropathy was attributed to diabetes without NCS/EMG. Peripheral neuropathy, the most common non-pulmonary manifestation, affects 50–75% of EGPA patients[1,9].

MPO-ANCA positivity is an independent risk factor; sural nerve biopsy characteristically shows epineurial vascular destruction with asymmetric axonal loss distinct from diabetic neuropathy[9]. Had NCS/EMG been performed, an atypical pattern could have triggered earlier ANCA testing.

Corticosteroid masking was evident: peritoneal fluid showed 22% eosinophils during steroid-free intervals but none during steroid-exposed admissions, a recognized diagnostic pitfall[1].

While asthma is present in >90% of EGPA cases, ANCA-positive patients less commonly present with prominent asthma[1], and the 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria do not require it for classification[4]. However, spirometry was never performed, so absent versus undiagnosed asthma cannot be resolved.

Seven opportunities for earlier diagnosis were identified across five specialties over 20 years (Fig. 1). Contributing factors included diabetic attribution bias, premature dismissal of vasculitis without ANCA testing; a pattern consistent with the finding by Bussmann et al. that prior pulmonology consultation paradoxically prolonged time to AAV diagnosis (HR 0.58, p = 0.003)[2], corticosteroid masking, misattribution of exudative effusions to volume overload, fragmented care, and socioeconomic barriers.

Limitations include the absence of renal biopsy (precluded by advanced chronic kidney disease), cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, NCS/EMG, sural nerve biopsy, and histologic evaluation of a gastroesophageal junction nodule (endoscopy aborted due to desaturation). The 7-year and 10-year gaps between early data points limit prodromal phase precision. Treatment response data are not yet available.

This case underscores the imperative for systematic ANCA testing in patients with unexplained eosinophilia and organ involvement, as recommended by the 2020 International Consensus on ANCA Testing and the 2024 European red flags consensus[3,10].
