Resolution of two conjectures by Erdős and Hall concerning separable numbers
Stijn Cambie, Wouter van Doorn

Abstract
Erdős and Hall defined a pair ( m , n ) (m,n) of positive integers to be interlocking, if between any pair of consecutive divisors (both larger than 1 1 ) of n n (resp. m m ) there is a divisor of m m (resp. n n ). A positive integer is said to be separable if it belongs to an interlocking pair. We prove that the lower density of separable powers of two is positive, as well as the lower density of powers of two which are not separable. Finally, we prove that the number of interlocking pairs whose product is equal to the product of the first primes, is finite. We hereby resolve two conjectures by Erdős and Hall.

Introduction

Following the terminology of Erdős and Hall
[
3
]
, we say that two positive integers
m
and
n

interlock
if between every two divisors of
n
(both larger than
1
) there exists a divisor of
m
, and between every two divisors of
m
(both larger than
1
) there exists a divisor of
n
. If this holds, then we call
(m,n)
an interlocking pair. For example,
(63,64)
is an interlocking pair, as we can order their divisors as follows:

1=1<2<3<4<7<8<9<16<21<32<63<64,

and note that the divisors of
63
and
64
alternate here.

A positive integer
n
is defined to be
separable
, if a positive integer
m
exists such that
m
and
n
interlock. In particular, both
63
and
64
are separable, which incidentally contradicts the claim in
[
3
]
that
2^{k}
cannot be separable if
k+1\geq 5
is prime. The reason that
2^{k}
can interlock with an integer
m
, even if
k+1
is prime, is that it is possible for the number of divisors of
2^{k}
and
m
to differ by
1
, see
1
.

In any case, denote with
A(x)
the number of separable
n\leq x
. In
[
3
]
, Erdős and Hall stated that they could not settle whether
A(x)=o(x)
holds, and this still seems like the most important open problem regarding separable numbers. On the other hand, they did manage to prove the lower bound
A(x)>\frac{cx}{\log\log x}
, for some absolute constant
c>0
and all large enough
x
.
As a positive integer seems more likely to be separable if its divisors are spread out, Erdős and Hall then conjectured that
n=2^{k}
is separable for almost all
k
. They furthermore conjectured that
m
and
n
do not interlock if
mn
is the product of the first
k
primes and
k
is large enough. In this paper we refute the first conjecture (see Theorem
2
and Theorem
6
), and confirm the second one (see Theorem
9
).

Main results and proofs

Let
\tau(n)
be the number of divisors of
n
, and let
d_{2}(n)
denote the smallest prime divisor of
n
. We then start off with a lemma on interlocking pairs that quickly follows from their definition.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(m,n)
be an interlocking pair with
d_{2}(n)<d_{2}(m)
. If
n<m
, then
\tau(m)=\tau(n)
, and if
n>m
, then
\tau(m)=\tau(n)-1
.

Proof.

Let the divisors of
m
be
1=d_{1}<d_{2}<\ldots<d_{\tau(m)}=m
, and let the divisors of
n
be
1=d^{\prime}_{1}<d^{\prime}_{2}<\ldots<d^{\prime}_{\tau(n)}=n.

If
n<m,

then
d^{\prime}_{2}<d_{2}<d^{\prime}_{3}<d_{3}<\ldots<d^{\prime}_{\tau(n)}<d_{\tau(m)},
implying
\tau(m)=\tau(n)
.
And if
n>m,

then
d^{\prime}_{2}<d_{2}<d^{\prime}_{3}<d_{3}<\ldots<d_{\tau(m)}<d^{\prime}_{\tau(n)},
implying
\tau(m)=\tau(n)-1
.
∎

In
[
3
]
it is conjectured that
2^{k}
is separable for almost all
k
. However, as it turns out, the lower density of
k
for which
2^{k}
is not separable, is positive.

Theorem 2
.

For all
k>2
with
k\equiv 1,2,9,10\pmod{12}
, we have that
n=2^{k}
is not separable.

Proof.

By contradiction; assume that
m
and
n=2^{k}
interlock, for some
k\equiv 1,2,9,10\pmod{12}
with
k>2
. Write
m=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{e_{i}}
and recall that we have
\tau(m)=\prod_{i}(e_{i}+1).
We will then deal with
\tau(m)
odd and even separately.

If
\tau(m)
is odd, then
e_{i}
must be even for all
i
, so that
m
is a square. Since
3
is the only integer between
2
and
4
, and
5,7
are the only odd integers between
4
and
8
, we then see that either
15^{2}\mid m
or
21^{2}\mid m
. However,
15^{2}
has the two divisors
9
and
15
which are both between
8
and
16
, while
21^{2}
has the two divisors
49
and
63
which are both between
32
and
64
; a contradiction in either case.

If
\tau(m)
is even, we apply
1
. Since
\tau(n)=k+1
and
d_{2}(n)<d_{2}(m)
, we then obtain
\tau(m)\in\{k,k+1\}.
And because
\tau(m)
is assumed even and
k\equiv 1,2,9,10\pmod{12}
, we get
\tau(m)\equiv 2,10\pmod{12}
. This implies that
\frac{\tau(m)}{2}\geq\frac{k}{2}>1
is not divisible by
2,3
or
4
, so that
e_{i}\geq 4
for all but at most one
i
. We therefore deduce that
m
is divisible by either
a_{1}=3^{4}\cdot 5,a_{2}=3^{4}\cdot 7,a_{3}=3\cdot 5^{4}
or
a_{4}=3\cdot 7^{4}
. Let us deal with them in order.

The integer
a_{1}
is divisible by
9
and
15
,
a_{2}
is divisible by
21
and
27
, and
a_{3}
is divisible by
75
and
125
, none of which are allowed. Finally, if
a_{4}
divides
m
, then
m
needs a further odd divisor
d
with
8<d<16
. If
d=9
, then
m
is divisible by
49
and
63
. If
d\in\{11,13\}
, then
m
is divisible by
3d
and
49
. And if
d=15
, then
m
is divisible by
5
and
7
. Since every case leads to two divisors in between two consecutive powers of two, this finishes the proof.
∎

On the other hand, the lower density of
k
such that
2^{k}
is separable, is also positive. Before we formally state and prove this, let us first recall the following two results from
[
2
, Prop. 6.8]
and
[
1
]
on prime gaps.

Lemma 3
.

For all
N\geq 2^{2^{5}}
, there exists a prime in the interval
\left(N,N\left(1+\frac{1}{25\log^{2}N}\right)\right]
. Moreover, for all
N\geq 2^{2^{50}}
, there exists a prime in the interval
(N,N+3N^{2/3}].

Secondly, we will also need the following result from
[
4
, Thm. 3]
.

Lemma 4
.

There exists an absolute constant
c
such that for all
x,y,z
with
2\leq y\leq z\leq x
there are at most
\frac{cx\log y}{\log z}
positive integers
n\leq x
which do not have a divisor in the interval
[y,z]
.

Now, define
t=\max(5+3\log c,50)
with
c
as in
4
. Furthermore define
C=\log 2\cdot 2^{t-2}
, and let
S
be the set of positive integers
n
with the property that if
1=d_{1}<\ldots<d_{\tau(n)}=n
are the divisors of
n
, then
d_{j}\leq e^{\max(C,d_{j-1})}
for all
j
with
2\leq j\leq\tau(n)
. Finally, for a given integer
x
, let
S(x)
be the set of integers
n\leq x
with
n\in S
. We then claim that
S
contains more than half of all positive integers.

Lemma 5
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\left\lvert S(x)\right\rvert>\frac{x}{2}
.

Proof.

We may, by the definition of
S(x)
, without loss of generality assume
x\geq e^{C}
. So with
l
defined as
\left\lfloor\log_{2}\log_{C}\log x\right\rfloor
, we then get
l\geq 0
. We furthermore define
y_{i}=\exp(C^{2^{i-1}})
and
z_{i}=\exp(C^{2^{i}})
for all
i
with
0\leq i\leq l
. By
4
, the number of positive integers
n\leq x
for which an
i
exists such that
n
does not have a divisor in the interval
[y_{i},z_{i}]
, is at most
\frac{cx\log y_{0}}{\log z_{0}}+\ldots+\frac{cx\log y_{l}}{\log z_{l}}
. Applying
t=\max(5+3\log c,50)
, we will bound this sum by using the lower bound
C=\log 2\cdot 2^{t-2}\geq\max\left(8\log 2\cdot 2^{3\log c},\log 2\cdot 2^{48}\right)>\max\left(5c^{2},100\right)
.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{l}\frac{cx\log y_{i}}{\log z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{l}\frac{cx}{C^{2^{i-1}}}

\displaystyle<cx\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}+\frac{1}{C}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{C^{2i}}\right)

\displaystyle=cx\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}+\frac{1}{C}+\frac{1}{C^{2}-1}\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{10cx}{9\sqrt{C}}

\displaystyle<\frac{x}{2}.

Here, the final two inequalities use
C>100
and
C>5c^{2}
respectively. In other words, the number of
n\leq x
such that
n
has a divisor in the interval
[y_{i},z_{i}]
for every
i
, is more than
\frac{x}{2}
.

Now, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
n
indeed has a divisor in all intervals
[y_{i},z_{i}]
, then we in particular get that for consecutive divisors
d_{j-1},d_{j}
of
n
, either
d_{j}\leq z_{0}=e^{C}
, or there exists an
i\geq 0
such that
y_{i}\leq d_{j-1}<d_{j}\leq z_{i+1}
. In the second case we have

\displaystyle\log\log d_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\log\log z_{i+1}

\displaystyle=2^{i+1}\log C

\displaystyle<C^{2^{i-1}}

\displaystyle=\log y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\log d_{j-1}.

Or, in other words,
d_{j}<e^{d_{j-1}}
. In either case we have
d_{j}\leq e^{\max(C,d_{j-1})}
, so we conclude
n\in S(x).

∎

The above lemma will help us show that the lower density of
k
such that
2^{k}
is separable, is positive.

Theorem 6
.

If
k\in 2^{t}S=\{k:k=2^{t}k^{\prime}\text{ for some }k^{\prime}\in S\}
, then
2^{k}
is separable. In particular, the lower density of the set of positive integers
k
for which
2^{k}
is separable, is at least
\frac{1}{2^{t+1}}
.

Proof.

For an integer
k\in 2^{t}S
, let
\prod_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}+1)
be the prime factorisation of
k
. That is,
e_{i}+1
is prime for all
i
,
e_{i}\leq e_{i+1}
for all
i
,
e_{i}=1
for all
i\leq t
, and
k=\prod_{i}(e_{i}+1)
. For all
i
with
4\leq i\leq r
, define
n_{i}
to be
2^{(e_{1}+1)\cdots(e_{i-1}+1)}
, and let
p_{i}
be the smallest prime larger than
n_{i}
. Then we will prove that
m=231\prod_{i=4}^{r}p_{i}^{e_{i}}
interlocks with
n=2^{k}
.

More precisely, let
1=d_{1}<\ldots<d_{8}=231
be the divisors of
231=3\cdot 7\cdot 11
, let
2^{d}>1
be a divisor of
n
, and write
d=c_{0}+\sum_{i=4}^{r}c_{i}(e_{1}+1)\cdots(e_{i-1}+1)
with
0\leq c_{0}\leq 7
and
0\leq c_{i}\leq e_{i}
for all
i\geq 4
.
1
1

1

It follows by induction that it is indeed possible to write
d
in such a way.
With the divisor
d^{\prime}:=d_{c_{0}+1}\prod_{i=4}^{r}p_{i}^{c_{i}}
of
m
, we will then show
2^{d}<d^{\prime}<2^{d+1}
. Since
\tau(m)=8\prod_{i=4}^{r}(e_{i}+1)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}+1)=k=\tau(n)-1
, we see that such a
d^{\prime}
would be unique and the proof would be finished.

Because
2^{c_{0}}\leq d_{c_{0}+1}
for all
c_{0}
with
0\leq c_{0}\leq 7
, and
n_{i}^{c_{i}}<p_{i}^{c_{i}}
by definition of

, the inequality
2^{d}<d^{\prime}
quickly follows. As for the inequality

, we need some upper bounds.

Claim 7
.

For all
i
with
t+1\leq i\leq r
, we have
e_{i}\leq n_{i}^{1/4}.

Proof.

As
C=\log 2\cdot 2^{t-2}
, we first of all get the estimate
e^{C}=2^{2^{t-2}}=n_{t+1}^{1/4}\leq n_{i}^{1/4}.
Secondly, with
D=(e_{t+1}+1)\cdots(e_{i-1}+1)
, we have
e^{D}<2^{2D}=\left(2^{8D}\right)^{1/4}<n_{i}^{1/4}
as
2^{t}>8
. Now, either
e_{i}\leq D<e^{D}\leq n_{i}^{1/4}
, or
e_{i}>D
. But in the latter case, note that both
D
and
e_{i}+1
divide
2^{-t}k
. As
D
is defined as the product of all prime divisors of
2^{-t}k
which are smaller than
e_{i}+1
, we then see that
D
and
e_{i}+1
must actually be consecutive divisors of
2^{-t}k
. And we then get
e_{i}+1\leq e^{\max(C,D)}\leq n_{i}^{1/4}
, since
2^{-t}k\in S
. ∎

By combining
7
with
3
we can show an upper bound on

.

Claim 8
.

For all
i
with
4\leq i\leq r
, we have
\left(\frac{p_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)^{e_{i}}<e^{1/4^{i}}.

Proof.

Recall that
e_{i}=1
for all
i\leq t
. Now, with
p_{4}=257
and
p_{5}=65537
one can check that the lemma holds for
i\in\{4,5\}
. For
i\geq 6
we apply
3
, from which we in particular get that there is a prime in the interval
\left(n_{i},n_{i}\left(1+\frac{1}{25\log^{2}n_{i}}\right)\right].
Since
\log(n_{i})=\frac{\log 2}{2}\cdot 2^{i}
if
i\leq t
, while
\frac{25\log^{2}2}{4}>1
and
1+\frac{1}{4^{i}}<e^{1/4^{i}}
, the claim follows for
6\leq i\leq t
. As for
t+1\leq i\leq r
, we know by
t\geq 50
that there is actually a prime in the interval
\left(n_{i},n_{i}+3n_{i}^{2/3}\right]
. This gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)^{e_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+3n_{i}^{-1/3}\right)^{e_{i}}

\displaystyle<e^{3e_{i}n_{i}^{-1/3}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{3n_{i}^{-1/12}}

\displaystyle<e^{1/4^{i}}.

The second to last inequality follows from
7
. As for the final inequality, note that we have
i+1<\frac{1}{12}\cdot 2^{i-2}
by
i>t>9
. We therefore see
3\cdot 4^{i}<4^{i+1}<2^{2^{i-1}/12}\leq n_{i}^{1/12}.

∎

Now, one can check that
\frac{d_{c_{0}+1}}{2^{c_{0}+1}}<\frac{10}{11}
holds for all
c_{0}\leq 7
. We moreover have the upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{p_{4}^{c_{4}}\cdots p_{r}^{c_{r}}}{n_{4}^{c_{4}}\cdots n_{r}^{c_{r}}}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=4}^{r}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)^{e_{i}}

\displaystyle<\prod_{i=4}^{\infty}e^{1/4^{i}}

\displaystyle=e^{1/192}

\displaystyle<\frac{11}{10}.

We therefore conclude
\frac{d^{\prime}}{2^{d+1}}=\frac{d_{c_{0}+1}p_{4}^{c_{4}}\cdots p_{r}^{c_{r}}}{2^{c_{0}+1}n_{4}^{c_{4}}\cdots n_{r}^{c_{r}}}<1.

∎

For our final result, define
P_{k}
to be the product of the first
k
primes. If
k>1
is odd, then the product
mn
of an interlocking pair cannot be equal to
P_{k}
by
1
, as
\left\lvert\tau(m)-\tau(n)\right\rvert\geq 2^{\frac{k-1}{2}}>1
in that case. However, even if
k
is even, Erdős and Hall conjectured that the product
mn
of an interlocking pair cannot be equal to

, if
k
is large enough. This turns out to be true.

Theorem 9
.

If
(m,n)
is an interlocking pair with
mn=P_{k}
, then
k\leq 8.

Proof.

Let
k
be larger than
8
and assume without loss of generality that
m
is divisible by
2
. This implies
3\mid n
and
5\mid m
, which (due to the divisor
10
of
m
) in turn implies that
7
and
11
must divide
n
. Continuing this process gives
p\mid m
for
p\in\{2,5,13,19,23\}
and
q\mid n
for
q\in\{3,7,11,17\}
. However, we now reach a contradiction as
23
and
26
are both divisors of
m
, while
n
cannot be a multiple of
24
or
25
.
∎

As Erdős and Hall note, for even
k\leq 8,
it is possible for an interlocking pair
(m,n)
to have

, by taking

and

to be the product of the first
\frac{k}{2}

\{2,5,13,19\}
and
\{3,7,11,17\}
respectively.
