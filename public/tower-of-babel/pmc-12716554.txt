Workplace sexual harassment and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms in nurses: a cross-sectional study in southeast Iran
Authors: Radhwan Hussein Ibrahim, Abdulhakeem Jamil Ahmed, Maryam Zeighami, Samaneh Behzadi Fard, Mahlagha Dehghan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine the association between workplace sexual harassment and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms among nurses in southeast Iran.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingFour teaching hospitals affiliated with Kerman University of Medical Sciences, Iran.ParticipantsA total of 283 female nurses with at least 1 year of clinical experience participated in the study.MethodsData were collected using convenience sampling and assessed using a demographic questionnaire, the Nurses’ Sexual Harassment Questionnaire, and the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R). Non-parametric tests, Spearman’s rho correlation and multivariate logistic regression were used for analysis.ResultsOverall, 39.1% of nurses reported experiencing sexual harassment sometimes to always. A total of 13.4% screened positive for PTSD. Sexual harassment was significantly associated with PTSD symptoms (OR: 1.26; 95% CI 1.164 to 1.364). Younger, single and less experienced nurses reported higher harassment levels, though sociodemographic variables were not associated with PTSD.ConclusionsWorkplace sexual harassment was significantly associated with increased PTSD symptoms among nurses. While demographic factors were related to harassment exposure, they were not linked to PTSD. Findings highlight the importance of preventive organisational strategies to mitigate harassment and protect nurses’ mental well-being.Keywords: Nurses, Health, Job Satisfaction

Introduction

Over the past few decades, sexual harassment has become a widespread issue that affects various professions, including healthcare workers.1 Nurses, in particular, are frequently subjected to offensive sexual behaviour in their workplace,2 making sexual harassment an undeniable international phenomenon and a serious problem.3 Such behaviours, which can be verbal, physical, psychological or visual in nature and are often repeated and unwanted, can be humiliating, insulting and threatening to the victim’s health.4 5 In the nursing profession, sexual harassment is defined as inappropriate sexual comments made directly or indirectly to nurses that threaten their ability to perform their duties.6 Research indicates that sexual harassment is a widespread problem in clinical settings, with 30%–72% of nurses reporting such experiences.3 7 8 However, the true incidence of sexual harassment may be even higher than reported due to under-reporting.9 The high incidence of sexual harassment among nurses can be attributed in part to the fact that nursing is a female-dominated profession. This can make female nurses more vulnerable to sexual harassment, especially when they are interacting with patients or their companions who use drugs or alcohol. Other factors are the absence of comprehensive legal protections for nurses in many jurisdictions and the abuse of power by male doctors, who may use their position of authority to exploit female nurses.10

Sexual harassment of nurses can cause shame and humiliation, impair their ability to perform their job properly and reduce their overall quality of work life. It also harms victims’ well-being and can reduce or lose appropriate nursing care, which adversely affects patient care.11,13 A 2020 study by Maghraby showed that over half of nurses in Egypt (58.1%) had been exposed to at least one form of sexual harassment, often verbal and gestural.14 Many incidents of workplace sexual harassment are never reported due to several factors, including cultural norms, negative consequences, pressure from colleagues, the victim’s reluctance to report due to a sense of futility or disillusionment with the reporting process, or a lack of knowledge about available policies and guidelines.15 16 Unfortunately, this reluctance to report only exacerbates the problem, creating a serious occupational hazard for nurses and leading to serious complications and disorders.11 17

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one of the mental health disorders that can result from sexual harassment.18 PTSD is characterised by re-experiencing traumatic events, avoidance behaviour, negative changes in thoughts and feelings, and hyperarousal.19 It is a major concern in public health and mental health and can have long-term emotional, social and financial consequences.20 The American Psychiatric Association notes that PTSD can develop in individuals who have experienced, witnessed or been threatened by traumatic events such as natural disasters, serious traumas, acts of terrorism, war or sexual assault.21 Studies show that 36% of rape survivors experience lifelong PTSD.22 Research by Malik and Farooqi also found that workplace sexual harassment is a significant predictor of PTSD symptoms.23 This finding is supported by a 2022 study by Hansen et al, which indicated that sexual harassment among social educators increases their risk of developing PTSD symptoms.24

Sexual harassment is a critical concern for organisations, especially in the healthcare sector where nurses are at an increased risk of experiencing such behaviour. The negative impact of sexual harassment extends beyond the individual victim and can have serious consequences for patient care and the healthcare organisation as a whole. In addition, sexual harassment can cause psychological and occupational problems for nurses. The nursing profession is critical to providing high-quality care to patients, but nurses themselves are at risk of experiencing mental and emotional injuries that can undermine the quality of their work and create tension and interpersonal problems. Given the high prevalence of PTSD and the potential impact of sexual harassment on nurses’ mental health, this study investigated the relationship between sexual harassment and PTSD among nurses.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association of sexual harassment with PTSD in nurses. The study population consisted of nurses working in teaching hospitals affiliated with Kerman University of Medical Sciences in southeastern Iran, including Shafa, Afzalipour, Shahid Beheshti and Shahid Bahonar hospitals. The data were collected in 2022.

Sample size and sampling

The study used convenience sampling to select all eligible nurses who met the inclusion criteria. To be included in the study, nurses had to have at least a bachelor’s degree, a minimum of 1 year of clinical work experience and be willing to participate.

Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2.), a power analysis was conducted to determine the required sample size. The calculation, based on a power of 80%, a significance level of p=0.05, number of groups=1 and number of measurements=1, indicated that 273 participants would be needed to detect a correlation coefficient of 0.15. Nearly 350 nurses were asked to participate in the study; of those, 316 agreed to complete the questionnaire. Of the 316 questionnaires which were collected from the nurses, 283 (89.5%) were fully completed and used for the final analysis. The non-response rate was 10.5% (n=33), mostly because the questionnaires were incomplete. Other reasons for the low response may include time constraints during shifts, reluctance to answer questions about sensitive topics (eg, sexual harassment) and issues with returning the physical questionnaires.

Measurement

A demographic information questionnaire, the Nurse’s Sexual Harassment Questionnaire, and the Impact of Event Scale (IES) were used to collect data.

The demographic information questionnaire included age, gender, marital status, education, shift work, department and work experience.

The Nurse’s Sexual Harassment Questionnaire, a 15-item scale, was used in this study. It is divided into two parts: latent sexual harassment (items 1–9) and manifest sexual harassment (items 10–15). Responses were measured on a 5-point scale: never (0), rarely (1), sometimes (2), often (3) and always (4). There were no inverse items. The minimum score of zero indicated no experience of sexual harassment, while the maximum score of 60 suggested a higher level of sexual harassment experience.25 26 Zeighami et al previously assessed the validity and reliability of the questionnaire. The questionnaire showed acceptable face validity, content validity and convergent validity. The exploratory factor analysis confirmed the bifactor structure of the questionnaire. The questionnaire also demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient=0.944, omega coefficient=0.945 and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)=0.917).25 26 The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the current study was 0.88.

Horowitz et al initially developed the IES as a diagnostic tool for PTSD, even though the criteria for the disorder had not yet been established in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, third edition (DSM-III).27 Marmar and Weiss designed the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R), a self-report questionnaire, to reflect the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth edition (DSM-IV).28 The scale, which assesses psychological symptoms following a traumatic event, consists of 22 items and three subscales: intrusion (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 16, 20), avoidance (5, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 17, 22) and hyperarousal (4, 10, 14, 15, 18, 19, 21). Scores are based on a five-point Likert scale (0=not at all, 4=extremely), yielding a total score from 0 to 88. A score of 33 or higher is considered a positive screen for PTSD due to its high diagnostic accuracy.29 Marmar and Weiss reported that the IES-R possesses strong psychometric properties, including good internal consistency, test-retest reliability and acceptable validity indices.30 The Persian version was developed by Kerman University of Medical Sciences faculty members, who performed translation, back-translation and psychometric validation.

Ten faculty members of Razi School of Nursing and Midwifery evaluated the content validity, confirming its appropriateness. To assess the reliability of the scale, we calculated its internal consistency on a sample of 200 individuals. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87, with subscale alphas of 0.8 (intrusion), 0.79 (avoidance) and 0.73 (hyperarousal), indicating acceptable reliability for the Persian version of IES-R.31 In the present study, we found an even higher overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93.

Data collection

The study was conducted following the receipt of the code of ethics and an official letter of introduction from Kerman University of Medical Sciences. The main researcher visited the relevant hospitals, including Shafa, Afzalipour, Shahid Beheshti and Shahid Bahonar. After identifying eligible nurses, the researcher explained the study’s objectives to them and obtained their informed consent. The nurses were then asked to complete the questionnaires carefully. All participants were assured that their responses would remain anonymous, and their information would be kept confidential and used solely for research purposes. To collect data, the researcher visited the hospitals in the morning, evening and night shifts. The sampling process was conducted in August, September and October 2021.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS V.20 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Questionnaires with more than 10% unanswered items were excluded during data cleaning to ensure analytical validity. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean and SD) were used to summarise the participants’ demographic characteristics, sexual harassment and PTSD scores. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normal distribution of sexual harassment and PTSD scores. As the scores did not follow a normal distribution, we used Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests to compare the mean scores of sexual harassment and PTSD based on qualitative variables. Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the association of sexual harassment with PTSD scores. Furthermore, a multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify the correlates of PTSD. A significance level was considered p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

None. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

All 283 participants were female nurses. Their average age was 32.91 years (SD=6.84, range 23–53). On average, they had 8.92 years (SD=6.43) of work experience ranging from 1 to 29 years. Regarding age groups, 36.8% were under 30 years old, 48.9% were between 30 and 40, and 14.3% were over 40. The majority were married (60.3%), while 35.5% were single, and 4.3% reported ‘other’ marital status. In terms of education, 81.1% had a bachelor’s degree, and 18.9% had a master’s degree. The majority worked as staff nurses (95.8%), with a small proportion serving as head nurses or supervisors (4.2%). Most nurses worked rotating shifts (92.2%), while 7.8% worked fixed shifts. In terms of work experience, 43.8% had 5 years or less, 18.8% had between 6 and 10 years, and 37.3% had more than 10 years of experience. The largest proportion worked in critical care units (25.8%), followed by emergency departments (20.5%), psychiatric wards (7.1%) and other wards (33.9%), while data on ward placement were missing for 12.7% of participants (table 1).

Associations between sociodemographic variables, sexual harassment, and PTSD

Group comparison analyses using Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests showed significant differences in sexual harassment scores across age groups (p=0.04), marital status (p=0.03) and work experience categories (p=0.04). For example, single nurses had a higher mean harassment score (6.17±5.81) compared with married nurses (4.88±5.70). These results indicate mean differences between groups and should not be interpreted as regression effects. Specifically, younger and less experienced nurses reported higher levels of sexual harassment than their older and more experienced counterparts. Moreover, married nurses reported experiencing less sexual harassment compared with single or unmarried nurses/others (p<0.05). Spearman’s rho correlation test showed a significant positive association between continuous harassment and PTSD scores (r=0.64, p<0.001). Associations between these continuous variables and categorical sociodemographic factors were assessed using Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests. No statistically significant differences in PTSD scores were observed across sociodemographic categories (table 3). To conduct further analysis on the correlates of PTSD, variables with p<0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic model, including age, shift and sexual harassment. The results indicated that only sexual harassment was significantly associated with a higher risk of PTSD among the participants (table 4).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between sexual harassment and PTSD among Iranian nurses. Overall, our findings indicated that the participating nurses reported low levels of sexual harassment and PTSD. We also observed significant associations between sexual harassment and age, marital status and work experience, with younger, single and less experienced nurses experiencing higher rates of sexual harassment.

Our results regarding demographic factors align with previous research. Specifically, Kagan et al in Israel and Somani et al in Pakistan reported that a nurse’s age and years of work experience influenced the frequency of sexual harassment they experienced, with younger nurses and those with less experience more likely to report harassment.32 33 Similarly, Maghraby et al in Egypt found that less work experience, single status and working in open units were associated with higher rates of sexual harassment in the workplace.14 These findings reinforce our results, suggesting that novice nurses may be particularly vulnerable to sexual harassment.

Gender differences in sexual harassment could not be assessed in our study due to its focus on female nurses. Sexual harassment is often perceived as a woman’s issue, based on the misconception that women are more vulnerable than men.34 However, evidence from other contexts indicates a more complex picture. Kim et al in Korea found that most sexual harassment victims were male, although women in their 20s experienced significantly more harassment than men in the same age group.35 Studies in Greece reported high levels of harassment among male nurses, with approximately 40% experiencing sexual harassment.36 37 Papantoniou also found that Greek female nurses were more likely than male nurses to report harassment at least once in their career.38 In the USA, McClain et al identified being female as a significant predictor of the number of sexual harassment incidents among medical students.39 Nonetheless, male victimisation may be underestimated due to social norms that discourage men from seeking support or reporting harassment.35 These findings highlight the importance of recognising and addressing sexual harassment among male nurses, particularly in healthcare settings.

The present study identified the most common forms of sexual harassment among participants as ‘staring at you in a sexually suggestive manner,’ ‘standing too close or invading personal space’ and ‘repeatedly requesting contact information.’ Similar patterns were observed in studies conducted in Egypt, where harassment included ‘calling with nicknames or using terms like beautiful, honey, etc.,’ ‘showing sexual gestures’ and ‘telling sexual stories or jokes with sexual connotations’.14 Another Egyptian study found harassment in the forms of ‘suggestive staring,’ ‘hearing sexual words,’ ‘telling sexual conversations or jokes,’ and unnecessary touching or contact.40 In Turkey, the most common forms were ‘unnecessary touch,’ ‘unwanted contact,’ ‘sexual conversations,’ sexual innuendos, jokes and frequent suggestive looking.41 Yosep et al reported nurses being kissed, touched or hugged from behind,42 while other studies found that 5% of nurses experienced forced hugs and kisses from patients.43 44 Sexual abuse by patients may also occur through verbal insults, such as humiliating female nurses for their physical appearance.45 These findings suggest that cultural, geographical and reporting factors may contribute to variations in the types of harassment experienced by nurses.

Regarding PTSD, our study confirmed its high prevalence among participants and demonstrated a significant correlation between sexual harassment and PTSD symptoms. This aligns with previous research indicating a strong association between harassment and PTSD.23 46 47 Malik et al reported a positive correlation of workplace harassment with PTSD among female doctors, family physicians and nurses.23 Nothling et al observed a high occurrence of PTSD among African women within the first 3 months after rape,46 and Shin et al reported high PTSD prevalence in individuals who experienced sexual abuse.47 Dworkin et al and Scott et al also highlighted the prevalence and severity of post-rape PTSD in women.48 49 These studies, although conducted in different populations, support the association observed in our study. Experiencing victimisation or unfair treatment due to hostile or violent behaviour from colleagues or others can profoundly impact mental health, even in cases of mild psychological abuse.23

Under-reporting of sexual harassment remains a significant challenge. Many victims do not receive adequate treatment for resulting mental health disorders.50 51 In cultural contexts such as Iran, discussing sexual harassment is often taboo, which can prevent victims from seeking support. Implementing measures to facilitate access to treatment and support services is crucial, as effective therapeutic interventions can reduce psychological harm and allow victims to lead normal lives.

Despite extensive research on sexual harassment in healthcare settings globally, studies focusing on Iranian nurses are scarce. Therefore, our study contributes valuable knowledge about sexual harassment among this population and may help raise awareness among policymakers. It is important to recognise that sexual harassment is frequently under-reported, which can obscure the problem in workplace environments.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from specific hospitals, limiting the generalisability of findings to all public, educational and private hospitals. Second, the study included only female nurses, leaving data on male nurses lacking. Third, reliance on self-reported measures within Iran’s cultural context may have led some nurses to under-report or inaccurately report experiences of sexual harassment due to fear or social norms, despite assurances of confidentiality. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant association between workplace sexual harassment and PTSD symptoms among nurses. Although certain demographic factors were related to harassment exposure, they were not associated with PTSD. These findings suggest that sexual harassment may contribute to psychological distress in nurses. Organisational measures to reduce harassment and support affected staff may help improve nurses’ well-being. Further longitudinal research is recommended to better understand temporal relationships between harassment and mental health outcomes.

Recommendations

Given the observed association between workplace sexual harassment and PTSD symptoms, healthcare institutions may consider implementing organisational policies that facilitate safe reporting pathways and supportive responses for affected staff. Efforts to improve workplace climate and reduce harassment may help minimise psychological distress among nurses. Future studies should explore which interventions are most effective in preventing harassment and mitigating its mental health consequences.
