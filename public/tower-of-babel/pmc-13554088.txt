NR3C1/PRKACG-mediated impairment of mitochondrial quality control underlies stress-induced hypothalamic neuronal injury
Authors: Guowei Zhang, Jingze Cong, Xiaowei Feng, Hongjian Xin, Weihao Zhu, Rui Shi, Chenyu Li, Yang Wang, Rufei Ma, Yingmin Li, Bin Cong, Weibo Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hypothalamus integrates autonomic, endocrine, and behavioral responses to stress, and stress-induced hypothalamic neuronal injury is implicated in various diseases. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Mitochondria, as stress-sensitive organelles, play a critical role in cellular injury through structural and functional alterations. Here, we investigate how stress triggers mitochondrial quality control (MQC) dysfunction via glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) signaling, contributing to hypothalamic neuronal injury. Using acute and chronic stress rat models, we demonstrate that stress induces hypothalamic neuronal damage. Transmission electron microscopy and WB analysis reveal that stress promotes excessive mitochondrial fission while suppressing fusion, disrupting mitochondrial dynamics. At the cellular level, ChIP-Seq and siRNA experiments confirm that glucocorticoids (GCs) downregulate PRKACG (protein kinase A catalytic subunit gamma) expression via NR3C1-mediated transcriptional repression, reducing DRP1 (dynamin-related protein 1) phosphorylation at Ser637 and leading to aberrant mitochondrial fission. Furthermore, acute and chronic stress differentially activate mitophagy pathways, resulting in mitochondrial depletion. Intriguingly, neuronal death shifts from apoptosis to necroptosis under prolonged stress. In conclusion, our findings establish that NR3C1/PRKACG-mediated MQC dysfunction is a key mechanism in stress-induced hypothalamic neuronal injury. This study not only elucidates how GCs disrupt MQC but also advances our understanding of mitochondrial dysregulation in stress-related neuronal damage, providing a foundation for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.

Introduction

Stress is a pervasive aspect of daily life, varying in intensity and frequency across individuals1. Although responses to stress differ, accumulating evidence indicates that excessive or chronic stress is a major contributor to both physical and mental health disorders. Epidemiological studies have shown that exposure to severe stressors during childhood significantly increases the risk of developing schizophrenia2. Moreover, stress is a key precipitating factor in the onset of depression and anxiety disorders. According to a report by the World Health Organization, the global prevalence of anxiety and depression rose by more than 25% in the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic3. In addition, stress is strongly associated with the increasing incidence of cardiovascular diseases4, immune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis5, and gastrointestinal conditions6. Despite growing recognition of its harmful effects, research into the mechanisms of stress-induced injury remains incomplete. As a result, effective strategies for the prevention and management of stress-related damage are still lacking.

Physiologically, stress activates the sympathetic-adrenomedullary axis and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, initiating neural and endocrine responses. As the initiating hub of the HPA axis and the chief regulator of sympathetic outflow, the hypothalamus orchestrates, through multi-tiered and exquisitely tuned control, the release of glucocorticoids (cortisol in humans, corticosterone in rodents) and a repertoire of complex autonomic reflexes. When challenged by stress, hypothalamic neurons switch to a state of high activity, integrating multifaceted signals to coordinate the organism’s adaptive response7. This activity demands substantial energy8, primarily supplied by mitochondria through oxidative phosphorylation. Mitochondria also act as cellular stress sensors, and their integrity is preserved by an integrated mitochondrial quality control (MQC) network that encompasses: (1) biogenesis driven by PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha)-NRF1/2 (nuclear respiratory factor 1/2)–TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A) signaling, which replenishes the healthy pool; (2) dynamic morphology, regulated by balanced fusion (mediated by mitofusins [MFN1/2] and optic atrophy 1 [OPA1]) and fission (mediated by dynamin-related protein 1 [DRP1]) that segregate damaged segments for removal9; (3) selective elimination of dysfunctional mitochondria via PINK1 (PTEN-induced putative kinase 1)/Parkin-dependent mitophagy and receptor-mediated pathways10; and (4) proteostasis, ensured by mitochondrial unfolded-protein response and intramitochondrial chaperones-proteases11,12. Failure at any level of this coordinated system disrupts neuronal energy supply and amplifies oxidative stress, contributing to the pathogenesis of diverse neuropsychiatric disorders. For instance, increased mitophagy is observed in the cortex and hippocampus of mice with memory impairments13. Similarly, activation of the PINK1-Parkin mitophagy pathway is linked to hippocampal neuronal injury following chronic social defeat14. In multiple sclerosis, inhibition of necroptosis has been shown to reduce oligodendrocyte loss15.

Physiological glucocorticoid (GC) pulses activate glucocorticoid receptors (NR3C1, nuclear receptor subfamily 3 group C member 1), which mediate negative feedback regulation of GC to maintain cellular and systemic HPA-axis homeostasis16. Under physiological conditions, low concentrations of GC pulses act through NR3C1 to (1) restrain further CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) and AVP (arginine vasopressin) release, (2) stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory efficiency, and (3) facilitate synaptic pruning that calibrates stress-adapted neurocircuitry. However, excessive GC exposure exerts cytotoxic effects17, damaging hypothalamic neurons and disrupting the regulation of stress hormone secretion and autonomic function. This sustained or excessive GC release perpetuates neuronal injury through NR3C1 signaling, forming a pathological cycle that contributes to systemic dysfunction18. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying stress-induced hypothalamic neuronal injury is essential for developing effective interventions. In our restraint stress model, we observed significantly elevated GC levels and increased expression of NR3C1 in the hypothalamus. We also observed abnormal mitochondrial morphology in neurons, suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to hypothalamic neuronal injury. We hypothesize that stress disrupts MQC via NR3C1, thereby promoting neuronal injury. To test this hypothesis, we conducted an in-depth mechanistic investigation based on our established stress model. These findings aim to inform the development of novel strategies for preventing and treating stress-related injuries.

Result

Stress induces damage to hypothalamic neurons

As the origin of the HPA axis and a key regulatory center of the sympathetic nervous system, the hypothalamus plays a central role in orchestrating the stress response. To investigate the impact of stress on hypothalamic neurons, we established a classical acute and chronic restraint stress model in rats. Stressed rats underwent daily 6-h restraint stress in a transparent acrylic tube, followed by 5-min cold-water immersion for 1 day (acute stress) or 7 days (chronic stress). The rats from their respective control groups underwent equivalent durations of food and water deprivation, with ad libitum access to food and water at all other times. Control group data were pooled from the 1-day and 7-day control groups, which did not significantly differ from each other except for body weight (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Model validity was confirmed through multiple indicators, including body weight, behavioral assessments (open field and elevated plus maze tests), and fecal pellet output. At baseline (day 0), body weights did not differ among groups. Although control and acute-stress animals showed comparable weights 24 h later, chronically stressed rats weighed significantly less than their controls on day 7, indicating that chronic stress retarded body-weight gain (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In the open-field test, overall locomotor activity was unchanged; however, travel in the center zone showed a decreasing trend after acute stress and was significantly reduced after chronic stress, suggesting reduced exploration of a novel environment (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). The chronic-stress group also produced more fecal pellets, consistent with increased anxiety (Supplementary Fig. 1e). In the elevated plus-maze, both acutely and chronically stressed rats entered the open arms less frequently and spent less time there, further indicating that stress, particularly when prolonged, lowers novelty-seeking behavior and promotes a depression-like state (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). Collectively, these results demonstrate the development of anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, confirming successful model establishment. Next, we examined neuronal morphology in the hypothalamus using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and cresyl violet staining. Neurons in acutely stressed rats appeared structurally intact. In contrast, chronic stress induced marked pathological changes, including tissue edema, neuronal pyknosis (Fig. 1a), and loss of Nissl bodies (Fig. 1b). Meanwhile, we quantified the mean area of Nissl bodies within the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus. The results showed that this area was significantly smaller in the chronic-stress group than in the control group (Fig. 1c). To further assess neuronal integrity, we evaluated the expression of the synaptic protein PSD95 (postsynaptic density protein 95) in the hypothalamus (Fig. 1d, e) by western blot (WB). Stress induces mitochondrial fission-fusion imbalance

As mitochondria are sensitive organelles perceiving cellular stress, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine the ultrastructural organization of mitochondria in hypothalamic neurons. Ultrastructural analysis revealed progressive, time-dependent mitochondrial injury (Fig. 2a). The control group displayed elongated, rod-shaped mitochondria with uniformly dense matrices, intact parallel cristae and sharply defined outer membranes. After 1 day of stress (acute group) the organelles were still mostly rod-shaped, but many had begun to swell: a subset of cristae appeared fragmented or slightly dilated, and the outer membrane retained its continuity. In the 7-day (chronic-stress) group, mitochondrial damage was marked. The majority of mitochondria were severely swollen and rounded, and cristae were extensively fragmented or completely effaced. We further used immunofluorescence staining of the mitochondrial outer membrane marker TOM20 (translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane 20) to observe mitochondrial morphology. Despite the potential for TOM20 hydrolysis (mitophagy; see below), its fluorescence labeling can to some extent reflect mitochondrial length in rat neurons. We found that mitochondrial length was significantly reduced after acute stress, with an even greater reduction observed under chronic stress (Fig. 2b, c). Meanwhile, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of TOM20 was significantly reduced only under chronic stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Mitochondrial morphology is governed by the balance between fission and fusion19. Consequently, we systematically assessed the expression changes of key proteins regulating this fission-fusion process. Our results indicate that both acute and chronic stress robustly activate mitochondrial fission. Specifically, phosphorylation of DRP1 at Ser637—an inhibitory site—was significantly lower in the two stress groups than in controls, whereas phosphorylation at the priming site Ser616 was markedly elevated under both stress conditions. Concordantly, acute stress also induced a significant increase in FIS1 (fission 1) protein levels, underscoring the enhanced fission machinery (Fig. 2d–g). Stress also resulted in a progressive dysfunction of mitochondrial fusion. While the expression of fusion proteins regulating the outer (MFN1 and MFN2) and inner membranes (OPA1) did not exhibit significant changes under acute stress, their expression levels were significantly reduced during chronic stress (Fig. 2h–k). In conclusion, stress induces mitochondrial fragmentation by disturbing the fission-fusion balance. The NR3C1–PRKACG axis regulates DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation and mediates stress-induced mitochondrial fission

To investigate the role of GC in stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we measured GC levels in serum and NR3C1 expression in the hypothalamus. Chronic stress significantly increased both circulating GC levels and hypothalamic NR3C1 expression (Fig. 3a–c). To determine whether elevated GCs contribute to hypothalamic neuronal injury via NR3C1, we established a cortisol-induced cellular stress model in SH-SY5Y cells using 100 μM cortisol. CCK-8 (cell counting kit-8) assays revealed that this concentration of cortisol significantly reduced cell viability (Fig. 3d). Cortisol treatment also markedly increased NR3C1 expression in cells, mirroring the in vivo pattern observed in stressed animals (Fig. 3e, f). Next, we employed ChIP-Seq (chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing) to explore whether NR3C1 mediates transcriptional regulation of mitochondrial fission-related genes. The summary statistics of the raw data before and after quality control are listed in Supplementary Data 2. Moreover, we present the distribution of reads on the reference genome (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the base composition and quality-score distribution for each sample. The results showed that cortisol significantly decreased NR3C1 binding to the promoter region of PRKACG (protein kinase A catalytic subunit gamma), a gene encoding the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A (PKA) (Fig. 3g). PKA phosphorylates DRP1 at Ser637, a modification that inhibits mitochondrial fission. RT-qPCR (real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction) analysis confirmed that cortisol treatment reduced PRKACG mRNA expression (Fig. 3h). WB analysis further demonstrated decreased levels of PRKACG protein and phosphorylated DRP1 at Ser637 following cortisol exposure (Fig. 3i–k). To confirm the involvement of NR3C1, we silenced its expression using siRNA (small interfering RNA), achieving approximately 63.7% knockdown efficiency (Fig. 3l). NR3C1 knockdown significantly reversed the cortisol-induced reductions in PRKACG mRNA and protein expression and restored DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation (Fig. 3m–p). Excessive mitochondrial fission activates distinct mitophagy pathways, leading to mitochondrial depletion in hypothalamic neurons

Beyond impairing mitochondrial fusion, excessive fission also promotes mitophagy by generating fragmented mitochondria that serve as substrates for mitophagic clearance. To evaluate mitophagy activation, we measured the levels of LC3-II/I (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) and LAMP1 (lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1), markers of autophagosome formation and lysosomal activity, respectively. Both acute and chronic stress significantly upregulated these markers, indicating enhanced mitophagy under stress conditions (Fig. 4a–c). To distinguish the mitophagy pathways activated by different stress durations, we further analyzed the expression of key pathway-specific mediators. Acute stress elevated the levels of PINK1 and Parkin, suggesting engagement of the PINK1–Parkin pathway. In contrast, chronic stress induced the expression of BNIP3 (BCL2 interacting protein 3), BNIP3L (BCL2 interacting protein 3 like), and PINK1, with no significant change in FUNDC1 (FUN14 domain-containing protein 1) levels under either condition (Fig. 4d–j). These findings indicate that acute stress primarily activates PINK1–Parkin-mediated mitophagy, whereas chronic stress involves persistent activation of the BNIP3/BNIP3L pathway. To assess mitochondrial biogenesis and overall mitochondrial content, we examined PGC-1α, TOM20 and VDAC1 (voltage-dependent anion channel 1) expression in hypothalamic neurons exposed to stress. Both acute and chronic stress suppressed PGC-1α, indicating impaired mitochondrial biogenesis. A shift from apoptosis to necroptosis occurs in hypothalamic neurons under acute and chronic stress

As changes in mitochondrial morphology can impair function, we next examined the impact of stress duration on mitochondrial integrity. Both acute and chronic stress increased cytochrome c (Cyt C) levels (Fig. 5a, b), indicating mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization. However, only chronic stress significantly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), suggesting a decline in mitochondrial function (Fig. 5c, d). Consistent with this, chronic stress markedly elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (Fig. 5e, f), further supporting the presence of mitochondrial dysfunction. To explore stress-induced cell death mechanisms, we evaluated mitochondrial-dependent cell death pathways. Acute stress induced significant neuronal apoptosis, whereas chronic stress elicited only a minimal apoptotic response that did not differ significantly from control levels (Fig. 5g, h). Measurement of intracellular ATP (adenosine triphosphate) levels revealed that chronic stress substantially decreased ATP production (Fig. 5i), suggesting impaired energy metabolism. Given that necroptosis is a form of programmed cell death independent of ATP, we investigated the activation of key necroptotic mediators. Acute stress increased RIP1 (receptor-interacting protein 1) expression but did not affect RIP3 (receptor-interacting protein 3), MLKL (mixed lineage kinase domain-Like protein), or phosphorylated MLKL at Ser358 (p-MLKL Ser358). Inhibition of excessive mitochondrial fission under chronic stress attenuates mitophagy and necroptosis

To elucidate the role of excessive mitochondrial fission in mediating mitophagy, rats exposed to 7 days of restraint stress were treated with the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 (mitochondrial division inhibitor 1). Mdivi-1 significantly reduced DRP1 phosphorylation at Ser616 and improved it at Ser637 respectively, and restored the expression of mitochondrial fusion proteins MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 induced by stress (Fig. 6a–f). Additionally, we morphologically assessed the effect of Mdivi-1 on the extent of mitochondrial fission. Results showed that Mdivi-1 pretreatment partially reversed the stress-induced reduction in mitochondrial length (excessive fragmentation) in rat neurons (Fig. 6g, h). These findings confirm that Mdivi-1 effectively rebalances the mitochondrial fission-fusion dynamic disrupted by chronic stress. Furthermore, Mdivi-1 treatment markedly attenuated the stress-induced upregulation of mitophagy markers LC3-II/I, LAMP1, and BNIP3/BNIP3L, while also reversing the reduction in TOM20 expression (Fig. 7a–f). These results indicate that suppression of mitochondrial fission mitigates stress-induced mitophagy activation. We next examined whether inhibiting mitochondrial fission also affects necroptosis. Mdivi-1 treatment significantly reduced Cyt C levels, downregulated necroptosis-related proteins, and restored PSD95 expression in the hypothalamus (Fig. 7g–l), suggesting a rescue of neuronal structural integrity. Additionally, we conducted behavioral assays. Having confirmed that 5% DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) itself exerts no detectable effects on basal behavioral performance (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), our results showed that Mdivi-1 administration partially reversed chronic stress-induced depressive-like behaviors: compared with stressonly rats, Mdivi-1-treated stressed rats exhibited greater locomotor distance in the central zone of the open-field and more entries into the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. 7m, n). To further clarify the role of necroptosis in chronic stress, we employed the specific necroptosis inhibitor Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) and performed p-MLKL (Ser358) IHC (immunohistochemistry) (Fig. 7o, p). Nec-1-pretreated stressed rats displayed significantly lower necroptotic activity than stressed rats treated with vehicle. Taken together, these data support our proposed pathogenic cascade: chronic stress leads to glucocorticoid accumulation, which activates NR3C1. This in turn suppresses PRKACG transcription, resulting in excessive mitochondrial fission. Discussion

The hypothalamus, as the central regulator of the body’s stress response, plays a crucial role in controlling autonomic, endocrine, and behavioral functions. Structural and functional changes within this region are essential for enabling major organs to respond promptly and effectively to stress. Previous research, including our own, has shown that stress causes dysfunction and injury in hypothalamic neurons20; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. To investigate this, we established a rat model combining acute and chronic restraint stress with cold water swimming to simulate varying durations of psychological and physical stress analogous to human conditions. This well-established stress model has been empirically validated in our laboratory and by other research groups20,21. In stressed rats, serum glucocorticoid levels were significantly elevated, consistent with the human stress response. Furthermore, increased abundance of glucocorticoid receptors in the rat brain, along with the emergence of depressive-like behaviors, effectively recapitulates stress-related conditions encountered in humans. The 1-day stress protocol was designed to model acute stress exposure, while the 7-day protocol simulates chronic stress. Morphological analysis of this model revealed significant hypothalamic neuronal injury characterized by pyknosis and Nissl body dissolution. Additionally, a pronounced decrease in postsynaptic density protein 95 indicated synaptic impairment. Transmission electron microscopy further demonstrated mitochondrial abnormalities, including cristae dissolution and fragmentation, in hypothalamic neurons following stress. Given mitochondrial sensitivity to internal and external stressors and their role as the primary energy source for neurons, we hypothesize that stress-induced mitochondrial morphological alterations contribute directly to neuronal damage in the hypothalamus.

Mitochondrial morphology is primarily regulated by the dynamic balance between fusion and fission processes, with mitochondrial function maintained through stringent quality control mechanisms22. Fusion involves the merging of the outer mitochondrial membrane, mediated by mitofusin 1 (MFN1) and mitofusin 2 (MFN2), and the inner membrane, facilitated by optic atrophy 1 (OPA1). This process promotes the exchange of mitochondrial DNA, proteins, and metabolites, enhancing cellular adaptability by enabling functional complementation through content mixing23. Conversely, mitochondrial fission is chiefly controlled by the dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and its receptor FIS1. Cytoplasmic DRP1 undergoes phosphorylation at serine residues 616 and 637, changing the strength of binding to FIS1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it oligomerizes and constricts the membrane to induce division24. Notably, phosphorylation at Ser637 and Ser616 has opposing effects: phosphorylation at Ser637 reduces DRP1’s GTPase activity and its translocation to mitochondria, thereby inhibiting fission25. Balanced fission is essential for the removal of damaged mitochondria and the preservation of cellular homeostasis, while excessive fission leads to cellular injury26. Our analysis revealed a significant elevation of mitochondrial fission markers (p-DRP1-Ser616, FIS1) under acute stress and this increase persists until chronic stress, while mitochondrial fusion only shows a significant decrease during chronic stress, indicating that stress induces excessive mitochondrial fission and disrupts the fusion-fission equilibrium over time.

Is stress-induced excessive mitochondrial fission linked to elevated GC levels? Glucocorticoids—cortisol in humans—are primary stress hormones whose genomic actions are mediated by the glucocorticoid receptor, a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor that regulates the expression of numerous genes. To determine whether cortisol links psychological stress to mitochondrial fission, we performed ChIP-seq to investigate the interaction between the glucocorticoid receptor and mitochondrial fission. Notably, we identified that PRKACG—a key regulator of mitochondrial fission—is transcriptionally regulated by NR3C1, a GC-dependent transcription factor. PRKACG encodes a catalytic subunit of PKA, which phosphorylates DRP1 at Ser637, thereby limiting its translocation to the mitochondrial membrane and inhibiting fission27. Following stress exposure, NR3C1 binding to the PRKACG promoter was significantly reduced, suggesting a stress-induced suppression of PRKACG transcription and translation. The resulting decline in PRKACG expression diminishes the inhibition of DRP1-mediated fission, thereby promoting excessive mitochondrial fragmentation. To validate this mechanism, we knocked down NR3C1 expression in cortisol-treated SH-SY5Y cells. Cells transfected with si-NR3C1 exhibited increased PRKACG levels and correspondingly reduced mitochondrial fission after cortisol treatment. These findings confirm that the NR3C1-PRKACG signaling axis plays a pivotal role in mediating stress-induced mitochondrial fission in neurons.

Excessive mitochondrial fission promotes mitophagy28. Accordingly, we examined mitophagy levels following stress exposure and observed sustained elevations in both mitophagy and lysosomal activity. Combined with a reduction in mitochondrial number, these findings suggest that mitophagy exerts excessive degradative effects post-stress, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent neuronal injury—consistent with previous reports29,30. In this context, although mitophagy appears to act as a compensatory mechanism during chronic stress, it is insufficient to effectively clear damaged mitochondria. Simultaneously, impaired mitochondrial biogenesis leads to a shortage of functional mitochondria, exacerbating neuronal damage. Notably, some studies report that GC treatment suppresses mitophagy and compromises its cytoprotective role, potentially due to variations in GC exposure duration, concentration, or the cellular context31. Interestingly, we observed a distinct switch in the dominant mitophagy pathway depending on stress duration: the PINK1-Parkin pathway was activated during acute stress, while the BNIP3/BNIP3L pathway became predominant under chronic stress. The PINK1-Parkin axis is considered the canonical mitophagy route, responsible for mitochondrial quality control under physiological conditions23,32. In contrast, the BNIP3/BNIP3L and FUNDC1 pathways are typically induced under hypoxic conditions—the former directly regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), and the latter modulated via oxygen-sensitive kinases33–35. Our current investigation did not include detailed assessments of neuronal oxygen metabolism beyond ATP quantification, and the mechanistic basis for this observed pathway switch remains unclear. Could this transition in mitophagy signaling represent a critical determinant in the dual roles—protective versus detrimental—of mitophagy in neurons? Addressing this question will be a central objective of our future studies.

Excessive mitochondrial fission triggers mitophagy, a quality-control mechanism that, when sustained, depletes the functional mitochondrial pool36. Concomitantly, proteolytic cleavage of the inner-membrane fusion protein OPA1 indicates impaired cristae remodeling and disassembly of respiratory chain supercomplex37. To assess the redox consequences of this morphological disruption, we measured cellular ROS levels. Our data show that total ROS increased in parallel with DRP1-S616 phosphorylation and a decrease in the JC-1 ratio (aggregates/monomers), linking fission-driven respiratory inefficiency to ROS accumulation and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential38. This self-amplifying loop establishes a vicious cycle that ultimately compromises ATP synthesis and cellular resilience under chronic stress. Although the ROS probe used cannot differentiate between specific reactive oxygen species or track their subcellular localization, it serves as a robust indicator for the overall intracellular ROS burden. Our findings highlight a critical role for ROS in this process.

Consistent with the increased outer mitochondrial membrane permeability implied by the previously noted reduction in OPA1 protein, the content of the crucial electron carrier Cyt C rapidly increased following stress. Contrary to reports indicating that both BNIP3 and BNIP3L promote Cyt C release39, we did not observe a further elevation of Cyt C following chronic stress. Accumulated Cyt C leaks from mitochondria into the cytoplasm via compromised mitochondrial membranes, inducing apoptosis40. However, TUNEL staining revealed that apoptosis was only significantly elevated during acute stress, which contrasts with the neuronal damage indicated by the reduced PSD95 levels observed under chronic stress conditions. Further analysis revealed a decrease in ATP levels during chronic stress, suggesting that mitochondrial damage within neurons leads to reduced ATP production. This transition from high to low ATP levels shifts the sensitivity of damaged neurons from apoptosis to necroptosis, although the underlying mechanism for this switch remains unclear41. Necroptosis is characterized by the partial or complete insertion of MLKL oligomers into the plasma membrane, creating pores or cation channels42. This results in the influx of Ca2+ and Na+, disruption of osmotic homeostasis, and ultimately culminates in cell lysis and death43. The neuronal cell membrane serves as the structural foundation for nerve signal transmission, and this foundation may be compromised by phosphorylated MLKL, thereby accounting for the observed decline in PSD95 levels during chronic stress44.

Finally, we employed Mdivi-1 to investigate the functional consequences of suppressing stress-induced excessive mitochondrial fission in neurons. We identified an optimal concentration of Mdivi-1 that selectively inhibited pathological fission without interfering with physiological fission. Interestingly, this dose also reduced mitochondrial fusion, suggesting a regulatory interdependence between these processes. This observation aligns with previous findings, such as those reported by Rambold et al.45, which demonstrated that enhanced mitochondrial fission can suppress fusion activity. Treatment with Mdivi-1 partially reversed the elevated levels of mitophagy and the reduction in mitochondrial number observed under chronic stress. Additionally, it attenuated the increase in necroptosis and the associated synaptic dysfunction resulting from mitochondrial impairment. These findings suggest that excessive mitochondrial fission heightens the vulnerability of hypothalamic neurons to necroptosis, thereby contributing to neuronal injury.

In conclusion, our study reveals that stress disrupts mitochondrial quality control and induces neuronal damage in the hypothalamus via disinhibition of mitochondrial fission mediated by the NR3C1-PRKACG axis. This pathological process is accompanied by a temporal switch in the dominant mitophagy pathways and a transition in neuronal cell death mode from apoptosis to necroptosis. However, the translational relevance of our results is limited by the inherent constraints of animal models, which cannot fully recapitulate the complex social dimensions of chronic stress in humans. Additionally, although this study focuses primarily on the role of neurons in stress‑induced behavioral changes—particularly via synaptic restructuring and subsequent disruption of neural signaling—astrocytes and microglia also display significant alterations under comparable stress conditions. Moreover, the cellular composition of the hypothalamus is highly diverse and heterogeneous. Consequently, the use of SH‑SY5Y cells alone does not adequately model the complex in vivo microenvironment of hypothalamic neurons exposed to glucocorticoid stimulation, thereby limiting the direct translation of our findings to clinical treatments for disorders such as depression. Future studies will employ a broader range of stressors, incorporate human pathological specimens, and apply multi‑omics approaches to further elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms underlying depression in humans.

Experimental animals and cell capture

Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (weighing 200 ± 20 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were maintained under controlled conditions: a constant temperature (22 ± 2 ℃), 50% humidity, and a 12-h light-dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Following a 7-day acclimatization period in the new environment, a total of 94 rats were randomly allocated into different treatment groups to establish the stress model. We complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All protocols of the animal experiments have been approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-Hebmu-2023011).

SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells were chosen for this study based on their human origin and their ability to model key in vivo responses to cortisol, such as decreased viability and increased glucocorticoid receptor expression. The SH-SY5Y cells (Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., ZQ0050) were cultured in complete medium, which is high-glucose DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells in complete medium were incubated at 37 ℃ under a 5% CO2 atmosphere until reaching 70–80% confluence. Following this, the medium was changed to complete medium containing cortisol at a final concentration of 100 μM, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h to proceed with subsequent experimental procedures.

Establishment of the rat stress model

A random number between 0 and 1 was generated for each rat using the RAND function in Excel 2021. The rats were then sorted in ascending order of their random numbers. After sorting, the first six rats were allocated to the control group (Control), the next six to the acute stress group (1 d Stress), and the last six to the chronic stress group (7 d Stress). The same randomization procedure was used for two-group and four-group designs as described for the three-group design. Rats in the stress groups underwent daily restraint for 6 h in a transparent acrylic tube, followed by 5 min of cold-water immersion immediately after release. Control group rats remained in their home cages with food and water deprivation for either 1 or 7 days21. The body weight of each rat was recorded prior to the fasting period. With the exception of body weight, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two control groups. For the groups involving the Mdivi-1 and Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), the chronic stress model was employed. All rats in these four groups were subjected to stereotaxic brain injections before restrained.

Stereotactic brain injection

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, which was administered using the gas anesthesia device (Reward Life Science Co., Ltd., R5835) at 3%. A burr hole was drilled 1 mm posterior and 1.5 mm right to the bregma. A guide cannula was implanted into the right lateral ventricle, and drugs were injected into the lateral ventricle at a depth of 4 mm. The lateral ventricle is located in front and above the third ventricle. The injected drug can spread along the cerebrospinal fluid and reach the hypothalamus located on the wall of the third ventricle. The cannula was secured using stainless steel screws and dental cement. Once the rats recovered from anesthesia, they were returned to their cages for 1 week of housing before the modeling and drug administration procedures commenced. For the drug dosing, Mdivi-1 or Nec-1 was dissolved in DMSO at 450 mmol/L, and then diluted DMSO with PBS (phosphate buffered saline) to a final concentration of 5%46. Both Mdivi-1 and Nec-1 were administered at a dose of 1 mg/kg, as established in previous studies47. Thirty minutes prior to each restraint, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and received the drug through a microinjection cannula (Shanghai Yuyan Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a rate of 1 μL/min.

Behavior test

Behavioral tests were performed 24 h following the establishment of the models in all experimental groups.

Open field test: The open field apparatus was a square box constructed from non-toxic, odorless medical-grade acrylic, measuring 100 cm × 100 cm × 40 cm in length, width, and height, respectively. The center zone was defined as the center of the arena (50 cm × 50 cm). After adapting to the testing room environment for 1 h, the rats were positioned in the center of the box, facing away from the experimenter. Simultaneously, a video tracking system was initiated to record their locomotor trajectories for 5 min48. Following the test, the rats were returned to their home cages, and the number of fecal pellets was counted. The interior of the open field box was then sprayed with 75% alcohol to eliminate residual odors that could influence subsequent trials. Once the alcohol had evaporated, the next rat was tested.

Elevated plus maze test: The maze featured two open arms (50 cm × 10 cm) and two enclosed arms (50 cm × 10 cm × 40 cm), positioned opposite and perpendicular to each other, forming a cross. A central platform (10 cm × 10 cm) was situated at the junction, with the maze elevated 50 cm from the floor. After adapting in the testing room for 1 h, rats were placed in the center of the elevated plus maze facing an open arm, and their activity was monitored by an overhead surveillance camera for 5 min. Post-test, the rats were returned to their home cages, and the maze was cleaned with 75% alcohol to prevent residual odors from affecting subsequent trials. Once the alcohol had evaporated, the next rat was tested. Following the completion of behavioral testing, the rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and their blood from the abdominal aorta was collected for subsequent experimental procedures. After the blood collection was completed, the rat were decapitated, and the hypothalamus tissues were quickly dissected and removed. The removed hypothalamic samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 ℃ in a refrigerator for future use. After centrifuging the blood (4 ℃, 12,000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was collected, and then rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃.

HE and cresyl violet staining

Rat brain tissues were fixed in 4% neutral formalin for 36 h, dehydrated through a graded series of alcohols and xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, serial coronal sections (5 µm thick) were cut from 7.92 mm to 6.84 mm relative to the interaural line. HE staining: Sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated via a graded alcohol series, stained with hematoxylin for 5 s, rinsed in tap water for 5 min, differentiated with 0.5% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 3 s, and counterstained with eosin for 3 s. Cresyl violet staining: Following dewaxing and rehydration, the sections were immersed in cresyl violet staining solution (Solarbio, G1430) and incubated at 37 ℃ in an oven for 1 h. Following rinsing with distilled water to remove excess stain, the sections were immersed in 95% ethanol for differentiation, and the duration of differentiation was adjusted according to the microscopic appearance. The sections were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared of alcohol using xylene, mounted with neutral resin, and examined under an optical microscope.

Western Blot

Protein extraction from the rat hypothalamus was consistently conducted on ice. Tissues were homogenized in RIPA (radio immunoprecipitation assay) lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃, the supernatant was collected as the protein extract. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA (bicinchoninic acid) Protein Assay Kit. Loading buffer was added in a volume ratio, and the sample was denatured at 95 ℃ for 10 min. In total, 50 or 100 μg protein from different samples were loaded into per gel lane. Samples were subjected to electrophoresis on 10% or 15% SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate)-PAGE (polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) gels, and the proteins were transferred onto PVDF (polyvinylidene difluoride) membranes that had been pre-activated with methanol using a blotting apparatus. The membrane was incubated with 5% BSA (bovine serum albumin) at room temperature for 1 h to block, followed by incubation with the primary antibody: PSD95 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #3450), DRP1 (1:1000), p-DRP1 Ser616 (1:500), p-DRP1 Ser637 (1:500), FIS1 (1:1000), MFN1 (1:500), MFN2 (1:1000), OPA1 (1:500), NR3C1 (1:1000), PRKACG (1:500), LC3 Ⅱ/Ⅰ (1:500), LAMP1 (1:1000), PINK1 (1:500), Parkin (1:100), BNIP3 (1:500), BNIP3L (1:500), FUNDC1 (1:5000), PGC-1α (1:500), TOM20 (1:1000), VDAC1 (1:1000), Cyto C (1:2000), RIP1 (1:500), RIP3 (1:500), MLKL (1:500), p-MLKL (1:250), β-Actin (1:2000), GAPDH (1:2000). Antibodies were diluted in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. Following three washes (10 min each) with TBST, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody (LI-COR: IRDye® 680RD Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Secondary Antibody, 1:5000; Rockland: Mouse IgG (H&L) Antibody DyLight™ 800 Conjugated,1:2000; Abbkine: Dylight 680, Goat Anti-Mouse IgG,1:2000) for 1 h. After three additional washes with TBST, the membrane was scanned using a Dual-color infrared fluorescence imaging system (Odyssey, 9120) and band grayscale values were quantified using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following anesthesia, cardiac perfusion fixation was performed on the rats using 4% paraformaldehyde. After isolation of the hypothalamic tissue, it was trimmed into 1 mm × 1 mm × 3 mm blocks. These blocks were rapidly placed in 4% glutaraldehyde for fixation and subsequently transferred to the Electron Microscopy Laboratory at Hebei Medical University. Prior to dehydration with graded acetone concentrations, the tissue was immersed in 0.1 M PBS containing 1% osmium tetroxide for 90 min49. Subsequently, the tissue was embedded in epoxy resin, and mitochondrial morphology was examined under a transmission electron microscope.

Immunofluorescent and Immunohistochemical staining

Five micrometre paraffin-embedded coronal brain sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated via a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 ℃ for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature (RT) for 2 h. After three washes in PBS (5 min each), sections were incubated with 3% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 30 min at room temperature to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Then sections were washed with PBS for 15 min and blocked with 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at 37 ℃. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with rabbit anti-TOM20 (1:50) and mouse anti-NeuN (1:100) primary antibody diluted in blocking buffer.

Immunofluorescent staining. Following six PBS washes, slides were incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibodies and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 ℃ in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, SouthernBiotech, 0100-20). Images were acquired using the confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica, SP8).

Immunohistochemical staining. Following six PBS washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit & mouse IgG for 1 h at room temperature. Following 6 × 5 min washes in PBST, immunoreactivity was visualized with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 2–5 min under microscopic control. The reaction was terminated by immersion in distilled water. Sections were counterstained with haematoxylin for 2 s, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured with a digital pathology scanner (Leica, APERIO CS2), and the number of positive cells was analyzed using ImageJ.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Corticosterone levels were quantified using the ELISA Kit (Beyotime, PC100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The supernatant of rat blood was obtained following high-speed centrifugation. Serum samples were first acidified and neutralized using the provided reagents (resulting in a 4-fold dilution) to release protein-bound corticosterone. Standard solutions were serially diluted to yield concentrations of 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25 and 0 ng/mL. Standards and pretreated samples (100 μL) were added to the antibody-coated plate, followed by the addition of HRP-labeled corticosterone (50 μL). After gentle mixing, the plate was sealed and incubated at 25–28 ℃ for 120 min in the dark. Following incubation, the plate was washed five times with 1× wash buffer (300 μL per well). TMB substrate (100 μL) was then added, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 15–20 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, 51119300). The standard curve was constructed by plotting the standard solutions against their corresponding absorbance values, and the regression equation was calculated to determine sample concentrations.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8)

SH-SY5Y cells were plated in 96-well plates. The stressed cell model was established as previously described. After modeling, 10 µL CCK-8 solution (ruipate, RC3028) was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 1 h in the incubator, and then the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq)

The chromatin DNA cross-linking with NR3C1 was extracted and purified using CST Ultrapure ChIP-seq Kit (#9005). Upon reaching 70-80% confluence, about 2 × 107 (per biological replicate) SH-SY5Y cells from two groups (n = 3) were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with 125 mM glycine (5 min). After two ice-cold PBS washes, nuclei were isolated with Lysis Buffer supplemented with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. Subsequently, the cells were harvested and centrifuged to extract chromatin, which was then digested with micrococcal nuclease for 20 min to yield 150-900 bp fragments. Sheared chromatin (equivalent to 10 µg DNA) was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with 2 µg of the NR3C1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #3660). Immune complexes were captured with fresh Protein G beads (2 h, 4 ℃). Beads were sequentially washed with Low-salt, and High-salt, and then eluted in 100 µL Elution Buffer (all included in kit #9005) for 30 min at 65 ℃. Eluates were incubated with Proteinase K (20 mg/mL) for 2 h at 65 ℃ to reverse cross-links. DNA was purified using the provided spin columns, and then proceeded with sequencing.

The sequencing libraries of immunoprecipitated chromatin DNA were generated with varied index label by NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA, cat. E7645L). End Repair Reaction Buffer (10×) and End Prep Enzyme Mix were added for end repair by the following condition: 20 ℃ for 30 min, 65 ℃ for 30 min. And then NEBNext Adaptor and Blunt/TA Ligase Master Mix were added to ligate adaptor at 20 ℃ for 15 min. USER Enzyme was added to remove base U of adaptor at 37 ℃ for 5 min. After purification with beads, products were amplified with PCR by the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 s; denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 s, annealing at 65 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s. The library size was selected and then purified with AMPure XP Beads. Qubit was used for library quantification and High-sensitivity DNA chip was used for test of library insert size. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Clean reads were aligned to the specified reference genome using Bowtie2 to obtain their genomic coordinates and sample-specific sequence characteristics. Peaks within biological replicates were called with MACS250 at a threshold of FDR ≤ 0.05. Genome data were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, Version 2.19.1) software.

Transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNAs)

Upon reaching a 50% confluence of SH-SY5Y cells, the transfection medium was prepared such that each 1 mL contained 40 nmol of si-NR3C1 or si-NC (Negative Control, obtained from Ribobio) and 4 μL of HighGene plus Transfection reagent (Abclonal, RM09014P), using the transfection-specific medium (ruipate, RE4009). Prior to cortisol treatment, the cells were incubated with the transfection medium for 24 h. Si-NC: transfection of si-NC; Si-NC+Cortisol: transfection of si-NC and treatment with 100 μM cortisol for 24 h; Si-NR3C1+Cortisol: transfection of si-NR3C1 and treatment with 100 μM cortisol for 24 h.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cell samples of each group via the chloroform extraction method, and its concentration was determined by nucleic acid quantification. Following reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA using Reverse Transcription Mix (Promega, A2791), qPCR was conducted. The reaction system consisted of 2 μL sample, 10 μL qPCR Master Mix (Promega, A6002), 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers, and 7.2 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 1 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 ℃ for 15 s and 60 ℃ for 1 min. GAPDH served as the internal control, and the relative mRNA expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. The primers used in this experiment were sourced from the Primer Bank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/), and their specificity has been verified through the NCBI BLAST database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/).

Primer sequences: NR3C1: Forward: GGAATAGGTGCCAAGGATCTGG, Reverse: GCTTACATCTGGTCTCATGCTGG; PRKACG: Forward: CTGAGCAAAGGCTACAACAAGG, Reverse: GATCTTCTCGTAGATCTGGATGG.

Detection of ROS level

Following dissection, the rat hypothalamus was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 ℃.The tissue was sectioned into frozen slices (9 μm thick) and then equilibrated to room temperature to manage moisture content. The dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probes (Servicebio, G1746) were added, and the sections were incubated in the dark at 37 ℃ for 30 min. They were then washed with PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min, three times in total. Finally, an anti-quenching mounting medium containing DAPI was applied. The relative average fluorescence intensity, reflecting ROS levels, was assessed using Image J software.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1)

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured with the Beyotime JC-1 assay (C2006). Fresh hypothalamic mitochondria were isolated with the Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3606) and kept on ice. Each well of a black 96-well plate received 50 µL of mitochondrial suspension containing 5 µg total mitochondrial protein (determined by Bradford Protein Assay Kit, Beyotime, P0006). JC-1 working solution (100 µL per well; final JC-1 concentration 2 µM) was prepared by diluting the 200× stock 100-fold in 1× JC-1 staining buffer (provided). After 20 min incubation at 25 ℃ in the dark, plates were washed twice with 200 µL ice-cold 1× JC-1 buffer and re-suspended in 100 µL of the same buffer. Fluorescence was read immediately on the Spark® Multimode Microplate Reader (TECAN): J-aggregates (polarized mitochondria) at Ex/Em 525/590 nm; JC-1 monomers (depolarized) at Ex/Em 490/530 nm; gain 100, 0.1 s integration. Data are expressed as the 590 nm/530 nm fluorescence ratio and were corrected for background (buffer-only blanks).

TUNEL stain

The kit was obtained from ZSGENTECH (ZS-C31009). Following dewaxing and rehydration of the rat brain sections, the experiment was performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protease K was added, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 20 min, followed by two PBS washes, each lasting 5 min. The pre-prepared TUNEL staining solution was added, and the sections were incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. The staining solution was washed off with PBS prior to DAPI counterstaining of the nuclei. Observation was performed using a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, DP80). The number of TUNEL-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

ATP content assay

ATP content was quantified with the Boxbio AKOP004M kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology, China). In brief, frozen tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1 mL ice-cold extraction buffer, centrifuged (10,000g, 4 ℃, 10 min), and the supernatant (500 µL) was deproteinized with an equal volume of chloroform, vortexed, and re-centrifuged (10,000g, 4 ℃, 5 min). The upper aqueous phase was kept on ice until assay. A freshly prepared reaction mixture (containing hexokinase, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, NADP⁺ and buffer) was added to 20 µL of sample or serial ATP standards (0.125–2.5 µmol/mL) in a 96-well UV plate. The increase in NADPH absorbance at 340 nm was recorded at 10 s (A1) and 190 s (A2) using a plate reader; ΔA = A2 − A1 was used to calculate sample concentrations from the standard curve. All steps were performed at 0–4 ℃ and reagents were used within their stated stability windows.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data obtained from semi-quantitative experiments were normalized against the mean of all control samples. All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. Depending on the data distribution, normally distributed variables were analyzed using either t-tests, one-way (Dunnett), or two-way (Tukey) ANOVA for inter-group comparisons, whereas non-normally distributed variables were evaluated with non-parametric tests. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Precise p-values are provided in Supplementary Data 1.
