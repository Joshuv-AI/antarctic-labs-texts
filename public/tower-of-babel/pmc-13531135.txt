Optimizing Computed Tomography-derived Surface Monitoring for Accurate Cancer Radiotherapy Setup
Authors: HAJIME OYOSHI, KAZUTO KANO, KENJI HOTTA, KOUTA HIROTAKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimSurface-guided radiation therapy (SGRT) is being increasingly applied in cancer radiotherapy to support markerless setup, respiratory management, and intrafraction surface monitoring. When computed tomography (CT)-derived digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) surfaces are imported into SGRT systems, the CT threshold used for external-contour generation may introduce systematic online delta values following image-guided setup correction. This phantom-based study investigated practical CT-threshold selection for accurate AlignRT-guided surface monitoring in cancer radiotherapy.Materials and MethodsTwo independent TrueBeam-AlignRT configurations were considered, one equipped with HD cameras and the other with Horizon cameras, using a cube phantom and an anthropomorphic thoracic phantom. External contours were generated for CT thresholds from –150 to –750 Hounsfield units (HU) in 100-HU increments. Following a cone-beam CT-based setup process and six-degree-of-freedom correction, DICOM surface selection was performed. AlignRT real-time delta values were recorded 2-5 s after surface selection. Introduction

Surface-guided radiation therapy (SGRT) has become an important component of modern cancer radiotherapy. Through the use of optical surface imaging, SGRT can support patient setup, intrafraction motion monitoring, respiratory management, and markerless workflows without requiring an additional imaging dose (1-14). These features are particularly relevant to breast and thoracic radiotherapy, for which the surface position, respiratory motion, and reproducibility of the arm or chest-wall positioning can affect the treatment delivery.

In SGRT workflows, the reference surface may be acquired directly by the optical system in the treatment room or imported as a digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) surface generated from the planning computed tomography (CT) dataset. The latter approach is attractive because it preserves the relationship among the planning CT coordinate system, treatment isocenter, and immobilization geometry. However, the surface generated from the CT dataset depends on the employed segmentation parameters, which include the CT number threshold used to define the external contour. If discrepancies exist between the imported DICOM surface and actual monitored surface, systematic online delta values may be introduced even after image-guided setup correction.

Both the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group Report 302 and the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology-Advisory Committee for Radiation Oncology Practice (ESTRO-ACROP) Guideline emphasize the importance of acceptance testing, commissioning, and quality assurance for SGRT systems (15, 16). These documents also recognize that reference-surface generation, imaging geometry, calibration, and institutional workflow can influence the SGRT performance. However, they do not specify a single Hounsfield unit (HU) threshold for the generation of CT-derived DICOM surfaces. Therefore, in clinical practice, institutions must individually determine whether their treatment planning system (TPS) settings and SGRT workflows provide sufficiently reliable reference surfaces.

Previous phantom-based studies have shown that threshold selection can alter the agreement between CT-derived surfaces and optical or X-ray-based localization systems (17-19). However, the magnitudes and directions of such discrepancies may depend on the phantom geometry, surface reflectance, CT acquisition parameters, SGRT system configuration, and the specific TPS used. Thus, a cautious, institution-specific technical evaluation may be more appropriate than the assumption of a universal threshold.

Therefore, this study aims to optimize CT-derived surface monitoring by evaluating practical CT-threshold settings for DICOM surface generation in an AlignRT-based SGRT workflow for cancer radiotherapy. Rather than establishing a universal threshold, we intend to identify threshold ranges that minimize post-cone beam CT (CBCT) AlignRT delta values under institutional clinical conditions. We also aim to clarify the commissioning aspects that must be considered before clinical implementation.

Materials and Methods

Study design and clinical context. A single-institution phantom-based experimental study was conducted, which was designed to support SGRT commissioning for cancer radiotherapy. Therefore, no human participants, patient images, or patient-specific clinical outcomes were evaluated. The study examined the technical behaviors of CT-derived DICOM reference surfaces in an end-to-end workflow that included planning CT acquisition, external-contour generation, CBCT-based treatment-room setup correction, and subsequent AlignRT surface monitoring.

The evaluated endpoint was the AlignRT real-time delta value that was recorded after CBCT-based six-degree-of-freedom correction. Therefore, the measured value was interpreted as a workflow-level residual delta rather than an isolated geometric effect of HU thresholding alone. This value may have been influenced by the following processes: CT surface extraction, CT-CBCT registration, couch correction, megavoltage (MV)-CBCT coordinate agreement, AlignRT calibration, phantom positioning, and optical surface monitoring.

Phantoms, CT acquisition, and surface generation. Two phantoms were used: a VisionRT cube phantom and a THRA-1 anthropomorphic thoracic phantom (Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan), as shown in Figure 1. In the case of the anthropomorphic phantom, the thoracic region was used to approximate surface-monitoring conditions relevant to breast and thoracic cancer radiotherapy. To improve the setup reproducibility, a Vac-Lok bag (CIVCO Systems, Orange City, IA, USA) was placed beneath the phantom.

Phantoms used in this study. (A) Cube phantom and (B) anthropomorphic thoracic phantom.

Planning CT images were acquired using an Aquilion ONE PRISM Edition (Ver. 10.12) scanner (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan) with the following acquisition parameters: tube voltage: 120 kV, tube current: 250 mA, slice thickness: 2 mm, field of view (FOV): 500 mm, matrix: 512 ´ 512, reconstruction kernel: FC13, mode: helical acquisition. External contours were generated for CT thresholds ranging from –150 to –750 HU in 100-HU increments. The RayStation TPS (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) was used for the primary analysis, with Eclipse (version 15.3; Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) being employed as a restricted supplemental TPS for comparison.

AlignRT system configurations and monitoring ROIs. Two independent clinical TrueBeam-AlignRT configurations were evaluated: Group A, comprising a TrueBeam unit with AlignRT (version 6.3.235.1) equipped with HD cameras, and Group B, comprising a separate TrueBeam-AlignRT configuration equipped with Horizon cameras. Note that these setups corresponded to independent treatment-room configurations rather than a single system containing both camera types. The specifications of the HD and Horizon cameras used in this study are provided in Table I.

Four monitoring regions were evaluated: one cube phantom region of interest (ROI) and three thoracic phantom ROIs (Figure 2). The ROI for the cube phantom was defined as the region encompassing the entire structure, excluding the base. For the body phantom, the middle ROI was centered on an isocenter located on the midline of the thorax, while the right and left ROIs were positioned relative to the corresponding isocenters and shifted laterally toward the respective sides. Lateral ROI of the body phantom was positioned at the mid-axillary line. The frame rate of the SGRT system varied depending on the ROI size, with larger ROIs generally resulting in slower response times. The frame rates were 17 fps for both the cube phantom and the central body phantom ROI, and 26 fps for the right and left body phantom ROIs. No significant differences in frame rate were observed between the two cameras. To avoid ambiguity, hereafter, the term “external contour” refers to the CT-derived DICOM surface generated by the TPS, whereas the term “monitoring ROI” refers to the region selected by the AlignRT surface monitoring system for surface matching.

Monitoring ROI configurations. (A) Cube phantom; (B) central ROI of the thoracic phantom; (C) right ROI; and (D) left ROI. ROI, Region of interest.

Calibration, setup procedure, and residual delta measurement. Before use of the treatment room, daily calibration checks were performed. Additionally, monthly system verification was performed using radiographic images of a dedicated phantom to confirm alignment with the MV treatment-beam isocenter. AlignRT calibration was performed using a dedicated cube phantom containing multiple radiopaque spheres. Specifically, MV images were acquired from four directions to align the camera system with the MV isocenter.

For each measurement, the phantom was positioned on the treatment couch and aligned using CBCT. Then, six-degree-of-freedom corrections were applied based on the CBCT registration result. Next, the CT-derived DICOM surface generated for each HU threshold was selected in AlignRT; real-time delta values were recorded 2-5 s after surface selection. The translational delta values were evaluated in the lateral (LAT), longitudinal (LNG), and vertical (VRT) directions. No rotational values were included in the present analysis because the primary purpose was to evaluate threshold-dependent translational shifts within a controlled phantom workflow.

Additional technical analyses. A restricted TPS comparison was performed using Group A and the thoracic phantom with the central ROI. The same measurement procedure was applied to the external contours generated by both RayStation and Eclipse. As this analysis was not performed across all phantom, ROI, and camera-configuration combinations, it was treated as a supplemental technical observation.

To evaluate the effects of the FOV and in-plane pixel size under clinically usable acquisition conditions, CT images acquired with FOVs of 500 and 450 mm were compared. These FOVs corresponded to in-plane pixel sizes of 0.977 and 0.879 mm, respectively. A 400-mm FOV was not included in the quantitative analysis because it did not fully cover the phantom surface and was therefore considered clinically unsuitable for CT-derived DICOM surface generation in this workflow. External contours were generated in RayStation at –650 HU. This analysis was limited to Group A and the thoracic phantom with the central ROI.

To assess the CT acquisition position, the thoracic phantom was manually displaced by –5 and –10 cm before CT acquisition, with the couch reference position remaining unchanged. This procedure should be regarded as an off-center CT acquisition test rather than a direct treatment-isocenter shift analysis. External contours were generated in RayStation at –650 HU, and the same CBCT-based correction and AlignRT measurement procedure was applied.

Data analysis. A descriptive technical evaluation was conducted in this study. Therefore, as repeated measurements were not acquired for each condition, no inferential statistical testing or formal between-group comparisons were performed, and no confidence intervals were determined. Thus, terms such as “significant” and “optimal” are avoided in this manuscript, and the results are reported as observed delta values or ranges obtained under the tested workflow.

Results

Integrated threshold-response across primary phantom conditions. The integrated threshold-response analysis is summarized in Figure 3. In the heatmap of absolute VRT delta values, higher HU thresholds generally produced larger vertical residuals, whereas lower thresholds reduced VRT delta values across most phantom and ROI conditions (Figure 3A). This tendency was most evident in the cube phantom, in which VRT delta values exceeded 1.0 mm at higher thresholds and decreased toward the lower-threshold range. In contrast, thoracic phantom measurements showed smaller overall VRT variation, but a similar tendency toward reduced residual values at lower thresholds.

Orange boxes indicate the lowest absolute vertical residual within each condition, and the dashed line indicates −650 HU. (B) Direction-specific summary of absolute residual delta values across the tested thresholds. (C) Change in absolute residual delta values from −350 to −650 HU. Direction-specific aggregation showed that the threshold-dependent effect was not uniform across translational axes (Figure 3B). LAT delta values were relatively stable across the tested threshold range, and LNG values showed only modest variation. In contrast, VRT values decreased substantially as the threshold was lowered from −150 HU toward the lower-threshold range. The lowest aggregated VRT value was observed around −650 HU, although adjacent thresholds, particularly −550 and −750 HU, showed comparable values in several thoracic phantom conditions.

The change from −350 HU to −650 HU further demonstrated the direction-dependent nature of the threshold effect (Figure 3C). Reductions in absolute residual delta values were larger for VRT and vector residuals than for LAT or LNG residuals. This indicates that the practical effect of CT threshold selection in the tested workflow was driven primarily by vertical surface-registration behavior rather than by lateral or longitudinal displacement.

Phantom- and ROI-specific observations. In the cube phantom, LAT and LNG delta values showed only small changes across thresholds in both system configurations, whereas VRT delta values showed larger threshold-dependent variation (Figure 4). At −550, −650, and −750 HU, VRT delta values were 0.5, 0.1, and 0.3 mm in Group A and 0.2, 0.1, and 0.6 mm in Group B, respectively. Thus, −650 HU yielded the smallest VRT delta value in the cube phantom for both configurations.

Residual delta values for the cube phantom in Groups A and B. (A) Group A and (B) Group B. HU, Hounsfield unit; LAT, lateral; LNG, longitudinal; VRT, vertical.

In the thoracic phantom, LAT and LNG delta values remained relatively stable, generally within the submillimeter range (Figure 5). VRT delta values varied less than in the cube phantom but still showed threshold-related changes. For the central ROI, the VRT delta values at −550, −650, and −750 HU were 0.1, 0.1, and 0.0 mm in Group A and 0.5, 0.5, and 0.4 mm in Group B, respectively.

Across the thoracic ROI configurations, the overall threshold-response pattern was similar, although LAT delta values varied according to ROI position. At −650 HU, the LAT values in Group A were −0.3, −0.5, and 0.1 mm for the central, right, and left ROIs, respectively. In Group B, the corresponding values were 0.2, −0.4, and 0.6 mm. These findings suggest that ROI geometry and lateral position can influence the direction of surface-monitoring delta values even when the same CT threshold is used. The ROI-dependent LAT residual pattern is further illustrated in Figure 6, supporting the need to evaluate representative central and lateral monitoring ROIs during local SGRT commissioning.

Additional technical analyses and uncertainty context. The additional technical analyses and measurement-chain context are summarized in Figure 7. In the treatment planning system comparison, RayStation and Eclipse showed similar overall threshold-dependent trends, but Eclipse exhibited larger VRT variation under the tested condition (Figure 7A). The observed ranges of VRT, LNG, and LAT delta values were 0.8, 0.3, and 0.0 mm for RayStation and 2.6, 0.7, and 0.0 mm for Eclipse, respectively. Thus, LAT variation was negligible, whereas VRT showed the largest observed variation under this restricted TPS-comparison condition. Because this comparison was limited to Group A and the thoracic phantom central ROI, it should be interpreted as a workflow-specific technical observation rather than as a generalized inter-TPS comparison. At −650 HU, comparison between the clinically usable 500- and 450-mm FOV settings showed only small residual-delta variation (Figure 7B). The measurement-chain context is shown in Figure 7C. Repeatability estimates and MV-CBCT coordinate discrepancy were in the submillimeter range, whereas the observed VRT threshold effect was larger than the LAT and LNG threshold effects. This comparison indicates that small residual differences, particularly those in LAT and LNG or in restricted secondary analyses, should be interpreted cautiously relative to the measurement-chain uncertainty. In contrast, the larger VRT threshold-dependent variation suggests that CT threshold selection can have a measurable effect on vertical AlignRT delta values in this workflow.

Discussion

This study addresses a practical, but clinically relevant source of uncertainty in SGRT-based cancer radiotherapy: the CT threshold used to generate DICOM reference surfaces for optical surface monitoring. The principal finding is that CT-threshold selection primarily affects vertical AlignRT delta values, whereas lateral and longitudinal values remain relatively stable. The integrated analysis (Figure 3) showed that lower thresholds, particularly approximately −550 to −750 HU, reduced vertical delta values under the tested workflow. A threshold of −650 HU yielded the smallest aggregated VRT value and the smallest VRT value in the cube phantom for both system configurations, whereas −550, −650, and −750 HU provided comparable results in several thoracic phantom conditions. These findings suggest that reliance on default external-contour settings should be avoided, and CT-derived surface generation should instead be verified during institutional SGRT commissioning.

This reframed interpretation is important in the context of clinical translation. In breast and thoracic cancer radiotherapy, SGRT is frequently used to support a tattoo-free setup, deep-inspiration breath-hold treatment, arm-position reproducibility, and intrafraction monitoring. Thus, a systematic discrepancy between the CT-derived DICOM surface and the monitored optical surface could generate persistent online delta values, thereby reducing user confidence in the SGRT workflow. Therefore, even submillimeter threshold-dependent differences may be relevant during commissioning, especially in the context of hospital departments aiming to standardize setup procedures across operators and treatment units.

At the same time, the clinical meaning of the measured delta values must be interpreted cautiously. In this study, all measurements were obtained following performance of CBCT-based six-degree-of-freedom correction. Therefore, the resulting AlignRT delta values constitute end-to-end workflow measurements rather than results reflecting the isolated effect of HU thresholding. Moreover, the contributions from the CBCT registration, couch correction, MV-CBCT coordinate agreement, phantom setup reproducibility, and AlignRT calibration cannot be fully separated. This point is particularly relevant because several of the observed differences were within the expected submillimeter uncertainty range of the measurement chain.

The finding that smaller residual delta values were observed for lower HU thresholds is consistent with the results of prior phantom studies on CT-derived reference surfaces and surface-guided localization systems (17-19). In particular, while using an AlignRT cube phantom, Zhou et al. (17) reported differences between DICOM- and SGRT-based reference surfaces. Similarly, Goodall et al. (18) and Baltz et al. (19) observed a tendency for positional errors to decrease when lower thresholds were used; however, in those studies, no single threshold was found to be optimal across all conditions. The present study supports the same practical message: threshold selection matters; however, the appropriate value is likely system-, phantom-, and workflow-dependent.

The anthropomorphic thoracic phantom was used to simulate the human body under controlled experimental conditions. In actual clinical settings, breast motion due to respiration typically ranges from 2 to 3 mm (20), and anatomical changes, such as edema, may also occur during treatment. These factors introduce various uncertainties that make it difficult to precisely determine an optimal HU threshold in patients. Therefore, validation under controlled conditions using an anthropomorphic thoracic phantom is useful for investigating appropriate CT-threshold values.

The thoracic-phantom results also highlight the importance of monitoring ROI and isocenter-related surface geometry. LAT delta values changed according to the monitoring-ROI position in both Groups A and B, and the shift direction differed between the central and lateral ROI conditions. Importantly, this ROI-dependent LAT residual pattern was observed consistently in both clinical system configurations. This finding was one of the clinically relevant observations of the present study because lateral surface-monitoring residuals may depend not only on the CT threshold but also on how the monitoring ROI is selected relative to the isocenter and phantom geometry. This observation may reflect surface geometry, camera-viewing geometry, optical sampling density, or calibration-related factors. However, because only two clinical configurations were evaluated, this behavior should not be considered intrinsic to AlignRT as a platform. Instead, these results indicate that institutions should test representative central and lateral monitoring ROIs during SGRT commissioning rather than relying only on a single central phantom setup. The restricted TPS comparison (Figure 7A) suggests that the external-contour generation process may also contribute to the workflow variability. Although similar trends were observed for RayStation and Eclipse in the restricted comparison, the larger VRT variation was obtained for Eclipse. This result may reflect differences in the contour-generation algorithms, interpolation, surface smoothing, or DICOM export behavior. However, as this comparison was limited to one system configuration and one thoracic ROI only, additional cross-platform measurements are required to support general conclusions.

From a practical commissioning perspective, the present results support evaluation of a lower CT-threshold range rather than adoption of a default value without verification. A value of approximately −650 HU may be a reasonable starting point for institutions using similar TrueBeam-AlignRT workflows; however, values of –550 and –750 HU may yield comparable performance depending on the phantom geometry and system configuration. Therefore, the most clinically useful recommendation is that a universal threshold should not be prescribed, but rather, the threshold-dependent surface behavior should be verified based on the local TPS, CT protocol, monitoring ROI, and SGRT system.

Study limitations. First, repeated measurements were not performed for each condition and, therefore, the measurement variability, confidence intervals, and statistical comparisons could not be reported. Second, the endpoint was an end-to-end post-CBCT AlignRT delta value and, thus, the geometric difference between the CT-derived and optically acquired surfaces was not isolated. Third, only translational values were analyzed. The rotational offsets may be relevant in cases where the thresholding changes the surface shape, particularly for asymmetric ROIs. Fourth, the anthropomorphic evaluation was limited to a female thoracic phantom with a white surface; thus, the results may not apply to other anatomical sites, body habitus, skin tones, or surface reflectance conditions. Fifth, additional TPS, FOV, and CT acquisition-position analyses were performed under restricted conditions only.

Despite these limitations, this study provides practical information for cancer radiotherapy departments that use CT-derived DICOM surfaces in AlignRT. The results emphasize the need to consider threshold selection, ROI definition, planning-system behavior, and measurement-chain uncertainty together during SGRT commissioning. Future studies should include repeated measurements, direct geometric surface comparisons, rotational analysis, patient-like surface materials, and multicenter evaluation across different SGRT platforms and TPS.

Conclusion

The CT-threshold selection influenced the results of CT-derived DICOM surface generation for AlignRT-guided SGRT, particularly in the vertical direction. Under the tested institutional workflow, lower HU thresholds, including values of approximately –650 HU, produced small residual AlignRT delta values; thus, such thresholds may provide a practical starting point to enable accurate surface monitoring in cancer radiotherapy setups. However, because the optimal threshold may vary according to the phantom geometry, monitoring ROI, TPS, camera configuration, and local imaging workflow, institution-specific commissioning is required before clinical implementation.
