Modeling spatially-resolved galactic H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha emission for galaxy clustering
Ivan Rapoport, Vincent Desjacques, Gabriele Parimbelli, Ehud Behar, Martin Crocce

Abstract
Near-infrared spectroscopic surveys target high-redshift emission-line galaxies (ELGs) to probe cosmological scenarios. Understanding the clustering properties of ELGs is essential to derive optimal constraints. We present a simple radiative transfer model for spatially resolved galactic H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha emission, which includes emission from the warm-hot diffuse interstellar medium. The atomic level populations are in steady-state and computed in the coronal approximation. The model is applied to multiple IllustrisTNG simulations in the redshift range 1 ≤ z ≤ 2 1\leq z\leq 2 to produce the luminosity function (LF) and the halo occupation distribution (HOD). Collisional processes account for a significant fraction of ≈ 40 % \approx 40\% of the total H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha luminosity ( L H ​ α L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} ). Our LFs are in reasonable agreement with measurements from H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha surveys if a uniform extinction of 0.3 < A H ​ α < 0.85 0.3<A_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}<0.85 mag is assumed. Our HOD is consistent with that of the Euclid Flagship galaxy mock up to differences that can be attributed to baryonic feedback, which is absent from the latter. When H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha luminosities are computed from an empirical relation between L H ​ α L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} and the total star formation rate (SFR) the resulting LFs are in tension with previous observations. Our approach can be extended to other atomic lines, which should be helpful for the mining of high-redshift galaxy spectra in forthcoming surveys.



I
Introduction

Emission-line galaxies (ELGs) are characterized by strong emission lines in their spectra, which can be exploited to obtain accurate redshift determinations. As a result, ELGs provide a (biased) sampling of the matter distribution across large comoving volumes. Measurements of their spatial distribution can be exploited to constrain viable cosmological models
(
Peebles 1980
;
Kaiser 1987
;
Efstathiou et al. 1990
;
Ballinger et al. 1996
;
Peacock et al. 2001
;
Tegmark et al. 2004
;
Cole et al. 2005
;
Eisenstein et al. 2005
;
Guzzo et al. 2008
;
Anderson et al. 2012
, e.g.)
. In particular, forthcoming measurement of ELG clustering statistics by ground- and space-based galaxy surveys such as
Euclid
, DESI, and SPHEREx
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024d
;
Aghamousa et al. 2016
;
Doré et al. 2014
)
will
probe the dark energy equation of state, the initial conditions of the Universe, the nature of dark matter and the mass of neutrinos with unprecedented accuracy.
In addition to their relevance as tracers of the underlying large scale structure, ELGs have the potential, through observables such as luminosities and line profiles, of revealing the history of galaxy evolution, star formation, and gas dynamics in a crucial epoch coinciding with the peak of galaxy formation.

The NISP instrument on board of the
Euclid
satellite
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024d
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024e
)
will obtain spectra in the wavelength range
0.92\,\mu{\rm m}<\lambda<2.02\,\mu{\rm m}
and allow the detection of a few thousand
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission line galaxies per square degree in the redshift range
0.9\leq z\leq 1.8

(
Geach et al. 2010
;
Pozzetti et al. 2016
;
Merson et al. 2017
)
.
An accurate modelling of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission line is essential to determine the window function of the
Euclid
survey, and maximize the cosmological information that can be extracted from the data.

\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission is prominent in HII regions surrounding young massive stars, where it is produced by recombinations of free electrons and protons. For this reason,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
has been used extensively as a tracer of the (instantaneous) star formation rate (SFR) and variability in star formation activity
(
Kennicutt 1983
;
Lee et al. 2009
;
Weisz et al. 2012
;
Shivaei et al. 2015
, e.g.)
. However, while the connection between
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity and SFR is firmly established, uncertainties remain in the modelling of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters due to additional sources of emission arising from e.g. the hot and diffuse gas in the interstellar medium (ISM). Narrow-band surveys of local galaxies indicate that emission from the diffuse ISM may contribute as much as 70% to the total galactic
\mathrm{H}\alpha
flux
(
Zurita et al. 2000
;
Kreckel et al. 2016
, e.g.)
.

Theoretical approaches to the modelling of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters achieve different levels of self-consistency and complexity. Self-consistent radiative transfer simulations propagate rays of ionizing and Lyman-continuum photons to calculate the emission and transport of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photons, allowing for the study of dust scattering and escape fractions
(
Katz et al. 2019
;
Wilkins et al. 2020
;
Tacchella et al. 2022
)
. While being highly detailed, such methods are often computationally too expensive to be applied to a large number of simulated galaxies. Alternative approaches utilize semi-analytical nebular emission models
(
Pellegrini et al. 2020
;
Hirschmann et al. 2023
;
Pei et al. 2024
)
or semi-analytical model of galaxy formation calibrated with observational data
(
Merson et al. 2017
;
Nusser et al. 2020
;
Ortega-Martinez et al. 2024a
;
Ortega-Martinez et al. 2024b
)
to predict emission line luminosities and cumulative flux counts.
The model implemented in the Flagship galaxy mock of the
Euclid
collaboration
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024g
)
relies on a combination of a halo occupation distribution
(
Benson et al. 2000
;
Peacock & Smith 2000
;
Scoccimarro et al. 2001
;
Berlind & Weinberg 2002
;
Zheng et al. 2005
, HOD, see e.g.)
with empirical relations in order to assign
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosities to central and satellite galaxies painted on top of dark matter halos.

In this work, we present a simple radiative transfer model of galactic
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission, which can be straightforwardly applied to hydro-dynamical simulations of galaxy formation and evolution. The uniqueness of our approach lies in its balance between a detailed modelling of the physics controlling
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission, computational tractability and versatility. Our model can be readily extended to other emission lines of the hydrogen atom and heavier elements. Here, we apply our approach to the IllustrisTNG simulations to study the halo occupation distribution of our mock
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters assuming the simplest halo mass-based description.
The HOD framework provides an empirical description of galaxy biasing
(for a review,
Desjacques et al. 2018
, and references therein)
which is routinely applied to either construct mock galaxy catalogues or analyze data
(see in particular
Gao et al. 2023
;
Garcia-Quintero et al. 2024
;
Khederlarian et al. 2024
, for the HOD modelling of the ELGs surveyed by the DESI experiment)
. Therefore, we shall also compare our HOD to the
Euclid
Flagship galaxy mock, and assess the sensitivity of our predictions to model systematics.

The paper is organized as follows: we briefly introduce the IllustrisTNG simulations along with key physical quantities in section §
II
; we describe our
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission model in §
III
; we present our results for the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity function and HOD in §
IV
; we conclude in §
V
.

The Illustris-TNG simulations

Specifications

II.2
Halos and subhalos

In each snapshot, particles are grouped into halos and subhalos with a two-stage hierarchical search using the
FoF
(friends-of-friends) and
SUBFIND
algorithms
(
Davis et al. 1985
;
Springel et al. 2001
)
, respectively. The
FoF
algorithm defines parent halos by assigning any two dark matter particles to the same group if their distance is below a fixed linking length of 0.2 times the mean inter-particle spacing. Other types of particles are included in the halos of their nearest dark matter particle. The
SUBFIND
algorithm classifies further the particles into subhalos by identifying virialized substructures within parent FoF halos. We define an ELG candidate as any subhalo hosting at least 1 gas particle and 1 star particle although, in practice, we will only consider ELGs with a
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line luminosity
\geq 10^{39}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}
. As a consequence, subhalo gas particles may extend much farther than the disk up to the boundaries of the dark matter subhalo. Hence, we shall model emission from both the interstellar medium and circumgalactic medium of ELGs.

To compute the halo occupation distribution (HOD) of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission line galaxies, we will use the IllustrisTNG flags to determine whether an ELG is a central or a satellite galaxy. In these simulations, the subhalo which has the largest total number of member particles is flagged as the central/primary subhalo of the host parent halo (this does not always result in the most massive subhalo flagged as the central galaxy due to the adaptive spatial discretization of the gas cells). The number of central ELGs residing in a host halo,
N_{c}
, can be either 0 or 1. By contrast, the number of satellite ELGs in the halo,
N_{s}
, can be any non-negative integer.

II.3
Properties of the gas cells

We model the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission at the level of (adaptive) gas cells using the information provided for each cell about the total mass
m_{\text{gas}}
, mass density
\rho_{\text{gas}}
, the hydrogen fractional mass
X_{\text{H}}
, the electron and neutral hydrogen abundance ratios
x_{e},\ x_{\rm{HI}}
and the internal energy per unit mass
u
, the instantaneous star formation rate
SFR
and the local flux
f_{\text{AGN}}
produced by active galactic nuclei. The key physical ingredients of our model are the electron, neutral and ionized hydrogen number densities
n_{e}
,
n_{\rm{HI}}
,
n_{\rm{HII}}
, the temperature
T
, the cell volume
V_{\text{gas}}
, as well as SFR and
f_{\text{AGN}}
.

The volume of the gas cell is computed as

V_{\text{gas}}=\frac{m_{\text{gas}}}{\rho_{\text{gas}}}\;.

(1)

The total hydrogen number density
n_{\text{H}}=n_{\rm{HI}}+n_{\text{HII}}
is computed from

n_{\text{H}}=\frac{X_{\text{H}}\rho_{\text{gas}}}{m_{p}}\;,

(2)

where
m_{p}
is the proton mass.
The number densities
n_{e}
,
n_{\rm{HI}}
and
n_{\rm{HII}}
are computed using the abundances as

\displaystyle n_{e}=x_{e}n_{\text{H}}

(3)

\displaystyle n_{\rm{HI}}=x_{\rm{HI}}n_{\text{H}}

\displaystyle n_{\rm{HII}}=(1-x_{\rm{HI}})n_{\text{H}}\;.

We note that due to the ionization of helium and heavier elements,
x_{e}
can be larger than unity. Likewise, we also have
x_{e}+x_{\rm{HI}}\geq 1
.

We write the mean molecular weight as

\mu=\frac{4}{1+3X_{\text{H}}+4X_{\text{H}}x_{e}}\;,

(4)

where we neglect the mass in metals and electrons. Values of
\mu
vary from 0.62 for (solar-abundance) fully ionized gas (
x_{e}\approx 1.2
) to 1.29 for neutral gas (
x_{e}=0
). The gas temperature
T
is then computed assuming the ideal gas equation of state (EOS)

T=\frac{(\gamma-1)\mu m_{p}}{k}\,u\;.

(5)

Here,
k
is the Boltzmann constant and we adopt an adiabatic index
\gamma=5/3
appropriate to a monoatomic gas. The thermodynamic temperature inferred above exhibits a floor around
\sim 10^{4}\ {\rm K}
due to the multi-phase nature of the hydrodynamics model implemented in the Illustris-TNG simulations
(
Springel & Hernquist 2003
)
.
While Eq. (
5
) correctly represents the temperature of the warm and dilute phase of the interstellar medium (ISM), it is not the correct temperature in regions hosting dense and cold star-forming cores, for which
T\ll 10^{4}\ {\rm K}
.
We assume a fiducial fraction of
10\%
for the mass in the warm phase if the cell is star-forming, and
100\%
otherwise.
This simple prescription accounts for the results of
Springel & Hernquist 2003
.
This way,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission from the warm ISM phase is computed uniformly for all the cells from the knowledge of the temperature (through Eq. (
5
))
and the rest of the model parameters, except that the emission is reduced to 10% for star-forming cells.

III
Modelling the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission line

We consider two sources of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in a galaxy: (i) the warm ISM phase and (ii) star-forming
\rm{HII}
regions. In the warm ISM,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission is induced by electron collisional excitation (CE) of neutral hydrogen, by photo-excitation (PE) of neutral hydrogen by the combined radiation field of nearby hot stars and local AGN, and by radiative recombination (RR) of protons with electrons. We obtain the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission rate of the ISM upon solving the set of rate equations for the population
n_{i}
of the hydrogen energy levels in steady state (
dn_{i}/dt=0
). The model includes all
N=25
bound levels with principal quantum numbers
1\leq n\leq 5
, and the ionized level
n_{N+1}
(a bare proton, i.e., the neutral H continuum).
The rate equation for the
i
th energy level takes the form

\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}Q_{ij}n_{j}=0\;.

(6)

Since ISM densities are low, populations of excited levels are also low, the only depletion of excited levels is through spontaneous emission, and all other transitions from excited levels can be neglected.
The transition rate
Q_{ij}
under this coronal approximation
is computed according to the ruleset

Q_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\sum\limits_{k<i}A_{ik}\;,&\text{if }j=i\\
R_{1i}^{\text{exc}}\;,&\text{if }1<i\leq N\\
A_{ji}\;,&\text{if }j>i\\
R_{\infty i}^{\text{RR}}\;,&\text{if }j=N+1\end{array}\right.

(7)

Here,
A_{ji}
denotes the Einstein coefficient for
j\to i
spontaneous emission.
R_{1j}^{\text{exc}}
is the total rate of CE and PE from the ground level to level i, and

R_{\infty i}^{\text{RR}}
is the rate for RR of a bare proton into level
i
. To have a non-trivial solution to the
N+1
dependent equations in Eq. (
6
), an additional normalization is required, for example
n_{1}=1
. We use the
CHIANTI
atomic database for all the hydrogen atomic data
(
Dere et al. 1997
;
Del Zanna et al. 2015
)
.

The assumption for a steady state is fulfilled as long as the relaxation timescale of the perturbed ISM toward steady state is shorter than the characteristic evolution timescale of a galaxy. Typical recombination rates in the ISM, assuming an electron density of
n_{e}=1
cm
-3
are
10^{-14}
s
-1
or higher. Excitation and decay rates are higher. Hence, relaxation times are of the order of millions of years, and the steady state assumption holds.

The total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photon emission rate per H particle,
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
, is then given by the following sum over all possible transitions from levels with principal quantum number
n=3
to
n=2

R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=\sum_{i\in\{n=3\}}\sum_{j\in\{n=2\}}n_{i}A_{ij}\;.

(8)

In the low-density coronal regime, excitation and recombination can be decoupled. As a result, the total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
is the sum of the luminosities arising from each physical mechanism,

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}+L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}+L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}+L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}+L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{HII}}\;.

(9)

We do not include
\mathrm{H}\alpha
dust extinction and model only intrinsic luminosities.

III.1
Collisional excitation in the warm-hot ISM phase

The contribution of collisional excitation to the galaxy
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity reads

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}=E_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\sum_{\text{gas cells}}V_{\text{gas}}\,n_{\rm{HI}}\,R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}\,\Theta_{\text{SFR}}\;,

(10)

where
E_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\approx 1.89\ {\rm eV}
is the energy of a resonant
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photon,
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}
is the total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photon emission rate (in s
-1
) per
\rm{HI}
particle due to CE, and the sum is over all the gas particles belonging to the subhalo of interest.
The operator
\Theta_{\text{SFR}}
encodes the reduction of the collisionally-induced
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in star-forming cells,

\Theta_{\text{SFR}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\lambda_{\mathrm{h}}\;,&\text{if SFR}>0\\
1\;,&\text{if SFR}=0\end{array}\right.

(11)

where the hot ISM fraction
\lambda_{\mathrm{h}}=0.1
is the same for all star-forming cells (see Section §
II.3
).

We adopt the formula of
Burgess & Tully 1992
for the rate coefficient
\alpha_{ij}^{\text{CE}}
for CE from levels
i\to j
with
E_{j}>E_{i}
,

\alpha_{ij}^{\text{CE}}=2\pi^{1/2}a_{0}\hbar m_{e}^{-1}\left(\frac{I_{\infty}}{kT}\right)^{1/2}\exp{\left(-\frac{E_{ij}}{kT}\right)}\frac{\Upsilon_{ij}}{g_{i}}\;.

(12)

Here,
I_{\infty}\approx 13.6\ {\rm eV}
is the hydrogen’s ionization energy (Rydberg constant),
E_{ij}=|E_{j}-E_{i}|
is the transition energy,
a_{0}
is the Bohr radius,
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant,
m_{e}
is the electron mass,
g_{i}=2J_{i}+1
is the multiplicity of the lower level
i
, and
\Upsilon_{ij}(T)\sim 1
is the Maxwellian-averaged collision strength. Note also that
2\pi^{1/2}a_{0}\hbar m_{e}^{-1}\approx 2.17\times 10^{-8}\ {\rm cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}
.
The
i\to j
excitation rate (per hydrogen atom) is then given by
n_{e}\alpha_{ij}^{\text{CE}}
. In light of the low ISM density, we neglect collisional de-excitations and set
\alpha_{ij}^{\text{CE}}=0
if
E_{i}\geq E_{j}
.
In this coronal approximation, the rate equations for
n_{i}
reduce to

\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=n_{e}\alpha_{1i}^{\text{CE}}n_{1}+\sum_{j}\left(A_{ji}n_{j}-A_{ij}n_{i}\right)=0\;,

(13)

where we set
n_{1}=1
and compute the emission rate
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}
according to Eq. (
8
) from
n_{i}
at steady state. It is obvious from Eq. (
13
) that the steady state populations satisfy
n_{i}\propto n_{e}
, and so does
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}\propto n_{e}
. Therefore, we may write

R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}=n_{e}\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}(T)\;,

(14)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}(T)
is a temperature-dependent effective rate coefficient for the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission induced by CE. The solution for
\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}(T)
is shown in figure
1
. The overall
\mathrm{H}\alpha
rate per particle peaks at
T\approx 4\times 10^{5}\ {\rm K}
, while the conversion probability (defined as the ratio of
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}
to the total CE rate) is maximized for temperature
T\approx 0.7\times 10^{5}\ {\rm K}
at which approximately 8% of CE events produce a
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photon. Note that the coronal regime breaks down when the population of the first excited state can grow significantly, which happens when
n_{e}\alpha_{12}^{\text{CE}}\gtrsim A_{21}
. This, however, implies number densities
n_{e}\gtrsim 10^{8}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}
, orders of magnitude larger than those found in the dilute ISM and in illustrisTNG gas cells.

\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{CE}}(T)
for
\mathrm{H}\alpha

photon in a CE event (dashed red curve). Both are shown as a function of the gas cell temperature

III.2
Photo-excitation by hot stars

The contribution of PE by hot stars to the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in the dilute ISM is computed as

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}=E_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\sum_{\text{gas cells}}\,V_{\text{gas}}\,n_{\rm{HI}}R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}\,\Theta_{\text{SFR}}\;,

(15)

where
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}
is a rate of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission due to PE per
\rm{HI}
atom.
To compute the photo-excitation rate of the diffuse ISM by stars in each gas cell, we consider first the excitation rate arising from a single source with photon luminosity density
L_{E}(E)
(in units of photons erg
-1
s
-1
). The photon flux decreases with the distance
r
from this source as

F_{E}(E)=\frac{L_{E}(E)}{4\pi r^{2}}\;.

(16)

The
i\to j
PE rate
R_{ij}
induced by a single source is obtained upon integrating over
E
the previous expression times the PE cross section
\sigma_{ij}(E)
, which exhibits a narrow peak around the transition energy
E_{ij}
. In cgs units, this is

\displaystyle R_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int F_{E}(E)\sigma_{ij}(E)dE

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{he^{2}f_{ij}}{4m_{e}c}\,\frac{L_{E}(E_{ij})}{r^{2}}\;,

where
e
is the electron charge and
f_{ij}
is the oscillator strength of the
i\to j
absorption. Approximating the spectral energy distribution (SED) of stars by perfect blackbodies, we have

L_{E}(E)=\frac{8\pi^{2}hR_{*}^{2}c^{2}}{(hc)^{4}}\frac{E^{2}}{e^{E/kT_{*}}-1}\;,

(18)

where
R_{*}
is the stellar radius and
T_{*}
the effective blackbody temperature. Note that the (bolometric) stellar luminosity recovers the Stefan-Boltzmann law of
L_{*}=\int EL_{E}(E)dE=4\pi R_{*}^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}T_{*}^{4}
where
\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.

The total PE rate
R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}
at the locations of interest (e.g. at the center of gas cells) depends on the distances from individual stars, which we do not have from the simulations. To remedy this lack of information, we could generate a Monte-Carlo realization of the stellar spatial distribution consistent with the properties of a given synthetic galaxy at the cell level, and sum
R_{ij}
over all stars to obtain
R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}
. However, such an approach would be computationally expensive. Therefore, we shall instead approximate
R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}
by its average over Monte-Carlo realisations of the stellar distribution. This approximation is justified so long as the number of stars per cell is large (which ensures that Poisson fluctuations are small). Furthermore, we shall take into account that radiation is attenuated as it propagates in the hot ISM.

Summarizing, we express the total PE rate as

R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}=\frac{he^{2}f_{ij}}{4m_{e}c}\,\big\langle J_{E}(E_{ij})\big\rangle\;,

(19)

where the mean photon specific intensity is defined as

\big\langle J_{E}(E_{ij})\big\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{n}\frac{L_{E,n}(E_{ij})}{r_{n}^{2}}\,e^{-r_{n}/\lambda_{\text{mfp}}}\right\rangle\;.

(20)

Here,
L_{E,n}
is the photon luminosity density of the
n
th star and
r_{n}
is its distance from the point of interest. The attenuation length
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
generally depends on the energy
E_{ij}
of the photons, the environment of the gas cell etc.

To estimate the ensemble average Eq. (
19
), consider the simplified case of Poisson distributed sources inside a sphere of radius
R
. Assuming a constant
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
for simplicity and adapting the results of
Zuo 1992
;
Meiksin & White 2003
for the mean specific intensity, the mean photon specific intensity
\langle J_{E}(E_{ij})\rangle
at the center of the sphere is given by

\langle J_{E}(E_{ij})\rangle=n_{*}\,\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\left(1-e^{-R/\lambda_{\text{mfp}}}\right)\,\big\langle L_{E}(E_{ij})\big\rangle\;,

where
\langle L_{E}(E_{ij}\rangle
is the average photon luminosity density of stars at energy
E_{ij}
and
n_{*}
is the mean stellar number density. This result provides a useful, and reasonable, approximation to Eq. (
19
) if the characteristic distance
r_{0}\equiv\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\left(1-e^{-R/\lambda_{\text{mfp}}}\right)
beyond which stars contribute negligibly is not much larger than the characteristic cell size
V_{\text{gas}}^{1/3}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)\ {\rm kpc}
of the simulations. In this case, stars from the cell under consideration will provide the dominant contribution to
\langle J_{E}(E_{ij})\rangle
. Consequently, the ensemble average can be computed locally (i.e. in each gas cell) with the cell-dependent stellar density
n_{*}
.

To ascertain whether this is the case, consider photons with energy
E_{\text{Ly}\alpha}
corresponding to the Ly
\alpha
resonance with cross-section
\sigma_{1s\to 2p}\simeq 10^{-17}\ {\rm cm^{2}}
. We expect that
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
is of order the average distance between dense gas clouds with column densities
\gtrsim 10^{17}\ {\rm cm^{-2}}
. In the reference run TNG300-1, the typical fraction of such high-column density systems in galaxies is a few percents. Ignoring the anisotropy of the spatial distribution of these strong absorbers (they are mainly located in and around the disk), this suggests that
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}
is a fraction of the typical size
R\sim\mathcal{O}(10)
kpc of a galaxy, i.e.,
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)\ {\rm kpc}\ll R
. Therefore, we estimate
r_{0}\simeq\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\simeq V_{\text{gas}}^{1/3}
, which motivates the local approximation

R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}=\frac{\pi he^{2}f_{ij}}{m_{e}c}\,n_{*}\,r_{0}\,\big\langle L_{E}(E_{ij})\big\rangle\;,

(21)

for the total PE rate in a given gas cell of stellar density
n_{*}
. For simplicity, we shall hereafter assume a single, fiducial value of
r_{0}=1\ {\rm kpc}
for all cells and galaxies regardless of the photon energy
E_{ij}
. The sensitivity of our predictions to the choice of
r_{0}
will be discussed in §
IV
.

Our approximation to the local PE rate depends on the average photon luminosity density
\langle L_{E}(E_{ij})\rangle
of the stars, which involves an integral over the stellar initial mass function (IMF)
\phi(M_{*})
. Our calculations assume a Kroupa IMF
(
Kroupa 2002
)

\phi(M_{*})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}M_{*}^{-0.3}&(M_{*}<0.08)\\
0.08M_{*}^{-1.3}&(0.08\leq M_{*}<0.5)\\
0.04M_{*}^{-2.3}&(0.05\leq M_{*}<1)\\
0.04M_{*}^{-2.7}&(M_{*}\geq 1)\end{array}\right.

(22)

which is normalized to unity for
M_{*}\in\left(0.05,150\right)M_{\odot}
. Furthermore, we will consider only hot stars with a mass
M_{*}\geq 15\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
, which provide the bulk of the PE photons. The short lifetimes of these stars (a few Myr)
allows us to estimate their number density from the knowledge of the instantaneous SFR of each gas cell. Using a unique lifetime
t_{*}=10{\ {\rm Myr}}
, our final expression for the PE rate of each gas cell is

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi^{3}e^{2}f_{ij}E_{ij}^{2}}{m_{e}h^{2}c^{3}}\,n_{*}r_{0}

(23)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathcal{N}_{M_{*}}\int_{15M_{\odot}}^{150M_{\odot}}\!\!\frac{R_{*}^{2}\,\phi(M_{*})}{\exp{E_{ij}/kT_{*}}-1}dM_{*}\;,

where

n_{*}=\frac{1}{V_{\text{gas}}}\int_{15M_{\odot}}^{150M_{\odot}}\!\!\frac{t_{*}\,{\rm SFR}}{M_{*}}\phi(M_{*})dM_{*}

(24)

is the local, cell-dependent stellar density. The normalization constant is
\mathcal{N}_{M_{*}}=(\int_{15M_{\odot}}^{150M_{\odot}}\phi(M_{*})dM_{*})^{-1}\approx 1.6\times 10^{3}
for the IMF considered here.
To carry out the integration over the IMF, we use the simplified stellar dependencies of
R_{*}
and
T_{*}
on
M_{*}
given by
(
Demircan & Kahraman 1991
)

\displaystyle T_{*}=T_{\odot}M_{*}^{0.475}

(25)

\displaystyle R_{*}=R_{\odot}M_{*}^{0.8}\;,

where
T_{\odot}=5785\ {\rm K}
and
R_{\odot}=6.96\cdot 10^{10}\ \text{cm}
.

For illustration, the photo-excitation rate
R_{ij}^{\text{PE,stars}}/f_{ij}
given by Eq. (
23
) is shown in Figure
2
as a function of the photon energy
E_{ij}
for a gas cell with
n_{*}=10^{3}{\ {\rm kpc}^{-3}}
. The vertical lines indicate transitions of the Lyman series as quoted in the figure. We repeat the procedure of §
III.1
and solve

\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=R_{1i}^{\text{PE,stars}}n_{1}+\sum_{j}\left(A_{ji}n_{j}-A_{ij}n_{i}\right)

(26)

at steady state to calculate the populations of photo-excited hydrogen atoms. The scaling
R^{\text{PE,stars}}_{1i}\propto n_{*}r_{0}
propagates to the steady state populations, which satisfy also
n_{i}\propto n_{*}r_{0}
. Therefore, the computation of the total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission induced by CE events from Eq. (
8
) can be recast into the form

R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}\,n_{*}\,r_{0}

(27)

where the coefficient

\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,stars}}=9.60\times 10^{-13}\ \text{kpc}^{2}\,\text{s}^{-1}

(28)

does not depend on the properties of the gas cell.
To conclude this Section, we note that including the clustering of the sources would not change the average PE rate in a given gas cell, but it would broaden the variance of fluctuations
(see
Desjacques et al. 2014
, for a related discussion)
.

III.3
Photo-excitation by AGNs

Likewise, the contribution of AGN radiation to
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in the dilute ISM is

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}=E_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\sum_{\text{gas cells}}V_{\text{gas}}\,n_{\rm{HI}}\,R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}\,\Theta_{\text{SFR}}\;,

(29)

where
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}
is the rate of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission per
\rm{HI}
atom following a photo-excitation by AGN radiation. To describe the SED of AGNs, we adopt a power-law with a photon luminosity density index
\Gamma=2
, such that
L^{\text{AGN}}_{E}(E)\propto E^{-2}
. The bolometric luminosity
L_{\rm{AGN}}\equiv\int EL^{\text{AGN}}_{E}(E)dE
can be used to write the photon luminosity density as

L_{\rm{AGN}}(E)=\frac{L_{\rm{AGN}}}{\ln(E_{+}/E_{-})}\frac{1}{E^{2}}=\frac{4\pi r^{2}f_{\rm{AGN}}}{\ln(E_{+}/E_{-})}\frac{1}{E^{2}}\;,

(30)

where
E_{-}=1\ {\rm eV}
and
E_{+}=10^{5}\ {\rm eV}
are the boundaries of the integration domain and we assume that the AGN radiation is isotropic. The last expression involves the bolometric AGN flux
f_{\text{AGN}}
, which is evolved in the IllustrisTNG simulations at a cell level. We have checked that our predictions are weakly dependent on the choice of
E_{\pm}
owing to the logarithmic dependence.

In complete analogy with Eq. (
17
), the AGN contribution to the
i\to j
PE rate is

R_{ij}^{\rm{PE,AGN}}=\frac{\pi he^{2}f_{ij}}{m_{e}c}\frac{f_{\rm{AGN}}}{(5\ln{10})E_{ij}^{2}}\exp{\left(-|\vec{r}_{\text{g}}|/r_{0}\right)}\;,

(31)

where we have assumed that the resonant AGN radiation is attenuated across the same (uniform) characteristic length
\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\simeq r_{0}\simeq\mathcal{O}(1)\ {\rm kpc}
as the stellar radiation, and
\vec{r}_{\text{g}}
is the position of the gas cell relative to the galaxy center (defined as the position of the most bound particle in IllustrisTNG). Observe that
R_{ij}^{\rm{PE,AGN}}\propto f_{\text{AGN}}
regardless of the choice for
L^{\text{AGN}}_{E}(E)
.

On repeating the steps outlined in §
III.1
and solving the rate equations

\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=R_{1i}^{\text{PE,AGN}}n_{1}+\sum_{j}\left(A_{ji}n_{j}-A_{ij}n_{i}\right)

(32)

at steady state, we arrive at

R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}f_{\text{AGN}}\cdot e^{-|\vec{r}_{\text{g}}|/r_{0}}

(33)

with a (SED-dependent) coefficient

\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{PE,AGN}}=4.45\times 10^{-10}\ \text{cm}^{2}\,\text{erg}^{-1}\;.

(34)

The coronal approximation breaks when the rate for resonant Ly
\alpha
absorption becomes of order the spontaneous,
2\to 1
decay rate, i.e.
R_{1{\rm s}\to 2{\rm p}_{1/2}}^{\rm{PE,AGN}}\sim A_{2{\rm p}_{1/2}\to 1{\rm s}}
. Ignoring extinction, this occurs at a critical bolometric flux of
f_{\text{AGN}}\sim 10^{16}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}
, which is orders of magnitude larger than the highest flux value
f_{\text{AGN}}\approx 10^{7}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\ \text{cm}^{-2}
among all the gas cells of TNG300-1 at redshift
z=1
.

III.4
Recombination In The Dilute ISM

The next physical process we consider is cascade emission from recombination to excited states. The recombination luminosity is computed as

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}=E_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\sum_{\text{gas cells}}V_{\text{gas}}\,n_{\text{HII}}\,R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}\,\Theta_{\text{SFR}}\;,

(35)

where
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}
is the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission rate per
HII
particle due to recombination events. The corresponding rate equations are

\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=n_{e}\alpha_{i}^{\text{RR}}n_{N+1}+\sum_{j}\left(A_{ji}n_{j}-A_{ij}n_{i}\right)\;,

(36)

where, unlike in the previous sections, excited levels are now populated by recombination from the bare state, which we normalize as
n_{N+1}=1
, and then proceed to relax to the ground state. Since these equations include only recombination transitions and spontaneous emission, the ground level never achieves steady state unlike the excited states. Therefore, we solve Eq. (
36
) at steady state for the levels
i\geq 2
using the case-A recombination rate coefficient of
Cillié 1932

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{\text{RR}}

\displaystyle=3.27\times 10^{-12}\left(\frac{10^{4}\text{K}}{Tn^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\exp{\left(\frac{\chi_{i}}{kT}\right)}

(37)

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Ei}\!\left(\frac{\chi_{i}}{kT}\right)\mathcal{S}_{i}\ \ {\rm cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}\;.

Here,
\chi_{i}=I_{\infty}-E_{i}
is the energy required to ionize a hydrogen atom in level
i
,
Ei
is the exponential integral,
n
is the principal quantum number of the electron orbital and we have added a multiplicative factor of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
for the following reason. Without
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, the above expression provides total rate coefficients as a function of
n
solely. However, we also need the fine structure in order to solve Eq. (
36
). For this purpose, we multiply the original expression of
Cillié 1932
by the relative statistical weight
\mathcal{S}_{i}=g_{i}/2n^{2}
of the electron orbital with (total angular momentum) quantum number
J_{i}
(and multiplicity
g_{i}=2J_{i}+1
).

Since
\alpha_{i}^{\text{RR}}
decreases sharply with
n
, an approximation with a finite number of levels above the ground state is sufficient to capture radiative recombinations accurately. Observe also that, like CE-induced emission, the steady state solutions satisfy
n_{i}\propto n_{e}
and
R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}\propto n_{e}
. Therefore, we can write

R_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}=n_{e}\,\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}(T)\;.

(38)

The function
\alpha_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{RR}}
is shown in Fig.
3
. The recombination conversion probability (the probability that a radiative recombination leads to the emission of a
\mathrm{H}\alpha
photon) varies with temperature. It decreases from
\sim 15\%
at the temperature floor to
\sim 5\%
at
T=10^{8}\ {\rm K}
.

III.5
Emission From HII Regions

HII regions are typically found in molecular clouds with recent or ongoing star formation, in which massive hot stars ionize the largely cold and neutral hydrogen in their surroundings.
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission can occur when the ionized gas recombines. Since the physical state of star-forming regions is not properly modeled in the simulations, we take the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission from gas cells to be directly proportional to the instantaneous SFR in analogy with the PE-induced
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission discussed in Section §
III.2
. Namely, we set

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\text{HII}}

\displaystyle=C_{\rm{HII}}\sum_{\text{gas cells}}\,\text{SFR}\hskip 4.0pt\,\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}

(39)

\displaystyle=C_{\rm{HII}}\,{\rm SFR}_{\text{gal}}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\;,

where
{\rm SFR}_{\text{gal}}
is the total star formation rate of an ELG (in unit of
\ {\rm M_{\odot}\,yr^{-1}}
). We adopt a constant of proportionality
C_{\rm{HII}}=10^{40.8}
equal to half the coefficient of the empirical SFR -
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation,
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=10^{41.1}{\rm SFR}_{\text{gal}}
. This relation was calibrated for
z\lesssim 0.5

(
Kennicutt 1983
;
Lee et al. 2009
;
Domínguez Sánchez et al. 2012
;
Koyama et al. 2015
)
, and at
2.08\leq z\leq 2.51

(
Shivaei et al. 2015
)
. We apply the relation Eq. (
39
) uniformly to all the galaxies and at all redshifts.

IV
Results

In this Section, we present predictions of our model for the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity functions and the HODs of central and satellite galaxies. Additionally, we compare the present predictions to HODs from the
Euclid
Flagship simulations
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024g
)
.
Unlike
Hadzhiyska et al. 2021
for instance, who developed augmented HOD prescriptions of color-selected mock galaxies that take into account the environment, we will stick to the simplest halo mass-based HOD modelling advocated in
Benson et al. 2000
;
Peacock et al. 2001
;
Scoccimarro et al. 2001
;
Berlind & Weinberg 2002
;
Zheng et al. 2005
. Extensions of this approach will be considered elsewhere.

IV.1
Properties of the Flagship mocks

The Flagship galaxy mock is a simulated galaxy catalogue designed to support the
Euclid
mission.
The procedure to build it is described in detail in
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024g
)
; we recap here the relevant steps for our work, which focuses on emission line galaxies with a
\mathrm{H}\alpha
flux
f_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}>2\times 10^{-16}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}
.

Dark matter halos are populated with a HOD prescription using the
Scipic
package
(
Carretero et al. 2017
)
.
Each halo possesses a central galaxy, while the number of satellites is drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean

\big\langle\mathcal{N}_{s}\big\lvert M\big\rangle=\left(\frac{M}{fM_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\alpha},

(40)

where
M_{\mathrm{min}}=10^{10}\ {\rm M_{\odot}}/h
is the minimum halo mass in the catalog,
f=15
and
\alpha=1
.

To obtain the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
flux for each galaxy,
Scipic
goes through the following steps.
First, luminosities in the SDSS
{}^{0.1}r
band are assigned to central galaxies to match the luminosity function given by
Dahlen et al. 2005
, based on multi-band data taken in the GOODS Survey
(
Giavalisco et al. 2005
)
and extrapolated up to
z=3
.
A scatter in luminosity is included to adjust the clustering amplitude that would otherwise result too low with respect to observations
(
Carretero et al. 2015
, e.g.)
.
Satellite luminosities are then assigned assuming that in each halo the luminosities follow a Schechter-like probability function.

Galaxies are then assigned a color
{}^{0.1}(g-r)
, adjusted in order to reproduce the low redshift projected clustering of the SDSS sample
(
Zehavi et al. 2011
)
.
From this, each galaxy is then assigned a SED, given by a linear combination of templates depending on redshift and color.
Such SEDs do not include emission lines, whose fluxes are instead computed through other physical properties.
First, the galaxy SED is integrated from 1500 to 2300 Å to obtain the unextincted UV flux.
Second, the SFR and the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
flux are computed assuming the relation of
Kennicutt 1998
for a Chabrier IMF
(
Chabrier 2003
)
.
Finally, a Gaussian random scatter of
\Delta\log f_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=0.05
is also introduced, resulting in a catalogue of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters with unextincted
\mathrm{H}\alpha
fluxes that can be compared to our mock sample constructed from the TNG simulations.

In this Section, we assess the extent to which variations in the
\mathrm{H}\alpha

\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity function, and the fraction of central and satellite galaxies above the
Euclid

\big\langle N_{c}\big\lvert{\it M}\big\rangle
asymptotes to a value

V
conclusions

We have presented a physically-motivated modelling of spatially-resolved, galactic
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission that can be applied to hydrodynamical simulations of galaxy formation. In particular, we have attempted to address the lack of a comprehensive theoretical framework for emission from the warm-hot, dilute ISM. We have assumed that the population of the atomic levels are in steady-state and can be computed within the coronal approximation (where excitation occurs either from the ground state or by recombination). These assumptions are justified by the relatively low densities and weak radiation fields in the ISM. We have derived simplified expressions for the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosities induced by collisional excitation, recombination and photo-excitation by stars and AGN, which can be directly related to the physical properties of simulated gas cells or particles. In addition, we have included
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission from HII regions, which are not spatially resolved in the IllustrisTNG suite of simulations considered here.

We have used our approach to extract mock
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters from the IllustrisTNG simulations, which mimic the ELGs surveyed by
Euclid
in the redshift range
1\leq z\leq 2
. We have explored the systematics of our model, and also compared the resulting HOD to that of the Flagship mocks developed by the
Euclid
collaboration
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024g
)
. Our results can be summarized as follows:

•

For the bright
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters surveyed by
Euclid
,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission mechanisms other than HII regions (i.e. collisional excitation, photo-excitation and radiative recombination) contribute an average
\approx 69\%
of the total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity of an ELG. This is consistent with expectations drawn from narrow-band imaging surveys, which suggest that the diffuse gas can account for as much as
70\%
of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission
(
Zurita et al. 2000
, see for example)
. Specifically, collisional excitation and radiative recombination contribute on average
\approx 40\%
of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity across the redshift range
1\leq z\leq 2
.

•

Photo-excitation by UV radiation is often the dominant source powering
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in galaxies hosting an AGN, while nearby AGNs can contribute up to a few percent of the total
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission of a galaxy even when its own AGN is switched off. We stress that this result sensitively depends on the implementation of absorption, AGN radiation geometry etc.

•

The
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity function predicted by our model is in reasonable agreement with observations after the inclusion of a uniform extinction of
A_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=0.85\ \text{mag}
consistent with empirical estimations. By contrast, using the SFR-
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation inferred from observations at
z\lesssim 1
yields a
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity function which underestimates the measurements. This shows that the latter cannot be reproduced with a simple SFR-
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
conversion, and other components are necessary.

•

The mean central occupation number
\big\langle N_{c}\big\lvert{\it M}\big\rangle
of our synthetic galaxies converges toward unity at the high (halo) mass end and, moreover, exhibits a local maximum around
M\sim 10^{12}\ {\rm{\it h}}^{-1}M_{\odot}
. A similar peak, albeit more pronounced, is also seen in the HODs of other high-redshift ELGs
(
Favole et al. 2016
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Okumura et al. 2021
)
.

•

The central and satellite occupation numbers predicted by our model are consistent with the Flagship HOD when the key model parameters are set to their fiducial values. However, the HOD of our mock
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters above the
Euclid
flux limit are rather sensitive to variations in the fraction of hot gas or the attenuation length of the radiation field in the ISM, and to the details of the baryonic feedback implementation.

We have not investigated HOD dependencies beyond the host halo mass. Using a semi-analytical model of galaxy formation,
Marinucci et al. 2023
found that, unlike color-selected galaxies,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission line galaxies appear to exhibit little assembly bias. However, they considered a single redshift (
z=1
), low values of the host halo mass (
10^{10}\lesssim M\lesssim 10^{12}\ {\rm M_{\odot}}
), and an emission line model which takes into account the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission from
\rm{HII}
regions solely. Therefore, it will be prudent to revisit their results. The model presented here can be straightforwardly applied to assess the importance of assembly bias in ELGs following the separate universe approach advocated by
Barreira et al. 2020
for optically-selected galaxies. Moreover, it can be used to quantify the impact of deviations from a Poissonian satellite occupation number and assumptions about the radial distribution of satellite galaxies on the nonlinearity and stochasticity of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters clustering
(see e.g.
Cacciato et al. 2012
;
Ginzburg et al. 2017
;
Salcedo et al. 2024
, for related discussions)
, and their Fingers-of-God (FoG) signature in particular.

Finally, our approach can be extended to other atomic lines such as H
\beta
,
\rm{[OII]\ 3727\AA}
,
\rm{[OIII]\ 5007\AA}
or
\rm{[SIII]\ 9533\AA}
for instance. While the H
\alpha
line remains the best observable out to redshift
z\sim 4-5
, a survey like SPHEREx will be able to confidently measure the intensity power spectra of the [OII] and [OIII] lines at
z\lesssim 4

(
Gong et al. 2017
)
. Furthermore, in the case of the
Euclid
survey, the [OIII] and [SIII] emission lines from interloper galaxies could be misidentified as
\mathrm{H}\alpha
lines
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024b
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024c
;
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024a
)
, which would lead to a dilution and a distortion of the clustering signal from the target (
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) galaxies
(
Pullen et al. 2016
, see, e.g.,)
. Our model can also be applied to study the effect of line interlopers on the clustering of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters.
To model those emission lines however, a ionization state model for heavier elements must be developed (the IllustrisTNG simulations provide this information only for hydrogen). Another challenge is the validity of the coronal approximation, which may not be applicable for atoms that have long-lived (metastable) states.
