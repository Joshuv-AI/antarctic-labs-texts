Advancing Impact: A National Approach to Knowledge Translation in Saudi Arabia-The Saudi National Institute of Health Experience
Authors: Maha M. Aleid, Manar A. Alharbi, Amal N. Almadouj, Ahmad I. Alrawaji, Lamia A. Alhaqbani, Fars K. Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To present a reproducible national model for translating research findings into evidence-based clinical practice guidelines.Methods:The Saudi National Institute of Health launched the initiative titled “Transforming the Outcomes of Published Health Research into Medical Guidelines that Enhance Medical Practices to Improve the Quality of Human Health” on February 3, 2025. The initiative aimed to identify and assess research outcomes published between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024 across 6 predefined thematic tracks relevant to the Saudi population. Eligible publications were collected within one month after the submission portal opened. Following closure of portal, all submissions were evaluated using the 14th Knowledge Translation Toolkits, and a weighted score was calculated using a Knowledge Translation Nexus Scoring System to generate final results.Results:A total of 390 publications were submitted through the online portal by 206 researchers from 128 institutions. The infectious diseases track accounted for the highest number of submissions (108; 28%), followed by oncology (70; 18%), and both rare and inherited diseases (47; 12%) and diabetes tracks (35; 9%). Ultimately, 40 publications were selected for recommendation. The liver track showed the highest median final score across all tracks and was significantly different from the cardiovascular, diabetes, and rare and inherited disease.Conclusion:This initiative established a structural national framework that strengthens local research in guideline development and promotes evidence-informed health decisions. This approach can be adopted and adapted across various settings.

Introduction

Globally, billions of dollars are invested annually in biomedical research by both the public and private sectors [1]. This investment reflects the pivotal role of biomedical research in driving healthcare innovation and improving patient outcomes [1]. However, the literature shows that healthcare systems often fail to use evidence optimally at all levels of care [2]. Studies have reported that 30%–45% of patients in the United States do not receive care based on the best available evidence [2]. Similar findings have been reported globally in both developed and developing countries [1]. Therefore, the accumulation of evidence alone does not ensure its optimal use in healthcare systems. Knowledge translation (KT) has been recognized as an important approach for bridging the “knowledge-to-action gap” by transforming evidence into practical outcomes that inform decision-making and healthcare delivery [3] In response, Canadian health authorities have made KT a major priority within research governance frameworks to improve accountability for research funding and move beyond the traditional “fund and forget” model [4]. The Canadian Institute of Health Research (CIHR) defined KT as “a dynamic and iterative process that includes synthesis, dissemination, exchange, and ethically sound application of knowledge to improve the health of citizens, provide more effective health services and products, and strengthen the health care system.” [5].

Saudi Vision 2030 commits to transforming the national economy, building a vibrant society, and improving the health and wellbeing of the population [6]. The Health Sector Transformation Program, one of the key programs of Saudi Vision 2030, aims to improve the quality and efficiency of health services [6]. This strategic direction aligns closely with the mission of the Saudi National Institute of Health (Saudi NIH), which was established to oversee and advance translational research and clinical trials across the health sector. The KT is a fundamental component of the Saudi NIH mandate. At the Saudi NIH, KT is viewed as a proactive and continuous approach involving the creation, mobilization, implementation, and collaborative application of research-based knowledge to improve health outcomes, enhance healthcare services, and strengthen health system performance [7]. According to the General Authority for Statistics in 2021, Saudi Arabia invested 14.5 billion SAR in research and development activities [8]. Moreover, the number of scientific publications reached 17,242 articles in 2024, representing a 40% increase compared with the previous year [9]. Despite this progress, the use of national findings in the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) remains limited. An evaluation of the National Approved Clinical Guidelines showed that only a small proportion of cited references originated from local research, with substantial reliance on international evidence. Although the underlying evidence may be similar, guideline recommendations can vary according to cultural and organizational contexts [10]. This challenge highlights the need to develop CPGs that are more strongly grounded in local evidence to ensure their relevance and applicability within the Saudi healthcare system.

In response to this challenge, the Saudi NIH, through its KT Department, developed KT frameworks and launched a national initiative titled “Transforming the Outcomes of Published Health Research into Medical Guidelines that Enhance Medical Practices to Improve the Quality of Human Health.” This initiative aimed to strengthen the contribution of local research to the development of national CPGs and to support the translation of research evidence into practical healthcare applications.

This study examines the institutionalization of KT at the Saudi NIH within the national research system, with a focus on its rationale, implementation strategies, and key outcomes.

Methods

Preliminary evaluation

We carried out a preliminary evaluation to assess the contribution of national evidence to the development of CPGs. The analysis began with the identification and collection of all CPGs formally published on government-authorized platforms, including the Ministry of Health, Saudi Medical Council, and Public Health Authority, between January 2011 and December 2024 [111213]. After data collection, duplicate guidelines were identified and removed. The references cited in the remaining guidelines were then systematically extracted and classified as either national (Saudi) or international to support further analysis.

Model overview

The framework was developed through a comprehensive review of the KT literature, supported by the authors’ practical experience from the initiative titled “Transforming the Outcomes of Published Health Research into Medical Guidelines that Enhance Medical Practices to Improve the Quality of Human Health.” The initiative was launched on February 3, 2025, in collaboration with 3 national health regulatory bodies to accelerate the adoption of KT products (KTPs), facilitate knowledge exchange, and support implementation planning. Moreover, 8 scientific and medical associations were involved because of their critical role in guideline development. The initiative consists of nine stages, as presented in (Fig. 1): (1) initiative launch, (2) submission, (3) initial screening, (4) knowledge creation (KC), (5) knowledge mobilization (KM), (6) scientific committee evaluation (SCE), (7) announcement of the result, (8) knowledge implementation (KI), (9) knowledge dissemination (KD).

The 9-stage process of the initiative.

The inclusion criteria comprised studies conducted among the Saudi population, published between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024, and aligned with one of the 6 initiative tracks: oncology, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), diabetes, infectious diseases, rare and inherited diseases, and liver diseases. The exclusion criteria included study designs that did not meet the eligibility requirements, including in vitro studies, in vivo studies, animal studies, case reports, case series, and review articles.

Fourteen KT assessment toolkits were developed, pilot-tested, and iteratively refined to evaluate the quality and contextual relevance of submitted publications. The KT team developed these toolkits internally through a comparative review of established international KT frameworks, followed by 3 rounds of expert review to ensure methodological rigor and alignment with the initiative's objectives [514]. The toolkits were applied across three core stages: (1) KC: 6 toolkits were used to assess methodological rigor and study strength, ensuring that the findings yield robust and reliable results; (2) KM and SC: 7 toolkits were used to evaluate the applicability and relevance of the research findings, with emphasis on their potential to inform clinical practice and address national health priorities. The SC toolkit supported the SC in assessing each study's knowledge claim, including feasibility within the Saudi healthcare context. (3) KI: one toolkit consisting of 4 modules was used, including an executive summary, governance structure, current assessment of the disease in Saudi Arabia, and recommendations with expected impact, along with a supported SC report.

Modeling research outcomes

Published research outcomes were collected and evaluated through an electronic submission form integrated into the Submittable platform [15]. The form captured key publication characteristics, including research area, methodology, knowledge claims, and journal details. After submission, data cleaning procedures were applied to address common inconsistencies such as duplicate records, missing values, and variations in terminology. The assessment process began with the application of predefined eligibility criteria. Eligible publications progressed to the KC stage, where they were evaluated using the relevant KT toolkits. Each publication was independently reviewed by 2 reviewers, and the final score was calculated as the mean of their assessments. The same approach was applied in the KM stage. At the SC stage, eligible publications were assigned to reviewers through an evaluation platform by the committee secretary based on predefined criteria, including subject specialization and research expertise. A weighted composite score was then calculated by the authors, integrating the results from all stages of the initiative according to predefined relative weights using the following formula:

Formula 1: Knowledge Translation Nexus Scoring System: Weighted score=0.40(∑i=1KCnWi Qi)+0.20(∑i=1KMnWi Qi)+0.40(∑i=1SCnWi Qi)

Where:

Qi: Questions of i-th evaluation form.

Wi: The weight of the i-th question.

KCn: Knowledge creation forms.

KMn: Knowledge mobilization forms.

SCn: Scientific committee form.

The formula of Knowledge Translation Nexus Scoring System was developed internally by the authors and reviewed and validated by a statistician and subject-matter expert. It was pilot-tested before implementation. The pilot yielded the weighted score as 91*.4+30*.2+24*.4 = 52, and a 95% confidence interval on the weighted score as (40.92, 63.10). The scoring system was designed based on the nature of the initiative. The relative weights assigned to each stage reflecting its conceptual importance within the KT framework. The KC stage accounted for 40% of the total score due to its central role in ensuring methodological rigor and generating valid and reliable findings. The KM and SC stages were considered components of the KM unit processes, and accounted for 20% and 40%, their weights were assigned based on the expected applicability of outputs, particularly given that many studies did not explicitly integrate KT principles across the entire research process. In cases where a publication was reviewed by more than one reviewer, weights were distributed according to the relevance of each reviewer's subject expertise to the manuscript content. After calculation of the weighted score of the Knowledge Translation Nexus Scoring System, publications falling below the cutoff point (<60%) were excluded [16]. The main reasons for exclusion included unclear target population, non-significant findings, weak methodology, and lack of measurable impact on morbidity or mortality indicators. SC recommended publications were then compiled to derive the final results. Following completion of the three-stage evaluation process, high-quality evidence was integrated into the KI toolkits for knowledge dissemination.

To assess the variability of the weighted scoring equation, theoretical variance was used as part of the validation process. The variance of the weighted score was calculated using the following formula:

The variance in the weighted scores was a value of 31.96; the standard deviation (SD) = 5.65.

Statistical analysis

R version 4.5.2 was used for data analysis [17]. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range for continuous variables, depending on data normality. Non-parametric testing using the Kruskal-Wallis's test was used to assess the effect of disease track on final scores. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (1995) was used to adjust the p-values for multiple comparisons [18].

Moreover, a Microsoft Excel dashboard was developed to analyze data extracted from the Submittable™ platform. The dashboard summarized overall publication distributions by track, geographic region, and initiative stage, along with 6 disease track metrics, including publication count, author affiliation distribution, publication year, and journal quartile ranking.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Saudi NIH Ethics Committee (EC), registered at King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology (KACST), number (H-01-R-133). The researchers signed the terms and conditions before submitting their publication, which includes consent to disseminate the findings to health policymakers before submitting the research publication. Moreover, the researcher had the full right to withdraw the submission at any time. All personal information from researchers was kept confidential and used only for KT processes.

Results

The preliminary assessment analyzed 3,774 references across 118 National Approved Clinical Evidence documents, showing that approximately 93% of the references were derived from international literature, while only 7% were based on national references.

A total of 390 publications were submitted by researchers through online portal. Following initial screening at the KC stage, 234 publications progressed to KM stage. Concurrently, 156 publications were excluded due to ineligible study designs or lack of relevance to national priorities. The SC reviewed 99 publications, resulting in 40 publications being recommended for the KI stage (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study describes the KT framework of the Saudi NIH within the national research system, emphasizing the transformation of local publications from static research outputs into actionable tools for decision-making. Most submitted publications were in the infectious diseases and oncology tracks across the 5-year period. The liver disease track recorded the lowest output during the same period, creating a marked gap between the highest- and lowest-performing tracks, while other tracks showed moderate variation. These findings may be partly explained by the inclusion of 2020, the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic during the study period, as well as the prominence of cancer as a national and global health priority that attracts substantial research funding and output. Moreover, national data from Saudi Arabia indicate that research productivity in gastroenterology, including liver diseases, is 3 to 4 times lower than that in infectious diseases and oncology, which helps explain the limited number of comparable observations within this initiative track [19]. The predominant tracks within case-control studies were oncology and rare and inherited disease, suggesting the suitability of this study design for conditions with long disease development timelines. Given the nature of Infectious disease, descriptive research, including a cross-sectional design, is essential in conducting epidemiological assessments and improving disease surveillance. Indeed, more than one-third of publications in the infectious disease track were descriptive studies. Overall, prospective designs were more common than retrospective designs across all tracks, except for oncology. This pattern is likely influenced by the availability of the national cancer registry, established in 1992 [20]. The population-based data provided by the registry facilitate cancer research by offering comprehensive datasets to investigators. Most submitted publications were in the top quartile journal, reflecting the high overall quality of the evidence base, as nearly half of the publications progressed to the final stage and were subsequently recommended. Overall, the final median scores were similar across tracks, except for liver disease, which demonstrated particularly noticeable performance, with almost all publications reaching the final stage being recommended. The highest recommendation type reported in the infectious track was guidelines, indicating an urgent need for preventive measures during outbreaks. All recommendation types directed toward the oncology track were services and other options, which may indicate a continuing need to establish innovative or non-standard services to improve cancer care. No comparable KT initiative has been designed and implemented on a national scale despite the presence of related KT programs led by national health research agencies, such as the CIHR. At CIHR, most KT activities are organized within its 13 institutes, indicating that KT operates within an overarching institutional framework, while its implementation and prioritization are tailored to the specific thematic focus of each institute [21]. Moreover, although the framework was primarily designed to inform guideline development, it also generated a wide range of KTPs, including policies and protocols, highlighting its broad applicability in supporting evidence-informed decision-making. The dissemination strategy focused on delivering concise and actionable recommendations through a structured KI toolkit. The toolkit is designed to facilitate implementation by enabling end users to act on KTPs. Similar implementation-oriented resources have previously been developed, such as SUPPORT Tools for evidence-based health policymaking (STP) [22]. The current approach was designed based on the anticipated nature of applications, as almost all submitted publications had not considered applying the principles of knowledge translation to the entire research process. Unlike the CIHR model, which incorporates knowledge translation prospectively during the research process [5].

This initiative aimed to bridge the gap between knowledge generation and implementation by disseminating KTPs to relevant decision-makers within targeted organizations. As an example of the implementation phase, one of the highest-scoring KTPs recommended by the SC focuses on updating cardiopulmonary arrest protocols. Although cardiopulmonary arrest remains associated with poor survival outcomes worldwide, particularly in cases of pulseless electrical activity or asystole [23]. Local data from Saudi Arabia reported 10,860 cases of cardiac and respiratory arrest, with a return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) rate of 11.16% [24]. These findings highlight the importance of developing or refining existing protocols issued by the regulatory body for managing sudden cardiac arrest, particularly by optimizing time-critical interventions. In response to this challenge, one of the KTPs generated through this initiative was designed to improve the clinical response to cardiopulmonary arrest. The protocol focuses on modifying a time-sensitive intervention during cardiopulmonary arrest to enhance sustained ROSC and improve 24-hour survival rates [25]. The supporting evidence was synthesized and incorporated into KI toolkits to assist decision-makers in adopting evidence-informed recommendations. The resulting recommendations were subsequently submitted to the relevant authorities for protocol revision and implementation.

Study limitations

Although this study provided valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on electronically submitted publications, which may not fully represent the breadth of contemporary national literature. Second, the active involvement of knowledge users and decision-makers throughout the research process is essential for enhancing relevance and utility. This limitation was partially addressed by engaging national experts during the SC stage to assess the alignment of the research outputs with the Saudi healthcare system.

In conclusion, this manuscript outlines the experiences of the KT Department at the Saudi NIH within a national KT initiative. The framework is intended to strengthen the contribution of local research to the development of national practice guidelines and reduce reliance on external evidence sources through a KT-focused approach. In addition to supporting guidelines, the framework generated a range of KTPs, including protocols, service-related outputs, and policies, highlighting its capacity to support diverse forms of evidence-informed decision-making. Despite the need for further refinement, this approach offers a new perspective on KT by providing a nationally coordinated framework across multiple disease tracks while strengthening the role of national regulatory bodies in utilizing locally published research. During the KI stage, the developed toolkit was designed to assist decision-makers in acting on the recommendations while evaluation of its impact remains ongoing. This approach was designed to be scalable and flexible, enabling its adaptation across various contexts.
