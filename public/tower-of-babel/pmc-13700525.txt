Machine learning–based radiomic analysis of T2-FLAIR white matter hyperintensity predicts hemorrhagic transformation after endovascular thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Qi Wu, Yandan Shi, Zhihao Zhang, Jupeng Zhang, Jiayu Wu, Ziyuan Yang, Yuyao He, Jinqin Su, Xiyuan Wang, Changhui Huang, Xiqi Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHemorrhagic transformation (HT) remains a major complication after endovascular therapy (EVT) in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS). This study aimed to develop and validate a machine-learning model based on T2-FLAIR white matter hyperintensity (WMH) radiomics combined with clinical variables to predict HT after EVT.MethodsIn this dual-center retrospective study, 446 patients (mean age 61.88 ± 10.98 years; 332 [74.4%] male) with AIS who underwent EVT were enrolled from two centers. Patients from the primary center were randomly assigned to a training cohort and an internal test cohort, and patients from the second center served as an independent external validation cohort. Clinical risk factors were identified by univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Preoperative T2-FLAIR images were used to segment WMH lesions and extract radiomics features. After feature selection, three predictive models were then developed: a clinical model based on the independent clinical predictors, a radiomics model based on the radiomics signature, and a combined model integrating both. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration analysis, DeLong testing, and decision curve analysis.ResultsAmong the 446 patients, 153 (34.3%) developed HT within 24–48 h after EVT. Multivariate analysis identified NIHSS score, occlusion site, and atrial fibrillation as independent clinical predictors of HT. In the internal test cohort, the combined model achieved the best discriminative performance (AUC = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.651–0.986), outperforming the clinical model (AUC = 0.740, 95% CI: 0.562–0.917) and the radiomics model (AUC = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.621–0.990). In the external validation cohort, the combined model maintained robust performance (AUC = 0.817, 95% CI: 0.746–0.889), which was comparable to the clinical model (AUC = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.742–0.894) and higher than the radiomics model alone (AUC = 0.764, 95% CI: 0.682–0.845).ConclusionsA combined model incorporating T2-FLAIR WMH radiomics and key clinical variables provides a feasible tool for individualized prediction of HT after EVT in patients with AIS, although its incremental discriminatory value over the clinical model alone was limited in the external validation cohort. This approach may help improve perioperative risk stratification and support personalized clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02599-3.Keywords: Machine learning, White matter hyperintensity, Hemorrhagic transformation, Endovascular thrombectomy, Ischemic stroke

Introduction

Endovascular therapy (EVT) has revolutionized the treatment landscape for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) caused by anterior circulation large vessel occlusion, significantly improving reperfusion rates and functional outcomes [1, 2]. However, hemorrhagic transformation (HT) remains a significant complication following EVT [3, 4]. Its mechanism is complex and is generally regarded as the result of the combined effects of reperfusion injury, disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and coagulation dysfunction [5, 6]. The occurrence of any type of HT post-surgery can exacerbate neurological deficits and substantially increase mortality rates [7]. Therefore, identifying patients at a high risk of HT prior to or shortly after EVT is critical for optimizing individualized perioperative management, guiding blood pressure targets, and tailoring antithrombotic regimens.

While various clinical and laboratory parameters, including stroke severity (e.g., admission NIHSS score) and cardioembolic etiology (e.g., atrial fibrillation), have been established as independent risk factors for HT [8, 9], predicting hemorrhage based solely on clinical features often yields limited accuracy due to the complex pathophysiology of reperfusion injury. Recently, the role of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) in stroke prognosis has garnered significant attention [10]. White matter hyperintensity (WMH), typically visualized on pre-operative T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, is a prominent neuroimaging marker of CSVD [11]. Severe WMH reflects underlying chronic microvascular damage, endothelial dysfunction, and increased BBB permeability [12, 13]. Recent evidence further supports the prognostic relevance of WMH in patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy, showing that moderate-to-severe WMH burden is associated with unfavorable functional outcomes after EVT [14]. Consequently, the ischemic brain in patients with a significant WMH burden is considered more susceptible to reperfusion injury and subsequent hemorrhagic conversion after EVT [15]. We hypothesize that a high burden of WMH reflects a chronic, underlying state of cerebral microvascular vulnerability. In the event of AIS, particularly following the rapid restoration of blood flow via EVT, this pre-existing fragile microvascular network is susceptible to the “double hit” of acute hemodynamic impact and ischemia-reperfusion injury. Such mechanical and biological stress may lead to vessel wall rupture and subsequent blood extravasation, ultimately manifesting as HT.

In routine clinical practice, WMH severity is conventionally assessed using qualitative visual grading scales [16, 17] or basic morphological descriptors (e.g., volume, shape irregularity) [18]. However, these visual evaluations are inherently subjective, prone to inter-observer variability, and fail to capture the subtle, high-dimensional spatial heterogeneity that reflects the true pathological microenvironment of the damaged white matter [19]. Radiomics, an advanced quantitative image analysis approach, overcomes these limitations by extracting mineable, sub-visual features (e.g., texture, shape, and intensity) from medical images [20]. When integrated with machine learning algorithms, radiomics has demonstrated tremendous potential in stroke research, particularly in predicting tissue viability, hemorrhage expansion, and clinical outcomes [21, 22]. Recent studies have further applied clinical-radiomics and multi-omics models to predict hemorrhagic transformation after mechanical thrombectomy, supporting the feasibility of combining quantitative imaging features with clinical variables for individualized risk stratification [23]. Nevertheless, the specific predictive value of T2-FLAIR-derived WMH radiomics for post-EVT HT, particularly when combined with clinical variables in a dual-center setting, remains underexplored.

To bridge this literature gap, this retrospective study with an external validation cohort from an independent institution aimed to develop and validate machine-learning models based on T2-FLAIR WMH radiomics and clinical variables to predict HT after EVT in patients with AIS, with the goal of providing an interpretable and clinically useful tool for individualized risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study has been granted approval by the Medical Ethics Committee of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities (No. 2026051401). As this is a retrospective study, there is no need for informed consent from participants.

We consecutively screened patients with AIS who underwent EVT from January 2023 to September 2025 at two independent centers. Patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities (Center A) were randomly assigned to the training cohort and internal testing cohort in an 8:2 ratio, whereas patients from Baise People’s Hospital (Center B), an independent institution, constituted the external validation cohort. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Adult patients aged 18 years or older; (2) AIS caused by anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (e.g., internal carotid artery, or M1/M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery), confirmed by baseline imaging; (3) Received EVT within the standard therapeutic time window (e.g., within 24 h of symptom onset); (4) Underwent pre-operative cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that included a T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence, with image quality satisfying the requirements for quantitative analysis; (5) Follow-up computed tomography (CT) or MRI was performed within a prespecified postoperative window of 24–48 h after EVT to assess the presence of HT. This follow-up time window was applied consistently across both institutions. Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of intracranial hemorrhage on baseline imaging prior to EVT; (2) Missing crucial clinical baseline data, or severe artifacts in MRI scans precluding effective radiomic analysis; (3) EVT procedure was interrupted or failed to achieve technical success (e.g., failed vascular recanalization). Following these criteria, a total of 446 patients were finally enrolled. The Center A cohort comprised 253 patients (80 with HT and 173 without HT), and the Center B cohort comprised 193 patients (73 with HT and 120 without HT). Clinical data collection

Patient baseline clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic medical records systems of the two centers. The collected data included demographics (age, sex, BMI) and lifestyle factors (smoking and drinking history). Clinical status at admission was characterized by systolic and diastolic blood pressure, hypertension, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation, time from symptom onset to surgery, and neurological severity assessed via the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Radiological parameters included the primary occlusion site, which was categorized according to baseline vascular imaging as internal carotid artery (ICA), M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery, or A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery. Stroke etiology was categorized according to the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) criteria, and the severity of WMH was graded using the Fazekas scale. Laboratory evaluations provided data on lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], and low-density lipoprotein [LDL]), homocysteine, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), uric acid, and coagulation function (prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time).

Definition of hemorrhagic transformation

The primary endpoint of this study was the occurrence of any type of HT on follow-up imaging (CT or MRI) at 24–48 h post-EVT. The diagnosis and radiological subtyping of HT were conducted according to the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study (ECASS) II criteria. HT was classified into four subtypes, encompassing HI-1, HI-2, PH-1, and PH-2. However, in this study, these subtypes were combined into a single HT-positive category. For the predictive modeling task, the outcome was formulated as a binary classification problem: the occurrence of any HT versus the absence of HT. Two experienced neuroradiologists, blinded to the clinical outcomes and baseline data, independently reviewed the follow-up images to determine the HT status. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus.

MRI image acquisition and segmentation

To eliminate technical variations in MRI acquisition, all images underwent systematic standardization. The specific workflow was as follows: first, N4Bias Field Correction from the SimpleITK library (version 2.0.2, http://simpleitk.org) in Python (version 3.7.6) was used to perform bias field correction on all MR images to compensate for inherent signal intensity inhomogeneity of scanners. Subsequently, all images were resampled to 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm isotropic voxel space using trilinear interpolation to unify spatial resolution. On this basis, z-score normalization based on global voxel intensity was performed for each image.

FLAIR images were converted into NIFTI format using MATLAB (version 2023b; MathWorks). The converted images were then imported into SPM12 (SPM12, MATLAB version 2023b; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Automatic segmentation of WMH lesions was performed using the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST, version 3.0.0), which incorporates the Lesion Prediction Algorithm (LPA) [24]. The initial WMH masks generated by LST were independently reviewed and manually corrected by two experienced neuroradiologists, each with more than five years of clinical experience, using ITK-SNAP software (version 4.0.2; http://www.itksnap.org/). Both readers were blinded to clinical outcomes and HT status. Manual correction was restricted to predefined procedures, including: (1) removal of non-brain tissues and exclusion of hyperintensities originating from the brainstem and cerebellum; and (2) refinement of WMH masks to exclude areas consistent with acute ischemic lesions, which may also appear hyperintense on FLAIR images. After independent correction, discrepancies between the two readers were resolved by consensus to generate the final regions of interest (ROIs) for radiomics analysis.

To quantify inter-reader agreement during manual correction, the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) was calculated for the binary WMH masks, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for WMH volume. In addition, radiomics features were extracted separately from the independently corrected masks of the two readers, and feature-level ICCs were calculated to evaluate the impact of manual editing on radiomics feature stability.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Radiomic features were extracted using the open‑source Python package PyRadiomics (http://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io). The extracted feature set included first‑order statistics, 3D shape features (which inherently encompass conventional macroscopic metrics such as WMH volume and shape irregularity indices like sphericity and elongation), and texture features derived from the gray‑level co‑occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray‑level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray‑level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray‑level size zone matrix (GLSZM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM). The reproducibility of radiomics features with respect to manual segmentation correction was assessed using the ICC based on the independently corrected masks from the two readers. Only radiomics features with an ICC value equal to or greater than 0.80 were considered robust against reader-dependent manual editing and were retained for subsequent dimensionality reduction and model construction.

To prevent model overfitting and eliminate redundant variables, a rigorous dimensionality reduction pipeline was applied. First, features demonstrating high stability and inter-observer reliability were retained. Subsequently, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression algorithm, optimized via 10-fold cross-validation, was employed in the training cohort to identify the most robust and predictive radiomic feature. The internal test cohort and the external validation cohort were not used during ICC filtering, LASSO feature selection, or Rad-score construction. The selected features were linearly combined using their respective LASSO coefficients to compute a radiomic signature (Rad-score) for each patient.

Model construction

Using the selected radiomics features, we constructed multiple radiomics models for different WMH locations by employing five machine learning algorithms: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), XGBoost, and LightGBM. In preliminary experiments, XGBoost demonstrated the best and most stable performance; therefore, it was selected as the final algorithm for the radiomics model, the clinical model, and the combined model. To identify independent clinical predictors, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. Categorical variables were entered into the regression models as categorical predictors rather than continuous variables. Specifically, occlusion site was transformed using dummy-variable encoding, with each vascular territory represented as a separate category. Clinical factors with a significance level of P < 0.05 were selected and then entered into the XGBoost-based clinical model. The radiomics model was built based on the optimal WMH radiomics features extracted from T2‑FLAIR images using the same XGBoost framework. A combined prediction model was then developed by integrating the Rad‑score with the independent clinical predictors. To intuitively display the prediction logic of the combined model and facilitate individualized clinical decision‑making, a nomogram was created to quantify the weight distribution of each predictive factor.

Model evaluation

After feature selection, algorithm selection, and hyperparameter tuning were completed in the training cohort, the final locked models were evaluated in the internal test cohort and the independent external validation cohort. The models’ performance for predicting HT was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy. The DeLong test was utilized to statistically compare the AUCs among the three different models to determine significance in performance variations.

To thoroughly evaluate the incremental predictive value of the combined model over the clinical or radiomics models alone, the agreement between the predicted probability of hemorrhagic transformation and the actual observed outcome was assessed via calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net clinical benefit of the models across a spectrum of threshold probabilities, with 0.20–0.60 considered a clinically relevant range for post-EVT HT risk management.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and model construction were conducted using Python (version 3.7.6, Python Software Foundation). For clinical baseline characteristics, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the normality of the data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages (%) and analyzed utilizing the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient population and clinical characteristics

A total of 446 patients with AIS who underwent EVT were finally enrolled, divided into a training cohort (n = 203), an internal test cohort (n = 50), and an external validation cohort (n = 193) by data usage. The mean age of the overall patient population was 61.88 ± 10.98 years.

Baseline clinical characteristics between the HT and non‑HT groups are presented in Table 1. Across all cohorts, HT occurred in 153 patients (34.3%) within 24–48 h post‑EVT, while 293 patients (65.7%) did not develop HT. Specifically, patients with HT had a longer time from symptom onset to surgery (23.34 ± 16.56 vs. 15.98 ± 14.20 h, P < 0.001), higher admission NIHSS score (P < 0.001), and lower mRS score (P < 0.001). In addition, significant differences were observed between the two groups in age (P = 0.02), history of diabetes (P = 0.009), atrial fibrillation (P = 0.009), Fazekas scale of white matter hyperintensities (P < 0.001), occlusion site (P = 0.002), TOAST classification (P < 0.001), high‑density lipoprotein (HDL), low‑density lipoprotein (LDL), and activated partial thromboplastin time. Supplementary table S1 further compares baseline characteristics between patients with and without HT in the training, internal test, and external validation cohorts. The difference in NIHSS score between the HT and non‑HT groups remained statistically significant across all three independent cohorts. In the external validation cohort, the distribution trends of time from onset to surgery, age, and mRS score were highly consistent with the overall analysis, confirming the representativeness of the external validation cohort.

Variables that achieved statistical significance in the univariate analysis were then entered into a multivariate logistic regression model for adjustment. The results showed that NIHSS score (OR = 1.149, 95% CI: 1.067–1.237, P = 0.002), occlusion site as a categorical variable (P = 0.003), and atrial fibrillation (OR = 3.596, 95% CI: 1.313–9.855, P = 0.037) were independent clinical predictors of HT after EVT. Model performance and evaluation

Inter-reader agreement for manual WMH correction was good-to-excellent. The two neuroradiologists’ independently corrected WMH masks yielded a mean DSC of 0.87 (range: 0.82–0.92). Through 10-fold cross‑validation, the LASSO regression identified five WMH radiomics features with non‑zero coefficients at the optimal penalty parameter λ (λ = 0.034). The LASSO selection process and feature importance plot are presented in Fig. 3. The performance of each prediction model in the internal test cohort and external validation cohort is detailed in Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 4. In the internal test cohort, the combined model demonstrated the best overall predictive performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.818 (95% CI: 0.651–0.986), a sensitivity of 0.867, and a specificity of 0.798. This performance was superior to that of the radiomics model alone (AUC = 0.806) and the clinical model alone (AUC = 0.740). In the independent external validation cohort, the combined model also showed high robustness, achieving an AUC of 0.817 (95% CI: 0.746–0.889), which was nearly identical to the clinical model (AUC = 0.818) and outperformed the radiomics model (AUC = 0.764). These findings suggest that, although WMH radiomics improved model performance in the internal test cohort, its incremental discriminatory value over clinical variables alone was not clearly demonstrated in the external validation cohort.

Development of the nomogram and clinical utility

To facilitate individualized risk assessment in clinical practice, a predictive nomogram was constructed by integrating the independent clinical predictors with the radiomic signature (Fig. 6). This visualization tool assigns specific points to each predictive variable, allowing clinicians to calculate the cumulative probability of HT for individual patients post-EVT. Calibration curves suggested acceptable agreement between the nomogram-predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes of HT in both the internal and external cohorts (Supplementary Figure S1). Furthermore, DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the models. The results showed that the combined model provided a higher net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities compared to the clinical-only or radiomics-only strategies (Supplementary Figure S2). This highlights the practical utility of the combined model as a decision-support tool for managing AIS patients undergoing endovascular treatment.

Discussion

In this dual-center study, we developed and validated a machine learning model integrating radiomics features of WMH from T2-FLAIR sequences with clinical variables to predict the risk of HT in patients with AIS undergoing EVT. Our results demonstrated that the combined model, incorporating the Rad-score, NIHSS score, occlusion site, and history of atrial fibrillation, achieved the best performance in the internal test cohort. However, in the external validation cohort, its AUC was nearly identical to that of the clinical model, indicating that the incremental discriminatory value of WMH radiomics over clinical predictors alone was limited in the independent external cohort. Therefore, the contribution of WMH radiomics should be interpreted cautiously, as complementary imaging information rather than as a consistently superior predictor across cohorts.

Our results strengthen the emerging role of white matter hyperintensity burden as a marker of hemorrhage susceptibility [25, 26]. WMH is widely regarded as an imaging signature of CSVD, reflecting chronic endothelial dysfunction, impaired autoregulation, and blood-brain barrier disruption [27, 28]. Boulouis et al. [29] demonstrated that high WMH burden has a higher incidence of functional outcomes and symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage in AIS patients, especially when undergoing mechanical thrombectomy treatment with existing stents/suction devices. A review has shown that CSVD is tightly linked to BBB failure, abnormal microvascular WMH permeability, and impaired cerebrovascular reserve, all of which may plausibly amplify reperfusion injury after EVT [30]. Some research reports suggest that AIS patients with higher WMH burden are at greater risk of adverse outcomes [12], while Rashid Ghaznawi et al. [18] found that larger and more irregular shaped WMH is associated with a higher risk of AIS vascular death. Thus, the value of WMH in our model may lie in identifying a microvascular milieu that is biologically primed for hemorrhagic conversion.

A further strength of this work is the use of radiomics on preoperative T2-FLAIR images rather than relying only on visual WMH grading. Conventional scales such as the Fazekas score are practical but inherently coarse and subject to observer variability, whereas radiomics can quantify lesion intensity, shape, and texture heterogeneity in a reproducible way. In our feature selection process, while conventional indices like total WMH volume were evaluated, they were ultimately eliminated by the LASSO algorithm in favor of higher-dimensional features. However, a specific shape irregularity feature (original_shape_Elongation) was retained, demonstrating that detailed morphological properties, when combined with texture heterogeneity, provide a more robust prediction than simple volume alone. This is particularly relevant for WMH, which is spatially heterogeneous and may contain biologically meaningful patterns that are not captured by simple burden scores [31, 32]. By utilizing LASSO regression, we filtered redundant variables and extracted optimal radiomic features from preoperative T2-FLAIR sequences. These quantitative features may capture aspects of WMH morphology and texture heterogeneity that are not fully represented by conventional visual assessment. However, the biological link between chronic WMH radiomic texture and acute HT after EVT remains inferential and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically definitive [33].

Clinical analysis identified the NIHSS score, occlusion site, and atrial fibrillation as independent predictors of HT. NIHSS score likely reflects the magnitude of irreversible ischemic injury, impaired collateral flow, and vulnerability of the blood-brain barrier [34, 35]. A higher NIHSS score typically correlates with larger infarct volumes and more severe ischemic damage [36, 37]. Occlusion site is also biologically plausible, because more proximal or hemodynamically severe occlusions tend to produce larger infarct cores and a greater likelihood of reperfusion injury [38, 39]. Studies have shown that M2 occlusion were protective against any HT subtypes compared to ICA occlusions [40]. Patients with atrial fibrillation, due to long-term use of anticoagulant drugs, have a hypercoagulable state in their blood. Thrombolytic therapy may further disrupt the balance between blood clotting and fibrinolysis, increasing the risk of brain microvascular rupture [41]. Atrial fibrillation may increase HT risk through cardioembolic stroke mechanisms, clot burden, delayed microvascular reperfusion, and more severe ischemic disruption before treatment [42–44]. These relationships are in line with prior EVT studies and with broader hemorrhagic-transformation literature [45]. Potential confounding should also be considered. Age and overall CSVD burden are closely related to WMH severity and may partly influence the association between WMH-derived features and HT risk. In this study, age and Fazekas score were evaluated in the baseline comparison and candidate clinical analysis, and the final model retained the variables selected through the predefined feature-selection procedure. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In particular, infarct size or infarct core volume was not directly incorporated into the present model, although NIHSS score and occlusion site may partially reflect stroke severity and infarct burden. Therefore, the observed association between WMH radiomics and HT should be interpreted as predictive rather than causal.

This study integrated the clinical predictive factors and imaging features of AIS patients who underwent EVT, thereby constructing a more comprehensive predictive model and improving the predictive performance of the model. The combined model outperformed the clinical-only and radiomics-only models in the internal test cohort, but this advantage was not reproduced as a higher AUC over the clinical model in the independent external validation cohort. This finding suggests that clinical predictors, particularly NIHSS score, occlusion site, and atrial fibrillation, may dominate discriminatory performance in external data, whereas WMH radiomics may provide complementary biological information related to cerebrovascular vulnerability rather than a clear incremental gain in AUC. This is consistent with the research results of DAGHER et al. [46]. By constructing a combined predictive nomogram, our study operationalizes these complex multi-domain parameters into an intuitive, clinician-friendly decision-support tool. The excellent concordance observed in our calibration curves across independent cohorts underscores the high reliability of the model. Furthermore, DCA revealed that applying this combined model yields a significant net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared to treat-all or treat-none strategies, as well as single-modal models. Consequently, this tool can assist neurologists in tailoring individualized perioperative care, such as optimizing blood pressure targets, personalizing the timing of antithrombotic regimens, and determining the frequency of neuroimaging follow-ups to mitigate HT risks.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design inevitably introduces selection bias, and the exclusion of patients with poor-quality MRI scans may limit the generalizability of our model. Second, while the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST) provided an automated initial segmentation of WMH, it still required manual correction, which is time-consuming and introduces a degree of observer dependence. Future integration of deep learning-based fully automated segmentation algorithms could streamline this workflow. Third, our radiomics pipeline exclusively utilized T2-FLAIR sequences; incorporating multiparametric MRI data, such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) or perfusion-weighted imaging (PWI), could potentially yield an even more comprehensive assessment of tissue viability and bleeding risk. In addition, although HT subtypes were adjudicated according to the ECASS II criteria, subtype-specific prediction for HI-1, HI-2, PH-1, and PH-2 was not performed because the present study focused on predicting the occurrence of any HT. Future studies with larger subtype-specific sample sizes may further investigate whether different HT subtypes have distinct clinical and radiomic predictors. Finally, although WMH radiomics improved discrimination in the internal test cohort, the combined model did not show a meaningful AUC improvement over the clinical model in the external validation cohort. This limits the strength of the conclusion regarding the independent added value of WMH radiomics. Future studies with larger multicenter cohorts should further evaluate incremental value using additional metrics such as net reclassification improvement, integrated discrimination improvement, calibration, and decision-curve analysis.

Conclusion

In summary, this dual-center study developed and externally validated a machine-learning model for predicting HT after EVT in AIS using T2-FLAIR WMH radiomics combined with clinical variables. The combined model demonstrated improved performance in the internal test cohort and comparable performance to the clinical model in the external validation cohort. NIHSS score, occlusion site, and atrial fibrillation were identified as independent clinical predictors, while WMH radiomics provided additional quantitative information reflecting underlying cerebrovascular vulnerability. These findings suggest that integrating routine clinical data with preoperative FLAIR-based radiomics may provide complementary information for early HT risk stratification after EVT, although the external incremental value of WMH radiomics requires further validation.
