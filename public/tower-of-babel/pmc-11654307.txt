Exploring the association between life’s essential 8 and gallstone disease in the US adult population: a population-based study utilizing NHANES data from 2017-2018
Authors: Yi Xiao, Wenting Zhao, Qiuchan Zhao, Kangfeng Pang, Qiaoping Gao, Xiaoqu Yang, Huadi Chen, Hao Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveGallstones are a prevalent health issue, with established risk factors such as diet body mass index (BMI) and physical activity. However, the role of Life's Essential 8 (LE8), a composite measure of health behaviors and biological markers, in predicting gallstone risk remains unclear. This study aims to examine the association between LE8 scores and gallstone prevalence in a U.S. adult population.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted on 2836 adults. LE8 scores were categorized into tertiles, and multivariable logistic regression models assessed the relationship between LE8 and gallstones, with additional nonlinear assessment using restricted cubic spline analysis.ResultsGallstones prevalence was 10.37%, decreasing with higher LE8 scores. The multivariable analysis showed participants in higher LE8 tertiles had significantly lower gallstone risks compared to the lowest tertile. The spline analysis confirmed a significant nonlinear inverse association between LE8 and gallstone risk.ConclusionsHigher LE8 scores correlate with a lower prevalence of gallstones, suggesting the potential of LE8 as a predictor of gallstone risk. This emphasizes the value of promoting LE8 components in public health initiatives to reduce gallstone prevalence and medical burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03545-9.Keywords: Life’s essential 8, Gallstones, NHANES, Population-based study

Introduction

Gallstones are calcified stones formed by deposits of substances like bile, bile acids, or cholesterol on the gallbladder and are a digestive system disease. The primary ingredient in the most prevalent kind of gallstone is cholesterol. Hemoglobin breaks down to form calcium bilirubinate, which is what causes black stones. Brown stones, on the other hand, are made of a mixture of calcium substrates, such as calcium bilirubinate, calcium phosphate or palmitate, cholesterol, and bile, and they develop in the context of bacterial or parasite illness [1]. Those with diabetes and other metabolic disorders frequently develop cholesterol stones. On the other hand, individuals who experience hemolysis or have an inflammatory disease like Crohn's disease tend to get black stones, whereas people with biliary strictures and infections from bacteria or parasites tend to get brown-pigmented stones [2]. The prevalence of gallstones in European adults is about 10% to 20%, while the prevalence in Asians is about 5% to 8% [3]. Gallstones are a complex disease resulting from the interaction of genetic and environmental factors [4]. Studies have shown a clear familial aggregation and racial disparity in the incidence of gallstones, with genetic factors accounting for approximately 25% of the population variance [5]. Eighty percent of gallstone patients are asymptomatic; however, without timely intervention, the clinical course of gallstones can progress from asymptomatic carriers to symptomatic complex conditions such as acute cholecystitis, cholangitis, pancreatitis, and, in rare cases, intestinal obstruction [6].

Prior research predominantly addresses the direct links between specific dietary patterns or genetic predispositions and the risk of gallstones. To further our understanding of gallstones beyond traditional risk factors, this study explores the association with Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), a comprehensive health metric initially developed to evaluate cardiovascular health. LE8 encompasses eight components that represent a balance of health behaviors and biological measures: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep duration, body mass index (BMI), non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, blood glucose, and blood pressure [7]. Each component is scored, contributing to an overall LE8 score that provides a holistic measure of an individual's lifestyle and health.

National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The survey is distinctive in that it incorporates both physical examinations and interviews. Several cross-sectional, nationally representative health examination surveys are part of the NHANES program. Questions about demographics, health insurance status, dietary habits, acute and chronic medical issues, mental health, and prescription drug use are all included in the health interview. Exam components can change between survey cycles but typically include blood pressure, dental exams, vision, hearing, dermatology, fitness, balance and strength testing, respiratory testing, taste and smell, and body measurements (weight, height, skin folds, body composition scans). Hematology, organ and endocrine function (e.g., thyroid, kidney), environmental exposure, dietary biomarkers, metabolic and cardiovascular health, and infectious disease are a few examples of laboratory components.

Although LE8 was not specifically proposed for the prevention and treatment of gallstones, its elements, such as a healthy diet and maintaining a healthy weight, may positively impact gallstone prevention [8, 9]. Subsequent studies have also found that dietary and lifestyle habits such as high-fat diets, smoking, heavy drinking, and lack of exercise, as well as diseases like obesity, diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and medications and treatments such as contraceptives and gastrectomy, can increase the risk of gallstones [10–12]. To date, no studies have investigated the association between LE8 and gallstones. In this study, we utilized data from the 2017–2018 NHANES to evaluate the association between LE8 and gallstones in the U.S. adult population.

Method

Study design and participants

The NHANES program is managed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which ensures that all protocols are approved by its Institutional Review Board. Consent is obtained in writing from all participants. For this analysis, the 2017–2018 NHANES cycle data were used, adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [13]. We included participants aged 20 years and older with complete data, including general demographic characteristics, LE8 scores, and gallstone status. The study initially included 9,254 participants. Among them, 3,700 participants had uncertain responses to the gallstone questionnaire, leaving 5,554 participants who clearly completed the gallstone assessment. Finally, only 2836 participants had complete data. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of this selection process. In brief, the inclusion criteria were samples with complete and clearly defined information on gallstones, LE8, age, sex, race, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), Body mass index (BMI), marital status, education, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cardiovascular disease, alcohol intake, and smoking. Exclusion criteria included any samples with missing values for the above information.

Measurement of LE8

The LE8 scoring system includes four lifestyle behaviors (diet, exercise, tobacco use, and sleep patterns) and four health measures (body mass index (BMI), non-high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, glucose levels, and blood pressure). The specific calculation method for LE8 scores based on each NHANES data marker has been detailed in previous publications. Succinctly, scores for the eight cardiovascular health indicators range from 20 to 100. The aggregate LE8 score is derived by taking the simple average of these eight indicators. In our analysis, we adhered to the established definitions and thresholds to assess and categorize scores for both health behaviors and factors, aiming to explore the link between LE8 sub-scores and gallstones. The diet metric was assessed using the Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI-2015) [14]. The HEI-2015 score was constructed and calculated by combining participants' dietary intake collected from two 24-h dietary recalls with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) Food Patterns Equivalents Database [15]. Physical activity, smoking, and sleep information, as well as diabetes history and medication history, were collected from participants' self-reported questionnaires. During the physical exam, measurements of blood pressure, height, and weight were taken. BMI was computed using the formula: weight in kilograms over the square of height in meters. Blood samples were gathered and forwarded to a central lab to analyze lipid profiles, plasma glucose levels, and hemoglobin A1c values.

Definition of gallstones

The presence of gallstones was determined through a questionnaire, which asked, "Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had gallstones?" Participants were grouped according to whether they had gallstones or not.

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, race, marital status, education level, cardiovascular disease, hyperlipidemia, NAFLD, hs-CRP, smoking status, alcohol use, and history of diabetes and hypertension.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables in the study were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were described using frequencies (percentages). To explore differences between groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables, and Chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the relationship between LE8 and the incidence of gallstones. This study established three different logistic regression models: Model 1 did not consider any covariate adjustments; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and race to control for basic demographic variables; and Model 3, a comprehensive model, included adjustments for factors affecting gallstone formation such as age, sex, race, education level, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, NAFLD, hs-CRP, smoking status and alcohol use. A restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to assess the relationship between LE8 and the risk of developing gallstones. The model was adjusted for covariates including age, sex, race, marital status, education, cardiovascular disease, hyperlipidemia, and alcohol use. To determine the effect of confounders on the relationship between LE8 and gallstones, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were performed. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analyses were performed with R (http://www.R-project.org, version 4.3).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 2836 participants were enrolled in this study. The LE8 scores were categorized into three groups based on tertiles: Tertile1 (20,61.15), Tertile2 (61.15,73.75), and Tertile3(73.75,100). The overall prevalence of gallstones was 10.37%, with a trend of decreasing rates in higher LE8 groups: Tertile1 (46%), Tertile2 (30%), and Tertile3 (24%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, there were statistically significant differences among the three LE8 groups in various baseline characteristics, including age. body mass index, hs-CRP,, race, marital status, education level, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, NAFLD, cardiovascular disease, and smoking status (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Relationship between LE8 and gallstone risk

Table 2 details the relationship between LE8 and the risk of gallstones. The lowest tertile of LE8 served as the reference group. In the initial analysis without adjusting for any covariates (Model 1), a significant inverse association was observed between LE8 and the risk of gallstones. Specifically, compared to the baseline group, participants in the second and third tertiles had a 44% and 65% lower risk of gallstones, respectively (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.36–0.86; OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.26–0.48). Model 2 included adjustments for age, sex, and race. Compared to the lowest tertile group, the second and third tertiles had a 42% and 63% lower risk of gallstones, respectively (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35–0.94; OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.24–0.57). Model 3, the most comprehensive model in this study, included all covariates from Model 2 along with education level, marital status, hs-CRP, presence of hyperlipidemia, NAFLD, cardiovascular disease status, smoking habits and alcohol consumption. In this model, the second and third tertiles groups showed a 36% and 59% lower risk of gallstones, respectively, compared to the first tertile(OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.41–0.99; OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.26–0.64). Furthermore, a restricted cubic spline analysis supported these findings, revealing a significant nonlinear inverse relationship between LE8 and the risk of gallstones (p-nonlinear = 0.0412), as depicted in Fig. 2.

Subgroup analysis

To explore the relationship between LE8 and the risk of gallstones, we conducted subgroup analyses based on gender, race, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, smoking status, and alcohol consumption (Table 3). The analysis revealed a negative association between LE8 and a decreased risk of gallstones in the female, non-Mexican American, non-diabetic, non-current smoker, and ever alcohol user subgroups (p < 0.05). However, no significant interaction effects were observed (all p-values for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the correlation is not dependent on gender, race, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, smoking status, or alcohol consumption. These findings underscore the consistency of the association across different subgroups.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate a significant inverse relationship between LE8 scores and the risk of gallstones. Notably, participants in the highest LE8 tertile showed significantly lower odds of developing gallstones, even after adjusting for a comprehensive set of covariates in Model 3. This suggests that LE8 may mediate its effects through mechanisms independent of confounding factors.

The relationship between lifestyle factors and gallstone risk has been widely acknowledged, with specific behaviors and demographic factors identified as high-risk.Previous research indicates that age, sex, dietary patterns, and family history are influential in gallstone development [16, 17]. A cohort study involving over 600,000 individuals in Shizuoka, Japan, showed that the risk of gallstones significantly increases with age. Compared to individuals under 40, those over 80 had a 3.44-fold increased risk of gallstones [18], likely due to reduced gallbladder function and decreased bile acid synthesis in older adults, leading to cholesterol supersaturation and stone formation [19]. The study also found that women had a 1.21-fold higher risk of gallstones compared to men, potentially due to endogenous estrogen in women, which can promote liver cholesterol synthesis and crystallization processes, leading to stone formation [20].

Similarly, dietary habits and the risk of gallstones have been extensively studied [21]. A cohort study of 88,837 nurses in the U.S. found that middle-aged women with high-energy diets had a 1.1-fold increased risk of gallstones compared to those with healthier diets [22]. Multiple cohort studies have linked high intake of saturated and trans fats to a significantly increased risk of gallstones [23]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 cohort studies indicated that smoking is a risk factor for gallstones, with smokers having a 17% higher risk compared to non-smokers [24]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis found that high alcohol intake significantly reduced the risk of gallstones compared to low alcohol intake, with a linear relationship observed: every additional 10 g of alcohol consumed per day reduced the risk of gallstones by an average of 12% [25]. There was also a systematic review and meta-analysis reported a nonlinear relationship between body mass index (BMI) and the risk of gallstones. For BMI under 40 kg/m2, the risk of gallstones increased sharply with rising BMI, while in the 40–45 kg/m2 range, the risk plateaued [26]. Mendelian randomization studies further confirmed a likely direct causal relationship between higher BMI and increased risk of gallstones [27]. Besides, it is reported that compared to individuals without obstructive sleep apnea, those with the condition had an elevated risk of developing gallstones [28]. These above reports indirectly support the findings of our study. In comparison, our study demonstrates that the composite LE8 score, which integrates these variables, remains significantly inversely associated with the incidence of gallstone disease, suggesting a potential synergistic effect among these factors.

Potential mechanisms by which LE8 may influence gallstone risk could include its impact on bile composition and gallbladder motility through the combined effect of its components. For example, maintaining a healthy BMI and engaging in regular physical activity can lower cholesterol levels, which may reduce cholesterol supersaturation in bile—a primary factor in gallstone formation. Similarly, smoking cessation and a healthy diet can improve lipid profiles, further decreasing gallstone risk. Understanding these mechanisms more thoroughly will require future research to explore how the interactions of LE8 components influence bile chemistry and the development of gallstones, potentially informing new preventive strategies. LE8 was designed primarily to assess cardiovascular health, specific adjustments could increase its relevance for gallstone prediction. For instance, additional focus on dietary patterns that specifically influence bile composition, such as reducing saturated fats and incorporating more fiber-rich foods, could strengthen LE8's predictive capacity for gallstone formation. Including a refined measure of alcohol intake that reflects its protective association with gallstone risk may also enhance prediction. These modifications could make LE8 more applicable for identifying individuals at high risk for gallstones, supporting earlier preventive interventions.

One of the major strengths of this study is the use of a large, nationally representative dataset from NHANES, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, comprehensive adjustments for a wide range of demographic and health-related covariates help to minimize potential confounding effects, providing a clearer understanding of the impact of LE8.

However, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts our ability to infer causality between LE8 and gallstone incidence, as we cannot determine the temporal sequence of exposure and outcome. While our findings suggest an association between LE8 scores and gallstones, it is unclear whether low LE8 scores directly contribute to gallstone formation, or if individuals with gallstones are more likely to adopt poorer lifestyle behaviors that influence LE8 scores. Longitudinal studies, particularly cohort studies, are needed to confirm these findings and establish a causal relationship by tracking changes in LE8 scores over time and their subsequent impact on gallstone risk. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires for gallstone diagnosis may introduce misclassification bias, as participants may inaccurately recall or underreport their medical history. This could affect the accuracy of our exposure assessment and potentially lead to an underestimation or overestimation of the true association between LE8 and gallstones. Such biases may also limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with more objective diagnostic measures or those who might not accurately recall medical information. Further research using more accurate diagnostic tools, such as imaging studies, and longitudinal designs will help validate the role of LE8 in gallstone prevention and address the limitations of self-reported data.

Our findings suggesting that higher LE8 scores are linked to lower gallstone prevalence. Public health initiatives that promote healthy behaviors—such as a balanced diet, regular physical activity, non-smoking, sufficient sleep, and management of key health factors like weight, blood lipids, blood pressure, and blood glucose—could help reduce the prevalence and medical burden of gallstones. Given its comprehensive nature, LE8 could be integrated into routine clinical assessments to help identify individuals at risk for gallstones. By incorporating LE8 into regular health check-ups, clinicians can use the overall score to pinpoint patients who may benefit from targeted lifestyle interventions. For example, individuals with lower LE8 scores may be counseled to adopt healthier dietary habits, increase physical activity, and manage weight more effectively. For symptomatic cases, medical interventions, including cholecystectomy, are often required. By improving LE8-related factors, individuals may lower their gallstone risk and potentially reduce the need for surgical intervention.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that higher LE8 scores are associated with a significantly reduced risk of gallstones. Future research should focus on validating these findings through longitudinal studies and examining the mechanisms by which LE8 influences bile composition and gallstone formation. Such efforts could lead to more specific nutritional and lifestyle recommendations, targeted at mitigating gallstone risk and improving overall health outcomes in at-risk populations.
