Visual Hand Gesture Recognition with Deep Learning: A Comprehensive Review of Methods, Datasets, Challenges and Future Research Directions
Konstantinos Foteinos, Jorgen Cani, Manousos Linardakis, Panagiotis Radoglou-Grammatikis, Vasileios Argyriou, Panagiotis Sarigiannidis, Iraklis Varlamis, Georgios Th. Papadopoulos

Abstract
The rapid evolution of deep learning (DL) models and the ever-increasing size of available datasets have raised the interest of the research community in the always important field of vision-based hand gesture recognition (VHGR), and delivered a wide range of applications, such as sign language understanding and human-computer interaction using cameras. Despite the large volume of research works in the field, a structured and complete survey on VHGR is still missing, leaving researchers to navigate through hundreds of papers in order to find the right combination of data, model, and approach for each task. The current survey aims to fill this gap by presenting a comprehensive overview of this aspect of computer vision. With a systematic research methodology that identifies the state-of-the-art works and a structured presentation of the various methods, datasets, and evaluation metrics, this review aims to constitute a useful guideline for researchers, helping them to choose the right strategy for delving into a certain VHGR task. Starting with the methodology used for study selection, literature retrieval, and the analytical framing, the survey identifies and organizes key VHGR approaches using a taxonomy-based format in various dimensions such as input modality and application domain. The core of the survey provides an in-depth analysis of state-of-the-art techniques across three primary VHGR tasks: static gesture recognition, isolated dynamic gestures and continuous gesture recognition. For each task, the architectural trends and learning strategies are listed. Additionally, the study reviews commonly used datasets —emphasizing on annotation schemes— and evaluates standard performance metrics. It concludes by identifying major challenges in VHGR, including both general computer vision issues and domain-specific obstacles, and outlines promising directions for future research.

Given the importance of the applications mentioned above, Hand Gesture Recognition (HGR) has grown into a broad research field, generally divided into two main approaches: vision-based and sensor-based. Vision-based HGR relies on visual data such as RGB and depth images, as well as features derived from them—like pose heatmaps or optical flow. Sensor-based HGR, on the other hand, uses data from devices such as wearable gloves or surface EMG (sEMG) sensors, and even non-wearable sources like acoustic signals. Researchers have also explored multi-modal methods that combine inputs from different sources. In this study, we focus on vision-based HGR, as sensor-based methods often involve high cost and complex setup, making them less practical for real-world use. Vision-based approaches better align with the goal of HGR: to enable more natural, unobtrusive, and accessible human-machine interaction.

Static HGR involves recognizing gestures from single frames or spatial data only. Isolated dynamic HGR extends this by analyzing short, pre-segmented sequences to capture both spatial and temporal patterns. Continuous dynamic HGR goes a step further by detecting multiple gestures within long, unsegmented video sequences. Each task presents its own unique challenges. For example, CHGR often relies on weak supervision, making optimization more difficult. At the same time, all tasks share common issues such as interference from irrelevant background movements or variations in lighting, viewpoint, and hand appearance.

Vision-based Hand Gesture Recognition (HGR) has a long history, beginning with traditional computer vision techniques based on hand-crafted features and evolving into complex deep learning pipelines
Molchanov et al. 2016
. Early approaches typically followed a three-stage process: pre-processing, feature extraction, and classification. Pre-processing often involved segmenting the hand region using skin color or other visual cues. Feature extraction relied on descriptors like Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), while classification was usually performed using models such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) or Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), depending on the task. With the rise of deep learning, these hand-crafted features were gradually replaced by learned representations, which offer better generalization. Modern deep learning architectures have become increasingly deep and sophisticated, especially for dynamic HGR tasks. These models first capture short-term motion patterns and then aggregate them to understand long-term context
Min et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2022
;
Hao et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2023d
;
Hu et al. 2023c
. Due to their superior performance, this study focuses primarily on deep learning-based methods.

Given the broad scope of vision-based Hand Gesture Recognition (HGR) within computer vision, it is crucial to identify the current state-of-the-art methods for the three tasks mentioned above, as well as the key challenges that have shaped the field’s development. This survey aims to 1) provide an overview of the field, enabling readers to quickly find the most relevant publications for their needs, 2) offer a detailed analysis of each method, highlighting their key contributions, and 3) present the state-of-the-art solutions, based on the previous analysis, and explain the main challenges that continue to drive research in HGR.

2
Literature Review Methodology

The review begins with a clear definition of the research methodology, establishing a structured foundation for the comprehensive exploration of the VHGR landscape. This systematic approach is intended to serve as a valuable reference for researchers and practitioners seeking to navigate and contribute to the field. The initial step involved defining the scope of the study, which informed the formulation of inclusion and exclusion criteria used to guide the selection of relevant publications. Subsequently, targeted queries were conducted across major scientific databases, including Scopus and IEEE Xplore, to retrieve works aligned with the established criteria. The final stage entailed a thorough examination of the selected literature, with the objective of distilling the key innovations, contributions, and methodological insights of each publication. These elements are further elaborated in the following sections.

2.1
Survey Scope

Positioned at the intersection of computer vision and human-computer interaction, this work sets out to map the evolving landscape of vision-based hand gesture recognition. By distilling the most impactful recent contributions, it seeks to clarify the field’s current trajectory and highlight its most pressing research needs. The review is structured around five foundational pillars:

2.2
Selection Criteria

To ensure the quality, relevance, and completeness of this review, a well-defined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria was established prior to initiating the literature search. The selection process was guided by three key axes:

•

Relevance
: Only works that directly address VHGR were considered. This includes methods focused on hand gesture recognition and sign language recognition based solely on visual modalities such as RGB images, infrared (IR), optical flow, motion history, depth maps, and pose representations (e.g., skeleton landmarks and heatmaps derived either from RGB-based pose estimators or visual data acquisition devices). Studies centered on Human Action Recognition (HAR), sensor-based techniques (e.g., using electromyography or radar), or multimodal approaches combining visual and non-visual data were excluded to maintain a focused scope.

•

Up-to-date
: To reflect the current state of the field, priority was given to publications from 2021 onward, particularly those introducing new methods. Exceptions were made for seminal works—defined here as milestone contributions post-2015 with high citation counts—that have significantly shaped VHGR research. In the case of datasets, no strict publication date was imposed, but only those actively used by the community or released within the past four years were included. All survey papers considered were published after 2021.

•

Impact
: To further focus on seminal and influential works, publications were sourced from highly cited venues (i.e. having an h5-index of 90 or above on Google Scholar), with a single exception for the IEEE International Conference on Automatic Face & Gesture Recognition, which is closely aligned with VHGR.
In addition to the above, and aiming to provide the theoretical context of the study, a small number of milestone papers has also been referenced, though not analyzed in the main methods section.

Categorizing the Identified Articles

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the remaining publications were carefully reviewed for relevance. Those that did not meet the criteria were excluded from further analysis. The selected papers were then categorized into three distinct groups: methods, datasets, and review papers. •

Architecture
: Building on the previous criteria, which focused on the nature of input and camera perspective, this criterion concerns the architectural design of the gesture recognition system, regarding the modeling stages performed by the backbone. Specifically, it refers to how spatial and temporal information is processed and integrated to model gesture dynamics. Three main categories are identified:

–

Spatial
architectures consider only spatial data, such as isolated RGB frames, and fed them into corresponding DNNs (mainly 2DCNNs) to capture spatial information.

–

Temporal
architectures extract only temporal features, utilizing sequential models to process sequences of raw data, such as coordinates of skeleton joints.

–

Spatiotemporal
modeling architectures, that simultaneously analyze both the spatial and temporal domain, such as 3DCNNs.

–

Spatial + Temporal
pipelines, that firstly extract spatial features (e.g., using 2DCNNs) and secondly pass them through a sequential model (e.g., BiLSTM) to perform temporal modeling.

–

Short-term Spatiotemporal + Long-term Temporal
pipelines, which aim, given a sequence of data, to capture local context (that is, they extract spatiotemporal features given small parts of the overall sequence) and then fed them into a temporal model to aggregate the partial information and obtain the global vector representation.

•

Learning Strategy
: This criterion explores the different training methodologies adopted by researchers to develop VHGR models, often influenced by data availability, task complexity, and desired generalization. The main strategies identified include:

–

Transfer Learning
, where models are pretrained on large-scale datasets (e.g., Jester
Materzynska et al. 2019
) and subsequently fine-tuned for specific downstream VHGR tasks
Lu et al. 2024b
.

–

Weakly Supervised Learning
, often used in Continuous Hand Gesture Recognition (CHGR), where temporal annotations are coarse or unaligned, lacking exact gesture boundaries
Wei and Chen 2023
.

–

Knowledge Distillation
, in which one part of the network (often a teacher model) transfers knowledge to another (student model) to enhance feature extraction and efficiency
Min et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2022
.

–

Contrastive Learning
, used either in a CLIP-based framework to support zero-shot/few-shot recognition by leveraging textual descriptions
Bilge et al. 2023
;
Rastgoo et al. 2023
;
Bilge et al. 2024a
, or to improve the discriminative capacity of learned representations
Zhao et al. 2024b
;
Zhao et al. 2024c
.

–

Multi-task Learning
, where a single model is optimized to perform multiple related tasks simultaneously, encouraging knowledge sharing and improved generalization
Li et al. 2025
.

–

Reinforcement Learning
, applied to optimize decision-making processes, such as keyframe selection, by rewarding effective representations
Gan et al. 2023
.

–

Few/Zero-shot Learning
, which enable the model to generalize to new gestures or languages with limited or no annotated examples, often leveraging semantic or textual guidance
Bilge et al. 2023
;
Rastgoo et al. 2023
;
Bilge et al. 2024a
.

–

Adversarial Learning
, used as a data augmentation strategy

to improve robustness, as demonstrated in adversarial video synthesis networks

This comprehensive taxonomy of vision-based hand gesture recognition (VHGR) methods, has been grounded in a diverse set of classification criteria that span from application domains to architectural and training strategies. Among these, the task criterion, distinguishing between static and dynamic gestures based on their temporal properties, emerges as particularly pivotal. It fundamentally shapes not only the selection of appropriate models and input modalities but also the interpretation of gestures in real-world scenarios. Due to its central role in determining the complexity and design of recognition systems, the task criterion serves as a natural point of departure for further analysis. The next three sections delve deeper into this dimension by systematically examining the core types of VHGR tasks—Static Visual Hand Gesture Recognition, Isolated Dynamic VHGR, and Continuous VHGR—each with its own challenges, methodologies, and applications.

4
Methods for Static VHGR

Static VHGR aims to identify gestures relying only on spatial data. As this approach neglects temporal information, is only suitable for recognizing static hand gestures. Proposed methods for static VHGR can be further categorized into
single-stage and multi-stage approaches
, based on whether the architecture consists of a single model or a multi-model processing pipeline, respectively. Indicative works of each category are briefly analyzed into the two following subsections. The third subsection concludes with the main features of state-of-the-art static VHGR methods, and discusses the open challenges that concern the community.

4.1
Single-stage Approaches

Single-stage approaches rely on a single model, which varies depending on the task. For gesture classification, fine-tuned variants of well-known 2D-CNN architectures are employed, such as VGG and DenseNet, or custom lightweight architectures are trained from scratch. When classification is combined with localization of gestures, variants of YOLO are adopted. As far as it concerns the application, static VHGR methods are used in Sign Language Understanding (SLU) and Human Computer Interaction (HCI). However, a few more general-purpose models have also been proposed:

•

For
Sign Language Understanding
, the authors in
Sharma and Singh 2021
developed G-CNN, a shallow 2DCNN to recognize Indian Sign Language (ISL) signs and slightly outperformed various versions of VGG on a self-collected dataset. The authors in
Ghorai et al. 2023
proposed the STN-ND-Net, a combination of a Spatial Transformer Network (STN) that improves robustness to spatial transformations, with a 2D-CNN backbone that uses deconvolution operations to reduce the pixel-wise and channel-wise correlations and eliminates the information redundancy of colored images. The success of vision transformer (ViT) motivated authors of SIGNFORMER
Kothadiya et al. 2023
to adopt it for ISL recognition, while authors in
Shin et al. 2024a
leveraged both skeleton and RGB data in their dual stream feature extractor and an MLP to perform intermediate fusion. A YOLO variant, namely YOLOv4-CSP
Alaftekin et al. 2024
has also been developed, to simultaneous detect and identify the hand gesture.

•

For gesture-based
Human-Computer Interaction
the authors in
Zhang et al. 2023b
proposed a lightweight 2D-CNN, named LHGR-Net, which combines a Multiscale Structure (MSS) that aims to make the model scale-invariant via atrous convolution with a Lightweight Attention Structure (LAS) based on CBAM. The authors in
Jafari and Basu 2023
proposed 2DPSTPP-Net, a 2D-CNN with four stacked convolutional blocks whose features are pooled using max pooling and a novel method, then concatenated to form the final representation, tailored for HCI scenarios. The authors in
Han et al. 2024
modified the YOLOv5 to make it more lightweight and developed a model that can be used for gesture-based user interfaces in smart sports stadiums, improving the experience of spectators and athletes.

•

General-purpose
models have also been developed. EDenseNet
Tan et al. 2021
extends DenseNet by adding convolutional layers between dense blocks. SpAtNet
Bhaumik and Govil 2024
is a lightweight 2DCNN with three streams: two MAFF modules for coarse-grained features and one Interleaved Module for high-level representations; their fused features are fed to an MLP for classification. MMFANet
Liang et al. 2024
performs gesture detection and recognition using a 2DCNN backbone with task-specific regressor and classifier branches.

Multi-stage Approaches

In contrast to single-stage, multi-stage approaches set up a multi-model processing pipeline. Typically, a hand detector or segmentation model is used to remove the background and normalize the spatial dimensions. These methods are primarily applied in Human Robot Interaction (HRI) and HCI scenarios, as detailed below:

•

Human-Robot Interaction

Bamani et al. 2024
is a three-stage approach using YOLOv3 for human cropping, HQ-Net for super-resolution to address long-range scenarios, and a classifier for gesture recognition. Similarly,
Sahoo et al. 2023b

•

Multi-stage static VHGR methods have also been proposed for
Human-Computer Interaction
. In
Alam et al. 2022

Zhou et al. 2024
introduced FGDSNet, a two-stage approach with dual-stream segmentation (local detail and semantic branches) and multi-level fusion for feature integration. The segmentation mask and original image are fed into a dual-stream classification module, where mid-level fusion combines features for final classification via an MLP.

5
Methods for Isolated Dynamic VHGR

Unlike static VHGR approaches, which often fail to capture temporal dynamics and struggle with motion-based gestures, recent advances in
Isolated Dynamic Vision-based Hand Gesture Recognition (IDVHGR)
have focused on recognizing individual gesture instances from spatiotemporal inputs such as RGB video sequences or skeleton joint trajectories.

When gestures are part of a sign language, the task is referred to as Isolated Sign Language Recognition (ISLR). A notable distinction is that ISLR models frequently leverage both manual features (e.g., hand movements) and non-manual features (e.g., facial expressions, upper body motion), which can significantly enhance recognition accuracy. As a result, many ISLR approaches go beyond hand-centric analysis to incorporate richer contextual cues.

In the following, we present IDVHGR methods grouped by input modality as (a)
unimodal appearance-based
, (b)
pose-based
, and (c)
multimodal
approaches. Then, we conclude with a summary of key innovations and state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance.

5.1
Unimodal Appearance-based Methods

Unimodal appearance-based methods rely on a single input type—such as RGB, optical flow, infrared (IR), motion history, depth, or depth flow—to classify hand gestures. As shown in Fig.
4
, these representations resemble color images, which allows for the application of similar computer vision techniques. These methods can be further categorized based on the architecture of the feature extractor, which typically follows one of three main pipelines: (1) spatiotemporal, (2) spatial + temporal, and (3) short-term spatiotemporal + long-term temporal backbones. The following paragraphs explain each of these architectures in more detail.

5.1.1
Spatiotemporal

Spatiotemporal backbones perform spatial and temporal modeling jointly, typically using 3D CNNs or (2+1)D CNNs—either custom-built or adapted from existing models.

In
Wang et al. 2022a
, the authors introduced a custom network, (2+1)D-SLR, composed of five stacked (2+1)D convolutional blocks (i.e., a 2D convolution followed by a 1D convolution). This structure lowers computational cost compared to traditional 3D convolutions, making the model more practical for deployment.
To enhance temporal modeling, authors in
Bharti et al. 2023
proposed a trainable key frame selection method based on error correction. VGG16 features are used to identify key frames, which are then preprocessed and fed into a custom 3D CNN for classification.

In
Wang et al. 2022c
, a dual-stage multi-stream pipeline for CSL recognition was developed. An adapted EfficientDet model—enhanced with cross-level Bi-FPN connections and a dual-channel spatial attention module (DCSAM)—detects both hands. The full image and cropped hand patches are passed through three parallel 3D-ResNet-18 models (following the P3D scheme), with their features fused and classified via an MLP.
Authors in
Hao et al. 2023
introduced TS3C-Net, a modified 3D ConvNeXt that adds an extra 3×3×3 convolutional branch to each block, enhancing spatiotemporal representation.
Finally,
Tang et al. 2021b
addressed limitations in fixed-pipeline designs by combining features from both ResC3D and ConvLSTM using the Selective Spatiotemporal (SeST) module. The fused features are passed through fully connected and softmax layers for classification.

5.1.2
Spatial + Temporal

These backbones first extract spatial features from each frame, typically using 2D CNNs or vision transformers, and then model the temporal dynamics with sequential modules such as GRUs or LSTMs. The resulting sequence embeddings are aggregated, and a classifier (e.g., MLP) outputs the final prediction.

In
Hax et al. 2024
, a method tailored for HCI tasks using only depth data was proposed. The method combined Inception-v3 that extracted spatial features with an LSTM for temporal modeling.
For ISLR, authors in
Venugopalan and Reghunadhan 2021
introduced a pipeline to recognize Indian Sign Language (ISL) words used by deaf farmers. They combined GoogleNet for spatial encoding with a BiLSTM for sequence modeling.

To reduce inference time, a lightweight ISLR model, called TIM-SLR has been proposed in
Wang et al. 2023a
. The model uses a 2D-CNN for per-frame feature extraction, followed by a Time Interaction Module (TIM)—a zero-parameter unit that fuses frame features into a compact representation.
Finally, the Swin Transformer presented in
Du et al. 2022
is a two-stage transformer-based model that extracts spatial features per frame, while a causally masked transformer processes the sequence, ensuring that only past and present frame features contribute at each step.

5.1.3
Short-Term Spatiotemporal + Long-Term Temporal

The methods in this category follow a two-stage architecture: the first stage captures short-term spatiotemporal patterns from segments of the input sequence, while the second stage aggregates these features to form a final representation of the entire sequence.

EgoFormer
Qazi et al. 2024
is a model for egocentric dynamic gesture recognition optimized for edge devices, which has been introduced in the context of autonomous driving. EgoFormer combines a custom 3D-CNN with a transformer module and an MLP for final classification.
For long-range gesture-based UGV control, authors in
Beeri et al. 2024
proposed a pipeline where a lightweight 2D CNN extracts per-frame features, which are used to select key frames. After cropping the human region, the selected frames are passed through a SlowFast network, followed by a transformer and an MLP.

To address data scarcity in sign language understanding,
Bilge et al. 2023
proposed a zero-shot learning framework. Sign videos are represented using BERT-encoded textual descriptions combined with hand-crafted attributes. This representation guides a dual-stream backbone composed of short-term spatiotemporal modules (e.g., I3D) and long-term models (e.g., BiLSTM).

5.1.4
Other

Some appearance-based methods fall outside the previously discussed categories but offer valuable contributions, especially in Sign Language Recogition (SLR).
For example, the method proposed in
Luqman 2022
for Indian SLR (ISLR) compresses an entire frame sequence into a single image, which is then processed by a MobileNet. A second MobileNet extracts per-frame features, which are passed through a two-layer LSTM. The outputs of both streams are concatenated and refined using a custom shallow 2D-CNN.
A different approach was presented in
Kothadiya et al. 2024
, where the authors introduced a dual-stream ISLR architecture combining Inception V4 with a novel convolutional module called stem.

In the context of pose-based ISLR,
Liu et al. 2024c
proposed a dual-stream model with keyframe selection. A 2D-CNN and attention mechanism filter out less informative frames. The remaining keyframes are processed by two branches: the Hand stream, which crops and analyzes hand regions with attention blocks, and the Full stream, which processes the entire frame. The fused features are used for final classification.
To improve signer-independence and reduce overfitting, the SwC GR-MMixer
Liu et al. 2024b
, another lightweight model based on MLP-Mixer, incorporates GRU units for temporal modeling and a shifted window concatenation strategy to enhance sequence representation.

5.2
Pose-based Methods

Pose-based methods, also known as skeleton-based approaches, rely on structured motion data—such as skeleton graphs and pose heatmaps—to classify gestures. These methods often incorporate various forms of pose information, including joint coordinates, bones, motion trajectories, and joint angles, sometimes in combination
Deng et al. 2024
. They also explore joint subsets related to specific body regions
Patra et al. 2024
, often adopting multi-stream architectures to handle these diverse inputs.

Given the natural graph structure of skeletal data, many methods employ Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) originally developed for human activity recognition. In the sections that follow, we group the approaches based on the structure of their backbone, following the same organization as in the previous subsection. Notably, pose information alone is often insufficient for accurate sign recognition
Moryossef et al. 2021
. Consequently, many models integrate additional cues—typically from RGB inputs—using 3D CNNs and perform score-level fusion to enhance performance.

5.2.1
Spatiotemporal

Pose-based methods that incorporate spatiotemporal representations simultaneously process spatial and temporal data to improve sign language understanding (SLU), sign language recognition (SLR), and human-computer interaction (HCI).

In Sign Language Understanding (SLU), pose-based methods aim to capture dynamic hand and body movements for gesture recognition. SKIM
Lin et al. 2024
uses a dynamic graph convolutional network (GCN) combined with temporal convolution (TCN) to analyze both global and local body regions, with a feature extraction module focusing on high-motion joints. Similarly, MC-LSTM
Özdemir et al. 2023
employs multiple streams (face, hand, and whole body) processed by temporal convolutions and GCN for improved feature ensembling. Another notable method, P3D
Lee et al. 2023
, uses a transformer-based model with part-wise and whole-body encoders to process landmark data for classification.
For improved performance, HWGAT
Patra et al. 2024
utilizes hierarchical spatial windows with attention mechanisms to highlight key body parts. TMS-Net
Deng et al. 2024
proposes a six-stream GCN-based approach for processing skeleton joints, bones, and motion data, with inter-stream information exchange. Additionally, methods like SLGTformer
Song and Xiang 2022
and VSCT
Bohacek and Hrúz 2023
address the challenge of data scarcity by leveraging transformers and few-shot learning, respectively.

In Sign Language Recognition (SLR), pose-based methods often focus on improving the accuracy and robustness of sign classification. Authors in
Shin et al. 2024a
present a dual-stream GCN model for Korean SLR, analyzing joint and motion data. Similarly, MULTISTAGE-CGAR
Miah et al. 2024
proposes a dual-stream architecture with separable temporal convolution and attention mechanisms for improved feature extraction. For multi-stream fusion, SwC GR-MMixer
Liu et al. 2024b
combines GCN with GRU units for temporal modeling and attention mechanisms to reduce overfitting.
Notably, L-TSSI-DenseNet
Laines et al. 2023
explores skeleton graph representations by converting joint coordinates into image-like matrices processed by DenseNet for classification. Moreover, STST
Zhao et al. 2024a
leverages a two-stage transformer-based approach for global and local spatial-temporal feature extraction, offering a dynamic alternative to traditional methods.

In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), pose-based methods address challenges such as data overfitting and the need for self-supervised learning frameworks. MAE-based frameworks
Ikne et al. 2024
use a masked autoencoder to reconstruct skeleton graphs, focusing on motion and spatial relationships. 3sISTGCN
Song et al. 2022
proposes a three-stream model to process joints, bones, and motions, applying modified ST-GCN layers and late fusion for improved classification. Another approach, DG-STA
Aich et al. 2023
, tackles class-incremental learning in HCI by training models on non-overlapping tasks without access to old training data.
DSTSA-GCN
Cui et al. 2025
addresses temporal expression limitations by stacking three stages of graph convolutions and multiscale temporal convolutions. Additionally, DSTA-SLR
Hu et al. 2024c
improves spatiotemporal modeling by introducing dynamic adjacency matrices and multi-stream fusion, enhancing the accuracy of joint, bone, and motion-based recognition tasks.

5.2.2
Spatial + Temporal

Many models use a two-step approach, where the spatial domain is first processed by a spatial perception module to extract spatial features. These features are then passed into a sequential model for temporal aggregation, resulting in a combined spatial-temporal representation of the input sequence.
For instance, GCN-BERT
Tunga et al. 2021
utilizes a two-layer GCN for spatial modeling, followed by BERT for temporal modeling.

Other general-purpose methods adopt a multi-stream, two-stage pose-based approach. In
Narayan et al. 2023
, three streams, each containing three 1D convolutional layers, are combined with a parallel module of average pooling layers. The extracted features are then concatenated and input into an LSTM, followed by a fully connected layer and softmax for classification.
Similarly, STr-GCN
Slama et al. 2023
employs a two-stage model where a GCN spatial feature extractor is first applied to obtain intra-frame features and then passes the features through a transformer encoder for modeling inter-frame temporal relationships. The final classification scores are generated by an MLP.

5.2.3
Short-Term Spatiotemporal + Long-Term Temporal

In this approach, the backbone first extracts features from smaller segments of the skeleton sequence to capture short-term spatial-temporal relationships. Then, long-term temporal modeling is applied to aggregate global context across the entire sequence.

For example, gesture recognition in autonomous driving has become an important research area. In
Guo et al. 2024b
, the authors propose a pose-based approach called MG-GCT for recognizing police traffic gestures. The model uses two graph convolution streams: one to process skeleton joints and another to analyze joint motions. The features extracted from both streams are fused and passed through a transformer encoder for temporal modeling, with the final classification performed by an MLP.
In another approach, TD-GCN
Liu et al. 2024a
introduces a pose-based method for IDHGR. Unlike traditional methods that use fixed skeleton topologies, TD-GCN employs temporal-sensitive topology learning to enhance feature quality. First, graph convolution is used to extract high-level spatiotemporal representations, which are then used to calculate temporal and channel-independent adjacency matrices. These matrices help model the skeleton, and the features are fused using the proposed TCF module.

5.2.4
Temporal

Some pose-based methods bypass spatial modeling and focus directly on sequential processing. Typically, keypoints are slightly preprocessed—often by extracting geometric features such as bone angles—and then passed into sequential models like RNNs or TCNs to produce a vector representation of the input sequence.

In
Qi et al. 2021
, the authors proposed a pose-based method for surgical robot teleoperation. Preprocessed skeleton joints are fed into an LSTM, and the resulting features are passed to an MLP for final classification. The model was evaluated on a custom dataset.
Similarly, in
Sabater et al. 2021
, a cross-domain gesture recognition approach was developed using geometric features (e.g., bone angles), which are processed by a TCN. The resulting features are summarized into a sequence descriptor, and a linear classifier predicts the final class.

To address data scarcity, the authors in
Nakamura and Jing 2025
introduced AVSN, an adversarial learning-based data augmentation framework. The method jointly optimizes augmentation parameters and the HGR classifier. A BiLSTM serves as the discriminator, while an MLP acts as the generator. The generator produces challenging augmented data that exploits weaknesses in the discriminator, which in turn learns to correctly classify gestures.

5.2.5
Other

Some architectures adopt more complex designs or alternative skeleton representations, placing them outside the previously defined categories.

In
Abdullahi et al. 2024
, the authors combined a Fourier CNN with a BiLSTM to extract spatiotemporal features from 3D skeleton joints and perform ISLR. A trainable binary mask was also used for feature selection to eliminate redundancy. Similarly, authors in
Alamri et al. 2024
performed ISLR using a feature thresholding technique that transforms skeleton features based on their variance and importance. These transformed features undergo exhaustive search for selection and are then processed by a deep CNN for classification.

To address limitations of color-coded feature maps, the authors in
Kishore et al. 2024
proposed the Joint Motion Affinity Map (JMAM) as a new skeleton representation for ISLR. JMAM is input to a custom multi-stream attention-based 2DCNN, which extracts features and performs classification.
Key frame selection was explored in
Gan et al. 2023
through the Frame Selection Network (FS-Net), trained using reinforcement learning. The agent selects frames for classification by DD-Net and receives a reward of +1 or -1 based on the correctness of the classification outcome.
Finally,
Li et al. 2024
proposed a lightweight dual-stream method to fully capture spatiotemporal features. One stream employs stacked attention-based modules, while the other uses an autoencoder. The outputs of both are classified independently, and their predictions are averaged to yield the final decision.

5.3
Multimodal Methods

Multimodal approaches combine different input types (e.g., RGB, Depth, Skeleton, Text) to improve gesture recognition performance by leveraging complementary information. IDVHGR multimodal methods do not seem to follow any trend, maybe due to the wide spectrum of alternative options (combinations of streams, fusion techniques, modalities, etc.), so we group them by application domain.

5.3.1
General Purpose

In
Li et al. 2023
, a dual-stream 3DCNN followed by average fusion was proposed for RGB-D gesture recognition. To filter out redundant visual features (
\upsilon
) and retain only the most discriminative components (
z
), a memory bank mechanism was introduced. The training objective maximizes mutual information between
z
and the gesture class
y
, while minimizing that between
z
and
\upsilon
. The memory bank is updated via read/write operations during training.
Similarly, addressing redundancy, authors in
Zhou et al. 2021a
applied Neural Architecture Search (NAS) and attention mechanisms. A modified I3D backbone replaces some inception modules with learnable NAS cells. These progressively transition from small-kernel to dilated convolutions. Dynamic Static Attention (DSA) modules focus on motion and hand/arm regions, aided by a parallel HeatmapNet trained jointly through an auxiliary loss. For RGB-D input, late fusion is used.

To move beyond the limitations of late fusion and exploit modality interactions more effectively,
Zhou et al. 2022a
proposed a decoupling-recoupling framework. Independent spatial (with a Focused Region Proposal (FRP) module) and temporal modeling (via attention) are first performed to extract modality-specific features. These are then fused mid-way using a shared MLP-AE module.
Multi-level fusion was further studied in
Hampiholi et al. 2023
, where 3DCNN backbones are combined with a Convolutional Transformer Fusion Block (CTFB). Injected at multiple layers, CTFB fuses intermediate features across streams for better spatial-temporal integration.

To address data scarcity, several works explored zero-shot learning (ZSL) by incorporating textual gesture descriptions encoded by pretrained language models (mainly BERT). In
Rastgoo et al. 2023
, a dual-stream model combined a ViT for spatial modeling and an LSTM-MLP for temporal modeling, learning visual features aligned with textual embeddings. Similarly,
Rastgoo et al. 2024
fused skeleton-derived geometric features with RGB-C3D-LSTM-AE representations into a shared semantic space for classification.
To reduce computational cost,
Garg et al. 2025
replaced the transformer’s self-attention mechanism with convolutional layers and a gated feedforward network, resulting in the lightweight ConvMixFormer architecture.

In
Wan et al. 2023
, knowledge distillation and contrastive learning were employed to enhance multimodal performance. Three 3DCNNs (for RGB, Depth, and Optical Flow) extract features fused via Coordinate Attention (CA). I3D serves as the teacher, and ResNet as the student. CLIP is also integrated for zero-shot gesture representation.
The limitations of standard 3DCNNs were explored in
Ma et al. 2023
, where 3D Central Difference Convolution (3D-CDC) replaces standard 3D convolutions. Residual hybrid blocks and a transformer module handle temporal modeling.
Yu et al. 2021
also adopted 3D-CDC in a multi-stream, multi-rate setup with multi-level fusion. NAS optimizes the configuration of CDC types, inter-stream connections, and frame rates.

5.3.2
Sign Language Understanding

Several recent works have proposed multimodal and pose-centric architectures for Isolated Sign Language Recognition (ISLR), focusing on combining RGB and skeleton data.

A prominent line of research is the SignBERT framework
Hu et al. 2021a
, which uses a spatial GCN with temporal and hand chirality encoding to extract gesture features. These are fed into a transformer encoder, while a hand-model-aware decoder reconstructs masked inputs during self-supervised pretraining. Predictions from a parallel RGB network are fused at inference time.
An enhanced version, SignBERT+
Hu et al. 2023b
, introduces spatial-temporal position encoding and an improved fusion module for RGB and pose data. The architecture is illustrated in Fig.
10
. Similarly, MASA
Zhao et al. 2024b
adopts the same GCN+Transformer backbone, trained with contrastive learning on pose reconstruction. BEST
Zhao et al. 2023
extends this by embedding pose triplets (left hand, right hand, body) with BERT and applying late fusion with RGB data.

5.3.3
Human-Computer Interaction

Motivated by the difficulty of recognizing fine-grained gestures in HCI applications, authors in
Zhang et al. 2024
proposed HandSense, a method relying on depth and RGB data. Two 3DCNNs are deployed to extract features for each one modality. These features are fused and fed into a SVM to obtain the final prediction.
Authors in
Karsh et al. 2024
proposed an approach relying on RGBD Dynamic Images (RDDIs). These images, are obtained by the combination of RGB and estimated depth data. The resulted RDDI is fed into a modified Xception, resulting from removing the top 4 Xception modules, in order to extract meaningful features. These features are finally used to perform the classification.

5.3.4
Other

Inspired by the success of transformers, GestFormer
Garg et al. 2024a

In
Gao et al. 2022

6
Methods for Continuous Dynamic HGR

In contrast to isolated dynamic hand gesture recognition (IDHGR), where each sample contains at most one gesture, continuous hand gesture recognition (CHGR) deals with unsegmented gesture sequences. The goal is to recognize gestures from untrimmed videos and generate the corresponding gesture sequence. CHGR is typically treated as a
weakly supervised task
, as the precise temporal boundaries of gestures are not provided; the model learns to identify them implicitly, often using Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss
Wei and Chen 2023
;
Graves et al. 2006
.

While CHGR has been explored in domains like autonomous driving
Fu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022b
, most research focuses on Sign Language Understanding (SLU), where it is commonly referred to as
Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR)
or sign2gloss. CSLR aims to produce a sequence of glosses
2
2

2

Glosses are atomic lexical units in sign language, analogous to words in spoken language.
from video input. Due to the importance of non-manual features (e.g., facial expressions and upper-body movements), many approaches also incorporate these regions to improve recognition accuracy. Some CSLR methods are further extended to support gloss-based sign language translation (SLT) by adding a gloss-to-text module
Guan et al. 2025
;
Zuo et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2022
.

In the following, we review recent advances in CHGR, grouped by input modality. We highlight key architectures, learning strategies, and benchmark results.

6.1
RGB-based

Most state-of-the-art RGB-based Continuous Hand Gesture Recognition (CHGR) models follow a
three-stage pipeline
architecture: (1) a spatial model (commonly a 2DCNN or a transformer) to extract frame-wise features; (2) a short-term temporal model (mainly 1DCNN or TCN) for local temporal modeling and gloss-level feature extraction; and (3) a contextual module (e.g., BiLSTM) for long-term sequence modeling and final gesture sequence prediction
Hu et al. 2022
;
Min et al. 2021
;
Hao et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2023c
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Hu et al. 2023a
;
Hu et al. 2023d
;
Hu et al. 2024a
;
Hu et al. 2024b
. This standard architecture was introduced in
Cui et al. 2019
. This decoupled spatial-temporal structure has been favored over unified spatiotemporal models like 3DCNNs due to the latter’s inability to define clear temporal boundaries
Adaloglou et al. 2022
.

Across the various methods that extend the initial three-stage pipeline, six broad enhancement strategies emerge: 1) Auxiliary Supervision & Multi‐Task Objectives, which enrich the standard three‐stage pipeline with extra losses and segmentation tasks; 2) Specialized Temporal Modeling, replacing or augmenting the 1D‐CNN with learned pooling, multi-scale fusion, or hierarchical boundary detectors; 3) Attention & Fusion Modules, inserting correlation, self‐emphasis, or transformer‐fusion blocks into the visual encoder; 4) Backbone Replacements & Hybrids, swapping 2D-CNNs for vision transformers or hybrid CNN–attention architectures; 5) Incorporating Linguistic Priors, aligning visual features with gloss embeddings via cross-modal decoders or contrastive losses; and 6) Data Scarcity & Multilingual Strategies, leveraging self-supervision, prototype memories, and automatic dictionary construction to overcome limited annotations and support multiple sign languages.

Many RGB‐based CHGR methods build on the canonical 2DCNN + 1DCNN + BiLSTM architecture by introducing auxiliary losses and multi‐task objectives. For example, VAC‐CSLR
Min et al. 2021
augments the visual and temporal modules with a Visual Enhancement (VE) loss and a Visual Alignment (VA) distillation loss to accelerate convergence and improve representation quality (see Fig.

Hao et al. 2021
adds two auxiliary classifiers—one after the 1DCNN and one after the BiLSTM—to perform synchronous gloss segmentation alongside recognition, using a three‐stage training schedule to mitigate gradient “spikes.” C

6.2
Pose-based

Pose-based (or skeleton-based) approaches for CHGR rely exclusively on body pose information, typically in the form of 2D/3D skeleton joints or pose heatmaps. These methods aim to model human motion while discarding appearance-based noise, making them attractive for real-time and privacy-preserving applications.

Several works exploit structured keypoint information by dividing the body into semantic regions such as the face, hands, and upper body. MSKA-SLR
Guan et al. 2025
processes each region independently via attention-enhanced temporal convolutions across four branches. The resulting features are fused to decode gloss sequences using a CTC loss, complemented by frame-level self-distillation. Similarly, CoSign
Jiao et al. 2023
employs group-specific GCNs for each subset and introduces regularization to preserve inter-stream consistency. Their dual-stream variant integrates joint positions and motion features using multi-level fusion to capture both static and dynamic cues.

Temporal localization of gestures has also been explored in the literature. TY-Net
Dozdor et al. 2023
draws inspiration from YOLO to detect gesture instances in time by predicting their boundaries and centers, analogous to spatial bounding boxes.

Beyond joint coordinates, pose heatmaps serve as an alternative input modality. The multi-cue framework in
Zhou et al. 2022b
combines spatial and temporal reasoning by localizing facial and hand regions via a pose estimator and subsequently encoding them with a temporal module, before decoding gloss sequences with a CTC head.
The FA-STGCN model
Fu et al. 2023
further improves robustness to motion scale and frequency variability. It normalizes skeleton inputs and applies alternating spatial and temporal GCNs. Spatial modules learn latent joint relationships, while temporal processing is split into global and local paths—extracting coarse-grained and fine-grained motion patterns, respectively.

Finally, in the context of autonomous driving, Wang et al.
Wang et al. 2022b
developed a two-stage pose-based system to classify road intersection gestures. The first stage extracts geometric features and encodes them via LSTM layers. The second stage uses a dual-stream setup, where one branch processes geometric descriptors and the other uses a ConvLSTM to handle co-occurrence matrices. Features are fused with directional orientation indicators for final classification.

6.3
Multimodal

Multimodal approaches for CHGR combine multiple input types—such as RGB frames, pose heatmaps, depth maps, and optical flow—to mitigate the limitations of unimodal methods and capture complementary information from different data sources.

A prominent example is TwoStream-SLR
Chen et al. 2022
, a dual-stream architecture designed for both signed language translation (SLT) and CSLR. It processes RGB and pose heatmaps in parallel using the first four blocks of S3D in each branch, with bidirectional lateral connections enabling multi-level information exchange. Additional supervision is provided through Sign Pyramid Networks and auxiliary prediction heads (video, keypoint, and joint). The model is trained using self-distillation, minimizing KL-divergence between the outputs of the three heads. An illustration of the architecture is shown in Fig.
16
. This model has also been applied to ISLR
Zuo et al. 2024
, using a sliding window mechanism and leveraging the same framework to extract sign boundaries and construct training dictionaries.

Other CSLR models have adapted multimodal ISLR backbones. Uni-Sign

Beyond sign language, general-purpose CHGR systems have explored multimodality across broader visual sensor inputs. For example,
Lu et al. 2024b
proposes a framework that segments RGB, depth, infrared, and optical flow inputs using a shared U-Net and processes them via separate 3D DenseNet-43 backbones. Extracted features are fused through Canonical Correlation Analysis to align shared representations across modalities.

7
Datasets

The performance and generalizability of hand gesture and sign language recognition (HGR/SLR) models are heavily influenced by the quality, diversity, and scale of the datasets used during training and evaluation. Over the years, a wide variety of datasets have been developed to support different HGR tasks, ranging from static and isolated gestures to continuous sign language recognition in real-world settings. These datasets vary widely in terms of the number of gestures or glosses, the types of modalities captured (e.g., RGB, depth, pose, IR), the presence of signer diversity, and the complexity of the sequences. In the following subsections, we categorize and review the most prominent datasets according to the specific recognition task they target: (i) Isolated and Continuous Sign Language Recognition, (ii) Semaphoric Gestures, and (iii) Other Gesture Recognition Tasks. This organization aims to provide a clear overview of the resources available for each task type and facilitate their appropriate use in future research.

7.1
Isolated and Continuous SLR

The datasets that have been developed to support video-based Sign Language Recognition (SLR) can be broadly categorized into those designed for Isolated Dynamic SLR (IDSLR) and Continuous SLR (CSLR). The former datasets typically focus on individual signs, recorded in controlled lab environments with a fixed vocabulary, making them well-suited for classification-based models. In contrast, continuous datasets present real-world sequences of signs, often extracted from broadcast material or spontaneous signing, requiring temporal segmentation and sequence modeling approaches. Additionally, some datasets support Sign Language Translation (SLT) by providing sentence-level gloss annotations and parallel spoken language transcripts.

The RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather 2014
Forster et al. 2014
and its extended version PHOENIX-2014-T1
Camgoz et al. 2018
are benchmark datasets offering sentence-level annotations in German Sign Language (DGS) captured from television weather forecasts. More recent efforts such as BOBSL
Albanie et al. 2021
and OpenASL
Shi et al. 2022
have significantly expanded the signer base and vocabulary, with hours of British and American Sign Language footage collected from TV and web sources, respectively. Lab-recorded corpora like KETI
Ko et al. 2019
, CSL-Daily
Zhou et al. 2021b
, and How2Sign
Duarte et al. 2021
incorporate multimodal inputs (e.g., pose, depth) and structured glosses, aiding both CSLR and SLT tasks. These datasets collectively enable the development and benchmarking of models under varying constraints of signer variability, language, modality, and task complexity.

On the other hand, isolated sign datasets such as MS-ASL
Joze and Koller 2019
, WLASL
Li et al. 2020
, and BSL-1K
Albanie et al. 2020
provide extensive vocabularies (up to 2,000 signs) and diverse signers, facilitating the study of vocabulary scalability and signer generalization. Many of these datasets, such as AUTSL
Sincan and Keles 2020
and BosphorusSign22k
Özdemir et al. 2020
, also include pose or depth modalities, enriching multimodal learning. While isolated datasets are primarily used for gesture classification, continuous datasets address the more complex problem of temporal segmentation, gloss prediction, and SLT. A few hybrid datasets like GSL SD/SI
Adaloglou et al. 2022
and Corpus FinSL
Salonen et al. 2020
provide data that can be used in both isolated and continuous settings. Together, these datasets serve a broad spectrum of research needs, from foundational gesture recognition to high-level translation across multiple sign languages.

7.2
Semaphoric HGR

Semaphoric hand gesture recognition (HGR) research is supported by a diverse range of datasets categorized into three main types based on task formulation: static, isolated dynamic, and continuous dynamic gesture recognition. These datasets vary in their application domains (e.g., Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), Human-Machine Interface (HMI), Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)), modalities (e.g., RGB, depth, pose, infrared), and in the number of gesture classes, subjects, and gesture samples they include. The statistics of datasets for semaphoric HGR are provided in tables
9
,
10
and
11
, regarding the task for which they are used (i.e. static, isolated dynamic and continuous HGR, respectively).

Static HGR datasets focus on recognizing gestures from single-frame input and are generally easier to process due to the absence of temporal complexity. Notable examples include LaRED
Hsiao et al. 2014
, one of the earliest RGBD datasets with 243,000 samples and 81 classes, and HaGRIDv2
Nuzhdin et al. 2024
, a large-scale RGB dataset designed for HMI with over 1 million samples, covering 34 classes. Smaller yet targeted datasets such as OUHANDS
Matilainen et al. 2016
and URGR
Bamani et al. 2024
focus on specific domains like HCI and HRI, while MD-UHGRD
Xu et al. 2024
offers moderately sized samples and class diversity.
Isolated dynamic HGR datasets contain gestures performed in isolation, with start and end points explicitly segmented. Datasets like ChaLearn IsoGD
Wan et al. 2016
and EgoGesture (isolated)
Zhang et al. 2018
feature rich modality (RGBD, IR, pose) and high gesture diversity, with up to 249 classes. Others like Jester
Materzynska et al. 2019
offer large-scale RGB-only data from over 1,300 subjects, making them suitable for data-driven deep learning approaches. More recent contributions such as DATE
Dang et al. 2024
and ZJUGesture
Xu et al. 2023
reflect growing interest in real-world HCI scenarios.
Continuous dynamic HGR datasets challenge models to identify and segment gestures from continuous video streams. The ChaLearn ConGD
Wan et al. 2016
dataset remains a benchmark with 22,535 sequences and 249 gesture classes. Similarly, EgoGesture
Zhang et al. 2018
and IPN Hand
Benitez-Garcia et al. 2021
include both isolated and continuous segments, enabling comprehensive model development and evaluation. Datasets like LD-ConGR
Liu et al. 2022
and ZJUGesture
Xu et al. 2023
contribute additional complexity by increasing subject diversity and contextual variability.

Each dataset supports specific sub-tasks within HGR research: static datasets aid in gesture classification, isolated dynamic datasets support temporal gesture modeling and classification, and continuous datasets allow for joint gesture spotting and classification—crucial for real-time and interactive applications. This diversity in dataset structure, scale, and modality reflects the evolving demands and challenges of semaphoric gesture recognition across interactive domains.

7.3
Other

In addition to standard semaphoric gesture datasets, several specialized datasets have been introduced to support research on more specific gesture types. The First-Person Hand Action (FPHA) dataset
Garcia-Hernando et al. 2018
, published in 2018, contains 1,175 video sequences captured from a first-person perspective by six participants. It focuses on isolated manipulative hand gestures across 45 action classes, offering RGB, depth, and skeleton data, and is particularly valuable for studies involving egocentric interaction.
Another notable example is the DP Dataset
Nakamura et al. 2023
, released in 2023, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the only publicly available benchmark specifically designed for pointing gesture recognition. Collected from 33 participants in two indoor environments, the dataset comprises approximately 2.8 million frames and 6,335 annotated gesture instances. It includes detailed annotations such as gesture timings and pointing directions, enabling both binary gesture classification (gesture vs. non-gesture) and directional estimation tasks.

8
Evaluation Metrics

In this section, we present the key evaluation metrics adopted in the literature for measuring the performance of hand gesture recognition (HGR) methods. Broadly, metrics fall into two main categories:
classification metrics
(used in static and isolated dynamic HGR) and
sequence-to-sequence metrics
(used in continuous dynamic HGR). Additionally, other indicators such as computational resource usage and overfitting-related metrics are also considered.

8.1
Classification Metrics

In multi-class HGR problems—especially when dealing with class imbalance—researchers often report both
micro-averaged
(per-instance) and
macro-averaged
(per-class) versions of common classification metrics.
Micro-averaged metrics aggregate contributions from all instances across classes, thus being sensitive to class imbalance. Macro-averaged metrics compute the metric independently for each class and then average, treating all classes equally regardless of their frequency.

Micro-averaged metrics:

\text{Accuracy}_{micro}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(TP_{i}+TN_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(TP_{i}+TN_{i}+FP_{i}+FN_{i})}

(1)

\text{Precision}_{micro}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}TP_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(TP_{i}+FP_{i})}

(2)

\text{Recall}_{micro}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}TP_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(TP_{i}+FN_{i})}

(3)

\text{Jaccard-Index}_{micro}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}TP_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(TP_{i}+FP_{i}+FN_{i})}

(4)

Macro-averaged metrics:

\text{Accuracy}_{macro}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{TP_{i}+TN_{i}}{TP_{i}+FP_{i}+TN_{i}+FN_{i}}

(5)

\text{Precision}_{macro}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{TP_{i}}{TP_{i}+FP_{i}}

(6)

\text{Recall}_{macro}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{TP_{i}}{TP_{i}+FN_{i}}

(7)

\text{Jaccard-Index}_{macro}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{TP_{i}}{TP_{i}+FP_{i}+FN_{i}}

(8)

F1-Score:
For both micro and macro cases, the F1-score is defined as the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall:

F_{1}=2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(9)

Here,
TP_{i}
,
FP_{i}
,
TN_{i}
, and
FN_{i}
denote the number of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives for the
i
-th class, respectively, and
N
is the total number of classes.

8.2
Sequence-to-Sequence Metrics

For continuous HGR (CHGR), particularly in Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR), sequence-level metrics are used.

Word Error Rate (WER)

Min et al. 2021
;
Adaloglou et al. 2022
is the standard metric, measuring the minimum number of substitutions (
S
), deletions (
D
), and insertions (
I
) needed to transform the predicted sequence into the ground truth sequence of length
N
:

WER=\frac{S+D+I}{N}

(10)

Edit Distance Accuracy

Fu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022b
is a complementary metric representing the ratio of correctly predicted elements in the sequence:

\text{Edit-Accuracy}=\frac{N-S-D-I}{N}

(11)

These metrics provide insight not only into classification accuracy but also into temporal and sequential consistency.

8.3
Computational Resource Metrics

In addition to recognition performance, computational efficiency is often evaluated, especially for real-time or embedded HGR applications. Common metrics include:

•

Inference Time:
Time (in ms or seconds) taken to process one input sample.

•

Frames Per Second (FPS):
Number of frames processed per second; higher values indicate faster processing.

•

Model Size:
The number of trainable parameters or total memory footprint of the model.

These metrics are crucial when deploying HGR systems on edge devices or in latency-sensitive environments.

8.4
Overfitting Indicators

To assess generalization and detect overfitting, the following indicators are used:

•

Train-Test Performance Gap:
A significant difference between training and testing accuracy (or loss) often signals overfitting.

•

Validation Loss Trend:
Increasing validation loss while training loss decreases is a common overfitting pattern.

•

Early Stopping Criteria:
Monitored via a plateau or increase in validation loss across epochs.

Current Challenges and Future Research Directions

As mentioned in the Methods sections (Sections
4
,
5
, and
6
), researchers have developed sophisticated techniques and large-scale datasets to achieve satisfactory performance. However, as shown in the relevant tables (Tables
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
), recognition accuracy remains low in many cases—particularly in more complex tasks such as Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR). In this section, we discuss the current challenges and limitations that hinder efficient Human Gesture Recognition (HGR), posing ongoing concerns for the research community and shaping future research directions. Additionally, we review the techniques proposed to address these issues, offering insights that may guide future efforts to overcome the identified challenges.

9.1
Gesture-Irrelevant Factors

The presence of gesture-irrelevant factors (or redundant information) hinders accurate recognition in diverse real-world scenarios. Some of these factors include the following
Tan et al. 2021
;
Alam et al. 2022
;
Jafari and Basu 2023
;
Bamani et al. 2024
;
Raj and Malu 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2021a
:

•

Environmental factors:
Cluttered background, varying illumination conditions, and self-occlusions make it difficult or even impossible to automatically spot the hand region, which is an important step for efficient HGR;

•

Subject characteristics:
Subject independence –—i.e., the ability to correctly identify a gesture regardless of the signer’s characteristics such as skin color, hand shape variations, or clothing—is a key prerequisite for achieving sufficient generalization and enabling real-world deployment.

•

Variations in perception device position:
The distance between the performer and the camera angle may reduce the recognition performance;

•

Input data quality:
Low resolution and blurriness may corrupt the discriminative characteristics of the hand gesture region.

Aiming to address these challenges, researchers have proposed segmentation models to crop hand regions and remove background interference
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Dang et al. 2023
;
Sahoo et al. 2023b
, object detectors for spatial normalization
Bamani et al. 2024
;
Alam et al. 2022
, super-resolution techniques to enhance image quality
Bamani et al. 2024
, attention mechanisms
Zhou et al. 2021a
;
Zhou et al. 2022a
to enforce the visual encoder to pay attention on the most informative regions, information-theoretic losses
Li et al. 2023
, and feature selection techniques
Tang et al. 2021b
to reduce information redundancy. Many of these approaches rely on computationally expensive off-the-shelf models, which increase the complexity of the overall HGR methods and set an upper bound to their accuracy. Thus, the presence of gesture-irrelevant factors could concern future research work, while potentially being combined with eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) approaches
Rodis et al. 2024
to facilitate this task.

9.2
Data Scarcity

Many existing datasets either contain a relatively small number of samples or lack sufficient diversity. For example, among static HGR datasets, only the versions of HaGRID
Kapitanov et al. 2024
;
Nuzhdin et al. 2024
have been collected from more than one hundred participants, despite some others having a plausible number of samples
Hsiao et al. 2014
;
Bamani et al. 2024
. A similar situation is observed in IDHGR datasets, where only the Jester dataset
Materzynska et al. 2019
includes data from over one hundred subjects. Furthermore, the amount of samples for specific modalities, such as IR data, is limited compared with the size of RGB-based datasets, an issue known as data imbalance
Wang et al. 2024
. The lack of large-scale, diverse datasets leads to overfitting and limited generalization, which is closely related to the challenges discussed earlier. This issue is further exacerbated in tasks that involve costly annotation processes and require data-intensive deep learning models with a large number of parameters, such as CSLR.

Several techniques have been explored to mitigate this challenge:

•

Multilingual Data Aggregation:
Combining datasets (e.g., CSL and DGS) to exploit cross-lingual visual similarities increases the overall amount of training data
Wei and Chen 2023
.

•

In the context of the self-supervised learning setting

•

Advanced Data Augmentation:

.

•

Few-/Zero-shot Learning
: Approaches using only a small number of samples to correctly recognize gestures—often based on CLIP—have also been proposed

;

;

;
Rastgoo et al. 2023
;

.

These approaches have led to improved recognition performance, even when trained on limited data. However, accuracy remains suboptimal, making data scarcity a persistent research challenge and a key direction for future work in the field.

9.3
Optimization Difficulties

Training the visual encoder effectively remains difficult, especially in deep architectures for CSLR. As shown in CTCA

(Fig.

CTC spike phenomenon
—where the model over-focuses on a few keyframes—can cause rapid overfitting and limit encoder training

;

As a result, optimization remains a significant challenge —one that may be further exacerbated by the continued development of even deeper models.

9.4
Limitations of Architectures

Despite the achievements of DL, the existing architectures suffer from the following limitations:

•

Block-fixed models:
The existing architectures are based on hand-crafted design, which may not be optimal
Zhou et al. 2021a
.

•

GCNs with fixed topology graphs:
Standard GCNs use fixed topology graphs based on hand anatomy, which may not capture optimal inter-joint relationships.

•

Ineffective multimodal fusion:
Most of the current works utilize late fusion, that does not fully leverage the available information
Hu et al. 2021a
;
Hu et al. 2023b
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2024c
;
Zhao et al. 2024b
;
Lin et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2021b
;
Jiang et al. 2021a
.

To address these limitations, researchers have proposed various strategies, including Neural Architecture Search (NAS) to identify optimal model structures
Zhou et al. 2021a
, learnable or adaptive skeleton adjacency matrices

;
Cui et al. 2025
;

;
Liu et al. 2024a
, and multi-level fusion for dual-stream approaches
Hampiholi et al. 2023
. Despite these advances, several aspects of deep learning architectures remain underexplored, leaving a substantial research gap.

10
Conclusions

The undeniable importance of vision-based hand gesture recognition (HGR) stems from its wide range of applications—spanning human-robot interaction to sign language understanding. This broad applicability has made it a vibrant and active research field. Recent advances in deep learning have played a pivotal role in driving progress, enabling the development of increasingly sophisticated architectures and training strategies aimed at tackling more complex and fine-grained tasks, such as continuous sign language recognition.

This survey focused on HGR methods that leverage visual input and deep learning. These methods were highlighted not only for their superior performance compared to traditional hand-crafted approaches, but also for their higher potential for deployment in real-world scenarios, unlike more cumbersome sensor-based techniques.
From the surveyed literature, it becomes clear that even relatively simple and shallow deep learning models can achieve high accuracy in static HGR tasks. In such cases, the primary challenges are typical of general computer vision problems and are not inherently tied to HGR itself.

Despite the significant progress achieved in vision-based hand gesture recognition, several open challenges remain that shape future research directions. A major concern is the generalization of models across diverse environments, lighting conditions, camera viewpoints, and user-specific variations, which often lead to performance drops in real-world settings. The lack of large-scale, diverse, and well-annotated datasets, especially for continuous gestures and sign language, also hinders the development of more robust models. Furthermore, model efficiency and scalability are critical for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices, necessitating advances in lightweight architectures and model compression techniques. The integration of multimodal data, such as combining vision with audio or inertial signals, remains underexplored yet promising. Finally, interpretability and trustworthiness of deep learning models are essential, particularly for safety-critical applications like healthcare or assistive technologies. Addressing these challenges will not only enhance the practical applicability of HGR systems but also contribute to more inclusive and accessible human-computer interaction paradigms.
