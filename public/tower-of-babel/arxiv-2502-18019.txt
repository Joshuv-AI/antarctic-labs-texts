An unconditional lower bound for the active-set method on the hypercube
Yann Disser, Nils Mosis

Abstract
The existence of a polynomial-time pivot rule for the simplex method is a fundamental open question in optimization. While many super-polynomial lower bounds exist for individual or very restricted classes of pivot rules, there currently is little hope for an unconditional lower bound that addresses all pivot rules. We approach this question by considering the active-set method as a natural generalization of the simplex method to non-linear objectives. This generalization allows us to prove the first unconditional lower bound for all pivot rules . More precisely, we construct a multivariate polynomial of degree linear in the number of dimensions such that the active-set method started in the origin visits all vertices of the hypercube. We hope that our framework serves as a starting point for a new angle of approach to understanding the complexity of the simplex method.

Introduction

The simplex method
[
13
]
is widely regarded as one of the most natural and important practical algorithms for solving linear programming problems.
However, its theoretical complexity remains an open question to this day.
This has to do with the fact that the simplex method’s behavior is highly sensitive to the choice of pivot rule, which determines how ties between improving edges at a vertex are broken and significantly influences the method’s behavior, such as whether it guarantees termination.

The discovery of a polynomial-time pivot rule for the simplex method would have profound implications.
In particular, it would most likely provide a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, thereby resolving the ninth open problem on Smale’s list of mathematical problems for the 21st century
[
35
]
.
While weakly polynomial algorithms for linear optimization are known, namely the ellipsoid and the interior-point method
[
38
,
27
]
, and while the latter has recently been adapted to a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programs with at most two variables per constraint
[
2
,
11
]
, barrier methods cannot yield strongly polynomial guarantees
[
3
]
.
For linear programs with zero-one vertices strongly polynomial simplex pivot rules are known
[
8
]
.
A general polynomial-time pivot rule for the simplex method would also imply a polynomial bound on the combinatorial diameter of polytopes, thus resolving the long-standing polynomial Hirsch conjecture
[
12
,
33
]
.

Over the years, super-polynomial lower running time bounds were established for various natural pivot rules.
Bounds for straightforward rules were found early-on by utilizing distorted hypercubes
[
5
,
31
,
29
,
26
,
24
,

]
.
More recently, linear programs derived from Markov Decision Processes have been used to handle more sophisticated, memory-based rules
[

20
,
21
,
22
]
.
In some cases, the exponential worst-case behavior of the simplex method could be attributed to its mightiness
[

18
,
1

, which can be seen as a conditional lower bound.
Nevertheless, results from smoothed complexity
[

,

,

,

suggest that the constructions underlying the above bounds are fragile, which indicates that fundamentally different approaches may be necessary to achieve an unconditional lower bound that applies universally across all pivot rules.
While existing hypercube constructions were generalized to small classes of pivot rules
[
4
,
16
]
, the existence of an unconditional super-polynomial lower bound remains wide open.

We approach this question by considering the active-set method
[
19
,
32
]
as a natural generalization of the simplex method to non-linear programs.
A case of particular importance for the active-set method are quadratic, concave programs for which, much like in the linear setting, only weakly polynomial algorithms are known
[
38
,
39
]
, and the active-set method is a promising candidate for the first strongly polynomial algorithm.
Similarly to the simplex method, the active-set method is governed by a pivot rule that determines the improving direction in each step in case there is more than one option.
Again, no general running time bounds for all pivot rules are known.

Our results.

We prove the first unconditional super-polynomial lower bounds on the running time of the active-set method
for all pivot rules
.
More precisely, we show the following.

Theorem 1.1

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>2}
, there is a multivariate polynomial
\mathcal{F}_{n}
of degree
n
such that the active-set method started in
\boldsymbol{0}
needs
2^{n}-1
iterations to optimize
\mathcal{F}_{n}
over the
n
-dimensional hypercube
[0,1]^{n}
, irrespective of the pivot rule.

In particular, our construction forces the active-set method to visit all vertices of the hypercube while traversing along its edges in a simplex-like fashion.

This result yields a new method of approach for unconditional lower bounds on the running time of the simplex method:
If we could lower the degree of the polynomial to
1
by considering more general polytopes, we would obtain a super-polynomial (even exponential) lower bound for the simplex method using any pivot rule.

As a first step in this direction, we can lower the degree of the polynomial, while maintaining a super-polynomial bound, simply by neglecting most of the input dimensions.

Corollary 1

For every

d(n)\leq n

\boldsymbol{0}

\mathcal{F}_{n}
over the
n

[0,1]^{n}

Importantly, we provide a single construction that addresses all pivot rules simultaneously (there is a unique improving direction in each step).
This means that lowering the degree of the polynomial for general polytopes would constitute progress towards disproving the monotone polynomial Hirsch conjecture
[
40
]
.

We observe that it is
NP

5

This already implies a super-polynomial lower bound on the running time of the active-set method, assuming
\textup{{P}}\neq\textup{{NP}}

The value of our contribution lies in the fact that we provide an

In fact, it turns out that the polynomials of Theorem

The aim of this section is to illustrate that the active-set method is a natural generalization of the simplex method.

Both algorithms are formulated in a way that makes their connection evident.
We now describe the behavior of both algorithms in more detail.

The simplex method
solves arbitrary linear programs of the form

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}

(LP)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle A\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b},

where
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
.
For simplicity, we assume that the feasible region
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}\colon A\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}\}
is bounded, and that the constraints are non-degenerate, i.e., we assume that every vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
of
\mathcal{P}
satisfies
|\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})|=n
, where we write
\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\{i\colon\boldsymbol{A_{i\cdot}}\boldsymbol{x}=b_{i}\}
.
The simplex method can be understood combinatorially as a traversal of
\mathcal{P}
along its edges.

More precisely, it maintains a subset
\mathcal{B}
, called a
basis
, of
n
constraint indices such that the system
A_{\mathcal{B}\cdot}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathcal{B}}
uniquely determines the current vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
.
By non-degeneracy, this is equivalent to
\mathcal{B}=\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})
.
As long as
\boldsymbol{x}
is not already an optimum, the simplex method moves along edges of
\mathcal{P}
to neighboring vertices with better objective function values.
We now describe how our formulation of
Simplex
reflects this behavior.

A vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
of
\mathcal{P}
is not optimal if and only if there is a direction
\boldsymbol{d}
that is feasible and improving, i.e., that satisfies
A_{\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}\leq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}>0
.
In particular, there is at least one improving edge direction

An edge direction in vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
is a vector of the form
\boldsymbol{d}\coloneqq\lambda(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})
where
\boldsymbol{y}
is a neighboring vertex of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\lambda>0
.
in each vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
that is not optimal.
Due to non-degeneracy, every edge direction
\boldsymbol{d}
corresponds to a unique index
\ell\in\mathcal{B}
with
\boldsymbol{A_{\ell\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}<0
and
{A_{\mathcal{B}\setminus\{\ell\}\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
In fact, a feasible direction
\boldsymbol{d}
is an edge direction in
\boldsymbol{x}
if and only if

|\{i\in\mathcal{B}\colon\boldsymbol{A_{i\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}=0\}|=n-1=|\mathcal{B}|-1.

Now, if the current vertex
\boldsymbol{x}
is not optimal,
Simplex
chooses an improving edge direction
\boldsymbol{d}
, deletes the corresponding index
\ell
from
\mathcal{B}
, and moves along
\boldsymbol{d}
as far as possible without becoming infeasible.
Note that, by boundedness of
\mathcal{P}
, this transitions to another vertex
\boldsymbol{y}
of
\mathcal{P}
.
The method then adds a new active constraint index
j\in\Eq(\boldsymbol{y})
to
\mathcal{B}
to obtain a basis uniquely identifying the new vertex.
By non-degeneracy, this index is uniquely determined by
j\in\Eq(\boldsymbol{y})\setminus\mathcal{B}
.

The number of iterations of the simplex method depends on the
pivot rule
that determines the improving edge direction
\boldsymbol{d}
chosen in each step in case there is more than one.
The question of whether or not there exists a pivot rule guaranteeing a polynomial running time of the simplex method is arguably one of the most famous open problems in (linear) optimization.
We refer to
[
12
]
for more details on the simplex method.

The active-set method
can be applied to non-linear programs with linear constraints of the form

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})

(NLP)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle A\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b},

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continously differentiable,
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
.
As before, we denote the feasible region by
\mathcal{P}
, and the set of indices of the constraints that are active in some
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}
by
\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})
.
For simplicity, we again assume that
\mathcal{P}
is bounded.

If
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}
is a local optimum of (
NLP
), then there are no feasible improving directions in
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}
.
That is, for all
\boldsymbol{d}
with
{A_{\Eq(\boldsymbol{x^{*}})\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}\leq\boldsymbol{0}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\nabla\!f(x^{*})}\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}\leq 0
.
The active-set method aims to find a
critical point
, i.e., a point satisfying this necessary optimality condition, by determining the set of constraints that are active in such a point.

More precisely, the active-set method maintains a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})
, called
active set
, of constraints that are active in the current solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}
.
As long as
\boldsymbol{x}
is not already a critical point, the method tries to move along an improving feasible direction
\boldsymbol{d}
that respects the active set, i.e., that satisfies
A_{\mathcal{A}\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
If there is no such direction, the method removes as few indices as possible from
\mathcal{A}
until such a direction
\boldsymbol{d}
can be found.
Now it moves as far as possible along
\boldsymbol{d}
without becoming infeasible, or until reaching a point
\boldsymbol{y}
with
\boldsymbol{\nabla\!f(\boldsymbol{y})}\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}\leq 0
.
If this movement is stopped by hitting the boundary of
\mathcal{P}
, the method adds a new active constraint to
\mathcal{A}
.
This procedure is iterated until a critical point for (
NLP
) is found.
We now describe how our formulation of
ActiveSet
allows this behavior.

A feasible point
\boldsymbol{x}
is
not critical
if and only if there is a feasible improving direction
\boldsymbol{d}
, i.e., a direction
\boldsymbol{d}
with
A_{\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}\leq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\nabla\!f(\boldsymbol{x})}\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}>0
.
In
ActiveSet
, we take such a direction
\boldsymbol{d}
which also maximizes the number of indices
j\in\mathcal{A}
with
\boldsymbol{A_{j\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}=0
, and can then remove indices
i
from
\mathcal{A}
with
\boldsymbol{A_{i\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}<0
(in separate iterations).
This corresponds to deleting a smallest set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
from
\mathcal{A}
that ensures the existence of a feasible improving direction
\boldsymbol{d}
with
{A_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{I}\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Now the method moves along the improving direction
\boldsymbol{d}
as far as possible without becoming infeasible or reaching a point where the derivative of the objective in direction
\boldsymbol{d}
is non-positive.
If the directional derivative is still positive in the new point, the movement was stopped by hitting the boundary of
\mathcal{P}
, so the method adds a new active constraint to
\mathcal{A}
.

Implementations of the active-set method are usually formulated for strictly concave, quadratic objective functions (see e.g.
[
19
,
32
]
), where they compute Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) points
[
28
,
30
]
of equality constrained subproblems until reaching a KKT point, that is, the unique global optimum solution, of (
NLP
).
More precisely, given a feasible solution
\boldsymbol{x}
of (
NLP
) and an active set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{i\colon\boldsymbol{A_{i\cdot}}\boldsymbol{x}=b_{i}\}
of constraint indices, the method computes a KKT point
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}
of

\begin{array}[]{rrclcl}\displaystyle\max&\lx@intercol f(\boldsymbol{y})\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\textrm{s.t.}&A_{\mathcal{A}\cdot}\boldsymbol{y}&=&\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathcal{A}}.&&\end{array}

Then, it moves from
\boldsymbol{x}
towards
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}
as far as possible without becoming infeasible.
If it reaches
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}
, the method removes a constraint from the active set to enable further progress.
Otherwise, it adds a new constraint to the active set.

Note that our formulation of
ActiveSet
allows this behavior for strictly concave, quadratic functions.
If the current solution
\boldsymbol{x}
is not already the KKT point of (
2
), the direction
\boldsymbol{d}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{x^{*}}-\boldsymbol{x}
is improving (by concavity of the objective) and satisfies
A_{\mathcal{A}\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
, i.e., maximizes
|\{i\in\mathcal{A}\colon\boldsymbol{A_{i\cdot}}\boldsymbol{d}=0\}|
.
Further, we have
\boldsymbol{x^{*}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\bar{\mu}\boldsymbol{d}
for

\bar{\mu}\coloneqq\inf\{\mu\geq 0\colon\boldsymbol{\nabla\!f(x}+\mu\boldsymbol{d)}\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}\leq 0\}.

If
\boldsymbol{x}
is the KKT point of (
2
), then there is no feasible improving direction
\boldsymbol{d}
with
A_{\mathcal{A}\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
, so
ActiveSet
removes constraints from
\mathcal{A}
preventing progress.

While concave quadratic functions allow for an efficient implementation, the computations in an iteration of
ActiveSet
are non-trivial for general functions.
In the following, we assume that
ActiveSet
has access to an oracle, which finds
\boldsymbol{d}
and computes
\mu
in each step.

The running time of the algorithm, i.e., the number of iterations, highly depends on the
pivot rule
that determines the direction
\boldsymbol{d}
and the indices
i\in\mathcal{A}
and
j\in\Eq(\boldsymbol{x})\setminus\mathcal{A}
in each iteration in case there is more than one choice.
It is an open problem whether there is a polynomial time pivot rule for the active-set method for (strictly) concave, quadratic objective functions.

3.3
Visiting all vertices of the hypercube

We will see that

Proposition 4

Let

The sequence

\boldsymbol{x^{i+1}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{x^{i}}+(1-2(x^{i})_{k_{i}})\boldsymbol{e^{k_{i}}},\quad\forall i\in[2^{n}-1],

(5)

Proof

By Lemma

2
, the choice of

\boldsymbol{x^{i}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{e^{n}}\}

\s(k_{i})\equiv_{2}(x^{i})_{k_{i}}

Consider the sequence

3.4
Proofs of the super-polynomial bounds

We define a matrix
C\in\{-1,0,1\}^{2n\times n}
by
\boldsymbol{C_{i\cdot}}=(\boldsymbol{e^{i}})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{C_{(i+n)\cdot}}=-(\boldsymbol{e^{i}})^{\top}
for all
i\in[n]
, and a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
by
c_{i}=1
and
c_{i+n}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.
So we have

[0,1]^{n}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\colon C\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{c}\}.

Fixing this implementation of the hypercube, we can now state our main result formally.
For the proof, we combine the insights of the previous subsections.

Theorem
1.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\textsc{ActiveSet}(\mathcal{F}_{n},C,\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{0})
solves

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})

s.t.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{n}

in
2^{n}-1
iterations, irrespective of the pivot rule.

Proof

We show that
ActiveSet
visits all vertices of the hypercube
[0,1]^{n}
.
Let
(\boldsymbol{x^{i}})_{i\in[2^{n}]}
denote the sequence of Proposition
4
.
The initial solution is given by
\boldsymbol{0}=\boldsymbol{x^{1}}
.
Now fix some
i\in[2^{n}-1]
and assume that
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}
is the intermediate solution at the start of iteration
i
of
\textsc{ActiveSet}(\mathcal{F}_{n},C,\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{0})
and that the current active set is
\mathcal{A}=\Eq(\boldsymbol{x^{i}})\coloneqq\{j\colon\boldsymbol{C_{j\cdot}}\boldsymbol{x^{i}}=c_{j}\}
.
Note that, according to Proposition
4
, we have
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}\neq\boldsymbol{e^{n}}
so we can fix
k\in[n]
as the unique dimension of Proposition
2
in the point
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}
.

All edge directions in
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}
are of the form

\boldsymbol{d^{j,\lambda}}\coloneqq\lambda(1-2(x^{i})_{j})\boldsymbol{e^{j}}

for some

and some
\lambda>0
.
Note that
\partial_{j}\mathcal{F}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\nabla\!\mathcal{F}_{n}}\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{e^{j}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla\!\mathcal{F}_{n}}\!^{\top}(-\boldsymbol{e^{j}})
for all

, so we have
\boldsymbol{\nabla\!\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x^{i}})\!^{\top}\boldsymbol{d^{j,\lambda}}>0
if and only if

and
(x^{i})_{j}=0

\partial_{j}\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x^{i}})<0

(x^{i})_{j}=1

Hence, by Proposition

\boldsymbol{x^{i}}
are given by
\boldsymbol{d^{k,\lambda}}=\lambda(1-2(x^{i})_{k})\boldsymbol{e^{k}}
for

.

Since the system

is non-degenerate and
\mathcal{A}=\Eq(\boldsymbol{x^{i}})

\boldsymbol{d}
in the vertex
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}

In particular, for every edge direction

in
\boldsymbol{x^{i}}

A_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\ell\}\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}

This yields that

3.5
The complexity of maximizing over the hypercube

The lower bounds in Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1
are unconditional, meaning they show that
ActiveSet
is inefficient on the hypercube even if
\textup{{P}}=\textup{{NP}}
.
It is folklore that the underlying decision problem is already
NP
-hard for polynomials of degree
3
.
We include a proof to make our presentation self-contained.

Proposition 5

Given a multivariate polynomial
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
3
, it is
NP
-complete to decide whether there is
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
f(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
.

Proof

We can evaluate polynomials of degree
3
efficiently, so the decision problem is in
NP
.
For hardness, we reduce from 3
SAT

[
9
]
.
Let an instance for 3
SAT
be given by clauses
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{j}\}_{j=1,\ldots,m}
over variables
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
.
Define the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(\boldsymbol{x})=-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\prod_{\{k\in[n]\colon z_{k}\in C_{j}\}}\prod_{\{\ell\in[n]\colon\neg{z_{\ell}}\in C_{j}\}}(1-x_{k})x_{\ell}.

Then, for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we have
f(\boldsymbol{x})=0
if and only if
\sigma\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\{0,1\}
given by
\sigma(z_{i})\coloneqq x_{i}
is an assignment satisfying the 3
SAT
instance.
Since
f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{n}
, we obtain
NP
-hardness of the decision problem.
∎

While the underlying problem is hard, the next statements show that our polynomial
\mathcal{F}_{n}
belongs to a class of functions for which linear time algorithms exist that find the optimum vertex of the hypercube.
We need some definitions before we can state this formally.

Every function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
attaining unique values on the vertices of
[0,1]^{n}
induces an orientation
\sigma_{f}
of the edges of the hypercube if we direct every edge
\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\}
from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{y}
if
f(\boldsymbol{x})<f(\boldsymbol{y})
, and from
\boldsymbol{y}
to
\boldsymbol{x}
if
f(\boldsymbol{y})<f(\boldsymbol{x})
.
We write
\boldsymbol{x}\to_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{y}
if edge
\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\}
is directed from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{y}
in
\sigma
.

An orientation
\sigma
of the edges of the hypercube is called
unique sink orientation
(USO) if there is exactly one
sink
, i.e., a vertex without outgoing edges, in every face of the hypercube.
For more details on USOs, see e.g.
[
23
,
37
]
.

We say that an orientation
\sigma
is
combed
if there is a dimension
i\in[n]
such that all edges along dimension
i
point in the same direction, i.e., if we have

\boldsymbol{x}\to_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{x}+(1-2x_{i})\boldsymbol{e^{i}}

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
x_{i}=\delta
, where
\delta\in\{0,1\}
is fixed.
An orientation is called
decomposable
if it is combed on every nonzero-dimensional subcube of
[0,1]^{n}
.

Proposition 6

The function
\mathcal{F}_{n}
induces a decomposable orientation of
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Proof

Let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
with
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
denote the support of some subcube
\mathcal{S}
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, i.e., the subcube
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
is
k
-dimensional and, for all
i\in[n]\setminus I
, there is
\delta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
such that
x_{i}=\delta_{i}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}
.
We will show that
\mathcal{S}
is combed in dimension
i_{k}
with respect to the orientation
\sigma
induced by
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}\colon x_{i_{k}}=0\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}\colon x_{i_{k}}=1\}
.

Since
\beta_{i}=0
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
for all
i\in[n]
, we have
\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{i-1}\alpha_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Fix some arbitrary
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
Then,
x_{j}=y_{j}
for all
j>i_{k}
and thus
\alpha_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=\alpha_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
i>i_{k}
.
Therefore, we have

\alpha_{i_{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\alpha_{i_{k}+1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\alpha_{i_{k}+1}(\boldsymbol{y})=1-\alpha_{i_{k}}(\boldsymbol{y}).

As
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1\}
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
for all
i\in[n+1]
and
\sum_{i=1}^{i_{k}-1}2^{i-1}<2^{i_{k}-1}
, this yields that we either have
\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})>\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
or
\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})<\mathcal{F}_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, i.e., the subcube
\mathcal{S}
is combed with respect to
\sigma
.
∎

Every decomposable orientation is also a USO, and can be solved in linear time
[
34
]
.
Hence, the polynomials of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1
do not induce
NP
-hard optimization problems (unless
\textup{{P}}=\textup{{NP}}
).

4
Future research

In this paper, we considered the active-set method as a natural generalization of the simplex method in order to derive the first unconditional running time bounds for all pivot rules.
A canonical next step towards a general bound for the simplex method would be to lower the degree of our polynomial.

Question 1

Is there a polynomial of degree
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
over the hypercube for which active-set takes super-polynomially many iterations for all pivot rules?

It might be possible to reduce the complexity of the objective function even more drastically by increasing the complexity of the feasible region.

Question 2

Is there a polynomial of constant degree over some polytope for which active-set takes super-polynomially many iterations for all pivot rules?

While a general bound for linear objectives, i.e., for the simplex method, would be a massive breakthrough, reaching quadratic and concave objectives would already be very interesting:
Much like in the linear setting, only weakly polynomial algorithms are known, namely ellipsoid and interior-point methods
[
38
,
39
]
, and the active-set method is a promising candidate for a strongly polynomial algorithm, due to its combinatorial nature.

Question 3

Is there a concave, quadratic polynomial over some polytope for which active-set takes super-polynomially many iterations for all pivot rules?
