Spin–Orbit Coupling in Helical Waveguides: A Local Duality Perspective and Emergent Gauge Fields Preprint: APS/123-QED
Lili Yang 1, Longlong Feng 1, Pengming Zhang 1

Abstract
Dual symmetry is an intrinsic property of Maxwell’s equations, corresponding to a global U(1) symmetry in vacuum, with helicity as the associated conserved quantity. In this paper, we investigate light propagation in a spin-degenerate medium using a field-theoretical approach and introduce an effective gauge field A s A_{s} that emerges from the localization of dual symmetry. Within the geometric optics approximation, we show that the helical trajectory of light rays reveals this gauge field as a manifestation of spin–orbit coupling. Although orbital–orbit coupling also arises in such systems, the spin–orbit interaction possesses deeper physical significance, as it originates from the intrinsic dual symmetry embedded in Maxwell’s equations.



Introduction

Symmetry is a cornerstone of modern physics. According to Noether’s theorem, every continuous symmetry in a system corresponds to a conserved quantity. In the case of the electromagnetic field, an important symmetry is dual symmetry, meaning that Maxwell’s equations remain unchanged under a dual transformation
Jackson and Fox 1999
.
In general, dual transformations can be expressed in a relativistic covariant form using four-vectors in the presence of magnetic charge
Zwanziger 1968
;
Han and Biedenharn 1971
;
Gambini and Salamó 1979
;
Singleton 1997
;
Kato and Singleton 2001
;
Arrayás and Trueba 2017
. This framework aligns with the concept of "electromagnetic democracy"
Berry 2002
;
Bliokh et al. 2013
, which treats electric and magnetic fields on equal footing. However, in the source-free vacuum, they can be conveniently expressed in three-vector form. Maxwell’s equations remain invariant under the transformation
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})\rightarrow(\mathbf{B},-\mathbf{E})
, which corresponds to a dual rotation by an angle
\theta=\pi/2
. As a result, the electromagnetic field exhibits a global
U(1)
symmetry, ensuring that helicity is conserved, as dictated by Noether’s theorem
Calkin 1965
.
Accordingly, a naturally arising question is, for generic media, whether the dual symmetry still holds. We will demonstrate that in a so-called spin-degenerate medium where the permittivity and permeability tensors are equal
\epsilon=\mu
, the dual symmetry is also applicable but in an optical-path dependent way, and thus helicity remains conserved
Fernandez-Corbaton et al. 2013
. Consequently, we extend the concept of local dual symmetry in a medium as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{D}\cos\theta({\bf x})+\mathbf{B}\sin\theta({\bf x})\\
&\mathbf{B}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}\cos\theta({\bf x})-\mathbf{D}\sin\theta({\bf x})\end{aligned}

(1)

where
\theta({\bf x})
is a function of coordinate
{\bf x}
. In the presence of a medium, the dual transformation should replace
\mathbf{E}
with the electric displacement field
\mathbf{D}
to maintain the source-free condition. Moreover, the corresponding rotation angle now depends on spatial coordinates, meaning that the original global
U(1)
symmetry may become a
local\,U(1)
symmetry.

Field-theoretic representation of the photon

This dual symmetry can be expressed in a form analogous to the phase transformation of a field. Accordingly, we define the photon wavefunction in the helicity basis as

\psi^{\pm}=\mathcal{D}_{\perp}\pm i\sigma_{3}\mathcal{B}_{\perp}.

(2)

Here,
\mathcal{D}_{\perp}=\{D_{+},D_{-}\}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\perp}=\{B_{+},B_{-}\}

denote the transverse components of the electromagnetic field vectors decomposed in the helicity basis with respect to a given direction, which can be aligned with the propagation direction of the incident light, e.g., without a loss of generality, taking the z-axis, satisfying
({\bf e}_{z}\cdot{\bf s}){\bf e}_{\pm}=\pm{\bf e}_{\pm}
where
{\bf s}=\{s_{i}\}^{jk}=-i\epsilon_{ijk}
is the adjoint representation of spin-1 matrices. The two-component wave functions
\psi^{\pm}
correspond to the photon and its time-reversed counterpart (antiphoton)
Keller 2005
. In contrast to conventional approaches that consider only positive-energy photon states and equate their polarization to spin degrees of freedom, our framework incorporates both positive and negative energy components. This enables the adoption of a four-component formalism that more closely parallels the spinor representation used in electron quantum theory. By introducing a four-component wave function,
\mathbf{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{+}\ \psi^{-}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
, Maxwell’s equations can be recast into an optical Dirac-like form when interactions with a medium are taken into account
Feng and Wu 2022
.

Since photons can also carry intrinsic orbital angular momentum (OAM), we consider a generalization of the photon wave function in the form of a twisted vortex field, expressed as
\psi e^{il\varphi}
, where
l
is the topological charge and
\varphi
is the azimuthal angle

. Under the local dual transformation (
1

\displaystyle\psi e^{il\varphi}\rightarrow e^{-i\hat{S}_{z}\theta(x)}\psi e^{il\varphi}

(3)

where the helicity operator
\hat{S}_{z}
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{3}&\\
&-\sigma_{3}\end{array}\right)

(4)

The wave function can be decomposed into two independent components:
\psi_{r}=(\psi_{1},\psi_{4})^{T}
(comprising the first and fourth components), and
\psi_{l}=(\psi_{3},\psi_{2})
(comprising the second and third components). These two components correspond to opposite helicity, as previously demonstrated in Ref.
Yang and Feng 2025
.

Since the wave function is defined in the transverse plane relative to the
z

\hat{S}_{z}
directly correspond to the photon’s helicity. Accordingly, the transformation in Eq. (
3

U(1)
subgroup of the spin group
SU(2)

e^{i\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}}

l

\psi_{\sigma}
transforms as

\psi_{\sigma}\rightarrow e^{-i\sigma\theta(s)}\psi_{\sigma}

(5)

where
\psi_{\sigma}
denotes the right- and left-handed helicity components,
\psi_{r}
and
\psi_{l}
, respectively.

In particular, the dual symmetry of the photonic system ensures that the Lagrangian remains invariant under dual transformations. The Lagrangian describing light propagation in a spin-degenerate medium, formulated in terms of the photon wave function, can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\psi^{\dagger}(i\gamma^{0}\partial_{0}-\hat{m}+\hat{L}^{z}_{0}\hat{S}_{z}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{\perp}\cdot\hat{p}_{\perp})\psi

(6)

where the Dirac representation of the
\gamma

\hat{L}^{z}_{0}
exhibits the characteristic features of orbital angular momentum and is defined as

\hat{L}^{z}_{0}=\frac{i}{2}(\hat{\bm{k}}_{\perp}\times\mathbf{q})\cdot\bm{e}_{z}

(7)

The effective mass operator takes the form

\displaystyle\hat{m}=

\displaystyle\hat{k}_{z}Q_{0}+\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\hat{k}_{z}}}\hat{k}_{+}q_{0}\hat{k}_{-}-\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\bm{k}}_{\perp}+\frac{i}{2}\bm{\nabla}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{q}

(8)

where
{\bf q}=\{Q_{+1},Q_{-1}\}
and
\hat{\mathbf{k}}=-i\bm{\nabla}
is the momentum operator; in the helicity space, the transverse components are
\hat{k}_{\perp}=(\hat{k}_{+},\hat{k}_{-})^{T}
with
\hat{k}=(\hat{k}_{x}\mp i\hat{k}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
. The components of the transverse momentum are expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{\pm}=\hat{k}_{z}Q_{\pm 2}-2\hat{k}_{\pm}Q_{\pm 1}+\frac{\hat{k}_{\pm}}{k_{z}}q_{0}\hat{k}_{\pm},\quad p_{z}=0

(9)

The multipole components in the above equations are defined through the inverse of the dielectric tensor
\epsilon^{-1}
as:

\epsilon^{-1}=\mu^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}Q_{0}&Q_{+2}&Q_{+1}\\
Q_{-2}&Q_{0}&Q_{-1}\\
Q_{-1}&Q_{+1}&q_{0}\end{array}\right)

(10)

in which,
Q_{0}
and
q_{0}
are two monopole components,
Q_{\pm 1}
and
Q_{\pm 2}
denote the dipole and quadrupole components, corresponding to the scalar (spin-0), vector (spin-1), and tensor (spin-2) modes respectively.

For the Lagrangian Eq.(
6

Feng and Wu 2022
;
Yang and Feng 2025

and the dielectric coefficient
Q_{i}

\mathcal{L}_{0}
, which is independent of spatial gradients in the medium, and a derivative term,
\mathcal{L}_{I}

Spin-orbit coupling in helical waveguide

To analyze light propagation in a helical waveguide, we consider the effective medium structure that emerges due to the waveguide geometry. In this setting, the effective parameters reduce to
Q_{\pm 2}=0
and the
Q_{0}=1.
The relevant components are given by

;

;

;
we

Q_{\pm 1}=\mp i\tau r_{\pm},\quad q_{0}=1+\tau^{2}r^{2}

(11)

where
r_{\pm}=(x\mp iy)/\sqrt{2}
,
\tau
is the torsion of the optical path. In the geometric approximation,
\tau
is a spatial slow variable and can be approximated to be a constant adiabatically, leading to
\hat{k}_{+}Q_{+1}=\hat{k}_{-}Q_{-1}=0
. Similarly, spatial derivatives of
q_{0}
can be neglected,
\hat{k}_{\pm}q_{0}\approx 0

;
we2

\mathcal{L}_{I}=\psi^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{z}\tau\psi=\sum_{\sigma}\psi^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\sigma\tau\psi_{\sigma}

(12)

which describes the interaction between the polarization of light and its extrinsic orbital characteristics. This term, known as the spin-orbit coupling

;
Bliokh et al. 2015
, arises from the medium-derivative contributions of the system.

Induced gauge field from local dual symmetry

According to the fundamental principles of gauge theory, the presence of local symmetry inherently implies the existence of corresponding gauge fields
Yang and Mills 1954
. The total Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
can be expressed as the sum of a free photon term
\mathcal{L}_{p}
, and the coupling between the photon and the gauge field
\mathbf{A}
term
\mathcal{L}_{A}
:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{p}+\mathcal{L}_{A}

(13)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
corresponds to a globally symmetric theory associated with duality invariance, and is identified with
\mathcal{L}_{0}
. The interaction term
\mathcal{L}_{A}
arises from the localization of this dual symmetry, with the inhomogeneous properties of the medium encoded in the transformation parameter
\theta(x)
. In this formulation, the coupling to the gauge field is equivalent to light propagation in anisotropic and inhomogeneous media, where spatial variations of the medium influence the dynamics and trajectory of electromagnetic waves.

The spin degeneracy condition implies that the system can be decomposed into two independently evolving subsystems upon redefinition of the wave function. In such media, the Lagrangian remains invariant under the local transformation defined in Eq. (
3
), owing to the anticommutation relationship between
\sigma_{3}
and
\sigma_{\perp}
.
In general, the infinitesimal transformation of the derivative term under a local transformation takes the form:

\delta(\partial_{i}\psi_{\sigma})=-i\sigma\partial_{i}(\theta(x)\psi_{\sigma})

(14)

where helicity
\sigma
represents the coupling constant between the photon and the medium. To ensure gauge invariance and compensate for this variation, a gauge field
Q_{i}A_{i}
is introduced, modifying the ordinary derivative into a covariant derivative such as
(Q_{i}\partial_{i})\rightarrow(Q_{i}D_{i})
, where
D_{i}=\partial_{i}+i\sigma A_{i}
and
A_{i}
are related to
\partial_{i}\ln\epsilon^{-1}
.

Rather than constructing the Lagrangian term by term, we focus on the regime where diffraction is negligible and field localization depends solely on the arc length
s
. The corresponding variation then simplifies to:

\delta(\partial_{s}\psi_{\sigma})=-i\sigma\partial_{s}(\theta(s)\psi_{\sigma}).

(15)

In a helical fiber system, the interaction term arising from the gauge field component
A_{s}
takes the form

\mathcal{L}_{A}=-\sum_{\sigma}\psi^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\sigma Q_{0}A_{s}\psi_{\sigma}

(16)

which differs from the standard quantum electrodynamics (QED) interaction. Here, the wave function represents the photon, the gauge potential originates from the material, and the coupling coefficient is determined by the total helicity. Under this transformation, the gauge field transforms as

A_{s}\rightarrow A_{s}+\partial_{s}\theta(s)

(17)

Furthermore, if we generalize the transformation in (
5
) to a stand
U(1)
symmetry, denoted by
e^{i\Theta(s)}
, the gauge field transforms in the familiar form:
A_{s}\rightarrow A_{s}-\partial_{s}\Theta(s)/\sigma
.

This transformation modifies the ordinary derivative into a covariant derivative,

D_{s}=\partial_{s}+i\sigma A_{s}

(18)

which ensures gauge invariance of the theory by canceling the variation terms such as those in Eq. (
15
).

Equivalence between gauge field and spin-orbit coupling

In the following, we show that Eq.(
12
) and Eq.(
16
) are equivalent representations in the case of helical optical fiber systems.
Consider an initial polarization vector of the displacement field
\mathbf{D}
aligned along the
x
-axis, expressed as
{\bf e}_{x}={\bf e}_{+}+{\bf e}_{-}
. Due to the geometric phase effect, the components of the right and left circular polarization acquire phase factors of opposite sign,
{\bf e}_{\pm}\rightarrow e^{\mp i\gamma(s)}{\bf e}_{\pm}
. We can introduce a new set of polarization vectors, which are related to the original ones by:

\displaystyle{\bf e}^{\prime}_{x}={\bf e}_{x}\cos\gamma(s)+{\bf e}_{y}\sin\gamma(s)

(19)

\displaystyle{\bf e}^{\prime}_{y}={\bf e}_{y}\cos\gamma(s)-{\bf e}_{x}\sin\gamma(s)

where
\gamma(s)
denotes the Berry phase, corresponding to the deflection angle of the polarization plane
Chiao and Wu 1986
;
Vinitskii et al. 1990
. These rotated vectors,
{\bf e}^{\prime}_{x}
and
{\bf e}^{\prime}_{y}
, can be identified with the transformed electric and magnetic field components,
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
, respectively. As a result, the electric field, magnetic field, and propagation direction together form a three-dimensional local coordinate frame. Together with the local propagation direction, they form a complete orthonormal triad, defining a three-dimensional local reference frame. Therefore, the dual transformation defined in Eq. (
1
) is formally analogous to a rotation, where the rotation angle
\theta(s)
is naturally identified with the geometric phase
\gamma(s)
. This relationship can be expressed as
Kugler and Shtrikman 1987

\theta(s)=\gamma(s)=\int\Omega(s)ds.

(20)

where
\Omega(s)
represents the angular velocity along the propagation path.Notably, when light propagates along a helical trajectory, the angular velocity corresponds to the torsion
\tau
of the path as defined in Eq. (
11
), i.e.,
\Omega(s)=\tau
.

The transformation in Eq. (
19
) is equivalent to shifting from a non-inertial reference frame to an inertial one
da Costa 1981
;
Kugler and Shtrikman 1987
;
Bliokh 2009
, wherein the redefined field vectors
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
are subjected to parallel transport. The gauge field serves as the connection governing parallel transport in this formulation, which leads to the vanishing of the effective gauge field in the inertial frame:

A_{s}+\partial_{s}\gamma(s)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad A_{s}=-\tau,

(21)

It is straightforward to verify that the interaction Lagrangian and the gauge field Lagrangian are equivalent, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{A}=\mathcal{L}_{I}.

(22)

This result demonstrates that spin-orbit coupling can be understood as an emergent effect of dual symmetry, manifesting prominently in geometrically structured systems like helical optical fibers.

Orbit-orbit coupling

For light carrying intrinsic OAM, an additional first-order correction term arises within the geometric optics approximation. This term, stemming from the OAM operator, contributes to the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{0}
at a level comparable to that of Eq. (
12
)
we2
:

\mathcal{L}^{l}_{0}=\psi^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{z}\tau\psi=\sum_{\sigma}\psi^{\dagger}_{\lambda_{\sigma}}l\tau\psi_{\lambda_{\sigma}}

(23)

where
\psi_{\lambda_{\sigma}}\equiv\psi_{\sigma}e^{il\varphi}
corresponds to the helicity eigenstates modified by a vortex phase with topological charge
l
.
Here, the total helicity
\lambda_{\sigma}
is defined as

\lambda_{\sigma}=\mathbf{J}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{k}}{k},

(24)

which, for a vortex beam with topological charge
l
, becomes
\lambda_{\sigma}=(l+\sigma)
, where
\sigma=\pm 1
denotes the spin eigenvalue of the photon. This term represents the orbital-orbit interaction.

Of course, a similar OAM term also appears in the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{A}
. However, to relate this term to the gauge field, it is necessary to generalize the original dual symmetry as follows:

\psi_{\lambda_{\sigma}}\rightarrow e^{-i\lambda_{\sigma}\theta(s)}\psi_{\lambda_{\sigma}}

(25)

This transformation implies a local rotation not only in the polarization basis but also in the wavefront structure, effectively shifting the azimuthal phase from
\varphi
to
\varphi-\theta(s)
. To preserve the invariance of the Lagrangian in Eq. (
6
) under this local symmetry transformation, the variation
\delta Q_{i}
of the medium must compensate for the non-vanishing commutator between the OAM operator
\hat{L}_{z}
and the Lagrangian. Physically, this variation captures the angular momentum transfer between the photon and the medium and may include contributions from induced moments.

Accordingly, reassigning a term from
\mathcal{L}_{p}
to
\mathcal{L}_{A}
necessitates the inclusion of a compensating term that accounts for the variation of the medium.
This additional contribution restores gauge invariance and underscores the fundamental equivalence between medium inhomogeneity and an induced gauge field.

Hall effect of light and Berry phase

The interaction between the gauge field and the photon wavefunction in momentum representation can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}_{A}=-\sum_{\sigma}\lambda_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\dot{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{A}\psi_{\sigma}.

(26)

which is consistent with the definition of angular velocity in momentum space,
\Omega(s)=-\mathbf{A}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{k}}

Bliokh 2009
. This formalism naturally extends to momentum space through the introduction of a covariant derivative,

\displaystyle D_{s}=\partial_{s}+i\lambda_{\sigma}A_{s}\rightarrow\frac{dk^{i}}{ds}D_{i}=\frac{dk^{i}}{ds}(\partial_{i}+i(\lambda_{\sigma}A_{i}))

(27)

Accordingly, the covariant derivative in momentum space is defined as

D_{i}=\partial_{i}+i\lambda_{\sigma}A_{i}

(28)

and its commutator yields

[D_{i},D_{j}]=\lambda_{\sigma}F_{ij}

(29)

Here,
F_{ij}
denotes the gauge field strength tensor. which is invariant under gauge transformations. This covariant differential structure also arises naturally in the framework of non-commutative mechanics

;
Duval and Horvathy 2001
;
Bérard and Mohrbach 2003
;
Duval and Horvathy 2005
. The gauge field
\mathbf{A}
, also known as the Berry connection, gives rise to the field strength vector
\mathbf{F}=\bm{\nabla_{k}}\times\mathbf{A}
, which manifests in momentum space as a "magnetic monopole" configuration. This so-called Berry curvature takes the form
\mathbf{F}=\lambda_{\sigma}\mathbf{k}/k^{3}
Chiao and Wu 1986
;
Bialynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 1987
;
Bliokh 2006
. These concepts have been used to explain various phenomena in optics, including Rytov’s law
Kravtsov and Orlov 2019
;
Chiao and Wu 1986
, as well as spin-dependent beam shifts such as the Imbert–Fedorov shift or spin Hall effect
Fedorov 2013
;
Imbert 1972
;
Murakami et al. 2003
;
Onoda et al. 2004
;
Mathieu et al. 2004
;
Sinova et al. 2004
;
Bliokh and Bliokh 2004
;
Bliokh and Bliokh 2005
;
Duval et al. 2006
;
Bliokh 2006
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
. The corresponding Berry phase accumulated along a closed loop in momentum space equals the solid angle subtended by the loop at the origin
Milione et al. 2012
;
Bliokh 2006
.

Treating the photon quantum mechanically via its wave function, the expectation value of Eq.(
26
) can be interpreted, within the geometric optics approximation, as the classical spin–orbit interaction term
Bliokh 2009
. The corresponding Euler–Lagrange equations for the classical variables
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{r}
reproduce the conventional ray trajectory. The inclusion of spin–orbit coupling introduces a first-order correction to the group velocity, resulting in the optical Hall effect

;
Bliokh et al. 2015

\dot{\mathbf{r}}\sim\lambda_{\sigma}\dot{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{F},

(30)

where
\mathbf{F}=\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A}
denotes the Berry curvature in momentum space.

The Berry phase can also be understood as a gauge-invariant Wilson loop in momentum space, arising from the integral of the field strength along a closed loop in momentum space. The Wilson loop is given by

U_{g}=\exp[i\lambda_{\sigma}\oint_{\Gamma}dp^{i}A_{i}(p)]=\exp[i\lambda_{\sigma}\oint_{\Sigma}dS^{ij}F_{ij}(p)]

(31)

where
\Gamma
is a closed path in momentum space, the
\Sigma
is the surface that spans the closed loop
\Gamma
, and the
dS^{ij}
represents an area element.

Conclusion

We investigate the localization of the dual symmetry of the electromagnetic field within a spin-degenerate medium using a field-theoretical framework, wherein a novel gauge field naturally emerges as a consequence of the local symmetry. In the geometric optics approximation, the spin–orbit interaction arising during the propagation of beams along helical trajectories is formally equivalent to the coupling between the helicity and an emergent gauge field. This correspondence allows the spin Hall effect of light to be understood within a unified gauge-theoretic framework, where such phenomena may be interpreted as analogues of axion-electromagnetic coupling
Wilczek 1987
;
Gasperini 1987
;
Nikitin and Kuriksha 2012
;
Visinelli 2013
. Analogous to the use of engineered materials to mimic gravitational interactions, this approach offers a novel perspective for simulating axionic fields through material platforms, such as topological insulators
Tong 2020
. For vortex beams, corrections due to orbital-orbit interactions remain significant; however, these interactions are fundamentally distinct from spin–orbit coupling, which originates from a deeper intrinsic symmetry. This distinction offers a novel perspective for differentiating between the spin and intrinsic OAM of light. In media with non-vanishing spatial variations of the
Q_{\pm 2}
and
Q_{\pm 1}
tensors, or in anisotropic systems where
\epsilon\neq\mu
, the appearance of a
\gamma^{5}
term lifts the spin degeneracy and promotes the underlying dual symmetry to a non-Abelian gauge symmetry, thereby inducing an effective non-Abelian gauge field in real space
Bliokh et al. 2007
;
Chen et al. 2018
.
