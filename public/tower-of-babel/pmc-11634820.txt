Assessment of Dementia in Minority Ethnic Groups in Europe: A 14‐Year Follow‐Up Survey
Authors: T. Rune Nielsen, Alexandre de Mendonça, Lutz Frölich, Sebastiaan Engelborghs, Dianne Gove, Daphné Lamirel, Clara Calia, Gunhild Waldemar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThere is no European consensus on good clinical practice for timely diagnosis and culturally appropriate care of people with dementia from minority ethnic groups. Despite significant advances in culture‐sensitive dementia diagnostics, little is known about how this has been implemented in clinical practice. The aims of this follow‐up survey were to explore current practices for assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups in Europe and to determine whether barriers in access to specialized dementia services have changed during the last 14 years.MethodsA cross‐sectional online survey was conducted in the European Alzheimer's Disease Consortium (EADC) in June 2023. The survey utilized questionnaires from a previous EADC survey from 2009 and focused on different points in the clinical assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups.ResultsThirty‐three centers from 15 countries participated in the survey, of which 21 were the same as in the 2009 survey. Patients from minority ethnic groups were seen on a regular basis in 91% of these centers, which was a significant increase compared to 2009 (69%, p = 0.04). Despite perceived clinical expertise increasing, implemented strategies for bypassing cultural and linguistic barriers were sparse and communication problems and lack of adequate assessment tools continued to be the main perceived barriers in diagnostic assessment of dementia in minority ethnic groups.ConclusionsPatients from minority ethnic groups are increasingly being referred for diagnostic assessment of dementia in Europe. Despite some improvements, diagnostic challenges generally remained the same as in 2009 and there is a continuous need to implement effective cross‐cultural communication and assessment practices.

Summary Experience and expertise in assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups had increased among European dementia specialists during the last 14 years.Diagnostic challenges generally remained unchanged, with communication problems and lack of adequate assessment tools being the main barriers in diagnostic assessment of patients from minority ethnic groups.Strategies for bypassing cultural and linguistic barriers, including implemented practices for employing professional interpreters and conducting cognitive assessments with culture‐sensitive instruments, were generally suboptimal.There is a continued need to implement effective strategies for bypassing cultural and linguistic barriers in diagnostic assessment of dementia in minority ethnic groups in Europe.

Introduction

There is no European consensus on good clinical practice for timely diagnosis and culturally appropriate care of people with dementia from minority ethnic groups [1], defined as access to accurate diagnosis and care at a time in the disease process when it can be of most benefit to them [2]. Fourteen years ago, we examined the status and practice for assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups in Europe through a survey among members of the European Alzheimer's Disease Consortium (EADC) [3], a network of European centers of clinical and research excellence specializing in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Patients from minority ethnic groups were defined as “patients who were first generation immigrants or refugees from countries outside the extended European Union (EU), Canada, United States of America, Australia, and New Zealand.” The survey revealed a high level of perceived challenges in the diagnostic evaluation due to language barriers and a lack of adequate assessment tools, and that specialized dementia services were generally unprepared to provide adequate services for patients and families from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds. In Europe, more than 6.5 million older people (65+ years) have migrant backgrounds, and of these an estimated 475,000 have dementia [4] and nearly 690,000 to have mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [5]. However, compared to majority ethnic populations, older people from minority ethnic groups with dementia are less likely to be identified and diagnosed [6, 7, 8, 9, 10], and less likely to receive anti‐dementia treatment [7, 8, 11, 12].

Barriers in access to dementia care in minority ethnic groups have been recognized in the past 14 years (e.g., refs. [13, 14]). This is also shown by the fact that issues concerning dementia in migrants and minority ethnic groups are now addressed in several national dementia plans [15, 16] and European national dementia care guidelines [17]. However, dedicated policies at the national or international level are still lacking [1, 15], which stands in contrast to the growing need. Persistent barriers include lack of knowledge regarding available services, misconceptions and stigma related to dementia, lack of services that focus on social needs, and a complex healthcare system that is difficult to navigate. Although these barriers may not be specific to minority ethnic communities, but rather reflect more general barriers in access to formal dementia care in European countries [18], these as well as other barriers are exaggerated by language and cultural issues [13, 14].

Despite significant advances in culture‐sensitive dementia diagnostics and care, this expertise seems to be localized rather than widespread [16, 19], and the gap between the increasing need and available culture‐sensitive services is still widening [20, 21]. There is a lack of multilingual and multicultural dementia specialists, standards for cross‐cultural and cross‐linguistic assessment, and properly validated culturally sensitive diagnostic tools [1, 19, 22].

Approaches to solve these challenges include the establishment of specialized multicultural memory clinics [23] and methods for cross‐cultural assessment of cognitive impairment [19]. Cross‐cultural cognitive testing found its way into dementia diagnostics around 20 years ago [24]. One of the first approaches was the Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale, which was first validated in Europe in 2013 [25]. Since then, the RUDAS and several other cross‐cultural cognitive tests have been developed and validated within and across several European countries [19].

Despite the increasing evidence base about barriers in cross‐cultural assessment of dementia, indicating the importance of efficient communication (e.g., through formal interpreters or cultural mediators) and access to culture‐sensitive diagnostic tools, little is known about how much of this has been implemented in clinical practice or whether challenges have changed. The aim of this survey was to explore current perceptions of EADC members concerning assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups and use of culture‐sensitive assessment methods in clinical practice, as well as to determine whether barriers in access to specialized dementia services have changed since 2009.

Materials and Methods

This cross‐sectional survey of members of the EADC utilized the questionnaire from the previous survey completed in June 2009 [3]. The survey included questions on the participating centers and their service delivery, the perceived influence of minority ethnic background on diagnostic procedures, issues perceived to influence clinical assessments, and general perceptions of challenges and skills in assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups. Based on previous responses and recent developments, small revisions to the original questionnaire included exclusion of questions concerning the diagnostic workup in patients from minority ethnic groups that turned out to be redundant (i.e., practically all centers reported adhering to evidence‐based clinical guidelines), addition of new questions concerning neuropsychological assessment and perceived challenges in diagnosing specific cognitive disorders, and changes in Likert scale response formats. Most questions required tick box responses to dichotomous (yes/no) or Likert scale questions (i.e., almost never to almost always or very difficult to very easy) and had an option for providing additional comments in free text. The revised questionnaire was converted into an online survey using SurveyXact (www.surveyxact.dk) and pilot‐tested in four participating centers (see Supporting Information S2: Appendix S1 for the survey questions). Based on feedback in the pilot study, the adopted definition of minority ethnic group was aligned with the one used in the 2009 survey.

Information about the survey, including a link to the questionnaire, was emailed to the contact person in each EADC center in June 2023 via the EADC member list, with the request to complete it by a clinical dementia expert in the center. Up to four reminders were sent, and the survey remained open for 3 months. Data was downloaded from SurveyXact and analyzed using SPSS, version 28.0. The 2009 survey generally adopted a five‐point Likert scale response format, whereas the 2023 survey generally adopted a four‐point Likert scale response format that avoided a neutral or middle option to prompt respondents to make a clear choice. Thus, to compare responses from the two surveys, Likert scale responses were dichotomized to give an overall affirmative (almost always or often) or negative (all other responses) perception of influence. Descriptive statistics and Chi‐square or Fisher's Exact tests were used to investigate the significance of differences in categorical variables. McNemar's test was used to investigate differences between related binomial proportions. A p‐value < 0.05 (two‐tailed) was considered significant.

To support and better understand the findings of the survey, feedback on indicators of culture‐sensitive services was obtained from two carers from the Indian Sikh and Black Caribbean communities, and a person with dementia from the Black Caribbean community through Alzheimer Europe's Public Involvement work. According to Danish legislation, ethical approval of an institutional review board was not required.

Results

In 2009, 36 EADC centers from 15 countries participated in the survey. In 2023, the EADC had 43 active member centers across 18 European countries. All currently active EADC centers were contacted, of which 38 centers (88%) from 16 countries responded to the follow‐up survey. Five respondents did not complete the full survey and were excluded. Thus, in total 33 EADC centers from 15 countries were included in the analyses (see Figure 1). These included EADC centers from 12 of the 15 original countries from 2009 (Belgium [n = 4], Denmark [n = 1], France [n = 4], Germany [n = 6], Greece [n = 2[, Italy [n = 2], the Netherlands [n = 2], Poland [n = 1], Portugal [n = 2], Sweden [n = 1], Switzerland [n = 2], United Kingdom [n = 2]) and EADC centers from three new countries (Bulgaria [n = 1], Czech Republic [n = 1], Slovenia [n = 2]). A total of 21 centers participated in both the 2009 and 2023 survey. Participating centers in the 2009 and 2023 surveys were generally comparable in their organization and service delivery, with no significant differences in the representation of medical specialties (neurology, psychiatry, and geriatrics), number of referred patients, proportion of patients diagnosed with dementia, or service coverage (all comparisons p > 0.05).

Changes in Assessment of Dementia in Minority Ethnic Groups in Europe

The survey revealed that an increasing number of patients from minority ethnic groups were being referred for assessment of dementia in Europe. Among the centers surveyed in 2023, 91% reported receiving referrals of patients from minority ethnic groups, which was a significant increase compared to 2009 (69%; Fisher's Exact Test, p = 0.04). In addition, patients from minority ethnic groups generally made up a larger proportion of referred patients (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). As in the 2009 survey, there were apparent differences in the ethnicity of patients referred for assessment in the centers, but the most common minority ethnic groups were immigrants from Middle Eastern and North African countries (especially Turkey and Morocco) followed by immigrants from non‐EU East European countries (especially Ukraine and Russia). Also, there was a trend for an increasing proportion of referred patients from minority ethnic groups being diagnosed with dementia (X 2(4, N = 53) = 8.51, p = 0.08). Compared to 2009, the proportion of centers reporting that more than 40% of newly referred patients from minority ethnic groups were diagnosed with dementia increased by 27% (2009: 30%; 2023: 57%). Moreover, in contrast to 2009 (30% vs. 81%; McNemar's Test, p < 0.001) these diagnostic rates were no longer significantly different from those reported in all referred patients (both from minority and majority ethnic groups) (57% vs. 72%; McNemar's Test, p = 0.29).

Indicators of culture sensitive dementia services had generally not changed much during the past 14 years, and remained sparse, with no indicator being reported by more than 40% of the centers (Figure 2). However, compared to 2009, there was a significant 21% increase in knowledge of specific services for minority ethnic groups in the area (6% vs. 27%; Fisher's Exact Test, p = 0.02). These services were mostly post‐diagnostic support services followed by diagnostic services. Furthermore, there was an 8% and 14% increase in the proportion of centers using translated or adapted versions of routinely used cognitive screening tests or specific cross‐cultural cognitive screening tests with patients from minority ethnic groups, in particular translated or adapted versions of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) [26] and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [27] for the main minority groups, and the RUDAS [24]. Moreover, some centers reported using other specified cross‐cultural cognitive screening tests, including the Cross‐Cultural Dementia Screening Test (CCD) [28], Multicultural Cognitive Examination (MCE) [29], Nine images test of the district of Seine‐Saint‐Denis (TNI‐93) [30], and EASY [31]. A new question in the 2023 survey concerned the European Cross‐Cultural Neuropsychological Test Battery (CNTB) [32], which was available to neuropsychologists in 20% of the centers. In contrast to these increases, there was a 10% decrease in the proportion of centers reporting having access to information and consent materials in the languages of minority ethnic groups.

When interpretation was necessary, 90% of the centers reported almost always or often relying on family members, 40% on professional interpreters, and 33% on multilingual staff members. While 26% of the centers reported that professional interpreter services were generally not available, such services were freely available to patients in 60% of the centers, and in 10% of the centers professional interpreter services were partly covered but had some costs for patients.

Changes in Perceived Challenges in Assessment of Dementia in Minority Ethnic Groups in Europe

Generally, the perceived influence of patients' minority ethnic background on diagnostic procedures, in particular the clinical interview (44% vs. 73%; Fischer's Exact Test, p = 0.05), cognitive testing (44% vs. 77%; Fischer's Exact Test, p = 0.03), and lumbar puncture (10% vs. 33%; Fischer's Exact Test, p = 0.09), had increased (Figure 3). Moreover, a new question in the 2023 survey concerned the perceived influence of patients' minority ethnic background on neuropsychological evaluations, which was confirmed in 73% of the centers. Compared to 2009, there was a general trend toward a higher proportion of centers reporting challenging issues in the clinical assessment of patients from minority ethnic groups (Figure 4), particularly concerning a lack of suitable test instruments and rating scales (44% vs. 80%; Fisher's Exact Test, p = 0.01).

Compared to the 2009 survey, there was a 22% increase in the proportion of centers who perceived their skills in assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups to be good (2009: 48%; 2023 70%), and a 14% decrease in the proportion of centers who found assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups to be more challenging than in patients from the majority ethnic group (2009: 64%; 2023: 50%). However, a new question in the 2023 survey concerned perceived challenges in assessing specific cognitive disorders in patients from minority ethnic groups, which revealed that the centers generally found it more challenging to assess FTD (76%; McNemar's Test, p = 0.04) and MCI (84%; McNemar's Test, p = 0.003), and less challenging to assess VaD (34%; McNemar's Test, p = 0.05), compared to all‐cause dementia (50%), whereas there were no significant differences for AD (41%), Mixed AD/VaD (44%) and dementia with Lewy bodies/Parkinson's disease dementia (52%) (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S2).

Sensitivity analyses, including only the centers who participated in both the 2009 and 2023 survey revealed the same overall results (Supporting Information S1: Figures S3–S5).

Feedback From Public Involvement

Concerning the cultural sensitivity of services, the Public Involvement work highlighted several remaining barriers, including a lack of culturally and linguistically appropriate information about dementia, language barriers, culturally biased assessment procedures, experiences of prejudice and discrimination, and challenges navigating complex healthcare systems. The importance of health professionals' cultural sensitivity‐ and awareness during clinical assessments was emphasized, including the choice of questions and tasks used in cognitive assessments and the clinical interpretation of behaviors and expressions. It was suggested that assessment by a multilingual health professional with shared ethnic background would not only help bypass language barriers, but also reduce the risk of overlooking or misinterpreting clinical signs and symptoms.

When it came to asking relatives to be interpreters during clinical consultations and assessments, the potentially negative impact on families of this approach was emphasized and it was suggested that relatives might, out of respect, be inclined to withhold important information when serving as interpreters. However, it was also acknowledged that relatives usually have a better understanding of the patient and that professional interpreters may struggle to build up trust.

Discussion

This study explored the status and practice of assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups in Europe and whether this had changed during the last 14 years.

This survey of EADC centers provides insights into the current views among European dementia specialists on assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups and enables some comparisons to the views reported in a previous survey. Overall, the results from the survey indicate that experience and expertise in assessing dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups has increased during the past 14 years. However, despite some improvements the diagnostic challenges generally remained the same as in 2009, with communication problems and lack of adequate assessment tools being reported as the main barriers in diagnostic assessment of patients from minority ethnic groups. Consistent with the findings in the previous survey, strategies for bypassing cultural and linguistic barriers were suboptimal. Most centers had not implemented practices for conducting cognitive assessments with culturally validated or cross‐cultural instruments, and although professional interpreters were commonly used, they more frequently relied on family members for interpretation.

Almost all centers received referrals of patients from minority ethnic groups. This was a significant increase from 2009, where almost one‐third of the surveyed centers reported that they did not receive referrals of patients from minority ethnic groups. On one hand, this may reflect demographic aging and increasing numbers of people with dementia in minority ethnic groups as the number of older migrants residing in the EU has increased 55% during the last decade; from 6,150,000 in 2014 to 9,550,000 in 2023 [33]. On the other hand, increasing referrals may also reflect that awareness and perceptions of dementia may be gradually changing among minority ethnic groups, leading to more help‐seeking for cognitive impairment [14]. During the last decade, there have been several European efforts to reach minority ethnic communities to increase knowledge and awareness about dementia [34, 35, 36, 37], which may have contributed to reducing known barriers for help‐seeking [14, 38]. Despite these improvements, the Public Involvement work highlighted a current dilemma for many people from minority ethnic groups between respecting the traditions and norms of patriarchal cultural and religious communities and a growing awareness of and desire for increased openness about dementia, which would be more conducive to timely assessment and diagnosis. Also, several barriers to help‐seeking remained, including language barriers, experiences of discrimination, and challenges navigating complex healthcare systems.

Consistent with the previous survey and a recent survey from Scotland [21], language barriers were one of the most often reported challenges in the diagnostic assessment. Despite significant advances in the clinical implementation of imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers in Europe during the last decade [39, 40], diagnostic assessment of dementia disorders is still very much a clinical discipline relying on verbal communication and observation of behavior. Not surprisingly, the main challenges in assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups were reported for the elements of clinical assessments with high demands on verbal communication. In the Public Involvement work, the importance of culturally sensitive communication was emphasized, which extended to the choice of questions and tasks used in cognitive assessments and the clinical interpretation of behaviors and expressions. It was suggested that some of these issues may resolved by involving multilingual health professionals with shared ethnic background in the assessment. Surprisingly, there was also an increase in reported challenges in obtaining consent to lumbar punctures.

Using interpreters may be crucial for securing efficient communication when assessing linguistically diverse patients. However, access to and utilization of interpreter services differ widely both within and between European countries [1, 41]. Family members were routinely used for interpretation in almost all centers and professional interpreters in less than half of the centers. These practices had not changed much since 2009 and were generally in line with the results from a recent survey of memory assessment services in England and Wales [42]. Although using family members as interpreters is generally discouraged due to the lack of professional standards and greater risk of miscommunication [1, 43], clinicians may frequently rely on untrained interpreters, including family members, during consultations and assessments due to cost and scheduling issues [44]. Additionally, some patients may insist upon having family members interpret for them due to trust, confidentiality, or other concerns [45, 46, 47]. However, the scientific evidence on medical interpretation recommends using professional interpreters due to a significantly lower likelihood of mistakes, greater clinician and patient satisfaction, and better patient outcomes [43, 46]. Although using professional interpreters may also come with challenges as they are typically not familiar with cognitive communication difficulties and standardized cognitive assessment procedures [41, 47, 48], interpretation is a skill that requires professional training [43]. We suspect that the poor implementation of professional interpreters may exacerbate the reported challenges in conducting clinical assessments with high demands on verbal communication and obtaining consent to do lumbar punctures. Patients are often reluctant to undergo lumbar punctures, but the likelihood of obtaining consent increase by providing detailed information about the procedure and the probability of experiencing adverse events [49]. Providing such information may be challenging if using untrained interpreters and may be further complicated by not having access to translated information and consent materials. Public Involvement feedback also emphasized the potential negative consequences of using relatives as interpreters. But at the same time, it was recognized that relatives usually have a better understanding of the patient and that professional interpreters may struggle to build up trust. This seems to be an important point, bearing in mind the prejudice and discrimination people from minority ethnic groups may have experienced, and may be an obstacle in relation to consent to obtaining invasive clinical procedures, such as lumbar punctures.

Compared to 2009, awareness of the limitations of assessing patients from minority ethnic groups with cognitive instruments that have only been standardized for the majority ethnic group had significantly increased, and more centers now reported using a variety of culturally validated versions of instruments such as the MMSE [26] or MoCA [27], or cross‐cultural instruments such as the RUDAS [24]. During the past 14 years, there have been several efforts to increase awareness of barriers in diagnostic assessment of dementia in minority ethnic groups in Europe, and to develop and validate cognitive assessment instruments that are less influenced by cultural, linguistic, and educational factors [19, 50]. This work has mainly been led by members of the European Consortium on Cross‐Cultural Neuropsychology (ECCroN) [22] and includes validation of several cross‐cultural cognitive instruments [19]. In line with the results from a recent European Delphi study [45], the RUDAS and CNTB were the most widely implemented instruments across the EADC centers, while use of the other cross‐cultural cognitive instruments was local rather than widespread. Also, perceived expertise in assessing patients from minority ethnic groups had increased. Although half of the respondents perceived assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups to be more challenging compared to patients from the majority ethnic group, this had improved compared to 2009, and the proportion of centers perceiving their skills in assessing the patients to be good had increased from less than half to more than two‐thirds. Also, the gap in diagnostic rates between patients from minority and majority ethnic groups had diminished as more patients were now diagnosed with dementia. It seems unlikely that these changes are due to improved cross‐cultural assessment practices alone. It probably also reflects that help‐seeking and referral patterns have changed during the last 14 years [14, 51], with fewer referred patients from minority ethnic groups having cognitive and behavioral symptoms associated with psychiatric disorders, rather than dementia.

Despite the general increase in perceived expertise in assessing patients from minority ethnic groups, there were evident challenges associated with assessing specific cognitive disorders. Thus, 84% found it more challenging to assess MCI, and 76% to assess FTD, in patients from minority ethnic groups compared to patients from the majority ethnic group. Both MCI and FTD are complex and multifaceted conditions and cultural influences on the expression of symptoms may make it extremely challenging to establish the diagnosis [52, 53]. The impact of culture may be particularly relevant for behavioral variant FTD, where the presence of diagnostic features is largely based on clinical judgment [54]. Although neuroimaging and CSF biomarkers are often used to confirm MCI and FTD diagnoses [55, 56, 57], detailed clinical assessments, including comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, is often required to reach the clinical diagnosis. Despite recent efforts [58, 59], appropriate cross‐cultural measures of language and social cognitive functioning are generally not available [45], and although the CNTB and a variety of memory tests [19] have been established for assessment of minority ethnic groups in Europe, these are generally poorly implemented in the EADC centers. Assessment of VaD was generally found less challenging, which may be related to the fact that, besides cognitive and functional impairment, the key diagnostic feature is the documentation of cerebrovascular abnormalities on neuroimaging [60].

Some limitations of the study should be considered. First, despite the high response rate (88%), not all EADC responded to the survey. Respondents may have been more experienced in assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups, therefore biasing the sample. Also, cross‐sectional surveys are subject to recall bias, which may have resulted in over‐ or under‐estimations in the responses. Second, missing data may also have affected the obtained results. Five respondents failed to answer more than only the first questions, which some stated was due to ethical concerns related to the specific study of minority ethnic groups. Third, statistical power in comparisons between the surveys were constrained by the cross‐sectional survey design and the fact that different Likert scale formats were used across the two surveys. However, although less robust, the comparisons do provide some insight into trends over the 14‐year period. Fourth, the survey only considered EADC member views and may not be representative of smaller, less specialized centers. For instance, in a recent survey among less specialized services in Scotland, 86% of the respondents found assessment of dementia in patients from minority ethnic groups to be more challenging compared to patients from the majority ethnic group, and only 14% found their skills in assessing the patients to be good [21]. The perceptions of dementia specialists in specialized European dementia centers does, however, give an impression of the current clinical practice for assessment of dementia in minority ethnic groups in Europe. Also, although further research in this area is needed, patient views from the Public Involvement work provide further insight into how cross‐cultural assessments should be delivered in clinical practice and their impact on outcomes, as well as the need to be aware of how historical and current structural discrimination continue to impact on assessment and diagnosis, despite the progress that is being made in this area. Conclusion

This survey identified enduring challenges in clinical practice for diagnostic evaluation of dementia in minority ethnic groups in Europe. There appears to have been a shift, with perceived clinical expertise increasing, alongside a reported increase in referrals of patients from minority ethnic groups. However, communication problems and lack of adequate assessment tools remained to be the main perceived barriers in diagnostic assessment of dementia in minority ethnic groups, highlighting the continued need to implement effective strategies for bypassing cultural and linguistic barriers.
