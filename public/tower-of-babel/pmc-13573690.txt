Glioblastoma recurrence: An NGS-centered seed-soil framework
Authors: Jun-yi Xu, Jingxian Chen, Wenyan Feng, Guo-zhong Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We propose an NGS-centered seed–soil framework for glioblastoma recurrence that unifies diverse observations.•The framework complements existing recurrence models rather than replacing them and remains to be experimentally validated.•Targeting NGS may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing or delaying GBM recurrence.

Introduction

Despite maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy [1], recurrence of IDH-wild-type GBM remains nearly inevitable and represents the principal cause of mortality [2]. Current models of GBM recurrence largely emphasize tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, including therapeutic resistance, cellular plasticity, and clonal evolution [3,4], which explain how tumor cells survive treatment. However, they do not fully explain how residual tumor cells subsequently reconstruct an aggressive recurrent tumor ecosystem within the post-treatment brain—a process rarely observable longitudinally in patients and therefore usually inferred from primary–recurrent comparisons or treatment-induced resistance models.

A seed–soil concept has been proposed for postsurgical GBM recurrence, in which residual tumor cells represent the "seed" and the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME) the "soil" [5,6]. Yet the nature of this soil remains incompletely defined: the postsurgical environment contains diverse stromal, immune, vascular, extracellular-matrix, and neural components, each capable of influencing residual tumor-cell behavior. The central question is thus not simply which residual cells survive, but which components of the surrounding brain provide the signals enabling them to persist and reconstruct recurrent disease.

Among these components, neurons are distinctive because they communicate directly and dynamically with glioma cells through NGS [7,8]. Unlike paracrine [9] or extracellular-vesicle [10] signaling, NGS provides activity-dependent synaptic input that directly influences glioma-cell behavior; glutamatergic [7,8] and cholinergic [[11], [12], [13]] NGS regulate calcium signaling, proliferation, invasion, migration, and tumor-network activity in GBM. These observations raise a broader question: could the neuron–tumor interface contribute to the evolution of recurrence rather than merely to primary tumor progression?

Because recurrence begins with residual disease after surgical resection, this question must be considered within the postsurgical environment. Here, we examine surgery as the biological starting point of recurrence evolution and ask whether persistent or re-established neuron–glioma communication may interface residual tumor cells with the surrounding brain. We then discuss the neuronal features of recurrent GBM, how NGS-associated signaling may participate in recurrence evolution, and the experimental and therapeutic questions that remain unresolved.

Surgery and the postsurgical environment

"Seed" cells may possess the ability of forming NGS

Maximal safe resection remains the cornerstone of GBM treatment, yet infiltrative tumor cells inevitably remain in the surrounding brain—and these residual cells are not necessarily a random sampling of the primary tumor. GBM exhibits extensive intratumoral heterogeneity, comprising mesenchymal-like (MES-like), astrocyte-like (AC-like), oligodendrocyte progenitor cell-like (OPC-like), neural progenitor cell-like (NPC-like), and neuron-like (NEU-like) states [14,15], as well as additional proliferative and metabolic states defined by pathway activity [16,17]. Spatial studies indicate that MES/AC-like populations are enriched toward the tumor core [18], whereas OPC/NPC/NEU-like populations localize preferentially at the invasive margin [19], exhibit invasive and migratory characteristics [12,19], and participate in neuronal interaction and NGS formation [20,21] (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1Biological basis in primary GBM for the NGS-centered seed–soil framework (A) Potential NGS-forming capacity of recurrence “seed” supported by intratumoral heterogeneity Edge regions are enriched in NEU/OPC/NPC-like cells, which exhibit invasive properties and preferentially participate in NGS formation. These observations provide a biological basis for considering residual invasive-margin cells as potential recurrence "seeds" that retain both invasive capacity and the ability to engage the surrounding neural environment. However, this should not imply a single cellular origin of recurrence: GBM recurrence likely arises from heterogeneous residual populations, and the contribution of individual cell states remains unresolved.

The postsurgical brain constitutes a dynamic recurrence "soil"

Following resection, the surrounding brain contains neurons and axonal projections together with reactive astrocytes, microglia/macrophages, vascular components, extracellular matrix, inflammatory mediators, and trophic signals, all of which may influence residual tumor-cell survival, plasticity, and recurrence evolution.

Neurons are nevertheless of particular interest, as they provide direct, activity-dependent communication with glioma cells. Because primary GBM engages both local and long-range neuronal circuits, including contralateral projections [11,12,22], surgical resection cannot eliminate all connected neuronal partners. Thus, resection removes most of the tumor while leaving a neural environment capable of supporting renewed neuron–glioma interactions (Fig. 1B).

At the same time, surgery profoundly remodels this environment [23,24]. Tissue injury induces inflammation, reactive gliosis, vascular and extracellular-matrix remodeling, and altered neuronal activity, with potentially opposing effects on NGS formation: injury-associated synaptogenic [25,26] and neurotrophic signals [27,28] may permit synaptic remodeling, whereas physical disruption of tumor–neuron contacts, neuronal or axonal loss, and inhibitory matrix changes [29,30] may restrict new synaptic connections. An NGS-centered seed–soil framework emerges from the postsurgical setting

The coexistence of potential recurrence seeds and a persistent but remodeled neural environment provides the context for an NGS-centered interpretation of recurrence. We propose that residual tumor cells represent the potential "seed", the postsurgical brain the broader "soil", and neurons a specialized neural component of that soil, with NGS serving as a potential interface between the two.

This framework implies neither that neurons are the sole determinants of recurrence nor that postsurgical NGS formation is necessarily enhanced. Rather, it proposes the neuron–tumor interface as one potentially important interaction through which the postsurgical environment may influence residual-cell evolution. The central question is whether NGS persists or becomes re-established after surgery and, if so, whether it contributes to the survival, adaptation, organization, or dissemination of residual tumor cells.

Several testable predictions follow. Recurrent GBM would be expected to exhibit enhanced neuronal and NGS-associated features; treatment may remodel neuron–glioma communication during the transition from primary disease to recurrence; and NGS-associated processes may contribute to reconstruction of local tumor systems or dissemination to distant sites. Recurrent GBM exhibits enhanced neuronal characteristics

One notable feature of recurrent GBM is the enrichment of neuronal and synaptic characteristics, including increased presynaptic contacts, a higher number of NGS per tumor cell, and axonal elongation [31]. Consistently, neuronal-associated markers, including MAP2, SYN1, and DAMM1, are upregulated in recurrent tumors [31]. At the cellular level, neurons [32,33], oligodendrocytes [32,33], and NEU-like tumor cells [31] all increase during recurrence, creating a cellular context that may be permissive for enhanced neuron–glioma communication. Transcriptomic analyses further support increased neuron–tumor interaction in recurrent disease. The invasive margin of recurrent GBM shows greater enrichment of synapse-associated pathways than primary GBM [32]. Moreover, recurrent NEU-like tumor cells are predicted to express NRXN proteins capable of interacting with neuronal NLGNs, with stronger predicted interaction than in primary tumors [33]. Because NRXN–NLGN interactions contribute to synaptic organization, these findings are consistent with enhanced NGS-associated remodeling during recurrence.

A potential challenge to a neuron-centered interpretation of recurrence is that recurrent GBM also frequently exhibits mesenchymal characteristics [32], including enrichment of mesenchymal tumor populations, increased myeloid infiltration, and broader remodeling of the immune microenvironment [34,35]. Neuronal and mesenchymal phenotypes, however, are not mutually exclusive and can coexist within recurrent tumors [32] (Fig. 3). One possibility is a spatial model, in which neuronal programs are preferentially enriched at invasive or neuron-rich regions, whereas mesenchymal programs are more prominent in hypoxic, immune-enriched, or otherwise remodeled tumor regions. A temporal model, in which neuronal characteristics precede later mesenchymal enrichment during recurrence evolution, is also conceivable. However, this sequential interpretation remains untested and should not be considered an established trajectory. NGS-associated processes during recurrence evolution

How might the postsurgical setting and the neuronal features of recurrent GBM be connected over time? We propose that neuron–glioma communication may be involved at several stages of recurrence evolution, from therapy-associated NGS remodeling to local network reconstruction and distant dissemination. Importantly, the following model integrates established experimental observations with hypothesis-driven interpretations; it does not imply that NGS has been proven to cause clinical recurrence.

Therapy-associated remodeling of NGS

Standard therapy represents a major transition point between primary disease and recurrence. In patient-derived xenograft models, prolonged TMZ exposure is accompanied by enrichment of synapse-associated transcriptional programs and increased neuronal characteristics [31]. Single-cell analyses further show an increased proportion of NPC-like tumor cells following TMZ treatment [40]. Because NPC-like populations overlap with residual tumor states proposed to function as recurrence “seeds”, these observations suggest that TMZ may enrich tumor populations with greater potential for neuron–glioma interaction. Radiotherapy appears to produce a more direct remodeling of the neuron–glioma interface. It could increase the number of neuronal connections per tumor cell and enhance NGS connectivity [12]. Radiation-associated neuronal hyperexcitability and action-potential bursts further indicate reciprocal changes in neuronal and tumor-cell activity. Pharmacological inhibition of AMPAR signaling reduces the radiation-associated increase in NGS and attenuates tumor progression in experimental co-culture and in vivo models [12]. Although these findings provide functional evidence that radiotherapy can remodel neuron–glioma connectivity, they derive from a single preliminary study and require independent validation.

Within the proposed framework, therapy-induced NGS remodeling may represent a transition between treatment and recurrence: residual cells must survive therapeutic stress, and NGS-associated Ca²⁺ signaling provides biologically plausible survival inputs [41] (Fig. 4A). Reconstruction of a new local tumor system

The predominant clinical pattern of GBM recurrence is local regrowth near the original lesion or resection cavity. Within the proposed framework, local recurrence may begin when residual tumor “seeds” remain within the postsurgical niche and progressively reconstruct a new tumor system.

Recurrent GBM contains multiple tumor-cell states and re-establishes an interconnected GBM network. NGS may contribute to this process at several levels. First, neuronal signaling may help restore intratumoral heterogeneity during recurrence [43]. At the network level, tumor cells with strong neuronal synaptic connectivity extend elongated processes corresponding to tumor microtubes (TM) [44]. Glu–AMPAR signaling promotes Ca²⁺ transients and TM formation [7], whereas Ach–CHRM3 signaling induces Ca²⁺ oscillations and enhances coordinated tumor-cell activity [11]. These observations provide experimental support for a link between neuronal input and reconstruction of tumor-cell connectivity.

The resulting TM-associated GBM network provides an internal communication system among multiple tumor cells and supports coordinated calcium and electrical signaling through gap junctions and adhesion junctions [[45], [46], [47]]. Once established, this network contributes to adaptive responses following chemotherapy and radiotherapy [48] (Fig. 4B).

An important implication is that NGS and TM networks may represent complementary rather than strictly linear mechanisms. In the early postsurgical phase, when much of the pre-existing tumor network has been disrupted by resection, NGS may provide external neural input to residual cells. As the tumor expands and the recurrent ecosystem is reconstructed, TM networks may provide additional internal communication and network-level coordination. This temporal relationship remains a proposed model requiring longitudinal validation.

Neuronal-like migration and distant recurrence

Distant recurrence represents a distinct spatial trajectory in which residual tumor cells disseminate beyond the immediate vicinity of the original lesion before establishing recurrent growth. In GBM, invasive cells are enriched for NEU/OPC/NPC-like states and exhibit branching migration, locomotion, and translocation patterns resembling developmental neuronal migration [19].

Emerging evidence suggests that neuronal activity and NGS-associated signaling can enhance these migratory behaviors. Neuronal inputs from distant brain regions promote glioma invasion [49], while tumor cells with greater synaptic connectivity exhibit increased invasion in experimental spheroid models [44]. Glu–AMPAR signaling promotes Ca²⁺-dependent TM dynamics and migration [19], whereas Ach–CHRM3 signaling induces a motile cellular state associated with increased invasion [11]. These findings provide a biological basis for proposing that NGS-associated signaling may facilitate neuronal-like migration of residual tumor cells.

However, migration should not be equated with distant recurrence. Dissemination is only one component of a multistep process that also requires survival during transit, adaptation to the new microenvironment, proliferation, and reconstruction of a supportive tumor ecosystem at the ectopic site. Thus, neuronal-like migration should be considered a potential contributor to distant dissemination rather than a demonstrated mechanism of distant recurrence.

An integrated model of recurrence evolution

Taken together, local and distant recurrence may represent different spatial outcomes of a common postsurgical evolutionary process: cells retained near the resection cavity progressively reconstruct a local tumor system, whereas a subset of residual cells undergoes neuronal-like dissemination to distant sites (Fig. 5). The model thus does not require a single obligatory NGS-to-recurrence pathway; rather, NGS-associated processes may influence several stages of recurrence evolution, providing a conceptual link between established neuron–glioma biology and the spatial patterns of recurrent GBM. Discussion and future perspective

The necessity and sufficiency of NGS remain unresolved

The most important challenge is establishing causality—and a related limitation must be acknowledged: most evidence for neuron–glioma communication derives from intact-brain xenograft models, whereas clinical recurrence arises in the post-surgical resection cavity, a markedly different microenvironment (Fig. 2). Encouragingly, this gap is beginning to close. The recent identification of a C1QL1–BAI3–RAC1 axis was established in a post-resection recurrence model [50]: GBM-secreted C1QL1 promotes tumor-microtube expansion, synaptic pruning, and malignant synapse formation after surgical removal of the tumor bulk, while pharmacological RAC1 inhibition impedes recurrence in this setting. This study provides the first direct evidence that neuron–tumor network remodeling remains operative—and therapeutically targetable—within the post-surgical niche. Nevertheless, RAC1 is a broadly acting regulator rather than an NGS-specific target; its inhibition therefore does not establish that the antitumor effect arises specifically from NGS disruption. More direct tests are needed: demonstrating necessity requires selective disruption of neuron–glioma synaptic communication followed by delayed recurrence, whereas sufficiency requires that experimental enhancement of NGS accelerate recurrence under otherwise comparable conditions. Critically, such experiments must be performed in faithful post-resection models that reproduce the wound-healing niche—models that are currently scarce and whose development should be a priority for the field.

Therapeutic implications: targeting the tumor–brain interface in the perioperative window

The framework broadens the therapeutic landscape beyond tumor-intrinsic targets. Pharmacological strategies against Glu/Ach signaling or synaptic receptors are one approach, although systemic disruption of neuronal signaling risks interfering with physiological brain function, making selective targeting of tumor-associated synaptic interactions particularly important. Neuromodulation provides a complementary direction: a recent study of an eye–brain neuroimmune stimulation axis prolonged survival in a murine GBM model with reduced pathological neuron–glioma connectivity [51] —proof-of-concept, though not yet a clinical strategy, that modulating neural activity can influence GBM progression. The perioperative period may be a particularly informative window for such interventions, as the recurrent ecosystem is then still being established. The ongoing PerSurge (NOA-30) phase II trial [52] exemplifies this logic, evaluating the AMPAR antagonist perampanel around re-resection in progressive GBM with tumor network connectivity as a translational endpoint. Future perioperative studies should move beyond tumor-growth measurements to directly assess NGS abundance, synaptic connectivity, residual-cell states, and longitudinal recurrence, thereby providing the temporal evidence currently missing between surgery, NGS remodeling, and recurrence.
