Three different surgical methods for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors in real-world practice
Authors: Luming Wang, Jiacong Liu, Wenzhen Xu, Muhu Tang, Yiqing Wang, Wang Lv, Pinghui Xia, Linhai Zhu, Jian Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVideo-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for a relatively large mediastinal tumor (5.0–10.0 cm) remains controversial. In addition, few studies have focused on comparing different surgical approaches for large mediastinal tumors. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the short-term outcomes between subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, and traditional sternotomy for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors.MethodsThe study consecutively enrolled 159 patients with large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm) who received surgery in our hospital between January 2018 and July 2022 (subxiphoid approach VATS: 52 patients, intercostal approach VATS: 70 patients, traditional sternotomy: 37 patients). We analyzed the clinical baseline data, intraoperative and postoperative outcomes, and postoperative complications of all patients. Then the patients were further divided into two groups according to whether there was a peripheral organ (such as pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein) invasion: group A, invasion of the surrounding organ, and group B, no invasion of the surrounding organ. Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes and postoperative complications were also analyzed in group A and group B, respectively.ResultsIn all patients, there were significant differences in blood loss (subxiphoid approach: 33.1 ± 46.0 ml, intercostal approach: 36.9 ± 44.1 ml, sternotomy: 113.0 ± 84.9 ml, P < 0.001) and duration of postoperative oral analgesics (subxiphoid approach: 3.4 ± 0.9 d, intercostal approach: 3.7 ± 1.4 d, sternotomy: 4.5 ± 1.5 d, P = 0.002) among the three methods. In group A, there was a significant difference in blood loss (subxiphoid approach: 50.0 ± 67.7 ml, intercostal approach: 90.0 ± 66.6 ml, sternotomy: 157.9 ± 90.2 ml, P < 0.001) among the three methods. In group B, there were significant differences in the duration of postoperative oral analgesics (subxiphoid approach: 3.2 ± 0.8 d, intercostal approach: 3.7 ± 1.4 d, sternotomy: 4.2 ± 1.1 d, P < 0.05) and blood loss (subxiphoid approach: 22.5 ± 19.3 ml, intercostal approach: 31.9 ± 38.5 ml, sternotomy: 65.6 ± 44.9 ml, P < 0.001) between the three methods. There were no significant differences in the postoperative complications.ConclusionsVATS is an effective, minimally invasive, and safe procedure for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm) without an invasion of the surrounding organs, and maybe a feasible and secure method for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors with an invasion of the surrounding organ (such as the pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein). Subxiphoid approach VATS is a less invasive procedure than intercostal approach VATS and traditional sternotomy due to its reduced blood loss and postoperative pain.Keywords: Large-sized anterior mediastinal tumor, Traditional sternotomy, Subxiphoid approach video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Intercostal approach video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery

Introduction

Currently, complete resection is the standard management for mediastinal tumors [1]. The integrity of mediastinal tumor resection is regarded as the uppermost decisive factor of survival rate, which contributes to the achievement of satisfactory short-term and long-term survival [2, 3]. To date, surgical approaches for the resection of anterior mediastinal tumors include traditional sternotomy and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS).

Traditional sternotomy is still the standard surgery. However, it is more invasive due to its high surgical trauma and postoperative complications [4]. In recent years, minimally invasive surgical approaches, such as VATS, including a lateral intercostal approach and a subxiphoid approach, have been applied treating mediastinal tumors and have gradually become the preferred surgical approach worldwide [5–7]. Compared with traditional sternotomy, VATS was associated with less intraoperative bleeding, shorter chest tube duration and hospital stay, and fewer postoperative complications, with at least comparable oncologic results [8, 9]. The most commonly used minimally invasive surgical technique is the lateral intercostal VATS approach [10]. However, it has some limitations. It might be difficult to make clear visualization of the contralateral phrenic nerve, and it might also lead to intercostal nerve injury and then give rise to postoperative chronic incision pain and numbness [11]. The subxiphoid VATS approach was developed to overcome the limitations of the lateral intercostal approach with better visualization of the anterior mediastinal space, minimal postoperative pain, and satisfactory cosmetic effects [12, 13]. Nevertheless, it also has drawbacks. It is difficult to perform hemostasis, clamping, and suturing with this method when hemorrhage is found in deep areas [14].

The widely accepted indication of VATS for mediastinal tumors is a diameter < 5.0 cm [15]. However, large mediastinal tumors (≥ 5.0 cm) are typically treated by median sternotomy, and whether a relatively large mediastinal tumor (≥ 5.0 cm) should be considered an indication of VATS remains controversial. Kimura et al. and Jurado et al. indicated that VATS may result in pleural dissemination due to capsular violation during manipulation [16, 17]. Nevertheless, Odaka et al. and Weng et al. revealed that VATS was feasible and less invasive for mediastinal tumors > 5.0 cm with comparable surgical and oncological results [18, 19]. To date, there are still few reports focusing on large mediastinal tumors (≥ 5.0 cm) as an indication for VATS. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, these reported clinical studies focused on only a certain kind of surgical approach. However, there have been no comparisons between subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, and traditional sternotomy. Therefore, we launched this retrospective study to assess the short-term outcomes between subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, and traditional sternotomy for large anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm) in the hope of filling this vacancy and exploring the feasibility of performing VATS for large anterior mediastinal tumors.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study included patients with large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors who received subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, or traditional sternotomy at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine from January 2018 to July 2022. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scans were conducted on every patient to identify the size, location, and invasion of adjacent structures of the lesions. We defined a large mediastinal tumor as a maximal tumor diameter measured on CECT scans ≥ 5.0 cm and ≤ 10.0 cm (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2022 IIT No. 1166), and kept in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from patients so that we could utilize their medical record information.

Patients with the following criteria were included: (I) age over 18 and under 80 years; (II) anterior mediastinal tumor with or without an invasion of the pericardium, lung, left innominate vein; (III) tumor diameter ≥ 5.0 cm and ≤ 10.0 cm; (IV) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade I to II; and (V) sufficient organ function and adequate cardiopulmonary function. The main exclusion criteria for patients were (I) absence of pretreatment imaging examination; (II) myasthenia gravis; (III) bleeding disorder; (IV) clinically significant concurrent malignant tumor; (V) invasion of the surrounding organ (except pericardium, lung, and left innominate vein); and (VI) distant metastases.

The surgical indications were as follows: (I) sufficient organ function and adequate cardiopulmonary function to withstand surgery; (II) without bleeding disorder; (III) without an invasion to the surrounding organ (except pericardium, lung, and left innominate vein); (IV) without distant metastases. The choice of surgical method (subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, or traditional sternotomy) was decided by surgeons after a thorough discussion. The surgeons were from the same medical team and received unified training to ensure consistency in surgical treatment.

We obtained data on patients through their regular examinations or treatment in the hospital. Data included the baseline characteristics [age, body mass index (BMI), sex, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), smoking status, drinking status, and comorbidity (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary heart disease), ASA status class, tumor diameter, pathology, and invasion to the surrounding organ], intraoperative and postoperative outcomes [operation time, blood loss, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, drainage duration, volume of drainage, postoperative hospital stay, duration of postoperative oral analgesics, white blood cell count (WBC) in postoperative day 1 (POD 1), c-reactive protein (CRP) in postoperative day 1 (POD 1), R0 resection, and conversion to open surgery], and postoperative complications (pleural effusion, pneumothorax, pulmonary infection, chylothorax, subcutaneous emphysema, arrhythmias, postoperative bleeding, phrenic nerve palsy, and re-surgery). The follow-up continued until three months after surgery. In our study, the main indicators of interest were the blood loss and duration of postoperative oral analgesics [nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), two times daily]. The other indexes of interest were the operation time, WBC, CRP in postoperative day 1, postoperative hospital stay, and postoperative complications.

Surgical approaches

Subxiphoid approach VATS

Under general anesthesia, the patient was intubated using a double-lumen endotracheal tube and placed in a supine position with legs separated and unilateral pulmonary ventilation during surgery. The surgeon stood between the patient’s legs, with the assistant on the patient’s right side. A longitudinal incision was created at the lower edge of the xiphoid as the observation port to introduce the 10-mm, 30-degree rigid thoracoscope. The rectus abdominis muscle was dissected vertically, and the posterior sternal space was then bluntly dissected with the surgeon’s finger to enlarge the working space. Two operation ports were made at the mid-clavicular line that crossed the bilateral costal arch to insert an ultrasonic scalpel and thoracoscopic grasping forceps. The anterior mediastinum was connected to the carbon dioxide (CO2) instrument to establish an artificial pneumomediastinum, maintaining the pressure at 8 cmH2O to expand the posterior sternal space and facilitate the operation. Both sides of the mediastinal pleura were opened to fully expose the intrathoracic vessels and phrenic nerves. En-bloc resection of the tumor with the surrounding fat tissues and invaded organs (such as the pericardium, lung, and left innominate vein) was performed during the operation. If the subxiphoid approach was not feasible, we changed it to the transthoracic approach or open sternotomy. All specimens were placed in a plastic bag and removed by subxiphoid incision. The length of the subxiphoid incision was adjusted according to the size of the specimen. A drainage tube was inserted into the mediastinum via one subcostal arch port and was removed when there was no air leakage and the volume of drainage was < 200 mL within 24 h. (Fig. 2)

Intercostal approach VATS

Under general anesthesia, the patient was intubated using a double-lumen endotracheal tube for split-lung ventilation during surgery and placed in a semi-lateral decubitus position at 30–45 degrees with the cranial and caudal sides down. This approach was performed through three ports: one observation port and two operation ports. The choice of these ports depended on the location and size of the tumor and the surgeon’s preference. During the operation, the tumor with the surrounding fat tissues as well as involved organs was resected. If the intercostal approach was not feasible, we changed it to the transthoracic approach or open sternotomy. All specimens were removed into a plastic bag. A drainage tube was inserted into the thoracic cavity and was removed when there was no air leakage and the volume of drainage was < 200 mL within 24 h.

Traditional sternotomy

The patient was placed in the supine position with arms tucked in the body under general anesthesia and was intubated with a double-lumen endotracheal tube for unilateral pulmonary ventilation during surgery. Through complete or partial sternotomy, the tumor with the surrounding fat tissues and invaded organs were resected en-bloc. One chest tube was left in the patient for drainage and was removed when there was no air leakage and the volume of drainage was < 200 mL within 24 h.

Statistical analysis

We expressed categorical variables in the form of frequencies and percentages, and differences between groups were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. We expressed continuous variables as the mean and standard deviation, and differences between groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal‒Wallis test. All analyses were performed with SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From January 2018 to July 2022, a total of 159 patients were included in this study: 52 patients underwent subxiphoid approach VATS, 70 underwent intercostal approach VATS, and 37 patients underwent traditional sternotomy. The baseline clinical characteristics among the three groups are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age, BMI, sex, FEV1, FVC, smoking status, drinking status, comorbidity, ASA status class, tumor diameter, and pathology. There were more patients suffering an invasion of the surrounding organ in the traditional sternotomy group (P < 0.001). As a result, after analyzing the intraoperative outcomes, postoperative outcomes, and postoperative complications of all patients, we classified them into two groups and conducted the same analysis: group A, invasion to the surrounding organ (pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein), and group B, no invasion to the surrounding organ. Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes

In all patients, there were significant differences in blood loss (subxiphoid approach: 33.1 ± 46.0 ml, intercostal approach: 36.9 ± 44.1 ml, sternotomy: 113.0 ± 84.9 ml, P < 0.001), volume of drainage (subxiphoid approach: 671.9 ± 835.6 ml, intercostal approach: 423.2 ± 404.1 ml, sternotomy: 804.6 ± 806.8 ml, P = 0.0027), and duration of postoperative oral analgesics (subxiphoid approach: 3.4 ± 0.9 d, intercostal approach: 3.7 ± 1.4 d, sternotomy: 4.5 ± 1.5 d, P = 0.002) among the three methods (Table 2). No significant differences could be seen in the operation time, ICU stay, drainage duration, postoperative hospital stays, WBC on postoperative Day 1, and CRP count on postoperative Day 1 (P > 0.05). The R0 resection rate of these three methods was 100%. There was no patient conversion to open surgery in the subxiphoid approach VATS and the intercostal approach VATS.

In group B (no invasion to the surrounding organ), there were significant differences in the operation time (subxiphoid approach: 129.0 ± 45.8 min, intercostal approach: 86.5 ± 33.7 min, sternotomy: 103.4 ± 30.2 min, P < 0.001), blood loss (subxiphoid approach: 22.5 ± 19.3 ml, intercostal approach: 31.9 ± 38.5 ml, sternotomy: 65.6 ± 44.9 ml, P < 0.001), duration of postoperative oral analgesics (subxiphoid approach: 3.2 ± 0.8 d, intercostal approach: 3.7 ± 1.4 d, sternotomy: 4.2 ± 1.1 d, P < 0.05) and WBC count on postoperative Day 1 (subxiphoid approach: 9.1 ± 2.4 10E9/L, intercostal approach: 10.6 ± 3.1 10E9/L, sternotomy: 11.4 ± 2.7 10E9/L, P < 0.05) among the three groups (Table 3). No significant differences were found in the ICU stay, drainage duration, volume of drainage, postoperative hospital stay, and CRP count on postoperative Day 1 (P > 0.05).

Postoperative complications

In all patients, the incidence of overall complications was similar and no significant differences were seen in postoperative complications among the three methods (P > 0.05) (Table 4). In group A (invasion to the surrounding organ), we only found a significant difference in terms of pneumothorax (P = 0.036) and there were no significant differences in the other postoperative complications (Table 5). In group B (no invasion to the surrounding organ), the incidence of complications was similar and we found no significant differences in postoperative complications between the three methods (P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

Recently, minimally invasive surgery has replaced traditional sternotomy in the treatment of mediastinal tumors. Although traditional sternotomy can offer good surgical vision and allow for en bloc resection of the tumor and invaded surrounding tissues, especially for advanced patients, it is associated with large wounds, massive intraoperative blood loss, increased postoperative pain, and high postoperative complications [13, 19, 20]. VATS with a lateral intercostal approach is characterized by a relatively inconspicuous wound because the incision is small and located at the lateral side [21]. Several studies have demonstrated that it could yield less intraoperative blood loss, shorter chest tube duration, hospital stay, and fewer postoperative complications, with at least comparable oncologic results, compared with traditional sternotomy [8, 9]. However, it also has some drawbacks. There exists a difficulty in discerning the contralateral phrenic nerve, and it is associated with postoperative chronic incision pain and numbness because of intercostal nerve injury [11]. VATS with a subxiphoid approach was developed to avoid intercostal nerve injury and reduce postoperative pain. Several studies have shown that it could achieve minimal postoperative pain and satisfactory cosmetic effects compared with an intercostal approach VATS [12, 13]. It can provide a wider surgical scope, fully expose bilateral phrenic nerves and bilateral intramammary vessels, and observe the upper mediastinum, especially the left innominate vein [22, 23]. We can use the sternal retractor to achieve better exposure to the innominate vein. Even if there is a damage on the innominate vein, or there is a need to cut off the innominate vein, it is easier to handle compared with the lateral intercostal approach. Moreover, it is convenient to take large specimens. Because there is no bone structure at the lower edge of the xiphoid, the incision can be extended and relatively large specimens can be taken out. In the present study, we compared the short-term results between subxiphoid approach VATS, intercostal approach VATS, and traditional sternotomy for anterior mediastinal tumors. We found that the duration of postoperative oral analgesics and blood loss of the subxiphoid approach were less than those of the other two methods. Thus, we concluded that the subxiphoid approach VATS was a less invasive procedure than the intercostal approach VATS and traditional sternotomy. The operation time of the subxiphoid approach was longer than that of the other two procedures. This may be due to the surgical team’s lack of experience and inherent learning curve. Further follow-up is necessary to confirm the long-term and oncological results of these three methods in the treatment of mediastinal tumors.

Until now, there has been no consensus on the maximum mediastinal tumor size in which VATS can be performed. The widely accepted indication of VATS for mediastinal tumors is < 5.0 cm in diameter [15]. However, VATS for a relatively large mediastinal tumor (≥ 5.0 cm) remains controversial. In general, large mediastinal tumors are treated by median sternotomy. Kimura et al. and Jurado et al. indicated that VATS may result in pleural dissemination due to capsular violation during manipulation [16, 17]. Nevertheless, Takeo et al. demonstrated that VATS was appropriate for patients with mediastinal tumors (≥ 5.0 cm) [15]. Gossot et al. revealed that VATS was feasible and safe for patients with bulky intrathoracic tumors (median diameter: 9.0 cm) [24]. Odaka et al. and Weng et al. found that VATS was less invasive for mediastinal tumors > 5.0 cm with comparable surgical and oncological results [18, 19]. These findings were by our study. In our study, we demonstrated that VATS was a practicable, less invasive procedure and safe for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm) with improved postoperative outcomes. In comparison to traditional sternotomy, VATS was associated with less blood loss, less WBC on postoperative Day 1, and shorter duration of postoperative oral analgesics in group B. Moreover, there was no patient conversion to open surgery and the R0 resection of VATS was 100% in our study, which ensured its safety and the completeness of tumor resection. Additionally, further follow-up is needed to confirm the long-term and oncological results of VATS and traditional sternotomy for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm). In summary, a relatively large mediastinal tumor (5.0–10.0 cm) without invading important structures should be considered an indication of VATS from our perspective.

In our actual clinical practice, VATS is still considered for patients with an invasion of the surrounding organ (such as the pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein). In our opinion, we can easily complete partial pericardiectomy, innominate vein dissection or resection, and pulmonary wedge resection through minimally invasive surgery. Therefore, VATS can be used not only for early mediastinal tumors but also for some locally invasive potentially resectable mediastinal tumors. In addition, the real condition observed intraoperatively may be inconsistent with the image performance evaluated before the operation. Of course, we always keep in mind that safe and complete surgical resection is the primary task for the treatment of mediastinal tumors. If complete resection could not be achieved by VATS, we switched to traditional sternotomy. In our study, we recruited some patients with an invasion to the surrounding organ (such as the pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein), which was different from other studies [13, 18]. We found VATS was associated with less blood loss and WBC on postoperative Day 1 compared with traditional sternotomy in group A. There was no patient conversion to open surgery and the R0 resection of VATS was 100%. A significant difference was seen in the duration of postoperative oral analgesics within all patients and Group B, but there was no significant difference in that within Group A. We explained that the sample of intercostal approach VATS in Group A was small, which may affect the result of postoperative oral analgesic. Therefore, VATS may be a feasible and secure method for mediastinal tumors with an invasion of the surrounding organ (such as the pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein), but this remains to be discussed due to the small sample size. More samples are necessary to confirm this.

Some limitations of our study include the small sample size, retrospective nature, short postoperative follow-up period, and heterogeneity of patients and treatment plans. These deficiencies may restrict the statistical power of our study and give rise to selection biases. We consecutively enrolled patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria, thereby eliminating selection bias to some extent and rendering the results representative.

In summary, VATS is an effective, minimally invasive, and safe procedure for large-sized anterior mediastinal tumors (5.0–10.0 cm) without an invasion of the surrounding organ. VATS may be a feasible and secure method for mediastinal tumors with an invasion of the surrounding organ (such as the pericardium, lung, or left innominate vein), but this remains to be discussed due to the small sample size. Subxiphoid approach VATS is a less invasive procedure than intercostal approach VATS and traditional sternotomy due to its reduced blood loss and postoperative pain. However, further follow-up and randomized controlled trials with larger scales are needed to confirm our results and evaluate long-term and oncologic outcomes.
