Bootstrapping the 3d Ising Stress Tensor
Cyuan-Han Chang 1,2 , Vasiliy Dommes 1 , Rajeev S. Erramilli 3 , Alexandre Homrich 4,5 , Petr Kravchuk 6 , Aike Liu 1 , Matthew S. Mitchell 7 , David Poland 7 , David Simmons-Duffin 1

Abstract
We compute observables of the critical 3d Ising model to high precision by applying the numerical conformal bootstrap to mixed correlators of the leading scalar operators σ \sigma and ϵ \epsilon , and the stress tensor T μ ​ ν T_{\mu\nu} . We obtain new precise determinations of scaling dimensions ( Δ σ , Δ ϵ ) = ( 0.518148806 ​ ( 24 ) , 1.41262528 ​ ( 29 ) ) (\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})=(0.518148806(24),1.41262528(29)) as well as OPE coefficients involving σ \sigma , ϵ \epsilon , and T μ ​ ν T_{\mu\nu} . We also describe several improvements made along the way to algorithms and software tools for the numerical bootstrap.

Along the way we have significantly improved several software tools and algorithms used for the numerical bootstrap. Experience has shown that bootstrap bounds tend to converge as one increases the derivative order, but these converged bounds are
not
exactly saturated by physical theories (though they may be nearly saturated). In other words, there is a limit to the strength of bounds extracted from a fixed, finite set of crossing equations. In the context of the hyperbolic bootstrap
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, there are essentially rigorous arguments that this non-saturation occurs
[
31
]
. Thus, if the bootstrap is to become a practical tool for solving a theory, we
must
explore larger and larger systems of crossing equations. We view our results as more evidence (along with
[
10
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
) that this is a fruitful thing to do!

Linear independence of structures

We emphasize that it is important to ensure that three-point structures for a given channel are linearly-independent for
all
allowed values of
\Delta
.
4
4

4

We observed empirically that one only needs to ensure linear independence for the values of
\Delta
allowed by unitarity and the gap assumptions. Presumably this happens because in
SDPB
the conformal blocks are eventually sampled at a large number of allowed values of
\Delta
and polynomially interpolated. This interpolation will very accurately reproduce any degeneracies in the allowed region of
\Delta
, but will likely break any degeneracies outside of this region where the polynomial interpolation is of poor quality.

Without care, it can happen that the space spanned by a naïve choice of conserved structures is degenerate at specific values of
\Delta
. This occurs, for example, if one solves the conservation equations in
Mathematica
in a generic basis.

These comments include the special case where structures have different asymptotic behaviors in the limit of
\Delta\to\infty
. When this happens, the subleading structures effectively become zero, so we have a linear dependence at
\Delta=\infty
. To address this issue, we choose to normalize our structures so that they all grow as
O(1)
as
\Delta\to\infty
.

When we first set up the
\langle TTTT\rangle
system, we hadn’t appreciated the significance of this requirement. We found to our frustration that
SDPB
never terminated with
dual feasible jump detected
. We eventually realized that any finite linear combination of functionals in our chosen basis would always lead to a degenerate matrix at some
\Delta
(including
\Delta=\infty
), so the solver could never find a solution where all matrices are strictly positive definite, a prerequisite for a dual feasible jump. Dual jumps are essential for efficient checkpointing
[
43
]
, and consequently for efficient use of the OPE cutting surface algorithm
[
32
]
. Thus, ensuring linear independence of three-point structures is a requirement for this kind of numerical study.

2.2
Ward identities

Some three-point functions involving the stress tensor satisfy Ward identities, which can be seen by integrating
T
over a surface to obtain conformal generators. For example, the dilatation operator on a surface
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle D(\Sigma)=-\int_{\Sigma}dS_{\mu}x_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu}.

(4)

For a three-point function
\langle\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}T\rangle
, choosing
\Sigma
to surround one of the
\mathcal{O}
’s then gives a Ward identity of the form

\displaystyle-\int_{\Sigma}dS_{\mu}x_{\nu}\langle\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}T^{\mu\nu}\rangle=\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\rangle,

(5)

which relates the OPE coefficients
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}T}
to
\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
.

The two-point function of stress tensors can be parametrized as

\displaystyle\langle TT\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{T}\frac{H_{12}^{2}}{X_{12}^{5}},

(6)

where
H_{12}
and
X_{12}
are standard embedding-space structures
[
39
]
, and
c_{T}
is called the ‘‘central charge”.
5
5

5

This is standard but imprecise terminology, since unlike in 2d,
c_{T}
is not actually the central charge of a symmetry algebra.
The three-point function of stress tensors takes the form

\displaystyle\langle TTT\rangle

\displaystyle=n_{B}\langle TTT\rangle_{B}+n_{F}\langle TTT\rangle_{F},

(7)

where
\langle TTT\rangle_{B}
and
\langle TTT\rangle_{F}
denote three-point functions in the theories of a free real scalar and a free Majorana fermion, respectively. The Ward identity (
5
) then implies that
c_{T}=c_{B}n_{B}+c_{F}n_{F}=c_{B}(n_{B}+n_{F})
, where

\displaystyle c_{B}=c_{F}=\frac{3}{2(4\pi)^{2}}

(8)

are the central charges of the free scalar and free Majorana fermion theories. The average null energy condition constrains
n_{B}
and
n_{F}
to be nonnegative:
n_{B}\geq 0
and
n_{F}\geq 0
, with saturation if and only if the theory is free
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

In our numerical setup, it is useful to work in conventions where the two-point function of the stress tensor is unit-normalized. Let us therefore define the normalized stress tensor

\displaystyle\widehat{T}\equiv\frac{T}{\sqrt{c_{T}}},\qquad\langle\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\rangle=\frac{H_{12}^{2}}{X_{12}^{5}}.

(9)

Three-point functions of
\widehat{T}
are given by

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{B}\langle\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\rangle_{B}+\lambda_{F}\langle\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\rangle_{F},

(10)

where
\langle\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\widehat{T}\rangle_{B,F}
are the corresponding 3-point functions in free theories, and

\displaystyle\lambda_{B}\equiv\frac{n_{B}}{(c_{T}/c_{B})^{3/2}}=\frac{n_{B}}{(n_{B}+n_{F})^{3/2}},\qquad\lambda_{F}\equiv\frac{n_{F}}{(c_{T}/c_{F})^{3/2}}=\frac{n_{F}}{(n_{B}+n_{F})^{3/2}}.

(11)

Note that these normalized coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{B}+\lambda_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n_{B}+n_{F})^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{T}/c_{B}}}.

(12)

Three-point correlators involving two scalars and a stress tensor are also constrained by Ward identities. For any scalar
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\phi\phi\widehat{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{\sqrt{c_{T}/c_{B}}}|0,2\rangle=-\Delta_{\phi}(\lambda_{F}+\lambda_{B})|0,2\rangle,

(13)

where
|0,2\rangle
denotes an
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{r}
structure
[
37
,
38
]
. On the right-hand side, we used the identity (
12
). The fact that
\lambda_{B},\lambda_{F}
appear linearly in Ward identities is convenient for our implementation: we can treat them like any other independent OPE coefficients, with the subtlety that they appear in multiple three-point correlators.

For the rest of the paper we implicitly assume unit-normalized stress tensors and therefore dispense with the hat notation.

2.3
Four-point structures and crossing equations

Next, we must establish a complete set of independent crossing equations for the four-point correlators in the
T\sigma\epsilon
system.

Four-point tensor structures

To describe four-point tensor structures we use the
q
-basis of
[
35
]
, as it does not suffer from redundancies which plague embedding-space bases
[
39
,
36
]
. A
q
-basis four-point tensor structure is denoted by
[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}
, where
q_{i}\in\{-\ell_{i},-\ell_{i}+1,\dots,+\ell_{i}\}
and
\ell_{i}
is the spin of the operator at position
i
. The sign
\pm
indicates the charge of the tensor structure under exchange of the cross-ratios
z\leftrightarrow\overline{z}
.

A general four-point function can be expanded as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}\rangle=\sum_{\{q_{i}\},\pm}[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}g^{[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}(z,\overline{z}).

(14)

The functions
g^{[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}}(z,\overline{z})
satisfy
g^{[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}(\overline{z},z)=\pm g^{[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}(z,\overline{z})
. We refer the reader to
[
35
]
for details. We also define coordinates
x,t
by
6
6

6

Note that these are different from the
x,t
-coordinates defined by
blocks_3d

[
38
]
.

\displaystyle z=x+it,\quad\overline{z}=x-it.

(15)

Permutation symmetries of the four-point functions split into crossing transformations and kinematic permutations. The latter are permutations that preserve the cross-ratios, see
[
35
]
for details. The group
\Pi
of kinematic permutations is given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{\langle TTTT\rangle}

\displaystyle=\{\text{id},(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\},

(16)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\langle TT\sigma\sigma\rangle}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\langle TT\epsilon\epsilon\rangle}=\{\text{id},(12)(34)\},

(17)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\langle T\epsilon\sigma\sigma\rangle}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\langle TTT\epsilon\rangle}=\Pi_{\langle T\epsilon\epsilon\epsilon\rangle}=\{\text{id}\}.

(18)

with analogous expressions for other orderings. Any
q
-basis structure can be symmetrized under
\Pi
, and we denote symmetrization of
[q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}]^{\pm}
by
\langle q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}\rangle^{\pm}
. Four-point functions can now be written as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}\rangle=\sum_{\{q_{i}\}/\Pi,\pm}\langle q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}\rangle^{\pm}g^{\langle q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}\rangle^{\pm}}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}(z,\overline{z}).

(19)

Finally, we can account for space parity by restricting the sum to those
q
for which the structure
\langle q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}\rangle^{\pm}
has the desired parity. The parity is always even in our setup, which restricts the sum
\sum_{i}q_{i}
to be even.

We do not provide the explicit lists of the more than one hundred four-point tensor structures. Instead, we provide some counting data in table

Conservation

The four-point functions must satisfy the conservation equations for
T
,

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}\cdots\rangle=0.

(20)

These are differential equations for each four-point function involving
T
, which have many components due to the index
\nu
and the spin indices of other insertions. These equations heavily constrain the functional degrees of freedom of our four-point functions. As we explain below, it is necessary to properly account for these functional degrees when choosing which crossing equations to include in a bootstrap computation. Due to the conformal invariance of the conservation operator
\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}
, the left-hand side can be expanded in a basis of conformally-invariant four-point tensor structures for a correlator in which
T
is replaced by a vector operator.

Using the expansion (
19
) the conservation equations can then be converted to systems of first-order partial differential equations for the functions
g^{\langle q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}\rangle^{\pm}}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}
.
7
7

7

Since the
q

[
41
]
.
Denoting the full set of such coefficient functions as a vector
\vec{g}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}
, one can express the collection of differential equations as

\displaystyle\left(M_{1}\partial_{t}+M_{2}\partial_{x}+M_{3}\right)\cdot\vec{g}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}=0,

(21)

where
M_{i}
are

M_{1}

M_{i}
has a non-trivial kernel, so in general we must specify some number of components of
\vec{g}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{3}}

x,t

t=0

The initial data at
t=0

N
be a matrix whose rows span the left null space of
M_{1}
. Multiplying (
21

N

\displaystyle\left(NM_{2}\partial_{x}+NM_{3}\right)\cdot\vec{g}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}=0,

(22)

which at
t=0
can be seen as a constraint that analogously reduces some of the initial data from “line” degrees of freedom to “point” degrees of freedom.

Yet further but nonetheless important constraints arise at
t=0
as this is a locus of enhanced symmetry: when
t=0
the four operators lie all on one circle. It can be shown that this forces some low-order

\vec{g}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{3}\mathcal{O}_{4}}
to vanish at

The complete enumeration of bulk, line, and point degrees of freedom is precisely the set of functional degrees of freedom on which we impose our crossing equations. In practice, the bulk degrees of freedom enter more or less as normal with the usual list of derivatives in our expansion, while the line and point degrees of freedom enter with just the derivatives along
z=\overline{z}
and without any derivatives, respectively. A summary of the various degrees of freedom in each of the four-point functions can be found in table

Linear independence of crossing equations

We must emphasize that it is strictly necessary to reduce the set of crossing equations to the minimal set of independent equations. Naïvely, one might expect that imposing additional crossing equations might at most slow down numerical computations, but ultimately would lead to equivalent and correct bounds on CFT data. In practice, we found that the presence of spurious crossing equations lead to nearly-flat directions in the SDP, begetting numerical instabilities which ultimately can lead to erroneously ruling out part of theory space. In fact, with typical parameters, it is sufficient to mistakenly impose “line equations” on the
\langle TTT\epsilon\rangle
point degrees of freedom (i.e., introduce a handful of extra derivatives to our functional) to have the appearance of excluding the full parameter space of the Ising CFT.

3.1
Generating SDPs

Semidefinite programs (SDPs) for bootstrapping correlators of
\sigma,\epsilon,T
are somewhat complex, and we needed to devote significant engineering effort to generating them efficiently. Two ideas are particularly important in our implementation:

•

Parallelism. Recall that
SDPB
scales well to several nodes and a large number of cores. (For example, each of our
\Lambda=43
results were computed on 16 nodes with 128 cores each, for a total of 2048 cores.) In order to ensure that
generating
SDPs did not become a bottleneck, we needed to achieve similar scaling for computing conformal blocks and assembling them into
SDPB
input. One way to improve parallelism is to, whenever possible, break computations into independent “chunks” that can be handled separately on different cores. However, one must also be careful to control the memory usage of each chunk, since otherwise the number of simultaneous chunks could become memory limited, degrading overall performance.

•

Memoization.
8
8

8

This is not a typo, see
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoization

We take care to recompute data only when necessary. For example, conformal blocks appearing in the four-point function of stress tensors
\langle TTTT\rangle
remain unchanged when parameters like
\Delta_{\sigma}
and
\Delta_{\epsilon}
are varied. Thus, we pre-compute them and store them in a central location for re-use. As another example, during an OPE scan, only OPE coefficients of external operators are changing — the remaining conformal blocks and crossing equations are unchanged, so we take care to only re-compute the contribution to an SDP from the external operator channel.

3.2
Optimizing
blocks_3d

A major contribution to SDP generation time comes from computing conformal blocks. We perform these computations using the program
blocks_3d

[
37
,
38
]
, which is capable of constructing numerical approximations to arbitrary conformal blocks in
d=3
CFTs.

Prior to this work,
blocks_3d
has been used in
[
38
,
34
,
18
,
48
,
49
]
in bootstrap problems involving spinors and spin-1 currents, for which its performance proved to be adequate. In our setup, however, we found it necessary to implement significant improvements to the design of
blocks_3d
. This was partly due to the need to frequently generate very complex conformal blocks (for instance,
\langle\epsilon TTT\rangle
blocks need to be generated anew for every value of
\Delta_{\epsilon}
), and partly due to the memory constraints of the clusters available to us.

In order to explain these improvements, we must give a brief overview of the function of
blocks_3d
. A single run of
blocks_3d
computes multiple conformal blocks, which all share the same external operator representations, four-point structure label, as well as the
j_{12}
and
j_{43}
parts of the three-point function labels. The program outputs conformal blocks with all possible values of
j_{120}
and
j_{430}
, where the index
0
refers to the exchanged operator, as well as all values of the exchanged spin
j
up to a cutoff
j_{\text{max}}
.

For each conformal block, an array of derivatives is returned, with each derivative approximated by a rational function of the exchanged scaling dimension
\Delta
. These approximations are computed using Zamolodchikov-like recursion relations derived in
[
37
]
, see
[
50
,
51
,
10
,
52
]
for earlier work. A starting point for the recursion is the function
h_{\infty,j}(z,\overline{z})
, given by a known analytic formula
[
53
,
37
]
, which controls the
\Delta\to\infty
limit of the conformal block.

We denote the conformal block schematically by
g
. Prior to this work, the algorithm of
blocks_3d
that computes
g
could be described roughly as follows.

•

In the first stage, the derivatives
\partial^{m}_{r}\partial^{n}_{\lambda}g
are computed.
12
12

12

Here,
r=\sqrt{\rho\overline{\rho}}
,
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(\rho/\overline{\rho})
, and
\rho=z/(1+\sqrt{1-z})^{2}
.
For this, the following steps are taken.

–

The derivatives
\partial_{r}^{m}\partial_{\lambda}^{n}h_{\infty,j}
are evaluated.

–

The derivatives
\partial_{r}^{m}\partial_{\lambda}^{n}h_{\infty,j}
are used as a starting point for the recursive computation of the derivatives
\partial^{m}_{r}\partial^{n}_{\lambda}g
. The recursions for different values of
n
are completely independent, and are performed in parallel.

–

For some conformal blocks, only the
\partial^{m}_{r}\partial^{0}_{\lambda}g
derivatives are required. In this case, only one recursion needs to be performed.

•

In the second stage, the derivatives
\partial^{m}_{r}\partial^{n}_{\lambda}g
are converted to the desired basis of derivatives (
x
,
t
-derivatives in our case).

Upon analyzing the performance of this algorithm as implemented in
blocks_3d
, we discovered several issues.

•

The evaluation of the derivatives
\partial_{r}^{m}\partial_{\lambda}^{n}h_{\infty,j}
(the base case) was taking up the majority of the computation time.

•

The parallelization by different values of
n
had significant drawbacks. Firstly, the number of different values of
n
for derivative order
\Lambda
is roughly
\Lambda/2
, which is much smaller than the typical number of cores available on a compute node (compare
\Lambda=43
to a typical 128-core machine).

•

The recursion part of the code used a significant amount of memory. Running
\Lambda/2
recursions simultaneously means that the memory consumption is multiplied by a factor of around 20 for
\Lambda=43
. This meant that such high-
\Lambda
calculations could not fit into the memory of a single machine.

•

An implementation error led to all derivatives
\partial^{m}_{r}\partial^{n}_{\lambda}g
being computed in all cases, even when only
n=0
was requested.
13
13

13

This was a minor performance loss in previous studies, but it makes a significant difference in our current study.

To address these issues, we implemented the following optimizations.

•

We significantly optimized the code that evaluates
\partial_{r}^{m}\partial_{\lambda}^{n}h_{\infty,j}
by adding memoization of some intermediate expressions (in addition to the memoization that was already in place).

•

We revamped the structure of parallelism in
blocks_3d
. Specifically, different values of
n
are now processed sequentially, and we parallelize the recursion step instead. This leads to much better scalability and dramatically improves memory usage by avoiding memory-intensive tasks running in parallel. This new structure also allowed us to parallelize the computation of
h_{\infty,j}
, leading to further performance gains.

•

As the restructuring required a significant refactoring of the codebase, we took the opportunity to implement many further minor optimizations. In particular, some memory-intensive memoizations have been removed without any negative impact on performance.

•

We fixed the implementation errors in the case when only
n=0
derivatives were requested.

These optimizations were implemented over a period of time of continuous development, and we were using the software the entire time. As a result, we have not performed a systematic comparison of the original version to the final version used in this project. However, in comparisons between intermediate versions we observed, for reasonably representative runs, a more than 10
\times
improvement in memory usage and a similar improvement in computation (wall) time. Note that the improvements in memory usage also mean that more
blocks_3d
instances can run on a single machine. This is parallelism with nearly perfect scaling, unlike the parallelization within a single
blocks_3d
instance,
14
14

14

This does not contradict the previously stated importance of this internal parallelization.
further reducing the time needed to generate all conformal blocks.

3.3
Improvements to SDPB

Our work benefited from major performance improvements in SDPB 3.0.
15
15

15

https://github.com/davidsd/sdpb/releases/tag/3.0.0
Here, we give a brief summary of the main changes. One of the most expensive global operations in each SDPB iteration is computing the matrix

Q\equiv P^{T}P
, where

\displaystyle P\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}P_{1}\\
P_{2}\\
\vdots\\
P_{J}\end{array}\right)

is a dense tall matrix of multiprecision numbers, and each submatrix
P_{i}
is calculated independently from a single positivity constraint. In this section, we refer to a positivity constraint as a “block,” since it usually comes from the contribution of a particular channel to a set of crossing equations.

To compute
Q=P^{T}P
, one naïve approach is to compute the local contribution
Q_{i}\equiv P_{i}^{T}P_{i}
from each block independently and then call reduce-scatter among cores to aggregate the results into the global distributed matrix
Q=\sum_{i}Q_{i}
. This algorithm was originally implemented in SDPB 2.0 and worked well for smaller problems.
However, it turned out to be a bottleneck as we proceeded to larger problems: reduce-scatter could take about 50% of total execution time and thus ruined performance scaling for multi-node computations (see figure
3
).

In SDPB 3.0, two of us (VD and DSD) implemented a new matrix multiplication algorithm utilizing BLAS, the Chinese Remainder Theorem and MPI shared memory windows. Firstly, we now compute the contribution to
Q
from the blocks on each node, and then perform reduce-scatter among nodes. This requires much less communication than reduce-scatter among cores and results in much better scaling.
Secondly, we made matrix multiplication on a node several times faster by utilizing highly efficient BLAS routines, which was not previously possible with multiprecision matrices.
To this aim, we split each multiprecision matrix
P^{\prime}
into several double-precision matrices
P^{\prime}_{(k)}
, where
P^{\prime}
is the part of the matrix
P
stored on a given node.
The matrices
P^{\prime}_{(k)}
are stored in a shared memory window, so that all cores on a node work together to calculate
Q^{\prime}_{(k)}=P^{\prime T}_{(k)}P^{\prime}_{(k)}
using BLAS.
Then we restore the multiprecision matrix
Q^{\prime}=P^{\prime T}P^{\prime}
from
Q^{\prime}_{(k)}
using the Chinese Remainder Theorem and call reduce-scatter to synchronize the result between nodes.

Figure

(This computation requires at least 4 Expanse nodes to fit in memory.) One can see that SDPB 3.0 is more than 2.5x faster than the previous version at 4 nodes, and scales well even for 10 nodes, achieving a factor of 6 speedup in that case.
A detailed description of the new matrix multiplication algorithm can be found at

3.4
Approximating conformal blocks with interpolation

In the course of this project we also found and implemented an improved approach to approximating conformal blocks using polynomials, leading to better-conditioned and more numerically stable SDPs. A commonly-used approach in the numerical bootstrap has been the following procedure
[
51
]
:

1.

Truncate the radial coordinate expansion of a conformal block at some order, giving at the crossing-symmetric point an approximation
g_{\Delta}\approx r^{\Delta}\frac{P(\Delta)}{\Pi_{p}(\Delta-\Delta_{p})}
with a polynomial numerator
P(\Delta)
and set of poles
\Delta_{p}
.

2.

Choose a subset of poles according to a parameter
\kappa
, and perform “pole-shifting,” where we approximate
R(\Delta)=\frac{P(\Delta)}{\Pi_{p}(\Delta-\Delta_{p})}
in terms of a lower-degree rational function
S(\Delta)
with only the subset of poles. This is usually done by imposing that some number of derivatives of
S(\Delta)
around
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\Delta=\infty
should match the corresponding derivatives of
R(\Delta)
.

We found that this procedure worked poorly in practice for our SDPs at high derivative order. The “pole-shifting” approximation was not accurate enough. In order to make it more accurate, we had to significantly increase
\kappa
, but this led to larger degree polynomials, worse performance, and less numerical stability (forcing us to increase precision further, degrading performance even more).

In our new approach, we approximate conformal blocks using interpolating polynomials, with a choice of sample points specially adapted to the class of functionals we wish to describe. In practice, this leads to much more accurate approximations with much lower degree polynomials, improving both numerical stability and performance. We will describe the details of our approach in a separate publication
[
54
]
.
16
16

16

The new interpolation scheme is already implemented and publicly available in the tool
pmp2sdp
that is bundled with
sdpb
.

3.5
Navigator and skydiving

The navigator function
[
55
]
and skydiving algorithm
[
56
]
are approaches to optimizing SDPs that depend on parameters
\vec{x}
(e.g. dimensions and OPE coefficients of “external” operators). The navigator function
\mathcal{N}(\vec{x})
is positive for disallowed points and negative for allowed points. Thus, one can then use gradient information about
\mathcal{N}(\vec{x})
to guide the search for allowed points. The skydiving algorithm is a way to simultaneously solve for
\mathcal{N}(\vec{x})
while moving in
\vec{x}
-space, potentially allowing one to find allowed points with a single
sdpb
run. Both ideas can potentially significantly reduce the number of
sdpb
iterations needed to map out an allowed space. However, a tradeoff is that one must typically build a very large number of conformal blocks and SDPs, since the external parameters
\vec{x}
change many times.

In this work, we explored using skydiving with the generalized free field (GFF) navigator of
[
55
]
.
Our external parameters are:

\displaystyle\vec{x}=\left(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon},\frac{\lambda_{B}}{\lambda_{TT\epsilon}},\frac{\lambda_{F}}{\lambda_{TT\epsilon}},\frac{\lambda_{\sigma\sigma\epsilon}}{\lambda_{TT\epsilon}},\frac{\lambda_{\epsilon\epsilon\epsilon}}{\lambda_{TT\epsilon}}\right).

(27)

We ran the skydiving algorithm with three different objectives:

1.

Minimize
\mathcal{N}(\vec{x})
to find a point deep in a bounded feasible region.

2.

Maximize
\Delta_{\sigma}
to bound the island from the right.

3.

Minimize
\Delta_{\sigma}
to bound the island from the left.

In practice, we found that skydiving was effective at lower derivative orders
\Lambda=11,19
(and partially at
\Lambda=27,35
). However, at higher derivative orders, the cost of generating many SDPs became prohibitive. It was more efficient to spend computational time in
sdpb
than in generating many new SDPs. Moreover, the gradient descent cannot be easily parallelized. Hence, at higher derivative orders we preferred the “cutting surface” algorithm and Delaunay triangulation approach of
[
32
]
.

4.1
Review: cutting surface algorithm

The crossing equations for
T,\sigma,\epsilon
system, when expanded in conformal blocks, contain contributions from the external operators
T,\sigma,\epsilon
themselves. Schematically, this can be written as

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I}\vec{\lambda}+\dots=0,

(28)

where

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}=(\lambda_{B},\lambda_{F},\lambda_{\sigma\sigma\epsilon},\lambda_{\epsilon\epsilon\epsilon},\lambda_{TT\epsilon})^{T}

(29)

is the vector of OPE coefficients involving the external operators and
\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I}
is a symmetric
5\times 5
matrix constructed out of derivatives of conformal blocks, and
I
labels different components of the crossing equations.

The basic approach in numerical bootstrap involves searching for a linear functional
\alpha_{I}
such that, among other constraints, the matrix
\alpha_{I}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I}
is positive semi-definite,

\displaystyle\alpha_{I}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I}\succeq 0.

(30)

Acting with such an
\alpha_{I}
on the crossing equations we get

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}^{T}(\alpha_{I}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I})\vec{\lambda}+\dots=0.

(31)

The constraint (
30
) ensures that the first term is non-negative. The other constraints on
\alpha_{I}
ensure that all the remaining terms are non-negative and that there is at least one positive contribution, leading to a contradiction. This idea allows us to exclude the values in the parameter space for which a suitable
\alpha_{I}
can be found.

It has been observed in
[
57
,
11
]
that the constraint (
30
) is in fact too strong. It also excludes solutions to crossing equations where there are several distinct contributions of the same form in the crossing equations,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\vec{\lambda}^{T}_{i}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I}\vec{\lambda}_{i}+\dots=0.

(32)

However, we know that in Ising CFT there are unique operators
\sigma,\epsilon,T
with the respective quantum numbers, leading to single term as in (
28
).

A better strategy is to treat the vector
\vec{\lambda}
as a parameter similar to the scaling dimensions
\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon}
, and to try to exclude a given value of
\vec{\lambda}
by searching for
\alpha_{I}
that instead of (
30
) satisfies

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}^{T}(\alpha_{I}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}}^{I})\vec{\lambda}\geq 0.

(33)

Importantly, this constraint is unchanged if we rescale
\vec{\lambda}
by a non-zero real number. Therefore, it only depends on the equivalence class
[\vec{\lambda}]
of
\vec{\lambda}
in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}
and we should treat
[\vec{\lambda}]
as the new parameter. If we are able to exclude all possible values of
[\vec{\lambda}]
for a fixed
(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})
, then the point
(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})
is excluded in the dimension space. As shown in
[
11
]
, this procedure yields a significantly smaller allowed region for
(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})
than using (
30
).

How can one exclude all possible values of
[\vec{\lambda}]
in a finite amount of time? The approach used in
[
11
]
was to discretize
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{1}
(their
\vec{\lambda}
had only two components) using a finite grid. If all points in the grid are excluded, one can be reasonably confident that there are no allowed points in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{1}
if the grid is fine enough. This approach worked well in
[
51
]
because their parameter space
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{1}
was one-dimensional. In our case the parameter space is
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}
and discretization appears computationally infeasible.

This problem was solved in
[
32
]
with the “cutting surface” algorithm. The key observation is simply that a finite set of functionals
\alpha_{I}^{(k)}
,
k=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, can be enough to exclude the full parameter space
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}
. Indeed, existence of a functional
\alpha_{I}^{(k)}
implies that all
[\vec{\lambda}]
which satisfy

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}^{T}(\alpha_{I}^{(k)}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}})\vec{\lambda}\geq 0

(34)

are excluded. This condition defines a closed set
E_{k}
, and it might very well be that the union of all
E_{k}
covers
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}
,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}E_{k}=\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}.

(35)

If (
35
) holds, then all
[\vec{\lambda}]
are excluded. If (
35
) doesn’t hold, then we can take a
[\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}]
that is not in
\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}E_{k}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}^{T}(\alpha_{I}^{(k)}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}})\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}<0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,\dots,N\},

(36)

and try to construct a linear functional
\vec{\alpha}^{(N+1)}_{I}
such that (along with all the other positivity constraints for other contribution to the crossing equations)

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}^{T}(\alpha_{I}^{(N+1)}\mathcal{G}_{\text{ext}})\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}\geq 0.

(37)

If such a functional
\alpha_{I}^{(N+1)}
is found, we can add it to the list, increment
N
, and check (
35
) again. If a functional cannot be found, it means that
[\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}]
is allowed and so are the current values of
(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})
. Typically, this process converges in a number of steps which is much smaller than the number of points in a fine enough grid on
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{4}
. It also has the benefit of not relying on a discretization.

The technical content of the cutting surface algorithm
[
32
]
is in the procedure for finding a solution
[\vec{\lambda}_{N+1}]
to (
36
) or determining that no solution exists.
17
17

17

When several solutions to (
36
) can be found, the cutting surface algorithm will try to select a solution that is the furthest away from the sets
E_{k}
in order to speed up the convergence of the whole process.
Abstractly, this can be formulated as the following problem: given quadratic forms
Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N}
, determine whether there exists a solution
\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
to the equations

\displaystyle\vec{\lambda}^{T}Q_{k}\vec{\lambda}<0,\quad k\in\{0,1,2,\dots,N\}.

(38)

This is a quadratically-constrained quadratic problem; this class of problems is in general NP-hard. For this reason, the cutting-surface algorithm of
[
32
]
uses a number of methods to try to solve (
38
). If no solution is found by these methods, the algorithm proceeds assuming that no solution exists, but this decision is based on heuristics and is not rigorous.

In this work we developed an efficient algorithm that can rigorously certify that no solutions to (
38
) exist. This does not contradict NP-hardness of the problem since our algorithm is not guaranteed to construct a certificate. However, in all cases when the cutting-surface algorithm of
[
32
]
terminated with “no solution found”, our algorithm was able to certify that no solutions exist. Therefore, all disallowed points in our plots have been ruled out rigorously.

Before we describe the algorithm, we note that in practice while the quadratic forms
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N}
are given by the functionals
\alpha_{I}^{(k)}
as

\displaystyle Q_{k}=\alpha_{I}^{(k)}\mathcal{G}^{I}_{\text{ext}},

(39)

the quadratic form
Q_{0}
is specified manually to restrict the search to an ellipse in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
. This bounding ellipse is chosen conservatively to include the region where the allowed points
[\vec{\lambda}]
are expected to be. See section
5.2
for details. In essence, one of our assumptions is that if any
[\vec{\lambda}]
is allowed at all, it is deep inside the region defined by
\vec{\lambda}^{T}Q_{0}\vec{\lambda}<0
. The technical meaning of this will be clarified below.

4.2
Certification algorithm

In what follows we will use the notation
Q(\lambda)=\lambda^{T}Q\lambda
for quadratic forms. Since
Q_{0}
is specified by us and is chosen so that
Q_{0}(\lambda)<0
represents an ellipsoid, it has signature
(1,n)
. By a change of basis, we can assume that

\displaystyle Q_{0}(\lambda)=-t^{2}+y^{2},

(40)

where
\lambda=(t,y)
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Recall that we are looking for a solution
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
to

\displaystyle Q_{k}(\lambda)<0,\quad k=0,1,\dots N.

(41)

Any solution satisfies
Q_{0}(\lambda)=-t^{2}+y^{2}<0
. Therefore,
t\neq 0
and by rescaling
x
we can always set
t=1
. Therefore, it is enough to find a solution
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to

\displaystyle Q_{k}((1,y))<0,\quad k=1,\dots,N,

(42)

such that
|y|<1
or prove that none exists.

We can write

\displaystyle Q_{k}((1,y))=c_{k}+\beta_{k}(y)+w_{k}(y),

(43)

where
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta_{k}
is a linear functional and
w_{k}
is a quadratic form. It was observed in
[
32
]
that in bootstrap applications the surfaces
Q_{k}((1,y))=0
are usually approximately flat inside the ball
|y|<1
. In other words, the coefficients in the quadratic form
w_{k}
are much smaller than
c_{k}
and
b_{k}
, and for
|y|<1
the contribution of
w_{k}(y)
to
Q_{k}((1,y))
is subleading. Neglecting
w_{k}(y)
would be useful since that would turn (
42
) into linear inequalities.

Since our goal is to construct a rigorous proof that no solutions to (
42
) exist, we cannot simply neglect
w_{k}(y)
. Instead, we will search for a rigorous linear lower bound on
Q_{k}
of the form

\displaystyle Q_{k}((1,y))\geq c_{k}+\delta c_{k}+\beta_{k}(y).

(44)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}||y|<1\text{ and }Q_{k}((1,y))<0,\quad k=1,\dots,N\}

(45)

be the set of solutions to (
42
) (our goal is to show that
\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
). Suppose we can show that

\displaystyle w_{k}(y)\geq\delta c_{k},\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{A}.

(46)

Then (
44
) holds on
\mathcal{A}
and therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[\delta c]=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|c_{k}+\delta c_{k}+\beta_{k}(y)<0,\quad k=1,\dots,N\}.

(47)

The set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c]
is defined using linear inequalities, and therefore can be studied using linear programming. In particular, it is easy to check whether
\mathcal{B}[\delta c]=\emptyset
. If we can show that
\mathcal{B}[\delta c]=\emptyset
, this would constitute a proof of
\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
. Our strategy will therefore be to construct various sets of coefficients
\delta c_{k}
for which (
46
) holds. Our notation
\mathcal{B}[\delta c]
stresses the fact that
\mathcal{B}[\delta c]
depends on the choice of the
\delta c_{k}
.

A simple choice is to set

\displaystyle\delta c_{k}=\delta c_{k}^{0}\equiv\inf_{|y|<1}w_{k}(y)=\min\{\lambda_{k,\text{min}},0\},

(48)

where
\lambda_{k,\text{min}}
the minimal eigenvalue of the matrix that defines
w_{k}
. With this choice (
46
) is obviously true, and therefore
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
. Typically, we find that the set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
is not empty. It is, however, usually much smaller than the unit ball
|y|<1
. This allows us to improve our choice of
\delta c_{k}
.

We construct an improved set of constants
\delta c_{k}^{1}
as follows. Let
\lambda_{k,i}
and
e_{k,i}
be the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
w_{k}
. We can then write

\displaystyle w_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\lambda_{k,i}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}\geq\delta c_{k}^{1},

(49)

\displaystyle\delta c_{k}^{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}>0}\lambda_{k,i}\inf_{y}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}+\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}<0}\lambda_{k,i}\sup_{y}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}.

(50)

We would like this to hold on
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
, this can be achieved by taking the
\sup
and the
\inf
over
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
. Note that
(e_{k,i}\cdot y)
is a linear function on the convex set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
, so its image is an interval which can be computed using linear programming. From this,
\inf_{y\in\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}
and
\sup_{y\in\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}
can be easily obtained.
18
18

18

We also implemented two minor improvements on the value of
\delta c^{1}
. Firstly, it is guaranteed that
(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}\leq 1
on
\mathcal{A}
, so we can replace
\sup_{y\in\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}
by
\min\{\sup_{y\in\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2},1\}
. Secondly, one can replace
\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}<0}\lambda_{k,i}\sup_{y}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2}
by
\max\{\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}<0}\lambda_{k,i}\sup_{y}(e_{k,i}\cdot y)^{2},\delta c^{0}_{k}\}
.

In the majority of cases when the cutting surface algorithm of
[
32
]
does not find a solution, we observe that the set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{1}]
is empty. Since
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{1}]
, this proves that
\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
and certifies the absence of solutions. When the set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{1}]
is non-empty, we can construct
\delta c^{2}
from
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{1}]
just as
\delta c^{1}
is constructed from
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
. If
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{2}]
is non-empty, we can continue to construct
\delta c^{3}
and so on. With rare exceptions discussed below, we found in practice that this process terminates with an empty set
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{m}]
for some
m>0
. The largest value of
m
that we observed was
m=3
.

Out of the 38 disallowed points in
(\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\epsilon})
space that we computed at
\Lambda=43
, 30 points were certified by
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{1}]=\emptyset
, 1 point by
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{2}]=\emptyset
, and 2 points by
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{3}]=\emptyset
. This produces certificates for 33 out of 38 points.

In these cases the sets
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{m}]
are typically quite small and deep in the interior of the unit ball
|y|<1
. However, for the 5 remaining points
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
is an unbounded set, and
\delta c^{1}
cannot be constructed. It appears that this happens when the cutting surface algorithm quickly excluded the majority of the unit ball
|y|<1
, and then spent some time ruling out a boundary region close to
|y|=1
.

To deal with such situations, we use convex quadratic optimization
19
19

19

We used the function
QuadraticOptimization
in
Mathematica
.
to compute

\displaystyle r_{\text{min}}\equiv\inf_{y\in\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]}|y|.

(51)

We found that in all 5 cases at
\Lambda=43
when
\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{m}]
iterations fail,
r_{\text{min}}
is at least 0.99. Since
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[\delta c^{0}]
, this shows that any solution to (
42
) satisfies
|y|>0.99
.

Recall that the ball
|y|<1
is the bounding ellipse that we choose conservatively to restrict the search region in the OPE space. In other words, we are assuming that all solutions, if any, lie deep inside the unit ball
|y|<1
. In particular, we assume that no solution can satisfy
|y|>0.99
. Therefore, the statement that all solutions satisfy
|y|>0.99
is a proof that no solutions exist under our assumptions.

5
Results

5.1
The
\langle TTTT\rangle
system

We first present new results in the system of only correlators of
T
, i.e. constraints on
\langle TTTT\rangle
. This was previously studied in
[
19

, where the authors found universal bounds for all local unitary 3d CFTs on the spectrum of low-lying operators as well as the surprising presence of features seemingly linked to the 3d Ising CFT in particular. As we have a completely new implementation of this system (which is a subset of our full
T,\sigma,\epsilon
system) and have the target of the 3d Ising CFT, it’s therefore worthwhile to dwell briefly on this
T
-only system. The results serve to validate the correctness of our implementation, to improve and refine the universal numerical results of
[
19
]
, and to reveal what more these universal bounds seem to know about the 3d Ising CFT.

6
Conclusions

Our results give very precise determinations of leading scaling dimensions and OPE coefficients of the 3d Ising CFT, including data involving the stress tensor. We expect that one can learn about other operators in the spectrum by extracting and analyzing the extremal functionals
[
62
,
63
]
corresponding to various boundaries of the 3d Ising island. This data should include trajectories of double-twist operators built out of the stress tensor which have so far not been studied in the numerical bootstrap. It will be very interesting to analyze these trajectories, comparing with predictions from the Lorentzian inversion formula
[
64
]
, in the spirit of
[
12
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
. This data will also give access to interesting observables associated with the ANEC operator in the 3d Ising CFT, such as energy correlators via the light-ray OPE
[
44
,
68
]
.

One amusing interpretation of our determinations of
\langle TTT\rangle
OPE coefficients is that the 3d Ising CFT is “
n_{F}/(n_{F}+n_{B})=1.3833683(35)\%
fermionic”. We may wonder how this percentage compares against other interacting CFTs, such as the critical
O(N)
vector models or CFTs built from interacting fermions or gauge fields. In particular, how these OPE coefficients may vary across different families of CFTs could well be nontrivial in light of conjectured bosonization/fermionization dualities
[
69
]
; see
[
70
]
for an excellent pedagogical review. Bootstrapping other mixed systems involving the stress tensor therefore would be an interesting line of inquiry. We leave these exciting directions to future work.

In section
5.1
, we found that even the
\langle TTTT\rangle
system alone can produce sharp features corresponding to the 3d Ising CFT. This observation leads to further questions: are there more such features in
\langle TTTT\rangle
bounds, and can they be related to other physical CFTs? (Note that the stress tensor bootstrap automatically excludes non-local CFTs, such as the long-range Ising model
[
71
,
72
]
.)
Concretely, there is a large region of parameter space to the interior of the universal bounds in figure
4
in which any local, unitary CFT in 3d must exist. Is it possible to map out this interior (and the analogous space for global symmetry currents) by extremizing other parameters? Preliminary explorations reveal that there is a rich structure here and we plan to continue studying it in future work.

Given the effectiveness of including
T_{\mu\nu}
for increasing the precision of bounds on the 3d Ising CFT, it is natural to ask: which operator is next? That is, which additional operator(s) should we include in the crossing equations to further isolate this theory? We do not have a rigorous answer to this question, but intuition from the lightcone bootstrap
[
12
,
73
,
74
]
suggests that low-twist operators could play an important role. Note that
\sigma
is the lowest-twist operator in the 3d Ising CFT, and
T_{\mu\nu}
is the next-lowest. After that, we have the leading spin-4 operator
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
, with twist approximately
\tau_{C}\approx 1.022
. However, it is daunting (from the point of view of numerics) to imagine the system of crossing equations we would obtain by studying four-point functions of
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
and other operators. As an example, the number of parity-even four-point structures in the
\langle CCCC\rangle
four-point function alone is 881, which can be contrasted with the 29 “bulk” crossing equations studied in this work. It is interesting to ask whether we can include only the low-twist components of
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
in an efficient way. An alternative approach is to consider higher-point crossing equations, which encode an infinite number of four-point crossing equations at once. See
[
42
,
27
,
75
,
76
]
for recent work in this direction.

Appendix C
Computing dependencies

In the build process described in section
3.1
, we need to compute dependencies of each element of an SDP so as to minimize redundant work. For example, we must be able to determine the
Block3d
’s that a given
CompositeBlock
depends on, or the
Block3d
’s and
CompositeBlock
’s that a given
PartChunk
depends on. One possible approach is to write and maintain separate routines for constructing an element and for determining its dependencies. However, a downside of this approach is that the two routines must be manually kept in sync with each other, and this is error-prone. It is common to make changes to many parts of a computation when debugging, and it is furthermore common to spin off smaller example computations, such as an SDP for
T
-
\sigma
correlators alone. We prefer not to have to worry about keeping track of dependencies during such changes.

Appendix D
Scheduler for building SDPs

In this appendix, we describe our ad-hoc scheduler for building SDPs. Recall from section
3.1
that each SDP depends on several elements: some number of
Block3d
’s,
CompositeBlock
’s,
PartChunk
’s, and
Part
’s. From these elements, we build a tree of tasks, where an edge indicates a dependency: a child task must be completed before the parent task can be started. We label each task with a memory estimate, a runtime estimate function (which maps a number of cores
n
to a time), and a minimum and maximum number of threads. (For example, our
CompositeBlock, PartChunk, and Part
tasks are not parallelized, so they each have
\mathtt{maxThreads}=\mathtt{minThreads}=1
.) We assume that we have access to
K
nodes, each with
C
CPUs, and a fixed amount of memory
M
. Our algorithm has two phases:

D.1
Phase 1: initial task distribution

To begin, we collect the tasks that have no dependencies that need to be built (which are usually
Block3d
tasks), and sort them in reverse order of memory usage. We find a distribution of these initial tasks to the
K
nodes by traversing the list in order. For a task
t
, we sort the nodes by how quickly they are expected to complete their current task assignments, and filter out nodes for which adding
t
would decrease their “score.” We assign
t
to the earliest-completing node whose score will be increased, and such that we will not exceed the memory of the node. When we encounter a
t
such that there doesn’t exist a node satisfying these requirements (for example, because none of the nodes have enough extra memory to accommodate
t
), the initial task distribution phase is over, and we proceed to the next phase.

The above algorithm depends on two definitions: a
completion time
and a
score
for a node with a given task assignment. We estimate
completion time
by finding a locally optimal allocation of CPUs to the assigned tasks that minimizes the maximum completion time of all assigned tasks (based on the runtime estimate function). We find this allocation in a simple way: we start with an even distribution of CPUs, and then steal CPUs from the fastest task and give them to the slowest task (while taking care not to violate the
minThreads
and
maxThreads
requirements), as long as overall performance increases. The
score
is a heuristic measure that we give to an allocation of CPUs to tasks, based on the idea that the completion times of the individual tasks should be similar.

After obtaining the initial task distribution, we start the corresponding tasks on their assigned nodes.

D.2
Phase 2: continually running tasks

Once tasks are running, we switch to phase 2. We maintain a priority queue of tasks that are ready to run. In practice, we choose the priority to be a tuple
(\mathtt{depth},\,\mathtt{memory})
, where
\mathtt{depth}
is the depth of the task in the tree (deeper tasks have higher priority), and
\mathtt{memory}
is the task’s memory estimate.

When a task finishes, we compute its reverse dependencies. If any of them are ready to be started (because all their child tasks have been completed), we add them to the priority queue. Each node keeps track of its number of free CPUs and amount of free memory and monitors the queue. If a node finds a task at the front of the queue that it can perform, given its free resources, it dequeue’s that task. If it has enough resources, a node can dequeue multiple tasks at once, in which case it allocates free CPUs to them using the same algorithm as in subsection
D.1
. It then begins running the newly dequeued tasks.

By running this algorithm, eventually all tasks will be completed.

D.3
Collecting resource usage data

The algorithm described above depends on a memory estimate and a runtime estimate for each task. When possible, we tried to build an analytical model predicting these resource requirements. This is painstaking because it requires intimate knowledge of how the task is performed, together with good measurements. These analytical models are needed when we perform a fresh build with new values of the parameters (
\ell_{\mathrm{max}}
,
order
, etc. discussed in appendix
E
) that we have not tried before.

However, during every build, we record the actual resource usage of each task. Once we have this data, we can use it for memory and runtime estimates in subsequent builds. (This is possible because changing parameters like
\Delta_{\sigma}
and
\Delta_{\epsilon}
does not affect resource requirements.) Thus, after collecting resource usage data, we have accurate information about resource requirements, and we are no longer reliant on ad-hoc analytical models.

\Lambda=35
and

are summarized in tables

For a given set of spins
0\ldots\ell_{max}
,
order
,
poles
, and
prec
, we computed the primal objective defined as the value of the GFF navigator function.

Then, we computed the accuracy
\delta
as the absolute difference of the primal objective and the most accurate primal objective (i.e. computed with the largest
\ell_{max}
and reasonable values of other parameters, see the last rows in tables
8
and
9
).
In a similar way, we computed the accuracy at a given
\ell_{max}
, denoted as
\delta^{(\ell_{max})}
, and
the accuracy at a given
\ell_{max}
and
order
, denoted as
\delta^{(\ell_{max},\textrm{{\tt order}})}
.

One can make the following observations from the accuracy tests:

•

Objective converges slowly with increasing
\ell_{max}
.
This means that, after a certain point, increasing
order
or number of poles for fixed
\ell_{max}
does not improve accuracy (see the column
\delta
in Tables
8
and
9
).

•

There is a sharp cutoff at low
order
s: for
\Lambda=35
, SDPB fails to converge at
\textrm{{\tt order}}=95
, converges to a completely wrong result for
\textrm{{\tt order}}=96
and
\textrm{{\tt order}}=97
, and behaves well starting from
\textrm{{\tt order}}=98
(see fig.
10
and the column
\delta^{(\ell_{max})}
in table
8
).

\Lambda=43
exhibits similar behavior and converges to a correct result starting from
\textrm{{\tt order}}=120
(see the column
\delta^{(\ell_{max})}
in table
9
).

•

There is a cutoff for precision as well.
For example, for
\Lambda=35
,
\text{{\tt prec}}=576
leads to SDPB crashing due to numerical errors.
Any precision starting from 640 yields the same result.

•

At fixed spins and
order
, results converge rapidly with increasing number of poles.
For example, for
\Lambda=35
,
\ell_{max}=88
,
\textrm{{\tt order}}=100
, increasing the number of poles to any value beyond 25 will change the objective by less than
10^{-22}
(see the column
\delta^{(\ell_{max},\textrm{{\tt order}})}
in table
8
).

Based on these test results, we chose the following parameters for our computations to ensure that the conformal block approximation and spin truncation induce a relative error which is negligible compared to other sources (e.g. the error due to the Delaunay search):

•

\Lambda=35
:
\ell_{max}=75
,
\textrm{{\tt order}}=106
,
\textrm{{\tt poles}}=35
,
\textrm{{\tt prec}}=832
.

•

\Lambda=43
:
\ell_{max}=90
,
\textrm{{\tt order}}=130
,
\textrm{{\tt poles}}=35
,
\textrm{{\tt prec}}=832
.

Let us emphasize in particular that it is important to systematically increase
order
(the order of the radial expansion of conformal blocks used by
blocks_3d
) as we increase
\Lambda
. From our testing, we estimate that
\textrm{{\tt order}}\sim 5.5\Lambda
is roughly sufficient to ensure that errors remain small. This might seem surprising, given that the radial expansion for conformal blocks
[
81
]
is an expansion in
r=3-2\sqrt{2}\approx 0.17
, which is numerically quite small. However, the coefficient of each power of
r
is a polynomial in external dimensions, and the growth in degree of these polynomials plays an important role in determining the error from truncating the series. We encourage users of
blocks_3d
to keep this in mind and be sure to test the effects of
order
on any given bootstrap problem.
