Quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes: a sequential approach to the Poisson compound decision problem
Stefano Favaro, Sandra Fortini

Abstract
The Poisson compound decision problem is a long-standing problem is statistics, for which empirical Bayes methods are commonly used to estimate Poisson means in static or batch settings. We consider this problem in a streaming, or online, framework. Building on a quasi-Bayesian approach based on Newton’s algorithm, we develop a sequential estimate that is easy to evaluate, computationally efficient, and has constant per-observation cost as the data accrue. We establish frequentist guarantees for the proposed estimate, including consistency and asymptotic optimality, with optimality understood as vanishing excess Bayes risk, or regret. Empirical performance is assessed through simulation studies and comparisons with benchmark procedures.



Keywords:
Empirical Bayes; Newton’s algorithm; Poisson compound decision; quasi-Bayes; streaming data

1
Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

Given
n\geq 1
observations modeled as independent Poisson random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
with means
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
, respectively, the Poisson compound decision problem concerns the estimation of
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
under squared-error loss. Empirical Bayes provides a general approach for compound decision problems
(
Robbins, 1956
;
Zhang, 2003
)
. If the
\theta_{i}
’s are i.i.d. as a prior
G
on
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then the best estimate of
\theta_{i}
is the posterior mean or Bayes estimate, namely

\hat{\theta}_{G}(y)=E_{G}[\theta_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y]=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\theta\frac{\text{e}^{-\theta}\theta^{y}}{y!}G(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\frac{\text{e}^{-\theta}\theta^{y}}{y!}G(\mathrm{d}\theta)}=(y+1)\frac{p_{G}(y+1)}{p_{G}(y)}\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(1)

where
p_{G}
denotes the probability mass function of the
Y_{i}
’s. Since
G
is generally unknown, empirical Bayes proceeds by estimating either
p_{G}
, in the so-called
f
-modeling strategy, or
G
, in the so-called
g
-modeling strategy
(
Efron, 2014
;
Efron, 2019
)
.

A prominent
f
-modeling strategy is Robbins’ method
(
Robbins, 1956
)
, which replaces
p_{G}
in (
1
) with the empirical distribution of the
Y_{i}
’s. Despite its conceptual appeal and computational simplicity,
f
-modeling can be numerically unstable and lacks robustness. In particular, it is sensitive to outlying observations, or more precisely to counts that occur rarely, which may yield exceptionally small or large estimates
(
Brown et al., 2013
;
Shen and Wu, 2026
)
.

Examples of
g
-modeling include estimates of
G
in (
1
) based on maximum likelihood and minimum distance methods
(
Jana et al., 2025
)
. A fully Bayesian alternative places a suitable hyperprior on
G
, yielding what is termed Bayes empirical Bayes
(
Deely and Lindley, 1981
;
Efron, 2019
)
. Compared with
f
-modeling,
g
-modeling typically produces more accurate estimates and allows prior information to be incorporated more naturally. Its main drawback is computational cost, especially in nonparametric and high-dimensional settings
(
Jana et al., 2023
;
Teh et al., 2025
)
.

The Poisson compound decision problem provides a flexible framework for modeling count data and has been successfully applied in settings where static or batch processing is appropriate, including microarrays
(
Efron, 2003
)
, insurance analytics
(
Efron and Hastie, 2021
, Chapter 6)
, deconvolution
(
Efron, 2016
)
, data confidentiality
(
Zhang, 2005
)
and sports analytics
(
Jana et al., 2025
)
. The growing prevalence of streaming data in some of these areas, as well as in fields such as social media, biosurveillance, healthcare and e-commerce, motivates extension of this framework to an online setting, where timely inference is required. Although existing empirical Bayes methods could in principle be adapted to streaming data, they typically rely on batch optimization or posterior computation and are therefore ill suited to sequential implementation. To our knowledge, no sequential methodology for the Poisson compound decision problem currently offers either theoretical guarantees or convincing empirical support.

1.2
Preview of our contributions

We propose a sequential
g
-modeling approach to the Poisson compound decision problem. Starting from an initial guess
G_{0}
for
G
, for each
n\geq 1
the method updates
G_{n-1}
after observing
Y_{n}
according to the recursive procedure of
Smith and Makov (1978)
, known as Newton’s algorithm
(
Newton et al., 1998
;
Martin and Ghosh, 2008
)
. This scheme can be interpreted as a quasi-Bayesian learning model and induces a predictive construction that is asymptotically equivalent, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, to a Bayesian model
(
Fortini and Petrone, 2020
)
. A quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes estimate of
\hat{\theta}_{G}(y)
is then obtained from (
1
) by replacing
G
with
G_{n}
. The resulting estimate,
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
, is straightforward to evaluate, computationally efficient, and has constant per-observation computational cost as data accrue. Moreover, viewing Newton’s algorithm as a learning model further enables the construction of asymptotic credible intervals through a Gaussian central limit theorem.

Frequentist guarantees are established for
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
under the assumption that the observations
Y_{n}
’s are i.i.d. from a Poisson mixture model with “true” mixing distribution
G^{\ast}
, referred to as the oracle prior. We give sufficient conditions on
G^{\ast}
under which
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
is asymptotically optimal as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, where optimality is understood as vanishing excess Bayes risk, or regret.

We assess the proposed methodology through empirical studies on synthetic and real data. For fixed
n\geq 1
,
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
outperforms Robbins’ method and achieves performance comparable to that of the nonparametric maximum likelihood and minimum distance estimates.

1.3
Related work

Our approach links the empirical Bayes literature to a recent and growing body of work on predictive or recursive inference, often referred to as the “post-Bayes” framework. This literature includes developments on predictive distributions and martingale posteriors
(
Fong et al., 2023
;
Fong and Yiu, 2024
;
Holmes and Walker, 2023
;
Battiston and Cappello, 2025
;
Yung et al., 2025
)
, as well as related work on generalized Bayesian updating
(
Bissiri et al., 2016
;
Knoblauch et al., 2022
)
.

2
Sequential
g
-modeling for the Poisson compound decision problem

2.1
Modeling assumptions

For a stream of observations
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we propose a sequential
g
-modeling methodology which, on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
, specifies the distribution of
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
through the conditional distribution of
Y_{n+1}
given
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
, for each
n\geq 0
. As in the Bayesian framework, the model is interpreted as a learning model and not as a data-generating process
(
Fortini and Petrone, 2025
)
.

Denote by
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
the natural filtration of
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. For each
n\geq 0
, the conditional distribution of
Y_{n+1}
given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is modeled as a mixture of Poisson distributions with the mean
\theta
ranging in compact
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
, namely

p_{G_{n}}(y)=\mathds{P}(Y_{n+1}=y\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(2)

where the mixing
G_{n}
in (
2
) is defined recursively through Newton’s algorithm
(
Newton et al., 1998
)
:

G_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\theta)=(1-\alpha_{n+1})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\alpha_{n+1}\frac{\text{Poisson}(Y_{n+1}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(Y_{n+1}\mid\theta)G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta)},

(3)

starting from an initial guess
G_{0}
for
G
, where the
\alpha_{n}
’s in
(0,1)
are such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\alpha_{n}=+\infty
and
\sum_{n\geq 1}\alpha^{2}_{n}<+\infty
. According to (
3
), after observing
Y_{n+1}
, the model updates
G_{n}
by taking a weighted average of
G_{n}
and its posterior distribution based on
Y_{n+1}
, with weight
\alpha_{n+1}
. The sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is referred to as the learning rate. A standard choice is
\alpha_{n}=(\alpha+n)^{-\gamma}
, with
\alpha>0
and
\gamma\in(1/2,1]
; see
Fortini and Petrone (2020)
for a discussion on the choice of the learning rate.

2.2
Quasi-Bayes properties

We show that the learning model (
2
)-(
3
) is quasi-Bayesian in the sense of
Fortini and Petrone (2020)
, yielding a predictive construction that is asymptotically equivalent, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, to a Bayesian model.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be as in (
2
)-(
3
). As
n\rightarrow+\infty
,
G_{n}
converges weakly
\mathds{P}-
a.s. to a random probability distribution
\tilde{G}
on
\Theta
, such that, by defining
p_{\tilde{G}}
as in (
2
) with
\tilde{G}
in place of
G_{n}
, it holds
\mathds{P}-
a.s. that
p_{\tilde{G}}(y)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}I(Y_{k}=y)
and
\mathds{P}(Y_{n+k}=y\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=E(p_{\tilde{G}}(y)\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})
, for every
n\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
and
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In particular,
\mathds{P}(Y_{n}=y)=\int p_{\tilde{G}}(y)d\mathds{P}
.

See
B.1
for the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Under the learning model (
2
)-(
3
), the future observations in
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are conditionally identically distributed given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, for each
n\geq 0
. By
Berti et al. (2004, Theorem 2.2)
, it follows that the
Y_{n}
’s are asymptotically i.i.d., conditional on the random limit
\tilde{G}
, with distribution
p_{\tilde{G}}
. This places the model within the quasi-Bayesian framework developed in
Fortini and Petrone (2020)
. From this viewpoint,
p_{\tilde{G}}
is the random distribution that would be assigned with access to an infinite sample, so that
\tilde{G}
, unavailable in practice, is the parameter of interest, and (
1
) becomes

\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)=(y+1)\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(y+1)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(y)}\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(4)

In contrast to the Bayesian framework, which places a prior on
G
and assumes the
Y_{n}
’s to be i.i.d. conditional on
G
, the quasi-Bayesian approach avoids repeated recomputation of the posterior distribution as new observations arrive, thereby yielding substantial computational savings in sequential applications.

2.3
Estimation and Gaussian approximation

Under the learning model (
2
)-(
3
), an estimate of
\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)
is obtained by replacing the unknown
\tilde{G}
in (
4
) with
G_{n}
, namely

\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)=(y+1)\frac{p_{G_{n}}(y+1)}{p_{G_{n}}(y)}\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(5)

We refer to
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
as the quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes estimate. Because the map
G\mapsto\hat{\theta}_{G}(y)
is nonlinear, the plug-in approximation
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
to
\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)
need not coincide with
E_{\tilde{G}}[\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{n}]
. As a corollary of Theorem
2.1
, for every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)\to\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)

\mathds{P}-
a.s. as
n\to\infty
; the next theorem gives a related conditional Gaussian central limit theorem for
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let (
2
)-(
3
) hold with
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
non increasing and
\sum_{n\geq 1}(\alpha_{n}^{2}/(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{k}^{2}))^{2}<+\infty
, and let
b_{n}=(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{k}^{2})^{-1}
. For every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the conditional distribution of
b_{n}^{1/2}(\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)-\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y))
, given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, converges weakly
\mathds{P}-
a.s. to a centered Gaussian kernel with the random variance

W_{\tilde{G}}(y)=\hat{\theta}^{2}_{\tilde{G}}(y)\sum_{z\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{G}}(z)\left(\frac{p_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\,|\,z)}(y)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(y)}-\frac{p_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\,|\,z)}(y+1)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(y+1)}\right)^{2}

(6)

where
p_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\,|\,z)}(y)=\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid z)
with
\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid z)\propto\text{Poisson}(z\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)
.

See
B.2
for the proof of Theorem
2.2
. If
W_{G_{n}}
is defined as in (
6
), with
G_{n}
in place of the unknown
\tilde{G}
, then, for every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
W_{G_{n}}(y)\rightarrow W_{\tilde{G}}(y)

\mathds{P}-
a.s. as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. It follows that, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
,
\hat{\theta}_{\tilde{G}}(y)
is approximately Gaussian with mean
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
and variance
b_{n}^{-1}W_{G_{n}}(y)
, for every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

2.4
Credible intervals

For a fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and with access to an infinite sample, the endpoints of a
(1-\alpha)
-level credible interval for
\theta
, given
Y=y
, are the
\alpha/2
and
1-\alpha/2
quantiles of the conditional distribution function

F_{\tilde{G}}(\theta\mid y)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\vartheta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)}{\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\vartheta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)}.

(7)

With
n
observations, we replace
\tilde{G}
by
G_{n}
and incorporate into the credible interval the additional variability induced by this substitution. The next theorem is the basis for this construction.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let (
2
)-(
3
) hold with
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
non increasing and
\sum_{n\geq 1}(\alpha_{n}^{2}/(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{k}^{2}))^{2}<+\infty
, and let
b_{n}=(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{k}^{2})^{-1}
. If
F_{G_{n}}(\cdot\mid y)
denotes the distribution function in (
7
) with
G_{n}
replacing
\tilde{G}
then, for every
\theta\in\Theta
, the conditional distribution of
b_{n}^{1/2}(F_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)-F_{\tilde{G}}(\theta\mid y))
, given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, converges weakly
\mathds{P}-
a.s. to a centered Gaussian kernel with the random variance

W^{\prime}_{\tilde{G},y}(\theta)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{G}}(z)\frac{p_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\mid z)}(y)^{2}}{p_{\tilde{G}}(y)^{2}}\left(F_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\mid z)}(\theta\mid y)-F_{\tilde{G}}(\theta\mid y)\right)^{2},

(8)

where
p_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\,|\,z)}(y)=\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid z)
with
\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid z)\propto\text{Poisson}(z\mid\theta)\tilde{G}(\mathrm{d}\theta)
, and
F_{\tilde{G}(\cdot\mid z)}(\theta\mid y)
as in (
7
) with
\tilde{G}(\cdot\mid z)
in the place of
\tilde{G}
.

See
B.3
for the proof of Theorem
2.3
. Let
\tilde{Y}\mid\tilde{\theta}\sim\text{Poisson}(\cdot\mid\tilde{\theta})
and
\tilde{\theta}\mid\tilde{G}\sim\tilde{G}
. To construct a
(1-\alpha)
-level credible interval for
\tilde{\theta}
, let
W^{\prime}_{G_{n},y}
be defined as in (
8
) with
G_{n}
in place of the unknown
\tilde{G}
. For fixed
\beta\in(0,1)
, define

\underline{F}^{\beta}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)=\max\{0,F_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)-z_{1-\beta/2}(b_{n}^{-1}W^{\prime}_{G_{n},y}(\theta))^{1/2}\}

(9)

and

\overline{F}^{\beta}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)=\min\{1,F_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)+z_{1-\beta/2}(b_{n}^{-1}W^{\prime}_{G_{n},y}(\theta))^{1/2}\},

(10)

where
z_{1-\beta/2}
is the quantile of the standard Gaussian distribution. For every
\theta\in\Theta
,
W^{\prime}_{G_{n},y}(\theta)\rightarrow W^{\prime}_{\tilde{G},y}(\theta)

\mathds{P}
-a.s. as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, and hence, for
n
sufficiently large,
\mathds{P}(F_{\tilde{G}}(\theta\mid y)\leq\underline{F}_{G_{n}}^{\beta}(\theta\mid y))\leq\beta/2
and
\mathds{P}(F_{\tilde{G}}(\theta\mid y)>\overline{F}_{G_{n}}^{\beta}(\theta\mid y))\leq\beta/2
.
Therefore, for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\alpha
, it follows from (
9
)-(
10
) that

I_{G_{n}}(y)=[L_{n}(y),U_{n}(y)]\cap\Theta,

(11)

with
L_{n}(y)=\sup\{\theta:\overline{F}^{\beta_{1}}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)\leq\beta_{2}/2\}
and
U_{n}(y)=\inf\{\theta:\underline{F}^{\beta_{1}}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)\geq 1-\beta_{2}/2\}
, is a
(1-\alpha)
-level credible interval for
\tilde{\theta}
. The tuning parameters
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, which account for the uncertainty in
\tilde{G}
and in
\theta
conditionally on
\tilde{G}
, respectively, are chosen by solving an optimization problem aimed at minimizing (numerically) the length of the resulting credible interval
I_{G_{n}}
. See
D.1
.

2.5
Frequentist guarantees

Under the “true” Poisson mixture model, the
Y_{n}
’s are assumed to be i.i.d. from a mixture of Poisson distributions with the “true” mixing distribution
G^{\ast}
, or oracle prior, namely

p_{G^{*}}(y)=\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)G^{*}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Let
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}
be the probability measure under which the “true” model holds. If
G^{\ast}
were known, the Bayes estimate in (
1
) would be
\hat{\theta}_{G^{*}}(y)
. Since
G^{\ast}
is unknown, we instead use the quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes estimate
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
in (
5
). The regret incurring by using
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
in place of
\hat{\theta}_{G^{*}}(y)
is

\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{*})=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)-\hat{\theta}_{G^{*}}(y))^{2}p_{G^{*}}(y);

(12)

see
Efron (2019, Section 3)
for background on the regret (
12
). As a frequentist optimality guarantee for
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)
, the next theorem shows that the regret (
12
) converges to zero
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}-
a.s. as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
G^{\ast}
be the oracle prior on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and, denoting by
KL
the Kullback–Leibler divergence, let
G^{\dagger}=\text{argmin}_{G\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\textsc{KL}(p_{G^{*}}\,\|\,p_{G})
, where
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
is the weak closure of the class
\mathcal{S}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to
G_{0}
. If
G_{n}
is defined as in (
3
) with
\Theta
compact, then for every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty

\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)\rightarrow\hat{\theta}_{G^{{\dagger}}}(y)\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}-a.s.

and

\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{\dagger})\!=\!\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}(y)-\hat{\theta}_{G^{\dagger}}(y))^{2}p_{G^{*}}(y)\rightarrow 0\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}-a.s.

If
G^{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
G_{0}
, then
\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{*})\rightarrow 0
,
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}-
a.s.

See
B.4
for the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Under additional assumptions on Newton’s algorithm, the rate of convergence of the regret to zero can also be derived; see
C
.

3
Numerical illustrations

3.1
Synthetic-data analysis

We empirically assess the quasi-Bayes empirical Bayes methodology, hereafter QB, by comparing it, for fixed
n\geq 1
, with three benchmark nonparametric procedures: (i) Robbins’ method (Robbins)
(
Robbins, 1956
)
; (ii) maximum likelihood (ML)
(
Jana et al., 2025
)
; and (iii) minimum squared Hellinger distance (MHD)
(
Jana et al., 2025
)
. Details on these procedures are provided in
D.1
.

For
n\in\{50,\,100,\,200,\,400\}
, generate i.i.d. data
\mathbf{Y}_{n}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
from a Poisson mixture model with Weibull prior
G
of scale
5
and shape
3
. Newton’s algorithm (
3
) requires numerical evaluation of an integral, i.e., the marginal likelihood, which we approximate by the trapezoidal rule. To this end, the density function of
G_{n}
is represented through its values on a fixed grid of
d\geq 1
quadratures points over
\Theta
where
d
control the numerical resolution. This representation is used only for numerical evaluation and imposes no modeling restriction on
\Theta
. Letting
U_{\Theta}=\max\{\max\{\mathbf{Y}_{n}\},\lceil Q_{n,0.99}+4(\max\{Q_{n,0.99},1\})^{1/2}\rceil\}
, with
Q_{n,0.99}=\textsc{Quantile}(\mathbf{Y}_{n};0.99)
, we use a uniform grid with
d=1{,}000
over
\Theta=(0,U_{\Theta})
, set
G_{0}
to be Uniform on
\Theta
, and take the learning rate to be
\alpha_{n}=(1+n)^{-0.99}
.

The performance of the QB estimate relative to the Robbins, ML, and MHD estimates is assessed through the empirical mean squared error (
e-MSE
). We also consider the oracle estimate (Oracle), obtained by replacing
G
in (
1
) with the Weibull prior distribution generating the data, which provides the empirical minimum mean squared error (
e-MMSE
). For the QB, Robbins, ML, and MHD estimates we further report the empirical regret, defined as
\textsc{e-REGRET}=\textsc{e-MSE}-\textsc{e-MMSE}
, that is, the excess empirical squared error relative to the oracle benchmark. See
D.1
. A sensitivity analysis with respect to the the number of quadrature points
d\in\{5000,1000,500,100,50,10\}
, reported in
D.3
, indicates that the performance of QB is robust to the choice of
d
.

A.1
Almost sure conditional convergence.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
, a kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a function
K:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\Omega
satisfying: i) for every
\omega\in\Omega
,
K(\cdot,\omega)
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; ii) for each
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the function
K(B,\cdot)
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}
. A kernel
K
is called a Gaussian kernel if
K(\cdot,\omega)
is a Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mu(\omega)
and covariance matrix
\Sigma(\omega)
, where
\mu
and
\Sigma
are random variables defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
. We denote the Gaussian kernel by
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
.

Definition A.1

(
Crimaldi (2009)
)
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of random variables adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{n})
and taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
n\geq 0
, let
K_{n}
denote a regular version of the conditional distribution of
X_{n+1}
, given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. If there exists a kernel
K
such that the sequence
(K_{n}(\cdot,\omega))_{n}
converges weakly to
K(\cdot,\omega)
for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, then we say that the sequence
(X_{n})
converges to
K
in the sense of almost-sure conditional convergence with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{n})
.

Whenever the underlying filtration is clear from the context, we denote the almost sure conditional convergence of
X_{n}
to
K
by

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.\;cond.}}{{\longrightarrow}}K.

The following results from
Crimaldi (2009)
and
Crimaldi et al. (2016)
provide the main tool in the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem A.2

(
Crimaldi (2009)
, Theorem A.1)
.

For each

, let

be a real valued martingale with respect to the filtration

\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}g_{\eta}^{*}(\vartheta_{i})\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{k(y\mid\vartheta_{i})}{p_{G_{\eta}^{*}}(y)}p_{G^{*}}(y)=1,

then (
C.9
) holds also for
i=d_{\eta}
.

\square

∎

Lemma C.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
with
\gamma<1
, for every
\delta<2-1/\gamma

||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||^{2}=o(n^{-\delta})\hskip 9.24994pt\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}\mbox{-a.s.}

Proof.

The random vectors
\mathbf{g}_{n}
and
\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
take values in the compact set
\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\eta}}:||\mathbf{v}||\leq 1\}
. By Corollary 4.7 in
Martin and Tokdar (2009)

\mathbf{g}_{n}
converges to
\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
,
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}
-a.s.
Moreover,
(\mathbf{g}_{n})
satisfies the
stochastic approximation in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\eta}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{n}+\alpha_{n+1}\mathbf{g}_{n}\circ\left(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{Y_{n+1}}}{p_{\mathbf{g}_{n}}(Y_{n+1})}-\bf 1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{n}+\alpha_{n+1}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{g}_{n})+\alpha_{n+1}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n+1},

with initial value
\mathbf{g}_{0}
, where

\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{g})=\mathbf{g}\circ\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\mathbf{k}_{y}}{p_{\mathbf{g}}(y)}p_{G^{*}}(y)-\bf 1\right),

and

{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n+1}=\mathbf{g}\circ\left(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{Y_{n+1}}}{p_{\mathbf{g}}(Y_{n+1})}-\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\mathbf{k}_{y}}{p_{\mathbf{g}}(y)}p_{G^{*}}(y)\right).

The thesis follows from Theorem 3.1.1 in
Chen (2005)
if we can prove that the following conditions hold:

T1

\alpha_{n}\rightarrow 0
,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}=\infty
,
\alpha_{n+1}^{-1}-\alpha_{n}^{-1}\rightarrow 0
;

T2

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{1-\delta/2}\epsilon_{n}
converges,
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}
-a.s.;

T3

\mathbf{h}
is measurable and locally bounded, and is differentiable at
\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
. Let
\mathbf{H}
be a matrix such that, as
\mathbf{g}\rightarrow\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
,

\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{g})=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*})+\mathbf{r}(g),\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{r}(g_{\eta}^{*})=\mathbf{0},\hskip 9.24994pt||\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{g})||=o(||\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||).

All the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{H}
have negative real parts.

Remark C.5
.

Theorem 3.1.1 in
Chen (2005)
also requests the following condition: T4
There exists a continuously differentiable function
\ell:\Delta_{\eta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\sup_{\delta_{1}\leq{\rm d}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*})\leq\delta_{2}}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{g})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{g}}\ell<0

for every
\delta_{2}>\delta_{1}>0
.
However, a close inspection of the proof of Theorem 3.1.1 in Chen shows that T4 is employed only to ensure convergence of
\mathbf{g}_{n}
towards
\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
. In our case, this property is known to be true.

T1 is obvious. To prove T2, notice that
(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}^{1-\delta/2}\epsilon_{k})_{n\geq 1}
is a martingale. Moreover, there exists a constant
C
such that, for every
i=1,\dots,{d_{\eta}}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{n}E_{G^{*}}((\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}^{1-\delta/2}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k,i})^{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}^{2-\delta}E_{G^{*}}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{k,i})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)^{\gamma(2-\delta)}<\infty,

since
\gamma(2-\delta)>1
and
({\bm{\epsilon}}_{n,i})
are uniformly bounded.
We now prove T3. The function
\mathbf{h}
is continuously differentiable at
\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}
and

\mathbf{H}_{i,j}=-\mathbf{g}_{\eta,i}^{*}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{k_{y}(\vartheta_{i})k_{y}(\vartheta_{j})}{p_{g_{\eta}^{*}}(y)^{2}}p_{G^{*}}(y).

The matrix
\mathbf{H}
can be seen as the product of the diagonal matrix
{\rm diag}(\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*})
, which is positive definite, times
-\mathbf{M}
, with
\mathbf{M}_{i,j}=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{k_{y}(\vartheta_{i})k_{y}(\vartheta_{j})}{p_{G_{\eta}^{*}}(y)^{2}}p_{G^{*}}(y)
. We now prove that
\mathbf{M}
is positive definite. Given a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\eta}}
with
||\mathbf{u}||=1
, we can write that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\eta}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{j}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{k_{y}(\vartheta_{i})k_{y}(\vartheta_{j})}{p_{G_{\eta}^{*}}(y)^{2}}p_{G^{*}}(y)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{p_{G^{*}{(y)}}}{p_{G_{\eta}^{*}}(y)^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\mathbf{u}_{i}k_{y}(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\mathbf{u}_{i}k_{y}(\vartheta_{i})=0
for every
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The only solution to the last equation in
\mathbf{u}
is the zero vector, which is inconsistent with
||\mathbf{u}||=1
. Since the function
\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}
is continuous on the compact set
||\mathbf{u}||=1
, then the minimum is attained, and by the above reasoning it is strictly positive. It follows that
\mathbf{M}
is positive definite.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
,
there exists a constant
C=C(d,\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{d})
such that for every
n\geq 1

\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G_{\eta}^{*})\leq C||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||^{2}.

Proof.

We can write that

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(\hat{\theta}_{g_{n}}(y)-\hat{\theta}_{g_{\eta}^{*}}(y))^{2}p_{g_{\eta}^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y+1}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}\left(g_{n}(\vartheta_{i})-g_{\eta}^{*}(\vartheta_{i})\right)\right)^{2}}{(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}g_{\eta}^{*}(\vartheta_{i}))^{2}}p_{G^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y+1}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}g_{n}(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}(g_{n}(\vartheta_{i})-g_{\eta}^{*}(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}g_{n}(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{i}^{y}e^{-\vartheta_{i}}g_{\eta}^{*}(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}}p_{G^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\leq 4{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{d_{\eta}}^{2}e^{2(\vartheta_{d_{\eta}}-\vartheta_{1})}||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\theta}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{1}{y!}\left(\frac{\theta\vartheta_{d_{\eta}}^{2}}{\vartheta_{1}^{2}}\right)^{y}G^{*}(d\theta)

\displaystyle\leq C||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||^{2},

with
C=4{d_{\eta}}\vartheta_{d_{\eta}}^{2}e^{2(\vartheta_{d_{\eta}}-\vartheta_{1})}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\theta({d_{\eta}}^{2}-1)}G^{*}(d\theta)<+\infty,

by (
C.5
).
Thus,

\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G_{\eta}^{*})\leq C||\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}_{\eta}^{*}||^{2},

with
C<+\infty
.
∎

D
Numerical illustrations: synthetic data

D.1
Preliminaries

We generate synthetic data from a Poisson mixture model, for various choices of the prior (mixing) distribution
G
. In particular, let
n\geq 1
and let
(Y_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(Y_{n},\theta_{n})
be random vectors distributed as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{Poisson}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\hskip 18.49988pt(i=1,\ldots,n),

(D.10)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\hskip 9.24994ptG.

First, we assume
G
to be a Uniform distribution on
[a,b]
; precisely, we set
a=0
and
b=3
. Then, we consider two examples of
G
belonging to the class
\mathcal{G}
of sub-exponential distribution of order
s
, which is defined as

\mathcal{G}=\left\{G\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{+}:\;G([t,\infty))\leq 2e^{-t/s}\ \text{for all }t>0\right\},\hskip 18.49988pts>0.

In particular, we assume
G
to be: i) a Weibull distribution with scale
a
and shape
b
, which belongs to
\mathcal{G}
for
b\geq 1
; ii) a half-Gaussian distribution, namely the distribution of the positive part of a Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, which belongs
\mathcal{G}
for
\sigma>0
. Precisely we set
a=5
and
b=3
for the Weibull, and
\sigma=1
for the half-Gaussian; under this parameterization the Weibull tail is lighter than the half-Gaussian tail. Finally, we consider an example of
G
belonging to the moment class
\mathcal{M}
defined, for any real
M_{p}
, as

\mathcal{M}=\left\{G\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{+}:\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\theta^{p}G(\mathrm{d}\theta)<M_{p}\right\},\hskip 18.49988ptp>0;

see
Shen and Wu (2026, Section 1)
. In particular, we assume
G
to be square-root of half-Cauchy distribution, namely the distribution of the square-root of the positive part of a standard Cauchy random variable. This distribution has heavier tail than both the Weibull distribution and the half-Gaussian distribution.

For each of the above choices of the prior
G
, we assess the QB estimate
\hat{\theta}_{G_{n}}
in (
5
) by comparing it, for fixed
n\geq 1
, with the Oracle, Robbins, ML, and MHD estimates. Consider the posterior mean, or Bayes estimate,

\hat{\theta}_{G}(y)=E_{G}[\theta_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y]=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\theta\,\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)\,G(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\theta)\,G(\mathrm{d}\theta)}=(y+1)\frac{p_{G}(y+1)}{p_{G}(y)},\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(D.11)

The Oracle estimate is obtained by replacing
G
in (
D.11
) with the “true”, or oracle, prior
G^{\ast}
, namely the Uniform, Weibull, half-Gaussian, or square-root of half-Cauchy priors that generate the
\theta_{i}
’s in (
D.10
), and evaluating the marginal likelihood
p_{G^{\ast}}
numerically via the trapezoidal rule. The Robbins estimate is obtained by replacing
p_{G}
in (
D.11
) with the empirical distribution
\hat{p}_{n}(y)=n^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}I(Y_{i}=y)
, for
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The ML and MHD estimates are obtained by replacing
G
in (
D.11
) with its nonparametric maximum likelihood estimate and its minimum Hellinger distance estimate, respectively, both computed via the vertex direction algorithm of
Jana et al. (2025, Algorithm 1)
, which is based on a discretization of
G
over a finite grid.

As a measure of estimation accuracy, we consider the empirical mean squared error (
e-MSE
). For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
be the values generated from
G
in (
D.10
), and let
(\hat{\theta}_{G}(y_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\theta}_{G}(y_{n}))
be the corresponding estimates, namely the Oracle, Robbins, ML, MHD, of QB estimates. The
e-MSE
is defined as

\textsc{e-MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{G}(y_{i})-\theta_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

When
\hat{\theta}_{G}
is the oracle estimate
\hat{\theta}_{G^{\ast}}
, the
e-MSE
is referred to as the empirical minimum mean squared error (
e-MMSE
), i.e.

\textsc{e-MMSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{G^{\ast}}(y_{i})-\theta_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

Finally, for the Robbins, ML, MHD, and QB estimates, we define the empirical regret (
e-REGRET
) as
\textsc{e-Regret}=\textsc{e-MSE}-\textsc{e-MMSE}
, namely the excess empirical squared error with respect to the oracle benchmark.

For each of the above choices of the prior
G
, we also provide the QB credible interval
I_{G_{n}}
in (
11
), which, for a fixed level
1-\alpha
, is constructed by considering a uniform grid of
50
pairs of tuning parameters
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
satisfying the constraint
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\alpha
and then selecting, by a simple grid search, the pair of parameters that yields the shortest credible interval. Specifically, we consider an equally spaced grid
\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{(\beta_{1,\ell},\beta_{2,\ell}):\ell=1,\ldots,50\}
, with
\beta_{1,\ell}\in(0,\alpha)
and
\beta_{2,\ell}=\alpha-\beta_{1,\ell}
. For each pair
(\beta_{1,\ell},\beta_{2,\ell})\in\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}
, we compute the lower and upper envelopes
\underline{F}^{\beta_{1,\ell}}_{G_{n}}(\cdot\mid y)
and
\overline{F}^{\beta_{1,\ell}}_{G_{n}}(\cdot\mid y)
defined in (
9
)–(
10
). We then obtain the QB credible interval

I^{(\ell)}_{G_{n}}(y)=[L^{(\ell)}_{n}(y),U^{(\ell)}_{n}(y)]\cap\Theta,

where

L^{(\ell)}_{n}(y)=\sup\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\overline{F}^{\beta_{1,\ell}}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)\leq\frac{\beta_{2,\ell}}{2}\right\}

and

U^{(\ell)}_{n}(y)=\inf\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\underline{F}^{\beta_{1,\ell}}_{G_{n}}(\theta\mid y)\geq 1-\frac{\beta_{2,\ell}}{2}\right\}.

Now, the selected pair of tuning parameters is the one that minimizes the length of the credible interval, namely

\ell^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{1\leq\ell\leq 50}\left\{U^{(\ell)}_{n}(y)-L^{(\ell)}_{n}(y)\right\},

and the reported QB credible interval is
I_{G_{n}}(y)=I^{(\ell^{\ast})}_{G_{n}}(y)
. This interval is compared with the corresponding Oracle credible interval of level
1-\alpha
, which is the
(1-\alpha)
-level posterior quantile obtained under the “true”, or oracle, prior
G^{\ast}
, namely the Uniform, Weibull, half-Gaussian, or square-root of half-Cauchy priors that generate the
\theta_{i}
’s in (
D.10
). In particular, in order to define the Oracle credible interval, for
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
let

F_{G^{\ast}}(\theta\mid y)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\vartheta)\,G^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)}{\int_{\Theta}\text{Poisson}(y\mid\vartheta)\,G^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta)},\hskip 18.49988pt\theta\in\Theta

be the posterior distribution function of
\theta
given
Y=y
under the prior
G^{\ast}
. The Oracle credible interval is then defined as

I_{G^{\ast}}(y)=[L_{\ast}(y),U_{\ast}(y)],

where

L_{\ast}(y)=\inf\{\theta\in\Theta\text{ : }F_{G^{\ast}}(\theta\mid y)\geq\alpha/2\},\hskip 18.49988ptU_{\ast}(y)=\inf\{\theta\in\Theta\text{ : }F_{G^{\ast}}(\theta\mid y)\geq 1-\alpha/2\},

with the posterior distribution function
F_{G^{\ast}}
being evaluated numerically via the trapezoidal rule, as for the Oracle estimate.

As a measure of comparison between the QB credible interval
I_{G_{n}}
and the corresponding Oracle credible interval
I_{G^{\ast}}
, we consider the empirical mean absolute relative length discrepancy (
e-MARLD
). For
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

\ell_{n}(y)=U_{n}(y)-L_{n}(y),\hskip 18.49988pt\ell_{\ast}(y)=U_{\ast}(y)-L_{\ast}(y)

denote the lengths of the QB and Oracle credible intervals, respectively. We define the absolute relative length discrepancy

D_{n}(y)=\left|\frac{\ell_{n}(y)}{\ell_{\ast}(y)}-1\right|=\frac{|\ell_{n}(y)-\ell_{\ast}(y)|}{\ell_{\ast}(y)},

and the
e-MARLD
by

\textsc{e-MARLD}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{n}(y_{i}).

The closer
e-MARLD
is to
0
, the closer, on average, the lengths of the QB credible intervals are to those of the Oracle credible intervals. In particular, a value
\textsc{e-MARLD}=x\in[0,1]
means that, on average, the length of the QB credible interval differs from that of the corresponding Oracle credible interval by a proportion
x
.

G
Multidimensional extension

Throughout, we denote by
k(\cdot\,|\,\theta)
the Poisson kernel with mean
\theta>0
, i.e.
k(y\,|\,\theta)=\theta^{y}\text{e}^{-\theta}/y!
for
y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The results in Section
2
extend, with minor modifications, to the k-dimension coordinatewise
independent Poisson model.
We assume that
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{k})
is a
k
-dimensional random vector with independent coordinates
\mathbf{Y}_{i}\sim\text{Poisson}(\bm{\theta}_{i})
, and denote

k(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}e^{-\bm{\theta}_{j}}\frac{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{\mathbf{y}_{j}}}{\mathbf{y}_{j}!},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k},\bm{\theta}_{j}\in\Theta\;(j=1,\dots,k).

(G.12)

Denote
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\theta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{k})\in\Theta^{k}
.
If
\bm{\theta}
has probability distribution
G
on
\Theta^{k}
, then the marginal probability mass function of
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{k})
is

p_{G}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\Theta^{k}}k(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})G(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}).

(G.13)

Given a sequence
(\mathbf{Y}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of random vectors with independent coordinates
\mathbf{Y}_{n,j}\sim\text{Poisson}(\bm{\theta}_{n,j})
, we consider the problem of estimating the unknown parameters
\bm{\theta}_{n,j}
.

If we knew that the random vectors
(\bm{\theta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
were i.i.d. with a known probability distribution
G
on
\Theta^{k}
, we could estimate
\bm{\theta}_{n,j}
, given that
\mathbf{Y}_{n}=\mathbf{y}
, with

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,j}(\mathbf{y})=\mathds{E}_{g}[\bm{\theta}_{n,j}|\mathbf{Y}_{n}=\mathbf{y}]=(\mathbf{y}_{j}+1)\frac{p_{G}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j})}{p_{G}(\mathbf{y})},

(G.14)

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is a
k
-dimensional vector with
\mathbf{e}_{j,i}=0
for
i\neq j
and
\mathbf{e}_{j,j}=1
.

Being
G
unknown, we can estimate it recursively as

G_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})=(1-\alpha_{n+1})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})+\alpha_{n+1}\frac{k(\mathbf{Y}_{n+1}\mid\bm{\theta})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})}{\int_{\Theta^{k}}k(\mathbf{Y}_{n+1}\mid\bm{\theta})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})},

(G.15)

and adopt for
\bm{\theta}_{G,j}(\mathbf{y})
the plug-in estimate

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n},j}(\mathbf{y})=(\mathbf{y}_{j}+1)\frac{p_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j})}{p_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})}\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k},

(G.16)

where

p_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\Theta^{k}}k(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})G_{n}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}).

(G.17)

We now model the sequence of observations
(\mathbf{Y}_{n})
, assuming that, given the information available at time
n
,
\mathbf{Y}_{n+1}
follows the probability mass function
p_{G_{n}}
. In other words, denoting by
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the natural filtration of
(\mathbf{Y}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we assume that
\mathbf{Y}_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\sim p_{G_{n}}
.
Under this assumption, the sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, converging
\mathds{P}
- a.s. as
n\rightarrow\infty
to a random probability distribution
\tilde{G}
on
\Theta^{k}
. In particular, for every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
, as
n\rightarrow+\infty

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n},j}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{G},j}(\mathbf{y})=(\mathbf{y}_{j}+1)\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j})}{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y})}\hskip 18.49988pt\mathds{P}\,\text{-}\,a.s.

(G.18)

Theorem G.1
.

Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be non increasing and satisfy
\sum_{n\geq 1}(\alpha_{n}^{2}/\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{n}^{2})^{2}<+\infty
, and let
b_{n}=(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_{k}^{2})^{-1}
.
For every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
,
b_{n}^{1/2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}))
converges, in the sense of almost-sure conditional convergence, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, to a centered (i.e., zero-mean) Gaussian kernel with the random covariance matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y})
, whose
(j,j^{\prime})
element is

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{\tilde{G},j,j^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{G},j}(\mathbf{y})\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{G},j^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y})\sum_{z\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{G}}(z)\left[\left(\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mid z)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j})}-\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}\mid z)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\left(\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j^{\prime}}\mid z)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{e}_{j^{\prime}})}-\frac{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y}\mid z)}{p_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\right].

The proof follows the same steps as the proof of Theorem
2.2
. For
\alpha_{n}=(\alpha+n)^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(1/2,1]
, the theorem holds with
b_{n}=(2\gamma-1)/n^{2\gamma-1}
. In particular, with
\alpha_{n}=(1+n)^{-1}
,
b_{n}=n
. Theorem
2.2
provides a tool to obtain asymptotic credible regions for
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{G}}(\mathbf{y})
, for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
.

We now consider the Bayesian regret under a “true” Poisson compound model for
(\mathbf{Y}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, under which, for any
n\geq 1
: i) the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}_{n}
given
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{n}
is a product of Poisson distributions with parameters
\theta^{\ast}_{n,j}
; ii) the
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{n}
’s are i.i.d. from the “true” prior distribution, say the oracle prior
G^{\ast}
. By the same techniques used in the univariate case, the frequentist guarantees can be studied in this more general setting. The regret becomes

\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{*})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}}||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G^{*}}(\mathbf{y})||^{2}p_{G^{*}}(\mathbf{y}).

(G.19)

The following result extends Theorem
2.4
to the multivariate setting.

Theorem G.2
.

Let
G^{\ast}
be an oracle prior on
(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{k}
, and, for each
n\geq 1
, let
G_{n}
be as in (
G.15
) with
\Theta
compact. Let
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
be the weak closure of the class
\mathcal{S}
of probability measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{k}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to
G_{0}
and let

G^{\dagger}=\text{argmin}_{G\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\textsc{KL}(p_{G^{*}}\,\|\,p_{G})
, where
KL
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence and, for every
G
,
p_{G}(\mathbf{y})=\int k(\mathbf{y}\mid\bm{\theta})G(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})
. Then,

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G^{{\dagger}}}(\mathbf{y})
for every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
and
\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{\dagger})\!=\!\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}}||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{y})-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{G^{\dagger}}(\mathbf{y})||^{2}p_{G^{*}}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow 0
,
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}
-a.s. In particular, if
G^{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
G_{0}
, then
\textsc{Regret}(G_{n},G^{*})\rightarrow 0
,
\mathds{P}_{G^{*}}
-a.s.

The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
2.4
.
