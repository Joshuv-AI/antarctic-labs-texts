Links between influenza epidemics, weather characteristics and all-cause mortality in the Czech Republic, 1982–2020
Authors: Hana Hanzlíková, Eva Plavcová, Jan Kyselý, Jan Kynčl, Marek Malý, Aleš Urban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn temperate climates, influenza follows a seasonal pattern with peak incidence in winter and contributes significantly to excess winter mortality. The relationships among weather variability, influenza, and human health are complex, and the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain. This study investigated how influenza epidemics and all-cause mortality in the nationwide population of the Czech Republic are linked to weather characteristics.MethodsA quasi-Poisson regression model was used to quantify excess mortality during 25 influenza epidemics over 1982–2020. Mean seasonal deviations of meteorological variables were calculated for periods from six weeks before to four weeks after the peak of influenza epidemics, categorised by their impact on excess mortality (mild, moderate or severe) and by the predominant influenza virus (A/H3N2, A/H1N1 or B). Temporal associations between individual meteorological variables and the onset of influenza epidemics were evaluated by bootstrapping and logistic regression-based explanatory model analysis.ResultsThe analysis revealed that temperature, followed by absolute humidity, was the most significant weather indicator associated with the epidemic onset. Severe epidemics with the highest mortality impacts, predominantly associated with the A/H3N2 virus, occurred during prolonged periods of significantly below-average temperatures, while mild epidemics with lower mortality impacts, typically associated with the A/H1N1 virus, occurred during prevailing average or above-average temperatures. In non-epidemic seasons, corresponding periods with increased influenza activity were characterised by significantly above-average temperatures.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that weather indicators, particularly air temperature and absolute humidity, play an important role in the timing and severity of epidemics, and that these parameters differ with the predominant influenza virus. In the temperate climate of Central Europe, the circulation of the A/H3N2 virus in the population, combined with prolonged periods of low temperatures, results in influenza epidemics with the largest excess mortality. These findings highlight the need for a better understanding of weather conditions that increase influenza transmission and survival, and for distinguishing between predominant virus subtypes when informing at-risk populations, implementing preventive measures, and mitigating negative impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25861-9.Keywords: Influenza epidemics, Weather, Air temperature, Humidity, Mortality, Temperate climate, Central Europe

Background

Excess winter mortality is a well-known phenomenon generally associated with low temperatures [1]. In temperate climates, influenza activity increases during the winter season [2], and its contribution to excess winter deaths has received increasing attention in recent years. Influenza poses a significant global health, social, and economic burden, causing approximately 290,000 to 650,000 respiratory-related deaths annually [3], which accounts for only a part of the total mortality burden of influenza [4–6]. In temperate regions, influenza activity typically peaks in winter, whereas in the tropics, respiratory pathogens exhibit year-round persistence, driven by the rainy seasons [7, 8].

In recent decades, two types of influenza viruses (A and B) have been circulating among the human population: two subtypes of the A virus (A/H3N2 and A/H1N1) and two lineages of the B virus (Yamagata and Victoria) [3]. Influenza can lead to severe health conditions, particularly in vulnerable population groups such as the elderly and young children [9]. The immune response is specific to the type and strain of the virus, and vaccination is one of the most effective and safe methods for preventing influenza infection.

Research has shown that exposure to low temperatures increases the likelihood of respiratory infections and cold-related deaths [10–12]. Moreover, other studies have indicated that fluctuations in temperature, rather than low values alone, are associated with an increased incidence of influenza illness [13, 14]. Besides air temperature, other meteorological factors that vary seasonally, such as absolute and relative humidity [11, 15–20], cloud cover, and UV radiation [21–23], may also influence seasonal variations in influenza. Other potential factors that influence the seasonality of influenza epidemics include virus characteristics [24], such as stability, survival time, and virulence at low air temperatures [12]. Behavioural, cultural, and social patterns, such as winter indoor crowding and holiday isolation, may also affect virus transmission [11, 25]. A better understanding of these complex mechanisms can support early-warning systems and guide the timing of public health measures.

While research has examined the links between influenza activity, excess mortality, and weather patterns in different parts of the world, significant gaps still remain. Previous studies have often focused on specific influenza seasons, did not differentiate between influenza virus types/subtypes, or analysed only a few meteorological variables. Furthermore, long-term analyses spanning several decades that integrate epidemiological and meteorological data remain rare, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe. In the Czech Republic, significant excess mortality associated with influenza epidemics was reported in several papers [6, 24, 26]. While previous studies have also identified increased mortality associated with cold spells [27]and other winter extreme weather events [28], our study links the two major topics – weather and influenza – by exploring how weather factors may modify the mortality impacts of influenza epidemics.

This study aimed to examine the associations among influenza epidemics, weather variability, and excess winter all-cause mortality in the Czech Republic (38 complete winter seasons, 25 registered influenza epidemics), with a focus on the predominant influenza virus type in each season, including A/H3N2 and A/H1N1 subtypes. We examined air temperature, sunshine duration, cloud cover, absolute humidity, and relative humidity as weather characteristics, identified meteorological conditions associated with epidemics, and determined the links between the timing and magnitude of influenza epidemics and weather characteristics. Influenza epidemics and associated weather patterns were compared with corresponding periods of increased incidence of acute respiratory infections (ARIs) in seasons when the epidemic threshold was not exceeded.

Methods

Meteorological data

Meteorological data, including daily mean air temperature, relative humidity, sunshine duration, and cloud cover, were obtained from the Czech Hydrometeorological Institute. The mean temperature series was calculated by averaging data from seven high-quality weather stations representative of the area of the Czech Republic [29]. The selection of the stations ensures that no important relocations occurred during the period under examination (1982–2020), and the sites are distributed fairly evenly across the area and population under study.

Influenza/ARI data

The National Institute of Public Health (NIPH) provided the weekly data on acute respiratory infection (ARI) morbidity, collected from general practitioners for reporting purposes. These ARI reports include every clinical diagnosis of acute upper respiratory tract infection (as defined by the International Classification of Diseases, tenth Revision (ICD-10), codes J00, J02, J04, J05, and J06) and influenza (ICD-10 codes J10.1, J10.8, J11.1, and J11.8). These are summary reports in which the individual ICD-10 codes are no longer distinguishable. The weekly counts of ARIs were interpolated to daily values to improve estimates of epidemic onset, peak, and termination, and to enable comparisons with daily mortality and meteorological data. The weekly values were decomposed into daily values via locally weighted regression smoothing [30], analogously to Royé et al. [31]. The minimum epidemic duration was set at seven days based on the weekly source data.

The onset of epidemics was identified when the reported weekly ARI counts exceeded the official national ARI threshold established by the NIPH, determined using the long-term records of ARI. An influenza epidemic occurred when the national epidemic threshold of 2000 reported ARI cases per 100,000 population per week was exceeded. Since 2009, a new threshold of 1600 reported cases per week has been adopted to compensate for the general decline in medical appointments resulting from changes in sick leave policy in 2008 [32].

The National Reference Laboratory for Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses records laboratory-confirmed cases of individual ARI viruses during each season. Surveillance samples are systematically collected from all regions, encompassing both child and adult populations. Influenza viruses predominate in ARI epidemics when the incidence per 100,000 population exceeds a designated threshold [26]; therefore, we refer to these epidemics as influenza epidemics throughout the text.

The analysis covers the period preceding the COVID-19 outbreak to prevent any significant impact on influenza epidemics caused by lockdowns, quarantine interventions, and potential competition among viruses for hosts. The last registered influenza epidemic in this study occurred between January 23 and February 10, 2020, before the first identified COVID-19 cases in the Czech Republic in early March and the subsequent national lockdown.

Mortality data

Daily all-cause mortality data were acquired from the Czech Statistical Office and the Institute of Health Information and Statistics of the Czech Republic. The national mortality dataset, covering the entire population, contains 4,436,890 records from January 1982 to June 2020.

This dataset was used to calculate daily excess mortality (relative mortality deviations from the baseline) for the follow-up analysis. A generalised additive model with quasi-Poisson distribution family with a log link function (the R package mgcv [33]) was used for this purpose, as the data overdispersion was confirmed by the Cameron & Trivedi test [34] (a functiondispersiontestfrom the R package AER; see Fig. S1 for more details). Analysis of links between meteorological patterns and influenza epidemics

The following steps were taken to analyse the links between weather patterns and influenza epidemics:

Each extended winter season (from November to March) of the study period was categorised as either "epidemic" or "non-epidemic", based on whether an influenza epidemic was identified (see the definition above). The dominant influenza virus (A/H3N2, A/H1N1, B) was assigned to each season based on influenza activity data taken from the National Influenza Surveillance coordinated by the National Influenza Centre at the NIPH [26].

In 'non-epidemic' seasons, influenza activity periods (IAPs) were defined as two weeks preceding and following the ARI seasonal peak—the day with the highest number of interpolated ARI incidence during a given season. This allowed us to compare weather patterns during influenza epidemics with corresponding periods of increased ARI incidence in seasons when the epidemic threshold was not exceeded. The two-week timeframe before and after the peak incidence of ARIs was selected based on the average length of influenza epidemics in our dataset.

Using excess mortality data determined by the quasi-Poisson regression model, the mean and cumulative excess mortality were calculated for each influenza epidemic and IAP. To adjust for seasonal patterns, daily deviations of individual meteorological variables were calculated as differences from their long-term averages for the corresponding day of the year over the study period. Consequently, mean meteorological patterns for the influenza epidemic/IAP groups were calculated as mean deviations of each variable on days D − 42/D + 28 before/after the peak of influenza epidemics and IAPs. The significance of the mean deviations over these 71-day sequences in the tested group of epidemics/IAPs was assessed via a bootstrapping method with R = 10,000. The average 71-day pattern of each tested variable was compared with the 5th, 10th, 90th, and 95th quantiles of a variable distribution obtained by randomly selected sequences of the same variable in the extended winter season. The number of sequences selected in each iteration was identical to the number of epidemics/IAPs in the tested group (i.e., this number may differ for each line in a plot). Any deviations that fell below the 5th or exceeded the 95th quantile of the distribution were considered statistically significant. The window of D − 42/D + 28 (− 6 weeks/+ 4 weeks) before/after the peak of influenza epidemics/IAPs covers the typical epidemic duration [e.g., 36, 37]. The peak-centred analysis enabled us to compare the results for influenza epidemics and their equivalent periods (IAPs) in non-epidemic seasons.

As the final step of the analysis, we employed a logistic regression-based explanatory model analysis to test the importance of each variable in the overall effect of weather variability on the epidemic onset. Based on the results from previous steps, we assessed the associations between two-week averages of the selected meteorological variables and the exact onset of influenza epidemics via a logistic regression model [38]. The epidemic onset was defined via a binary variable that indicated days of the influenza epidemic onset as "1" and all other days of the study period as "0". All meteorological variables were considered together in one model. Consequently, we employed an explanatory model analysis approach introduced by Biecek and Burzykowski [39]to assess the importance of individual weather variables in the logistic regression model. The mean-variable importance for epidemic onset was quantified as the mean difference in the log-likelihood function (Log-Loss difference) over 1000 permutations of the logistic regression model. Log-Loss is a goodness-of-fit function suitable for logistic regression models that quantifies the overall performance of a predictive model. The explanatory model analysis compares the change in log-likelihood if a selected predictor is omitted from the main model. The larger the mean Log-Loss difference, the more important the given predictor is in the model [39]. We used a model explainer in the R packageDALEX to run the explanatory model analysis.

Results

Statistics of influenza epidemics

The characteristics of the epidemic and non-epidemic seasons from 1982/83 to 2019/20 are shown in Table 1. During the 38 winter seasons, 25 influenza epidemics occurred. Overall, two influenza A virus subtypes (A/H1N1 and A/H3N2) and B viruses circulated in the population; the A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 subtypes were predominant in 14 and 7 seasons, respectively, while the B virus predominated in 4 seasons. The pandemic A/H1N1pdm09 virus was first observed during the 2009/10 season, causing the only epidemic that started in November throughout the study period and replacing the former A/H1N1 virus in the population in the following seasons. After examining the mortality effects of the pre-2009 A/H1N1 and the post-2009 A/H1N1pdm09 viruses, we identified only slight differences. The daily excess mortality associated with the four A/H1N1pdm09 epidemics averaged 5.0%, while the previous three pre-2009 A/H1N1 epidemics had an average daily excess mortality of − 2.0% (Table 1). Consequently, we use the term A/H1N1 to encompass both lineages in our analyses. Given that mortality rates for both remained close to baseline levels and their overall impact was relatively minor compared with A/H3N2 and influenza B epidemics, we categorised them jointly under the A/H1N1 label. Another reason for not analysing the two A/H1N1 lineages separately was that such a comparison might reflect long-term changes in population vulnerability rather than true differences between the viruses themselves.

The mean estimated cumulative excess mortality associated with the influenza epidemics was 1245 (95% CI: 778 to 1712), equivalent to an average daily excess mortality of 12% across all epidemics. Mortality varies widely between epidemics depending on the magnitude of influenza activity (Fig. 1b) and the predominant virus type/subtype circulating in the population, and mortality was considerably lower for the A/H1N1 virus epidemics than for the A/H3N2 and B epidemics (Fig. 1c). The average number of excess deaths associated with the A/H3N2 virus epidemics was 1791 (95% CI: 1195 to 2387; representing a 17.3% increase in daily excess mortality), whereas 144 excess deaths (95% CI: − 125 to 413; 2.0%) were associated with the A/H1N1 epidemics. For the B virus epidemics, the daily excess mortality average ranged from 5.5% to 18.3% (Table S3).

Eight severe epidemics were predominantly associated with the A/H3N2 virus, except for one, in which a dominant B virus was identified. An extraordinary influenza epidemic in the 1995/96 season had the largest impact on mortality during the study period (3470 excess deaths corresponding to a 51% increase in mortality). Two different A/H3N2 viruses co-circulated, likely contributing to the exceptionally high excess mortality [24]. This epidemic was also unusual because of its early onset and peak in mid-December.

The influenza epidemics peaked, on average, two weeks after crossing the epidemic threshold, as shown in Fig. 1 and Table S2. For simplicity, we considered D − 14 as an indicator of the onset of the epidemic/IAP when describing the mean patterns of the meteorological variables. Additionally, severe epidemics typically lasted longer; therefore, ARI morbidity was significantly larger four weeks before the peak, and their onset was earlier (on average, D − 21, Fig. 1b) than that of mild and moderate epidemics.

Air temperature

The temperature patterns during epidemics differ considerably from those in non-epidemic seasons (Fig. 2a). While influenza epidemics were associated with significant negative temperature deviations (below the 5th quantile) during the 14 days before the ARI peak (with an average value of − 1.5 °C), IAPs were associated with average to above-average temperatures two weeks before the ARI peak (Fig. 2a, blue line). Figure 2b indicates that the intensity and duration of negative temperature deviations were largest during severe epidemics (− 2.1 °C, below the 5th quantile for the 25 days before the ARI peak). Additionally, a similar pattern was observed for epidemics associated with the A/H3N2 and B viruses, whereas temperature patterns during mild epidemics corresponded to A/H1N1 virus epidemics (Fig. 2b, c).

Before the epidemics' onset, a noticeable drop in temperature was observed, with the largest deviations occurring before the severe epidemics. Figure 2b shows that the timing of this temperature drop varies, with a time lag relative to the start of epidemics—the average onset of mild/moderate and severe epidemics occurred on days D − 10/D − 11 and D − 21, respectively (Table S2). In contrast, during non-epidemic seasons, a sharp increase in temperature was observed around the onset of IAPs, reaching significantly above-average values two weeks before the ARI peak (Fig. 2a, blue line).

Around the peak of the epidemics (day D0), there was a noticeable temperature increase, which remained above average until the end of the epidemics, when the incidence of ARIs dropped below the epidemic threshold and continued to decline, on average, two weeks after the peak of the epidemics (approximately day D + 14, Figs. 1 and 2).

The results revealed that severe epidemics occurred during prolonged periods of unusually low temperatures, which may have contributed to the highest cumulative excess mortality associated with these epidemics. To investigate the relationship between prolonged periods of low temperatures and the severity of epidemics, we calculated the total number of consecutive days with a daily mean temperature below 0 °C during each epidemic (Table S2). The chosen threshold aligns with research findings in temperate climates, which indicate that the peak of influenza activity occurs below this threshold or that influenza virus detection decreases above it [11, 13, 40]. During severe epidemics, the daily mean temperature was below 0 °C for an average of 20 consecutive days, accounting for 53% of their mean duration. Notably, four A/H3N2 epidemics with low mortality occurred during periods with only five consecutive days of temperatures below 0 °C, accounting for 20% of the epidemics' lengths. In moderate/mild epidemics, on average, there were nine/seven consecutive days with temperatures below 0 °C, accounting for 37%/35% of the mean duration.

These findings show that both the intensity and duration of cold periods are primary factors influencing the epidemic's impact on mortality, and that the temperature pattern in the weeks following the onset of an epidemic may affect the duration, magnitude, and termination of the outbreak.

Sunshine duration, cloud cover, absolute humidity and relative humidity

The onset and peak of the epidemics were associated with distinct patterns in other weather variables than air temperature, as illustrated in Fig. 3 showing deviations from the mean annual cycles of sunshine duration, total cloud amount, absolute humidity, and relative humidity 6 weeks before and 4 weeks after the peak of ARI incidence, analogously to Fig. 2. The absolute humidity, in particular, followed the temperature pattern, as both variables are highly correlated. During moderate and severe epidemics associated with excess mortality, absolute humidity was significantly below average two weeks before the ARI peak (Fig. 3b). Conversely, during the IAPs, the absolute humidity was above average (Fig. 3a). The relative humidity was significantly higher during the epidemics, whereas it was well below the seasonal average in the corresponding IAPs during non-epidemic seasons (Fig. 3a).

In addition, sunshine duration and cloud cover showed distinct patterns from D − 28 to D − 14, approximately two weeks before the epidemic onset. In non-epidemic seasons, there were above-average levels of sunshine duration during this period (Fig. 3a), whereas in mild and moderate epidemics, the deviations in sunshine duration were close to zero (Fig. 3b). Below-average sunshine duration was observed around the onset of severe epidemics. The opposite pattern was observed for cloud cover, which was accompanied by above-average relative humidity two weeks before and during the epidemics.

The results for individual influenza viruses (shown in Fig. 3c) indicate that specific weather conditions favour the predominance of a particular type/subtype of influenza virus. A/H3N2 epidemics were associated with significantly above-average cloud cover and negative deviations in sunshine duration around the peak, as well as significantly above-average relative humidity. The weather conditions accompanying the A/H1N1 epidemics were different. Above-average absolute humidity and relative humidity prevailed two weeks before the onset and peak. The B virus epidemics occurred in late winter, during unusually cold and dry weather, with significantly negative deviations in both absolute and relative humidity and more sunshine, which differed from the A/H3N2 and A/H1N1 epidemics.

Comparison of the epidemics based on their timing within the season, average temperature and duration is shown in Fig. 4. Epidemics that peaked later during the season (depicted as the second half of February/March in Fig. 4) were associated with below-average temperatures, which typically lasted longer (Fig. 4c) and had a stronger impact on mortality (Fig. 4a). The three longest epidemics recorded (in the 1985/86, 2002/03, and 2017/18 seasons, lasting 64, 44, and 48 days, respectively; see Table 1), peaking in late February/early March, were associated with long-lasting periods of below-average temperatures that persisted into March (see Table S3 in Additional file 1).

Discussion

Influenza virus as a primary determinant of seasonal mortality patterns

The results show that mortality during influenza epidemics varies considerably between seasons, and the magnitude of overall excess winter mortality largely depends on the dominant influenza type/subtype in a given season, with much higher mortality impacts associated with the A/H3N2 virus than with the A/H1N1 (including the A/H1N1pdm09 virus, which replaced the A/H1N1 virus during the 2009 pandemic). Similar influenza-subtype-related mortality patterns have been reported, e.g., in Spain [42], Portugal [43], Italy [44], Greece [45], the Netherlands [46], and the U.S. [47], suggesting that circulating virus types/subtypes are the main factors driving the seasonal variation in influenza-related mortality. Moreover, a study of global seasonal influenza–associated respiratory mortality showed that the predominant circulating subtype in each season was the key predictor of excess respiratory mortality, with the most severe seasons linked to circulation of influenza A/H3N2 among older adults [48].

Our study highlights the need to analyse influenza types/subtypes separately to understand how weather influences influenza-related mortality. Many studies lack long-term data on circulating types/subtypes, making it difficult to determine their specific impacts. Examining only influenza A and B can be inconclusive, as grouping A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 into a single "A" category overlooks their different mortality impacts, circulation patterns, and temperature and humidity preferences. This may underestimate or obscure the true influence of weather on influenza-related mortality. Quantifying subtype-specific mortality can enhance the understanding of influenza's public health impact, particularly in relation to weather effects.

Can winter weather influence the predominant type of influenza virus during a given season?

The results of this study indicate that epidemics of different predominant influenza viruses are associated with specific weather patterns. The A/H1N1 epidemics differ from others in being preceded by low sunshine levels, high cloud cover, and high relative humidity. The A/H3N2 epidemics are distinct in their weather conditions, with long-lasting, significantly below-average temperatures, anomalously low absolute humidity and sunshine hours, and high relative humidity. Finally, the B virus epidemics occur in anomalously low temperatures, low absolute and relative humidity, and high levels of sunshine. The A/H1N1 epidemics did not occur in late winter or spring across the 38 seasons studied and dominated in average winter weather conditions with low impacts on mortality, in contrast to the severe A/H3N2 and B virus epidemics, associated with anomalously low temperatures, absolute humidity and high mortality, peaking also in late winter and early spring. These findings suggest that winter weather conditions may influence the prevalence of certain viruses during that season. While some studies support our finding that the incidence of influenza viruses A and B was significantly associated with low temperature and absolute humidity [e.g., 12, 49], other studies did not find any significant association between the predominant influenza virus during epidemics and weather patterns [50]. However, a limitation of these studies is that influenza A subtypes (A/H1N1 and A/H3N2) were not analysed separately. Chong et al. [49] found that absolute humidity had a stronger association with influenza epidemics than relative humidity, and that high relative humidity was less associated with B virus epidemics, which is consistent with our results. Research into weather conditions favourable for virus transmission and retention of infectivity will be essential for understanding the behaviour of influenza viruses in the future.

Temperature- or virus-dependent mortality?

The results suggest that the impact of epidemics on mortality depends on both temperature and the predominant influenza virus. In particular, the coincidence of A/H3N2 epidemics with anomalously low temperatures leads to prolonged outbreaks and higher mortality, whereas circulation of the same virus during average winter temperatures results in milder epidemics of shorter duration. These findings indicate that different influenza viruses thrive differently at specific temperatures, resulting in varying impacts on health and mortality. Previous research has shown that in temperate climates, low temperatures and respiratory infections can exacerbate excess winter mortality [51], while prolonged exposure to cold increases the risk of infection [52]. Although many studies document a general association of influenza A and B epidemics with low temperature and low absolute humidity [e.g.,10, 11, 12, 15, 53], a few studies have shown different effects of temperature and humidity on influenza prevalence by subtype [e.g., 54]. In addition, several studies confirm that A/H3N2 epidemics have a larger impact on mortality and morbidity than other subtypes [45]. These findings highlight the complex associations between weather variability, influenza epidemic outbreaks and increased mortality risk during winter/cold season that require further investigation.

The Christmas phenomenon

A decline in the incidence of ARIs has been documented annually during the Christmas and New Year holidays. However, this reduction in morbidity does not accurately reflect the actual situation, as a considerable number of individuals experiencing illness during this period do not seek medical attention, except for cases requiring hospitalisation (it is customary for general practitioners to refrain from offering their services during the Christmas holidays or to do so only to a limited extent). After the New Year, however, the incidence of ARIs usually increases, most likely because the population returns to school and employment and because the influenza virus spreads further. Nevertheless, this period may contribute to delaying the epidemic, as many patients recover and the disease does not spread further. A shift in the typical seasonal epidemic trajectory and a reduction in influenza transmission during the Christmas holidays were also reported in the U.S. [55]and Belgium [56]. The authors concluded that school closures altered influenza dynamics, delaying epidemic peaks rather than age-specific interactions or travel. Our study revealed that, in addition to the population's behaviour during the Christmas holidays, intraseasonal air temperature variability may significantly affect the timing of seasonal influenza epidemics by creating favourable conditions for virus transmission and survival. The period following the New Year is important for the onset of an influenza epidemic in central Europe, as there is typically a rise in ARIs. The virus may spread more easily in favourable weather conditions, such as low temperatures and low absolute humidity [20]. This could result in an earlier exceedance of the ARI morbidity epidemic threshold and an earlier peak in observed cases. The results demonstrate a comparable pattern in the occurrence of temperature drops preceding the onset of epidemics, despite their peaks occurring at different phases of the winter season and varying time lags from the end of the Christmas holidays (see Fig.5). Furthermore, the onset of epidemics reaching their peak in the first half of February was preceded by two weeks of unusually high temperatures. This suggests that the onset of these epidemics might be delayed compared with those peaking in January because of a later temperature drop.

Onset and termination of epidemics

Our results further showed a decrease in temperature before the start of influenza epidemics. The combination of relatively high temperature and absolute humidity, followed by a sudden drop in these meteorological parameters, has been identified as the factor with the strongest impact on the risk of influenza in Finland [13]and Japan [57]. Additionally, Sundell et al. [58] reported a strong association between the onset of influenza epidemics and a decrease in temperature one week before the outbreak in Sweden. The authors suggested that a sudden decrease in air temperature could trigger an influenza epidemic by facilitating aerosol transmission in dry air.

The rising temperatures towards the end of winter could affect the duration and termination of epidemics. Experimental research has shown that influenza virus survival depends on a specific temperature optimum, with low temperatures and UV radiation preserving its infectivity [22]; therefore, the temperature rise at the end of the winter season, together with UV radiation, may lead to virus deactivation and epidemic termination. Our study indicates that the end of epidemics is associated with a significant increase in temperature to average and above-average levels. This relationship was most evident during epidemics in late winter and spring, when temperature fluctuations are most pronounced. This aligns with a study by Peci et al. [11], who reported decreases in virus A detection at temperatures above 0 °C and in virus B at temperatures above 15 °C in Toronto, Canada. Our study also found that epidemics lasting into late winter and early spring were associated with unusually low temperatures, which suggests that anomalous winter-like weather conditions during this period favour the survival and spread of influenza.

Humidity and insolation

In addition to air temperature, other factors such as anomalously low absolute humidity and solar insolation play important roles in the transmission of the influenza virus in the population and the timing of individual influenza epidemics [11, 12, 14, 21–23]. Our findings show that, besides air temperature, absolute humidity is the second most important meteorological factor associated with influenza epidemics. Given that absolute humidity, temperature, and relative humidity are interconnected, it is challenging to isolate the effect of each factor on influenza transmission [59, 60]. Despite this, we observed distinct differences, even between specific influenza viruses. Absolute humidity was significantly lower than average, mainly before and during A/H3N2 epidemics. Relative humidity was higher than average during the A/H3N2 and A/H1N1 epidemics, with a more pronounced effect for A/H3N2.

Epidemiological analyses, driven by experimental data on influenza virus transmission and stability, have recognised absolute humidity and temperature as climatic indicators for predicting influenza epidemics in temperate regions [11, 61]. Transmission experiments conducted in the guinea pig model suggest that the connection between environmental factors and the seasonal influenza patterns is attributed to their influence on the effectiveness of respiratory droplet transmission [62]. It has recently been demonstrated that influenza A/H3N2 and A/H1N1 viruses decay at intermediate relative humidity levels in droplets containing saliva, whereas low and high relative humidity levels facilitate their spread [63]. Dry and cold air alters the size and persistence of exhaled aerosols, improves the stability of influenza viruses, and weakens host airway defences, thereby promoting transmission [59, 64–66]. According to Marr et al. [59], relative humidity represents an external factor influencing the stability of viruses through the regulation of evaporation, which affects the dimensions and thereby influences the resulting chemical microenvironment to which the virus is exposed; therefore, relative humidity may also play a role in influencing influenza incidence and seasonality. Moreover, an experimental study shows that at low relative humidity, influenza A/H1N1 maintains maximum infectivity, and the virus rapidly inactivates at higher relative humidity (≥ 43%) after coughing [65]. Peci et al. [11] noted that higher relative humidity correlated with increased detection of influenza A virus in Canada; however, they did not differentiate between A virus subtypes.

In addition to lower-than-average temperatures and absolute humidity, influenza epidemics tend to occur after periods of reduced sunshine hours. This supports a study across six Northern European countries that identified low temperatures and UV indices as key meteorological predictors of influenza epidemics [22]. Such conditions may enhance the virus's transmission and spread within the population. An experimental study shows that under clear-sky conditions, the transmission of the influenza A/H1N1 virus through long-range aerosols is unlikely, whereas lower light levels generally slow viral inactivation, thereby increasing the likelihood of aerosol-based transmission [23]. Our data showed that severe A/H3N2 virus epidemics with high mortality impacts were associated with increased cloud cover and preceded by low sunshine levels two weeks prior to the epidemics. Around the peak of the epidemics, sunshine duration was anomalously low during A/H3N2 epidemics and, conversely, anomalously high during influenza B epidemics. We assume that sunshine duration and cloud cover are among the factors that contribute to the overall effects of meteorological variables on influenza viruses, but that temperature and absolute humidity are the main determinants.

Influenza epidemics and climate change

This study demonstrated that air temperature significantly influences the influenza virus. Low temperatures are associated with a high number of influenza-related deaths, suggesting that warmer winters, which are associated with climate change, may lead to lower mortality rates during future influenza epidemics [67]. However, when making long-term projections, it is important to consider several questions, such as the adaptability of the human population and individual viruses. The A/H3N2 virus thrives in periods of low temperatures, whereas in milder winters at above-average temperatures, it does not cause serious illnesses that lead to many deaths. However, even in mild winters, prolonged periods of very low temperatures can occur, creating conditions for a severe epidemic to develop (as observed in several epidemic seasons over the last decade). The role of genetic drift in the influenza virus and the emergence of new strains that favour mild winters and result in higher mortality rates may also be significant [68]. Additionally, it is important to consider the size of the population susceptible to influenza, particularly those with compromised health or a history of previous influenza exposure, a number that continues to increase. When predicting the impact of future influenza epidemics on mortality, it is crucial to consider the combined effects of these factors [69]. It can be assumed that extended cold and dry periods may impact influenza epidemics in a changing climate, even during warmer winters. Severe influenza epidemics with high mortality rates may occur even during mild or warmer-than-average winters if the A/H3N2 virus is circulating in the population and coincides with periods of significantly below-average temperatures and low absolute humidity.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from national datasets covering 38 years. All-cause mortality data are less prone to subjective bias than cause-of-death data, which may be subject to coding errors. We used primary care surveillance data as a proxy for identifying cases of influenza infection in the population. These data are based on information from general practitioners and include all ARIs, including influenza. Influenza virus types/subtypes predominating in the population in a given season were confirmed by the National Reference Laboratory for Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses. Because general practitioners only report total ARI counts, and most cases are not virologically tested, the analysis could not have been based on accurate records of influenza incidence.

In our analysis, we considered the dominant virus subtype or lineage for each season to help interpret variations in epidemic severity and their associations with weather conditions. However, we were unable to perform a fully stratified analysis for each subtype or lineage every season, as surveillance data providing positivity rates for individual viruses were limited during the earlier part of the study period.

The effect of seasonal influenza vaccination was not considered; however, vaccination coverage was consistently low in the Czech Republic during the study period, accounting for 5–8% of the total population and approximately 20–24% of the elderly population [26]. Variations in vaccination coverage were small between seasons and did not influence the results. The vaccine was also approximately the same in terms of efficacy; however, if a different circulating strain than the one selected for the vaccine emerges, efficacy may decrease. Nevertheless, given the low vaccination rate, this does not significantly affect the results.

Since this study is based on aggregated national-level data spanning several decades, it limits the ability to account for individual determinants, such as chronic health conditions, obesity, or socio-economic status. These factors are known to influence susceptibility to influenza infection, disease severity and health-seeking behaviour [70, 71]. Future research combining epidemiological surveillance with individual and socio-economic data could provide more insights into these influences.

After 2008, the incidence of reported ARIs during influenza epidemics decreased significantly, which was attributed to a change in sickness benefit coverage. As a result, there was a reduction in the number of physician visits. Importantly, however, the total morbidity rate may have been higher. In response to a decrease in the number of ARI reports by general practitioners since 2009, the NIPH has modified the definition of an epidemic accordingly. In this study, we employed ARI morbidity data to identify the onset of influenza epidemics and to determine the peak incidence of ARIs. Therefore, this adjustment is unlikely to have influenced the results.

Because mortality data were not age-stratified in the earlier period of the study, we could not perform analyses by age group. Older adults and those predisposed to chronic diseases of the circulatory and respiratory systems are at increased risk of death during epidemics of influenza [71]. Laboratory tests performed at the National Reference Laboratory for Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses confirmed that in elderly individuals (60 + years of age), A/H3N2 virus infections are identified more often than A/H1N1 [26]. Given that epidemics with the predominant A/H3N2 virus subtype are associated with the largest mortality impacts, we can assume that elderly people are most at risk of developing complications leading to death during these epidemics. This finding aligns with other European studies examining the mortality impacts of influenza in various age groups [e.g.,72, 73]. More research is needed to understand the aetiology of influenza in different age groups to better target prevention measures.

Influenza transmission is driven not only by local conditions but also by epidemic activity in neighbouring countries, which can influence outbreak patterns. Furthermore, although this study relied on country-level average weather data, we acknowledge that finer spatial variations both within and across borders may provide additional insights. Addressing these factors by incorporating cross-border transmission dynamics and higher-resolution weather data remains a priority for future research.

Conclusions

This study found that air temperature and absolute humidity are the weather variables most closely associated with influenza epidemics, with temperature being a more important factor affecting the epidemics' onset. Severe epidemics with the highest excess mortality, predominantly caused by the A/H3N2 virus, were associated with the largest temperature drop before the onset and a prolonged period of significantly below-average temperatures during the epidemics. By contrast, in non-epidemic seasons, above-average temperatures and absolute humidity were observed during periods of increased influenza activity. Mild epidemics with low mortality impacts, mainly those caused by the A/H1N1 virus, usually occurred after periods of above-average temperatures. Overall, the epidemics' peaks were associated with a temperature change, and the mean temperature rose significantly above average as the epidemics subsided.

The results suggest that the temperature pattern in the weeks following the epidemic onset can influence the length and severity of the epidemic. Additionally, the length of the period with below-average temperatures affects the impact of epidemics on excess mortality. The findings support the hypothesis that both meteorological conditions and virus subtype jointly influence the timing, severity, and public health impact of influenza epidemics, emphasising that monitoring the predominant virus type, air temperature, and absolute humidity deviations during the influenza season could assist early warning systems and guide public health preparedness measures.

The study highlights the importance of analysing influenza types and subtypes separately, as their circulation patterns, mortality impacts, and responses to meteorological conditions differ. Grouping influenza A/H3N2 and A/H1N1 subtypes together can mask these differences and obscure the true influence of weather on influenza. Subtype-specific analysis, therefore, allows a more accurate assessment of how meteorological conditions shape epidemic severity and public health impact.

A better understanding of meteorological conditions that modulate influenza activity is crucial for more accurate prediction of epidemic outbreaks. Such knowledge can help mitigate the impacts of influenza epidemics on the public health sector by better targeting at-risk populations through awareness, preventive measures such as vaccination and antiviral administration, and planning for increased hospital demand. Further research is needed to clarify how environmental factors and virus characteristics interact to shape epidemic patterns and to optimise strategies for reducing influenza-related mortality.
