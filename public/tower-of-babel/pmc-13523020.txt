Nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers for sustainable agriculture: integrating targeted pathogen suppression with controlled nutrient release and enhanced crop productivity
Authors: Sanduni Dabare, Imalka Munaweera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The agricultural sector is facing greater challenges than ever to increase crop productivity while reducing chemical use and environmental impacts. By incorporating targeted pathogen suppression with controlled nutrient release mechanisms, nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers can address these complex challenges. The information available on the development, characterization and application of nanostructured fertilizers with antimicrobial agents such as metal oxide nanoparticles (ZnO, CuO, and TiO2), silver nanoparticles and bioactive compounds is summarized in the present review. It discusses their multi-functional properties that suppress soil-borne and foliar pathogens and enable the slow release of macronutrients and micronutrients. The combination of antimicrobial activity and controlled nutrient delivery can reduce disease pressure, chemical pesticide use, and nutrient leaching losses while enhancing nutrient bioavailability and resource-use efficiency. Field trials have demonstrated that the application of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers is much more effective than the application of conventional fertilizers in terms of yield, quality of the crops and soil health parameters. However, there are still issues with cost-effectiveness, scalability, long-term environmental persistence and regulation. This review aims to highlight key knowledge gaps and recommendations for further development of this technology for commercialization and implementation in sustainable agriculture.

Introduction

The world of agriculture is in a critical situation. Agriculture is facing growing pathogenic pressure, soil degradation, freshwater scarcity, and a reduction in arable land, while the global population is projected to reach approximately 10 billion by 2050, and agricultural intensification is expected to increase reliance on monoculture farming.1 These challenges are exacerbated by the environmental consequences of conventional agricultural practices, which rely heavily on synthetic fertilizers and chemical pesticides. Overuse of fertilizers causes nutrient runoff, eutrophication of water bodies and greenhouse gas emissions, and overuse of pesticides increases pathogen resistance and harms beneficial soil microbes.2

Sustainable agriculture has become a new paradigm that focuses on maximizing production while reducing external inputs and environmental impacts. This balance can be achieved through innovative technologies that solve multiple agronomic issues with a holistic solution instead of a problem-by-problem approach. The conventional fertilizers, whether organic or synthetic, are usually soluble, and their soluble nutrients are easily leached out in aqueous environments and volatilized into the atmosphere. Conventional fertilizers are also chemically inert and do not offer any natural resistance to plant pathogens, and their nutrient-use efficiencies are often less than 50%, especially for nitrogen in cereal crops.3–5 This means that farmers need to use extra fungicides and bactericides to control disease pressures, which raises production costs and environmental chemical usage. Current agricultural technology is fundamentally inefficient because pathogen management and nutrient delivery are done separately.

Nanotechnology is the science and engineering of materials at the nanoscale (1–100 nm) that provide unparalleled opportunities to design materials with specific chemical, physical and biological properties. Nanotechnology has been increasingly applied in agriculture, with nanoparticles, whose properties differ from those of bulk materials, being investigated for their potential to deliver nutrients, suppress pathogens, mitigate abiotic stress, and monitor crops, among other applications.6 Metal oxide nanoparticles like zinc oxide (ZnO), copper oxide (CuO), and titanium dioxide (TiO2) have been shown to have a wide range of antimicrobial properties against bacteria, fungi, and viruses, including the generation of oxidative stress, disruption of cell membranes, and damage to nucleic acids.7,8 At the same time, these materials are important sources of micronutrients: zinc and copper are important cofactors in plant enzymatic systems, and titanium has been demonstrated to improve the photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance. These materials can be used together in a single fertilizer blend to suppress disease and provide micronutrient supplementation.

Nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are a converging technology that combines several functional components: antimicrobial agents at the nanoscale to suppress pathogens, macronutrient sources (N, P, and K) in controlled-release formulations, micronutrient nanoparticles (Zn, Cu, B, etc.) with enhanced bioavailability, and carrier matrices that control nutrient release kinetics and protect antimicrobial agents. The synergistic effects of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are reduced disease incidence, better nutrient-use efficiency, reduced chemical pesticide reliance, and better crop productivity and resilience. Types of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers

Several types of nanoparticles have been employed in fertilizer development, including SiO2, ZnO, CuO, Fe, and Mg nanoparticles, which provide plants with increased nitrogen fixation, improved seed germination, enhanced drought stress tolerance, increased seed weight, and improved photosynthesis ability.9 These nanomaterials demonstrate proven antimicrobial, insecticidal, and nematicidal activities, making them promising alternatives to chemical pesticides.

The development of antimicrobial nanofertilizers includes various categories: antibacterial nanoparticles composed of metals, metal oxides, metal salts, metal hydroxides, organic nanocarriers loaded with antibacterial agents, hybrid materials, and polymers with inherent antimicrobial properties.10 Metal-based nanoparticles like Ag or Cu are chosen for rapid, strong antimicrobial effects or boosting plant resilience against pathogens. In contrast, metal oxide nanoparticles such as TiO2, ZnO, and Fe3O4 provide better chemical stability for long-term use, enhancing photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, or stress resistance tolerance.11 Nanoparticles are also used as nano priming agents for antibacterial and antifungal activities against phytopathogens, while enhancing plant growth, with ZnO and CuO nanoparticles showing broad-spectrum antimicrobial activities and effectiveness, typically achieved with diameters in the 10–100 nm range.12

Metal-/metal oxide nanoparticle-based fertilizers (ZnO, CuO, and Ag)

Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles are the most commonly studied antimicrobial agents in fertilizer development, with ZnO and Ag nanoparticles being the most prevalent types.10 Among metal nanoparticles, Au, Ag, and Zn are extensively used for their antimicrobial features. In contrast, metal oxide nanoparticles, including SiO2, ZnO, MgO, and TiO2, are widely studied for their ability to act as disinfecting photocatalytic agents and UV blockers.13 The antimicrobial effectiveness of these materials stems from their diverse mechanisms of action. Metallic nanoparticles, such as Ag and Cu, exhibit potent antimicrobial activity against a wide variety of microorganisms due to their dimensions, shapes, charges, high surface area-to-mass ratios, and high reactivity.14

Certain metal and metal oxide nanoparticles have been found to have significant applications in agriculture. AgO nanoparticles show significant promise due to their antimicrobial properties, making them effective against plant pathogens and pests, while also enhancing seed germination, nutrient uptake, and plant growth.15 Ag nano-fertilizers have been shown to increase nitrogen uptake and photosynthetic efficiency in crops such as wheat and maize, significantly impacting the yield, while also providing antimicrobial activity that protects plants from pathogens such as Pseudomonas syringae, Xanthomonas campestris, and Fusarium oxysporum.16 ZnO and CuO nanoparticles exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activities, with the most effective properties typically achieved at diameters in the 10–100 nm range.12 ZnO nanoparticles exhibit attractive antibacterial properties due to their increased specific surface area as the particle size decreases, leading to enhanced surface reactivity and the generation of reactive oxygen species, including hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, and peroxides.12,17 TiO2 nanoparticles show potent antimicrobial activity against Xanthomonas perforans through their photocatalytic properties, CuO nanoparticles exhibit insecticidal activity against Spodoptera littoralis, and Ag nanoparticles demonstrate nematicidal activity against Meloidogyne species.9 These nanoparticles have been used as nano priming agents to enhance antibacterial and antifungal activities against phytopathogens while promoting plant growth.12

The choice of metal-based or metal oxide nanoparticles is determined by the application time and goals. Metal-based nanoparticles like Ag or Cu are frequently selected for their rapid and strong antimicrobial properties or when the plant needs to be more resilient in the presence of pathogens. Meanwhile, TiO2, ZnO, and Fe3O4 are more chemically stable and can be used for longer-term interventions that aim to enhance photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, or stress resistance, with the addition of advanced formulations that allow for the gradual release of metal oxide nanoparticles over time, providing continuous antibacterial activity against pathogens.11,18

The antimicrobial activity of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles is very high, but their role in a fertilizer system is not limited to direct antimicrobial activity. The incorporation of antimicrobial nanoparticles in fertilizer formulations offers a multifunctional approach, which includes disease suppression, micronutrient delivery, nutrient availability, and plant stress tolerance. For instance, ZnO nanoparticles can be used as a source of Zn and as an antimicrobial agent, with controlled release of Zn2+, providing nutritional benefits to the plants and generation of ROS, and surface interactions, providing pathogen inhibitory benefits. Similarly, Cu-based nanoparticles are also effective against a wide range of phytopathogens and are also used as micronutrients.19,20

The key benefit of nano-enabled antimicrobial fertilizers over conventional formulations is the ability to enhance nutrient delivery efficiency by high surface area, controlled dissolution and targeted release mechanisms. The effectiveness and sustainability of these systems are dependent on the characteristics of the nanoparticles, the dose applied, the properties of the soil, the transformation behavior, and interactions with non-target organisms.21,22 Thus, designing antimicrobial fertilizer systems should take into account the balance between pathogen suppression, nutrient supply, and maintenance of beneficial soil functions, not just the highest possible antimicrobial activity.23

Chitosan- and other biopolymer-based antimicrobial fertilizers

The synergistic effect of chitosan with metal nanoparticles has resulted in improved antimicrobial systems, which are suitable platforms for antimicrobial fertilizer development due to their favorable biological properties such as biodegradability, biocompatibility, non-allergenicity and inherent antimicrobial activity with low animal and human toxicity.29 Biopolymers have emerged as suitable platforms for metal nanoparticles because they can act as carriers to stabilize them against agglomeration and simultaneously serve as biodegradable active materials.10 The combination of chitosan with metal nanoparticles creates enhanced antimicrobial systems through synergistic effects. Combined formulations comprising metal or metal oxide nanomaterials encapsulated or embedded in chitosan exhibit improved antimicrobial potential, with nanocomposite Ag-chitosan exhibiting greater antibacterial activity.30 The recurring mechanism is not simple additive killing; the carrier changes the metal's local concentration, release kinetics, and sometimes the metal phase itself. Polymer hydrogels, nanogels, and coatings can hold Ag, ZnO, CuO, or mixed oxides at the target surface, release ions in a controlled way, and improve mechanics or biocompatibility at the same time. Reviews explicitly frame this as a multicomponent system with diverse functionalities, and as a way to boost activity against both planktonic cells and biofilms while reducing cell toxicity and antimicrobial dose.31–33 A chitosan/ZnO hydrogel released vancomycin over 96 hours in acidic media and improved the antibacterial activity; a ZnO-loaded alginate/gum acacia nanohydrogel prolonged ZnO release to over two weeks and was less toxic than free ZnO; and metal ion hydrogels showed favorable biocompatibility plus activity against MSSA, MRSA, E. coli, and C. albicans.34–36 Chitosan and chitosan/Ag nanoparticles have also been proposed as promising alternatives to overcome antimicrobial resistance.14 Specific chitosan-metal nanoparticle combinations have demonstrated effectiveness against various plant pathogens. Cu nanoparticles, ZnO nanoparticles, and chitosan–Zn–Cu nanocomposites have been chemically fabricated and evaluated against plant pathogenic fungi, including A. alternata, R. solani, and B. cinerea.30 The antifungal activities of Zn–chitosan and Cu–chitosan at concentrations of 30, 60, and 90 µg mL−1 effectively inhibited the growth of Rhizoctonia solani. They indicated effective control of cotton seedling damping off under greenhouse conditions.30

Other biopolymer-based systems include gelatin and starch-based nanocomposites. Low-cost biodegradable nanocomposite hybrid films containing chitosan, gelatin, and ZnO nanoparticles exhibit enhanced antimicrobial activity against E. coli.37,38 Starch-based nanocomposite films incorporating Ag, CuO, and ZnO nanoparticles showed improved antimicrobial activities due to their synergistic effects.37 Advanced biopolymer systems offer controlled release capabilities and enhanced functionality. Nanocomposites of ZnO and chitosan preserve the antibacterial properties of metal oxides while benefiting from the biocompatibility and biodegradability of chitosan.18 Modifying metal oxide nanoparticle surfaces with biopolymers can improve the adhesion to plant surfaces and enhance nanoparticle retention time, resulting in prolonged antibacterial action.18 Biopolymer matrices can be further enriched with plant extracts containing flavonoids, terpenes, and alkaloids, thereby developing multifunctional biomaterials with enhanced antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.39,40Table 2 summarizes the recent studies evaluating chitosan materials against fungal and bacterial phytopathogens.

Biochar/nanoclay hybrids with antimicrobial additives

Biochar and nanoclay are promising hybrid platforms for antimicrobial fertilizers, offering structural and functional benefits. They can carry and stabilize metal nanoparticles, preventing agglomeration, while serving as biodegradable active carrier materials.10 Nanoclay materials have demonstrated particular effectiveness as antimicrobial platforms in various applications. The efficacy of nanoclays as antimicrobial agents and their potential use in active systems have been shown in several in vitro experiments.10 These clay-based nanomaterials can be combined with metal nanoparticles to create hybrid systems that benefit from both the structural properties of the clay matrix and the antimicrobial activity of incorporated metal species. Biochar serves as another valuable hybrid platform due to its porous structure, high surface area, and ability to provide slow-release functionality for embedded antimicrobial agents. When combined with antimicrobial additives such as metal nanoparticles or plant extracts, biochar-based fertilizers can provide sustained pathogen protection while improving the soil structure and nutrient retention.

Plant extract- and metabolite-loaded fertilizers (green NAFs)

Plant extract and metabolite-loaded fertilizer systems represent an advanced approach that combines the metal nanoparticles exhibiting antimicrobial properties with the bioactive compounds found in plant extracts. Biopolymer matrices, including polymer-stabilized nanoparticles, can be further enriched with plant extracts, which contain flavonoids, terpenes, alkaloids, and other active compounds, thereby contributing to the development of multifunctional biomaterials.39,40,47 These active substances have antiseptic and antioxidant properties that complement the antimicrobial effects of metal nanoparticles.39,40

The synergistic interaction between plant extracts and metal nanoparticles significantly enhances antimicrobial activity compared with that of the individual components. The interaction of plant extracts with metal nanoparticles and polymers can dramatically improve the antimicrobial activity of the materials.39 This enhanced effectiveness is particularly notable in multi-component nanoparticles, where the combined targeting of multiple mechanisms improves antimicrobial performance against a wide range of bacteria, including multidrug-resistant strains.39,48 Specific plant extracts like tea tree, turmeric, thyme, and turnip are used in antimicrobial fertilizers to enhance their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.39 These plant-derived compounds, particularly flavonoids, serve as robust defenders against oxidative stress and inflammation while providing additional protection against plant pathogens.40 Incorporating plant extracts into biopolymeric nanocarriers enables controlled, targeted delivery of bioactive compounds. These biodegradable, biocompatible, non-toxic nanoparticles allow long-term release and can encapsulate plant extracts, essential oils, and antimicrobials, creating protective systems for agricultural applications.47

Mechanisms of antimicrobial action relevant to soil and rhizosphere

Nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are a new soil management strategy that integrates nutrient delivery and microbial control. They are composed of nutrient-rich nanoparticles that have antimicrobial activity to nourish plants and control soil pathogens. The benefits of their nanostructured design include enhanced surface area for microbial interaction, controlled release, and enhanced penetration of the biofilm. These fertilizers are exposed to complex microbial communities, both beneficial and harmful. The challenge is to selectively target pathogens without killing beneficial microbes. These include polymer nanocomposites for controlled release of nutrients and antimicrobials, metal oxide nanoparticles such as ZnO, CuO and AgO, and carbon-based nanomaterials.

Chemical mechanisms

There are a number of different chemical mechanisms that are used in nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers to control microbes in soil environments. The primary mechanism involves electrostatic interactions between positively charged nanoparticles and negatively charged bacterial cell surfaces, thereby promoting particle adhesion and penetration into microbial membranes (Fig. 2).49–52 This electrostatic attraction leads to membrane disruption, bacterial flocculation, and reduced cell viability through direct physical contact and subsequent membrane destabilization.49,53 A second important mechanism is the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and hydrogen peroxide.54–56 These highly reactive molecules overwhelm microbial antioxidant defenses and cause oxidative damage to essential cellular components, including proteins, lipids, RNA, and DNA.50,57–60 Metal oxide nanoparticles such as ZnO and TiO2 can act as photocatalysts, generating ROS upon light exposure, thereby enhancing their antimicrobial efficacy.7,50,53,54,61

Another significant chemical mechanism is the release of antimicrobial metal ions. Ag nanoparticles release Ag+ ions that bind to thiol groups on enzymes and structural proteins, disrupting the metabolic pathways and impairing DNA replication and transcription.50 Similarly, ZnO and CuO nanoparticles release Zn2+ and Cu2+ ions, respectively, which bind to bacterial proteins, nucleic acids, and cell envelope components, leading to cellular dysfunction.50,53,57 Additional chemical mechanisms include interference with essential cellular processes such as inhibition of protein synthesis, RNA production, and enzyme activities including H+-ATPase.46,63 The nanoparticles can also disrupt DNA replication and cell division processes, compromising genetic integrity.49,56,64 In addition, the capping agents used in the synthesis of nanoparticles from plant extracts often contain bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, saponins, or alkaloids, which can also contribute to their antimicrobial activity, leading to synergistic effects.57 The multiple chemical mechanisms frequently work together, making it hard for pathogens to become resistant to these drugs, as they do with traditional single-target antibiotics.50,65–68

Physical and topographical mechanisms

The physical and topographical characteristics of the nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers offer unique mechanisms for microbial control in addition to the chemical mechanisms.49,69 Studies have shown that smaller nanoparticles (4.8 ± 1.6 nm) have much higher antimicrobial activity than larger particles (7.8 ± 2.3 nm) because of their ability to penetrate bacterial cell membranes and biofilms.69

The high surface area-to-volume ratio allows for more surface contact with pathogen surfaces, which means more points of contact for antimicrobial activity.70,71 This increased surface contact allows for the physical disruption of cell walls through direct mechanical damage, where the sharp nanosurfaces of the nanoparticles bind to the surface of the pathogen and penetrate the membranes causing leakage of the cytoplasm and cell death.65 Particle shape significantly influences antimicrobial efficacy through topographical interactions. Triangular nanoparticles, such as Ag nanoparticles (Fig. 3), display notably higher activity than spherical or rod-shaped counterparts due to their sharp edges and high surface energy, which promote stronger interactions with bacterial membranes.72,73 Surface functionalization enhances physical mechanisms by modifying particle microbe interactions. Cationic surface modifications enhance bacterial membrane penetration and targeting, whereas hydrophobic surfaces prevent microbial adhesion and biofilm formation.72,74,75 Positively charged nanochitosan particles interact electrostatically with negatively charged fungal and bacterial membranes, increasing permeability and causing leakage of intracellular components.76

The multifactorial nature of these physical mechanisms creates simultaneous attacks on multiple cellular targets, making it difficult for microorganisms to develop resistance compared to single-target conventional antibiotics.50,66 Physical mechanisms can be categorized as microbiostatic (growth inhibition) versus microbicidal (cell killing), with many nanoparticles exhibiting both effects depending on concentration and exposure conditions.78,79

Biofilm inhibition and disruption mechanisms

Nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers combat soil pathogens through biofilm inhibition and disruption. Prevention is achieved by blocking early adhesion; hydrophobic surface modifications on nanoparticles create an environment that prevents microbial adhesion and subsequent biofilm formation, resulting in long-term antimicrobial efficacy.74,80

One of the most important mechanisms for the destruction of established biofilms is matrix disruption. Nanoparticles can penetrate the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix that protects bacteria within biofilms, allowing them to reach and kill embedded microorganisms.70,81 Ag nanoparticles are particularly effective at binding to and degrading biofilm matrix components while targeting the viability of bacterial populations within the protective structure.50Fig. 4 illustrates the diverse mechanisms through which nanoparticle-based antimicrobial systems overcome biofilm-associated resistance, including metal ion interactions with EPS, ROS generation, physical disruption, and carrier-assisted delivery of antimicrobial agents to bacterial cells.

A notable mechanism involves quorum sensing disruption, where nanoparticles interfere with bacterial communication systems that regulate biofilm formation and maturation.60,63 Studies demonstrate that biosynthesized Ag nanoparticles can significantly downregulate quorum sensing genes responsible for biofilm development, swarming motility, and virulence factor production.50,83 Sustained ion release provides ongoing antibiofilm activity by inhibiting enzymes involved in cell wall synthesis and by continuing to disrupt biofilm architecture.80 The generation of reactive oxygen species creates oxidative stress that damages biofilm components and overwhelms the antioxidant defenses of embedded bacteria.58,80

External stimulation can enhance antibiofilm activity, where responsive nanoparticles activate under specific conditions, such as pH changes, light exposure, or magnetic fields, to provide targeted biofilm destruction.70 These mechanisms are multifactorial, which results in simultaneous attacks on multiple components of the biofilm, making it extremely difficult for microorganisms to develop resistance to these agents as opposed to conventional single-target approaches.50,57,66

Indirect and plant-mediated mechanisms

The nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are based on advanced indirect mechanisms, which act via plant-mediated pathways to increase pathogen resistance and plant health. The main indirect mechanism is the activation of plant defense mechanisms, which is the activation of the plant's innate immune system, but not the killing of pathogens by the nanoparticles.46 This way, sustainable protection is achieved by enhancing the plant's natural ability to defend itself against microbial invasion. One of the most important plant-mediated mechanisms is enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity. Manganese-based nanomaterials have been shown to significantly boost the activity of defense enzymes like phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and peroxidase (POD), which play crucial roles in plant immune responses, including the production of antimicrobial compounds and reinforcement of cell walls against pathogen infiltration.84 These enzymes help plants produce antimicrobial compounds and strengthen cell walls against pathogen penetration.

Systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and induced systemic resistance (ISR) are triggered by nanostructured fertilizers, resulting in systemic immunity against a wide range of pathogens, including antiviral, antibacterial and antifungal properties.65 Nanoparticles can create antimicrobial layers on roots and shoots, preventing pathogen adhesion and invasion, and improve nutrient availability and soil health, which indirectly aids in pathogen control by boosting overall plant vigor and resistance.65 For example, MoS2–Cu nanomaterials form protective layers on leaf surfaces that increase capillary density and prevent bacterial infection, while ZnO nanoparticles enhance adhesion to plant surfaces, thereby improving antimicrobial properties.84 Improved nutrient availability and soil health contribute to indirect pathogen control by strengthening overall plant vigor and resistance.65 Nanoparticles can enhance nutrient availability by increasing the number of soil particles they interact with, provide sustained nutrient release, and can bind heavy metals that may otherwise negatively affect plant health.

Immune system modulation is not just limited to the plant, but also affects the wider soil ecosystem. Specific nanoparticles can enter host cells and regulate immune responses, increasing cytokine production and antigen presentation, which leads to long-term protection against biofilm forming pathogens and helps maintain soil health. These indirect mechanisms are multifactorial and offer the benefit of acting on more than one mechanism, as well as providing systemic protection to limit the potential for pathogen resistance to emerge, which is not possible with direct antimicrobial mechanisms.50,66–68

Effects on soil microbial diversity and community structure

In all studies, the strongest effects of nanomaterials on community composition are observed relative to the measures of overall diversity. Important patterns at the phylum level may appear stable following short exposures, but abundance and diversity of orders and genera may change, indicating that important ecological shifts may be overlooked if analysis is too coarse. Similarly, the effects of different nanoparticles on soil bacterial diversity were more apparent at the subphylum level in soil microcosms, with sulfur nanoparticles causing the greatest inhibition among the tested materials,85 while multiwalled carbon nanotubes did not always inhibit bacterial diversity, but rather functionalized MWCNTs altered the overall bacterial community composition and the relative abundance of dominant phyla at high concentrations, whereas raw MWCNTs did not.86

A second broad pattern is that metal and metal oxide nanoparticles often cause the strongest disruptions in soil microbial diversity and structure. Reviews and experiments repeatedly report altered abundance, reduced diversity, and taxonomic shifts after exposure to Ag, ZnO, Cu-based, and TiO2 nanomaterials.87–90 In one study, Cu2O and Ag2O nanoparticles significantly reduced bacterial diversity and shifted the community toward groups such as Bacteroidetes and alpha-Proteobacteria, showing that nanoparticle exposure can both suppress some taxa and favor others with traits linked to chitin degradation or nitrogen fixation.90 Similarly, nano-TiO2 and nano-ZnO reduced microbial biomass and bacterial diversity, with ZnO showing stronger effects than TiO2.88,91,92

These changes are not always even. Some groups go down, some go up, so nanomaterials can change the community, rather than just reduce the abundance of all groups. The restructuring is important since the same total biomass can be present in a soil, but with a loss of certain groups that are important for certain functions or plant interactions.88,92 Fullerene exposure resulted in a decrease of 3–4 fold in the abundance of fast growing bacteria, with an increase in other groups such as cyanobacteria and unknown archaea in contaminated or wastewater-irrigated systems.88,93 This kind of restructuring matters because a soil can keep similar total biomass while losing specific groups that support key functions or plant interactions.

Fungi and plant-associated symbionts can be especially important targets. Evidence summarized by Ghadimi et al. (2020) indicates that fungi may recover less well than bacteria after some carbon nanotube exposures, and that FeO and Ag nanoparticles can reduce fungal biomass, although effects are not universal across all nanomaterials or fungal species.94 More recent work on graphene oxide also suggests that high exposure can reduce fungal richness and diversity in the rhizosphere and alter arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal community structure, with likely consequences for host nutrient uptake and mycorrhizal network function.95,96

At the same time, the literature does not support a simple rule that nanomaterials always reduce diversity. Some materials show little detectable toxicity under certain conditions, and some formulations can even steer rhizosphere communities in a favorable direction. Ghadimi et al. (2020) note examples of materials with little effect on common microbial indicators, even though other studies on the same broad class of materials found strong impacts.94 In wheat, larger negatively charged Ag nanoparticles increased beneficial plant growth-promoting bacteria, while other Ag nanoparticle types caused shifts judged less favorable for plants.97,98 Engineered delivery systems can also matter: a Janus emulsion formulation increased the Chao1 index relative to a conventional delivery system and reduced the negative effect of pyraclostrobin on microbial diversity.99–101 Nanobubble irrigation also shifted community composition in gas-specific ways and enriched taxa linked to nutrient turnover, pollutant degradation, and pathogen suppression.102

An important extension of “community structure” is the idea of microbial interaction networks, not just taxon lists. Nano TiO2 in ginseng altered not only the structure and composition of rhizosphere and cultivated soil microbiomes but also competitive and cooperative relationships within microbial networks.97,103 The nanobubble study similarly found effects on keystone taxa in co-occurrence networks.102 This is important because two soils can have similar alpha diversity but differ strongly in stability, resilience, and disease suppression if their microbial interaction networks are reorganized.

Last, evidence indicates that the size and direction of a response are highly context-dependent. Exposure time is a major factor: some short-term studies show limited effects, while longer exposures reveal stronger and more persistent shifts, as in long-term Ag nanoparticle experiments.88,92 Soil texture, pH, and other physicochemical traits can be as important as concentration for determining effect expression.91,104 Particle properties also matter: size, charge, functionalization, and solubility can change which organisms are filtered out or favored.86,94,97,98 This is why broad claims about “nanomaterial safety” are usually less useful than asking which material, in which soil, at which dose, for how long, and in the presence of which plant.

Theoretical foundations for microbiome regulation in nano-agriculture

A useful theoretical starting point is to treat nano agriculture as a problem of ecological control rather than only material delivery. Soil and rhizosphere microbiomes are assembled by environmental filtering, resource competition, host selection, and species interactions; nanomaterials can enter each of these steps by changing chemical exposure, nutrient release, water relations, and microbial contact surfaces. This is why recent reviews describe nanomaterials as capable of manipulating rhizosphere or agriculturally important microbes and improve their functionality, but only under regulated conditions of dose, exposure time, type, and size. In other words, nanomaterials can be used to steer microbiomes, but not in a one-size-fits-all way.105–107

This steering idea rests on the second foundation: the plant soil microbiome system is functionally coupled. Microbes regulate nutrient solubilization, nitrogen fixation, hormone production, pathogen inhibition, and stress buffering, so changing the microbiome can change plant performance even if the plant is not the direct target of the nanomaterial. Reviews on plant microbiomes and soil microbes emphasize that bacteria, fungi, and archaea help maintain plant soil system stability through decomposition, nutrient cycling, symbiosis, and disease suppression. This makes microbiome regulation in nano agriculture conceptually similar to managing a hidden part of the crop phenotype: nanomaterials may work partly by shifting the microbial partners that control nutrient use efficiency, resilience, and suppression of pathogens.107–111

The third theoretical point is that microbiome regulation by nanomaterials is often indirect. Nanomaterials can alter the rhizosphere by changing nutrient availability, fertilizer release, water retention, heavy metal bioavailability, or the delivery of active compounds and inoculants. This means that they may regulate microbiomes not just by killing or sparing taxa, but by redesigning the local habitat and resource landscape in which microbes compete and cooperate. This idea is central to work on nano-fertilizers, smart delivery systems, and biopolymer coated formulations, which aim to shape the soil microenvironment, so that beneficial functions are favored.112–115

This leads to a practical design principle: microbiome safe steering depends on selective pressure, not maximum antimicrobial strength. The goal is not broad suppression of soil life, but selective shaping of communities and functions, so that beneficial guilds are maintained or promoted while pests or pathogens are controlled. Several papers support this idea by showing that some nanomaterials can increase beneficial groups or metabolic activity, improve nutrient cycling, or work well when paired with microbial inoculants, while other materials or higher doses suppress key guilds such as nitrogen fixers. This is why the integration of nanomaterials with beneficial microorganisms is now framed as a promising route for sustainable agriculture, provided that the formulation supports rather than overrides microbiome function.105,113,116,117

The fourth foundation is the idea of dose windows and hormesis-like response zones. Multiple reviews now point to the fact that low or moderate doses can have neutral or even stimulatory effects, whereas higher or repeated doses can shift communities toward nonselective toxicity. This is especially important for nano agriculture, because useful regulation depends on staying within an operational window where pathogen control, nutrient delivery, or stress protection can occur without irreversible microbiome damage. Recent reviews describe such dose windows explicitly for antifungal nanoparticles and note that low-dose exposures can stimulate enzyme or microbial responses while higher doses inhibit them.6,100,118,119

The fifth theoretical basis is that time matters as much as concentration, because microbiome regulation is dynamic. McKee et al. (2016) argue that short- and long-term toxic effects, species interactions, and chemical processes can create cascading and even catalytic effects in soil, which may explain why low concentrations become important after longer exposure. This supports a system view in which there is shift not only in the immediate abundance of nanomaterials but also in their succession, interaction networks, and process coupling over time. Under this view, regulation is not a single event at application; it is a trajectory in which particle transformation, microbial adaptation, and plant feedbacks together determine the final microbiome state.104,120,121

Another important concept is that the target is not always the total diversity, but rather the functional importance guilds and interactions. A soil microbiome may be very diverse in terms of taxonomy but may fail to provide essential services if the nanomaterials inhibit ammonia oxidizers, nitrogen fixers, mycorrhizae, rhizobia, or disease-suppressive groups. The theoretical implication is that the microbiome regulation in nano agriculture should be aimed at maintaining or enhancing nutrient cycling, symbiosis and pathogen control. This is particularly relevant because the beneficial outcomes like disease suppression are dependent on diversity in the community and the appropriate abiotic environment, rather than diversity in itself.101,110,117,122

Engineering wise, the field is shifting towards design for compatibility. This involves selecting coatings, particle chemistry, biodegradability, release rate and application method to ensure that the nanomaterial carries out its agronomic function without causing widespread microbial stress. The reviews now explicitly request for eco-friendly and controlled release nano formulations, microbiome engineering and molecular level assessment frameworks for nano-pesticides and nano-fertilizers. Theoretically, this is a change from the question of whether a particle is active enough to whether it is selective, degradable, and ecologically compatible enough for the plant-soil system.106,118,123

The concept of microbiome regulation in nano-agriculture can be described in a simple manner: nanomaterials are programmable ecological filters. They can influence the colonizing of roots, the persistence of guilds, nutrient flow and rhizosphere resilience to stress. However, these effects can be conditional and can be cumulative over time, and the most defensible approach is to match the type, dose, timing and formulation of the nanomaterial to the soil, the crop and the function to be achieved, and to assess success based on agronomic outcomes and microbiome stability.105,107,118,120

Design and synthesis of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers

Nanotechnology offers a new paradigm in agriculture by combining crop nutrition and pathogen suppression into a single formulation. In nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers, nutrients (N, P, K, and micronutrients) are incorporated into nanoscale carriers that also deliver antimicrobial agents or possess inherent biocidal activity. Such systems can target disease sites while controlling nutrient release, thereby boosting plant health and yield with reduced chemical inputs.5,124 Nanofertilizers, alone (without antimicrobials), have already been shown to improve nutrient use efficiency and plant growth; for example, foliar spraying with ZnO nanoparticles (20 mg mL−1) enhanced biomass in maize.124 Similarly, metal nanoparticles like Ag, ZnO and CuO both supply micronutrients and exert broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects against bacteria and fungi.124 These dual action materials (e.g. ZnO nanoparticles) illustrate the potential to reduce crop disease incidence and improve nutrition simultaneously.124 Studies show that mesoporous and polymeric nanocarriers can be functionalized to carry fertilizers and pesticides. For example, porous silica nanoparticles and liposomal or polymeric capsules have been engineered to encapsulate herbicides, fungicides, or peptides, enabling sustained release and improved effectiveness stability.

Synthesis methods and routes

The synthesis of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers employs two fundamental approaches: top-down and bottom-up methods. Top-down processes involve grinding bulk materials into nanoparticle size using techniques such as planetary or unitary ball mills, while bottom-up methods build from molecular or atomic clusters through chemical precipitation, sol–gel methods, co-precipitation, hydrothermal synthesis, microemulsion, and electrochemical synthesis.125 Both physical (top-down) and chemical (bottom-up) approaches are used to produce nanomaterials, with targeted nutrients loaded directly for cationic nutrients (NH4+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) and after surface modification for anionic nutrients (NO3−, PO42−, and SO42−).126 Nanofertilizers fall into three types based on structure. Nanoscale fertilizers are nanoparticles containing nutrients. Nanoscale additives include particles to improve performance without being direct nutrients. Nanoscale coating fertilizers have macroscale fertilizers coated with nanoscale films for gradual nutrient release.127,128

The encapsulation approach offers three primary synthesis routes: nutrients can be encapsulated within nanoporous materials, coated with thin polymer films, or delivered as particles or emulsions at the nanoscale.126 Engineered nano porous zeolites (aluminosilicates) serve as effective carriers due to their enhanced ion exchange properties and adsorption capabilities, featuring micro- (<2 nm), meso- (2–50 nm), and macro-pores (>50 nm) that can be synthesized with large surface areas, tunable pore sizes, and high thermal stability.128 Advanced synthesis strategies aim to produce particles with controlled size, shape, and contents to boost fertilizer effectiveness. These include controlling size and shape to improve plant interactions, encapsulation to protect nanoparticles and manage release, chemical functionalization for stability and nutrient transfer, targeted delivery to specific plant parts, and combining with biofertilizers or growth regulators for synergistic effects.129 Specialized fertilizer types include nano-encapsulated fertilizers with biodegradable or inorganic capsules that protect nutrients and enable soil-triggered controlled release. Nano-coated fertilizers use nanomaterial coatings to prevent nutrient loss and control release through coating adjustments or pH-sensitive mechanisms. Nanocomposite fertilizers embed nanoparticles like nanoclays or carbon nanotubes into fertilizers to boost nutrient retention and soil water retention.129,130

The choice between green and chemical synthesis methods depends on application requirements and environmental considerations. Green synthesis uses biological agents such as plant extracts, microorganisms, or enzymes to reduce metal salts into nanoparticles, avoiding toxic chemicals and producing highly biocompatible particles suitable for agricultural applications.131,132 Chemical synthesis employs chemical precursors via processes such as chemical reduction, sol–gel synthesis, and hydrothermal methods, which are commonly used to develop controlled-release fertilizers and agrochemicals.131,133

Carrier systems and fertilizer matrix integration

Polysaccharide-based carriers represent a significant class of biocompatible carrier systems for nanofertilizer applications. Alginate and chitosan are widely used polysaccharides for synthesizing nanocarriers due to their nontoxic, biocompatible, biodegradable, inexpensive, bioabsorbable and bactericidal properties.134 The controlled release of these nanofertilizers depends on the stability of the nutrient–carrier complex, due to high surface tension and strong intermolecular interactions that prevent immediate soil dissolution application.134,135 The optimization of potassium nanofertilizer synthesis using alginate chitosan carriers via ionotropic pre-gelation has demonstrated that only 14.6% of potassium was released after 24 hours, confirming effective controlled release properties.134

Chitosan nanoparticles show particular promise for encapsulating NPK fertilizers due to their polymeric, cationic, biodegradable, bioabsorbable, and bactericidal characteristics.136 FTIR results indicate electrostatic interactions between chitosan nanoparticles and nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, with higher colloidal suspension stability observed with nitrogen and potassium additions than with phosphorus, due to differences in anion charges.136

Inorganic carrier matrices such as nanoporous zeolites (aluminosilicates) offer strong platforms for nutrient encapsulation and delivery. These carriers have micro-, meso-, and macropores (<2 nm, 2–50 nm, and >50 nm), which can be synthesized with large surface areas, tunable pore sizes, and high thermal stability, thanks to their enhanced ion exchange and adsorption properties.128 Natural zeolites act as nutrient reservoirs, releasing nutrients slowly, providing a controlled release mechanism in soil environments.137,138 Mesoporous silica systems are controllable carriers for fertilizers. Mesoporous silicon dioxide with honeycomb channels is a common carrier for silicon nanomaterials, with modifiable surfaces and high biocompatibility.139,140 Core-shell silica matrix composite nanoparticles feature a unique hierarchical structure that can be modified to regulate material properties, showing great potential in agriculture and other fields.139,141 Hydroxyapatite-based carrier systems enable multi-nutrient delivery platforms. Urea-modified hydroxyapatite serves as a rich source of nitrogen, calcium, and phosphate, with nanoparticles of copper, iron, and zinc incorporated to enhance fertilizer efficiency.142,143 Hydroxyapatite nanoparticles can be synthesized via one-step wet chemistry precipitation and incorporated into nanocomposites to achieve multifunctionality, with significantly higher phosphorus use efficiency than chemical fertilizers.144

Hybrid and composite carrier systems combine multiple materials to enhance functionality. The design involves nanocapsules containing plant nutrients, incorporated into matrices of clay and organic polymers that act as carriers, with nanostructural properties enabling controlled release as a function of time or environmental interactions.136,138,145 Hybrid nanofertilizers typically comprise nanomaterial carriers (often silica or polymer nanoparticles), essential nutrients, and additional components such as growth regulators, with the carrier encapsulating nutrients to protect them from degradation and facilitate controlled release.142

Mesoporous silica and montmorillonite carriers gave a sustained slow release profile, and in soil only 11.5% and 11.9% of loaded metalaxyl had been released after 30 days versus 76% for free metalaxyl.146 A rice seed coating developed in 2024 using UiO-66 and a tannic acid-Zn(ii) shell enabled pH- and temperature-triggered release, achieved 84.09–93.10% control efficacy against Fusarium fujikuroi, and exhibited low biotoxicity, suggesting that it may preserve beneficial soil bacteria.147

For microbiome compatibility, the best evidence is from carrier-based composites rather than bare metal oxides. An iron carbon nanofiber nanocomposite coated with acylated homoserine lactone improved the abundance of Lactobacillus, Bacillus, and Pseudomonas after 30 days, and the same study reported better growth and suppression of Fusarium wilt in chickpea.148 The review level rationale is consistent with that pattern: encapsulating metal nanoparticles is described as a way to protect them and deliver “controlled release with prolonged efficacy.”33

Bare metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, by contrast, often look harsher on soil function. In a 2022 study, ZnO and Fe2O3 nano agrochemicals reduced bacterial CFUs by 32% and 19%, fungal CFUs by 28% and 23%, and N mineralization by 62% and 29%, with a lower radish dry matter yield.149 A separate 60 days mesocosm found persistent detrimental effects from copper-containing nanoparticles on non-target microbial communities.150 The picture is not one-way, though: a 2024 soil study found that low-dose metal-based nanomaterials increased microbial Shannon diversity and reduced Fusarium abundance, so microbiome compatibility depends on dose, soil, and particle chemistry as much as on whether the material is encapsulated.151

Novel carrier materials are being developed from waste and sustainable sources. Nanostructured water treatment residuals have been successfully used as carriers for plant nutrients, providing eco-friendly, cost-effective solutions through sustainable reuse of natural sources.152 Additionally, biomimetic calcium phosphate nanoparticles doped with potassium and nitrogen provide a slow, gradual release of macronutrients with different kinetics, enabling a 40% reduction in nitrogen supply compared to conventional treatments.153

Functionalization and surface engineering

Surface engineering represents a critical strategy for optimizing nanoparticle properties in fertilizer applications. Material properties such as size, morphology, composition, and surface characterization can all be tuned to control release, accumulation, and translocation of nutrients in plants.144,154,155 Surface modification and functionalization processes involve adding inorganic and organic molecules to nanoparticle surfaces, depending on their covalent or polar nature, which significantly enhances controlled-release properties.125,156,157

Amino functionalization of mesoporous carriers provides enhanced functionality for nutrient encapsulation. Amino-functionalized mesoporous silica (mSiO2-NH2) serves as an effective shell material in core–shell architectures, offering easily modified inner and outer surfaces with high biocompatibility.139,140,158 These functionalized carriers enable better nutrient absorption and utilization than commercial fertilizers while reducing eutrophication risks through improved controlled-release properties.125,159

Bio-inspired surface modifications offer sustainable approaches to functionalization. Polydopamine (PDA), a renewable, biodegradable biopolymer rich in catechol and amino groups, exhibits strong adhesion properties similar to those of mussel foot proteins and is widely used for the surface modification of nanocarriers.139,160 Humic substances can be assembled with hydroxyapatite nanoparticles via simple dipping processes, leveraging interactions between polyphenolic groups and nanoparticle surfaces to create multifunctional fertilizers with tunable release profiles.161

Advanced surface engineering strategies focus on creating biomimetic and doped systems for enhanced nutrient delivery. Biomimetic calcium phosphate nanoparticles can be doped with potassium and nitrogen to provide slow and gradual release of macronutrients with different kinetics, enabling significant reductions in nutrient supply compared to conventional treatments.153 Core–shell architectures with specific hierarchical structures enable the modification and regulation of composite material properties, showing great potential for agricultural applications.139,141

Functionalization using dendrimers provides molecular control in agriculture. Future developments should be directed towards surface modification, doping with beneficial elements and encapsulation methods to enhance the uptake of the nanoparticles, controlled release properties and target specificity, which will allow for cost-effective commercial production of poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers for encapsulating and delivering natural compounds, such as essential oils and plant-based antimicrobials.162,163

Stimuli-responsive and smart designs

Stimuli-responsive nanofertilizers are a new generation of innovative agricultural systems that release nutrients and antimicrobials in response to environmental changes or biological stimuli. These nanocarriers can release plant nutrients and pesticide-active ingredients depending on the crop requirements and the changes in environmental conditions (pH, temperature, light, and redox potential) or on biological stimuli (enzymes and other biomolecules).164,165

The fundamental mechanisms of stimuli-responsive controlled release operate through two primary pathways: reversible or irreversible structural changes in the nanocarrier upon external stimulation that trigger release, and the breaking of chemical and/or hydrogen bonds between the nanocarrier and nutrients upon stimulation.164 Environmental conditions such as temperature, pH value, and humidity, as well as stimuli-responsive mechanisms such as biodegradation or enzyme-mediated degradation, control the release of nutrients from carrier materials.166

Multifunctional stimuli-responsive systems combine multiple responsive mechanisms to develop intelligent delivery systems. Next-generation antimicrobial materials are multifunctional and stimuli-responsive biopolymer-based systems, which integrate biopolymer matrices with metal nanoparticles, bioactive compounds derived from plants, and smart delivery mechanisms.39,167,168

The stimuli-responsive systems based on chitosan exhibit advanced protection and controlled release properties. The ability of chitosan to form complexes with fertilizer molecules increases the accessibility of the molecules and protects them from harsh environmental conditions, such as pH changes, light exposure and temperature fluctuations, while also providing protection for the plant cells from sudden nutrient release, making chitosan nanoparticles suitable carriers for the controlled release of NPK fertilizers in agricultural environments.166

Advanced encapsulation techniques provide greater stability and allow for targeted delivery via stimuli-responsive systems. Nano- or micro-carriers can be used to improve the stability, bioavailability, and controlled release of active substances, while also providing enhanced antimicrobial activity, targeted action, and adaptive response to environmental stimuli through the incorporation of biodegradable polymer matrices and nanofillers.39 These systems bridge biotechnology and materials science, offering enhanced antimicrobial efficacy, targeted action, and adaptive responses to environmental stimuli by integrating biodegradable polymer matrices with nanofillers.39,169

Controlled release of nutrients and antimicrobials

Controlled release mechanisms in nanofertilizers operate through several fundamental approaches that enable sustained nutrient delivery. The most common strategy involves encapsulation, in which nutrients can be encapsulated within nanoporous materials, coated with thin polymer films, or delivered as nanoscale particles or emulsions.126,138 The controlled release property of these nanofertilizers is attributed to the stability of the nutrient–carrier complex, arising from high surface tension and stronger intermolecular interactions that prevent immediate dissolution after soil application.134,135

Polysaccharide-based systems demonstrate effective controlled release performance through optimized formulations. Potassium nanofertilizer synthesized using alginate-chitosan carriers via ionotropic pre gelation shows that only 14.6% of potassium was released after 24 hours, confirming effective controlled-release properties under optimized conditions, including a potassium-to-alginate ratio of 1.5 : 1, a calcium chloride-to-alginate ratio of 6.5 : 117.5, and a pre-gelation time of 40 minutes.134 Advanced stimuli-responsive mechanisms enable controlled release in response to environmental triggers.164–166 These systems can achieve controlled release via two general mechanisms: reversible or irreversible structural changes in the nanocarrier upon external stimulation and the breaking of chemical and/or hydrogen bonds between the nanocarrier and nutrients upon stimulation. Environmental conditions such as temperature, pH value, humidity, and stimuli-responsive mechanisms such as biodegradation or enzyme-mediated degradation control nutrient release from carrier materials.166

Different nanofertilizer formulations use various controlled release methods. Nano-encapsulated fertilizers have nano-sized capsules of biodegradable or inorganic materials that protect nutrients from degradation and leaching. Release is triggered by soil moisture, temperature, or microbial activity.129 Nano-coated fertilizers use nanomaterial coatings to control nutrient release via coating thickness or pH-sensitive layers. Nanocomposite fertilizers embed nanoparticles like nano-clays or carbon nanotubes into fertilizer matrices to boost nutrient retention and soil water-holding capacity.129,130

Novel hybrid systems provide sustained release over extended periods. Chitosan/alginate nanocapsules cross-linked with humic acid demonstrate sustained cumulative release of NPK over 30 days, reaching 33.2%, 47.8%, and 68.3%, respectively, with release kinetics following the Korsmeyer-Peppas mathematical model showing superior performance compared to non crosslinked nanocapsules.170 Urea-modified hydroxyapatite systems incorporated with copper, iron, and zinc nanoparticles enable slow and sustainable release of multiple nutrients, including nitrogen, calcium, phosphate, and micronutrients.142,143

The crystalline structure of nanoparticles promotes slow nutrient release, with their controlled-release mechanisms precisely delivering nutrients when needed to reduce application frequency and address the limitations of conventional chemical fertilizers.127,159 Chitosan-based systems form complexes with fertilizer molecules that enhance accessibility while protecting from harsh environmental conditions including pH changes, light exposure, and temperature fluctuations, extending the duration of active substance release and protecting plant cells from sudden nutrient release.166

Effects on plants, soil health, and microbiome

The integration of beneficial microorganisms with nanomaterials can enhance the overall effectiveness of these systems by improving nutrient availability, water retention, and targeted delivery of microbial inoculants to plant root systems.113

Critical to their successful implementation is the regulated application of dosage, exposure duration, and particle characteristics to ensure positive outcomes for both plants and soil microbes.105,106 The targeted delivery and controlled-release properties of these systems help minimize nutrient runoff and reduce environmental impact compared to traditional fertilizers, promoting more sustainable agricultural practices.171

Plant physiological responses and growth outcomes

Research demonstrates that nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers can significantly enhance various features of plant physiology and growth when properly formulated and applied. Studies with nanophosphorous fertilizers showed notable improvements in seed germination, plant height, leaf number, chlorophyll content, and total sugar and protein levels in maize compared to controls.172,173 These treatments also enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, including catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase, which can act as plant growth regulators and induce resistance against phytopathogens.172,173

Metal oxide nanoparticles have shown particular effectiveness in enhancing crop yields under disease pressure. Foliar applications of CuO, MnO2, and ZnO nanoparticles improved the yields of tomato and eggplant with Fusarium wilt disease by reducing disease severity, demonstrating their dual function as both nano-fertilizers and nano-pesticides.174 ZnO nanoparticles specifically enhanced crop growth and yield, particularly in wheat and maize, by improving root development and increasing the photosynthetic efficiency.8 In wheat under drought conditions, ZnO nanoparticle-coated urea significantly reduced the time to panicle initiation by 5 days and increased the grain yield by 39–51% compared to controls.174,175

Nano-fertilizer treatments consistently enhanced crop yields across diverse experimental contexts. The strongest yield responses occurred with combined NP treatments, particularly nanobiochar mixed with CuO nanoparticles, which increased wheat grain yield by 62% compared to untreated controls.176 Combined Zn and iron oxide NP treatments produced a 39% grain yield increase in wheat under field conditions.177 For peanut, nano ZnO outperformed conventional ZnSO4 by 34% under greenhouse conditions and achieved 29.5% and 26.3% yield increases over two consecutive field seasons.178

Biochar-based nanomaterials showed particularly strong effects on wheat production. Beyond direct grain yield increases, nanobiochar elevated biological yield by 25–35% and increased yield components including spikelet number per spike by 17–33% and plant height by 11–25%.176 The combination of nanobiochar with copper oxide nanoparticles enhanced grains per spike and 100-grain weight by 28%.176

Nano-N and nano-Zn formulations demonstrated the potential to reduce conventional fertilizer requirements while maintaining yields. The application of 75% of the recommended nitrogen dose supplemented with foliar nano-N and nano-Zn sprays produced statistically equivalent yields to the full recommended dose in wheat-maize rotations, effectively reducing nitrogen requirements by 25%.179 These treatments also improved yield components including effective tillers, ear length, and grains per ear.179

Quality parameters were significantly enhanced alongside yield improvements. Combined nano-ZnO and nano-Fe2O3 treatments increased wheat grain protein content to 13.30% while elevating zinc and iron concentrations to 48.83 and 51.17 mg kg−1,177 respectively. Nanobiochar combined with copper oxide nanoparticles increased wheat nitrogen uptake by 80% and copper uptake by 91–128% compared to controls.176 Nano ZnO treatments in sorghum significantly increased the grain zinc content while enhancing the nitrogen and potassium accumulation.180

Plant physiological responses to nano-fertilizers extended beyond yield parameters. The chlorophyll content increased by 29% in wheat treated with nanobiochar combined with copper oxide nanoparticles.176 The combined nano-ZnO and nano-Fe2O3 treatment produced the highest chlorophyll index (SPAD 50.93) among all treatments.177 Zinc oxide nanoparticles combined with plant growth-promoting bacteria significantly enhanced the chlorophyll content in both black wheat and HD3086 varieties.181

Root growth and development showed positive responses to several nano-fertilizer formulations. Zinc oxide and mesoporous silica nanoparticle treatments enhanced root lengths compared to controls.181 Nano-ZnO improved the root volume and dry weight in peanut.178 Nanobiochar applications increased the root length and root fresh weight at specific application levels.182 Nano-ZnO treatment improved root membrane permeability and enhanced nutrient translocation to grains.177

Early growth parameters responded favorably to nano-fertilizer seed treatments. Peanut seeds treated with 1000 ppm nanoscale zinc oxide showed enhanced seed germination and seedling vigor, leading to early establishment in soil manifested by early flowering.178 Plant height increased by 11% with nanobiochar alone and 25% with the nanobiochar and copper oxide combination.176

Stress tolerance indicators improved under nano-fertilizer treatments. In maize grown under saline conditions, nano-Zn and nano-Si significantly increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes including superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase.183 Nano-Si reduced malondialdehyde content by 41% and hydrogen peroxide content by more than half under saline conditions.183 These treatments also increased proline content, proteins, relative water content, and chlorophyll content, demonstrating improved osmoregulation, metabolic homeostasis, water balance, and photosynthetic efficiency under stress.183

Primary and secondary metabolite production was enhanced by nanobiochar applications. Both wheat varieties showed significant increases in total soluble sugar, protein, and free amino acids.182 The contents of secondary metabolites including phenolic acids and flavonoids were significantly elevated, along with antioxidants like catalase and peroxidase. The activities of nitrogen metabolic enzymes including nitrite reductase and nitrate reductase were enhanced.182

Soil biochemical and physicochemical impacts

Research demonstrates that nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers can significantly enhance soil biochemical properties through multiple mechanisms. Nanophosphorous fertilizers showed positive shifts in total bacterial count, nitrogen fixing bacteria, and phosphate and potassium solubilizers compared to controls.172 Soil enzyme activities were significantly improved in treated soils, with real-time PCR and total soil protein content analysis revealing enhanced microbial populations.172 The combination of nanozeolite and nanochitosan with Bacillus species significantly enhanced the total microbial count by 16.89% and NPK-solubilizing bacteria by 46%, 41.37%, and 57.14%, while increasing soil health indicator enzyme activity up to twofold over controls.173

The integration of nanomaterials with beneficial microorganisms can enhance the overall soil fertility by improving nutrient availability and cycling. Rock phosphate nanoparticles combined with bio-fertilizers decreased phosphorus retention and increased available phosphorus after soil incubation.197 Nano silicon fertilizers notably improved soil properties and microbial diversity in wheat cultivation, providing substantial benefits for soil health and productivity.198,199 These systems can promote beneficial microbial activity and create favorable conditions for soil microorganisms, thereby enhancing nutrient cycling and organic matter decomposition.171

Specific nanoparticle types show varying effects on soil physicochemical properties. ZnO nanoparticles can improve soil health by acting as a slow release source of Zn, particularly important in Zn-deficient soils, as their small size allows better dispersion and more uniform nutrient availability.8,200 GO nanoparticles may enhance soil properties by improving the water-holding capacity, which could be particularly beneficial for plants in drought-prone areas.4,201

However, nanomaterials can also negatively impact soil systems by disrupting the physicochemical balance. Ag nanoparticles, widely used for antimicrobial properties, may disturb the balance of beneficial soil microbes, leading to reduced microbial diversity and impaired nutrient cycling.4 Soil nutrient composition, particularly macronutrients and micronutrients, can account for over 50% of variation in microbial community structure, with calcium, phosphorus, and nitrate being the most influential factors.202 Chemical fertilizers can lower the soil pH, suppress microbial nutrient cycling and enzymatic activity, and reduce organic matter, while causing nutrient imbalances that restrict specific groups of microorganisms.203,204

The interaction between nanomaterials and soil ecosystems requires careful assessment of effects on soil structure, soil functioning, and organic matter, as well as related processes, to develop an understanding of nanoparticle behavior and fate in soil environments.46 Prolonged exposure may lead to nanoparticle accumulation in soil, potentially affecting microbial communities and soil health over time.4

Microbiome responses: beneficial microbes, pathogens, and diversity metrics

Research reveals that nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers can significantly enhance beneficial microbial populations when properly formulated. Nanophosphorous fertilizers showed positive shifts in total bacterial count, nitrogen fixing bacteria, and phosphate- and potassium-solubilizing bacteria compared to controls, with soil enzyme activities significantly improved in treated soils.172 The combination of nanozeolite and nanochitosan with Bacillus species significantly enhanced the total microbial count by 16.89% and NPK-solubilizing bacteria by 46%, 41.37%, and 57.14%, while increasing soil health indicator enzyme activity up to twofold over controls.173 Nano-chitosan application has been shown to enhance bacterial populations, particularly beneficial plant growth-promoting groups, thereby enhancing plant growth.205

Soil microbial biomass responded positively to nano-fertilizer treatments. Nanobiochar alone increased microbial biomass nitrogen by 57%, while the combination with copper oxide nanoparticles further elevated it by 61%.176 Microbial biomass carbon showed dramatic increases of 129% with the nanobiochar and copper oxide mixture.176 Individual microbial populations were significantly enhanced by nano boron nitride and nano sulphur treatments, with bacterial populations reaching 13.1 × 105 cfu g−1 soil, fungi at 22.4 × 103 cfu g−1, and actinomycetes at 12.5 × 103 cfu g−1.184

Soil nutrient availability showed substantial improvements across multiple nutrients. Nanobiochar increased available nitrogen by 28%, phosphorus by 64%, and potassium by 13%.176 The combination of nanobiochar with copper oxide nanoparticles provided additional benefits, increasing nitrogen by 18%, phosphorus by 41%, and potassium by 38% compared to nanobiochar alone. Soil copper content increased by 146% with copper oxide nanoparticles alone and 123% when combined with nanobiochar.176 Nano boron nitride and nano sulphur treatments resulted in enhanced nutrient uptake, with nitrogen reaching 114.9 kg ha−1, phosphorus 34.30 kg ha−1, and potassium 79.20 kg ha−1.184

Soil enzyme activities demonstrated significant enhancement under nano-fertilizer treatments. Dehydrogenase activity, a key indicator of overall microbial activity, increased to 2.61 µg TPF g−1 h−1 with nano-boron nitride and nano-sulphur treatments.184 Combinations of ZnO nanoparticles with plant growth-promoting bacteria significantly increased both soil dehydrogenase and invertase activities. Soil organic carbon showed remarkable increases of 528% in HD3086 wheat variety when treated with CP4 bacteria combined with zinc oxide nanoparticles.181

Microbial diversity and community structure were altered by nano-fertilizer treatments. In wheat-maize rotations, application of 75% recommended nitrogen with nano-N and nano-Zn sprays enhanced soil microbial diversity and maintained microbial biomass carbon at levels statistically similar to full conventional fertilization.179 Specific beneficial microbial groups including Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria were positively correlated with the yield and microbial biomass carbon.179 Biogenic zinc oxide nanoparticles combined with bioslurry increased beneficial bacterial phyla abundances, with Acidobacteria increasing by 6%, Actinobacteria by 2%, and Firmicutes by 2%.206 Functional analyses revealed increased pathways related to motility, chemotaxis, and metabolic processes in treated soils.206

However, there are serious concerns about the use of metal-based nanoparticles for soil microbial communities. Ag nanoparticles caused more than 30% decrease in archaeal relative abundance, compromising their involvement in nitrogen cycling and nitrification processes.187 Plant-associated bacteria like Bradyrhizobium canariense, which fixes nitrogen for plant protein synthesis, were highly susceptible to silver nanoparticles, along with beneficial soil bacterium Pseudomonas chlororaphis207,208 At environmentally relevant concentrations, Ag nanoparticles cause toxic effects on soil microorganisms and reduce microbial diversity.207,209

Of special concern is the effect on mycorrhizal fungi, which play a key role in forest ecosystem functions and nutrient transfer. The diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi was reduced on pine roots exposed to Ag nanoparticles, with only one genus (Laccaria) found on roots in soil contaminated with 350 mg Ag nanoparticle per kg and no mycorrhizal fungi observed at higher contamination levels.210,211 Long-term exposure to Ag nanoparticles altered soil fungal communities, with 40% of the community dominated by a single species after 12 months.207,212

The disruption of beneficial microorganisms can lead to unintended increases in pathogenic populations. The abundance of potentially phytopathogenic fungi in arctic soils was significantly increased by Ag nanoparticles, possibly due to adverse effects on bacteria that normally provide natural biocontrol against these fungi, with up to 5-fold increases in relative abundance observed for certain fungal genera.187 In arctic soils, Ag nanoparticles increased the abundance of Hypocreales fungi from 1% of fungal sequences under control conditions to 70%, while nitrogen-fixing Rhizobiales bacteria appeared vulnerable to toxicity.207,213 Metal oxide nanoparticles show variable effects depending on the composition and concentration. CuO and silver oxide nanoparticles altered soil bacterial community composition by significantly reducing bacterial diversity and altering bacterial abundance, with shifts toward bacterial groups responsible for chitin degradation and nitrogen fixation.214 Some studies reported that Cu–Ag nanoparticles changed the soil bacterial community composition by significantly decreasing bacterial diversity and altering bacterial abundance, with changes towards bacterial groups that degrade chitin and fix nitrogen, which may be beneficial for plant health.2,215

The impact on the environment is highly dependent on the characteristics of the particles, their concentration and soil conditions. Low levels of Ag nanoparticles can be beneficial to microbial processes without compromising beneficial plant microbe interactions. Fate, transport and toxicity are also influenced by soil physicochemical properties such as texture, pH, cation exchange capacity and organic matter content.216,217 Dose-dependent effects indicate that Ag nanoparticles have distinct impacts on soil biodiversity and microbial processes, with some engineered nanomaterials showing antimicrobial activity that alters soil microbial communities, which, in turn, can lead to changes in plant growth promoting rhizobacteria and mycorrhizal fungi, thereby increasing the susceptibility of the crop to disease.120,216,218

Exposure to Ag nanoparticles can also decrease soil nitrification rates by 25% after 30 days, and inhibit soil bacteria such as Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter, which are important for nitrogen fixation and nitrification, respectively.16,163 These disruptions can compromise the soil's ability to suppress pathogens and maintain ecosystem balance.113

Long-term soil ecology considerations

The potential long-term ecological effects of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are significant for sustainable agriculture, and there is increasing evidence that chronic exposure can have a profound impact on the dynamics of soil ecosystems. The effects are also problematic for beneficial soil bacteria, such as plant-associated nitrogen-fixing species like Bradyrhizobium canariense and biocontrol bacterium Pseudomonas chlororaphis, which were found to be highly susceptible to silver nanoparticle toxicity.207,213

Nanoparticle accumulation and persistence can lead to long-term degradation of critical ecosystem functions. After 30 days, soil nitrification rates are decreased by 25%, and key soil bacteria, such as Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter, involved in nitrogen fixation and nitrification, respectively, are inhibited.16 Long-term exposure changes the composition of the soil fungal community, with 40% of communities dominated by a single species after 12 months and a significant decrease in the overall diversity of fungi.207,212

Particularly concerning is the disruption of mycorrhizal relationships, which are essential for forest and agricultural ecosystems. Ag nanoparticle contamination reduced ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity on pine roots from five genera in controls to only one genus at moderate contamination levels and eliminated mycorrhizal fungi at higher concentrations.207,211 The disruption of beneficial microorganisms can result in unintended increases in potentially harmful fungal genera, as up to 5-fold increases were observed after exposure to silver nanoparticles.187

The complexity of ecosystem-level impacts extends beyond immediate microbial effects to broader ecological processes. Nanoparticles can alter energy flows and material cycles in ecosystems, affecting the overall stability and functionality through interconnected environmental and climatic factors.219 Metal nanoparticles in soil can rapidly dissolve, releasing metal ions that accumulate in plants or form complexes with environmental components, creating cascading effects throughout agricultural systems.219 Accumulation and bioaccumulation are constant risks to long-term sustainability, especially when metal nanoparticles in soil can rapidly dissolve releasing metal ions that can be accumulated in plants or form complexes with environmental components, which can have cascading effects throughout agricultural systems. Nanoparticles can be incorporated into the soil in agricultural systems via fertilizers, pesticides, or biosolids and can affect soil microbial communities and soil health, which can have implications for entire food webs.16

The disruption of beneficial microorganisms extends to essential agricultural processes, with some engineered nanomaterials exhibiting antimicrobial properties that cause microbial imbalances, leading to increased crop susceptibility to diseases by affecting plant growth promoting rhizobacteria and mycorrhizal fungi.120,218,220 Prolonged nanoparticle exposure may disrupt beneficial soil microorganisms critical for nutrient cycling, impairing nitrogen fixation and reducing nutrient availability for crops, thereby diminishing long-term agricultural productivity.221–223 High concentrations of nanoparticles may disrupt microbial communities, suppress beneficial microorganisms, and interfere with key enzymatic processes, with specific metal-based nanoparticles negatively affecting the diversity and abundance of beneficial soil microbes.163 The physicochemical properties of soil, including texture, pH, cation exchange capacity, and organic matter, significantly influence nanoparticle fate, transport, and toxicity, with effects that vary with environmental conditions and particle characteristics.202,217,224

The potential for long-term accumulation and ecological persistence of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers, however, needs to be considered before their use on a large scale in agriculture. Repeated use can lead to progressive deposition of nanoparticles in soil matrices, where they can undergo transformation processes (dissolution, aggregation, and interaction with organic matter), which can affect their bioavailability and toxicity. Long-term exposure experiments have shown that metal-based nanoparticles, especially Ag nanoparticles, can impact microbial biomass, functional microbial groups related to nitrogen cycling, and soil microbial diversity. Future studies should focus on long-term field-scale monitoring, transformation studies, and controlled release or biodegradable carrier systems to reduce persistent accumulation, but still provide antimicrobial activity. These will be necessary to ensure that nano-enabled antimicrobial fertilizers will be able to balance pathogen control, nutrient management and long-term soil ecosystem sustainability.

Environmental safety, toxicology, and regulatory considerations

The use of engineered nanoparticles in fertilizers raises complex environmental and safety concerns that are distinct from conventional agrochemicals. Nanoparticles have unique physicochemical properties that generate novel routes for environmental exposure and biological interactions, including their small size, large surface area, and potential for changing in environmental conditions. These products may interact with soil constituents, translocate through environmental media, and may have effects on non-target organisms that are not well understood. The increasing attention on sustainable agriculture and precision farming has driven the advancement of these technologies, increasing the need for comprehensive safety evaluations and regulatory oversight.

Ecotoxicological risks to soil fauna and non-target organisms

The ecotoxicological risks associated with nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers represent a critical knowledge gap that requires urgent attention. The ecotoxicological risks of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers represent a key knowledge gap that needs to be addressed urgently.225,226 Remarkably, only 21 studies in recent research have evaluated the ecotoxicity of nanoagrochemicals to non-target organisms, such as zebrafish, water fleas, honeybees, ladybugs, earthworms, nematodes, and soil microorganisms.164

Particularly, soil ecosystems are a concern for metal-based nanoparticles that are commonly used in antimicrobial fertilizers. Ag nanoparticles, which are commonly used for antimicrobial applications, have been shown to have long-term effects on microbial biomass, activity, and nitrogen-fixing bacteria in soils, even at low concentrations, disrupting critical microbial communities.4,16 Studies demonstrate that even low concentrations of Ag nanoparticles can significantly reduce microbial biomass, activity, and the abundance of nitrogen fixing bacteria, with effects that persist over extended periods.4 Similarly, Cu nanoparticles have been shown to cause long-term effects on microbial biomass, activity, and nitrogen-fixing bacteria in soils, even at low concentrations, disrupting critical microbial communities.194

These nanomaterials have risks that are not limited to the places where they are used, due to their bioaccumulation potential. Agricultural runoff releases nanoparticles into aquatic environments, which can be absorbed by plant roots and transported to aerial parts of the plants, potentially entering food chains when ingested by livestock or humans.11 Agricultural runoff disperses nanoparticles into aquatic ecosystems, where they can dissolve into highly toxic ionic forms that interfere with respiration and reproduction in aquatic organisms like Daphnia magna and zebrafish.16

One of the most alarming issues is the possibility of the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. The uptake and intracellular localization of nanoparticles are controlled by their high surface area, which can also cause oxidative stress, changes in membrane properties, and changes in bacterial motility, leading to the emergence of resistant microbial strains, similar to antibiotic resistance.194 The agglomeration of nanoparticles can also cause changes in surface properties and differential biological responses.227

Another major concern is the genotoxic effects, which are caused by some nano-agrochemicals that interact with the nucleic acids of cells, causing changes in DNA, chromosomal aberrations, and reduced DNA repair mechanisms. These materials may also affect plant-associated microbiomes, such as rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities, which can affect key processes like N fixation and PO43− solubilization.228

The environmental persistence and transformation of these materials under a variety of field conditions are not well understood. Perseverance rates vary widely depending on particle size, surface functionalization, and soil physicochemical conditions, with some materials remaining stable for months without significant degradation.229 Comprehensive ecotoxicological risk assessments across multiple trophic levels and long-term field studies evaluating cumulative effects on ecosystem balance are urgently needed.223,229

Metal oxide nanoparticles have been shown to have broad-spectrum antimicrobial activities, but recent studies indicate that their ecological impacts are not necessarily linked to indiscriminate microbial suppression. These materials can selectively alter soil microbial communities at appropriate application rates, by decreasing the pressure of pathogens and increasing or maintaining beneficial microbial groups. For instance, Hao Li et al. (2024) found that CuO and ZnO nanoparticles applied at 5 mg kg−1 significantly suppressed soil-borne Fusarium pathogens, reducing fungal abundance by more than 40% while increasing beneficial Pseudomonas populations by 29.7–96.2%. The results suggest that the effect of the nanoparticles is not only antimicrobial but also dependent on dose, exposure and microbial ecological interaction.151

The pathogen suppression and microbiome maintenance observed may be explained by the concentration-dependent effects of nanoparticles. Low or optimized concentrations of nanoparticles can be below the toxicity limit for beneficial microorganisms and have a higher inhibitory effect on sensitive pathogenic taxa. However, overuse or over-frequent use can break this window of opportunity and has a negative impact on key microbial guilds that play a role in nutrient cycling and plant growth promotion. Thus, the application of the microbiome-safe approach should not be based on the highest possible dose of antimicrobial activity.230

Moreover, the new idea of bifunctional metal nanoparticles offers a platform to reconcile their antimicrobial and nutritional functions. Rosas-Díaz et al. (2026) suggested that metal nanoparticles could be used as nanofungicides and nanofertilizers, with the disease suppression and nutrient delivery being regulated by the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles, including their size, surface charge, composition, and transformation behavior. Therefore, the goal of nano-enabled antimicrobial fertilizers should not be to eliminate microbes, but to steer the microbiome, suppressing the pathogens while maintaining beneficial microbial functions.231

Human and food-safety implications

The human health effects of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are an important issue, involving agricultural workers, consumers and various exposure routes. Nanoparticles have been shown to cause serious damage to human cells, such as damage to the plasma membrane, mitochondrial toxicity, nuclear damage, formation of reactive oxygen species, and disruption of important cellular signaling pathways, in studies.232 Direct exposure to the nanoparticles is a risk, especially when using dry nanoscale fertilizers, which can lead to drift losses and negative effects on human respiratory health.175

The main pathway of concern for food safety is the bioaccumulation pathway, where the nanoparticles are taken up by the roots of plants and then moved to edible parts. Metal-based nanoparticles commonly used in antimicrobial fertilizers, such as Ag, Cu, and ZnO, can accumulate in crop tissues and subsequently enter the human food chain.11,16 Research demonstrates that copper nanoparticles can accumulate at high levels in plant roots, causing phytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and membrane damage, with potential implications for food safety when these crops are consumed.194 Evidence from two studies indicated nanoparticle dissolution as the primary mechanism of metal uptake. XRD analysis detected no intact ZnO nanoparticles in wheat tissues,233 and root exudates (citrate) were shown to dissolve copper from CuO nanoparticles at pH 5.191 These findings suggest that plants primarily absorb dissolved metal ions rather than intact nanoparticles.

Groundwater contamination is another risk factor for human exposure. Some metal nanoparticles are persistent in environmental systems, which can lead to long-term contamination of water quality in systems that depend on groundwater or irrigation.11,221 The persistence of specific metal nanoparticles in environmental systems means that contamination can accumulate over time, potentially affecting water quality for extended periods.

One of the major issues in evaluating human health risks is the lack of comprehensive toxicological data and standardized testing protocols. Only one study carried out a formal food safety risk assessment. The study on radish calculated the Daily Intake of Metal (DIM) and Health Risk Index (HRI) values for Zn and Fe using US Environmental Protection Agency reference doses.149 Both metals remained within safe limits, with HRI values below 1 indicating no health risks from consuming radish grown in soil treated with ZnO and Fe2O3 nanoparticles at 1 g kg−1 soil. Specifically, zinc concentrations ranged from 0.072 to 0.164 mg kg−1 and iron from 0.281 to 0.631 mg kg−1 in radish roots, well below threshold levels.149 Most available safety information comes from limited laboratory studies rather than long-term human exposure assessments.227 In particular, the properties of nanoparticles, such as their ability to penetrate biological barriers, interact with biological components, and change in biological systems, necessitate specialized evaluation methods that are very different from traditional chemical safety evaluations.163

The development of suitable regulatory frameworks for assessing the safety of nanomaterials in agriculture is a challenge for regulatory agencies around the world. There is also uncertainty regarding current risk assessment procedures, which do not necessarily consider the unique properties of nanoparticles, such as size-dependent cellular uptake, surface reactivity, and potential for bioaccumulation.234 The absence of globally harmonized safety standards further increases uncertainty, as different countries use different evaluation criteria for the assessment of nano-enabled agricultural products.235

Fate and transport in soil and groundwater-leaching and transformation

The fate and transport of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers in soil and groundwater systems represents a key knowledge gap that directly affects environmental safety assessments. The current risk assessment methods for nanomaterials in agrochemicals are still largely in the laboratory phase and are not sufficiently evaluated in real-world scenarios because of the variability of the results obtained under different environmental conditions.225 This is a major obstacle to the development of predictive models for environmental fate and long-term impact assessment.227

The fate and behavior of nanoparticles in soil can be highly variable and depend on several factors such as the size of the nanoparticles, their surface functionalization, and the physicochemical properties of the soil. Research demonstrates that persistence rates can range widely, with some materials remaining stable for months without significant degradation.229 Metal-based nanoparticles commonly used in antimicrobial fertilizers, such as Ag and Cu, can dissolve and release metal ions into the environment, creating dynamic contamination scenarios where the original nanoparticle properties change over time.56,75 Groundwater contamination is a pathway of concern for human exposure, as demonstrated by the ability of metal-based nanoparticles to dissolve and release metal ions into the environment, leading to dynamic contamination scenarios where the properties of the original nanoparticles change over time.4 Agricultural runoff also carries nanoparticles into water bodies, where they can be released as highly toxic ions that disrupt the respiration and reproduction of aquatic organisms, such as silver nanoparticles.16,186

Bioaccumulation potential provides extra transport pathways in biological systems. Bioaccumulation can occur when specific metal nanoparticles, such as Cu or Ag, or carbon nanotubes are absorbed by the roots of plants and then moved to aerial parts of the plants, which can then be ingested by livestock or humans, resulting in exposure beyond the point of application and long-term exposure scenarios that are hard to predict and control.11

Existing regulations are not well suited to the special fate and transport properties of nanomaterials. Current risk assessment approaches do not consider these nanoscale-specific processes, such as agglomeration, dissolution, or translocation through plant tissues and soil matrices, resulting in high uncertainty for long-term environmental effects.163

Studies show that nanoparticles can have long-term adverse impacts on soil structure, fertility and microbial communities. Long-term exposure may also impact beneficial soil microorganisms that are important for nutrient cycling, which can include nitrogen fixation and long-term agricultural productivity, as well as exposure pathways through runoff to nearby water bodies that can impact aquatic life and cause bioaccumulation along food chains.221,223

Long-term field studies to assess the cumulative effects of repeated applications of nanomaterials to soil quality and ecosystem balance will be important.139,223 Comprehensive physicochemical characterization and development of predictive models for species-specific sensitivity patterns will be important for evidence-based policy development.185

Current regulatory landscape and gaps for nano-enabled agrochemicals

One of the most important challenges to the safe and widespread use of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers is the lack of well-established regulatory frameworks. Specific risk assessments are needed to inform the development of appropriate regulations and promote public acceptance of nanomaterials in the agri-food sector,198 which is still evolving.242

One of the most important challenges for the safe and broad use of nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers is the regulatory framework. Specific risk assessment is needed to inform the development of suitable regulations and public acceptance of the use of nanomaterials in the agri-food sector,200 which is still in a process of evolution. One of the main challenges is the lack of a global standardization of testing protocols and harmonisation of regulatory approaches. Several regulatory bodies, such as the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States, the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) in the European Union, and other regulatory bodies around the world, have started to develop rules for the registration and assessment of products containing nanoparticles. However, the absence of a harmonized testing approach leaves uncertainty for developers and may result in delays to approval and market entry in different countries, as different countries have different regulatory measures, such as guidance and legislation, to ensure the safety of nano-based products in agriculture, feed and food.66,68,234,243

The European Union has taken significant steps with new requirements that came into effect on January 1, 2020, to register and evaluate the safety of nanomaterials by manufacturers, importers and users. These regulations recommend that protocols be developed to determine nanomaterials' adsorption/desorption, degradation, exposure scenarios, and ability to cross biological membranes.159,244 In Europe, the EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) recommends that nanomaterials undergo the same level of scrutiny and thorough risk assessment as conventional pesticides or fertilizers, emphasizing evaluation at each stage of their lifecycle, including production, storage, application, and disposal.11 However, significant gaps remain in regulatory coverage. The regulatory frameworks for the application of nanotechnology in agriculture are still emerging and some countries do not have legislation to cover agriculture, which makes the regulation of nanotechnology-based products a priority.159 The definition of a nanoparticle is not precisely defined by many regulatory agencies, and the differences in definitions make it difficult to draw clear lines for the safe use of nanotechnology in agriculture.163 Many regulatory agencies struggle to define nanoparticles precisely, with variations in definitions making it difficult to establish clear guidelines for their safe use in agriculture.163

Current risk assessment procedures are not well suited to the specific characteristics of nanomaterials. Unlike conventional fertilizers and pesticides, nanoparticles exhibit nanoscale-specific behavior that current regulatory guidelines may not adequately address.163 Current risk assessment frameworks typically focus on bulk chemicals and may not account for unique interactions of nanoparticles with biological tissues, ecologies, or agro-environmental systems, often overlooking particle size, shape, surface reactivity, and functionalization, key parameters that govern nanoparticle behavior and bioavailability.163 The lack of comprehensive safety data further complicates regulatory decisions. At present, relevant regulatory frameworks for nano-based products, including nanofertilizers, are unavailable, with leading countries still developing safety standards, risk assessment protocols, and regulatory frameworks.232 Safety and regulatory issues represent a prominent challenge, requiring health risk evaluations and practical regulatory frameworks to ensure safe use for farmers, consumers, and the environment.245

International organizations are working to address these gaps. Organizations such as the OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) and ISO (International Organization for Standardization) are developing protocols for nano-safety assessment. Yet, these guidelines are not consistently enforced globally, and discrepancies persist across regions.11 However, comprehensive data on nanoparticle toxicity, environmental persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and effects on non-target organisms are typically required by these frameworks.235 The establishment of comprehensive risk assessment frameworks with robust international regulations based on scientific evidence will be vital for the responsible and sustainable use of nanotechnology in agriculture.246 Coordinated efforts between regulatory figures, researchers, and industry participants are needed to develop clear guidelines that balance safety assessments with innovation, empowering responsible use of nanotechnology.234 The development of clear, practical guidelines for appropriate handling, storage, and application, along with globally recognized testing protocols and standards, will be essential for the safe deployment of these technologies.247

Risk mitigation strategies

Research development of biodegradable and green synthesis methods

Green synthesis approaches involve the synthesis of environmentally friendly, biodegradable, less toxic nanoparticles using plant extracts or natural materials that are effective against microorganisms.194,248 These green nanoparticles have reduced environmental risks and are still effective against microorganisms. Biodegradable formulations involve developing nanomaterials that degrade into non-toxic byproducts to minimize concerns about persistence and bioaccumulation.223 The focus is on using biodegradable polymers such as chitosan for encapsulation, which has proven active in reducing toxicity and leaching.246 Controlled agglomeration: control nanoparticle agglomeration to enhance bioavailability, limit environmental spread, and improve safety, as agglomeration can alter surface properties and trigger differential biological responses.227

Comprehensive risk assessment and testing protocols

Current testing is largely conducted under laboratory conditions, resulting in knowledge gaps for real agricultural scenarios: standardized testing methodologies.131,164 Current testing remains limited mainly to laboratory conditions, creating knowledge gaps for real agricultural scenarios. Life cycle assessment (LCA) involves conducting robust LCA to thoroughly evaluate environmental impacts and design suitable fertilizer products with appropriate dosages.227 Lifecycle approaches are implemented to assess the environmental impacts from production through disposal. Comprehensive long-term field studies are conducted to evaluate the cumulative effects of repeated nanomaterial applications on plant health, soil quality, and ecosystem balance, considering impacts on both target crops and non-target organisms, including pollinators, soil fauna, and aquatic ecosystems.131 Ecotoxicological assessments establish permissible concentration limits through detailed ecotoxicological studies evaluating long-term impacts on soil, water, and non-target organisms.131,247

Safe-by-design approaches

A comprehensive design strategy optimizes controlled-release properties while considering interactions with crop plants, pests, non-target organisms, and transformation fate in agricultural environments.164,249,250 Built-in safety mechanisms develop nanofertilizers with built-in safety mechanisms such as biodegradable coating systems or triggered-release formulations that respond to specific environmental conditions or plant physiological states.185 Biocompatible additives include biocompatible additives and controlled release mechanisms in formulations, as research shows that chelating agents can reduce heavy metal toxicity while improving nutrient uptake. Risk-stratified approaches prioritize safer formulations for agricultural implementation while requiring enhanced monitoring protocols for higher-risk materials, particularly metal oxide nanoparticles compared to materials like clinoptilolite.185

Regulatory framework development

Harmonized safety standards are comprehensive risk-assessment frameworks established by international regulatory bodies based on scientific evidence to ensure the responsible and sustainable application of these technologies.234,246 Standardized regulatory protocols are developed as globally recognized testing standards for nanomaterials, including mandatory pre-market field trials and post-market monitoring to detect site-specific risks.185,247 Clear and practical guidelines and labelling are developed for responsible and sustainable application, including standardized labelling requirements and certification schemes that are based on local soil conditions, crop sensitivities and climatic variables.185,247

Environmental monitoring and management

Proper nanowaste disposal methods are established to avoid environmental contamination and promote circular economy principles.251 Strict regulations regarding usage and dumping of nanowaste are implemented to avoid microbes from developing nanoparticle resistance.252 Long-term monitoring programs are implemented to track the environmental fate of nanoparticles and assess the long-standing impacts on ecosystems and human health.251 The use and disposal of nanoparticles are incorporated into sustainable agricultural practices like integrated pest management (IPM) to help reduce potential risks while retaining protective benefits.194,253

Collaborative research and development

Interdisciplinary collaboration fosters coordinated efforts between regulatory bodies, researchers, and industry stakeholders to develop clear guidelines that balance safety assessments with innovation.234 This includes collaboration among nanotechnologists, agronomists, microbiologists, and environmental scientists.163 Global toxicity databases are utilized to disseminate verified information globally, promote uniform regulatory decisions, and improve risk management.251 Public awareness and education campaigns are conducted to encourage responsible use and promote transparency in agricultural practices involving nanoparticles, while addressing misconceptions.163

Economic Considerations and scale-up challenges in nano-fertilizer production

The manufacturing of nanofertilizers is significantly more expensive than conventional fertilizers, primarily because of the need for specialized equipment, sophisticated process control, and accurate quality control. Reported production costs of nano-fertilizers range from approximately USD 2000–8000 per ton, compared with USD 400–800 per ton for conventional fertilizers, creating a major economic challenge for large-scale adoption.254,255 The use of nanogranulators, high-shear mixers, dispersion systems, and analytical instruments increases capital and operational expenses compared with traditional fertilizer manufacturing systems.255 However, these higher initial costs may be compensated by the improved nutrient-use efficiency of nano-fertilizers, which can provide 20–30% greater nutrient utilization and achieve comparable crop performance with 50–70% lower application rates. For instance, the use of nano-iron fertilizers can help to maintain the efficacy of traditional iron fertilizers while using significantly less active nutrient content, because of the increased solubility and bioavailability.256 In addition, the use of nano-encapsulation technologies allows for controlled release over a longer period of time (around 10–14 days) and can reduce nutrient losses by up to 60%, increasing the value of each unit of fertilizer applied.257 While coating technologies and hybrid formulations may add to the material and processing costs, the economic feasibility of these technologies can be enhanced by reduced fertilizer inputs, fewer application cycles, and improved crop productivity.121,258,259

Even though there are some economic benefits, the production of nano-fertilizers is still in its early developmental stage and has several technical and commercial barriers. The optimization of the synthesis of nanoparticles on an industrial scale is necessary to obtain a consistent particle size, stability, and nutrient release properties, while reducing energy consumption and production costs. The manufacture of uniform nanoparticles, such as ZnO nanoparticles, remains energy intensive, contributing to higher production expenses compared with conventional fertilizer processes.260 In addition, reliable and cost-effective raw material supply chains are necessary to support continuous large-scale manufacturing.261 Maintaining batch-to-batch consistency in particle size distribution, encapsulation efficiency, and nutrient release behavior is another major challenge for commercial production and quality assurance.121,159 Furthermore, uncertainties regarding the long-term ecological effects of nanoparticle accumulation in soil plant systems require comprehensive environmental risk assessments before widespread application.260 Regulatory limitations, including the lack of standardized toxicity evaluation methods and certification guidelines, also delay commercialization and market acceptance.121,129,260 To overcome these limitations, greener synthesis approaches, such as plant extract and microorganism-mediated nanoparticle production, are being explored as alternatives for reducing manufacturing costs and improving environmental compatibility. However, more studies are needed to get to industrial scale production. Likewise, nutrient retention and minimisation of environmental leakage can be improved using biodegradable polymer-based encapsulation systems, but there is a need to consider the additional processing steps and material requirements needed to achieve the agronomic benefits.260

Challenges and knowledge gap

The challenges of developing and implementing nanostructured antimicrobial fertilizers are complex and interrelated, both technically and in the environmental, economic and regulatory fields. Another technical challenge is the complex interaction between nanoparticles and biological systems, where the physico-chemical properties of the nanoparticles are uncertain and complex due to their small size (<100 nm), shape, atomic arrangement and structure, and therefore, their compatibility and fate in the soil-plant system.227 The physico-chemical characteristics of nanostructures influence their behavior including solubility, stability, and nutrient release rate, requiring close collaboration between researchers in nanochemistry, crop nutrition, and agronomy.262

Another challenge are the environmental and safety concerns, as the introduction of nanoparticles in agriculture has significant implications for the long-term environmental effects, including the accumulation of nanoparticles in different ecosystems and the potential impacts on flora and fauna.263 Challenges regarding stability, toxicity, and environmental impacts of nanomaterials require careful assessment of their implications for the environment, health, and society.129,218 Nanofertilizers have been found to be more phytotoxic than conventional nanoparticles, and therefore, careful assessment of their implications for the environment, health, and society is required before their pre-application.185

The lack of standardized protocols and methodological consistency creates significant barriers for research validation and commercialization. Limitations in existing research include the lack of comparative studies and methodological inconsistencies, which make it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about the relative merits of nano- and non-nano-fertilizers.255 Variability in nanoparticle size, shape, and chemical composition across studies complicates comparison and generalization, highlighting the urgent need for standardized characterization and testing protocols.221

There is no uniformity in protocols and methodological consistency, which poses a major problem for research validation and commercialization. Existing studies have limitations such as the absence of comparative studies and methodological inconsistencies, which hinder the ability to draw clear conclusions regarding the relative merits of nano- and non-nano-fertilizers, and there is a need for standardized characterization and testing protocols.255 Economic and scalability issues also hinder the widespread adoption. The use of green synthesis techniques, which rely on biological systems as environmentally friendly alternatives to chemical synthesis, is becoming more popular, but these methods are not scalable, and it is difficult to make nanomaterials more affordable for farmers around the world.223,264

Regulatory and validation gaps are important hurdles for commercialization. Some major challenges are the absence of specific regulations for agro nanotechnology products, initial public resistance, and the need for detailed life cycle assessments of these materials.246 The field requires comprehensive risk assessment and biocompatibility studies for nanoparticle acceptance, with novel analytical methods needed to develop nanomaterials with unique properties, to detect and validate them, to assess their effects under field surroundings, and to assess associated toxicity.188 Most studies focus on short-term crop yield improvements, while long-term effects on soil health, microbial bionetworks, and environmental protection remain poorly understood.221

Future directions

Novel analytical methods are needed to develop nanomaterials with unique properties, to detect and validate them, to assess their effects under field conditions, and to assess their associated toxicity.188 The implementation of nanofertilizers on commercial scales must be preceded by mandatory standardized testing protocols that integrate both efficacy and safety assessments from the earliest stages of material design.185 Key priorities include scaling up production, developing standardized protocols for safe and effective use, and harmonizing regulatory frameworks to ensure public trust and adoption.221

Future research should include long-term field studies to determine potential risks and benefits, as well as to create sustainable, cost-effective formulations, and multiple field studies should be conducted across a variety of sites to evaluate the performance of the developed nanobioformulations in terms of efficacy and environmental impact.188,255 Future research must address the identified limitations through comprehensive physicochemical characterization, long-term fate studies under realistic field conditions, and the development of predictive models for species-specific sensitivity patterns.185

Green synthesis methods using biological systems such as plant extracts, fungi, and bacteria are gaining popularity as eco-friendly alternatives to conventional chemical synthesis.223,264 Advances in green synthesis can enable cost-effective production, facilitating wider adoption in resource-limited settings.223,242 Future research challenges include enhancing the scalability of nanomaterial production and lowering the production costs to make nanomaterials more accessible to farmers around the world.223

The development of multifunctional nanofertilizers combining nutrient delivery with traits such as pest resistance or abiotic stress tolerance could revolutionize crop management.221 Next-generation platforms should integrate micronutrient cocktails or biostimulants within such coatings, creating multifunctional granules that sustain yield while reducing synthetic fertilizer loads.185 Future research directions should focus on incorporating nanotechnology-driven systems for precision agriculture and advancing multifunctional platforms to mitigate the impacts of climate change, extreme weather events, and global food insecurity.229

Future research should aim to combine nano-enabled antimicrobial fertilizers with smart and precision agriculture technologies to optimize the agronomic efficiency and reduce environmental impacts. Nanomaterials could be combined with Internet-of-Things (IoT) sensors, nanosensors, artificial intelligence (AI), remote sensing, drone-assisted monitoring, and variable-rate application systems to enable the real-time detection of nutrient deficiencies, pathogen outbreaks, and soil health, thus enabling site-specific and demand-driven delivery of nano-enabled inputs. This integration would help to increase the efficiency of nutrient use, minimize losses of agrochemicals, and optimize sustainable crop production. In addition, interdisciplinary collaboration between nanotechnologists, agronomists, plant pathologists, soil scientists, environmental scientists, materials scientists, data scientists, and regulatory agencies will be necessary to advance nano-agricultural technologies. The development of field-scale validation, long-term environmental and food safety assessment, standardized risk evaluation protocols, and the development of regulatory frameworks should be emphasized in collaborative research to ensure safe and responsible commercialization of nano-enabled agricultural systems.265–267

Establish guidelines for the responsible use of nanomaterials in agriculture and a roadmap to reduce the risk associated with the use of nano-based products.188 Future research efforts should prioritize the development of biodegradable or more environmentally benign nano-formulations, thorough ecotoxicological studies in pertinent agricultural ecosystems and the establishment of strong regulatory frameworks.18 The advancement of nano-fertilizer science requires tiered research pipelines that progress from controlled laboratory conditions to crop-diverse, season-long field evaluations, supported by robust regulatory frameworks that balance innovation with environmental and human health protection.185

The knowledge of the practical application of nano-biofertilizers should be transferred from the researchers to the authorities and sectors of industry, and new spaces for discussion should be created to emphasize the importance of nano-biofertilizers in agriculture, economy and human life, where interdisciplinary research will be essential to understand the interaction of nanoparticles in agroecosystems.221

Conclusions

The use of nanostructured materials in agriculture offers opportunities and challenges, requiring a comprehensive strategy. The risk-stratified methodologies will ensure that safer formulations are used and that strict monitoring protocols are in place for higher risk materials, including metal oxide nanoparticles, to ensure protection of ecosystems and human health. Developing a comprehensive regulatory framework with harmonized safety standards is critical for ensuring responsible usage. This includes standardized test protocols and guidelines for handling and application of nanoparticles specific to local conditions. Furthermore, proper disposal and long-term environmental monitoring are needed to minimize the risks of nano waste. The need for interdisciplinary cooperation, such as between nanotechnology, agronomy and environmental sciences, is essential to tackle the challenges of nanoparticle interactions in biological systems. The development of global toxicity databases will aid in making consistent regulatory decisions, and public awareness campaigns will help to raise awareness and uptake of agricultural solutions with the use of nanoparticles. However, the issues of stability, toxicity and environmental effects of nanomaterials are still major hurdles to implementation. Standardized protocols are needed to overcome the inconsistencies in research and to allow for more meaningful comparisons between nano and conventional fertilizers. In addition, the economic issues and the scalability of the production methods should be taken into account to make nanotechnology available to all farmers.
