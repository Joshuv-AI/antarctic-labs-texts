Health system responsiveness and associated factors among outpatients in primary healthcare units in Southern Ethiopia: a facility-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Habtamu Esubalew Bezie, Zeleke Hailemariam, Mustefa Glagn, Biniyam Demisse Andarge, Tsegazeab Ayele Meshesha, Edlework Wondmagegn Atlaw, Kidus Temesgen Worsa, Sayih Mehari Degualem, Habtamu Wondmagegn Atlaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the level of health system responsiveness (HSR) and its associated factors among outpatients attending primary healthcare units (PHCU) in Arba Minch, South Ethiopia.DesignFacility-based cross-sectional study.SettingThree PHCUs (one primary hospital and two health centres) in Arba Minch town, Southern Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 379 outpatients aged 18 years and above were selected using a systematic random sampling.Outcome measuresPrimary outcome: level of HSR, measured across seven domains (communication, confidentiality, basic amenities, dignity, choice, prompt attention and autonomy) using a 28-item tool adapted from the WHO HSR framework. Secondary outcome: factors associated with HSR, identified via bivariate and multivariable linear regression.ResultsThe overall HSR was 59.4%. The highest-performing domains were confidentiality (73.9%) and dignity (70.7%), while the choice of healthcare provider was rated lowest (34.6%). In multivariable linear regression analysis, factors significantly associated with HSR score were travel time to reach the health facility on foot (β = −0.26, 95% CI −0.37 to −0.14); out-of-pocket payment for transport (β = −6.51, 95% CI −8.33 to −4.70); patient satisfaction score (β=1.57, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.88) and perceived quality of healthcare score (β=0.32, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.49).ConclusionHSR among outpatients in PHCU was moderate, with several individual and service-related factors associated with patient experiences. These findings suggest the need for focused interventions to improve responsiveness domains, although more research is required to demonstrate causal relationships.

Introduction

The WHO has identified three major goals for the health system: promoting population health, boosting the responsiveness of the health system to the population it serves and ensuring financial protection from catastrophic healthcare costs.1 Health system responsiveness (HSR) was first recognised in the 2000 World Health Report and is now universally recognised as a major goal of national health systems.1 2

HSR refers to the capacity of the health system to meet the reasonable expectations of the people regarding the non-medical aspects of healthcare.3 This includes patients’ dignity, clear communication, confidentiality, autonomy, prompt attention, access to social support, the quality of basic amenities and the choice of healthcare provider.4 As a multidimensional construct, HSR is increasingly utilised as an indicator of health system quality, enhancing clinical effectiveness and financial protection by emphasising patient-centredness and ethical standards.3 5 6

Responsiveness offers a holistic framework for evaluating health system interactions with patients and communities.3 7 A fundamental component of modern and effective health systems is responsiveness to the needs of their clients, and enhancing responsiveness is one of the main objectives of health systems.8 9 The WHO incorporated the responsiveness indexes into the World Health Reports to encourage and guide nations towards this issue.4

Patients’ entire experiences when receiving medical care depend greatly on how responsive the health system is to their expectations. Patients comprehend and evaluate the aspects of their experiences while receiving care at a medical facility.10 The well-being of the patient can be directly affected by HSR. The overall health of patients is maintained and improved by ensuring patient comfort. As a result, improving responsiveness is the main objective for health systems.11

According to the WHO, the high-income countries such as those in Western Europe and Japan exhibit higher responsiveness scores, while African and Latin American nations report lower levels.12 In Ethiopia, study conducted in the outpatient department (OPD) revealed that the overall HSR performance was 66.2%.13

A diverse set of factors influence the healthcare system’s responsiveness, including sociodemographic factors, individual-related factors and health system-related factors.14 Evidence suggests that age, sex, education level and socioeconomic status affect expectations and perceptions of healthcare quality.15 16 Previous studies in different parts of the world found that age is a significant factor of HSR.1519 For instance, a study found that expectations of patient involvement in treatment decisions were high, particularly among younger people.15 Other studies from Nigeria10 and Tanzania20 reported that sex, educational attainment and income significantly influenced responsiveness, with male gender, higher income and greater education associated with better HSR. Marital status also demonstrated varying effects, with marriage negatively associated with HSR in some settings.19 In addition, studies found that time to reach the health facility, out-of-pocket (OOP) payment for transport and type of health facility were significantly associated with HSR.13

Furthermore, the existing works of the literature revealed that patients’ good satisfaction and perceived quality of care about the services they received with the provision of healthcare services.132124 Emerging evidence suggests that mental health conditions can negatively affect patients’ perceptions of how responsive the health system is.25 Depression is significantly linked to a greater sense of barriers to care and feelings that healthcare providers do not respond to patient concerns.26

Despite WHO’s commitment to operationalising responsiveness evaluation, there is a paucity of systematic, domain-based studies on HSR in Ethiopia, and none addressing the context of Arba Minch town. While a few studies have examined HSR in other regions of Ethiopia, their results might not be generalisable to Arba Minch, which serves a diverse population that includes rural farming, urban and semipastoral communities.13 21 Such population diversity contributes to differences in patient expectations and perceptions of care, consistent with evidence showing that sociocultural and demographic factors shape how individuals evaluate responsiveness domains.15 16 Geographic, demographic and infrastructure differences are also known to affect responsiveness outcomes in health systems.8 This study aims to address this gap by assessing the level of HSR and its associated factors among outpatients in primary healthcare unit (PHCU) in Arba Minch town, Southern Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional study design was conducted from March to April 2023 in PHCU of Arba Minch town, Southern Ethiopia. The town has one public primary hospital and two public health centres offering OPD services. Since the total number of eligible primary healthcare facilities was small, all public PHC facilities in the town were included in the study. This full inclusion ensured that the facilities were representative and removed the need for facility-level sampling. Health posts, which are part of the PHCU in Ethiopia, were excluded because they do not have OPDs.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated for both objectives. For the first objective, the sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula, with a proportion (P) of 0.662 from previous study,13 a 95% confidence level (Zα/2=1.96) and a margin of error (d) of 5% (0.05).

For the second objective, a two-population comparison formula was used to estimate the sample size needed to detect the difference between HSR and key exposure variables identified from previous studies. We used the following assumptions Zα/2=1.96 at 95% confidence level, Zβ = 80% statistical power, P1=probability of outcome in unexposed, OR and r=ratio of unexposed to exposed. Furthermore, the adequacy of the sample size for multivariable linear regressions was assessed using the rule of thumb of at least 10–15 observations per independent variable. Given that the final model had 16 independent variables and a total sample size of 379, meeting the minimum required criterion was met. This method was used to ensure enough statistical power to detect significant differences in primary explanatory variables.

Because the study had two objectives, we performed separate calculations for sample size. We selected the largest sample size to ensure adequate precision for all study objectives. A 10% non-response rate was included. The final sample size was 383 participants. During data collection, we approached 379 out of 383 participants (see table 1).

Sampling procedure

A systematic random sampling technique was used to select participants from the three PHCUs in Arba Minch town. First, the total sample size was proportionally allocated to each public health facility based on the OPD flow in the previous month. Then, the sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total patient flow by the allocated sample size. The total patient flow across the three adult OPDs was 2919, comprising 1092 from Dil Fana Primary Hospital, 996 from Shecha Health Centre and 861 from Woze Health Centre. To achieve the required sample size of 383, a sampling interval (k) of 8 was determined by dividing the total population by the sample size. Starting from a randomly selected number between 1 and 8, every eighth patient visiting the OPD during the study period was invited to participate until the required sample size for each facility was met (see details in figure 1)

Flowchart of systematic random sampling with proportional allocation to three PHCUs in Arba Minch, Southern Ethiopia. PHCU, primary healthcare unit.

All patients aged 18 and above attending the OPD of PHCU in Arba Minch town for any reason were eligible for inclusion. Patients under 18 years were excluded since they are minors and cannot give independent consent in Ethiopia. In addition, their health-seeking behaviour and expectations may also differ from those of adults. Moreover, critically ill patients who were unable to participate in the interview were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from the WHO HSR framework and related literature.3 27 28 The instrument included sections on sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, residence, educational status, marital status, occupation and household’s monthly income), health service accessibility (time to reach health facility on foot, type of health facility, current Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) membership and OOP payment for transport to reach the health facility) and individual factors (perceived quality of healthcare, perceived satisfaction, depression and responsiveness assessment questionnaires). Responsiveness was measured using 28 items distributed across seven domains: communication (5), confidentiality (3), quality of basic amenities (5), dignity (5), choice of providers (3), prompt attention (3) and autonomy (4). Each domain was rated on a five-point likert scale, from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). Outpatients assessed the quality of basic amenities based on their experience with the facility during their visit. This included the waiting area, sanitation, cleanliness and safety. This evaluation matches the WHO responsiveness framework for assessing non-clinical aspects of care at all service levels.12 13 The full HSR questionnaire used in this study is provided as online supplementary table 1. Perceived healthcare quality was assessed using 11 items adapted from SERVQUAL questionnaire.21 28 Perceived satisfaction was measured with five items on a five-point scale (1=very dissatisfied, 5=very satisfied).13 29 Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9).30

Data collectors, after receiving comprehensive training on the study protocol and ethical principles, conducted face-to-face interviews using the structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample in a nearby facility, and minor wording adjustments were made for clarity. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The HSR scale showed excellent reliability, α=0.94. The satisfaction scale had α=0.98, the perceived quality of healthcare scale had α=0.87 and the PHQ-9 had α=0.91, all indicating good reliability. The participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose and procedures before data collection. Participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. Willing participants were interviewed in quiet and comfortable setting to minimise response bias. The data collection tool was translated into Amharic and back-translated into English to ensure linguistic accuracy, with the Amharic version administered to participants.

Measurement of variables and cut-point determination

Since there are no accepted clinical cut-off points for HSR, patient satisfaction or perceived healthcare quality, we used descriptive categorisation with the demarcation threshold formula: this approach identifies the theoretical midpoint of the scale range. It has been widely used in Ethiopian health services research when no validated thresholds exist.13 24 31

Accordingly

HSR (28 items, range: 28–140) was dichotomised as ‘good’ (≥84) versus ‘poor’ (<84).

Perceived satisfaction (five items, range: 5–25) was categorised as ‘satisfied’ (≥15) versus ‘dissatisfied’ (<15).

Perceived quality of healthcare refers to how patients evaluate different aspects of service delivery, including personal interactions and technical details. This is measured using items derived from SERVQUAL and looks at the quality of the services provided. Perceived quality of healthcare (11 items, range: 11–55) was classified as ‘high’ (≥33) versus ‘low’ (<33).

The PHQ-9 total score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. A cut-off score of 10 or higher was used to indicate clinically significant depressive symptoms, following established validation studies that show the best diagnostic accuracy at this level.32

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26. Descriptive statistics summarised the distribution of variables. HSR was analysed as a continuous variable. We first used simple linear regression to examine the relationships between each independent variable and the HSR score. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in the simple linear regression analysis were included in the multivariable linear regression model. We reported adjusted beta coefficients (β) along with 95% CIs. We considered results statistically significant if the p-value was less than 0.05. We checked the assumptions of linear regression before modelling. We assessed the normality of residuals with histograms and normal P–P plots. We examined homoscedasticity using plots of residuals versus fitted values. We checked for multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation Factor, with a value under 5 deemed acceptable. No major violations were found. See online supplementary file 1.

The estimated intraclass correlation coefficient for HSR across the three facilities was 0.007. This shows that there was very little clustering of responses within the facilities. The overall regression model explained a substantial proportion of the variance in health service responsiveness, accounting for 68.8% of the variability (R² = 0.688). The results are reported by texts, tables and figures.

Result

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 379 individuals participated, resulting in a response rate of 99%. Most of the participants (60.6%) were aged between 30 and 49 years. Most of the participants (58.3%) were men. The majority were married (77%) and lived in urban areas (86%). Regarding educational status, 133 participants (35.1%) had attended high school and above, while 118 participants (31.1%) were government employees. Nearly half of the participants (43.8%) identified as followers of the Orthodox religion (see table 2 for details).

Factors associated with HSR

In simple linear regression, variables with p<0.25 were entered into the multivariable model. In the multivariable linear regression analysis, time to reach the health facility, OOP transport payment, patient satisfaction score and perceived health service quality score were significantly associated with the HSR score, with all p values less than 0.05.

For every additional minute it took to reach the health facility, the HSR score decreased by 0.26 points (β = −0.26, 95% CI −0.37 to −0.14). This shows that longer travel time was significantly associated with poorer perceived responsiveness

Patients who paid OOP for transport had much lower HSR scores than those who did not (β = −6.51, 95% CI −8.33 to −4.70). This suggests that the financial burden of transport significantly affects how responsive the system seems.

The patient satisfaction score was positively related to the HSR score (β=1.57, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.88). Each one-point increase in satisfaction score led to a 1.57-point increase in the HSR score, showing a strong and significant positive association

Similarly, the perceived healthcare service quality score was positively related to the HSR score (β=0.32, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.49; p=0.001). This indicates that a higher perceived quality of care is associated with better responsiveness ratings (See table 5 for details).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess HSR and its associated factors among patients visiting OPD at PHCU in southern Ethiopia. The overall HSR reported by the study participants was found to be 59.4%, indicating suboptimal performance with notable variability across domains. The study found walking time to the health-facility on foot, OOP transportation payment, perceived satisfaction with health services and perceived quality of health services to be significantly associated with HSR.

This study found higher report in the overall HSR compared with a study conducted in Nigeria which reported that the overall HSR in outpatient care was found to be 47%.33 This difference may be attributed to study setting, and the strong presence of the health extension programme in Ethiopia. This improves the system’s ability to respond to patient needs. Similarly, the result was higher compared with studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia. HSR performance reported as 45.8% for delivery care,24 55.3% for HIV patients,22 53.0% for maternity care34 and 45.1% in conflict-affected areas.31 The justification for this might be the difference in the study setting and the study participants. The current study assessed HSR among all outpatients from each primary healthcare facility; however, in different regions of Ethiopia, studies conducted either on case-specific responsiveness like among HIV patients or on specific services such as delivery and maternal care.

In contrast, a study conducted in primary healthcare facilities in Ethiopia reported an overall HSR of 66.2%.13 These variations likely reflect differences in patient expectations, service delivery models and contextual challenges. Urban patients, such as those in Arba Minch, may anticipate superior facilities, advanced technology and expedited services due to their environment. Consequently, they may rate HSR lower even when the quality of services is adequate.

Among the assessed domains, confidentiality (73.9%) and dignity (70.7%) demonstrated the highest levels of performance. These domains consistently achieved high scores across multiple studies. For example, confidentiality was the highest rated domain in studies conducted in Ethiopia (71.7%),13 Tanzania (86.7%),20 Iran (81.7%)35 and Brazil (91.3%).36 Dignity also exhibited strong performance, with reported scores of 71.7% in Ethiopia,13 81.4% in Tanzania,20 83.5% in Iran35 and 97.9% in Brazil.36 The consistently high scores in these two domains indicate strong adherence to fundamental ethical principles in healthcare delivery. These principles are typically less resource-intensive to maintain compared with other domains.

Conversely, the choice of healthcare provider’s domain scored the lowest, with only 34.6% of participants rating its performance as good. This consistently low performance for ‘choice of healthcare provider’ is a recurring pattern in other studies. For example, an Ethiopian study reported 37% for this domain,13 a Brazilian study found 24.4% for this domain21 and a Nepalese study recorded it as the lowest with 22.6%.37 Limited choice can indicate a scarcity of diverse providers, geographical barriers to accessing alternatives or rigid referral pathways. In resource-constrained settings, the system might prioritise efficient allocation of limited resources over offering patients a wide array of choices, leading to a less patient-centred approach in this domain.38

Several independent factors were significantly associated with HSR. These associated factors include travel time to the health facility on foot, OOP transportation payment, perceived satisfaction and perceived quality of health services. The identification of these factors provides actionable insights for improving patient experience in primary healthcare settings.

The result revelled that, for every additional minute it took to reach the health facility, the HSR score decreased by 0.26 points. The observed negative correlation between travel time and HSR supports the evidence that geographical accessibility is a major determinant of patients’ perceptions of health system performance. This result is consistent with findings from multiple countries that highlight travel time as a significant factor leading to poor responsiveness39 and also other research indicating that geographical access consistently scores the lowest ratings among responsiveness criteria.20 The measured impact indicates that even small increases in travel time can significantly diminish patients’ views on system responsiveness, which may mirror the combined challenges of transportation expenses and accessibility issues that disproportionately burden vulnerable groups.13 33 These results highlight the vital necessity of achieving geographical equity in health system design and imply that improvements in responsiveness must focus on overcoming structural barriers to accessibility.24 31

Financial barriers, including OOP transportation expenses, significantly reduced HSR. Individuals who paid for transportation OOP were significantly less likely to report good HSR compared with those who did not. Transportation expenses deter healthcare utilisation and decrease adherence, particularly among vulnerable populations. This result supports findings from Ethiopia, where patients not required to pay for transport reported notably higher responsiveness scores,13 and is consistent with evidence of wealth-related gaps in responsiveness across China, Ghana, India, Russia and South Africa.39 This finding highlights the necessity of financial equity within health systems and is consistent with research demonstrating that reduced transportation costs enhance HSR. The financial strain of seeking care extends beyond direct medical costs. It also includes indirect expenses, which disproportionately affect low-income households.40

The patient satisfaction score was positively related to the HSR score. The strong positive correlation between patient satisfaction and HSR underscores the conceptual alignment of these constructs, with satisfaction reflecting patients’ overall assessments of their care experiences and responsiveness addressing distinct non-clinical expectations. This result aligns with previous research from Ethiopia, where satisfied patients demonstrated markedly elevated responsiveness scores,13 and similar associations were observed in delivery care settings where good satisfaction was significantly linked to higher HSR.24 The strength of this association shows that advancements in domains such as dignity, communication and prompt attention are likely to foster greater patient satisfaction, thereby supporting theoretical perspectives that position responsiveness as a fundamental determinant of overall care experience.41 Evidence from conflict-affected regions in Ethiopia further substantiates this connection, with satisfied mothers consistently attributing higher ratings to HSR.31 Collectively, these findings reinforce the validity of responsiveness as a critical patient-centred outcome and suggest that targeted improvements in responsiveness domains may substantially enhance both patient satisfaction and broader health system performance.

Patient perceptions about the quality of healthcare are significant factors associated with HSR. Studies from Ethiopia provide strong statistical support: satisfied patients in primary healthcare settings are far more likely to report good HSR, with similar outcomes observed across regions and healthcare services, including delivery care.13 24 This association is not limited to personal satisfaction but extends to broader quality indicators. For example, in Thailand, women’s satisfaction with delivery care correlates with high responsiveness scores across nearly all components except provider choice.17 Evidence from Ghana supports these findings, showing that private healthcare facilities, typically rated higher for quality, receive better responsiveness ratings than public facilities among older adults.42 The consistent results from Ethiopia, Thailand and Ghana, together with evidence from Iran that healthcare system interventions play a greater role in responsiveness, highlight that perceived quality and system responsiveness are closely associated. Enhancing service quality leads directly to improved patient perceptions of how well health systems address their non-medical needs.35

The findings have significant implications for health systems seeking to improve patient-centred care. The connection to travel time shows the need to improve geographic accessibility by decentralising services. The impact of transport costs reveals the necessity to expand financial protection mechanisms to cover indirect expenses. The ongoing lack of patient choice regarding providers requires new workforce strategies and shifting tasks within limited resources. Additionally, the strong connection to patient satisfaction and perceived quality suggests that improving communication and waiting experiences at facilities is crucial. Regularly measuring responsiveness should become part of routine health management information systems to help guide and monitor these improvements.

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, so the observed associations should be viewed as correlational rather than causal. Second, data were collected using self-reported measures, which may be influenced by recall and social desirability bias. Third, since the study population consisted of outpatients, some participants may have had limited exposure to inpatient services or broader facility infrastructure, especially related to basic amenities. Finally, the study took place in a single urban setting, which may restrict the applicability of the findings to rural areas or other health system contexts.

Conclusion

This study found that HSR among outpatients was moderate, with the choice of provider being the lowest domain. Longer travel time to reach the health facility on foot and paying OOP for transport were associated with lower HSR, while higher patient satisfaction and perceived quality of healthcare were associated with better responsiveness. These findings suggest potential areas for improvement in geographic access, financial barriers and interpersonal aspects of service delivery. However, given the cross-sectional design, the results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are required to validate these associations and suggest context-specific interventions in similar primary care settings.
