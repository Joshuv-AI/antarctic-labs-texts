Identical Free Boundaries in two partially Segregated Systems
Farid Bozorgnia

Abstract
. We compare two singularly perturbed elliptic systems modeling partially phase segregation. Although the formulations are fundamentally different, we prove that their limiting configurations have identical free boundaries. The result shows that interface geometry depends only on basic structural properties of the limit segregation, harmonicity in positivity sets, and boundary data, while the limiting solution values may differ. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical findings.

35R35, 35J47, 49J45, 35B25.

1.
Introduction

The study of segregation phenomena in elliptic systems has attracted considerable attention
due to its relevance in population dynamics, phase separation, and combustion theory.
In these models, multiple species or phases compete for spatial resources,
leading to configurations where different components occupy disjoint regions of the domain.

A canonical model for such phenomena is the Caffarelli–Lin system
[
7
,
9
,
13
]

(1)

\Delta u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{u_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j}^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

which, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, leads to
pairwise segregation
:
u_{i}\cdot u_{j}=0
for all
i\neq j
.
The limiting configuration is characterized as a harmonic map into a singular target space,
and the free boundaries between phases exhibit rich geometric structure. For numerical approximations of segregation problems, we refer to

[
3
,
1
]
.
The uniqueness of limiting solutions in strongly competing systems
was established in
[
2
]
.

We study the limiting solution of two singularly perturbed elliptic systems called partially segregated systems.
System A provides an explicit characterization of the interface through a harmonic difference structure, whereas System B enforces segregation through energy minimization, with limiting interfaces determined only indirectly from solution behavior. Although these systems have different mechanisms, we prove (Theorem
4.9
)
that they produce
identical free boundaries
. However, the solution values may differ (Example
4.10
).
This result clarifies that the geometry of segregation is universal and independent of the formulation,
while function values depend on the specific method.

Our aim in this work is to show that two different systems of partial segregation problems produce identical free boundaries. The finding has important implications for both theory
and numerical practice, clarifying the robustness of segregation patterns across different formulations.

2.
Problem Setting

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with a smooth boundary.
We consider two systems of partial segregation with
m=3
components. The first system reads as
[
8
,
5
]

(2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Delta u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\prod_{j=1}^{3}u_{j}^{\varepsilon}&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\phi_{i}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\quad i=1,2,3.

The second system is the energy-minimizing penalization approach
[
11
,
12
,
4
]
:

(3)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Delta u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{u_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\prod_{j\neq i}(u_{j}^{\varepsilon})^{2}&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\phi_{i}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\quad i=1,2,3.

System B is the Euler-Lagrange equation for minimizing the penalized energy:

(4)

E_{\varepsilon}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\right)^{2}\,dx,

over the constraint set

(5)

\left\{(u_{1},\cdots,u_{m})\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{m}:u_{i}\geq 0,\,u_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{i}\right\}.

The minimization problem (
4
) can be viewed as a penalized version of the minimization of

(6)

E_{0}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,dx,

over the nonconvex set

(7)

\mathcal{S}=\left\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{m}:u_{i}\geq 0,\;\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}=0\ \text{in }\Omega,\;u_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{i}\right\}.

Moreover, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the penalized energy functional
E_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
E_{0}
. For a rigorous proof of this result, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The boundary data
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
satisfy:

(i)

\phi_{i}\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
for
i=1,2,3
,

(ii)

\phi_{1}(x)\phi_{2}(x)\phi_{3}(x)=0
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
(segregation at boundary).

Definition 2.2
.

For
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
, let
h_{ij}\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})

denote the unique harmonic function satisfying:

(8)

\begin{cases}\Delta h_{ij}=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
h_{ij}=\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Properties:
h_{ij}=-h_{ji}
and
h_{ik}=h_{ij}+h_{jk}
.

3.
Basic Facts and Background

Consider the solution of System B. By the maximum principle for linear elliptic equations,

u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad\|u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|\phi_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}.

Let
h_{i}
be the harmonic extension of the boundary data
\phi_{i}
:

\begin{cases}-\Delta h_{i}=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
h_{i}=\phi_{i}&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The function
h_{i}
is independent of
\varepsilon
and satisfies
\|h_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega,\phi_{i})
.

Recall that
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}
solves

-\Delta u_{i}^{\varepsilon}+c_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

where
c_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\prod_{j\neq i}(u_{j}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\geq 0
.
Subtracting
\Delta h_{i}=0
from (
3
):

-\Delta(u_{i}^{\varepsilon}-h_{i})+c_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,u_{i}^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{in }\Omega.

Since
c_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
(u_{i}^{\varepsilon}-h_{i})|_{\partial\Omega}=0
,
by the maximum principle:
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\leq h_{i}
in
\Omega
, so
0\leq u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\leq h_{i}

in
\Omega
. Testing equation (
3
) with
u_{i}^{\varepsilon}-h_{i}
and integrating:

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla h_{i}\,dx\leq\|\nabla u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore,

\|\nabla u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla h_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega,\phi_{i}).

The family
\{u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
By compactness, up to a subsequence:

u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{i}\quad\text{weakly in }H^{1}(\Omega),\quad u_{i}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{i}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega).

Minimizers
u^{\varepsilon}
satisfy the comparison inequality
E_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon})\leq E_{\varepsilon}(h)
,
where
h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})
is any segregated competitor.
Since the Dirichlet energy of
h
is bounded independently of
\varepsilon
:

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{j}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx\leq C,

which gives
\int_{\Omega}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{j}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dx\to 0
. Therefore:

(9)

\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(x)=0\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
4
]

Let
u^{\varepsilon}=(u^{\varepsilon}_{1},u^{\varepsilon}_{2},u^{\varepsilon}_{3})
be a minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
.
Then any limit point
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
as
\varepsilon\to 0

is a minimizer of the constrained Dirichlet energy:

\min_{(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,dx.

Proof.

This follows from the
\Gamma
-convergence of
E_{\varepsilon}
to
E_{0}
(Theorem 3.1 of
[
4
]
).
By the fundamental theorem of
\Gamma
-convergence (cf.
[
6
]
, Theorem 1.8),
minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
converge to minimizers of the
\Gamma
-limit
E_{0}
, where
E_{0}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx
on
\mathcal{S}
and
E_{0}=+\infty
outside
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

4.
Main Results

We establish our main result, stating that both systems produce identical free boundaries. We present an explicit form of the asymptotic solution for system A, which is given in
[
5
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The limit
(u_{1}^{A},u_{2}^{A},u_{3}^{A})
of System A is given by:

\displaystyle u_{1}^{A}

\displaystyle=\max\bigl(\max(h_{12},h_{13}),0\bigr),

\displaystyle u_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{A}-h_{12},

\displaystyle u_{3}^{A}

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{A}-h_{13}.

This satisfies: (i)
u_{i}^{A}\geq 0
, (ii)
u_{1}^{A}u_{2}^{A}u_{3}^{A}=0
a.e., (iii)
u_{i}^{A}-u_{j}^{A}=h_{ij}
(harmonic).

Proof.

The construction is a unique configuration that satisfies the harmonic difference relations and the segregation constraint.
Non-negativity follows by construction. At each point, either
u_{1}^{A}=0
or
u_{1}^{A}=\max(h_{12},h_{13})
.
In the latter case, either
u_{2}^{A}=0
(if
h_{12}\geq h_{13}
) or
u_{3}^{A}=0
(if
h_{13}>h_{12}
). Thus
u_{1}^{A}u_{2}^{A}u_{3}^{A}=0
.
∎

We prove that the limiting solution for System B has the harmonic property in the support interior. This property, combined with segregation and boundary conditions, is sufficient to prove that System B interfaces coincide with System A interfaces.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon},u_{3}^{\varepsilon})
be the solution to
System B, and let
(u_{1}^{B},u_{2}^{B},u_{3}^{B})
denote its limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then:

(10)

\Delta u_{i}^{B}=0\quad\text{in }\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}^{\circ}\quad(i=1,2,3),

where
\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}^{\circ}
denotes the open interior of the support of
u_{i}^{B}
.

Proof.

The proof uses first-order optimality conditions from the energy minimization problem. By Lemma
3.1
(
\Gamma
-convergence of
E_{\varepsilon}
to
E_{0}
), the limit
(u_{1}^{B},u_{2}^{B},u_{3}^{B})
is a minimizer of the constrained Dirichlet energy
E_{0}
subject to the constraint set
\mathcal{S}
.

Since minimizers of
E_{0}
are locally Hölder continuous, positivity persists in a neighborhood. Let
x_{0}
be in the interior of the support of
u_{i}^{B}
, so there exists a small ball
B_{\delta}(x_{0})\subset\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}^{\circ}
such that
u_{i}^{B}(x)>c
for some constant
c>0
. For any test function
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\delta}(x_{0}))
, the perturbation

u_{i}^{B}+t\eta
satisfies:

•

Boundary conditions:
(u_{i}^{B}+t\eta)|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{i}

(since
\eta
has compact support in
B_{\delta}\subset\Omega
)

•

Non-negativity: for small
|t|
we have
u_{i}^{B}+t\eta\geq c/2>0
, since
u_{i}^{B}>c
and
\eta
is bounded.

•

The segregation constraint is unaffected by perturbations of
u_{i}
, i.e,

(u_{i}^{B}+t\eta)\prod_{j\neq i}u_{j}^{B}=0.

Therefore,
u_{i}^{B}+t\eta
remains in
\mathcal{S}
for small
|t|
. By minimality of
u_{i}^{B}
:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{0}(u_{1}^{B},\ldots,u_{i}^{B}+t\eta,\ldots,u_{3}^{B})\bigg|_{t=0}=0.

Computing the directional derivative:

\frac{d}{dt}(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u_{i}^{B}+t\eta)|^{2}\,dx)\bigg|_{t=0}=2\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{i}^{B}\cdot\nabla\eta\,dx=0\quad\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{\delta}(x_{0})).

Integrating by parts and using the fact that
\eta
has compact support in
B_{\delta}(x_{0})
yields

\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{0})}\Delta u_{i}^{B}\cdot\eta\,dx=0.

This shows

\Delta u_{i}^{B}=0\quad\text{in }B_{\delta}(x_{0}).

Since
x_{0}\in\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}^{\circ}
was arbitrary, we have:

\Delta u_{i}^{B}=0\quad\text{in }\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}^{\circ}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.1.
Interface Characterization

Definition 4.3
.

For a solution
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
satisfying
u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}=0
, the bulk and two-phase regions are:

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\{u_{1}>0,\,u_{2}=u_{3}=0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\{u_{2}>0,\,u_{1}=u_{3}=0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle=\{u_{3}>0,\,u_{1}=u_{2}=0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{12}

\displaystyle=\{u_{1}>0,\,u_{2}>0,\,u_{3}=0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{13}

\displaystyle=\{u_{1}>0,\,u_{3}>0,\,u_{2}=0\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{23}

\displaystyle=\{u_{2}>0,\,u_{3}>0,\,u_{1}=0\}.

In the following, we recall Weyl’s Lemma
[
10
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open. If
u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)
satisfies

\Delta u=0
in the distributional sense, then
u
is smooth and harmonic
in the classical sense.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in S
that minimizes the Dirichlet energy
E_{0}
,
the interfaces between pure regions lie on harmonic level sets:

(11)

\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\partial\Omega_{j}\subseteq\{h_{ij}=0\}\cap\Omega.

Proof.

We prove the result for
\Gamma_{12}:=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
; the
remaining cases follow by relabeling indices. If
\Gamma_{12}=\emptyset
, the
statement is vacuously true, so assume
\Gamma_{12}\neq\emptyset
. Define

w:=u_{1}-u_{2}
in
\Omega
. Since
\Gamma_{12}\subset\partial\{u_{1}>0\}\cap\partial\{u_{2}>0\}
, continuity implies
u_{1}|_{\Gamma_{12}}=u_{2}|_{\Gamma_{12}}=0
,
so
w=0
on
\Gamma_{12}
.

Let
x_{0}\in\Gamma_{12}
be arbitrary. Since
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
,
every neighborhood of
x_{0}
intersects both
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, which are open
subsets of
\{u_{3}=0\}
. Let
U
be a connected open neighborhood of
x_{0}
such that

u_{3}\equiv 0
on
U\cap(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\cup\Omega_{12})
, where

\Omega_{12}=\{u_{1}>0,u_{2}>0,u_{3}=0\}
. Define

V:=U\cap(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\cup\Omega_{12})
; this is a non-empty open set
(since
U
intersects
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
) where
u_{3}\equiv 0
.

In each subregion of
V
, the function
w
is harmonic: in
\Omega_{1}\cap V
we have

w=u_{1}
; in
\Omega_{2}\cap V
we have
w=-u_{2}
; in
\Omega_{12}\cap V
we have

w=u_{1}-u_{2}
, all harmonic by Lemma 4.2. To verify harmonicity across

\Gamma_{12}\cap V
, we use optimality. Let
W\Subset V
be open with

W\cap\Gamma_{12}\neq\emptyset
, and let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(W)
. Since

u_{3}\equiv 0
in
W
, the segregation constraint is unaffected by perturbations
of
(u_{1},u_{2})
. For
\eta^{\pm}:=\max(\pm\eta,0)
, the perturbations

(u_{1}-t\eta^{-},u_{2}+t\eta^{-},u_{3})
and
(u_{1}+t\eta^{+},u_{2}-t\eta^{+},u_{3})

lie in
S
for sufficiently small
t>0
. First-order necessary conditions for
minimality yield
\int_{W}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\eta^{\pm}\,dx=0
. Decomposing

\eta=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
gives
\int_{W}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\eta\,dx=0
.
Since
W
was arbitrary,
\Delta w=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(V)
, and Weyl’s lemma
implies
w\in C^{\infty}(V)
is classically harmonic.

Both
w
and
h_{12}
are harmonic in
V
. On
\partial V\cap\partial\Omega

(if non-empty), we have
w=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}=h_{12}
. The difference

v:=w-h_{12}
is harmonic in
V
.

We claim
v\equiv 0
in
V
, which implies
w=h_{12}
. To see this, observe
that both
w
and
h_{12}
extend as harmonic functions to larger domains. Since
they agree on
\partial\Omega
(where
w=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}=h_{12}
), and
the harmonic extension from boundary data is unique, we have
w=h_{12}
wherever
both are defined and harmonic. In particular,
w=h_{12}
in
V
.

Since
w=0
on
\Gamma_{12}
and
w=h_{12}
, we conclude
h_{12}(x_{0})=0
.
As
x_{0}\in\Gamma_{12}
was arbitrary, this establishes

\Gamma_{12}\subseteq\{h_{12}=0\}\cap\Omega
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The variational argument establishes the transmission condition
\partial_{\nu}u_{1}|_{\Omega_{1}}+\partial_{\nu}u_{2}|_{\Omega_{2}}=0
on
\Gamma_{12}
, ensuring
w
has no distributional Laplacian mass on the interface.

Proposition 4.7
.

For any
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in S
that minimizes
E_{0}
, the interfaces between two-phase
regions lie on harmonic level sets:

(12)

\partial\Omega_{ij}\cap\partial\Omega_{ik}\subseteq\{h_{jk}=0\}\cap\Omega,\quad i,j,k\text{ distinct}.

We prove for
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega_{12}\cap\partial\Omega_{13}
. Let

w:=u_{2}-u_{3}
. Since
u_{2}|_{\Gamma}=u_{3}|_{\Gamma}=0
by continuity, we
have
w=0
on
\Gamma
.

In
\Omega_{12}
, we have
u_{3}=0
, so
w=u_{2}
is harmonic. In
\Omega_{13}
,
we have
u_{2}=0
, so
w=-u_{3}
is harmonic. For energy minimizers, the
first-order optimality condition for the interface position gives

|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}=|\nabla u_{3}|^{2}
on
\Gamma
(see

[
7
,
5
]
).
Since both
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
vanish on

and are harmonic in their

\Gamma
, yielding the

\partial_{\nu}u_{2}|_{\Omega_{12}}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}|_{\Omega_{13}}
.
This ensures
\Delta w=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{12}\cup\Gamma\cup\Omega_{13})
.

By Weyl’s lemma,
w
is classically harmonic. Since
w=h_{23}
on

\partial\Omega
and both are harmonic, uniqueness gives

.
Since
w=0
on
\Gamma
, we conclude
\Gamma\subseteq\{h_{23}=0\}\cap\Omega
.

Remark 4.8
.

The interface between
\Omega_{ij}
and
\Omega_{ik}
is determined by the level set
of the difference
h_{jk}
, i.e., the components that transition across
the interface. This principle ensures the partition is uniquely determined by the
level sets
\{h_{12}=0\}
,
\{h_{13}=0\}
,
\{h_{23}=0\}
of harmonic differences,
which depend only on boundary data.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(u_{1}^{A},u_{2}^{A},u_{3}^{A})
denote the limit of System A as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
and let
(u_{1}^{B},u_{2}^{B},u_{3}^{B})
denote the limit of System B as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Then:

(1)

The free boundaries coincide:

\Gamma_{i}^{A}:=\partial\{u_{i}^{A}>0\}=\partial\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}=:\Gamma_{i}^{B},\quad i=1,2,3.

(2)

The domains partition identically:

\Omega_{i}^{A}:=\{u_{i}^{A}>0\}=\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}=:\Omega_{i}^{B},\quad i=1,2,3.

(3)

The two-phase regions are identical:

\Omega_{ij}^{A}:=\{u_{i}^{A}>0,u_{j}^{A}>0,u_{k}^{A}=0\}=\Omega_{ij}^{B},\quad i,j,k\text{ distinct}.

Proof.

Both System A and System B limits satisfy the following common properties:

\begin{cases}(i)\quad u_{i}\geq 0,\quad u_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{i}\\
(ii)\quad u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}=0\text{ a.e.}\\
(iii)\quad\Delta u_{i}=0\text{ in }\{u_{i}>0\}.\end{cases}

System A additionally satisfies the harmonic difference property
u_{i}^{A}-u_{j}^{A}=h_{ij}
in
\Omega
(by construction). However, this structure is not needed to determine the interface geometry; properties (i)-(iii) alone are sufficient.

The key insight is that properties (i)-(iii) uniquely constrain the location of all interfaces through level sets of harmonic functions. By Lemma
4.5
, any solution
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
satisfying (i)-(iii) must have the interface between pure regions
\Omega_{i}
and
\Omega_{j}
lie on the level set
\{h_{ij}=0\}
. Similarly, by Proposition
4.7
, the interface between two-phase regions
\Omega_{ij}
and
\Omega_{ik}
lies on
\{h_{jk}=0\}
.

The level sets
\{h_{12}=0\}
,
\{h_{13}=0\}
, and
\{h_{23}=0\}
are fixed
(d-1)
-dimensional manifolds determined entirely by the boundary data through Definition
2.2
. These level sets partition
\Omega
into disjoint regions according to the ordering of
h_{ij}
values. The three constraints:

\displaystyle\text{Interface between }\Omega_{1}\text{ and }\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{h_{12}=0\},

\displaystyle\text{Interface between }\Omega_{1}\text{ and }\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{h_{13}=0\},

\displaystyle\text{Interface between }\Omega_{2}\text{ and }\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{h_{23}=0\}

uniquely determine which regions of
\Omega
have
\{u_{1}>0\}
,
\{u_{2}>0\}
, and
\{u_{3}>0\}
.

Since both System A and System B limits satisfy properties (i)-(iii), they must both produce the same spatial partition:

\{u_{i}^{A}>0\}=\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,3.

This immediately implies the free boundaries coincide:

(13)

\Gamma_{i}^{A}=\partial\{u_{i}^{A}>0\}=\partial\{u_{i}^{B}>0\}=\Gamma_{i}^{B}.

The two-phase regions coincide as a direct consequence of the partition equivalence:

\Omega_{ij}^{A}=\{u_{i}^{A}>0,u_{j}^{A}>0,u_{k}^{A}=0\}=\{u_{i}^{B}>0,u_{j}^{B}>0,u_{k}^{B}=0\}=\Omega_{ij}^{B}.

∎

4.2.
Geometric Structure of the Partition

The three harmonic differences
h_{12},h_{13},h_{23}
satisfy the linear constraint

(14)

h_{13}=h_{12}+h_{23},

equivalently
h_{12}+h_{23}+h_{31}=0
, so only two of these functions are independent. The sign patterns of
(h_{12},h_{23})
determine distinct regions in
\Omega
. Using the explicit formulas from Proposition
4.1
:

u_{1}=\max(\max(h_{12},h_{13}),0),\quad u_{2}=u_{1}-h_{12},\quad u_{3}=u_{1}-h_{13},

we identify which components are positive in each region.

In the region where
h_{23}>0
and
h_{13}>0
, the constraint (
14
) gives
h_{13}=h_{12}+h_{23}>h_{12}
, so
\max(h_{12},h_{13})=h_{13}
. Thus
u_{1}=h_{13}>0
,
u_{2}=h_{13}-h_{12}=h_{23}>0
, and
u_{3}=h_{13}-h_{13}=0
. This is the two-phase region
\Omega_{12}=\{u_{1}>0,u_{2}>0,u_{3}=0\}
.

In the region where
h_{23}<0
and
h_{12}>0

For the partition to be well-defined with smooth interfaces, we require a generic position condition: the harmonic functions
h_{ij}
should have non-vanishing gradients on their zero sets,

(18)

\nabla h_{ij}\neq 0\quad\text{on }\{h_{ij}=0\}\cap\Omega.

This ensures that each level set
\{h_{ij}=0\}
is a smooth
(d-1)
-dimensional manifold. The condition (
18
) holds for generic boundary data satisfying Assumption
2.1
: by the unique continuation property for harmonic functions, if
\nabla h_{ij}=0
on an open subset of
\{h_{ij}=0\}
, then
h_{ij}\equiv\mathrm{const}
, which is excluded when
\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}
is non-constant on
\partial\Omega
.

Under this non-degeneracy condition, the level sets
\{h_{12}=0\}
,
\{h_{13}=0\}
,
\{h_{23}=0\}
are smooth hypersurfaces that partition
\Omega
into three two-phase regions as described above. Pure regions have empty interior, and triple junction points form a
(d-2)
-dimensional set. This geometric structure is universal: it depends only on the boundary data through the harmonic extensions
h_{ij}
, independent of whether the solution arises from System A or System B.

Although interfaces coincide, solution values may differ, as shown in the following example.

Example 4.10
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)
with boundary conditions:

\phi_{1}(0)=1,\quad\phi_{1}(1)=0,\qquad\phi_{2}(0)=0,\quad\phi_{2}(1)=1,\qquad\phi_{3}(0)=1,\quad\phi_{3}(1)=1.

The harmonic differences are:
h_{12}(x)=1-2x
,
h_{13}(x)=-x
,
h_{23}(x)=x-1
.
The limiting solution of System A is

\displaystyle u_{1}^{A}

\displaystyle=(1-2x)^{+},\quad u_{2}^{A}=(2x-1)^{+},\quad u_{3}^{A}=\begin{cases}1-x&x\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}],\\
x&x\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1].\end{cases}

Note that
u_{3}^{A}
has a kink at
x=1/2
. The solution of System B is:

\displaystyle u_{1}^{B}

\displaystyle=(1-2x)^{+},\quad u_{2}^{B}=(2x-1)^{+},\quad u_{3}^{B}\equiv 1\quad\text{(smooth)}.

The free boundary point for both systems is
x=1/2
, but
u_{3}^{A}\neq u_{3}^{B}
.

5.
Numerical Simulation

We validate the theoretical predictions through numerical simulations of both systems.
The domain
\Omega=[-1,1]^{2}
is discretized using a uniform Cartesian grid with mesh size

h=\Delta x=\Delta y=0.01
, resulting in a
200\times 200
grid.
The elliptic systems are discretized by standard finite difference schemes.

Parameters are chosen as the penalization parameter chosen to enforce segregation
\varepsilon=10^{-10}
, mesh: size
h=0.01
, interface threshold:
\delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon}=10^{-5}
for distinguishing active regions. Although the mesh does not fully resolve the
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
transition layer, our goal is interface localization rather than accurate resolution of the boundary layer. The chosen threshold
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
robustly identifies interfaces.

Note that the mesh size should be small enough to resolve interface layers of width
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})=10^{-5}
. However, the numerical goal is interface localization rather than full resolution of the transition layer. The threshold choice
\delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is motivated by the segregation decay rate: in bulk regions far from interfaces, components should be exponentially small with a characteristic decay length
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. This threshold is small enough to capture true interfaces while large enough to avoid round-off error.
