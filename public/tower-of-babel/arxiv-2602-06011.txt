Excursion decomposition of the XOR-Ising model
Tomás Alcalde López †, Avelio Sepúlveda ∗

Abstract
. We study the excursion decomposition of the two-dimensional critical XOR-Ising model with either + + or free boundary conditions. In the first part, we construct the decomposition directly in the continuum. This construction relies on the identification of the XOR-Ising field with the cosine or sine of a Gaussian free field (GFF) ϕ \phi multiplied by α = 1 / 2 \alpha=1/\sqrt{2} , and is obtained by an appropriate exploration of two-valued level sets of the GFF. More generally, the same construction applies to the fields : cos ⁡ ( α ​ ϕ ) : :\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!: and : sin ⁡ ( α ​ ϕ ) : :\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!: for any α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . In the second part, we show that the continuum excursion decomposition arises as the scaling limit of the double random current decomposition of the critical XOR-Ising model on the square lattice. To this end, we exploit the rich Markovian structure of the discrete decomposition and strengthen the convergence of the double random current height function to the continuum GFF by establishing joint convergence with its cosine and sine. We conjecture that for α ∈ [ 1 / 2 , 3 / 2 ) \alpha\in[1/2,\sqrt{3}/2) the continuum excursion decompositions arise as the scaling limit of those of the Ashkin-Teller polarisation field along its critical line.



1.
Introduction

The XOR-Ising model is a particular instance of the Ashkin-Teller model
[
13
]
and can be obtained as the product of two independent Ising models. In the early 2010s, numerical simulations suggested that certain geometric properties of the critical XOR-Ising model are related to specific level sets of the Gaussian free field (GFF)
[
62
]
. More recently, inspired by the bosonisation framework in physics, it was shown that its correlation functions are related to those of observables of the GFF
[
14
,
40
]
. In this paper, we show that this correspondence holds also at the level of excursion decompositions in the continuum.

Excursion decompositions are ubiquitous in the study of statistical physics models. They provide a way to uncover independent structure in dependent systems and, at the same time, allow one to reformulate questions about correlation functions as connectivity problems in suitable percolation-type models.

We say that a random (generalized) function
\psi
admits an
excursion decomposition
if there exist positive measures
(\mu_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and independent random signs
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that

(i)

The excursions
C_{k}
, that is to say the supports of the measures
\mu_{k}
, are connected and pairwise disjoint.

(ii)

The field can be recovered as

\psi=\sum_{k\geq 1}\xi_{k}\mu_{k},

where the sum is understood in an appropriate distributional sense.

The first excursion decomposition appearing in the literature was constructed by Itô
[
39
]
to describe the Brownian motion. About a decade later, the so-called Edwards–Sokal coupling
[
36
,
34
]
provided an excursion decomposition of the Ising model by coupling it to the Fortuin-Kasteleyn (FK) random cluster model
[
36
]
. Over the last decade, many new excursion decompositions have been discovered, both for discrete and continuum models.

On the discrete side, three notable examples of models whose excursion decompositions were described in recent years are the discrete Gaussian free field
[
49
]
, the XOR-Ising model
[
48
,
26
]
, and the Ising model
[
46
,
1
]
. Importantly, the latter two decompositions are based on a double random current (DRC) representation and can be viewed as dual to one another.

On the continuum side, it has been shown that scaling limits of discrete excursion decompositions yield continuum ones. In
[
20
,
19
]
, it was proved that, in two dimensions at criticality, the FK–Ising coupling converges to an excursion decomposition of the continuum magnetisation field. In
[
1
]
, the convergence of the DRC-based decomposition of the Ising model was established, defining a natural coupling between the magnetisation field and the GFF. Furthermore, in
[
7
]
, the excursion decomposition of the continuum GFF was built and shown to be the scaling limit of the excursion decomposition of the metric graph GFF.

The continuum decompositions in
[
7
]
and
[
1
]
are closely related to the theory of level sets of the Gaussian free field
[
10
,
5
,
6
,
11
]
. Namely, these decompositions can be described through suitable iterative explorations of (two-valued) level sets of the Gaussian free field, and the resulting measures can be identified as the conformal Minkowski contents of their supports.

In the present paper, we focus on constructing the excursion decomposition of the scaling limit of the critical XOR-Ising model in two dimensions. Our starting point is the bosonisation of the Ising model, i.e. the identification of the continuum XOR-Ising field with a trigonometric function of the Gaussian free field
[
63
,
30
,
33
,
17
,
40
]
. More generally, we construct a decomposition for the fields
:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:
and
:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We subsequently show that the discrete decomposition of the critical XOR-Ising model converges to its continuum counterpart.

We present our results in two parts. First, we state the excursion decompositions of the continuum fields. Then, we explain the scaling limit results obtained. We also devote a short section to discuss the Ashkin-Teller polarisation field.

(vii)

(viii)

Note that the discrete XOR-Ising model lacks any simple Markov property. This makes the existence of an excursion decomposition even more surprising, as usually these decompositions are chosen from the zero-level set of the field and are proved using the Markov property. This is the only (natural) example we know of a non-Markovian model that has an excursion decomposition.

To prove Theorem
1.2

:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:

:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:

:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:

:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:

[

A short note on the AT polarisation

On a final note, let us briefly discuss potential generalizations to the Ashkin-Teller (AT) polarisation field, in the spirit of

, is given by the probability measure

\mathbf{P}^{AT}_{J,U}(\sigma_{\delta},\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta})=\frac{1}{Z_{J,U}^{AT}}\exp\left\{\sum_{x\sim y}\left(J\big(\sigma_{\delta}(x)\sigma_{\delta}(y)+\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta}(x)\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta}(y)\big)+U\sigma_{\delta}(x)\sigma_{\delta}(y)\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta}(x)\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta}(y)\right)\right\}

on pairs of interacting spin configurations
(\sigma_{\delta},\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta})\in\{\pm 1\}^{D_{\delta}}\times\{\pm 1\}^{D_{\delta}}
. The AT polarisation is defined as

\tau_{\delta}=\sigma_{\delta}\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta},

which at the decoupling point
U=0
is precisely the XOR-Ising model. Along the critical line

51
]
given by

(1.15)

\sinh(2J)=e^{-2U}\quad\text{and}\quad U\leq J,

it has been predicted in the physics literature

,

,

]
that the scaling limit of the polarisation field is given by
:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:
and
:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
for an explicit value

We refer to

2.1.
Gaussian free field

Let us start by defining the two-dimensional Gaussian free field (GFF) and introducing the basic properties we make use of. For further background we refer the reader to
[
59
,
61
,
16
]
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain. The Gaussian free field
\phi
with zero boundary conditions in
D
is the centred Gaussian process with covariance given by the Green’s function
G_{D}
of the Laplacian with zero boundary conditions in
D
. Recall that

G_{D}(z,w)=-\log|z-w|+\log\mathrm{CR}(w,D)+o(1)\qquad\text{as }z\to w,

where
\mathrm{CR}(w,D)
denotes the conformal radius of
D
seen from
w
(see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 1.23]
). Since the Green’s function is singular on the diagonal, the GFF cannot be defined pointwise and is instead a random distribution (generalised function). More precisely, it can be defined as a centred Gaussian process
\{(\phi,f):f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)\}

with covariance given by

\mathbb{E}\left[(\phi,f)(\phi,g)\right]=\iint_{D\times D}f(z)G_{D}(z,w)g(w)dzdw.

It is known (see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 1.45]
) that for every
s<0
the GFF admits a version in the Sobolev space
H^{s}(D)
. If
\rho_{z,\varepsilon}
denotes the uniform probability measure on the circle
\partial B(z,\varepsilon)
of radius
\varepsilon
around
z
, then the circle average process

\phi_{\varepsilon}(z):=(\phi,\rho_{z,\varepsilon})

is well defined and continuous in
(z,\varepsilon)
(see
[
16
, Section 1.12]
). Moreover,
\{\phi_{\varepsilon}(z):z\in D,\varepsilon>0\}
is a centred Gaussian process with covariance given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{\varepsilon}(z)\phi_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})\right]\ \begin{cases}=G_{D}(z,z^{\prime}),&\text{if }B(z,\varepsilon),B(z^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})\subset D\text{ and }|z-z^{\prime}|\geq\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime},\\[5.69046pt]
=-\log(\varepsilon\vee\varepsilon^{\prime})+\log\mathrm{CR}(z,D),&\text{if }z=z^{\prime}\text{ and }B(z,\varepsilon)\subset D,\\[5.69046pt]
\leq G_{D}(z,z^{\prime}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

3.
Construction of the decomposition

The purpose of this section is to describe the excursion decompositions considered throughout the paper. The key idea is to understand how a given two-valued set “acts” on the fields
:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:
or
:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
by describing the conditional law of the field (or, equivalently, the restriction of the field to the two-valued set). For the sake of exposition, we will write for example

to denote the “action” of exploring the values of

for the labels of the loops

to emphasize that they are i.i.d. coin tosses. Slightly abusing notation, we write any of the following interchangeably

\ell
.

Remark 3.1
.

The discussion above suggests a natural distinction between

and
conditionally thin sets

either

\displaystyle(\psi,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})\to 0\ \ \text{ as }n\to\infty,

for the case of conditionally thin sets, or

\displaystyle\sum_{\square\subset[A]_{n}}|(\psi,f\mathbf{1}_{\square})|\mathbf{1}_{\{A\cap\square\neq\emptyset\}}\to 0\ \ \text{ as }n\to\infty,

for the case of absolutely thin sets.

The reason why this distinction is useful is that basic operations between thin sets (such as union, intersection or containment) do not always result in thin sets. However, if one of the sets is absolutely thin, then everything works as expected. We stress that all known proofs of thinness starting from an assumption on the dimension prove absolute thinness.

4.1.

We begin by stating some straightforward correlation inequalities that we use extensively. For convenience, we write

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

(i)

For any

\displaystyle\langle:\!\overline{\mathrm{cos}}(\alpha\phi(z)+u)\!::\!\overline{\mathrm{cos}}(\alpha\phi(w)+u)\!:\rangle\leq\langle:\!\sin(\alpha\phi(z))\!::\!\sin(\alpha\phi(w))\!:\rangle.

Proof.

(i)

For odd

(ii)

This follows from a direct computation and the fact that

for all

(iii)

Again, this follows from a direct computation using

∎

Remark 4.2
.

For

, the second (resp. third) inequality states that the truncated two-point function of XOR-Ising with

[

We now turn to study a different feature of the two-point correlation functions: monotonicity in the domain. Surprisingly, proving this monotonicity is subtle, needing to first study the behaviour of the two-point functions under infinitesimal deformations of the domain. This monotonicity will be a crucial input in the sequel. In particular, (

:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
in an arbitrary domain

. As a consequence,
:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
can be defined in any domain (see Proposition

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
D,\tilde{D}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be two simply connected domains such that
D\subseteq\tilde{D}
. Let
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
be two GFFs in the respective domains. We have that:

(i)

For any
\alpha\in(0,\sqrt{2})
and any

(4.2)

\langle:\!\cos(\alpha\phi(z))\!::\!\cos(\alpha\phi(w))\!:\rangle\geq\langle:\!\cos(\alpha\tilde{\phi}(z))\!::\!\cos(\alpha\tilde{\phi}(w))\!:\rangle.

(ii)

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
and any
z,w\in D

(4.3)

\langle:\!\sin(\alpha\phi(z))\!::\!\sin(\alpha\phi(w))\!:\rangle\leq\langle:\!\sin(\alpha\tilde{\phi}(z))\!::\!\sin(\alpha\tilde{\phi}(w))\!:\rangle.

Some comments about this result are due. For
\alpha=1/\sqrt{2}
, the statements directly correspond to the known monotonicity of the Ising two-point function: it is decreasing (resp. increasing) under
+
(resp. free) boundary conditions. In the continuum, however, this statement is purely a statement about the Green’s function. Namely, the two-point function of the cosine (resp. sine) consists of a decreasing term, coming from the conformal radii, times an increasing term, given by
\cosh(\alpha^{2}G(z,w))
(resp.
\sinh(\alpha^{2}G(z,w))
). Thus the monotonicity is determined by whether the increasing term wins over the decreasing term, and the subtlety of the statement becomes apparent: for
x,y\in D
close to each other,
\cosh(\alpha^{2}G(z,w))\sim\sinh(\alpha^{2}G(z,w))
. Finally, we highlight the (rather surprising) range of values of
\alpha
for which the statement holds, needing to restrict to
\alpha\in(0,1]
for

to be true.

The following general result concerning variations of the Green’s function is the central piece in the proof of Theorem

Let

(4.4)

\partial_{t}f_{t}(z)=-zf^{\prime}_{t}(z)\frac{\zeta_{t}+z}{\zeta_{t}-z},\quad f_{0}(z)=z.

Theorem 4.4

(Hadamard’s Variational Formula –

Consider the radial Loewner equation driven by

Inverting the setup of the previous discussion is also standard, and will be very useful in the proof of Theorem

4.2.
Boundary integrability

The main goal for this section is to identify how small a local set must be to ensure it is thin for the imaginary chaos. Our approach towards thinness statements will be to consider the limits of dyadic approximations for the relevant quantities. This approach matches the one in e.g.
[
57
,
61
]
.

In what follows, for any given closed set
A\subset\mathbb{D}
, we use
[A]_{n}
to denote the union of all dyadic squares of level
n
that intersect
A
. A nice upshot of such approximations is that, as they can take only finitely many values, we can define the random variables
(\phi,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})
or
(\phi,f\mathbf{1}_{D\setminus[A]_{n}})
. Indeed, one can simultaneously define
(\phi,f\mathbf{1}_{a})
for any possible value

of
[A]_{n}
and see that

(4.24)

(\phi,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})=\sum_{a}(\phi,f\mathbf{1}_{a})\mathbf{1}_{\{[A]_{n}=a\}}\quad\text{a.s.}

This is especially useful for
random
closed sets, possibly with Lebesgue measure zero. While this choice of approximation scheme is very practical (especially to derive thinness criteria in terms of the size of
A
), it is somewhat arbitrary. We refer to
[
57
, Section 5]
for further discussion on discrete approximation schemes of the same flavour. For our dyadic approximation scheme, as in
[
57
]
, we consider the following definition.

Definition 4.5
.

A set
A
is a thin for the field
:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:
if for any continuous, bounded function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})=0,

where the limit is in probability. The same definition applies to the fields
:\!\cos(\alpha\phi)\!:
and
:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:
.

The first step to identify thin (two-valued) local sets for
:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:
is to study the integrability of the field along a deterministic fractal boundary. This is why, as already discussed in Section
2
, it is crucial for us to be able to test our fields against functions that intersect the boundary of the domain. It will also be necessary when proving convergence of the discrete XOR-Ising decomposition in Section
9
. We start with a key consequence of the monotonicity in Theorem

Proposition 4.6

(Boundary Mass – Sine)
.

Let

be a bounded, simply connected domain. Fix any

to be a (deterministic) closed subset of the boundary such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(:\!\overline{\mathrm{cos}}(\alpha\phi+u)\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})=0,

where the limit is, again, almost sure and in

Proof.

The first claim follows by Theorem

\displaystyle\int_{[A]_{n}}\int_{[A]_{n}}\langle:\!\sin(\alpha\phi(z))\!::\!\sin(\alpha\phi(w))\!:\rangle dzdw

Given the result above, one immediately identifies the expectation of

as the

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Let

be a bounded, simply connected domain. Fix any

to be a (deterministic) closed subset of the boundary such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})]=0.

\lim_{n\to\infty}(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})=0,

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})]=\int_{[A]_{n}}\mathrm{CR}(z,D)^{-\alpha^{2}/2}f(z)dz\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\int_{[A]_{n}}d(z,\partial D)^{-\alpha^{2}/2}dz.

Our assumption on the dimension of

(4.33)

\displaystyle\int_{[A]_{n}}d(z,\partial D)^{-\alpha^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}^{-\alpha^{2}/2}\varepsilon_{k}^{2-\dim_{M}(A)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon_{n}^{2-\alpha^{2}/2-\dim_{M}(A)}\longrightarrow 0.

To prove (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(:\!\overline{\mathrm{cos}}(\alpha\phi)\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(:\!\sin(\alpha\phi)\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(:\!e^{i\alpha\phi}\!:,f\mathbf{1}_{[A]_{n}})]^{2},

6.1.
Local finiteness of the decomposition

Before proving the main theorems, we handle one leftover result we need in the proofs. Namely, we must show that the iterative steps described in Section
3
define a locally finite collection of loops. Note that, while each two-valued set is locally finite,
a priori
the successive sampling of them need not be.

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider the iteration of two-valued sets given in Section
3
. For any
\rho>0
, the number of loops with diameter greater than
\rho
is finite almost surely. In particular, the iteration stops after an almost surely finite number
N\equiv N(\rho)
of steps.

The key observation to prove this result is that in each iterative sampling of
\mathds{A}_{-2\lambda,2\lambda}=\mathrm{CLE}_{4}
there is a positive chance that all loops are microscopic, i.e. have diameter smaller than
\rho
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\rho>0
there exists
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{D}\mathbb{P}(\text{every loop of }\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(D)\text{ has diameter less than or equal to }\rho)\geq c,

where the infimum is taken over all open sets
D\subseteq\mathbb{D}
such that
D
is a countable union of pairwise disjoint simply connected domains.

To prove this statement, we assume the reader is familiar with the loop soup construction of CLE
4
in
[
58
]
. In fact, we only need these four properties:

\bullet

Monotonicity:
If
D\subseteq D^{\prime}
are two domains as in Lemma
6.2
, then CLE
{}_{4}(D^{\prime})
and CLE
{}_{4}(D)
can be coupled such that every loop of CLE
{}_{4}(D)
is surrounded by a loop of CLE
{}_{4}(D^{\prime})
.

\bullet

Restriction property:
Let
D\subseteq D^{\prime}
. Define
D^{*}
to be the (random) domain obtained by removing from
D
all the loops of CLE
{}_{4}(D^{\prime})
not contained in
D
together with their interiors. Then, conditionally on
D^{*}
, the law of all the loops of CLE
{}_{4}(D^{\prime})
contained
D
is that of CLE
{}_{4}(D^{*})
. See Figure
5
.

\bullet

No loop hits the boundary:
No loop of CLE
{}_{4}(D)
intersects
\partial D
.

\bullet

Scale invariance:
For any
\lambda>0
and
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, the law of CLE
{}_{4}(\lambda D)
is that of
\lambda
CLE
{}_{4}(D)
.

We highlight that the uniformity in the domain claimed in Lemma
6.2
is obtained as a direct consequence of the monotonicity property. This uniformity is crucial in the proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

By monotonicity, it suffices to show that for any
\rho>0
there exists
c>0
such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{every loop of }\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})\text{ has diameter less than or equal to }\rho)\geq c.

Define

\displaystyle B:=\{\rho>0:\mathbb{P}(\text{every loop of }\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})\text{ has diameter less than or equal to }\rho)=0\}.

We want to show that
B=\emptyset
. Note that
B
must be an interval, as if
\rho\in B
then
\rho^{\prime}\in B
for all
\rho^{\prime}<\rho
. Since we know that

, it is enough to show that

is both open and closed in
(0,1]
.

We first prove that it is closed. Since CLE
{}_{4}(\mathbb{D})
is locally finite, if
\rho_{n}\nearrow\rho
then, up to an event of zero probability,

\displaystyle\{\text{every loop of }\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})\text{ has diameter less than or equal to }\rho_{n}\}

\displaystyle\hskip 68.99895pt\nearrow\{\text{every loop of }\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})\text{ has diameter less than or equal to }\rho\},

which yields the claim by the monotone convergence theorem.

To prove that

is open, assume that
\rho\in B
. We need to find

such that
\rho+r\in B
.
By local finiteness and the fact that no loop of
\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})

intersects the boundary, there exists

\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(\mathbb{D})

. See Figure

We now use the restriction property with

and

.
The fact that

implies that, with probability one,
\mathrm{CLE}_{4}(D^{*})
has a loop
of diameter greater than or equal to

∎

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r(X)s(Y_{n})\mid A]\ \mathbb{E}[t(Z_{n})\mid A]\ \mathbb{E}[u(W_{n_{0}})\mid A]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r(X)s(Y_{n})t(Z_{n})\mid A]\ \mathbb{E}[u(W_{n_{0}})\mid A].

Since

(7.13)

W_{n_{0}}\longrightarrow\phi^{\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{i}}\equiv(\phi^{\mathcal{O}_{1}},\ldots,\phi^{\mathcal{O}_{N}})

almost surely as

, and all functionals are bounded, the claimed conditional independence follows.

8.2.

To introduce the discrete excursion decomposition of the XOR-Ising model, we need to introduce the double random current (DRC) model. A current

9.

The goal of this final section is to prove the convergence of the discrete excursion decomposition of the XOR-Ising model. We recall that, as discussed in Section

and, up to taking a subsequence, add the joint convergence of both XOR-Ising models. The same local set argument as in Theorem
9.5
characterizes any such subsequential limit uniquely.

It remains to prove (
1.12
). Let
\rho^{\varepsilon}
be a standard
\varepsilon
-mollifier given by

(9.53)

\rho^{\varepsilon}(w):=\varepsilon^{-2}\rho(w/\varepsilon),

for a non-negative, radially symmetric function
\rho
with unit mass. We define

(9.54)

\sigma^{\varepsilon}(z):=(\sigma,\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-\cdot)),

and note this is a smooth function.

To see that
:\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:
is well-defined, one may check via a direct computation that

(9.55)

\mathbb{E}[((\sigma^{\varepsilon}\tilde{\sigma}^{\varepsilon},f)-(\sigma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\tilde{\sigma}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}))^{2}]\longrightarrow 0

as
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
, for any continuous, bounded
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
in a valid domain. Moreover, one can similarly see that all moments of
:\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:
match those of the XOR-Ising, e.g.

(9.56)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\sigma_{\varepsilon}\tilde{\sigma}_{\varepsilon},f)]

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}[(\sigma,\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-\cdot))]\ \mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\sigma},\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-\cdot))]f(z)dz

(9.57)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\left(\int_{D}\mathrm{CR}(x,D)^{-1/8}\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-x)dx\right)\left(\int_{D}\mathrm{CR}(y,D)^{-1/8}\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-y)dy\right)f(z)dz

(9.58)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathrm{CR}(x,D)^{-1/8}\left(\int_{D}\mathrm{CR}(y,D)^{-1/8}\left(\int_{D}\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-x)\rho^{\varepsilon}(z-y)f(z)dz\right)dy\right)dx

(9.59)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\int_{D}\mathrm{CR}(x,D)^{-1/4}f(x)dx

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that, crucially, the field
:\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:
is measurable with respect to the pair
(\sigma,\tilde{\sigma})
.

Now, it suffices to show that

(9.60)

(\sigma_{\delta},\ \tilde{\sigma}_{\delta},\ \tau_{\delta})\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}(\sigma,\ \tilde{\sigma},\ :\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:)

as
\delta\to 0
. Indeed, we already know that

(9.61)

\big(h_{\delta},\ \sigma_{\delta},\ \tilde{\sigma}_{\delta},\ \tau_{\delta},\ \sigma_{\delta}^{\dagger},\ \tilde{\sigma}_{\delta}^{\dagger},\ \tau_{\delta}^{\dagger}\big)

converges in law as
\delta\to 0
to

(9.62)

\big(1/(\pi\sqrt{2})h,\ \sigma,\ \tilde{\sigma},\ :\!\cos((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:,\ \sigma^{\dagger},\ \tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger},\ :\!\sin((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:\big).

But then by (
9.60
), and its free boundary condition counterpart, we know that the marginal laws in the coupling (
9.62
) are such that

(9.63)

:\!\cos((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:\ \overset{(d)}{=}\ :\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:\ ,\quad\ :\!\sin((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:\ \overset{(d)}{=}\ :\!\sigma^{\dagger}\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}\!:\ .

But, in particular, it follows that

(9.64)

:\!\cos((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:\ ,\quad:\!\sin((1/\sqrt{2})h)\!:

are measurable functions of the pairs
(\sigma,\tilde{\sigma})
and
(\sigma^{\dagger},\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger})
, respectively, and a.s. equality must hold.

The proof of (
9.60
) relies on a simple (especially using the independence of the two Ising models) joint moment computation. Firstly, by expanding into the integral forms, we see that

(9.65)

\mathbb{E}_{\delta}[(\sigma_{\delta},f)^{n}(\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta},f)^{m}(\tau_{\delta},f)^{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{\delta}[(\sigma_{\delta},f)^{n+k}]\ \mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\sigma}_{\delta},f)^{m+k}].

On the other hand, since
(\sigma^{\varepsilon}\tilde{\sigma}^{\varepsilon},f)
has uniformly bounded moments of all orders, direct computations similar to that in (
9.59
) show that

(9.66)

\mathbb{E}[(\sigma,f)^{n}(\sigma,f)^{m}(:\!\sigma\tilde{\sigma}\!:,f)^{k}]=\mathbb{E}[(\sigma,f)^{n+k}]\ \mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\sigma},f)^{m+k}].

The claim now follows from the convergence of Ising correlation functions
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

Remark 9.7
.

As already explained in the introduction, the identification (
1.12
) combined with
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
implies the measurability of the limit of the height function with respect to the four Ising magnetisation fields. Interestingly enough, both in the discrete and the continuum, given the XOR-Ising fields one only recovers the
gradient
of the height function (recall (
8.15
)), and then imposes boundary conditions to fix the additive constant. In
[
4
]
, it is discussed whether the GFF should be measurable with respect to
only
its sine or
only
its cosine. The correspondence with discrete models suggests that the answer should be negative, and perhaps one can even use the continuum excursion decomposition to prove this claim.
