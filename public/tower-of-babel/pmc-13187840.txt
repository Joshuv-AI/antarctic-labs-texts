Outcomes and Treatment-limiting Adverse Events of Patients Receiving BPaL and BPaL-FQ in California
Authors: Shereen S Katrak, Rebecca C Wang, Chris E Keh, Scott A Nabity, Reiko C Okada, Ann Raftery, Kristen A Wendorf, Rocio Agraz-Lara, Melony Chakrabarty, Jennifer R Chevinsky, Amit S Chitnis, Shom Dasgupta-Tsinikas, Lisa Goozé, Ankita Kadakia, Cameron Kaiser, Janice K Louie, Julie Morgan, Tuan Dung Nguyen, Jeffrey Percak, Andrea H Polesky, Allen Radner, Harleen Sahni, Meera Sreenivasan, Rachel Sherrick, Jeanne D Soukup, Che Waterman, Preethi Yeturu, Salina Yu, Pennan M Barry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe regimen bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid (BPaL), with or without moxifloxacin, is recommended for treating multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR TB). We describe real-world outcomes and tolerability of BPaL, with or without fluroquinolone (BPaL-FQ) in the U.S.MethodsUsing data collected by the California MDR TB Service for patients receiving BPaL and BPaL-FQ during 2020–2024, we analyzed favorable outcomes (cure or treatment completion), relapse, and adverse event frequency and severity.ResultsNinety-seven patients were started on any pretomanid-containing regimen during 2020–2024; 76 (78%) of these had pulmonary TB, 21 (22%) had concurrent or exclusive extrapulmonary disease. Sixty-nine patients received at least 4 weeks of BPaL or BPaL-FQ and had final outcome documented. Favorable treatment outcomes were reported in 64 (93%) patients overall and in more than 80% of patients in almost all subgroups, regardless of underlying resistance pattern, site of disease, or comorbidities. There were no microbiologically confirmed TB relapses. Two deaths occurred in patients receiving BPaL-FQ. Adverse events that led to a drug change occurred in 40% of patients and included peripheral neuropathy, cytopenias, gastrointestinal upset, and headache or dizziness. The majority were attributed to linezolid 600 mg daily and associated with linezolid trough above 2 µg/mL; fluroquinolones were also frequently implicated.DiscussionIn a cohort of patients receiving BPaL or BPaL-FQ in California during 2020–2024, a majority had a favorable outcome. More than one third of patients required drug dosage change or discontinuation to complete therapy. Linezolid was the main driver of poor tolerability and regimen changes.Keywords: drug safety and tolerability, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, TB outcomes, TB treatment

BACKGROUND

In August 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration approved the drug pretomanid (Pa) in combination with bedaquiline (BDQ) and linezolid (LZD) for use in treating adults with pulmonary extensively drug resistant (XDR) tuberculosis (TB), and treatment-intolerant or nonresponsive multidrug-resistant (MDR) TB. Subsequent clinical trial data [1, 2] and guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Infectious Diseases Society of America, and World Health Organization (WHO) established the 6- to-9-month regimen of bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid (BPaL), with option of including moxifloxacin (BPaL-M), as first-line for treatment of MDR TB [3–5]. Early experience proved feasibility of using the BPaL regimen in the United States; however, little has been published about patterns of use, tolerability, real-world outcomes, and adverse events of patients on BPaL and BPaL-M in the United States [6]. Additionally US medical providers may choose to use levofloxacin along with BPaL in the United States rather than moxifloxacin, due to different side effect profiles of fluroquinolones and data suggesting moxifloxacin is more strongly associated with cardiac arrhythmias [7–9]. However, [1, 2], early case series data have generally included a larger proportion of patients on BPaL rather than BPaL plus a fluoroquinolone (BPaL-FQ).

California is a state with higher TB incidence than most of the United States. Of the more than 2000 persons diagnosed with TB disease in the state annually, 1%–2% have MDR TB. The California Department of Public Health routinely provides clinical and public health consultation for patients with MDR TB through the California MDR TB Service and collects data on patients with MDR TB for both consultation and surveillance purposes [10]. The California MDR TB Service has been recommending the BPaL and BPaL-FQ regimens since 2020, whereas also working with providers who choose individualized therapy for their MDR TB patients based on molecular and phenotypic drug susceptibility results. With this analysis, we aim to describe the patterns of use of Pa-containing regimens, as well as real-world outcomes and frequency and severity of adverse events (AEs) for patients on BPaL and BPaL-FQ. We also aim to describe AEs observed with Pa-containing regimens that do not meet criteria for BPaL or BPaL-FQ but are sometimes used in individualized TB therapy.

METHODS

During 2020–2024, the California MDR TB Service collected surveillance and clinical information for Pa-based regimens used throughout the state; this included all patients receiving BPaL or BPaL-FQ regimens as well as patients receiving individualized regimens. Patients were evaluated and treated by local physicians with input from the California MDR TB Service, usually via monthly conference calls. All patients who received at least 1 dose of Pa from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2023 were included in our first analysis, which described use of all Pa-containing regimens in California. Descriptive characteristics of interest for this analysis included type of regimen, duration of TB treatment, age and sex of patient, site of TB disease, drug-resistance pattern, BDQ susceptibility, patient history of prior TB treatment, immune suppression, comorbidity, and time to BDQ start after identifying indication for using BDQ. BPaL and BPaL-FQ regimens were defined as treatment that included simultaneous bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid, with or without an FQ but with no other TB drugs. Patients who received Pa, but also received additional drugs beyond BDQ, LZD, or FQ, were categorized as “Pa-other” regimens. MDR TB was defined as TB with resistance to isoniazid and rifampin, XDR TB was defined using the 2021 WHO definition of MDR TB with resistance to FQ and at least 1 second-line injectable drug, and BDQ-resistant TB was defined as TB with presence of a high-confidence BDQ resistance mutation as defined by WHO [11], or documented phenotypic BDQ resistance. Time to BDQ start was defined as time from indication for BDQ start (either date of diagnosis of rifampin-resistant TB or date of treatment-limiting drug intolerance) to the date that patient began taking BDQ. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for LZD, including trough and 2-hour peak levels, was recommended by the California MDR TB Service after at least 5–7 days of treatment and performed at 1 of 2 laboratories: the University of Florida Infectious Disease Pharmacokinetics Laboratory (Gainesville, FL, USA) or the National Jewish Health Advanced Diagnostic Laboratories (Denver, CO, USA). Real-world timing and ability to obtain TDM varied.

For the outcomes analysis, patients receiving a minimum of 4 weeks of BPaL or BPaL-FQ regimens who had a final outcome documented by 30 September 2024 were included. Outcomes of interest for this analysis included the proportion of patients completing TB treatment with favorable outcomes (cure or treatment completion), the proportion of patients who culture-converted within 8 weeks of treatment start, and the proportion of patients with cure or treatment completion who experienced microbiologically confirmed TB relapse within 12 months of treatment completion. Outcomes were defined using the 2021 WHO TB treatment outcome definitions [12], with minor modifications to reflect US guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). Definitions for composition, dosage, and duration of TB treatment were based on the 2022 WHO Operational Handbook on Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis [3]; however, levofloxacin at a dosage of 750 mg daily was an acceptable substitute for moxifloxacin, at the discretion of the treating provider. Deaths occurring during TB treatment were reviewed by the treating physicians and at least 1 physician from the California MDR TB Service and adjudicated as TB-related, not TB-related, or unlikely TB-related, or unknown.

The AE analysis included patients receiving BPaL, BPaL-FQ, or “Pa-other” individualized regimens using clinical data charted by their local treating physicians, who generally evaluated patients monthly. Patients who did not have clinical exam, laboratory, electrocardiogram, and imaging results charted monthly throughout therapy and at least once at the end of therapy were excluded from AE analysis. Outcomes of interest for this analysis included the frequency, timing and severity of adverse events, frequency of adverse events resulting in drug dosage change or discontinuation, and frequency of AEs present at end of TB therapy. Patients were assessed for AEs after their first dose of pretomanid. Local treating providers were encouraged to report any new or worsening AE that occurred during treatment, irrespective of suspected relationship to TB medications, to the California MDR TB Service. Adverse event data were reviewed by at least 1 nontreating physician and were entered into a REDCap database (Supplementary Table 2) by the California MDR TB Service. Laboratory-defined AEs were graded in accordance with the 2017 National Institutes of Health Division of AIDS (V2.1) [13]. Clinically defined AEs were not graded because there was not sufficient information available to investigators for all patients. Drug-specific attributions were determined by consensus opinion between the treating physician and California MDR TB Service physician upon review of the records.

All data were tabulated in Microsoft Excel; Excel was used to calculate percentages of the assessable patients with favorable outcome or AE, and to create graphics depicting types of AE that caused drug change or discontinuation. No formal statistical tests were performed. Analysis of routinely collected MDR TB Service data was reviewed and determined by California Health and Human Service Agency's Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects to be nonresearch. This activity was also reviewed by the CDC, deemed not research, and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (45 C.F.R. part 46.102(l)(2), 21 C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. §241(d); 5 U.S.C. §552a; 44 U.S.C. §3501 et seq).

RESULTS

In total, 97 patients reported to the California MDR TB Service were started on a TB regimen containing Pa from 2020 to 2024 (Figure 1). Of these, 94 patients received at least 4 weeks of Pa. A total of 71 patients received at least 4 weeks of BPaL or BPaL-FQ without other drugs; of these, 69 (97%) remained in California for the entirety of their TB care, had a final outcome reported by 30 September 2024 and were included in the TB outcomes analysis. Three patients died within 1 week of starting Pa-containing therapy; these patients were excluded from final outcomes analysis but included in descriptive analysis of deaths. A total of 62 patients (on regimens that included BPaL, BPaL-FQ, or Pa-other) had sufficient clinical data for AE analysis.

Characteristics of patients receiving Pa-containing regimens (including BPaL, BPaL-FQ, and Pa-other) are presented in Table 1. The age of patients treated with Pa-containing regimens ranged from 18 to 85 years, with a mean age of 49 years. Of the 97 patients who started on Pa-containing regimens, 22 (23%) received BPaL alone, 49 (51%) received BPaL-FQ, and 23 (23%) received “Pa-other” (Pa plus additional drugs beyond BDQ, LZD, or FQ); the remaining 3 patients (3%) died within a week of starting a Pa-containing regimen (2 of these were receiving Pa-other, 1 BPaL). The majority of patients started on Pa-containing regimens had MDR TB, either fluroquinolone-sensitive (n = 57, 57%) or fluroquinolone-resistant (n = 10, 10%), but a smaller proportion of patients had rifampin-sensitive (n = 16, 16%), rifampin-resistant without isoniazid resistance (n = 9, 9%), or XDR TB (n = 5, 5%). Drug susceptibility testing for BDQ, either phenotypic or molecular, was performed and result was available for 68 of 97 patients. No patients in the analysis cohort had a high-confidence BDQ mutation or phenotypic BDQ resistance. Two patients had BDQ mutations of low or uncertain significance, but both had isolates with documented phenotypic susceptibility to BDQ. Most patients received TB treatment before their final Pa-containing regimen, either rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol (HRZE) (n = 29, 30%), a bridging regimen with an injectable aminoglycoside (n = 24, 25%), or a bridging regimen without an injectable aminoglycoside (n = 27, 28%). Additional details about prior TB treatment are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

A total of 76 patients (78%) had pulmonary disease only; smaller proportions had concurrent pulmonary and extrapulmonary disease (n = 15, 16%) or extrapulmonary disease alone (n = 6, 6%). No patients had TB disease of the central nervous system. There were 7 patients with bony disease, 6 with spinal disease, and 1 with disease in the femur. A total of 10 patients had chronic liver disease, whereas fewer than 10 each had chronic kidney disease (n = 6), cardiac disease (n = 7), HIV (n = 5), history of solid organ transplant (n = 3), or other immune suppression (n = 3). A total of 51 patients (53%) waited 2 weeks or more for BDQ procurement after an indication for BDQ was identified; 22 of these waited 4 weeks or longer.

There were 5 deaths among patients receiving pretomanid-containing regimens (Supplementary Table 4). Three deaths occurred in patients who received less than 1 week of a Pa-containing TB regimen (2 received Pa-other, 1 received BPaL). The remaining 2 deaths occurred after 14 and 15 weeks of BPaL-FQ, respectively: 1 occurred in a person with HIV, disseminated TB including spinal disease, and concurrent Coccidioides meningitis and was adjudicated as not TB-related; the other death occurred overseas after a gastrointestinal bleed and was adjudicated as unlikely to be TB-related.

In total, 71 patients received at least 4 weeks of either BPaL or BPaL-FQ, without use of other drugs; 69 of these (97%) had a final outcome documented in California and 56 had sufficient data to document date of sputum culture-conversion (Table 2). For patients receiving BPaL, 19/21 (90%) had a favorable treatment outcome, with 18/18 (100%) of these culture-converting within 8 weeks. For patients receiving BPaL-FQ, 45/48 (94%) had a favorable treatment outcome, with 36/38 (94%) culture-converting within 8 weeks; for 2 patients who did not culture-convert within 8 weeks, 1 was persistently culture-positive at 8 weeks, whereas the other had insufficient sputa collected to document the date of culture-conversion. Favorable treatment outcomes were reported in more than 80% of patients in almost all subgroups of BPaL and BPaL-FQ users, regardless of site of disease, drug-resistance pattern, documented BDQ susceptibility, or presence of comorbidity. Favorable treatment outcomes were reported in more than 90% of patients who were able to complete at least 26 weeks of BPaL or BPaL-FQ therapy. Unfavorable outcomes occurred in a total of 5 patients: 3 experienced treatment failure because of adverse events (2 on BPaL, 1 on BPaL-FQ) and were able to complete TB therapy on alternate regimens, and 2 patients died after receiving BPaL-FQ for less than 26 weeks (Supplementary Table 4). There were no microbiologically confirmed TB relapses in any group.

A summary of adverse events experienced by patients on BPaL, BPaL-FQ, and Pa-other regimens is presented in Table 3. Of the 97 total persons receiving a Pa-containing regimen during the study period, 62 (64%) had sufficient clinical data for adverse event analysis. Adverse events during therapy were common, occurring in 49 (79%) of patients overall, including 13 (65%) of persons receiving BPaL, 27 (82%) of persons receiving BPaL-FQ, and 9 (100%) persons receiving Pa-other regimens, respectively. The most frequently reported AE was aggregate gastrointestinal (GI) complaints, including nausea, vomiting, dysgeusia, and anorexia, reported in 32% of patients. Elevated alanine aminotransferase and “other AEs” (a category that included a wide variety of organ systems and complaints, including tremor, generalized weakness or fatigue, insomnia, black hairy tongue, temporary decreased visual acuity without documented optic neuropathy, and multiple laboratory abnormalities) were also frequently reported across all groups. The most reported AE in the BPaL group was GI complaints in 35% of patients. In the BPaL-FQ group, anemia occurred in 33% of patients and GI AEs (nausea, vomiting, dysgeusia, anorexia) occurred in 30%. Few patients had AEs that persisted at end of treatment. The median time from regimen start to first report of AE was 2 months (Supplementary Table 5). However, in a few patients, AEs were first reported after 6–8 months of therapy.

Across all regimen types, the most common AEs that resulted in a drug dosage change or discontinuation were peripheral neuropathy and cytopenias, followed by GI (nausea, vomiting, anorexia, dysgeusia), and headache, dizziness, and brain fog (Figure 2A and 2B). QTc prolongation resulted in treatment change for one patient receiving BPaL-FQ and one receiving an expanded “Pa-other” regimen. Other causes of drug change included myalgia or arthralgia, transaminitis, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS, also known as drug-induced hypersensitivity syndrome), tremor, tendinopathy, and black hairy tongue. The majority of treatment-limiting AEs were attributed to linezolid, followed by moxifloxacin or levofloxacin. Bedaquiline, pretomanid, and isoniazid were each implicated once as causing AE-associated drug changes.

Finally, patient-level drug regimens, results from TDM, AEs associated with drug changes, and resulting drug changes are presented in Table 4. AE-driven dosage adjustments and discontinuations occurred in a total of 25 patients (40%) overall. Although common in all regimen types, the proportion of AEs leading to a drug change was highest in the Pa-other group (56%), compared to the BPaL (35%) and BPaL-FQ (39%) groups. Across all groups, a total of 17/25 (68%) patients with AE-driven drug changes had AEs attributed to linezolid at a dosage of 600 mg daily. Most of these were associated with a linezolid trough of >2 µg/mL and led to a linezolid dosage adjustment (12/17 instances), rather than drug discontinuation. In contrast, AEs associated with fluoroquinolones more often led to drug discontinuation (7/8 instances); in only 1 of these instances was an alternate fluroquinolone successfully substituted into the TB regimen. The BPaL group included 2 patients who experienced treatment failure due to AEs, both after receiving approximately 5 weeks of BPaL: 1 patient, who had experienced DRESS with initial therapy of rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, experienced recurrent DRESS on BPaL, and 1 patient experienced transaminitis that resolved with discontinuation of Pa. Patients with documented comorbidities had a higher frequency of AE-driven drug changes, compared to patients without documented comorbidities (47% vs 38%, Supplementary Table 6).

DISCUSSION

In a cohort of patients primarily with MDR TB receiving Pa-containing regimens in California during 2020–2024, a majority of patients on BPaL or BPaL-FQ culture-converted within 8 weeks and had a favorable treatment outcome, regardless of underlying drug resistance pattern, site of disease, or comorbidities. One in 4 patients on a Pa-containing regimen transitioned off an alternate regimen that included an injectable aminoglycoside, thus avoiding potential toxicities associated with this drug class. However, adverse events were common and included GI complaints, elevated alanine aminotransferase, and a wide variety of other symptoms and laboratory abnormalities. More than one third of patients experienced an adverse event that caused a drug dosage adjustment or discontinuation, largely peripheral neuropathy and cytopenias, a majority of which were attributed to linezolid or fluroquinolones. Despite frequent drug dosage changes or drug discontinuations of linezolid or fluroquinolones, there were no instances of microbiologically confirmed TB relapse in patients completing BPaL or BPaL-FQ therapy.

Our cohort reflected US practice patterns and TB demographics and included patients who were on average older and had more comorbidities than patients in BPaL and BPaL-M clinical trials. Roughly 1 in 4 patients had a documented chronic disease involving the heart, liver, or kidneys, reflecting the reality that in the United States, treating TB often involves comanagement of chronic illnesses and consideration of drug–drug interactions. In our cohort, most patients were being treated for MDR TB or XDR TB. However, 15% were receiving therapy for rifampin-sensitive TB, most of these with previous drug intolerances to HRZE, the standard regimen for drug-sensitive TB. Adverse events related to HRZE are common, and drug intolerances are associated with increasing age [14, 15]. Better-tolerated regimens are needed for drug-sensitive TB; for now, BPaL-FQ is an important option for patients who are truly intolerant. A notable detail from our analysis, however, is that the single patient who was switched to BPaL after experiencing DRESS/DIHS on HRZE experienced recurrent DRESS/DIHS on BPaL. Further work is needed to clarify the optimal management of DRESS/DIHS in patients with TB, taking into account the potential for multiple drug hypersensitivity syndrome, the long half-life of BDQ, and the need to protect patients from acquired drug resistance.

BDQ has advanced the treatment of MDR TB; unfortunately, almost one quarter of patients in our analysis waited 4 weeks or longer to start BDQ after identification of drug resistance or treatment intolerance. In the United States, BDQ cannot be ordered through commercial pharmacies and is obtained through a sole source distributor. Furthermore, BDQ currently costs more than $20,000 for a 6-month course. Applying for prior authorization or to a patient assistance program for BDQ may cause delays in acquisition of this critical medication, without which administration of Pa is considered off-label.

The majority of patients in our analysis who received at least 26 weeks of BPaL or BPaL-FQ had a favorable outcome even in the setting of extrapulmonary disease, chronic disease, or immune suppression. This is consistent with emerging data from other low TB-incidence settings [16]. Although patients with TB osteomyelitis and arthritis were excluded from efficacy trials of BPaL and BPaL-M [2, 4], our analysis contributes to the body of evidence in the United States describing favorable treatment outcomes in patients with extrapulmonary disease [6], including 6 patients with bony disease who were successfully treated with BPaL-FQ or “Pa-other” regimens (that included BDQ, Pa, LZD, FQ, plus additional drugs) for durations of 32–52 weeks. Additional work is needed to provide guidance for the treatment of osteoarticular forms of drug-resistant TB, including clarifying pharmacokinetics and bone penetration of bedaquiline and pretomanid, and elucidating the optimal duration of treatment [17–20].

Although the majority of patients in our cohort on BPaL or BPaL-FQ experienced favorable outcomes with no instances of culture-confirmed TB relapse regardless of whether BDQ susceptibility testing was performed, our analysis occurs in the context of the first reported U.S. patients with BDQ-resistant TB [21]. TB relapse and acquired drug resistance after BPaL regimens has been reported in patients with cavitary TB disease, baseline FQ resistance, slow culture-conversion, TB treatment interruption, and comorbidities such as diabetes [21–23]. Few public health laboratories in the United States currently perform drug susceptibility testing for BDQ and Pa. Whole-genome and targeted next-generation sequencing for drug resistance are available through national and state reference laboratories, but turnaround times of several weeks are common. The California MDR TB Service continues to emphasize the importance of documenting BDQ susceptibility when using BDQ-based regimens and is working with state and national partners to make standardized testing for BDQ susceptibility more easily available.

Most patients in this cohort experienced at least one adverse event during therapy. Reassuringly, most were able to complete TB therapy and few had adverse events that persisted at end of treatment. Our results are consistent with descriptive analyses from low-incidence settings [16, 24, 25], which suggests that clinically significant QTc prolongation or laboratory abnormalities are rare, and most patients are able to complete BPaL and BPaL-FQ regimens safely. Of note, a greater proportion of our patients receiving individualized Pa-containing regimens experienced AEs, including treatment-limiting AEs, compared to those receiving BPaL or BPaL-FQ alone. Additionally, within the group of patients receiving BPaL-FQ who experienced treatment-limiting adverse events, more than half had adverse events that were attributed to fluroquinolones. This is notable as we expand our use of Pa-containing regimens in the setting of BDQ-resistant TB, and important for clinicians and programs that routinely use “extra” TB drugs outside of the BPaL or BPaL-FQ regimens. Adverse events related to fluroquinolones are well-described, particularly in the elderly [26, 27], and additional drug such as cycloserine are also associated with serious AEs [28, 29]. Our data suggest that additional drugs in an expanded regimen may lead to overall reduction in tolerability; each component drug in a regimen needs to be carefully considered.

In our patients, most AEs that resulted in drug dosages adjustments or discontinuation were neuropathy or cytopenias attributed to LZD. All of these occurred in patients with a starting dosage of LZD 600 mg daily and most had a LZD trough >2 µg/mL. The optimal dosage for LZD as part of the BPaL and BPaL-FQ regimens is evolving. Initial efficacy trials using 1200 mg daily [2], which resulted in high rates of peripheral neuropathy and cytopenias; the current recommended dose is 600 mg daily [5]. Clinical trial follow-up supports the possibility of further LZD dose reduction [30]. Therapeutic drug monitoring with a goal trough of below 2 µg/mL has been proposed as a means to reduce LZD toxicity [31], and it is notable that in our patients with LZD-associated AEs in whom TDM was performed, 12 of 14 had trough levels >2. Our results suggest TDM is an important component of long-term LZD use and a tool to improve tolerability of LZD as part of BPaL and BPaL-FQ regimens. It also supports the need for ongoing dose-finding studies for both linezolid and new oxazolidinone drugs [32, 33].

Our study was limited by being a retrospective observational study; although the California MDR TB Service recommends a standardized program of examination, laboratory, and electrocardiogram monitoring for AE, we did not directly evaluate patients and were reliant on treating providers to perform evaluation and report data. Attribution of relatedness of AEs to a particular drug may have introduced error. In our analysis of outcomes for particular sites of disease or drug-resistance patterns, we were limited by small numbers, and lack of formal statistical testing means we could not make any inferences about significance. Finally, because BDQ has a very long half-life, AEs occurring beyond treatment completion may not have been captured.

The landscape of MDR TB treatment has changed dramatically in the past decade, shifting away from the 18- to 24-month individualized regimens [34] and toward 6- to 9-month oral regimens including BPaL and BPaL-M [5]. In a cohort of patients receiving BPaL or BPaL-FQ in California from 2020–2024, most with MDR TB, a majority had a favorable outcome with no instances of TB relapse. Adverse events were common, but with careful clinical monitoring and drug changes, most patients successfully completed TB therapy. LZD at a dosage of 600 mg daily was the main driver of poor tolerability and regimen change.
