Agitation in dementia: Evidence-based consensus recommendations
Authors: Ying-Yu Chen, Chien-Shu Tai, Hsiu-Han Wen, Hsin-Jou Yi, Yu-Chen Chuang, Ting-Wei Hsu, Wen-Ting Lee, Ssu-Chieh Huang, Chieh-Ning Huang, Jou-Yen Wu, Jong-Ling Fuh, Mu-N Liu, Ming-Hsien Lin, Liang-Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgitation is among the most disabling behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, contributing substantially to caregiver burden, institutionalization, and healthcare utilization. Considering the modest efficacy and significant adverse effects associated with psychotropic medications, person-centered approach (PCA) and non-pharmacological interventions are recommended as first-line management strategies. We conducted a comprehensive search on PubMed, Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library for systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials evaluating PCA and non-pharmacological interventions for agitation in dementia published before September 30, 2025. The quality of evidence and strength of recommendations were evaluated using the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Moderate-quality evidence supports the use of massage and touch therapy, particularly individualized music therapy, tailored activity programs, and multicomponent interventions. By contrast, sensory-based approaches such as aromatherapy and Snoezelen demonstrated inconsistent or low-quality evidence. The organizational-level implementation of PCA and structured need-driven assessments are associated with improved quality of life and appear to enhance the effectiveness of subsequent non-pharmacological interventions. The most consistent and clinically meaningful benefits were observed when individualized strategies were preceded by systematic PCA-based assessment. Overall, PCA-guided, multifaceted non-pharmacological interventions provide the preferred foundation for managing agitation in dementia, reducing reliance on pharmacotherapy while promoting care quality, dignity, and overall well-being. Future efforts should focus on optimizing implementation through structured staff training, interdisciplinary collaboration, and technology-assisted assessment models to improve feasibility and scalability across diverse care settings.Keywords: Agitation, Behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, Dementia, Non-pharmacological intervention, Person-centered care

1. INTRODUCTION

Dementia affects over 57 million people worldwide and is projected to affect 152 million by 2050.1 In Taiwan, currently approximately 370 000 people live with dementia (PwD), with the number expected to rise to approximately 690 000 by 2041.2 This growing burden is associated with increased healthcare utilization, caregiver strain, and reduced quality of life, as well as higher risks of morbidity, mortality, depression, and premature institutionalization.2–4

Behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) develop in over 90% of PwD, among which agitation is one of the most prevalent and distressing manifestations.4–7 Agitation affects approximately 27% of community-dwelling PwD and up to 58% of residents in long-term care facilities (LTCFs).7–9 Its prevalence increases with advancing age, disease severity, and in specific dementia subtypes such as frontotemporal and Lewy body dementia.7,8,10,11 Agitation substantially increases the risks of falls, hospitalization, caregiver depression, and institutionalization and frequently leads to the use of physical restraints and psychotropic medications.4–7

Agitation is increasingly recognized as a behavioral expression of unmet care needs, environmental stressors, and caregiver-related factors rather than a purely neuropsychiatric symptom. During the Coronavirus disease 2019 lockdowns, agitation severity increased by 20% to 36%, underscoring the vulnerability of PwD to environmental disruption.12 International guidelines consistently recommend person-centered approach (PCA) and non-pharmacological interventions as first-line strategies for agitation management.13–21

Herein, we developed an evidence-based clinical guideline and consensus recommendations based on a systematic review of current evidence.

2. METHODOLOGY

2.1. Stage I: Systematic literature review

A systematic search on six major databases (PubMed, Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library) was performed. Search terms included dementia, agitation, PCA, unmet care needs, and non-pharmacological intervention (Supplementary 1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A419). We included systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials focusing on person-centered and non-pharmacological interventions published in English before September 30, 2025. Observational studies were included when randomized evidence was unavailable. Studies primarily evaluating pharmacological interventions were excluded, unless they provided comparative or adjunctive data relevant to non-pharmacological strategies. Moreover, we applied the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology to assess both the quality of evidence, rated as high (A), moderate (B), low (C), very low (D), or expert opinion (E), and strength of recommendations, classified as strong (1) or weak (2).22

2.2. Stage II: Guideline framework and consensus formation

Taipei Veterans General Hospital Dementia Taskforce was established for guidance development on February 19, 2025, and the working group for non-pharmacological interventions included one expert neurologist, one expert psychiatrist, two expert geriatricians, two registered nurses, four clinical psychologists, two occupational therapists, and two care managers. After reviewing the published evidence and guidelines, the consensus recommendation was developed based on consideration of patient values and preferences, balance of benefits and harms, resource use, feasibility, and equity in real life after monthly consensus meetings.23 The final consensus recommendations were drafted and approved on October 22, 2025, and a conceptual illustration for assessment and mangement of agitation was developed (Fig. 1).

3. PERSON-CENTERED APPROACH

The PCA emphasizes holistic assessment of the physical, psychological, social, and self-fulfillment needs underlying BPSD. In PwD, agitation frequently reflects unmet needs that cannot be effectively communicated because of cognitive and language impairments.24 PCA prioritizes understanding the individual’s life history, values, preferences, and interpersonal context to identify triggers and perpetuating factors of agitation.14,25,26

At the organizational level, PCA implementation has been associated with improved quality of life and care processes, although its direct effect on agitation reduction remains modest and heterogeneous.24,27–29 Meta-analytic evidence suggests that PCA-based interventions are associated with a small and non-significant reduction in agitation (standardized mean difference [SMD]: –0.11, 95% CI, –0.29 to 0.07).24 However, organizational-level PCA implementation has demonstrated a significant improvement in overall quality of life (SMD: 0.16, 95% CI, 0.03-0.28).27–29 Based on these findings, PCA is recommended primarily as a foundational care philosophy that supports quality of life and individualized care, rather than as a stand-alone intervention for agitation (strong recommendation based on moderate quality of evidence; GRADE 1B) (Table 1). Longer and more intensive PCA programs—typically ≥30 minutes per session and ≥16 total hours—may confer greater benefits.27–29

3.1. Dementia care mapping

Dementia care mapping (DCM) operationalizes PCA through structured observation of behaviors, mood states, and engagement. Endorsed by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), DCM enables interdisciplinary teams to identify triggers, address unmet needs, and implement tailored care plans.13,29 Evidence shows that DCM-based interventions may reduce agitation in institutional settings, although the overall quality of evidence remains limited (weak recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 2C) (Table 1).13,28–31

Complementary frameworks, such as the Antecedent–Behavior–Consequence model and Describe–Investigate–Create–Evaluate approach, provide simplified and pragmatic structures for caregivers to identify behavioral triggers and evaluate care responses.14,31–33 These models are particularly useful in community and resource-limited settings, where full DCM implementation may not be feasible.

3.2. Unmet care needs

Unmet care needs across physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains are major contributors to agitation and other BPSD in dementia. These needs frequently remain unrecognized because PwD often cannot articulate discomfort or distress.34,35 Empirical studies have demonstrated strong associations between loneliness and verbal agitated behaviors, as well as between pain or physical discomfort and agitation severity.36

Common unmet needs include physiological factors (pain, discomfort, hunger, thirst, and sleep deprivation), psychological needs (loneliness, boredom, and insecurity), cognitive challenges (communication impairment, disorientation, impulsivity, and unpredictability), social factors (stigma, prejudice, neglect, and limited engagement), environmental stressors (unfamiliar settings, noise, overcrowding, and excessive light), and self-accomplishment needs (loss of autonomy, low self-esteem, diminished sense of usefulness, and discrimination).35–38

PCA facilitates need-driven care by systematically identifying and addressing unmet needs across these domains. In a multicenter cluster randomized trial conducted across 23 nursing homes in Belgium, need-driven care interventions significantly reduced agitation severity and caregiver distress among residents with mild–moderate dementia.34 These findings support the role of structured need assessment as a core component of non-pharmacological agitation management.

3.3. Pain control

Chronic pain is a common but frequently underrecognized trigger of agitation in PwD, with prevalence estimates ranging from 34% to 56%.39–41 Accurate pain recognition is challenging because cognitive impairments and communication deficits limit self-report, leading to underassessment and undertreatment.39–41 Consequently, pain-related agitation is often misinterpreted as a primary neuropsychiatric symptom and managed with physical restraints or psychotropic medications rather than appropriate analgesic strategies.40,41

Large observational studies have consistently demonstrated a significant association between pain severity and agitation. In a cohort study involving 56 577 nursing home residents with dementia, severe pain was associated with a higher likelihood of agitation (odds ratio [OR]: 1.17, 95% CI, 1.13-1.2).42 These findings underscore pain as a modifiable contributor to agitation and highlight the clinical importance of systematic pain assessment in dementia care settings.40,41

Standardized observational pain assessment tools—such as the Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia, Doloplus-2, and the Pain Assessment Checklist for Seniors with Limited Ability to Communicate—can improve pain detection and guide individualized treatment.40 In a cluster-randomized clinical trial conducted across 18 nursing homes in Norway, systematic analgesic treatment significantly reduced agitation scores measured by the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI, ***p < 0.001) and decreased aggressive (*p = 0.037), physically non-aggressive (**p = 0.008), and verbally agitated behaviors (***p < 0.001) assessed by the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory without worsening other BPSD symptoms.43,44

Although the overall evidence base remains limited by heterogeneity and a small number of randomized trials, routine pain assessment and appropriate analgesic treatment are recommended as core components of agitation management in PwD (strong recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 1C) (Table 1).39–42 Systematic pain control may reduce the unnecessary use of physical and chemical restraints while improving comfort and quality of life.39–42

3.4. Empowerment

Empowerment is a dynamic and interactive process involving PwD, their caregivers, and the surrounding social and care environment. It emphasizes the preservation of self-identity, restoration of self-affirmation, redefining self-worth, maintaining autonomy, and active participation in decision-making, even as cognitive impairment progresses.45,46 Rather than viewing PwD as passive recipients of care, empowerment reframes dementia care as a collaborative process that respects individual preferences, values, and remaining capacities.45,46

Persistent paternalistic attitudes toward dementia continue to undermine empowerment efforts. According to the World Alzheimer Report 2024, 60% to 69% of respondents believed that family responsibilities should be removed from PwD to prevent stress, and 29% to 33% viewed institutional care as the preferred option even when it conflicted with patients’ expressed wishes.47 Such attitudes contribute to social exclusion, reduced opportunities for meaningful engagement, and erosion of autonomy, thereby increasing psychological distress and caregiver–patient conflict.

Empowerment-based interventions—typically incorporating education, shared decision-making, skills training, and environmental support—can improve psychological well-being, self-esteem, and caregiver competence.45,46,48,49 By promoting shared responsibility and collaborative care, empowerment approaches may reduce interpersonal tension, enhance caregiver confidence, and improve attitudes toward dementia and BPSD management.48,49 Education and skills training on the PCA and care needs assessment are the most feasible strategies to address the paternalistic attitudes toward dementia and realize the ideal of empowerment.

Although direct evidence linking empowerment-based interventions to agitation reduction remains limited, empowerment constitutes a core principle of person-centered dementia care. By addressing psychosocial stressors, preserving autonomy, and strengthening caregiver–patient relationships, empowerment may indirectly mitigate triggers of agitation and enhance tolerance to behavioral symptoms. Accordingly, the development of dementia-friendly environments that integrate formal care services with informal community and family support is strongly encouraged as part of holistic agitation management.45,46,48,49

4. NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL INTERVENTIONS

Non-pharmacological interventions are universally endorsed as first-line strategies for managing agitation in PwD.13–21,50,51 Excessive reliance on pharmacological treatments is associated with increased hospitalization and reduced quality of life, whereas deprescribing interventions maintain behavioral stability without worsening outcomes.52,53

4.1. Sensory interventions

Sensory impairments, including hearing and visual loss, are well-established risk factors for dementia and agitation. In addition, altered sensory processing associated with neurodegeneration further disrupts interactions with the environment and caregivers.54 Sensory-based interventions aim to deliver nonverbal stimuli through one or multiple sensory pathways to reduce sensory deprivation, modulate arousal, and alleviate emotional distress, thereby mitigating agitation. Common modalities include massage, tactile stimulation, thermal therapy, aromatherapy, music therapy, and multisensory stimulation approaches such as Snoezelen.20,55–57

Massage and touch therapy provide structured nonverbal interaction that conveys comfort, safety, and emotional reassurance. These interventions may reduce agitation by alleviating physical discomfort and emotional stress, potentially mediated through relaxation responses and oxytocin-mediated calming effects.57,58 Meta-analyses demonstrate a significant reduction in agitation following massage or touch therapy (SMD: −5.22, 95% CI, −8.21 to −2.49), although reported effect sizes are heterogeneous and influenced by study design and sample size.57,58 Higher intervention frequency is associated with greater benefit across both community and institutional settings. Accordingly, massage and touch therapy are recommended as effective and low-risk interventions for agitation management (strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence; GRADE 1C) (Table 1).57,58

Music therapy utilizes familiar auditory stimuli to evoke emotional responses, support affect regulation, and reduce anxiety or fear.57,58 Although its direct effect on agitation is modest and not consistently significant (SMD: −0.05, 95% CI, −0.27 to 0.17), music therapy demonstrates broader benefits for overall BPSD severity (SMD: −0.31, 95% CI, −0.6 to −0.02) and depressive symptoms (SMD: −0.23, 95% CI, −0.42 to −0.04).56,59 Individual music therapy is more effective than group formats for agitation reduction (SMD: −0.31, 95% CI, −0.6 to −0.02), particularly among residents in LTCFs and individuals aged 80 years or older (weak recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 2C) (Table 1).57,58

Aromatherapy provides pleasant olfactory stimulation that may elicit positive emotional responses and implicit autobiographical memories in some PwD. Essential oils such as Lavandula angustifolia and Melissa officinalis have been most frequently investigated, with inconsistent and heterogeneous results across trials. Considering the limited quality and variability of evidence, aromatherapy may be considered an adjunctive intervention in selected individuals (weak recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 2C) (Table 1).57,59,60

Snoezelen, a multisensory stimulation approach originally developed for individuals with learning disabilities, has not demonstrated consistent benefit for agitation in dementia.20,55–57 Moreover, concerns regarding overstimulation and potential exacerbation of agitation have been raised; consequently, some guidelines have been proposed to discourage its use.20,55–57 Therefore, Snoezelen is not recommended for agitation management in PwD (weak recommendation based on expert opinion; GRADE 2E).

4.2. Social contact

Social contact interventions aim to reduce agitation by addressing emotional distress, loneliness, and insecurity through companionship and affective engagement. These approaches include doll therapy, animal-assisted interventions, and simulated presence techniques, which provide varying forms of emotional reassurance and social connection within care environments.

Doll therapy facilitates attachment-based interactions by evoking nurturing memories and emotional engagement.61,62 Meta-analytic evidence indicates significant reductions in agitation (SMD: −0.94; 95% CI, −1.36 to −0.51) and overall BPSD severity (SMD: −0.42; 95% CI, −0.74 to −0.10), particularly among residents in LTCFs.61–63 Interventions incorporating empathy dolls, active caregiver involvement, session duration shorter than 2 hours, and sustained implementation for at least 12 weeks are associated with greater effectiveness. Based on available evidence, doll therapy is recommended as a targeted intervention for agitation in institutional settings (weak recommendation based on very low quality of evidence; GRADE 2D) (Table 1).61–63

Animal-assisted interventions, delivered through live animals or robotic companions such as Paro, may reduce agitation by providing companionship, sensory stimulation, and emotional comfort. However, pooled effect estimates for agitation reduction do not reach statistical significance (SMD: −0.39, 95% CI, −0.89 to 0.1), although some studies have reported modest benefit for depressive symptoms (MD: −2.87, 95% CI, −5.24 to −0.5) Limited evidence suggests modest benefits for depressive symptoms rather than agitation per se (weak recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 2C) (Table 1).57,64–66

Simulated presence therapy, which uses audio or video recordings of family members to recreate familiar interaction, has shown inconclusive effects on agitation because of small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity.67 Accordingly, these interventions may be considered on an individual basis but cannot be routinely recommended for agitation management (weak recommendation based on expert opinion) (GRADE 2E).

4.3. Activities-based interventions

Activities-based interventions are safe, low-cost strategies with high caregiver acceptability and are widely endorsed in international dementia care guidelines.13,15,20 The World Alzheimer Report 2025 further emphasizes their role in supporting physical, psychological, and social well-being in PwD.68,69 Meaningful activities help fulfill basic needs, promote social engagement, and may exert neurobiological effects through muscle–brain crosstalk mechanisms involving myokines and brain-derived neurotrophic factor.69,70 Moreover, they serve as indicators for monitoring functional capacity and emerging care needs.20,50,68–70

Personally tailored activities demonstrate modest improvements in agitation (SMD: −0.26, 95% CI, −0.53 to 0.01), with more consistent benefits observed among residents in LTCFs (SMD: 0.88, 95% CI, 0.43-1.32).71 Structured physical activity programs show borderline effects on agitation in both community-based settings (NPI-agitation score: −0.81, 95% CI, −1.98 to 0.36) and LTCFs (NPI-agitation score: −0.15, 95% CI, −0.46 to 0.16).72 Greater intervention intensity, adherence rates exceeding 70%, and aerobic exercise components are consistently associated with improved outcomes.71,72 Optimal protocols typically involve sessions lasting for a minimum of 30 minutes, conducted three or more times per week, for a minimum duration of 12 weeks (strong recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 1C) (Table 1).14,57,71,72

Beyond agitation reduction, activities-based interventions contribute to positive affect, functional maintenance, and monitoring of emerging care needs.68–72 Although their effect on agitation alone is modest, their favorable safety profile and broad psychosocial benefits support their integration into routine dementia care as part of a comprehensive, person-centered management strategy.

4.4. Environmental modification

Environmental modification aims to reduce agitation by optimizing physical surroundings to minimize overstimulation and enhance comfort, orientation, and safety. Key elements include adjustments to lighting, noise, color schemes, temperature, spatial layout, and familiarity of the environment.73–75 By reducing sensory overload and environmental stressors, these modifications may mitigate triggers of agitation, particularly in individuals with advanced dementia.

Light therapy has been investigated primarily for circadian rhythm regulation and the management of sundowning-related symptoms. Evidence for direct agitation reduction remains inconsistent, with pooled analyses showing small and non-significant effects (SMD: −0.28, 95% CI, −1.02 to 0.45).74,75 Daytime exposure to moderate light intensity (<2500 lux) may confer benefit in selected individuals with Alzheimer’s disease, whereas excessive bright light (>100 000 lux) or nighttime light exposure may exacerbate agitation, particularly in those with severe dementia (weak recommendation based on low quality of evidence; GRADE 1C) (Table 1).25,73–75

White noise interventions may reduce agitation by masking disruptive environmental sounds and promoting relaxation.76–78 A non-randomized Taiwanese study reported significant reductions in agitated behaviors following four weeks of white-noise exposure.78 However, the evidence base remains limited, and findings require confirmation in randomized trials. Accordingly, white noise may be considered an adjunctive strategy in selected settings (weak recommendation, expert opinion; GRADE 2E).

4.5. Cognitive-behavioral therapy

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT)-based interventions in dementia encompass a range of approaches, including reminiscence therapy, functional analysis-based interventions, validation therapy, and storytelling approaches. These modalities aim to support emotional regulation, reinforce self-identity, and enhance coping through meaningful cognitive and interpersonal engagement.79–83 Although effective for mood and behavioral symptoms in cognitively intact older adults, their applicability in dementia is constrained by disease severity and communication capacity.

Evidence supporting CBT-based interventions for agitation reduction in dementia remains limited and heterogeneous. Reminiscence therapy, the most extensively investigated modality, shows inconclusive effects on agitation (SMD: 0.03, 95% CI, –0.17 to 0.24), despite modest benefits for mood and quality of life.79–83 Individualized CBT approaches may benefit selected individuals with mild dementia and relatively preserved communication abilities. Overall, CBT-based interventions may be considered as adjunctive options rather than primary treatments for agitation (strong recommendation based on moderate quality of evidence; GRADE 1C) (Table 1).

4.6. Combination or multicomponent therapy

Multicomponent interventions integrate two or more non-pharmacological approaches, with or without concomitant pharmacological treatment, to address the multifactorial nature of agitation in dementia.57,58 These strategies are based on the recognition that agitation often arises from interacting physical, psychological, social, and environmental factors and may therefore respond better to combined rather than single-modality interventions.57,58

Compared with those on single interventions alone, most studies on multicomponent interventions have reported additive or synergistic effects. Common combinations include activity programs paired with sensory-based therapies, environmental modification, or caregiver education. Although overall evidence quality remains moderate, pooled analyses suggest that combination approaches are more likely to yield clinically meaningful improvements in agitation and overall BPSD severity (weak recommendation based on moderate quality of evidence; GRADE 2B) (Table 1).14,20,58

Among evaluated combinations, music therapy combined with massage or touch therapy demonstrates superior efficacy for agitation reduction in network meta-analyses (SMD: –0.91, 95% CI, –1.75 to –0.07).14,20,58 Nature-based interventions, particularly those delivered in indoor or socially integrated formats, may also confer benefit.84 From a feasibility perspective, combinations of activities, music therapy, aromatherapy, and massage or touch therapy are well suited for community settings with support from LTCFs, whereas environmental modification, doll therapy, and CBT-based approaches may be more feasible in LTCFs with greater resources and specialized expertise.

5. BARRIERS AND FACILITATORS

Despite strong endorsement in international guidelines, the real-world implementation of non-pharmacological interventions for agitation in PwD remains limited. Observational studies have shown that such interventions are applied in only 21.3% of community settings and 37.8% of LTCFs before the initiation of pharmacological treatment.85 This implementation gap highlights substantial challenges in translating evidence-based recommendations into routine clinical practice.

Common barriers to implementation include insufficient staffing, limited training in behavioral management, time constraints, high caregiver burden, and pressure for rapid behavioral control.85–87 Additional challenges include multimorbidity, rapid staff turnover, and low awareness or confidence in non-pharmacological strategies. In Taiwan, shortages in manpower resources, heavy caregiving workloads, limited experience in managing BPSD, and incomplete coverage by long-term care services further exacerbate reliance on antipsychotic medications.88 Methodological limitations—including heterogeneity of interventions, small sample sizes, variable adherence, and inconsistent outcome measures—further hinder the generalizability and adoption of non-pharmacological approaches.89

Nevertheless, several facilitators have been identified, including structured assessment tools, simplified intervention protocols, caregiver education, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Building interdisciplinary collaboration models helps in achieving the following: (1) identifying complex care needs in aged PwD with coexisting geriatric syndromes, (2) reducing polypharmacy and anticholinergic pill burdens, (3) connecting with potential formal and informal resources, and (4) providing a supporting network for each other and family caregivers. Interdisciplinary collaboration helps to improve awareness of BPSDs, dementia, and geriatric syndromes. Moreover, it relieves the care burden among healthcare professionals when coping with BPSDs, especially agitation. When supported by organizational commitment and policy alignment, non-pharmacological interventions can be feasibly integrated into routine dementia care across diverse settings.85–89

6. CARE MANAGEMENT

Care management integrates formal healthcare services with informal caregiving to address the complex needs of PwD and their caregivers through coordinated assessment, individualized care planning, caregiver education, and psychosocial support.90–92 By enhancing continuity and coordination of care, care management aims to optimize resource use and support aging in place.

Evidence from systematic reviews suggests that care management interventions improve caregiver-related and selected patient outcomes. A Cochrane review reported improvements in the quality of life of caregivers at 12 months (SMD: 0.21, 95% CI, 0.06-0.37), reduced institutionalization of PwD at 18 months (OR: 0.25, 95% CI, 0.10-0.61), and reductions in overall BPSD severity at 18 months (SMD: –0.35, 95% CI, –0.63 to –0.07).18,89 However, effects on agitation specifically remain inconsistent across studies.

Considering its potential to mitigate BPSD and agitation, enhance caregiver confidence and tolerance, and support aging in place for PwD, further high-quality studies are warranted to validate the effectiveness of care management programs. This recommendation aligns with the NICE guidance, which advocates for multidisciplinary, person-centered case management as a cornerstone of comprehensive dementia care.13,89–91

Although direct effects on agitation are limited, care management may indirectly mitigate agitation by enhancing caregiver competence, improving tolerance to behavioral symptoms, and facilitating timely identification of unmet needs. Its role as a multidisciplinary, person-centered strategy aligns with NICE and other international guidelines; however, further high-quality studies are required to clarify its effectiveness for agitation-specific outcomes.13,89–91

7. CONCLUSION

Agitation in dementia significantly compromises patient dignity, caregiver well-being, and healthcare sustainability. PCA-guided and non-pharmacological approaches form the foundation of effective agitation management. Massage or touch therapy and doll therapy are recommended, whereas music therapy, activity-based interventions, environmental modification, cognitive-behavioral approaches, and multicomponent interventions are suggested depending on care context and feasibility. Snoezelen is discouraged because of low benefits and potential harm. Future research should prioritize scalable, technology-assisted frameworks to bridge the gap between evidence and real-world implementation, particularly in community-based settings within super-aged societies.
