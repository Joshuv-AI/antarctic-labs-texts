Analysis of the association of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, MPV-to-platelet ratio, and clinical risk factors for mortality in ICU patients with candidemia: a 10-year retrospective analysis
Authors: İlker Ödemiş, Eren Arkalı, Süheyla Serin Senger, Duygu Tekin, Gürsel Ersan, Sabri Atalay, Merve Nur Filiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandidaemia is a leading cause of mortality in hospitalized patients. The determination of risk factors is necessary to predict mortality. We aimed to evaluate whether the neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts; red blood cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophil/lymphocyte (N/L) and MPV/platelet count (MPV/plt) ratios, and other clinical characteristics are risk factors for mortality.MethodsThis study was retrospective and observational. Patients aged 18 years and older who were hospitalized in the intensive care unit between January 1, 2014, and January 1, 2024, who had Candida detected via blood culture, were included in the study. Demographic data, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts; RDW, MPV, N/L, and MPV/plt ratios, and clinical characteristics of patients were recorded in the patient form. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors.ResultsThe mean age of the 468 patients was 64.26 ± 15.9 years, and 204 (43.6%) were female. A total of 321 patients (68.6%) died. The most common comorbid disease was malignancy (n = 171, 36.5%), whereas 482 candidemia episodes were detected. Candida parapsilosis (n = 239, 49.6%) was the most frequently isolated strain. Diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy, N/L, and MPV/plt ratios were found to be associated with mortality in univariate analysis, but none were found to be risk factors for mortality in multivariate regression analysis. Higher age, congestive heart failure (CHF), higher neutrophil count, RDW > 17.1 /%, low albumin level, low platelet count, lymphocyte count < 1000 /mm3, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), mechanical ventilation (MV), immunosuppressive drug use and Candida glabrata were independent risk factors for mortality.ConclusionsMortality remains high among ICU patients with candidemia. This ten-year analysis identified advanced age, CHF, elevated RDW, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, TPN, MV, immunosuppressive therapy, and candidemia caused by C. glabrata were identified as independent risk factors for mortality. Integrating haematological and clinical parameters in a risk stratification model may help to reduce mortality rates.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The global incidence of candidemia has risen by approximately 50% over the past decade, with reported rates ranging from 2.4 to 15 cases per 100,000 population, depending on the country [1]. Between 2000 and 2003, the incidence of candidemia in a hospital in Turkey was 0.56 per 1,000 patients, and 53% of patients diagnosed with candidemia were hospitalized in the intensive care unit (ICU) [2]. Transplantation, intensive care unit admission, immunosuppression, advanced age, intra-abdominal surgery, the use of central venous catheters (CVC) or total parenteral nutrition (TPN), and a history of major trauma or burns are among the principal risk factors for the development of candidemia [3]. Despite the use of broad-spectrum antifungal agents, mortality from candidemia remains high, reaching up to 70% in some units due to comorbid diseases, immunosuppression, inappropriate use of antifungals and resistance to antifungals [4–9].

A reduction in mortality can be achieved by identifying risk factors associated with mortality and implementing appropriate interventions for modifiable risk factors. Kutlu et al. [4] reported that TPN, thrombocytopenia, high Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores and renal failure were risk factors for mortality, and the removal of a central venous catheter (CVC) resulted in a decrease in mortality. In a study aiming to determine the predictive role of cytokines in mortality prediction, it was concluded that interleukin 6 (IL-6) and interferon-gamma levels were useful in predicting mortality in candidemic patients [10]. However, these biomarkers are expensive and not easily available at all centers.

Among the leukocyte types, neutrophils, lymphocytes and monocytes are involved in immune functions against bacterial, viral and fungal agents, whereas eosinophils and basophils are involved in parasitic infections and allergic responses. Neutrophils exert fungicidal activity through both oxidative and nonoxidative mechanisms [11]. These mechanisms are essential for the immune response in controlling Candida infections. The white blood cell count was found to be associated with candidemia mortality, but the relationships between specific leukocyte subtypes and mortality have not been examined [5, 12]. Lymphopenia has been shown to be associated with mortality in candidemic patients, and IL-17 secreted by T helper 17 cells, a subgroup of lymphocytes, activates neutrophils [11, 13, 14]. Both the quantity and functional activity of neutrophils and lymphocytes are critical for effective immunity. The neutrophil count/lymphocyte count (N/L) ratio may indirectly indicate the correct functioning of the immune system. Platelets rapidly bind to Candida species, and Candida species cause platelet aggregation [15–17]. Moreover, platelet microbicidal proteins contribute to the killing of some Candida spp. [16, 17]. Thrombocytopenia has been associated with mortality in candidemic patients [4, 18, 19]. Studies have shown that a high N/L ratio and high mean platelet volume (MPV)/platelet count (MPV/plt) ratio are associated with poor prognosis in sepsis patients [20, 21]. The lack of data on the MPV/plt and N/L ratio in studies examining factors associated with mortality in patients with candidemia raises questions about the usefulness of these markers.

Predicting mortality in candidemia is critical for both individual patient management and public health practice. Early identification of patients at high risk of mortality makes it possible to direct limited resources, such as intensive care beds and healthcare personnel, to the patients who need them most. This approach helps optimize the allocation of limited healthcare resources. Predicting mortality in candidemia helps in making better treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes at the individual level and is vital for effective resource management, disease control and the development of better health policies at the public health level.

In our study, we aimed to investigate the utility of neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, and monocyte counts; hemoglobin, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and MPV levels, and the MPV/plt and N/L ratios in the prediction of 28-day mortality in patients with candidemia.

Methods

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective observational study. Demographic data, vital signs, comorbid diseases, drug use, mechanical ventilation status, central venous catheter use, and laboratory data were extracted from the hospital database.

Study population

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: aged 18 years or older, admission to the ICU between January 1, 2014, and January 1, 2024, and at least one blood culture positive for Candida spp. Recurrent episodes of candidemia within 30 days after the first candidemia attack (if the Candida strain and antifungal susceptibility are the same) were excluded from the study. Patients who were transferred to another healthcare facility within 28 days after the first positive blood culture or who were discharged at their own request while treatment was ongoing were excluded from the study, as reliable data on 28-day mortality could not be obtained for these cases.

Candidemia was defined as the detection of Candida spp. in blood cultures. The day of blood culture on which Candida spp. were detected was considered day 0. Death within 28 days from day 0 was recorded as 28-day mortality. Steroid use was defined as corticosteroid use at a daily dose of 20 mg prednisolone for more than seven days or prednisolone 10 mg/day for more than 14 days in the four weeks before the diagnosis of candidemia.

Fungal identification

The samples were injected into BacT/Alert medium bottles and incubated in a BacT/Alert 3D system (BioMérieux, France) for five days. Gram staining was performed on samples from signal-positive blood culture bottles, and yeast cells were observed microscopically. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of blood was aseptically inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar, blood agar, and eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar (BioMérieux, France) using a sterile syringe. The inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. After 24 h of incubation, yeast colonies were identified using a MALDI-TOF MS system (BD Bruker, Germany).

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 23.0. Continuous measurements are summarized as the means and standard deviations (median and minimum-maximum where necessary), and categorical measurements are summarized as numbers and percentages. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. For the comparison of continuous variables between groups, Student’s t- test was applied to variables with a normal distribution, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. The median values of the neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, and monocyte counts; hemoglobin, MPV; RDW and the N/L and MPV/plt ×10⁶ ratios were used as cutoff points and were analyzed as categorical variables. Continuous variables are described as the mean ± standard deviation if they followed a normal distribution [based on skewness and kurtosis values between − 2 and + 2] and as the median (minimum–maximum) if they did not follow a normal distribution. Independent risk factors associated with mortality were identified via logistic regression analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 482 candidemia episodes were detected in the 468 patients included in the study. Among the 468 patients with a mean age of 64.26 ± 15.9 years, 204 (43.6%) were female. A total of 321 (68.6%) of the patients died. Malignancy was present in 171 (36.5%) patients, and cancer was the most common comorbid disease. The distributions of demographic characteristics, comorbidities and fungal pathogens are shown in Table 1. According to the univariate analysis, increased age (p < 0.001), malignancy (OR: 1.680, 95% CI: 1.102–2.561, p = 0.015), diabetes mellitus (OR: 0.594, 95% CI: 0.358–0.988, p = 0.043), congestive heart failure (CHF) (OR: 2.281, 95% CI: 1.151–4.523, p = 0.016) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (OR: 2.174, 95% CI: 1.094–4.320, p = 0.024) were associated with mortality. In addition, CVC (OR: 1.643, 95% CI: 1.080–2.501, p = 0.020), mechanical ventilation (MV) support (OR: 2.979, 95% CI: 1.950–4.551, p < 0.001), receiving immunosuppressive drugs (OR: 1.679, 95% CI: 1.068–2.639, p = 0.024), TPN support (OR: 2.537, 95% CI: 1.685–3.819, p < 0.001) and steroid use (OR: 1.927, 95% CI: 1.184–3.137, p = 0.008) were associated with mortality in the univariate analysis. Discussion

The mortality rate of candidemic patients ranges widely from 38 to 69% and is high [4–7]. The mortality rate in our patient group was 68.6%, which is comparable to that reported in previous studies but higher than that reported by Zimmermann et al. [4–6]. The fact that the patients in the Zimmermann et al. [6] study were nonneutropenic and that their mean age was lower than that in our study may be the reason for this difference.

Leukocytes are key components of immunity, and there are five main types of white blood cells: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes. Schroeder et al. and Asai et al. reported a relationship between leukocytosis and mortality [5, 12]. However, the relationship between the number of white blood cell types and mortality has not been analyzed. According to our findings, a high neutrophil count is an independent risk factor for mortality. We believe that analyzing neutrophil, lymphocyte and monocyte counts separately would be useful in terms of clarifying this issue. We also concluded that a lymphocyte count < 1000/mm³ was associated with a 2.4-fold increase in mortality. Ortega-Loubon et al. [14] reported that a lymphocyte count less than 700 increased the mortality risk 5-fold. IL-7 enables T cells to proliferate, and IL-7 treatment has been shown to decrease mortality in fungal infections [22]. The results of these studies suggest that lymphocyte count and function directly and indirectly affect mortality in candidemia and support the findings of our study.

The lack of data in the literature on the relationship between the N/L ratio and mortality in candidemia has caused this issue to remain in the dark. In 203 patients with sepsis, including a small number of patients with fungemia, the N/L ratio in the first seven days was associated with mortality [23]. A meta-analysis including 1371 sepsis patients revealed that a high N/L ratio was associated with poor prognosis in sepsis patients [20]. In our study, no statistical relationship was found between mortality and the N/L ratio. In sepsis studies, bacteremic and candidemic patients are considered together. Aldeen et al. [24] reported that the response of the immune system to bacteremia and candidemia differed and that the levels of IL-2, IL-6 and IL-10 were greater in candidemic patients than in bacteremic patients. Elevated levels of these cytokines in candidemia, compared with bacteraemia, may influence neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, thereby weakening the predictive value of the N/L ratio.

Orak et al. [25] reported that thrombocytopenia and high MPV were associated with mortality in sepsis patients, but the number of patients with candidemia was small, and this relationship was not investigated in candidaemic patients as a subanalysis. Aydin et al. [26] found that an MPV value greater than 10.25 predicted mortality in patients with nosocomial candidemia with 79% sensitivity and 49% specificity, and identified MPV as an independent risk factor for mortality. Our findings did not support a significant association between the MPV or the MPV/platelet ratio and 28-day mortality; however, a low PLT emerged as an independent risk factor. Jia et al., Kutlu et al. and Zhang et al. reported that thrombocytopenia is associated with mortality in candidemic patients [4, 18, 19]. In the Zhang et al. [18] study, the thrombocytopenia cutoff level was 100 000/mm³, and thrombocytopenia was shown to increase mortality 5.7-fold. This strong relationship between thrombocytopenia and mortality in candidemia is remarkable. In light of these findings, clinicians should monitor platelet counts closely and explore the potential benefits of platelet supplementation in patients with candidemia, as this may contribute to improved clinical outcomes.

In inflammation, proinflammatory cytokines inhibit erythrocyte maturation, leading to increased RDWs due to the release of large, immature reticulocytes into the peripheral circulation [27]. RDW has been shown to be a useful predictor of all-cause mortality, as well as mortality in sepsis, cardiovascular disease, and acute stroke; however, its specificity is limited, as elevated RDWs can occur in a wide range of conditions [27, 28]. To our knowledge, the association between RDW and mortality in candidemia has not been previously investigated. In our cohort, an RDW >17.1% was associated with a 2.5-fold increase in mortality risk. With this result, we think that we will contribute to the literature by adding candidemia to the list of conditions in which RDW is useful in predicting mortality.

It has been suggested that the enhanced proliferation of Candida spp. in environments rich in lipids and glucose may contribute to the increased incidence of candidemia among patients receiving TPN [29]. Although candidemia is more common among patients receiving TPN, the 1.1- to 3.9-fold increase in mortality risk associated with TPN use reported by Nakada-Motokawa et al. and Kutlu et al., together with the 2.7-fold increase observed in our study, supports the notion that candidemia tends to follow a more severe clinical course in these patients [4, 30]. These findings highlight the potential role of TPN-associated metabolic changes in the gastrointestinal tract in disrupting host immune responses. Therefore, careful monitoring and judicious use of TPN may be crucial to reduce the risk and severity of candidemia in ICU patients. In addition to host and treatment related factors, species related differences in virulence and antifungal susceptibility may also influence patient outcomes. The most common Candida spp. exhibiting antifungal resistance are C. glabrata and, more recently, C. auris. In our study, low albumin levels and C. glabrata was identified as an independent risk factor for mortality. Zhang et al. [18] reported that hypoalbuminemia, C. albicans, and C. parapsilosis were risk factors for mortality. The differences in the results may be attributed to the inclusion of patients who underwent surgical procedures and were admitted to both the ICU and general wards in the study by Zhang et al. [18], as well as to epidemiological differences between the region where their study was conducted and ours.

Diabetes, malignancy, renal failure, advanced age, and other comorbid conditions, as well as intra-abdominal surgery and invasive procedures such as CVC insertion, are recognized as major risk factors for candidemia [3, 31]. Kutlu et al. [4], who investigated the impact of comorbid diseases on mortality, identified acute renal failure as a risk factor; Nakada-Motokawa et al. [30] reported advanced age and acute renal failure; and Li et al. [32] identified respiratory dysfunction. Consistent with previous studies, our study also identified advanced age, MV, and immunosuppressive drug use as independent risk factors for mortality. Although intra-abdominal surgery, a well-known risk factor for candidemia, was not associated with mortality in many studies or in ours [4, 19, 30, 32, 33]. We think that the lack of an association between intra-abdominal surgery and mortality may be explained by two factors: the early initiation of antifungal therapy in surgical patients through the use of early warning systems such as the Candida score, and the decreased frequency of high-risk surgical procedures performed during the pandemic.

The limitations of our study are that the data were obtained from a single center and therefore the study has a limited sample size. Other limitations of our study are that we could not examine the effects of the SOFA score and Acute Physiologic Assessment of Health and Chronic Evaluation of Health II score, concomitant bacterial infections, antifungal resistance and antifungal therapy on mortality. We believe that these findings are valuable, as this is the first study to assess the prognostic value of the monocyte count, RDW, MPV, MPV/PLT ratio, and N/L ratio in patients with candidemia, and to demonstrate that the RDW, lymphocyte count, and C. glabrata are independent risk factors for mortality in candidemic patients.

Conclusions

This study found that mortality remains high and identified several independent predictors reflecting host- and infection-related factors. Advanced age, CHF, elevated RDW, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, TPN, MV, immunosuppressive therapy, and candidemia caused by C. glabrata were identified as independent risk factors for mortality.

These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of mortality in candidemia and highlight the importance of laboratory and clinical parameters in prognostic evaluation. Integrating this haematological and clinical parameters in a risk stratification model may help to reduce mortality rates.
