A study of Kock’s fat Delta
Tom de Jong, Nicolai Kraus, Simona Paoli, Stiéphen Pradal

Abstract
Motivated by the study of weak identity structures in higher category theory we explore the fat Delta category, a modification of the simplex category introduced by J. Kock and provide a comprehensive study via the theory of monads with arities. Specifically, by proving that the free relative semicategory monad is strongly cartesian and identifying a dense generator, the theory of monads with arities immediately gives rise to the nerve theorem. We characterise the essential image of the nerve via the Segal condition, and show that fat Delta possesses an active-inert factorisation system. Building on these results, we also establish an isomorphism between two presentations of fat Delta.

Introduction

Higher categories come with many levels of structure, and each of the various approaches to higher category theory needs to provide a way to organise, represent and work with this structure.
One very fruitful approach to higher categories has been to use simplicial and multi-simplicial objects, that is functors from the opposite of the simplicial category
\Delta
and its products, satisfying additional conditions that encode the composition of higher morphisms and their behaviour.

This simplicial approach has given rise to models of (
\infty
,​
1

[

,

]
and

[

\infty
,​

[

]
, that is categories with morphisms in all dimensions but weakly invertible in dimension higher than
n

These have found important applications to mathematical physics in the field of TQFTs
[

,
Ber11
,
GP22
]
.
Multi-simplicial models have also been used to describe truncated higher categories, that is those with morphisms only in dimension up to
n

These arise naturally in homotopy theory, where, in the higher groupoidal case, they describe the building blocks of spaces, called homotopy
n
-types.
Such models include the Tamsamani model
[
Tam99
]
and the weakly globular

-fold categories of one of the authors

The current paper studies the so-called

A variation of

Motivation from higher category theory

It is well known that strict
n
-categories (that is, those where higher morphisms have compositions that obey associative and unit laws strictly) are insufficient for many applications.
For instance, strict
n
-groupoids cannot model homotopy
n
-types. Explicit examples have been found in which strict
3
-groupoids cannot model the homotopy
3
-types of spaces with non-trivial Whitehead products
[
Sim98
]
.
Likewise, higher order cobordism categories necessitate weak higher categories
[
Lur08
, §2.2]
: that is, those where compositions of higher morphisms obey associative and unit laws only up to an invertible higher morphism in the next dimension, and these associativity and unit isomorphisms are sufficiently compatible or coherent.

C. Simpson conjectured in
[
Sim98
]
that any such model of weak
n
-category should be suitably equivalent to one in which the composition laws are strictly associative, that is they hold as equalities, and the unit laws hold up to coherent isomorphism.
This is usually referred to as the
weak units conjecture
for truncated weak
n
-categories.
J. Kock used the fat Delta category to give a precise formulation of the weak units conjecture (namely, as stating that the Tamsamani
n
-categories are suitably equivalent to fair
n
-categories)
[
Koc06
]
.
The category of fair
n
-categories is built from functors from the opposite of
\underline{\Delta}
and its products in an inductive way, formally analogous to the Tamsamani model, but with the simplex category replaced by fat Delta.
A main difference however is that in the fair
n
-categories model, all Segal maps are isomorphisms, hence all compositions of higher morphisms are (strictly) associative, while the unit laws hold only up to isomorphisms.
The combinatorics of fat Delta allows precisely to decouple the units structure from the composition structure to make this possible, unlike in the simplicial approach.

For general
n
, this conjecture is open.
However, in the case
n=2
this has been proved by one of the authors using the intermediary of weakly globular double categories
[
Pao25
]
, and it is hoped that this approach could in the future be extended to higher dimensions.
For
n=3
, A. Joyal and J. Kock have given a proof for one-object 3-groupoids
[
JK07
]
.
More recently, S. Henry has given a proof of the conjecture for regular
\infty
-groupoids
[
Hen18
]
.
The study of models of weak and semistrict (
\infty
,1)-categories based on
\underline{\Delta}
will appear in the forthcoming PhD thesis of the last author.

We have so far described weak units in the context of a strict composition structure.
It is possible to weaken the unital structure even if the composition structure is already weak.
In the context of infinity categories, studies in this direction include
[
Har15
]
and
[
Hau21
]
.
This direction is closely related to what we discuss next, namely the motivation for
\underline{\Delta}
stemming from type theory.

Motivation from type theory

A further motivation for studying
\underline{\Delta}
comes from
type theory
, a foundation for mathematics that is used as a basis for programming languages and proof assistants
[
Mar84
,
NPS90
]
.
As a foundational system, type theory is often compared to set theory, where the primitive objects are
sets
.
In contrast,
types
(the primitive objects of type theory) behave more akin to
spaces
, i.e.
\infty
-groupoids or homotopy types, a view that
homotopy type theory (HoTT)

[
Uni13
]
is built upon.

There have been extensive discussions about the philosophical and practical differences between set theory and type theory (cf.
[
CKS19
]
) that are unimportant for the current paper.
It suffices to understand that, while the predominant approach is to
define
the notion of a space in terms of sets (for example, as a Kan simplicial set), every object in homotopy type theory
is
a space.
In a nutshell, homotopy type theory is a language in which spaces are the primitive objects and all operations one can talk about are invariant under homotopy
[
Shu21
]
.

In homotopy type theory, the collection of all (small) spaces/types is a so-called universe
\mathcal{U}
.
When studying (
\infty
,​
1
)-categories, it seems natural to work with complete Segal spaces, with spaces being the objects in
\mathcal{U}
.
The definition one might expect would be a functor from
\Delta
to
\mathcal{U}
, of which one requires Reedy fibrancy as well as a Segal and a completeness condition as additional properties.
The issue is that this formulation requires imposing functor laws and, as strict equalities are not automatically homotopy invariant, the language of homotopy type theory cannot even state them.

1

1

While it is easily justifiable to internalise strict equality in the language, as done in
two-level type theory

[
ACK16
,
Ann+23
]
, doing so necessitates manually checking that one stays within the fibrant framework, something that does not seem possible here.

But it turns out that (contravariant) Reedy fibrant diagrams over certain categories can be defined in a way that never leaves type theory’s world of homotopy-invariant constructions
[
Shu15
,
Ann+23
]
.
For example, a Reedy fibrant space-valued contravariant diagram over the inverse category
\mathcal{C}
given by

is fully described by a space

of vertices and, for all pairs

in

, a space

of edges.
This is a complete definition of the space of Reedy fibrant functors from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{U}
without explicitly mentioning equalities; all components of the usual definition can easily be derived.

Clearly, the strategy employed here can only work if the index category is
direct
, i.e. if every sequence
A\leftarrow B\leftarrow C\leftarrow\ldots
of non-identity morphisms is finite.
The simplex category
\Delta
is not direct, but
\underline{\Delta}
(as well as the similar category suggested by C. Sattler and a current author
[
KS17
]
) is, making it suitable for homotopy type theory.

Relative Semicategories

A

\mathcal{C}
(also known as

) consists of a class of
objects

\mathcal{C}_{0}
and of a set of
morphisms

\mathcal{C}(x,y)

Simply put, it is a category without identity structure.
A

in
\mathcal{C}
an object
F(c)
in
\mathcal{D}
and to every morphism

in
\mathcal{C}

F(f)\colon F(c)\to F(c^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{D}
such that

We denote by
\SemiCat

Generally, the notion of

[

]
.
For our interests, we would like to look at semicategories with certain morphisms that behave like identities and functors that preserve them.
Hence, this leads us to work with semicategories equipped with a class of morphisms. We call such objects

More precisely, a relative semicategory

consists of a carrier semicategory

and a wide subsemicategory

Monads with arities

The theory of monads with arities

BMW12

This concept is particularly useful when we study higher structures geometrically rather than algebraically (such as simplicial sets for categories), as it allows for the construction of nerve theorems and the analysis of Segal conditions.
In this section, we will first recall the concept of a monad and introduce tools such as induced monads and local right adjoints.
Then, we will define a monad with arities as a monad on a category equipped with a dense generator such that a composition preserves colimits related to the density.

and
[
BMW12

The category

We present the original description, useful for intuition, in terms of relative semiordinals (

We then give another one, more practical, also defined in

in terms of a subcategory of the category of epimorphisms in

In this paper, we make use of the intuition of the relative semiordinals, but we exclusively work with the definition of

The current section also introduces tools and notation to simplify the manipulation of the objects of

Relative semiordinals

In
[
Koc06
]
, J. Kock focuses on a conceptual approach to
\underline{\Delta}
by defining it as the full subcategory of
\RelSemiCat
consisting of finite non-empty “coloured semiordinals”. We present this description here to provide intuition for the objects and morphisms of
\underline{\Delta}

A
semiordinal
is a semicategory associated with a total strict order relation. In particular, the relation is not reflexive, so there are no identity morphisms.
The category of finite non-empty semiordinals is naturally identified with the semisimplex category
\Delta_{+}
(i.e. the simplex category
\Delta
restricted to its monomorphisms), as the lack of identities allows only monomorphisms between semiordinals.
Thus, a
relative semiordinal
is a semiordinal equipped with a wide subsemicategory.
The category
\underline{\Delta}
is defined as the category of finite non-empty relative semiordinals and marking-preserving (i.e. colour-preserving in
[
Koc06
]
) functors between them.

Remark 3.1
.

Ordinals can also be described as the free categories on linearly ordered graphs.
Similarly, relative semiordinals are the free relative semicategories on linearly ordered relative graphs.
We will make this precise in
Section

4
.

Since
\underline{\Delta}
is characterised here as a full subcategory of
\RelSemiCat
, this description induces a fully faithful functor
\mathsf{rso}\colon\underline{\Delta}\hookrightarrow\RelSemiCat
.
This helps with intuition as we can represent objects of
\underline{\Delta}
as a string of arrows, some of which are marked, and maps as inclusions that respect the marking.
For instance, in
\underline{\Delta}
we have the usual semiordinals

\langle\bullet\rangle,\quad\langle\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\rangle,\quad\langle\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\rangle,\quad\langle\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\rangle,\quad\ldots

(4)

as well as partially marked semiordinals

\langle\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\rangle\quad\text{or}\quad\langle\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\rangle

(5)

and fully marked semiordinals

\langle\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\rangle,\quad\langle\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\rangle,\quad\langle\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\rangle,\quad\ldots,

(6)

where the red arrows encode the marking.
Morphisms between them are strictly monotone injections that map red arrows to red arrows.
This is a very intuitive way to think about
\underline{\Delta}
, but it is not the most practical for computations.

Epimorphisms in
\Delta

We now introduce the primary definition of
\underline{\Delta}
used in this paper.
This characterisation is more suitable for our purposes and studies.
Henceforth, we will use the notation
\underline{\Delta}
exclusively to refer to the category described in this section.

The category
\underline{\Delta}
is defined as
(\Delta^{\to}_{-})_{\Mono}
, where
\Delta_{-}
is the restriction of the simplex category to its epimorphisms.
Therefore, the objects are epimorphisms
\eta\colon[m]\twoheadrightarrow[n]
in
\Delta
, i.e. composition of degeneracies, and morphisms
f\colon\eta_{0}\to\eta_{1}
are commutative squares in
\Delta

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[m_{0}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[m_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[n_{0}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[n_{1}]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{f}}

(7)

whose vertical arrows are epimorphisms and whose horizontal top arrow is a monomorphism.
Intuitively, the domain of the epimorphisms corresponds to the length of the semiordinal and the marked edges are the ones that are sent to the identity.
For instance, objects as in (
5
) can be represented diagrammatically by the epimorphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}

where a marked edge is collapsed to a single vertex.

Remark 3.2
.

Given a map
f\colon\eta_{0}\to\eta_{1}
in
\underline{\Delta}
, as in (
7
) above, the bottom morphism
\underline{f}
is entirely determined by
\overline{f}
,
\eta_{1}
and any section
s
of
\eta_{0}
.
Indeed, we have
\underline{f}=\eta_{1}\overline{f}s
.

The restriction of the domain and codomain functors provides projections

\overline{\pi}\colon\underline{\Delta}\to\Delta_{+}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{\pi}\colon\underline{\Delta}\to\Delta,

respectively.
The functor
\underline{\pi}
is significant as it induces a Dwyer-Kan equivalence between the homotopical categories
(\Delta,\Iso)
and
(\underline{\Delta},\mathcal{V})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the class of morphisms mapped to identities in
\Delta
by
\underline{\pi}

[
Sat17
]
.
In addition,
\underline{\pi}
is an
ambifibration
, which endows it with some nice lifting properties.
The literature on ambifibrations is quite limited but, for more details, we refer to
[
CSZ26
, Section 5.3]
and to the unpublished note
[
Sat17
]
or to Kock’s comment on
n
-Category Café
[
Koc10
]
.

There is a ‘horizontal’ inclusion

(\mathord{\hskip 1.07639pt\underline{\hskip 4.30554pt}\hskip 1.07639pt})^{\flat}\colon\Delta_{+}\hookrightarrow\underline{\Delta},

which maps a semiordinal
[n]
to the corresponding identity morphism
\id_{[n]}
and a monomorphism
[n]\hookrightarrow[n^{\prime}]
to the evident square.
The objects in the image of the horizontal inclusion intuitively correspond to the relative semiordinals with nothing marked. The composite

\Delta_{+}\xhookrightarrow{(\mathord{\hskip 0.75346pt\underline{\hskip 3.01389pt}\hskip 0.75346pt})^{\flat}}\underline{\Delta}{\mathrel{\to}^{\underline{\pi}}}\Delta

factors the canonical inclusion
\Delta_{+}\hookrightarrow\Delta
.
It is in this setting that one may consider
\underline{\Delta}
as a direct replacement of
\Delta
.
Indeed,
\underline{\Delta}
is a direct category and
(\underline{\Delta},\mathcal{V})
has the desired structure since
\underline{\pi}
induces a Dwyer-Kan equivalence with
\Delta
.
This former property is of particular relevance in the context of HoTT-related frameworks, as it allows us to inductively construct the type of presheaves over the direct category, as discussed in
Section

1.2
.
We also have a ‘vertical’ inclusion

(\mathord{\hskip 1.07639pt\underline{\hskip 4.30554pt}\hskip 1.07639pt})^{\sharp}\colon\Delta_{+}\hookrightarrow\underline{\Delta}

which maps a semiordinal
[n]
to the canonical map
[n]\twoheadrightarrow[0]
and a monomorphism
[n]\hookrightarrow[n^{\prime}]
to the obvious square with the bottom map being the identity
\id_{[0]}
.
The objects in the image of this inclusion intuitively correspond to the relative semiordinals with everything marked.

Active and inert morphisms

As we will show in
4.24
and
4.25
using the theory of monads with arities and by giving a direct proof, the category
\underline{\Delta}
also possesses a special orthogonal factorisation system
(\underline{\Delta}_{a},\underline{\Delta}_{0})
called
active-inert
.
This type of factorisation system usually appears in the context of the
Segal conditions
and
nerve functors
as it makes it possible to express the Segal conditions in terms of a restriction along the right class
[
BMW12
]
.
This is thus an important tool to have to study higher categories. It was first introduced as a general abstract notion in category theory by M. Weber in
[
Web04
,
Web07
]
after deriving ideas from A. Joyal and C. Berger
[
Joy86
,
Ber02
]
. It was further studied by C. Berger, P.A. Melliès and M. Weber
[
BMW12
]
, and was then called
generic-free
.
The more recent terminology active-inert is due to J. Lurie
[
Lur17
]
.
This new terminology has been extensively used in recent publications
[
CH21
,
Ber22
,
HK24
,
Sha22
]
and especially in the context of
decomposition spaces
in
[
GKT18
]
and follow-ups.
In the simplicial case, the active and inert morphisms have a very neat characterisation: they correspond to endpoint and distance-preserving maps, respectively.
In
\underline{\Delta}
the characterisation is similar, with the difference that the maps respect the marking.

Fat Delta via monads with arities

In this section, we use the theory of monads with arities extensively.
We begin by constructing the semicategory monad
\mathtt{f}
and the free relative semicategory monad
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
on the categories of graphs and relative graphs, respectively.
We show that
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
is strongly cartesian by using the result that
\mathtt{f}
is also strongly cartesian.
Following this, we develop the category of arities for
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
and make precise its connection to
\underline{\Delta}
.

The free semicategory monad

We denote by
\Graph
the category of

(also known as quivers) and graph morphisms.
More precisely,
\Graph

, see
[

.
Since we have a fully faithful embedding

, we get a forgetful functor
\mathsf{U}\colon\SemiCat\to\Graph
by mapping a semicategory
\mathcal{C}
to the graph

.
Taking the left Kan extension along the Yoneda embedding we get the free-forgetful adjunction
[

, Proposition 3.2.2]

\mathsf{F}\colon\Graph\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup\cr\kern-1.29167pt\cr\begin{picture}\roundcap\polyline(0,0)(1,0)
\polyline(0.5,0)(0.5,1)
\end{picture}\cr\kern 0.86108pt\cr\leftharpoondown\joinrel\relbar\cr}}}\SemiCat\penalty\mspace{6muplus1mu}{:}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu\mathpunct{}\mspace{2mu}\mathsf{U}.

An explicit description of the functor

is given by the

of a graph.
That is, for any graph

Suppose that

Remark 4.2

Let

The free relative semicategory monad

The category of
relative graphs

\RelGraph
consists of directed graphs

equipped with a wide subgraph, represented by a bijective-on-objects monomorphism

In other words, the category

corresponds to the full subcategory

\mathsf{bo}
denotes the class of bijective-on-objects monomorphisms.

Remark 4.6
.

The category

This category consists of

and a morphism

Remark 4.7
.

Similarly to

2.1
, we have a quadruple adjunction

As a consequence of

induces a monad on

The arities of the free relative semicategory monad

In this section, we construct the category of canonical arities
\mathsf{Ar}_{\mathscr{A}}(\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}})
for the free relative semicategory monad
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
.
Before doing this, we introduce a particular dense generator
\mathscr{A}
and give some intuition for our choice.

Intuition for
\mathscr{A}

Thanks to
Theorem

2.12
, we know that
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
has (canonical) arities, which depend on the chosen dense generator.
For both categories and semicategories, the smallest dense generator of
\Graph
is taken for the construction of the canonical arities. For
\underline{\Delta}
, we need to consider a non-trivial dense generator.
Indeed, if we take the smallest dense generator of
\RelGraph
(the full subcategory generated by
[0]
and
[1]^{\sigma}
, with
\sigma\in\{\flat,\sharp\}
) then, by
Proposition

4.12
, the canonical arities correspond to the full subcategory of linearly ordered graphs
[0]
and
[n]^{\sigma}
, for
n>0
, that are either fully marked or unmarked.
This only captures a subcategory of
\underline{\Delta}
, therefore one needs to refine the dense generator to include the partially marked objects.
The dense subcategory which we consider is the full subcategory
\mathscr{A}
of alternatingly marked linearly ordered graphs, and we motivate this choice from two perspectives.

The first perspective comes from the
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
-generic factorisations and the description of
\underline{\Delta}
as (finite non-empty) relative semiordinals.

Proposition

4.12
shows that maps
[1]^{\sigma}\to\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}\mathtt{1}
factor generically through
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}[n]^{\sigma}
where
[n]^{\sigma}
is a fully (un)marked string of some length
n
.
Consequently, any relative graphs with consecutive edges of the same marking can be seen as being generated from
[1]^{\sigma}
by
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
.

An arbitrary relative semiordinal
\eta
is a sequence of fully marked and unmarked strings of any length, where the marking of the strings alternates.
Each of these strings can be seen as being generated from
[1]^{\sigma}
by
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
.
Therefore, one can expect
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
to build
\eta
from a sequence of
[1]^{\sigma}
, where
\sigma
corresponds to the marking of the string.
This reasoning leads to the choice of the full subcategory of those linearly ordered graphs with no two adjacent edges of the same marking, which we denote by
\mathscr{A}
.

With an eye on a future study of the coherences of weak identity structures in higher categories, we would like to interpret the marked morphisms in relative semicategories as weak identities and morphisms between marked strings as coherences.
The simplest and minimal identity coherences for categories are the left and right neutrality, which can be represented by morphisms between
[1]^{\flat}
and marked linearly ordered graphs as in (
5

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overset{\rotatebox{-90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overset{\rotatebox{-90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overset{\rotatebox{-90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underset{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underset{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underset{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\langle$}}{\bullet}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\scriptstyle{\id f}}

Therefore, to extend (

To maintain simplicity and minimality of the coherences, we only add edges in an alternating marking pattern.
The more complex ones are then constructed as the canonical arities using
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
.
For instance, for a string of length

\displaystyle\langle\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\longrightarrow}}\bullet\longrightarrow\bullet\rangle,

of

Most importantly, this category is dense in

As should be clear from (

It follows from the decomposition

Remark 4.17
.

Let

be a graph morphism of linear graphs. Writing

and
\mathsf{t}
for the source and target maps, and

and
g_{1}
for the object and morphism parts of

\mathsf{t}g_{1}=g_{0}\mathsf{t}
and
\mathsf{s}g_{1}=g_{0}\mathsf{s}
.
Let

denote the

g_{0}(i+1)=g_{0}\mathsf{t}(e_{i})=\mathsf{t}g_{1}(e_{i})

Since

is an edge of length
1
from

we get that
g_{0}(i+1)=g_{0}(i)+1

In particular, morphisms of linear graphs are distance preserving and monomorphisms.
This property extends to the relative case, as the graph morphisms are the same with the additional requirement that they preserve marked edges.

As

Main results from monads with arities

We have seen that
\underline{\Delta}_{0}
is isomorphic to the category of arities of the monad
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
, and from now on we will say that the monad
\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}
has arities
\underline{\Delta}_{0}
.
We will now show that the theory

associated with

(see

\underline{\Delta}
.
Consider the composition of the functors

The category

is defined by factoring this composition into a bijective-on-objects functor followed by a fully faithful functor.
The inclusion

is the identity-on-objects as
\underline{\Delta}_{0}
is a wide subcategory of
\underline{\Delta}
.

Lemma 4.21
.

The canonical functor
\mathscr{i}\colon\underline{\Delta}\to\RelSemiCat
is fully faithful.

Proof.

The functor
\mathscr{i}
maps an object

to the linear semicategory

equipped with the wide subsemicategory of non-trivial edges that are sent to identities by
\eta

Thus, the induced map on morphisms

to the top arrow

.
This is well-defined, as the commutativity of the square

preserves the marking of the edges and, since

is a monomorphism, it restricts to linear semicategories.
The fact that

.
∎

In particular, the composition
\underline{\Delta}_{0}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{j}}\underline{\Delta}\xhookrightarrow{\mathscr{i}}\RelSemiCat
factors

as a bijective-on-objects followed by a fully faithful functor.
Therefore, we have the following result:

Theorem 4.22
.

The category
\Theta_{\mathtt{f}^{\mathsf{r}}}
is isomorphic to
\underline{\Delta}
.∎

Since

corresponds to the category of free relative semicategories over the category of relative linear graphs, the theory associated with

is the category of non-empty finite relative semiordinals.

]
and

can either be described in terms of relative semiordinals or epimorphisms of

.

The main result of this section, the nerve theorem for

.
