Secondary terms in the counting functions of quartic fields I
Arul Shankar, Jacob Tsimerman

Abstract
We prove that the smoothed counting function of the set of quartic fields, satisfying any finite set of local conditions, can be written as a linear combination of X , X 5 / 6 ​ log ⁡ X , X 5 / 6 X,X^{5/6}\log X,X^{5/6} , upto an error term of O ⁡ ( X 13 / 16 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) O(X^{13/16+o(1)}) . For certain sets of local conditions, namely, those cutting out “ S 4 S_{4} -families” of quartic fields, we explicitly determine the leading constants of the secondary terms. We moreover express these constants in terms of secondary mass formulas associated to families of quartic fields.



In our proof, we introduce a new method to count integer orbits on representations of reductive groups, one which allows for the recovery of lower order terms. This new method contains aspects of the tools used by Sato–Shintani to analyze zeta functions associated to prehomogeneous vector spaces and the geometry-of-numbers techniques pioneered by Bhargava.

2.1
Proof Strategy : Theorem
1

Our proof of Theorem
1
relies heavily on the formalism of Shintani zeta functions
[
26
]
. Using this formalism, one immediately gets an asymptotic expansion for the count of all
rings
satisfying any finite number of local conditions. We then follow the standard strategy of sieving down from rings to fields by imposing maximality conditions at more and more primes. Specifically, we have:

N_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)=\sum_{n}\mu(n)N_{\Sigma\cap\Sigma^{\rm nmax}_{n}}(\psi;X)

where
\Sigma^{\rm nmax}_{n}
is the set of conditions insisting that our ring is non-maximal at primes dividing
n
.
There are four ingredients needed to carry out this sieve:

1.

For
n
small, we need a power series expansion for
N_{\Sigma\cap\Sigma_{n}^{\rm nmax}}(\psi;X)
, recovering the first and second terms, with good
n
-dependence on the error term.

2.

For
n
large, we need good uniformity estimates for the number of triples with bounded discriminant, which are non-maximal at all primes dividing
n
.

3.

We need to prove that the leading constants of the possible second main terms
c_{n}^{\prime}X^{5/6}\log X
and
c_{n}X^{5/6}
of
N_{\Sigma\cap\Sigma_{n}^{\rm nmax}}(\psi;X)
, when summed over
n
, to converge.

4.

We need to prove that the contributions of the possible lower order terms
d_{n}^{\prime}X^{3/4}\log X
and
d_{n}X^{3/4}
of
N_{\Sigma\cap\Sigma_{n}^{\rm nmax}}(\psi;X)
, when summed over
n
, to not dominate the secondary term of size
X^{5/6}
.

The first problem is made more difficult in this setting than in that of cubic rings because the dimension of the corresponding prehomogeneous vector spaces jumps from
3
to
12
. In the language of geometry-of-numbers, this means that we can only take
n
up to
X^{\frac{1}{24}}
before our balls don’t ‘fit’, instead of up to
X^{\frac{1}{8}}
in the cubic case. In the language of Shintani zeta functions, this shows up in the discriminant factor gaining a factor of
n^{24}
instead of
n^{8}
in the functional equation.

In the previous execution of this strategy in the cubic case
[
10
,
29
]
, one optimized the error in these methods by combining bounds on Fourier transforms of (non)maximal congruence conditions, together with a uniformity estimate of
\frac{X}{n^{2}}
derived from studying cubic rings directly. An issue that arises is that the congruence condition defining non-maximality is quite complicated and has a large Fourier transform. We improve on this method by breaking the non-maximal congruence condition at
p
into two (see §
10
for more details). Loosely speaking these are:

•

Those that correspond to being non-maximal of index
p
, and

•

Everything else

The trick is that the first function has a very small Fourier transform, whereas the second has a better uniformity estimate of
\frac{X}{p^{4}}
. Combining these makes the method work.

To solve the second problem of bounding the constant terms
c_{n}
, we simply use our asymptotic formula and plug in an optimal value of
X
. Combined with the improvements coming from the splitting derived above, this ends up being strong enough to make the sum converge.

Remark 5

We use the language of
Switching Correspondences
analogously to the authors previous paper with Bhargava
[
10
]
where we broke up the non-maximal condition
precisely
into two simpler ones induced by correspondences. In the quartic case, while it is possible (though non-trivial, at least to the authors!) to break up the non-maximal conditions into a large sum of functions corresponding to ‘switching’, doing so introduces large coefficients (both positive and negative, of course) which ends up making it difficult to control the error. Hence, we use this less precise version, where we use exactly one switching correspondence, and treat the (multi) set of remaining nonmaximal quartic fields as an error term to be bounded.

2.2
Proof Strategy: Counting

If one wants to obtain actual asymptotics as opposed to just proving the existence of an asymptotic, one needs to actually compute the residues of the Shintani zeta function. Shintani’s methods produce mysterious terms that are difficult to get a handle on. Instead, we use geometry of numbers methods to count in a fundamental domain directly, and derive a precise asymptotic up to an
o(X^{5/6})
. Note that this yields a count for triples
(Q,C,r)
, not for fields. However, for this approach one may impose finitely many congruence condition without making the problem any more difficult. Therefore, we use this method to compute the residues of the Shintani zeta function (to the right of 5/6), and then we turn around and plug these values into the sieving formalism described above.

As in Bhargava’s work
[
5
]
, we start with needing to evaluate an integral of the form

\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\#(L\cap g{\mathcal{B}})^{\Delta<X}dg,

for a fundamental domain
{\mathcal{F}}
for the action of
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\times{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
on
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})\times{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, where
L\subset V({\mathbb{Z}})
is an
S_{4}
-set defined by finitely many congruence conditions, and
{\mathcal{B}}
is a (in our case, a smoothed out) ball. Bhargava approximates the main term of this integral via the following procedure.

•

For
g
in the ‘main body’, one may simply approximate the number of points by the volume.

•

For
g
near cusps the box becomes skewed, and these contributions are bounded using Davenports lemma.

•

For
g
deep in the cusp, enough ‘co-ordinates’ are forced to become 0 (by virtue of being integers) that the lattice points represent non
S_{4}
-rings and this region can be discarded.

For our purposes, since we need higher order terms, we must compute the number of points in skewed balls precisely. We proceed as follows:

•

In each box, we divide the coordinates into one of three ranges: zero, small, and large.

•

For the coefficients whose ranges are large, we may approximate their contribution by the volume in that direction, and ‘project’ onto the remaining coefficients

•

For the coordinates whose ranges are small, we do not approximate at all, and instead record the sum over these coordinates as a Mellin integral over an appropriate zeta function (possibly in several variables).

•

We then combine the zeta integrals with the integral over the group to obtain higher-dimensional integrals with polar divisors, where certain polar contributions constitute the higher-order poles of the Shintani zeta function.

•

Using the Iwasawa (
NAK
) decomposition, it turns out that only the toric contribution needs to be kept. The compact
K
can be discarded by making our box
K
-invariant, and the unipotent
N
only ever adds coordinates with zero range to other coordinates (not affecting the count), or coordinates with small ranges to ones with large range, not affecting the projection. This allows for a clean analysis of polar divisors.

•

We use the Shintani formalism to rule out potential polar contributions at values of
s
ruled out by the Bernstein polynomial.

Remark 6

1.

In principle, our procedure should allows us to compute the residues without requiring the
S_{4}
-condition. However, this assumption simplifies our task greatly as it allows us not to venture into certain regions of the cusp (as explained later, these regions are:
a_{11}=b_{11}=0
and
\det A=0
). This cuts down the number of possibilities and cusps we have to consider. We expect that our method can be used to tackle the question of smoothly counting all quartic fields, but this requires going deeper into the cusp. We undertake this in forthcoming work.

2.

While we use the Shintani formalism to rule out certain poles, our method also rules out certain poles that are theoretically permitted by the Shintani formalism. In particular, for
S_{4}
-families, we show that there is only a simple pole at
s=5/6
, as opposed to a double pole.

Outline of the paper

In Part II we set up the main tools used in the paper. First, in §3, we review the parametrization setup for the prehomogeneous space corresponding to triples
(Q,C,r)
, the corresponding Shintani Zeta functions, and our algebraic groups. Section 4 introduces the Mellin transform and describes how we use it to translate questions about counting points smoothly to zeta functions. We also introduce convenient notation for zeta functions in multiple variables to streamline arguments we make such as ‘projecting away’ large variables. It is important to do this carefully, as we can pick up the behavior of the smooth count as a variable transitions from a large range to a small range via certain residues of an appropriate zeta function. In §5 we set up the “global zeta integral” formalism, which allows us to compute the residues of Shintani zeta functions from smooth counts.

In Part III we execute the count. §6 and §7 are similar, dealing with different parts of the integral corresponding to whether or not we are near the
{\rm SL}_{2}
-cusp. The second main term ends up coming from the cusp regions studied in §7, and we obtain it as a special-value of a different Shintani zeta function corresponding to symmetric 3
\times
3 matrices. In §8 we compute this special value using work of Ibukiyama–Sato. We also re-express the answer in language of rings, and compute it explicitly for maximal orders. This computation is merely to get an explicit answer, and we do not have any special insight about the final answer. Having done this, Theorem
3
follows.

In Part IV we execute the sieve. This part black-boxes the results in Part III completely. We begin in §10 by recalling Bhargava’s results on non-maximal quartic rings, and also develop the “switching correspondences” that we need. Then in §11, we prove uniformity estimates on various types of non-maximal rings, relying on work of Bhargava and Nakagawa. We also use heavily the functional equation of the Shintani zeta function, and as such need estimates on the
Fourier transforms
of the characteristic functions of non-maximal elements. Here we use the work of Hough

. Finally in §12 we execute the sieve, which allows us to prove Theorems

, can be naturally identified with the set of pairs of integral

symmetric matrices. For any ring

Let

\widehat{\phi}(y):=\frac{1}{N^{12}}\sum_{x\in V({\mathbb{Z}}/N{\mathbb{Z}})}e\Big(\frac{[x,y]}{N}\Big)\phi(x).

We will note for future use that the Fourier transform of

\widehat{g\phi}=g^{-T}\widehat{\phi}

4-2i

The functions

4
Introducing the counting tools

In this section, we set up the notation and preliminary results for the techniques needed for the proof of Theorem
2
; specifically, results on multiple zeta functions, counting points with Poisson summation, and the Mellin transform.

4.1
Signed multiple zeta functions

We say
f:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a periodic function if it is defined by congruence conditions modulo some positive integer. We use
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
to denote the coordinates on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Let
t\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Writing
\vec{s}
for
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, we define the multiple zeta function
\zeta_{f,t}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
associated to
f
and
t
by

\zeta_{f,t}(\vec{s}):=\sum_{t\cdot\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{n}}f(\vec{a})\prod_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|^{-s_{i}}.

Note that since
f
is periodic, its values are absolutely bounded. Hence
\zeta_{f,t}(\vec{s})
converges absolutely for
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with
{\rm Re}(s_{i})>1
for each
i
.

Definition 4.1

Let
S,T\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be disjoint subsets. Denote the complement of
S\cup T
by
R
. For each element
v\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{T}
we define
f_{S;T}(v)
to be the average value of

f
on the set
\{0_{S}\}\times\{v\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{R}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Here, by
\{0_{S}\}\times\{v\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{R}
, we mean the subset of elements
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
such that
a_{i}(w)=0
for
i\in S
and
a_{i}(w)=a_{i}(v)
for
i\in T
. For
t\in\{\pm 1\}^{T}
and
s_{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{T}
, we then define

\zeta_{f,t}(S=0;T)(s_{T}):=\sum_{t\cdot\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{T}}f_{S,T}(\vec{a})\prod_{i\in T}|a_{i}|^{-s_{i}}.

By convention, we will write
\zeta_{f,t}(T)(s_{T})
for
\zeta_{f,t}(\emptyset=0;T)(s_{T})
when
S
is empty. When
T=S^{c}
, we write
\zeta_{f,t}(S=0)(s_{T})
for
\zeta_{f,t}(S=0;S^{c})(s_{T})
. Note that
\zeta_{f,t}(S=0;\emptyset)
is simply a complex number, namely, the density of
f
on the set
\{0_{S}\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{S^{c}}
. We will denote this density by
\nu(f|_{S})
. If
L
is a set whose characteristic function
\chi_{L}
is periodic we shall write
\zeta_{L}
for
\zeta_{\chi_{L}}
and
\nu(L|_{S})
for
\nu(\chi_{L}|_{S})
. We define
\zeta_{f}(S=0;T)
to be the vector indexed by
(\pm 1)^{T}.

We shall need the following results.

Lemma 4.2

Let
f:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{C}}
be a periodic function. Then we have

\zeta_{f,t_{0}}(S=0;T)(s_{T})=(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{t{\rightarrow}t_{0}}\zeta_{f,t}(S\cup T)(0_{S}\times s_{T}),

where
t_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{T}
and the sum is over every
t\in\{\pm 1\}^{S\cup T}
agreeing with
t_{0}
in all the
T
-coordinates.

Proof:

We may replace
R
by
\emptyset
and
f
by
f_{S,T}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{S\cup T}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{C}}
without changing either side of the equation. We thus assume that
S\cup T=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Since both sides are linear in
f
, we may also assume
f
is a product of separate functions in each coordinate, which reduces us to case
T=\emptyset
and
S=\{1\}
.

We may thus assume that
f
is the characteristic function of numbers congruent to
a
modulo
n
. If
\gcd(a,n)
is not 1 then we may divide both sides of the equation by
\gcd(a,n)
, reducing to the case when
\gcd(a,n)=1
. If
a=n=1
, then the LHS is the constant
1
and the RHS is
(-1)(\zeta(0)+\zeta(0)
. The claim then follows from
\zeta(0)=-\frac{1}{2}
. If
n>1
, then the left hand side is the constant
1/n
. To evaluate the right hand side, note that
\zeta_{f,t}(s)
is a linear combination of
L
-functions
L(\chi,s)
, where every
\chi
is an even Dirichlet
character. The statement follows since for even nontrivial characters
\chi
, we have
L(0,\chi)=0
(as can be seen from the functional equation, for example). Hence the only contribution is from the trivial character, which is weighted by
1/n
as required.

\Box

Next we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3

If
1\not\in S\cup T
, then

\zeta_{f,\overline{t}}(S=0;T)={\rm Res}_{s_{1}=1}\zeta_{f,t}(S=0;T\cup{1}).

Proof:

We have

\displaystyle{\rm Res}_{s_{1}=1}\zeta_{f,t}(S=0;T\cup\{1\})(s_{T},s_{1})

\displaystyle={\rm Res}_{s_{1}=1}\sum_{t\cdot\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{T}}\prod_{i\in T}|a_{i}|^{-s_{i}}\sum_{m>0}\mu_{S,T\cup\{1\}}(\vec{a}\times\{m\})|m|^{-s_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\neq 0}^{T}}\prod_{i\in T}|a_{i}|^{-s_{i}}\cdot f_{S,T}(\vec{a})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{f,\overline{t}}(S=0;T)

as desired, where we have used the fact that the natural density of the function
\mu_{S,T\cup\{1\}}(\vec{a}\times\{m\})
over
m>0
is
f_{S,T}(\vec{a})
.

\Box

4.2
Counting points smoothly using Poisson summation

It is a well-known fact in analysis that smooth point counts (as opposed to sharp counts) can be evaluated with a super-polynomial error term. We prove a version of this which is applicable to our setting.

Let
f
be a periodic function on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
of modulus
Q
, and let
{\mathcal{B}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a fixed smooth function of compact support. Let
g=ud\in{\rm GL}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
be a upper triangular matrix with positive diagonal entries, where
u
is unipotent with entries of size
O(1)
, and
d
is diagonal. We further assume that the diagonal entries are non-decreasing. We use the standard inner product on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, and recall the transformation formula for the Fourier transform:

\widehat{g{\mathcal{B}}}(y)=\det(g)(g^{-T}\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}})(y).

For
1\leq r\leq s\leq n
we define
g_{r,s}
to be the induced action on
{\mathbb{R}}^{s-r}
thought of the sub-quotient of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where we restrict to the subspace
\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\rangle
and quotient out by the vector space
\langle e_{1}\dots,e_{r}\rangle.
We similarly consider
B_{r,s}
as a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{s-r}
by first restricting to the subspace
\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\rangle
and then projecting to the quotient space. We write
f_{r,s}
to mean
f_{R,S}
in the notation of the previous section, for
R=\{1,\dots,r\}
and
S=\{s+1,\dots,n\}
.

We shall often use the following theorem to simplify our smooth counts by projecting away the variables which get ‘stretched’, and restricting to
0
those variables which get ‘compressed’.

Theorem 4.4

Let the notation be as above, with
f,B
fixed and all other parameters varying. Suppose for some parameter
Y
we have
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq Y
and
d_{s+1},\dots,d_{n}\leq Y^{-1}
. Then

\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}(gB)(\ell)f(\ell)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\sum_{\ell_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s-r}}(g_{r,s}B_{r,s})(\ell_{0})f_{r,s}(\ell_{0})+O_{A}(Y^{-A}).

Proof:

Since
B
has compact support, for
Y\gg 1
we see that
gB(\ell)\neq 0
implies that
\ell\subset\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\rangle
. Hence we may restrict the sum to that subspace, which we denote
{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
. We restrict and apply Poisson summation:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}(gB)(\ell)f(\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}}(gB)(\ell)f(\ell)

\displaystyle=\det(g)M^{-1}\sum_{\ell^{*}\in Q^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}}(g^{-T}\hat{B})(\ell^{*})\hat{f}(\ell^{*})

\displaystyle=\det(g)M^{-1}\sum_{\ell^{*}\in Q^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}}(\hat{B})(d^{T}u^{T}\ell^{*})\hat{f}(\ell^{*}).

Now since
B
is smooth of compact support we see have that
\hat{B}(\ell)=O_{A}(|\ell|^{-A})
. It follows that the contribution of all the terms in the above sum which are not contained in
\langle e^{*}_{r+1},\dots,e^{*}_{s}\rangle
is
O_{A}(Y^{-A})
. Next, note that for
\ell^{*}\in\langle e^{*}_{r+1},\dots,e^{*}_{s}\rangle
we have

\hat{B}(\ell^{*})=\widehat{B_{r,s}}(\overline{\ell^{*}}),\hat{f}(\ell^{*})=\widehat{f_{r,s}}(\overline{\ell^{*}})
. Hence, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}(gB)(\ell)f(\ell)

\displaystyle=\det(g)M^{-1}\sum_{\ell^{*}\in Q^{-1}\langle e^{*}_{r+1},\dots,e^{*}_{s}\rangle}(\hat{B})(d^{T}u^{T}\ell^{*})\hat{f}(\ell^{*})+O_{A}(Y^{-A})

\displaystyle=\det(g)M^{-1}\sum_{\ell_{0}^{*}\in Q^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{s-r}}(\hat{B_{r,s}})(g^{T}\ell_{0}^{*})\widehat{f_{r,s}}(\ell_{0}^{*})+O_{A}(Y^{-A})

\displaystyle=\det(g)\det(g_{r,s}^{-1})\sum_{\ell_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s-r}}(g_{r,s}B_{r,s})(\ell_{0})f_{r,s}(\ell_{0})+O_{A}(Y^{-A}),

which completes the proof.

\Box

4.3
The Mellin transform

We shall heavily employ the Mellin transform, often in many variables at once. To this end, recall that if
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a function on the positive real line, we define its Mellin transform
\widetilde{f}(s)
via

\widetilde{f}(s)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}f(x)|x|^{s}d^{\times}x

when the integral converges. The integral will converge in a strip
{\rm Re}(a)<c<{\rm Re}(b)
, and for any such
c
we have the Mellin inversion formula:

f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re}(s)=c}\widetilde{f}(s)x^{-s}ds=\int_{c}\widetilde{f}(s)x^{-s}ds,

where
\int_{c}
denotes
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Res}(s)=c}
.
We also recall the following facts:

1.

The Mellin transform satisfies the identity
\widetilde{x^{a}f(x)}(s)=\widetilde{f}(s+a)
for
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.

For differentiable functions
f
, we have
\widetilde{f^{\prime}}(s+1)=-s\widetilde{f}(s)
.

3.

If
f
is a smooth function, then
\widetilde{f}(s)
has super polynomial decay along vertical strips, uniformly in any compact region in
(a,b)
.

4.

If
f
has compact support on
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, then
\widetilde{f}
is entire.

5.

We have the special value
\tilde{f}(1)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}f(x)dx=\hat{f}(0)
.

Suppose now that
\lim_{x\to 0}

f(x)
exists. Then the Mellin transform can have poles.

Lemma 4.5

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function with bounded support. Then
\widetilde{f}(s)
has an analytic continuation to the entire complex plane with at most simple poles at
\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
. Moreover, we have
{\rm Res}_{s=0}\widetilde{f}(s)=f(0)
.

Proof:

It is clear that the integral defining
\widetilde{f}
converges for
{\rm Re}(s)>0
. In fact, the same is true for all the derivatives
f^{(n)}
of
f
. Point 2 above gives a functional equation for
\widetilde{f}
, in terms of
\widetilde{f^{(n)}}
, allowing analytic continuation to all of
{\mathbb{C}}
, with at most simple poles at
0
and the negative integers. Finally, we compute the residue of
\widetilde{f}
at
0
to be

{\rm Res}_{s=0}\widetilde{f}=\lim_{s\to 0}s\widetilde{f}(s)=-\widetilde{f^{\prime}}(1)=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}f^{\prime}(x)dx=f(0),

as desired.

\Box

We shall require the following lemmas about the functions
\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}(s_{1},s_{2})
and
\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}(t)

Lemma 4.6

•

The Mellin transform
\widetilde{\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}}(v_{1},w_{1})
is holomorphic except for poles at
v_{1}=0,w_{1}=0
. The function
\min(1,|v_{1}|)\min(1,|v_{2}|)\widetilde{\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}}(v_{1},w_{1})
is bounded on any right half-plane.

•

The Mellin transform
\widetilde{\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}}(t)
is holomorphic except for poles at
t=0
. The function
\min(1,|t|)\widetilde{\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}}(t)
is bounded on any right half-plane.

Proof:

We only handle the case of
{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
as the other case is handled the same way and is easier. Note that we may write
\delta_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}(s_{1},s_{2})=C\delta_{s_{1}>C_{1}}\delta_{s_{2}>C_{2}}+R_{1}(s_{1})\delta_{s_{1}>C_{1}}+R_{2}(s_{2})\delta_{s_{2}>C_{2}}+H(s_{1},s_{2})
where
R_{1},R_{2},H
are compactly supported functions away from
0
(for either of the co-ordinates) and constants
C,C_{1},C_{2}
with
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. It is clear that
\tilde{R_{i}}
is holomorphic everywhere and bounded on right half-planes. The theorem now follows from the fact that
\tilde{\delta}_{s_{1}>C_{1}}(s)=\frac{C_{1}^{-s}}{s}.

\Box

4.4
Counting points smoothly using the Mellin transform

We will use the Mellin transform in order to sum smooth functions over integer points. We will first do an example in the one variable case.
Suppose
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function with compact support, and that we would like to evaluate
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\phi(n)
.
As discussed previously, if
\phi
is spread out, then this can be done effectively using Poisson summation. However, if
\phi
is not very well spread out then some subtleties arise which can be difficult to detect with the Fourier transform, and we find the Mellin transform to be much more suitable. We use Mellin inversion to write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}}\phi\Bigl(\frac{n}{X}\Bigr)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\int_{2}\widetilde{\phi}(s)X^{s}n^{-s}ds\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle\int_{2}\zeta(s)\widetilde{\phi}(s)X^{s}ds\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle X\widehat{\phi}(0)+O(X^{o(1)}).\end{array}

In the situation when
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function, and we are summing over all the integers, we can do much better. In fact, we can use this method to recover the same super-polynomial decay obtainable using Poisson summation.
Indeed, define the function
\phi_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\phi_{0}(x):=\phi(x)+\phi(-x)
. Then the poles of
\widetilde{\phi_{0}}
are only at the negative even integers. This can be seen by the fact that the Taylor expansion of
\phi_{0}
is supported on even powers. Then we write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\phi\Bigl(\frac{n}{X}\Bigr)&=&\displaystyle\phi(0)+\sum_{n>0}\phi_{0}\Bigl(\frac{n}{X}\Bigr)\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle\phi(0)+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\int_{2}\tilde{\phi_{0}}(s)X^{s}n^{-s}ds\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle\phi(0)+\sum_{-A<n\leq 1}X^{n}\textrm{Res}_{s=n}(\zeta(s)\widetilde{\phi_{0}}(s))+O_{A}(X^{-A})\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle\phi(0)+X\hat{\phi}(0)+\zeta(0)\phi_{0}(0)+O_{A}(X^{-A})\\[7.22743pt]
&=&\displaystyle X\hat{\phi}(0)+O_{A}(X^{-A}),\end{array}

where we have used the fact that
{\rm Res}_{s=0}\widetilde{\phi_{0}}(s)=\phi_{0}(0),\zeta(0)=-\frac{1}{2}
and
\zeta(n)=0
when
n
is a negative even integer.

When we have a function
B
in
n
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, for each
\vec{t}\in(\pm)^{n}
we set

\tilde{B}_{\vec{t}}(\vec{s})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}B(t_{1}x_{1},\dots,t_{n}x_{n})\prod_{i}x_{i}^{s_{i}}d^{\times}\vec{x}

and
\tilde{B}(\vec{s})
to be the element in
{\mathbb{C}}^{(\pm)^{n}}
whose co-ordinates are

\tilde{B}_{\vec{t}}(\vec{s})
.

Definition 4.7

Given a function
B:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{R}}
and disjoint sets
S,T\subset[n]
we write
B_{S;T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{T}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{R}}
to be the function

B_{S;T}(\vec{s}_{T}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{R}}B(\vec{0}_{S},\vec{s}_{T},\vec{s}_{R})d\vec{s}_{R}

for

. In other words, we restrict the

co-ordinates to be

and integrate over the remaining co-ordinates except for

. We also write

B_{S}
to denote

.

We shall require the following multi-variable version.

Theorem 4.8

Given a smooth bounded function
B
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and a periodic function

on

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\neq 0}^{n}}f(\vec{a})B(\vec{a})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re}(\vec{s})=2}\widetilde{B}(\vec{s})\cdot\zeta_{f}(\vec{s})d\vec{s}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}f(\vec{a})B(\vec{a})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\displaystyle\sum_{S\subset[n]}\int_{{\rm Re}(\vec{s})=2}\widetilde{B_{S}}(\vec{s})\cdot\zeta_{f}(S=0)(\vec{s})d\vec{s}

Proof:

The first claim is a direct application of Mellin inversion. The second claim follows from shifting the integral on the right hand side left, and using Lemma
4.3
.

Finally, the following lemma will be convenient when we begin shifting our integrals and analyzing poles:

Lemma 4.9

Let

which is invariant under negating a subset of the variables, so that

Proof:

Note that a-priori the only potential polar divisors of

are at

are at non-positive integers. Therefore, by symmetry, it is sufficient to prove that there are no poles at
s_{1}=m
for any negative integer

and

. For
\epsilon=\pm 1

so that

Now
B_{+1}
is symmetric around the origin, and hence its taylor series around

only has even coefficients, and so its Mellin transform only has (simple) poles at non-positive even integers. Moreover,

is symmetric around the origin, and so
\zeta_{f_{+1}}(\vec{s})
can be written as a sum of products of Dirichlet L-functions in one variable, with the Dirichlet characters
\chi
that occur of the variable

satisfying
\chi(-1)=1
. So
\zeta_{f_{+1}}(\vec{s})
has zeroes along
s_{1}=m
for an even integer

, since for such

Likewise,

f_{+1}
is anti-symmetric around the origin, and so

can be written as a sum of products of Dirichlet L-functions in one variable, with the

\zeta_{f_{+1}}(\vec{s})

5.1
The global zeta integral

Fix
i\in\{0,1,2\}
, and let
{\mathcal{B}}:V({\mathbb{R}})^{(i)}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function with compact support away from the discriminant zero locus. Let
A_{i}=(i)!(4-2i)!2^{2i}=\#{\rm Aut}({\mathbb{R}}^{2i}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{i})
. Let
N
be a positive integer and let
\phi:V({\mathbb{Z}}/N{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
G({\mathbb{Z}}/N{\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant function. The
global zeta integral

Z({\mathcal{B}},\phi;s)
is defined to be

Z({\mathcal{B}},\phi;s):=\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}(\lambda(g))^{-12s}\Bigl(\sum_{x\in V({\mathbb{Z}})}\phi(x)(g{\mathcal{B}})(x)\Bigr)\nu_{G}(g).

(8)

If
\phi
is the characteristic function of a set
L
, we will use
Z_{L}({\mathcal{B}},s)
to denote
Z({\mathcal{B}},\phi;s)
. The following result, relating global zeta integrals to Shintani zeta functions, is well known (see, for example
[
22
, §5]
).

Proposition 5.1

With notation as above, we have

Z({\mathcal{B}},\phi;s)=A_{i}\xi_{i}(\phi,s)\int_{x\in V({\mathbb{R}})^{(i)}}|\Delta(x)|^{s-1}{\mathcal{B}}(x)\nu_{V}(x).

It is clear from our assumptions on
{\mathcal{B}}
that the integral above gives an entire function in
s
. In the sequel, we will compute the residues of the poles of Shintani zeta functions at
5/6
by computing the residues of the poles of the global zeta integrals.

5.2
Expressing residues in terms of point counts

Let
i\in\{0,1,2\}
be fixed, let
N
be a positive integer, and let
\phi:V({\mathbb{Z}}/N{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
G({\mathbb{Z}}/N{\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant function. Assume that the lift of
\phi
to

is the characteristic function of a set corresponding to an

-family of rings. The counting results of Bhargava
[

at

Over the next few sections, we will prove that

has a simple pole at

We simplify our notation by working with
\xi_{i,L}(s)
, where

is the support of

. Let
{\mathcal{B}}:V({\mathbb{R}})^{(i)}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth
K
-invariant function with compact support away from the discriminant zero locus.
First note that by Proposition

, the poles of

are determined by the poles of the global zeta integral

.
Let

be a smooth function with compact support away from

. Since

is meromorphic away from a possible simple pole at

and possible double poles at

and

\int_{2}Z_{L}({\mathcal{B}},s)\widetilde{\psi}(12s)X^{s}ds=C_{1}X+C^{\prime}X^{5/6}\log X+C_{5/6}X^{5/6}+O(X^{3/4+\epsilon}),

(9)

where

,

are constants. Moreover, if

(which we will later prove to be the case), then the

. In this situation, the constants

and

are given by

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}C_{1}&=&\widetilde{\psi}(12){\rm Res}_{s=1}Z_{L}({\mathcal{B}},s)&=&A_{i}\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(12){\rm Res}_{s=1}\xi_{i,L}(s)V_{1}({\mathcal{B}}),\\[10.84006pt]

(10)

where we define for a real number

to be

(11)

Note in particular that
{\mathcal{V}}_{1}({\mathcal{B}})={\rm Vol}({\mathcal{B}})
.
Therefore, for the purpose of computing the residues of the Shintani zeta functions, it is enough to evaluate the LHS of (

.

It will be convenient for us to replace the measure
\omega_{G}(g)
with

. This will only change

by the constant factor

defined in (
6

We use Mellin inversion and write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{2}Z_{L}({\mathcal{B}},s)\widetilde{\psi}(12s)X^{s}ds&=&\displaystyle J\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\Bigl(\sum_{x\in L}(g{\mathcal{B}})(x)\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{2}\widetilde{\psi}(12s)(\lambda(g))^{-12s}X^{s}ds\Bigr)dg\\[14.45377pt]

where

(13)

\frac{J}{12}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)=XA_{i}\widetilde{\psi}(12){\rm Res}_{s=1}\xi_{i,L}(s)V_{1}({\mathcal{B}})+X^{5/6}A_{i}\widetilde{\psi}(10){\rm Res}_{s=5/6}\xi_{i,L}V_{5/6}({\mathcal{B}})+o(X^{5/6})

The aim then is to evaluate

up to an error term of

5.3
Breaking up the integral

Our general process of evaluating global zeta integrals outlined in §2.2 admits some technical simplifications. Specifically, our fundamental domain
{\mathcal{F}}
has three torus parameters, namely,
t
,
s_{1}
, and
s_{2}
. However, for our purposes, we will only need to break

into two parts, corresponding to whether

is small or
t
is large. To this end, let

be a smooth and compactly supported function such that

for

. Let

denote the function defined by
f(x)=1-f_{0}(x)
. Throughout this paper, we fix

, which will be assumed to be small. For a real number

, define the functions

and

by setting

For

written as

in its Iwasawa decomposition, we define

and

We break up

(15)

and

{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)=\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{x\in L}(g{\mathcal{B}})(x)\psi\Big(\frac{\lambda(g)}{X^{1/12}}\Big)f^{X}(g)dg.

(16)

In the first and second sections of the next part, we will evaluate

{\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
and
{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, respectively, by following our general procedure. Our strategy is as follows:

1.

For
{\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, we will first prove that it is enough to fiber by
a_{11}
and
b_{11}
- the contribution from the regions where
a_{12}
is forced to be small is negligible. Next we will shift the integral and pick up some main terms with a sufficiently small error.

2.

For
{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, we will first prove that it is enough to fiber by all the coefficients of
A
and
b_{11}
. Second, we will show that in fact, it is enough to fiber just by
A
- the difference between the
A
and
b_{11}
fiber and the
A

A
fiber, to pick up main terms (in terms of the Shintani zeta function of ternary quadratic forms) upto small error.

We make the following remark before moving on to Part III

Remark 5.2

Our assumption that
L\subset V({\mathbb{Z}})
is an
S_{4}
-subset, i.e., every

corresponds to a quartic order inside an

-field is crucial for the above strategy. Indeed, since

contains no points with

or

with

,

and

, we see that for the purposes of evaluating
{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, it only remains to compute

\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{A}\nu(L_{A})(g{\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}}(A)\psi\Big(\frac{\lambda(g)}{X^{1/12}}\Big)f^{X}(g)dg.

(29)

In this section, we relate this quantity to Shintani zeta functions associated to ternary quadratic forms.

Let
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
denote the set of elements in
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
having positive determinant. Let
S_{3}
denote the space of symmetric (half-integral)
3\times 3
matrices, with the action of
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
given by
g_{3}A=g_{3}Ag_{3}^{t}
. The set of elements in
S_{3}
having nonzero determinant splits up into four
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
-orbits, one for each possible signature. Denote the orbit with signature
\sigma
by
S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{\sigma}
. We let
dA
be Euclidean measure on
S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, normalized so that
S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
has co-volume
1
. Let
A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
be an element with nonzero determinant and signature
\sigma
. The stabilizer of
A
in
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
is
{\rm SO}_{A}({\mathbb{R}})
. The Haar measure
\nu_{{\rm GL}_{3}}
on
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
, along with a fixed Haar-measure on
{\rm SO}_{A}({\mathbb{R}})
gives a measure, respecting the
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}
-action, on
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}/{\rm SO}_{A}({\mathbb{R}})
which is naturally identified with
S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{\sigma}
. We choose
dh
to be the Haar-measure on
{\rm SO}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
such that the corresponding measure on
S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{\sigma}
is
(\det A)^{-2}dA
. Following Kimura
[
22
, p.163]
, we then define
\mu(A)
to be
{\rm Vol}({\rm SO}_{A}({\mathbb{R}})/{\rm SO}_{A}({\mathbb{Z}}))
, where the volume is computed with respect to
dh
.

Let
\theta:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a periodic
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant function.
For a signature
\sigma
, we define the Shintani zeta function
\xi_{\sigma}^{tq}(s,\theta)
to be

\xi_{\sigma}^{tq}(s,\theta):=\sum_{A\in{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\backslash S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})^{\sigma}}\frac{\mu(A)\phi(A)}{|\det(A)|^{s}}.

(30)

This Shintani zeta function is closely related to the
global zeta integral
: Let
\Phi:S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth and super-polynomially decaying function. We define

Z(\Phi,\theta;s):=\int_{g\in{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\backslash{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{+}}\det(g)^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\\
\det(A)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\theta(A)(g\Phi)(A)\nu_{{\rm GL}_{3}}(g).

(31)

Then we have the following result. (See Kimura’s book
[
22
, Section 5]
for a clear exposition.)

Theorem 7.6

The zeta integral
Z^{\sigma}(\Phi,\theta;s)
converges absolutely for
{\rm Re}(s)
large enough. Moreover, we have

Z(\Phi,\theta;s)=\sum_{\sigma}\xi_{\sigma}^{tc}(s,\theta)\int_{A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{\sigma}}|\det(A)|^{s-2}\Phi(A)dA,

where
\sigma
goes over all signatures.

We can write
{\mathcal{F}}
as
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\cdot{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
. For fixed
\lambda
, we write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}\sum_{A}\nu(L_{A})(g{\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}}(A)f^{X}(g)dg&=&\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}\sum_{A}\nu(L_{A})((\lambda g_{2}g_{3}){\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}}(A)f^{X}(g_{2})dg_{2}dg_{3}\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{6}\int_{t>0}\int_{{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}\sum_{A}\nu(L_{A})\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{t}g_{3}{\mathcal{B}}\Bigr)_{\emptyset;\{A\}}(A)t^{4}f^{X}(t)d^{\times}tdg_{3}.\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle C\lambda^{6+3s}\int_{3+\epsilon}Z\bigl(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}},\nu(L_{A});s\bigr)\widetilde{f^{X}}(4-3s)ds.\end{array}

Above, the constant
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
is chosen to account for the measure changes implicit in the determination of the final line. We will later be able to determine
C
from purely formal arguments.

By the general theory, the global zeta integral has analytic continuation to the entire complex plane, with poles at most at negative the roots of the associated Bernstein–Sato polynomial
(x+1)(x+3/2)(x+2)
. The function
\widetilde{f^{X}}(s)=\widetilde{f}(s)X^{\delta s}
has a simple pole at
s=0
with residue
1
. Therefore, we may shift the integral over
s
above to
{\rm Re}(s)=1+\epsilon
, picking up poles at
2
,
3/2
, and
4/3
, and obtaining for some constants
C
and
c
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{3}}\sum_{A}\nu(L_{A})(g{\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}}(A)f^{X}(g)dg&=&C\lambda^{12}{\rm Res}_{s=2}Z\bigl(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}},\nu(L_{A});s\bigr)\widetilde{f^{X}}(-2)\\[14.45377pt]
&&+\displaystyle c\lambda^{21/2}X^{5\delta/2}\widetilde{f}(5/2)+C\lambda^{10}Z\bigl(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}},\nu(L_{A});4/3\bigr)\\[14.45377pt]
&&+\displaystyle O(\lambda^{9+O(\delta)+o(1)}).\end{array}

Integrating over
\lambda
, and combining this with Proposition
7.3
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)&=&\displaystyle C\widetilde{\psi}(12){\rm Res}_{s=2}Z(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;{A}}\nu(L_{A});s)\widetilde{f^{X}}(-2)X+C\widetilde{\psi}(10)Z\bigl(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}},\nu(L_{A});4/3\bigr)X^{5/6}\\[10.84006pt]
&&\displaystyle+c_{0}X^{21/24+5\delta/2}+c_{1}X^{21/24-3\delta}+c_{2}X^{21/24-3\delta/2}+O(X^{4/5+\epsilon})\end{array}

completing the proof of Proposition
7.1
.

8
Computing the first two residues

Let
L\subset V({\mathbb{Z}})
be a
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant set, defined by finitely many congruence conditions, such that every triple
(Q,C,r)
corresponding to a
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbit on
L
is an
S_{4}
-triple. In this section, we determine the residues of the poles at
1
and
5/6
of the Shintani zeta functions corresponding to
L
.

8.1
The work of Ibukiyama-Saito

We summarize (and generalize very slightly) in this section some of the main results of Ibukiyama–Saito
[
20
]
. Following their notation, for a ring
R
, let
S_{3}(R)
denote the set of ternary quadratic forms with coefficients in
R
(represented as
3\times 3

symmetric matrices), and for
d\in R
, let
S_{3}(R,d)
denote the subset of such matrices of determinant
d
. Let
(\omega_{p})_{p}
be a collection of functions
\omega_{p}:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, for every prime
p
, satisfying the following two conditions.

(1)

For every prime
p
, the function
\omega_{p}
is invariant under the actions of
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
;

(2)

For sufficiently large primes
p
, the function
\omega_{p}
is the characteristic function of
S_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
.

Let
\omega:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by
\omega(x)=\prod_{p}\omega_{p}(x)
. Fix
i
with
0\leq i\leq 3
, and abusing notation, let
i
also denote the signature
(i,3-i)
.
Consider the Shintani zeta function
\xi_{i}^{tq}(s,\omega)
, defined in (
30
), corresponding to the function
\omega
and the signature
i
.
2
2

2

Note that Ibukiyama-Saito have an extra factor of
c_{3}
(defined below) in their definition of the Zeta function so as to cancel out some irrational factors, compared to Kimura.
We define the constants

\delta_{i}:=(-1)^{3-i};\quad\epsilon_{i}:=(-1)^{{4-i\choose 2}};\quad c_{3}:=2\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma(i/2)\pi^{-3}=\pi^{-2}.

For a prime
p
, and an element
x\in S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
with nonzero determinant, we define the quantity
\alpha_{p}(x)
as in
[
20
, pp.1104]
:

\alpha_{p}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{k\to\infty}p^{-3k}|O(x_{k})|,

(32)

where
x_{k}
is the reduction of
x
modulo
p^{k}
and
O(x_{k})
is the orthogonal subgroup of
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})
preserving
x_{k}
.
Finally, let
\epsilon_{p}(x)
denote the Hasse invariant of
x
, and let
\omega^{\prime}_{p}:=\omega_{p}\epsilon_{p}
. We define our Hasse-invariants as in
[
20
]
, so that if
x
can be diagonalized over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
with entries
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, then
\epsilon_{p}(x)=\prod_{i\leq j}(a_{i},a_{j})_{p}
where
(,)_{p}
is the Hilbert symbol.

Let
f=(f_{p})_{p}
be a collection, over all primes
p
, of
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
-invariant functions
f_{p}:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\to{\mathbb{R}}
. For an integer
d\neq 0
, we define the quantity

\lambda_{p}(d,f)=\sum_{x_{p}\in S_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p},d)/\sim}f_{p}(x_{p})\alpha_{p}(x_{p})^{-1}

where
\sim
denotes
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
equivalence, and
\alpha_{p}(x_{p})
is the normalized stabilizer in (
32
).
Finally, define

\lambda(d,f):=\prod_{p}\lambda_{p}(d,f)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\zeta_{f,i}(s):=\sum_{\delta_{i}d>0}\lambda(d,f)2^{3s}|d|^{2-s}.

Then we have the following result

Proposition 8.1

Fix
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and let
\omega=\prod_{p}\omega_{p}:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the function from the start of this subsection. Then we have

\xi^{tc}_{i}(s,\omega)=\zeta_{\omega,i}(s)+\epsilon_{i}\zeta_{\omega^{\prime},i}(s).

Proof:

This is proved in
[
20
, prop 2.2]
when
\omega_{p}
is the characteristic function of
S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
, but the proof extends verbatim to our slightly more general setting of finitely many congruence conditions. Note that is essential that our functions
\omega_{p}
be
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
-equivalent in order to apply Siegel’s formula.

\Box

We next prove that the zeta functions
\zeta_{\omega,i}(s)
and
\zeta_{\omega^{\prime},i}(s)
satisfy product formulas.

Lemma 8.2

We have

\zeta_{\omega,i}(s)=2^{3s}\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{p}(p^{k},\omega)p^{k(2-s)}\Bigr);\quad\zeta_{\omega^{\prime},i}(s)=2^{3s}\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{p}(p^{k},\omega^{\prime})p^{k(2-s)}\Bigr).

Proof:

We first claim that
\lambda_{p}(d,\omega)
and
\lambda_{p}(d,\omega^{\prime})
depends only on
v_{p}(d)
. Note first that this only depends on the square class of
d
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, as can be seen by acting by an element of
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
with unit determinant on the set
S_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p},d)/\sim
.
Moreover, it is invariant by the cubes of units, as can be seen by the action of scaling by a unit on the set
S_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p},d)/\sim
, which preserves the Hasse invariant. This completes the proof.

Set
\lambda^{\prime}_{p}(d,f):=\frac{\lambda_{p}(d,f)}{\lambda_{p}(1,f)}
. Then, for
f=\omega
or
\omega^{\prime}
, we may write
\zeta_{f,i}(s)
as

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta d>0}\lambda(d,f)2^{3s}|d|^{2-s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d>0}\prod_{p}\lambda(p^{v_{p}(d)},f)2^{3s}|d|^{2-s}

\displaystyle=\lambda(1,f)\sum_{d>0}\prod_{p}\lambda^{\prime}_{p}(p^{v_{p}(d)},f)2^{3s}|d|^{2-s}

\displaystyle=2^{3s}\lambda(1,f)\prod_{p}\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda^{\prime}_{p}(p^{k},f)p^{k(2-s)}

\displaystyle=2^{3s}\prod_{p}\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{p}(p^{k},f)p^{k(2-s)},

as desired.

\Box

In the above proof we establish that
\lambda_{p}(d,f)
depends only on the valuation of
d
. This motivates the following definition. For a function
\phi:S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define

\zeta_{p}(\phi,s):=\int_{S_{3}^{ss}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})}\phi(x)|\det(x)|_{p}^{s}dx

where
dx
assigns measure
1
to
S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
. Unwinding the definition of
\alpha_{p}(x_{p})
and using the fact that
\lambda_{p}(d,\omega)
depends only on the valuation of
d
, we immediately obtain (as in
[
20
, p.1109]
) that
\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{p}(p^{k},f)p^{k(2-s)}
is proportional to
\zeta_{p}(f,s-2)
. It follows that

\zeta_{\omega,i}(s)=\alpha_{i}2^{3s}\prod_{p}\frac{\zeta_{p}(\omega_{p},s-2)}{1-p^{-1}},\quad\zeta_{\omega^{\prime},i}(s)=\alpha_{i}2^{3s}\prod_{p}\frac{\zeta_{p}(\omega^{\prime}_{p},s-2)}{1-p^{-1}}

(33)

for some fixed constant
\alpha_{i}
, and similarly for
\omega^{\prime}
, as in the bottom of
[
20
, P. 1108]
.
Let
\iota_{p}
denote the characteristic function of
S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
. Comparing with
[
20
, Thm 1.2]
gives the following:

\zeta_{p}(\iota_{p},s)=2^{-s\delta_{p=2}}\frac{(1-p^{-1})(1-p^{-3})}{(1-p^{-1-s})(1-p^{-3-2s})}

(34)

and

\zeta_{p}(\iota^{\prime}_{p},s)=2^{-s\delta_{p=2}}\frac{(1-p^{-1})(1-p^{-3})}{(1-p^{-2-s})(1-p^{-2-2s})}

(35)

and therefore that
\alpha_{i}=\frac{\zeta(3)}{24c_{3}}=\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{4}
, independent of
i
. We denote
\zeta_{\omega,i}
and
\zeta_{\omega^{\prime},i}
by
\zeta_{\omega}
and
\zeta_{\omega^{\prime}}
, respectively. Summarizing, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 8.3

Let
\omega=\prod_{p}\omega_{p}
be as above, and
0\leq i\leq 3
. Recall that
\epsilon_{i}=(-1)^{{4-i\choose 2}}
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rl}\xi^{tc}_{i}(s,\omega)=\displaystyle\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{4}\cdot 2^{3s}&\!\!\!\displaystyle\Bigl(\prod_{p}(1-p^{-1})^{-1}\cdot\int_{S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})}\omega_{p}(x)|\det(x)|_{p}^{s-2}dx\\[14.45377pt]
+&\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}\prod_{p}(1-p^{-1})^{-1}\cdot\int_{S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})}\omega_{p}(x)\epsilon_{p}(x)|\det(x)|_{p}^{s-2}dx\Bigr).\end{array}

For a place
v
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
we define the function
\kappa_{v}:S_{3}({\mathbb{Q}}_{v})\backslash\{\det=0\}\to\{0,1\}
by setting
\kappa_{v}(A)=1
when
A
is isotropic and
\kappa_{v}(A)=-1
otherwise.
For a real number
\kappa
, we define the quantities

\mathcal{W}_{\kappa}({\mathcal{B}}):=\int_{V({\mathbb{R}})}{\mathcal{B}}(A,B)|\det(A)|^{-\kappa}dAdB;\quad\mathcal{W}_{\kappa}^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}}):=\int_{V({\mathbb{R}})}{\mathcal{B}}(A,B)\kappa_{\infty}(A)|\det(A)|^{-\kappa}dAdB.

As a simple consequence of the Theorem
8.3
, we compute the residue at
s=2
and special value at
4/3
of the global zeta integral
Z({\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A},\nu(L_{A});s)
arising in the power series expansion of
{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}},X)
.

Corollary 8.4

We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\rm Res}_{s=2}Z({\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A},\nu(L_{A});s)&=&\displaystyle 16\zeta(2)\zeta(3)\cdot{\rm Vol}({\mathcal{B}})\nu(L);\\[10.84006pt]
Z({\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A},\nu(L_{A});4/3)&=&\displaystyle\zeta_{\nu(L_{A})}(4/3)\mathcal{W}_{2/3}({\mathcal{B}})-\zeta_{\nu(L_{A})^{\prime}}(4/3)\mathcal{W}_{2/3}^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}}).\end{array}

Proof:

We apply Theorem
7.6
to write

{\rm Res}_{s=2}Z({\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A},\nu(L_{A});s)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}{\rm Res}_{s=2}\xi_{i}^{tq}(s,\nu(L_{A})\int_{A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})^{(i,3-i)}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A}(A)dA.

From Theorem
8.3
, we see that the residue at
s=2
of
\xi_{i}^{tq}(s,\omega)
(for any acceptable
\omega
) is in fact independent of
i
(since the product in the second summand converges absolutely at
s=2
). Therefore, we obtain

{\rm Res}_{s=2}Z({\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A},\nu(L_{A});s)=16\zeta(2)\zeta(3)\Bigl(\int_{A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})}{\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A}(A)dA\Bigr)\prod_{p}\int_{A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})}\nu(L_{A})dA.

The first claim above follows immediately since the integral of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\emptyset;A}(A)
over
A
is
{\rm Vol}({\mathcal{B}})
and the integral of
\nu(L_{A})
over
A
is
\nu(L)
. The second claim follows similarly by noting that for
A\in S_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
having signature
(i,3-i)
, we have
\kappa_{\infty}(A)=-\epsilon_{i}
.

\Box

8.2
Computing the residues of the quartic Shintani zeta functions

We are now ready to return to our quartic Shintani zeta functions
\xi_{i,L}(s)
. For
i\in\{0,1,2\}
, let
{\mathcal{B}}:V({\mathbb{R}})^{(i)}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be a smooth and compactly supported function. We define the ratios

\mathcal{Q}_{i}:=\frac{\mathcal{W}_{2/3}({\mathcal{B}})}{{\mathcal{V}}_{5/6}({\mathcal{B}})},\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}:=\frac{\mathcal{W}_{2/3}^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}})}{{\mathcal{V}}_{5/6}({\mathcal{B}})},

which we will subsequently prove are independent of
{\mathcal{B}}
. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 8.5

Let
L\subset V({\mathbb{Z}})
be an
S_{4}
-set defined by finitely many congruence conditions, and let
i\in\{0,1,2\}
. Then
\xi_{i,L}
has simple poles at
s=1
and

with residues given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\rm Res}_{s=1}\xi_{i,L}(s)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2A_{i}}\zeta(2)^{2}\zeta(3)\nu(L);\\[10.84006pt]
{\rm Res}_{s=5/6}\xi_{i,L}(s)&=&\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{32A_{i}}\Bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{i}\zeta_{\nu(L_{A})}(4/3)-\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\prime}\zeta_{\nu(L_{A})^{\prime}}(4/3)\Bigr).\end{array}

Proof:

Recall that we write
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)={\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)+{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, and evaluate the latter two terms in Propositions
6.1
and
7.1
. Combining those two results gives us a power series expansion for
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, up to an error term of
O(X^{4/5+o(1)})
. Turn now to (
9
), (
10
), and (
12
): taken together, they also yield a (different) power series expansion for
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)
in terms of the residues of the Shintani zeta function of
\xi_{i,L}(s)
. Comparing the exponents of the terms that arise in these two power series expansions, we see that all the terms in both power series expansions other than the
X
- and
X^{5/6}
- terms must vanish for formal reasons! This immediately implies the first claim of the result, namely that
\xi_{i,L}(s)
has at most simple poles at
1
and
5/6
.

Next, equating the coefficient of the
X
-term in
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)
from (
14
) with the sums of the coefficients of the
X
-terms in
{\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
and
{\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
, we obtain

\frac{12}{J}A_{i}\widetilde{\psi}(12){\rm Res}_{s=1}\xi_{i,L}(s)={\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})\Bigl(\int_{g_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}f_{0}^{X}(g_{2})dg_{2}\Bigr)\nu(L)+(16\zeta(2)\zeta(3))C\widetilde{f^{X}}(-2)\nu(L).

The left hand side above is patently independent of
f
and
\delta
. The right hand side must be so too. Since we have

\widetilde{f^{X}}(-2)=\int_{g_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}}(1-f_{0}^{X})(g_{2})dg_{2},

it follows that
C={\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})/(16\zeta(2)\zeta(3))
. Therefore, using the definition of
J
from (
6
), we see that the residue at
s=1
of
\xi_{i,L}(s)
is given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\rm Res}_{s=1}\xi_{i,L}(s)&=&\displaystyle\frac{J}{12A_{i}}{\rm Vol}_{dg_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{2}){\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})\nu(L)\\[10.84006pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2A_{i}}{\rm Vol}_{\nu_{{\rm SL}_{2}}}({\mathcal{F}}_{2}){\rm Vol}_{\nu_{{\rm SL}_{3}}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})\nu(L)\\[10.84006pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2A_{i}}\zeta(2)^{2}\zeta(3)\nu(L).\end{array}

as claimed by the theorem.

Note that
{\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
has no
X^{5/6}
-term. We equate the coefficients of the
X^{5/6}
-terms of
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{B}};X)
and
{\mathcal{I}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{B}};X)
from (
14
) and Proposition
7.1
. Using Corollary
8.4
for the special value of
\xi_{i}^{tq}
and the recently computed value of
C
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{12}{J}A_{i}{\rm Res}_{s=5/6}\xi_{i,L}V_{5/6}({\mathcal{B}})&=&\displaystyle CZ\bigl(({\mathcal{B}})_{\emptyset;\{A\}},\nu(L_{A});4/3\bigr)\\[10.84006pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})}{16\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}\bigl(\zeta_{\omega}(\nu(L_{A}),4/3)\mathcal{W}_{2/3}({\mathcal{B}})-\zeta_{\omega^{\prime}}(\nu(L_{A}),4/3)\mathcal{W}_{2/3}^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}})\bigr).\end{array}

Now the volume
{\rm Vol}_{dg_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{2})
can be computed by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem to be
\pi/6
. Hence we have

\frac{J}{12}\frac{{\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})}{16\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}=\frac{J{\rm Vol}_{dg_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{2}){\rm Vol}_{dg_{3}}({\mathcal{F}}_{3})}{32\pi\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}=\frac{3}{16\pi}\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi}{32}.

Therefore, the value of
{\rm Res}_{s=5/6}\xi_{i,L}(s)

.

10.1
Nonmaximality in
V({\mathbb{Z}})

We have the following result due to Bhargava.

Lemma 10.1

[
4
, Lemma 22]

If
Q
is any quartic ring that is not maximal at
p
, then there exists a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis
1,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
of
Q
such that at least one of the following is true.

(i)

{\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{1}/p)+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot\alpha_{2}+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot\alpha_{3}
forms a ring;

(ii)

{\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{1}/p)+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{2}/p)+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot\alpha_{3}
forms a ring;

(iii)

{\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{1}/p)+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{2}/p)+{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot(\alpha_{3}/p)
forms a ring.

Let
(A,B)
be an element in
V({\mathbb{Z}})
which is nonmaximal at
p
. The following points are noted in the discussion following the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 22]
. Assume that we are not in Case (iii) of the above lemma. Then Case (i) occurs if and only if
(A,B)
can be transformed via an element of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
so that its coefficients satisfy the following condition (see
[
4
, (43)]
:

a_{11}\equiv b_{12}\equiv b_{13}\equiv 0\pmod{p},\mbox{ and }b_{11}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}.

(41)

Case (ii) occurs if and only if
(A,B)
can be transformed by an element of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
so that one of the following two conditions are satisfied (see (a) and (b) just following
[
4
, (43)]
):

a_{11}\equiv a_{12}\equiv a_{22}\equiv b_{11}\equiv b_{12}\equiv b_{22}\equiv 0\pmod{p};

(42)

b_{11}\equiv b_{12}\equiv b_{22}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}},\mbox{ and }b_{13}\equiv b_{23}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

(43)

Finally, Case (iii) occurs if and only if the
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
span of
A
and
B
is 1-dimensional, which implies that
(A,B)
can be transformed by an element of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
to ensure that we have

B\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

(44)

10.2
The switching correspondence

Let
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}:=G({\mathbb{Z}})\backslash V({\mathbb{Z}})
denote the set of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits on
V({\mathbb{Z}})
. Then
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
is in bijection with the set of isomorphism classes of quadratic maps
\{(r:W\to U)\}
, where
W
is a three-dimensional lattice,
U
is a two-dimensional lattice. In this language, given a sub-pair
(Q^{\prime},C^{\prime})
of
(Q,C)
, and corresponding maps
(r:W{\rightarrow}U,r^{\prime}:W^{\prime}{\rightarrow}U^{\prime})
we may find inclusions
W^{\prime}\subset W,U^{\prime}\subset U
inducing
r^{\prime}
from
r
, such that
[W:W^{\prime}]=[U:U^{\prime}]
. In this way, we may interpret the correspondences of subpairs and overpairs of a given index purely representation theoretically. We specifically isolate subpairs of index
p
and apply this observation there. These are particularly important since - as we shall prove below - they account for the majority of non-maximal rings.

The Type-
1
switching correspondence

Given an element
v:U\to{\mathbb{Z}}
in
U^{\vee}
, we compose to get a quadratic form
q_{v}:W\to U\to{\mathbb{Z}}
on
W
. Let
p
be a fixed prime.
Let
r:W\to U
be an element of
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
. Consider the set of all elements
(w,v)\in{\mathbb{P}}(W_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})\times{\mathbb{P}}(U^{\vee}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})
such that
for all
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in U^{\vee}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}\times W_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}

q_{v^{\prime}}(w,w)\equiv q_{v}(w,w^{\prime})\equiv 0\mod p,\quad q_{v}(w,w)\equiv 0\mod{p^{2}}.

(45)

Given such a
(w,v)
we define the element
r^{\prime}:W^{\prime}\to U^{\prime}
, where
W^{\prime}\subset W
and
U^{\prime}\subset U
are given by
W^{\prime}=w+pW
and
U^{\prime}=p({\rm ker}v)
and
r^{\prime}
is the restriction from
W
to
W^{\prime}
.

Conversely, suppose we are given
r^{\prime}:W^{\prime}{\rightarrow}U^{\prime}
together with a choice of 2-dimensional subspace
L\subset W^{\prime}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
and
v\in{\mathbb{P}}(U^{\prime\vee}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})
such that
q_{v}
vanishes on
L
.
Then we define
r:W{\rightarrow}U
by setting

W=L+pW^{\prime},U={\rm ker}v

where
r
is the restriction of
r^{\prime}
.

Let
T_{p}(1)
denote the set of pairs
(r:W\to U,r^{\prime}:W^{\prime}\to U^{\prime})
as above, so that
T_{p}(1){\rightarrow}\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}\times\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
is an (asymmetric) correspondence. We denote the projection maps to the
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
on the left and to the
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
on the right by
\pi_{\rm sub}
and
\pi_{\rm over}
, respectively. Then the following is a consequence of Lemma
10.1
:

Lemma 10.2

For
y\in T_{p}(1)
, we have
\pi_{\rm sub}(y)
is an index-
p
subpair of
\pi_{\rm over}(y)
. Moreover, if
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
are such that
x
is an index-
p
subpair of
x^{\prime}
, then there exists a unique
y\in T_{p}(1)
such that
\pi_{\rm sub}(y)=x
and
\pi_{\rm over}(y)=x^{\prime}
.

We deduce the a number of implications of the above lemma. Given
x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
corresponding to
r:W\to U
, let
M_{p}^{1}(x)
denote the number of pairs
(w,v)\in{\mathbb{P}}(W_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})\times{\mathbb{P}}(U^{\vee}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})
satisfying the conditions of (
45
). Given an element
x\in V({\mathbb{Z}})
, we will have to distinguish between some types of divisibility of
\Delta(x)
by various prime powers. Specifically, for a quartic ring
Q
and a prime
p
, we say that
p^{2k}\mid\Delta(Q)

well
if either
Q
has index at least
p^{k}
in its maximal order, or
Q
has index at least
p^{k-1}
in the maximal order of an
overramified
quartic field (i.e., a quartic field with splitting type
(1^{2}1^{2})
,
(2^{2})
, or
(1^{4})
at
p
). For a square number
q^{2}\geq 1
, we say that
q^{2}\mid\Delta(Q)

well
if every prime power
p^{2k}
dividing
q
divides
\Delta(Q)
well. Note that if
p^{2k}
divides
\Delta(Q)
but does not divide it well, then
Q
must be an index-
qp^{k-1}
suborder of a
(1^{3})
-maximal order, for some
q
with
(p,q)=1
. In particular, if
p^{2k+2}\mid\Delta(Q)
, then
p^{2k}\mid\Delta(Q)
well. Finally, for
x\in V({\mathbb{Z}})
, we say that
q^{2}\mid\Delta(x)
well if
q^{2}\mid\Delta(Q)
well, where
Q
is the quartic order corresponding to
x
. It can easily be checked that this is equivalent to the condition that
x
corresponds to the triple
(Q,C,r)
, where the index of
C
is at least
q
.

We have the following proposition.

Proposition 10.3

Let
x
be an element of
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
. Then the following are true.

1.

If
M_{p}^{1}(x)\geq 1
, then
x
is nonmaximal at
p
.

2.

If
M_{p}^{1}(x)>1
, then
p^{4}\mid\Delta(x)
well.

Proof:

The first claim is immediate since if
M_{p}^{1}(x)\geq 1
, then there exists
y\in T_{p}(1)
with
\pi_{\rm sub}(y)=x
. Then
\pi_{\rm over}(y)
is an overpair of
x
, implying that
x
is nonmaximal.
To prove the second claim, let
r:W\to U
correspond to
x
. Then there exist two pairs
(w,v)
and
(w^{\prime},v^{\prime})
in
{\mathbb{P}}(W_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})\times{\mathbb{P}}(U^{\vee}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})
satisfying the conditions of (
45
). We divide into three cases: first,
w=w^{\prime}
; second,
v=v^{\prime}
; and third,
w\neq w^{\prime}
and
v\neq v^{\prime}
. For the first case, we complete
w
to a basis of
W
and take
\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle
to be a basis of
U
, yielding an element
(A,B)
of the form

\left[\begin{pmatrix}0^{2}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{*}&{*}\\
{0}&{*}&{*}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0^{2}&0&0\\
0&{*}&{*}\\
0&{*}&{*}\end{pmatrix}\right],

where the
0
means that the coefficient is divisible by
p
and the
0^{2}
means the coefficient is divisible by
p^{2}
.
Since the element
(1,{\rm diag}(p^{-1},1,1))\in G({\mathbb{Q}})
leaves the above pair integral, it follows that
p^{8}\mid\Delta(x)
.
For the third case, we complete
w
and
w^{\prime}
to a basis of
W
and complete
v
to a basis of
U
, obtaining an element
(A,B)
of the form

\left[\begin{pmatrix}0^{2}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{0^{2}}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{*}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&{*}&{*}\\
{*}&{0}&{*}\\
{*}&{*}&{*}\end{pmatrix}\right].

Now either
b_{12}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, in which case
(A,B)
satisfies the conditions of (
42
), or
b_{12}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, in which case
(A,B)
can be transformed via an action of
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
to satisfy the conditions of (
43
). In either case, we are in Case (ii) of Lemma
10.1
implying that
p^{2}\mid{\rm ind}(x)
and hence
p^{4}\mid\Delta(x)
well. Finally, in the third case, we complete
\langle w,w^{\prime}\rangle
to a basis for
W
and take
\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle
to be a basis for
U
yielding an element of the form

\left[\begin{pmatrix}0^{2}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{*}\\
{0}&{*}&{*}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&{0}&{*}\\
{0}&{0^{2}}&{0}\\
{*}&{0}&{*}\end{pmatrix}\right].

This element satisfies the conditions of (
42
), again implying that
p^{2}\mid{\rm ind}(x)
and hence
p^{4}\mid\Delta(x)
well.

\Box

11
Uniformity estimates

In this section, we prove uniform estimates on the number of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits on the set of elements in
V({\mathbb{Z}})
having nonzero bounded discriminant, where each orbit is weighted by certain
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant functions.

11.1
Uniformity estimates on Rings

Let
K
be an étale quartic algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
p
be a prime. We say that
p^{2}\mid\Delta(K)

well
if the splitting type of
p
at
K
is
(1^{2}1^{2})
,
(2^{2})
, or
(1^{4})
. For a squarefree number
q
, we say that
q^{2}\mid\Delta(K)
well if
p^{2}\mid\Delta(K)
well for all primes
p
dividing
q
.
We begin with the following result.

Theorem 11.1

Let
q
be a squarefree number. The number of étale quartic algebras
K
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with
|\Delta(K)|<X
and
q^{2}\mid\Delta(K)
well is bounded by
O(X^{1+o(1)}/q^{2})
.

Proof:

We break up the set of étale quartic algebras
K
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
into four subsets: first, the subset of fields
K
whose Galois closures have Galois group
S_{4}
or
A_{4}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. These are exactly the algebras
K
such that both
K
and the cubic resolvent of
K
are fields. The required estimates for this subset of fields follows from
[
5
, Proposition 23]
. (Proposition 23 of
[
7
]
implies the estimate when
q
is a prime, but the same proof carries over without change for arbitrary squarefree
q
.) Second, the subset of
D_{4}
-
V_{4}
-, and
C_{4}
-quartic fields
K
. These fields
K
contain a quadratic subfield
K_{2}
, and we have
\Delta(K)=\Delta(K_{2})^{2}N_{K_{2}/{\mathbb{Q}}}(\Delta(K/K_{2}))
, where
N_{K_{2}/{\mathbb{Q}}}
denotes the norm function from
K_{2}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
\Delta(K/K_{2})
denotes the relative discriminant. Given an integer
D
with
|D|<X
and
q^{2}\mid D
, there are only
O(|D|^{o(1)})
choices for
\Delta(K_{2})
, thereby fixing
K_{2}
up to
O(|D|^{o(1)})
choices. From
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
, it follows that the number of quadratic extensions of
K_{2}
with relative discriminant having norm
D/\Delta(K_{2})^{2}
is also bounded by
O(|D|^{o(1)})
. Since there are
O(X/q^{2})
possible values of
D
, the result follows for this subset of fields.

Third, we consider the algebras
K
which are of the form
{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus K_{3}
, for which the claim follows essentially from work of Davenport–Heilbron (see
[
2
, Lemma 2.7]
). Finally, we consider algebras of the form
K=K_{2}\oplus K_{2}^{\prime}
, where
K_{2}
and
K_{2}^{\prime}
are étale quadratic algebras over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The set of all
K
is parametrized by pairs of squarefree (away from
2
) integers, and the claim follows immediately by noting that both these integers must be multiples of
q
(up to a factor of
2
).

\Box

Next, we have the following result, due to Nakagawa, estimating the number of suborders of an étale quartic algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with fixed index.

Proposition 11.2

Let
K
be a quartic étale algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with ring of integers
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and discriminant
D\neq 0
. Then the number
N({\mathcal{O}}_{K},q)
of suborders of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
having index
q
is
\ll q^{o(1)}N(q,D)
, where

N(q,D):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{2}\nmid D\\
p^{3}||q\end{subarray}}p\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{2}\nmid D\\
p^{e}||q,\ e\geq 4\end{subarray}}p^{\lfloor e/2\rfloor}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{2}\mid D\\
p^{e}||q,\ e\geq 2\end{subarray}}p^{\lfloor e/2\rfloor}

(46)

Note that we have
N(q,D)=N(q,(q,D))
.
Next we prove that Nakagawa’s result implies a bound of the same strength when counting sub-pairs
(Q,C)
of given fixed index inside an étale quartic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Corollary 11.3

Let
K
be an étale quartic algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, with ring of integers
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and discriminant
D
. Let
N_{K}(q)
denote the number of pairs
(Q,C)
, where
Q
is a quartic suborder of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
having index
q
and
R
is a cubic resolvent ring of
Q
. Then
N_{K}(q)\ll q^{o(1)}N(q,D)
.

Proof:

Let
Q
be one of the
N({\mathcal{O}}_{K},q)
suborders of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
of index
q
. Bhargava proves in
[
4
, Corollary 4]
that the number of cubic resolvent rings of
Q
is equal to the sum of the divisors of the content of
Q
, where the
content
of
Q
is the largest integer
c
such that
Q={\mathbb{Z}}+cQ^{\prime}
for some quartic ring
Q^{\prime}
. This quartic ring
Q^{\prime}
then has content
1
and index
q/c^{3}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. The number of pairs
(Q^{\prime},C^{\prime})
, where
Q^{\prime}
is an index
q/c^{3}
quartic suborder of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
of content
1
, and
R^{\prime}
is the (unique) cubic resolvent ring of
Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
N({\mathcal{O}}_{K},q/c^{3})
. Therefore, we have

N_{K}(q)\ll\sum_{c^{3}\mid q}c^{1+o(1)}N({\mathcal{O}}_{K},q/c^{3})\ll\sum_{c^{3}\mid q}c^{1+o(1)}N(q/c^{3},D)\ll q^{o(1)}N(q,D)

as needed.

\Box

We derive the following consequence of Theorem
11.1
and Corollary
11.3
.

Corollary 11.4

Let
a
and
b
be coprime squarefree positive integers. Then the number of triples
(Q,C,r)
, where
Q
is a quartic ring,
C
is a cubic resolvent of
Q
,
0<|\Delta(Q)|<X
, and
a^{2}b^{4}\mid\Delta(Q)
well is bounded by
O(X^{1+o(1)}/a^{2}b^{4})
.

Proof:

We partition our set of triples
(Q,C,r)
into subsets with fixed value of the index
{\rm ind}(Q,C)={\rm ind}(Q)
. Let
n
be a positive integer occurring as an index of some such triple
(Q,C,r)
. Then we must have
b\mid n
(up to a possible factor of
6
, which we will ignore) since the discriminant of a quartic maximal order cannot be divisible by
p^{4}
for a prime
p\geq 5
. Write
n=ba_{1}b_{1}n_{1}
, where
a_{1}\mid a
,
b_{1}\mid b
, and
(n_{1},ab)=1
. Let
(Q,C)
be a pair with index
n
, and denote the maximal order of
Q\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
by
{\mathcal{O}}
. Since
a^{2}b^{4}\mid\Delta(Q)
well, it follows that
(a_{2}b_{2})^{2}\mid\Delta({\mathcal{O}})
, where
a_{2}=a/a_{1}
and
b_{2}=b/b_{1}
. Let
b_{1}^{\prime}
be the largest integer dividing
b_{1}
such that
b_{1}^{\prime 2}\mid\Delta({\mathcal{O}})
.
Since we have
|\Delta({\mathcal{O}})|<X/n^{2}
and
a_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{2}b_{1}^{\prime 2}\mid\Delta({\mathcal{O}})
well, Proposition
11.1
implies that the number of choices for
{\mathcal{O}}
is
\ll X/(n^{2}(a_{2}b_{2}b_{1}^{\prime})^{2-o(1)})\ll((ab)^{o(1)})X/(a^{2}b^{4}b_{1}^{\prime 2}n_{1}^{2})
.
By Corollary
11.3
, it follows that the number of suborders of any such
{\mathcal{O}}
with index
n
is
\ll b_{1}^{\prime}n^{o(1)}N(n_{1},n_{1})
. Therefore, the number of pairs
(Q,C)
satisfying the conditions of the statement of the corollary, such that
{\rm ind}(Q,C)=n
is
\ll((abn)^{o(1)}N(n_{1},n_{1})X)/(b_{1}n_{1}^{2})
. Since the sum over
n_{1}
of
N(n_{1},n_{1})/n_{1}^{2}
converges, and since
\sum_{b_{1}\mid b}b_{1}^{-1}\ll b^{o(1)}
, our result follows.

\Box

11.2
Weighted uniformity estimates

Recall the function
M_{p}^{1}:\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
defined in §8.2. Let
\delta_{p}^{{\rm nm}}
denote the indicator function on
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
of the set of elements which are nonmaximal at
p
. Define the function
M_{p}^{2}:\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
by
M_{p}^{2}=\delta_{p}^{\rm nm}-M_{p}^{1}
. The functions
M_{p}^{1}
,
\delta_{p}^{\rm nm}
, and
M_{p}^{2}
are defined modulo
p^{2}
. We will abuse notation and use
M_{p}^{1}
,
\delta_{p}^{\rm nm}
, and
M_{p}^{2}
, to also denote the corresponding functions
V({\mathbb{Z}})\backslash\{\Delta=0\}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and
V({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{2}{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For a positive real number
X
, let
\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}
denote the set of elements
x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}
with
0<|\Delta(x)|<X
. We have the following result.

Proposition 11.5

We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,M_{p}^{1}(x)=1\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{2}};\\[10.84006pt]
|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,M_{p}^{1}(x)>1\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{4}};\\[10.84006pt]
|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,M_{p}^{1}(x)\gg_{\epsilon}p^{\epsilon}\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{5}};\\[10.84006pt]
|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,M_{p}^{1}(x)\gg_{\epsilon}p^{1+\epsilon}\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{8}}.\end{array}

Proof:

First, if
M_{p}^{1}(x)\neq 0
, then
x
corresponds to a triple
(Q,C)
where
Q
is nonmaximal at
p
. Then Corollary
11.4
provides the required bound. Second, if
|M_{p}^{1}(x)|>1
, then
p^{4}\mid\Delta(x)
well by Proposition
10.3
. The required bound now follows from Corollary
11.4
.
Third, assume that
|M_{p}^{1}(x)|\gg_{\epsilon}p^{\epsilon}
, where
x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}
corresponds to
(Q,C,r)
. We claim that the index of
Q
in its maximal order
{\mathcal{O}}
is at least
p^{3}
. Indeed, there are
\gg_{\epsilon}p^{\epsilon}
distinct pairs
(Q^{\prime},C^{\prime})
such that
Q
has index-
p
in
Q^{\prime}
. But the maximal order of each
Q^{\prime}
is
{\mathcal{O}}
. Hence by Corollary
11.3
, it follows that the index of any such
Q^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{O}}
is at least
p^{2}
, which implies that the index of
Q
in
{\mathcal{O}}
is at least
p^{3}
. The required result now follows from bounding the number of possible choices for
{\mathcal{O}}
by
O(X^{1+o(1)}/p^{6})
using Theorem
11.1
, and the number of choices for
(Q,C)
given
{\mathcal{O}}
by
O(p)
using Corollary
11.3
. The fourth and final estimate follows in identical fashion by observing that if
|M_{p}^{1}(x)|\gg_{\epsilon}p^{1+\epsilon}
, where
x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}
corresponds to
(Q,C)
, then the index of
Q
in its maximal order is at least
p^{5}
.

\Box

We prove the analogous result for
M_{p}^{2}
.

Proposition 11.6

We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,|M_{p}^{2}(x)|\geq 1\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{4}};\\[10.84006pt]
|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,|M_{p}^{2}(x)|\gg_{\epsilon}p^{\epsilon}\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{5}};\\[10.84006pt]
|\{x\in\overline{V({\mathbb{Z}})}_{X}:\,|M_{p}^{2}(x)|\gg_{\epsilon}p^{1+\epsilon}\}|&\ll&\displaystyle\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{p^{8}}.\end{array}

along with an “error term” of

\int_{-1}\xi_{i}(M^{\Sigma}_{a,b};s)X^{s}\widetilde{\psi}(s)ds=-\int_{2}\xi_{i}(M^{\Sigma}_{a,b};1-t)X^{1-t}\widetilde{\psi}(1-t)dt.

Applying the functional equation (
50
), we see that the error term is
\ll

X\int_{2}\Big(\frac{(ab)^{24}}{X}\Big)^{t}\widetilde{\psi}(1-t)\gamma(t-1)\sum_{j\in\{0,1,2\}}c_{ji}(t)\xi_{j}^{*}(\widehat{M^{\Sigma}_{a,b}};t)\ll XN(|\widehat{M^{\Sigma}_{a,b}}|,(ab)^{24}/X).

Since we have
\widehat{M^{\Sigma}_{a,b}}=\widehat{M_{a,b}}\cdot\widehat{\delta_{\Sigma}}
, the result follows from Corollary
11.10
.

\Box

12.3
Estimating the residues at
1
,
5/6
, and
3/4

In this section we derive estimates for
r(a,b,c)
and
r_{2}(a,b,c)
for
c\in\{1,5/6,3/4\}
. Pick a smooth and compactly supported function
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
whose Mellin transform is non-zero at
1
,
5/6
, and
3/4
. From Corollary
11.4
and Proposition
12.2
, we obtain

\sum_{c\in\{1,\frac{3}{4},\frac{5}{6}\}}X^{c}\left(\log X\tilde{\psi}(c)r_{2}(a,b,c)+\tilde{\psi}(c)r(a,b,c)+(\tilde{\psi})^{\prime}(c)r_{2}(a,b,c)\right)\ll\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{a^{2+o(1)}b^{4+o(1)}}+a^{3+o(1)}b^{12+o(1)}.

We claim that the above implies bounds of the same strength for each of the six individual terms

\{X^{c}r_{2}(a,b,c),X^{c}r(a,b,c):c\in\{1,5/6,3/4\}\}.

This is implied by the following simple lemma.

Lemma 12.3

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
, and
e_{1}>e_{2}>\dots>e_{n}>0
, be real numbers satisfying the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(a_{i}X^{e_{i}}\log X+b_{i}X^{e_{i}}\bigr)=O(M_{1}X^{1+o(1)}+M_{2}),

(51)

for all
X>1
, for some fixed positive real numbers
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.
Then we also have

\max_{i=1}^{n}\left(\max(|a_{i}X^{e_{i}}|,|b_{i}X^{e_{i}}|\right)\ll X^{o(1)}\cdot(M_{1}X+M_{2}).

Proof:

For a real number
1<r<2
, replacing
X
with
rX
in (
51
), yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[r^{e_{i}}\Bigl(1+\frac{\log r}{\log X}\Bigr)a_{i}X^{e_{i}}\log X+r^{e_{i}}b_{i}X^{e_{i}}\Big]=O(M_{1}X^{1+o(1)}+M_{2}).

The claim would now follow immediately if we find
2n
such numbers
r_{i}
, such that the matrix
E_{\vec{r}}
, whose
j
’th row is

\left(r_{j}^{e_{1}}\Big(1+\frac{\log r_{j}}{\log X}\Big),r_{j}^{e_{1}},\dots,r_{j}^{e_{n}}\Big(1+\frac{\log r_{j}}{\log X}\Big),r_{j}^{e_{n}}\right),

is invertible with determinant
X^{o(1)}
. By subtracting appropriately scaled even columns from odd ones, and multiplying the odd columns by
\log X
, we see that the determinant of
E_{\vec{r}}
is equal to
(\log X)^{-2n}
times the determinant of
F_{\vec{r}}
, whose
j
’th row is

\left(r_{j}^{e_{1}}\log r_{j},r_{j}^{e_{1}},\dots,r_{j}^{e_{n}}\log r_{j},r_{j}^{e_{n}}\right).

It is elementary that the functions
r^{t}\log r,r^{t}
of the variable
r
are independent as
t
varies over
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, so a general choice of
\vec{r}
will do. This completes the proof.

\Box

We derive the following consequence of the above lemma:

Proposition 12.4

We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\max\bigl(r_{2}(a,b,1),r(a,b,1))&\ll&a^{-2+o(1)}b^{-4+o(1)};\\[8.67204pt]
\max\bigl(r_{2}(a,b,5/6),r(a,b,5/6))&\ll&a^{-7/6+o(1)}b^{-4/3+o(1)};\\[8.67204pt]
\max\bigl(r_{2}(a,b,3/4),r(a,b,3/4))&\ll&a^{-3/4+o(1)}b^{o(1)}.\end{array}

Note in particular that sum over
a
and
b
of the residues at
5/6
converges.

Proof:

From Lemma
12.3
, it follows that

\max_{c\in\{\frac{3}{4},\frac{5}{6},1\}}\max(r(a,b,c),r_{2}(a,b,c))X^{c+o(1)}=O\left(\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{a^{2+o(1)}b^{4+o(1)}}+O(a^{3+o(1)}b^{12+o(1)}\right).

We optimize by setting
X=a^{5}b^{16}
. This yields the bound

\max_{c\in\{\frac{3}{4},\frac{5}{6},1\}}\max(r(a,b,c),r_{2}(a,b,c))=O(a^{3-5c+o(1)}b^{12-16c+o(1)}),

(52)

proving the result.

\Box

12.4
Proof of Theorem
1
: Sieving down to maximal orders in quartic algebras

We finally prove our main Theorem
1
. Let
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be a smooth function with compact support. For
c\in\{1,5/6\}
, define

C_{2}(c;\psi):=\sum_{(ab,P)=1}\mu(ab)r_{2}(a,b,c)\tilde{\psi}(c),

and

C_{1}(c;\psi):=\sum_{(ab,P)=1}\mu(ab)\left(r_{2}(a,b,c)(\tilde{\psi})^{\prime}(c)+r(a,b,c)\tilde{\psi}(c)\right)

From (
49
) and Corollary
11.7
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}N^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 1\\
(ab,P)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)N^{(i)}(M_{a,b}^{\Sigma};\psi;X)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}\leq X\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)N^{(i)}(M_{a,b}^{\Sigma};\psi;X)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}>X\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)N^{(i)}(M_{a,b}^{\Sigma};\psi;X)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}\leq X\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)N^{(i)}(M_{a,b}^{\Sigma};\psi;X)+O\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}>X\end{subarray}}\frac{X^{1+o(1)}}{a^{2}b^{4}}\Big).\end{array}

We estimate the first sum above using Proposition
12.2
:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}\leq X\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)N^{(i)}(M_{a,b}^{\Sigma};\psi;X)\\[8.67204pt]
=&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}\leq X\end{subarray}}\mu(ab)\sum_{c\in\{1,\frac{3}{4},\frac{5}{6}\}}X^{c}\left(\log X\tilde{\psi}(c)r_{2}(a,b,c)+\tilde{\psi}(c)r(a,b,c)+(\tilde{\psi})^{\prime}(c)r_{2}(a,b,c)\right)\\[18.06749pt]
&\displaystyle+O\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(ab,P)=1\\
a^{5}b^{16}\leq X\end{subarray}}a^{3+o(1)}b^{12+o(1)}\Big).\end{array}

Next, we expand the sum over
a
and
b
and
c\in\{1,5/6\}
in the main term of the RHS above to all
a
,
b
with
(ab,P)=1
, bounding the residues of the tail using Proposition
12.4
. This yields

\begin{array}[]{rcl}N^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\{1,5/6\}}\left(C_{2}(c;\psi)X^{c}\log X+C_{1}(c;\psi)X^{c}\right)\\[14.45377pt]
&&\displaystyle+O\Bigl(X^{o(1)}\sum_{a^{5}b^{16}\leq X}\Bigl(a^{3}b^{12}+\frac{X^{3/4}}{a^{3/4}}\Bigr)\Bigr)+O\Bigl(X^{o(1)}\sum_{a^{5}b^{16}>X}\Big(\frac{X}{a^{2}b^{4}}+\frac{X^{5/6}}{a^{7/6}b^{4/3}}\Bigr)\Big)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\{1,5/6\}}\left(C_{2}(c;\psi)X^{c}\log X+C_{1}(c;\psi)X^{c}\right)+O(X^{13/16+o(1)}).\end{array}

(53)

To recover
N_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)
from
N^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)
, note that
F^{\prime}(\Sigma)\backslash F(\Sigma)
consists exactly of algebras of the form
{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus K_{3}
,
K_{2}\oplus K_{2}^{\prime}
,
{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus K_{2}
, and
{\mathbb{Q}}^{4}
, where
K_{3}
is a cubic field and
K_{2}
and
K_{2}^{\prime}
are quadratic fields. Smoothed counts for the cubic fields are carried out in
[
27
]
, with two main terms of magnitude
X
and
X^{5/6}
, with an error term of size
O(X^{2/3+o(1)})
. Smoothed counts for sums of quadratic fields follow from an elementary application of Dirichlet’s hyperbola method, combined with a standard squarefree sieve. This yields main terms of size
X\log X
and
X
, with an error term of size
oO(X^{3/4+o(1)})
. Smoothed counts for quadratic fields follow from an elementary squarefree sieve, giving a main term of size
X
with an error of
O(X^{1/2+o(1)})
. Subtracting this from the power series expansion of
N_{\Sigma}^{\prime}(\psi;X)
gives

N_{\Sigma}(\psi,X)=C_{1}(\Sigma,\psi)\cdot X+C^{\prime}_{5/6}(\Sigma,\psi)\cdot X^{5/6}\log X+C_{5/6}(\Sigma,\psi)\cdot X^{5/6}+O(X^{13/16+o(1)}),

for some constants
C_{1}(\Sigma,\psi)
,
C_{5/6}^{\prime}(\Sigma,\psi)
, and
C_{5/6}(\Sigma,\psi)
. Note that the leading constant of the
X\log X
term much be zero after the subtraction, since the number of quartic fields with discriminant bounded by
X
is known to be
O(X)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
\Box

Remark 12.5

The argument above is set up to be dependent of where the potential poles actually are, and would give a power series expansion with an error of
X^{13/16+o(1)}
taking into account all the poles strictly larger than
13/16
.

12.5
Proof of Theorem
2
:

Let
\Sigma=(\Sigma_{v})_{v\in S}
be an
S_{4}
-family as in the theorem statement. Let
P=\prod_{p\in S}p
. For a prime
p\in S
let
L_{p}\subset V({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
denote the open subset corresponding to
\Sigma_{p}
. For a squarefree
n
coprime to
P
we define
D_{n}:=\prod_{p\in S}\chi_{L_{p}}\prod_{p\mid n}\delta_{p}^{{\rm nm}}
, and let

D_{a,b}:=\prod_{p\in S}\chi_{L_{p}}\prod_{p\mid a}M_{p}^{1}\prod_{p\mid b}M_{p}^{2}
.

By Theorem
8.5
, the corresponding zeta functions
\xi_{i}(D_{n};s)
have only simple poles at
s=1
and
s=5/6
. Moreover, since
\Sigma
is an
S_{4}
family, it follows from (
53
) that we have

N_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)=N^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(\psi;X)=C_{1}(1;\psi)X+C_{1}(5/6;\psi)X^{5/6}+O(X^{13/16+o(1)}),

where for
c\in\{1,5/6\}
, we have

C_{1}(c;\psi)=\sum_{(ab,P)=1}\mu(ab)\widetilde{\psi}(c)r(a,b,c).

By the relation
\delta_{p}^{{\rm nm}}=M_{p}^{1}+M_{p}^{2}
, we may rewrite this as

C_{1}(c;\psi)=\tilde{\psi}(c)\sum_{(n,P)=1}\mu(n){\rm Res}_{s=c}\xi_{i}(D_{n};s).

Now Theorem
2
follows directly from the computation of these residues in Theorem
8.8
.
\Box
