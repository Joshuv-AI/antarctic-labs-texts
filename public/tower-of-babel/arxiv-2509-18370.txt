1.
Introduction

Imagine taking a thin rectangular strip of paper and simply joining the ends creating a cylinder (or annulus). You could first add a half-twist, then join the ends to create a Möbius band. You could also add in any number of half-twists before joining the ends together. If there are an odd number of half-twists you create a
multi-twist paper Möbius band
, if there are an even number of half-twists, you create a
multi-twist paper annulus
. We pause to be clarify that the Möbius bands and annuli arise from a smooth isometric embedding of a flat Möbius band or cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(For the formal definition see
[
36
]
and Section
2.3
.)

Alternatively, you could take the thin, rectangular strip of paper and tie a knot in it before joining the ends. In each of these scenarios, the center line of the rectangular strip is a knot in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, while the rectangular strip gives a framing of the knot. The twisted annuli and Möbius bands are thus unknots with a particular framing. In this paper we will also consider
folded ribbon knots
, which occur when the knotted rectangular strips of paper have been folded flat in the plane. Figure
1
shows two different Möbius bands and, on the right, a folded ribbon trefoil knot. Working in the plane simplifies many of the computations. We can think of a folded ribbon knot as a piecewise-linear immersion of an embedded-knotted Möbius band or annulus into the plane where the fold lines correspond to the singularities of the immersion. (See
[
10
]
for a survey article on folded ribbon knots and Section
2
for the formal definition.)

Over the years many people have tried to find the
infimal aspect ratio
of the rectangular strip of paper used to tie any multi-twist Möbius band or annulus. In the language of folded ribbon knots, this is the same as the
ribbonlength problem
: to find the infimal folded ribbonlength of a particular knot or link type. The
folded ribbonlength
of a folded ribbon knot is the infimal ratio of the length of the knot to the width of the ribbon.

In 2024, Richard Schwartz
[
36
]
proved a long standing conjecture of Ben Halpern and Charles Weaver
[
20
]
, that any smoothly embedded paper Möbius band must have aspect ratio greater than
\sqrt{3}
. This paper also includes a review of the history of this problem. A secondary result of
[
36
]
is that any sequence of embedded paper Möbius bands with aspect ratio converging to
\sqrt{3}
must converge to a limiting example which is a three fold wrapping of an equilateral triangle. If you stare at the left image in Figure
1
you might be able to imagine this limiting example. In a second paper, Schwartz
[
35
]
gives an explicit estimate for this convergence.

What about embedded cylinders or multi-twist constructions? In 2023, Brienne Brown and Schwartz
[
2
]
gave two different constructions showing a Möbius band made of three half-twists has aspect ratio greater than
3
. Note that the center image in Figure
1
is such a Möbius band, but the infimal aspect ratio of this geometric configuration is
3\sqrt{3}
(see
[
14
]
). In 2025, Schwartz and Noah Montgomery
[
33
]
wrote a joint paper on the
twisted paper cylinder
. This is an isometric embedding of a flat cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the images of the boundary components are linked. An annulus made with two half-twists is an example of such a twisted paper cylinder. They proved that any twisted paper cylinder has aspect ratio greater than 2 and that this bound is sharp.

More recently, Aidan Hennessey
[
21
]
showed that there are multi-twist paper Möbius bands and annuli with as many twists as desired but with aspect ratio less than 8. In
[
33
]
, Schwartz refers to unpublished work of Jan Neinhaus that shows that for the Möbius band, the aspect ratio of
3\sqrt{3}+\epsilon
suffices for any
\epsilon>0
. One of the goals of this paper is to provide a published proof of this result (see Theorem
11
).

A secondary goal of this paper is to apply the constructions used for the multi-twist Möbius band to folded ribbon knots. Like the embedded paper Möbius bands, folded ribbon knots in the form of “tight” folded ribbon trefoil knots have appeared in recreational mathematics
[
9
,
23
,
39
]
. Since 2004, there have been a number of papers which tackle the folded ribbonlength problem by constructing a folded ribbon knot for an infinite family of knots. These constructions give upper bounds on the infimal folded ribbonlength for this family of knots. The ribbonlength problem can be viewed as a 2-dimensional version of the
ropelength problem
which asks for the minimum amount of rope needed to tie a knot in a rope of unit diameter. (See for instance
[
3
,
4
,
12
,
16
,
19
,
32
]
.) Knotted ribbon shapes also appear in DNA structures and some of these are circular
[
18
,
31
]
. The folding of two and three-dimensional structures in ribbons has also appeared in robotics
[
38
]
, and in genetics with a ribosomal walking robot
[
34
]
.

A separate, but equally interesting, open problem is to relate the infimal folded ribbonlength of a knot type
[K]
to its crossing number
{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)
. The
ribbonlength crossing number problem
asks for positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},\alpha,\beta
such that

(1)

c_{1}\cdot{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)^{\alpha}\leq{\operatorname{Rib}}([K])\leq c_{2}\cdot{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)^{\beta}.

There is a long standing conjecture by Yuanan Diao and Rob Kusner, that
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=1
in Equation
1
. In 2017, Grace Tian
[
37
]
proved that
\beta\leq 2
. In 2021, the first author in
[
11
]
proved that
\beta\leq\frac{3}{2}
, and with coauthors in
[
13
]
proved that
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
for both knots and links. In 2024, Hyoungjun Kim, Sungjong No, and Hyungkee Yoo
[
30
]
gave a solution for one part of this conjecture by proving that that
\beta\leq 1
. Specifically, they proved that for any knot or link,

(2)

{\operatorname{Rib}}(K)\leq 2.5{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)+1.

In this paper, we will prove the second part of this conjecture by showing that
\alpha=0
. Specifically, in Theorem
16
we will prove that any
(2,q)
-torus knot
T(2,q)
can be constructed so that the folded ribbonlength
{\operatorname{Rib}}([T(2,q)])\leq 13.86
. We give a second proof by of this result by looking at twist knots
T_{n}
. Theorems
23
and
24
tell us
{\operatorname{Rib}}([T_{n}])\leq 17.59
when
n
is odd, and

{\operatorname{Rib}}([T_{n}])\leq 15.86
when
n
is even.

Finally, we remind the reader that there have been many papers
[

,

,

,

which give upper bounds on the ribbonlength in terms of crossing number for various families of knots. These bounds are often better than the bounds in Equation
2
for specific families of knots and also for knots with low crossing number. We will see that there is often a difference between the folding techniques used to find the best upper bounds on small crossing knots versus the techniques used for infinite families.

The work in this paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we give the formal definition for folded ribbon links and relate this to the work for embedded paper Möbius bands. We also carefully discuss the framing of folded ribbon links and how this is related to folded ribbonlength. The key idea is that a folded ribbon knot can be well-approximated by embedded paper Möbius bands or annuli (Lemma
3
). This means that we can use folded ribbon knot constructions and folded ribbonlength to give upper bounds on the infimal aspect ratio of embedded paper Möbius bands.

In Section
3
we introduce the idea of an accordion fold which is a key part of our Construction
8
for a folded ribbon multi-twist Möbius band. In Theorem
11
and Corollary
12
we show that the aspect ratio of a paper multi-twist Möbius band is bounded above by
3\sqrt{3}+\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
.

The remaining sections of the paper apply these ideas to folded ribbon knots and links. In Sections
4
and
5
, we look at the
(2,q)
-torus knots and twist knots. Both of these knots are constructed from a number of half-twists. The ends of the half-twists are then joined in specific ways to create the knots (see Figures
10
and
15
). The idea is that like the multi-twist Möbius band, the half-twists can be made with finite ribbonlength, no matter how many half-twists are added. There is then a second finite amount of ribbon needed to join the ends of the half-twists to construct the torus and twist knots. These constructions prove that any
(2,q)
-torus knot and twist knot with arbitrarily high crossing number can be made with finite folded ribbonlength (see Theorems
16
,
23
, and
24
). These results reveal just how different folded ribbon knots are from knots made out of physical rope. We know that the ropelength of any knot
K
is bounded below by the crossing number:
{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)^{3/4}\leq{\operatorname{Rop}}(K)
.

We pause here to encourage the reader to find long strips of paper with which they can recreate the constructions of multi-twist Möbius bands and folded ribbon knots found in this paper. Many of the constructions will make more sense that way.

2.3.
Multi-twist Möbius Bands and Annuli

Following Schwartz
[
33
,
36
]
, we formally define an
embedded paper Möbius band
of aspect ratio
\lambda
as a
C^{\infty}
-smooth, arc-length preserving embedding of
M_{\lambda}
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Here,
M_{\lambda}
is the flat Möbius band obtained by identifying the length-1 sides of a
1\times\lambda
rectangle:

M_{\lambda}=([0,\lambda]\times[0,1])/((0,y)\sim(\lambda,1-y)).

Similarly, an
embedded paper cylinder of aspect ratio
\lambda
is a
C^{\infty}
-smooth, arc-length preserving embedding of the flat cylinder
\Gamma_{\lambda}=([0,\lambda]\times[0,1])/((0,y)\sim(\lambda,y))
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We refer to these embeddings collectively as
smooth paper bands
.

We pause to note that smooth paper bands are a more general class of objects than folded ribbon knots. Many paper bands can be folded flat in the plane forming folded ribbon knots, for example the two Möbius bands found in Figure
1
. However, there are smooth paper bands that cannot be folded flat in the plane. For example, the Möbius band made of three half-twists forming a cup-like shape found in
[
2
]
.

The language of ribbon linking number helps us formalize the definition of multi-twist Möbius bands and annuli. Following Hennessey
[
21
]
, the centerline of a smooth paper band is the image of
[0,\lambda]\times\{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\}
. We define an
n
half-twist paper band to be:

•

A paper Möbius band for which the boundary and centerline have linking number
\pm n
(for
n
odd).

•

A paper annulus for which one of the boundaries and the centerline have linking number
\pm n/2
(for
n
even).

One of the other key results in
[
21
]
is that there is a folded ribbon unknot which is a

•

topological Möbius band with ribbon linking number
\pm n
(for
n
odd).

•

topological annulus with ribbon linking number
\pm n/2
(for
n
even).

There are thus two different, but related objects of study. To help keep things straight we also call an
n
half-twist paper Möbius band/annulus a
(multi-twist) paper Möbius band/annulus
. We will call a framed folded ribbon unknot a
(multi-twist) folded ribbon Möbius band or annulus
as appropriate. In
[
15
]
, the first author with Troy Larsen gave the first upper bounds on the folded ribbonlength of folded ribbon annuli.

Hennessey achieves his upper bounds on the aspect ratios of smooth paper bands by proving that a folded ribbon unknot (with appropriate ribbon linking number) can be well approximated by smooth paper bands (see
[
21
]
Section 3.3). We can generalize his arguments to show that any folded ribbon knot can be well approximated by a smooth paper band. After all, folded ribbon knots are either topologically a Möbius band or annulus.

Lemma 3

(Approximation)
.

For every folded ribbon knot
K_{w}
, there is a sequence of smooth paper bands in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which converge pointwise to
K_{w}
, and whose aspect ratios converge to
{\operatorname{Rib}}(K_{w})
. The centerline of
K_{w}
and the center line of the paper bands have the same knot type. ∎

3.
Accordion Folds and the Möbius band

We wish to construct a folded ribbon Möbius band with as many half-twists as we like. To do this, we follow Hennessey’s construction which uses the “belt trick” (see
[
26
]
). Here a strip of paper is coiled around itself many times, then the ends are pulled apart without allowing any rotation. This turns the coils into half-twists, and is a physical interpretation of the well-known “link, twist, writhe” formula (
{\operatorname{Lk}}(K_{w})={\operatorname{Tw}}(K_{w})+{\operatorname{Wr}}(K_{w})
) for a knotted ribbon (see for instance
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
17
,
40
]
). A moment’s experimentation reveals that the tricky part of this construction involves moving the end of the ribbon at the center of the coil so it may be joined to the end on the outside of the coil. Hennessey’s solution
[
21
]
is to create an “escape accordion” of fixed length as illustrated in Figure
5
. In the notation and language of folded ribbon knots, this corresponds to creating an alternating sequence of left then right overfolds each of fold angle
\pi/2
. If the escape accordion is unfolded, then the fold pattern on the ribbon is a sequence of mountain and valley folds of parallel lines at a
\pi/4

.

Assume there is an accordion fold with fold angle
\theta
and distance

-unit along the knot is

Proof.

The distance

3.2.
Möbius band

We now give a detailed description of the construction of a multi-twist folded ribbon Möbius band using an escape accordion (following Hennessey’s ideas
[
21
]
). Starting with an oriented piece of folded ribbon, we construct an escape accordion with fold angle
\theta
and distance
d
. At this point we could continue the accordion fold by adding
V
-units, or we can wrap the ribbon around the escape accordion. We wrap the ribbon around the escape accordion by constructing a number of
half-wraps
: here, we fold an alternating sequence of a left underfold followed by a right overfold, each with the same fold angle
\theta
and the same constant distance
d
between each vertex of the corresponding knot diagram. Thus, in Figure
7
, the center image shows an escape accordion with one half-wrap; while the right image shows an escape accordion with two half-wraps. Each half-wrap corresponds to a half-twist for the Möbius band.
We observe that the knot diagram for an accordion fold and a sequence of half-wraps looks the same, since the knot diagram does not show the folding information.

To help keep track of the distances, we label the half-wraps by the vertex of the knot diagram at the start of the half-wrap. Thus the end vertex of the escape accordion is the same vertex as the first wrap, or
v_{E}=w_{1}
. We add in labels

as we add in each half-wrap. Since the knot diagram is the same for a sequence half-wraps as it is for an accordion fold, we can compute the distances using the same arguments as Lemma
6
.

Corollary 7
.

Assume there is an escape accordion, with fold angle
\theta
and distance
d
, that is followed with a sequence of

half-wraps labeled

as described above. Then the distance between vertices

and

of the half-wraps along the knot,
d_{K}(w_{1},w_{2n+1})=2nd
. The distance between these points in the plane is
d(w_{1},w_{2n+1})=ns=2nd\sin(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})
.

Proof.

By construction, the distance between any two consecutive vertices

(for all

). Recall that distance
s=2d\sin(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})
is the distance between the start and end vertices of a

-unit. This is the same as the distances
d(w_{2k-1},w_{2k+1})
or
d(w_{2k},w_{2k+2})
, for all
k\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 10
.

It is straightforward to compute the ribbon linking number of these folded ribbon Möbius bands using the shortcuts developed in by the first author and Troy Larsen in
[

. These arguments were also used by Hennessey (see Section 3.2 in
[

Theorem 11
.

Any multi-twist folded ribbon Möbius band can be folded so that its folded ribbonlength is
3\sqrt{3}+\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
. This Möbius band is constructed from an escape accordion followed by half-wraps each with fold angle
\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{3}}
.

Proof.

Construct a multi-twist folded ribbon Möbius band
\mathcal{M}
from an escape accordion followed by
2n+1
half-wraps as described above. Proposition
9
gives the folded ribbonlength of this construction. We differentiate this folded ribbonlength with respect to
\theta
, and ignore the term involving distance
d
, as this may be made as small as we like.

\displaystyle\frac{d{\operatorname{Rib}}(\mathcal{M})}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\tan(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})\sec(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})+\frac{1}{2}\sec^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sec^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})-\csc^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sin^{3}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})+2\sin^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})-\cos^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})}{2\cos^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})\sin^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sin(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})+1)^{2}(2\sin(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})-1)}{2\cos^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})\sin^{2}(\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2}})}

When
\frac{d{\operatorname{Rib}}(\mathcal{M})}{d\theta}=0
, we find the solution
\theta=\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{3}}
in our range
0<\theta<\pi
. Another computation reveals that this critical point is a minimum of
{\operatorname{Rib}}(\mathcal{M})
.
Finally, when
\theta=\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{3}}
, we compute that

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Rib}}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\cos(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{6}})}+\frac{1}{\cos(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{6}})\sin(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{6}})}+\tan(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{6}})+2nd(1+\sin(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{6}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+2nd(1+\frac{1}{2})=3\sqrt{3}+3nd\leq 5.197.

Since distance
d
can be made as small as we like the last inequality and theorem follow.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Assume a folded ribbon
(2n+1)
-twist knot has been made following Construction
21
. Given the corresponding diagram in Figure
20
, the distance along the knot diagram from
M
to
N
to
P
is
d_{K}(M,P)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
, the planar distance
d(P,M)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{1}{2}
, and the distance from
J
to
T
is
d(J,T)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
.

Proof.

To compute the distance
d_{K}(M,P)
, we look at diagram on the left in Figure
20
. This shows the details in Step 2 of the clasp in Construction
21
. Fold angle
\angle MNP=\frac{\pi}{6}
, thus the geometry of the fold at
N
means angle
\angle MNQ=\frac{\pi-\pi/6}{2}=\frac{5\pi}{12}
. From Step 2, we know that line segment
MN
is perpendicular to
PQ
, thus angle
\angle NQM=\frac{\pi}{12}
. We have assumed the width of the folded ribbon
w=1
, thus side
MQ
has length
\frac{1}{2}
. From triangle
\triangle MNQ
, we have
\frac{d(M,N)}{1/2}=\tan(\frac{\pi}{12})=2-\sqrt{3}
, hence
d(M,N)=1-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
. From triangle
\triangle MNP
, we have
\frac{d(M,N)}{d(N,P)}=\cos(\frac{\pi}{6})=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, hence
d(N,P)=\frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}-1
. Putting this altogether, we find

d_{K}(M,P)=(1-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})+(\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}-1)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}.

To find the planar distance
d(P,M)
, observe that
\frac{d(P,M)}{d(M,N)}=\tan(\frac{\pi}{6})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
. Therefore

d(P,M)=\frac{1-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}{\sqrt{3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{1}{2}.

To compute the distance
d(J,T)
, we look at the diagram on the right in Figure
20
. This shows the details in Step 4 of the clasp in Construction
21
. The path from
V
to
J
to
T
along the knot is the path end
D
takes as it folds to the left with fold angle
\frac{\pi}{3}
. Thus angle
\angle VJT=\frac{\pi}{3}
and the geometry of the fold at
J
means that angle
\angle QJT=\frac{\pi}{3}
too. Distance
d(Q,T)=\frac{1}{2}
as it is half of the ribbon width. Thus in right triangle
\triangle QTJ
, we have
d(J,T)=\frac{1/2}{\tan(\pi/3)}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
.
∎

We know from from Lemma
3
, that any folded ribbon knot can be well approximated by embedded paper bands with the same knot type. We expect the infimal aspect ratio of an embedded paper twist knot to be the same as in Corollary

.

How does Corollary
25
relate to other twist knots? Again, in
[
5
]
, we proved that any
n
-twist knot
K
can be constructed with folded ribbonlength
{\operatorname{Rib}}(K_{w})=n+6
. This means a figure-8 knot (a
T_{2}
knot) can be constructed with folded ribbonlength

, the lowest found so far. This bound also beats Corollary
25
for
n=1,2,3,\dots,9,11
. This again illustrates the difference between the constructions for small crossing knots versus constructions showing universal upper bounds on folded ribbonlength.

We also observe that Corollary
25
gives us a second knot family that proves Theorem
17
(which gives the lower bound on the folded ribbon crossing number problem). Are there other infinite families of knots with uniform upper bounds on folded ribbonlength? The pretzel link family
P(p,q,r)
is a family of knots and links which are made of three sets of half-twists joined in a specific way. Thus we might expect to be able to again modify Construction
15
to show this family also has a uniform upper bound on folded ribbonlength. On the other hand, we don’t expect this result to be the best upper bound for pretzel knots with a small crossing number. Our joint work in
[
5
]
gives an upper bound on folded ribbonlength of
P(p,q,r)
pretzel links of
{\operatorname{Rib}}([P(p,q,r)])\leq|p|+|q|+|r|+6
.

We do not expect the methods here to generalize to other torus knots as there are more strands involved with the twists. Indeed, in
[
13
]
, the first author and co-authors proved that any
(p,q)
torus link type
L
(with
p\geq q\geq 2)
contains contains a folded ribbon link
L_{w}
such that
{\operatorname{Rib}}(L_{w})\leq 2p
. When we bring in crossing number, we get
{\operatorname{Rib}}([L])\leq c({\operatorname{Cr}}(L))^{1/2}
for a constant
c
(see
[
13
]
). However, is it also true that
c({\operatorname{Cr}}(L))^{1/2}\leq{\operatorname{Rib}}([L])
in this case? What about other knot and link families? We end with the following open question.

Open Question 26
.

Is there an infinite family of knots for which
c\cdot{\operatorname{Cr}}(K)^{\alpha}\leq{\operatorname{Rib}}(K)
must have
\alpha>0
for some constant
c
? If there is such a family, can we find one where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
?
