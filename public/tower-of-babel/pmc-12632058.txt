Prognostic value of antepartum LDH serum levels in predicting adverse maternal outcomes in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Nguyen Manh Thang, Nguyen Dinh Ky, Truong Thanh Huong, Nguyen Quynh Anh, Nguyen Dieu My, Nguyen Thi Huyen Anh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are a leading cause of maternal mortality, particularly in low-resource settings. While lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) has been proposed as a prognostic biomarker, its clinical utility is limited by a lack of consensus on a standardized threshold for interpretation. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive performance of antenatal LDH for adverse maternal outcomes and establish a clinically applicable cutoff for risk stratification.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 336 pregnant women with HDP admitted to a national tertiary referral hospital in Vietnam. Serum LDH levels were measured before delivery, and treating clinicians were blinded to the results to prevent performance bias. The primary outcome was a composite of adverse maternal events: eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, acute kidney injury, hepatic injury, pulmonary edema, and maternal death. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were performed to assess associations and predictive performance.ResultsAs a single predictor, LDH demonstrated good overall discrimination for adverse maternal outcomes (AUC = 0.793). In a multivariable logistic regression model, adjusting for proteinuria, highest diastolic blood pressure (DBP), highest systolic blood pressure (SBP), urea, creatinine, maternal age, gravidity, body mass index (BMI), platelets, gestational diabetes mellitus, an LDH level ≥ 254.7 U/L remained a strong and independent predictor of the primary outcome (aOR 9.67; 95% CI 3.29–28.46). Notably, in this high-risk cohort, traditional factors like proteinuria and maternal age were not significant independent predictors in this adjusted model. The multivariable model demonstrated strong internal validity, with all the predictors having a variance inflation factor (VIF) < 5, indicating an absence of multicollinearity and, therefore, stable regression estimates.ConclusionAntepartum LDH level ≥ 254.7 U/L is a strong, independent predictor of adverse maternal outcomes in patients with HDP. These findings, combined with the test’s affordability and accessibility, underscore the potential of LDH as a valuable tool for improving risk stratification, particularly in low-resource settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08293-6.

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) affect approximately 5% − 10% of pregnancies worldwide and are a significant cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [1]. Their impact is particularly profound in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where they account for nearly 25% of all maternal deaths [2]. This disproportionate burden highlights the urgent need for accurate yet feasible prognostic tools that can be deployed across various levels of healthcare infrastructure, particularly in resource-limited settings where timely referral and intervention are crucial.

Several risk predictive models, such as the fullPIERS and miniPIERS models, have been developed to assist in the risk stratification of women with preeclampsia, with an area under the curve (AUC) ranging from 0.70 to 0.88 [3, 4]. However, these models are not yet widely adopted in clinical practice, particularly in LMICs, owing to their dependency on multiple clinical and laboratory inputs. In parallel, interest in low-cost, widely accessible biomarkers that reflect underlying disease severity is increasing. One such biomarker is lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), an intracellular enzyme released during cellular damage and hemolysis. Its elevation in serum has been pathophysiologically linked to endothelial dysfunction and microangiopathic injury, which are hallmarks of severe HDP [5, 6].

Although multiple studies have investigated the prognostic utility of LDH in HDP, the proposed clinical thresholds vary considerably. For example, Moharana et al. reported significantly high levels (> 800 U/L) in severe preeclampsia, whereas Nasir et al. reported much lower mean values (303–489 U/L) in similar populations [7, 8]. This inconsistency, which sometimes exceeds a twofold difference, even across LMIC settings, reflects a lack of consensus and challenges clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the predictive performance of antenatal LDH for adverse maternal outcomes in women with HDP, thereby establishing a clinically applicable cutoff for risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective cohort study was conducted at the National Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology in Hanoi, Vietnam, a 1,000-bed national tertiary referral center for obstetric and neonatal care. As a national tertiary referral center, the National Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology primarily manages complex and severe cases referred from lower-level hospitals.

Participants

Our study participants included consecutive women who were diagnosed with HDP and who presented to the outpatient clinic or obstetric ward at the National Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology. Additional inclusion criteria were being 18 years or older and having a confirmed pregnancy with a gestational age of 20 weeks or more. Women were excluded if they had preexisting chronic renal disease, significant hepatic dysfunction, or known major hematological disorders; known significant fetal anomalies or intrauterine fetal demise at the time of enrollment; or were concurrently participating in an interventional clinical trial. All women provided written informed consent before enrollment in the study.

For descriptive analysis, patients were classified into three distinct subgroups based on their clinical presentation at admission:

Non-preeclampsia group: This group included women with hypertensive disorders who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for preeclampsia. It comprised two subgroups: women with gestational hypertension (new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation without proteinuria or severe features) and women with preexisting chronic hypertension. Mild preeclampsia: This group was defined by the onset of hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation, accompanied by new-onset proteinuria, but without any severe features. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 235 participants. To increase the statistical power for multivariable regression analyses and to account for a potential rate of approximately 15% for incomplete data or loss to follow-up, we aimed for a larger enrollment target. Therefore, our final study cohort of 336 participants was considered more than adequate to ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings.

Variables and data sources

Primary exposure and covariates

Upon enrollment and before any delivery-related interventions, a 5 mL venous blood sample was collected. The primary exposure variable, serum LDH, was quantified within two hours by the hospital’s central laboratory via a standardized enzymatic spectrophotometric assay (Cobas c702, Roche Diagnostics). LDH results were used for research purposes only to mitigate performance bias and were not disclosed to treating clinicians or participants. Covariates, including maternal demographics, antenatal characteristics, and medical history, were extracted from electronic medical records and cross-verified with the participants. Blood pressure was measured twice while the participants were in a seated position via a validated automated sphygmomanometer, and the mean value was recorded.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was a composite measure defined as the first occurrence of any of the following seven adverse maternal outcomes: eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, acute kidney injury, hepatic injury, pulmonary edema, and maternal death. The secondary outcomes included the individual components of the composite, gestational age at delivery, and mode of delivery. All potential outcomes were prospectively recorded until hospital discharge. Adverse outcomes based on standardized clinical criteria and extracted directly from patient records by the study investigator, who was blinded to LDH levels.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0. All variables included in the analysis were complete, with no missing values identified; therefore, no imputation techniques were required. Continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, while categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The primary predictive performance of LDH was assessed for discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The optimal cutoff value for LDH to predict the primary composite outcome was determined from this analysis by maximizing the Youden index. To assess whether LDH was an independent predictor, a multivariable logistic regression model was used to estimate the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Based on clinical significance, the following covariates were included in the final model: proteinuria, highest DBP, highest SBP, urea, creatinine, maternal age, gravidity, body mass index (BMI), platelets, gestational diabetes mellitus. Multicollinearity was evaluated with VIFs, with a VIF < 5 considered acceptable.

Discussion

This study provides strong evidence for the prognostic utility of antenatal LDH in women with HDP. The AUCs for LDH in predicting adverse maternal outcomes in the overall cohort, mild preeclampsia cohort, and severe preeclampsia cohort were 0.793, 0.811, and 0.778, respectively. These values highlight the potential of LDH for early risk identification, situating our findings within a growing body of evidence. For example, Teklemariam et al. reported a higher AUC of 0.963 using a cutoff of 376.5 U/L in an Ethiopian cohort [11]. Moreover, our identified optimal LDH cutoff for the total population, 254.7 U/L, yielded not only a clinically meaningful balance of sensitivity (70.83%) and specificity (85.07%), but also a useful PPV of 44.15% and a strong NPV of 94.59%. LDH also demonstrated independent prognostic strength in our multivariate logistic regression model. After adjustment for major confounders, LDH ≥ 254.7 U/L remained significantly associated with adverse maternal outcomes, with a nearly tenfold increased risk of adverse maternal outcomes (aOR = 9.67; 95% CI: 3.29–28.46). Burwick and Feinberg reported an aOR of 3.05 (95% CI: 1.4–6.7) for LDH ≥ 400 U/L [12]. Similarly, Xia et al. reported that even slight LDH increases (144–167 U/L) were independently associated with severe preeclampsia (aOR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03; p = 0.003) and shortened gestational duration (HR = 1.009; 95% CI: 1.006–1.013; p < 0.001) [13].

No universal LDH threshold exists for prognostication in HDP, with reported cutoffs ranging from under 300 U/L to over 800 U/L [14]. Our identified threshold of 254.7 U/L lies at the lower end of this spectrum, a finding that becomes clinically logical when contextualized within different care settings. In South Asian studies, thresholds exceeding 600–800 U/L often reflect patients presenting late in the disease course, scenarios where LDH serves as a marker of existing organ damage [7, 15–17]. In contrast, more moderate thresholds (e.g., Nasir et al. reporting 303–489 U/L, Teklemariam et al. reporting 376.5 U/L) are observed in settings with earlier detection or milder endpoints [8, 11]. Our study, which was conducted at a national tertiary referral center with early intervention protocols, likely captured women at a transition point before the onset of overt multiorgan dysfunction. Thus, a lower LDH cutoff may serve as a sensitive early warning marker. These threshold differences are not contradictory but rather reflect various positions along the HDP severity spectrum, shaped by factors such as access to healthcare, disease stage, and timing of management. While a level greater than 800 U/L may indicate crisis-level damage, a threshold of 250 U/L may identify patients requiring early escalation of care. This perspective reframes LDH as a dynamic, context-dependent biomarker, supporting the need for population-specific, intervention-relevant cutoffs rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

The strong prognostic performance of LDH observed in our study is firmly grounded in its role as a direct biochemical surrogate for the multisystemic endothelial injury that defines the progression of HDP. Placental-derived antiangiogenic factors induce widespread endothelial dysfunction, microvascular damage, and tissue hypoperfusion, all of which contribute to elevated LDH levels [6]. Two principal mechanisms account for this rise: (i) microangiopathic hemolysis, which releases intracellular LDH and is the defining pathogenic event of HELLP syndrome, and (ii) ischemic injury from vasospasm affecting multiple organs, leading to hepatocellular necrosis, cerebrovascular complications, and renal dysfunction [18, 19]. Thus, elevated LDH reflects both hemolysis and multiorgan ischemia, explaining its robust association with severe maternal outcomes and supporting its potential role in clinical risk stratification.

Although international guidelines rely on hypertension, proteinuria, and organ damage for diagnosing and stratifying preeclampsia, real-world evidence suggests that these markers may be insufficient to detect early or atypical cases [9, 20]. One of the most striking findings of this study was that neither elevated blood pressure nor proteinuria - the classic hallmarks of preeclampsia - were independent predictors of adverse maternal outcomes in our adjusted model. We attribute this to a likely “ceiling effect” within our cohort at a tertiary referral center. In this setting, a majority of patients were already severely hypertensive upon admission, which diminishes the discriminatory power of blood pressure to predict which individuals will develop further complications. This finding does not imply that blood pressure and proteinuria are unimportant for diagnosis. Instead, it suggests that once the diagnosis of severe HDP is established, these traditional markers may be insufficient for prognostication. These limitations highlight the importance of evaluating our findings within the landscape of established prognostic tools for HDP. Angiogenic biomarkers, particularly the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, exhibit excellent predictive performance (AUC > 0.85), but their implementation is limited in LMICs due to resource requirements, including the need for specialized platforms [21]. Alternatively, models such as fullPIERS achieve good accuracy (AUC = 0.75–0.88), but they require multiple inputs, including laboratory data, which are not always available in resource-limited settings [3, 22]. To address these constraints, the miniPIERS model was developed as a simplified, nonlaboratory-based tool for low-resource contexts [4]. Our LDH-based model demonstrated comparable performance (AUC = 0.793) when a single, widely available biomarker was used. Rather than replacing existing models, LDH could be most effective when integrated with miniPIERS in a two-step hybrid strategy: miniPIERS for initial triage at the community level, followed by LDH measurement at referral centers to refine prognosis and guide escalation of care. This approach aligns with the concept of “smart risk stratification”, which is a scalable, cost-effective framework that optimizes both accessibility and physiological relevance and could meaningfully reduce maternal morbidity and mortality in LMICs.

A notable clinical characteristic of the study cohort was the exceptionally high rate of cesarean deliveries, which ranged from 81.8% to 96.7% (Table 1). This rate is consistent with the obstetric context at our institution, a tertiary referral center with a general cesarean rate of 54.96% [23]. At our center, expedited delivery via cesarean section is the standard of care for managing preeclampsia to mitigate the risk of adverse maternal outcomes. Consequently, this high prevalence of cesarean delivery represents a standardized clinical protocol for this specific high-risk population, rather than an intervention bias that could confound the prognostic value of LDH. Nevertheless, labor induction was offered to suitable candidates, which resulted in 10 successful vaginal deliveries within the study.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, it was conducted at a single tertiary referral center with a selected patient population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader obstetric cohorts. Secondly, LDH was measured only once upon admission, which precluded an assessment of the dynamic progression of LDH levels throughout pregnancy. Finally, the exceptionally high odds ratio for creatinine should be interpreted with caution, as its wide confidence interval suggests statistical instability, likely due to the small number of events in one subgroup. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight clear directions for future research. To address the issue of generalizability, multicenter studies are needed to validate the proposed LDH cutoff in more diverse populations. Furthermore, longitudinal studies tracking LDH levels at multiple time points are warranted to determine if the trend of LDH change is a more powerful predictor than a single measurement. Ultimately, this work should aim to develop and validate robust multiparametric prognostic models that incorporate LDH with other clinical indicators to enhance risk stratification and guide more tailored clinical management.

Conclusion

This study highlights the prognostic value of antepartum serum LDH in predicting adverse maternal outcomes in patients with HDP. An LDH threshold of ≥ 254.7 U/L was independently associated with a nearly tenfold increased risk of severe complications. In contrast, traditional markers, such as blood pressure and proteinuria, showed no significant predictive value in our multivariable model. These findings support the incorporation of LDH into routine risk assessment, particularly in low-resource settings. As a widely available and low-cost test, LDH may be integrated into future multiparametric models to enhance early risk stratification and guide individualized clinical management.
