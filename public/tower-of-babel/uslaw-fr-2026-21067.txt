Investigation Into Conditions Affecting Shipping in the Foreign

AGENCY: Federal Maritime Commission.

ACTION: Notice of proposed rulemaking.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

SUMMARY: The Federal Maritime Commission (Commission) proposes to 
adjust or meet unfavorable shipping conditions created by the 
Government of Spain. The Commission proposes a per-voyage fee on all 
Spanish vessels of $200,000 and restrictions on the import of certain 
Spanish goods into the United States. The Commission also proposes 
using additional statutory authority to impose these remedies because 
the Government of Spain has unduly impaired the access of U.S. flag 
vessels to trade between foreign ports. The Commission herein offers an 
opportunity for a public hearing. The Commission invites public comment 
on all aspects of this proposed rule.

DATES: Comments and related material must be received by the Commission 
on or before December 14, 2026.

I. Background

A. Legal Authority

    The Federal Maritime Commission (Commission) is charged with 
investigating and prescribing regulations to adjust or meet conditions 
unfavorable to shipping in the foreign trade of the United States which 
arise out of or result from the laws or regulations of a foreign 
country. 46 U.S.C. 42101(a). The Commission must also investigate when 
a foreign government is unduly impairing the access of a vessel 
documented under the laws of the United States to ocean trade between 
foreign ports and take appropriate action to remedy any such violation. 
46 U.S.C. 41108(d). The Commission is empowered to remedy foreign 
government actions that impair the access of U.S. flag vessels to ocean 
trade between foreign ports by Chapter 423 of Title 46, United States 
Code. The remedies provided by Chapter 421 and Chapter 423 of Title 46 
authorize the Commission to charge fees to foreign-flagged vessels 
entering U.S. ports and limit the import into the United States of 
amounts or types of cargo.

B. Investigation

    The Commission initiated this investigation in December 2024 under 
Title 46, Chapter 421 of the United States Code to determine whether 
the Government of Spain (Spain) is creating general or special 
conditions unfavorable to shipping in U.S. foreign trade by barring 
certain vessels from its ports, including vessels transporting cargo 
under the United States Maritime Administration's (MARAD) Maritime 
Security Program (MSP). Docket No. 24-30; FMC-2024-0022. Initial 
reports that prompted this investigation indicated that Spain had 
refused port access in November 2024 to three MSP vessels on the 
grounds that they were carrying cargo bound for or coming from Israel. 
Spain reportedly cited European Union (EU) regulations as the basis for 
these port refusals.\1\

A. Government of Spain 3
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \3\ Government of Spain (FMC-2024-0022-8217).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Spain submitted a comment in response to the Commission's December 
2024 notice but not the December 2025 notice. In its comment, Spain 
argued that the Commission does not have power to compel Spain's 
participation in this proceeding, the Commission does not have 
jurisdiction over Spain given sovereign immunity, and the appropriate 
remedy is for the Commission to pursue a diplomatic solution through 
the U.S. Secretary of State. Moreover, Spain argued that it must follow 
Spanish, EU, and international laws that require it to prevent the 
transit of defense and dual-use goods through its ports and Spain is 
doing so in a non-discriminatory manner. Spain's arguments do not 
address the questions posed by the Commission, and Spain's unquestioned 
sovereignty to act within its borders does not affect the Commission's 
authority to investigate and regulate conditions unfavorable to U.S. 
foreign shipping under Chapter 421.
    First, the Commission cannot compel Spain to participate in this 
proceeding. The Commission's power to remedy unfavorable conditions to 
shipping in foreign trade is the power to take action within the 
jurisdiction of the United States. The Commission can deny vessels 
entry into the United States, impose fees on vessels coming to the 
United States, and take other appropriate actions to adjust or meet 
unfavorable shipping conditions. 46 U.S.C. 42106. The Commission cannot 
force Spain to take any action or repeal any Spanish or EU law or 
regulation. Likewise, Spain cannot infringe on U.S. sovereignty by 
demanding the Commission take or refrain from any action.
    Further, Spain's compliance with Spanish, EU, and international law 
is not a defense to a Chapter 421 investigation. Chapter 421 
presupposes that Spain's actions are consistent with its laws and 
regulations. Section 42101 obligates the Commission to prescribe 
regulations meeting unfavorable conditions that ``arise out of or 
result from laws or regulations of a foreign country.'' This statutory 
language shows Congress assumed that the unfavorable conditions arise 
from compliance with foreign laws or regulations, not non-compliance or 
illegal behavior. The Commission's investigation is into whether the 
conditions created by Spain result in unfavorable conditions affecting 
shipping in the foreign trade, not whether Spain was required by its 
own legal system to create those conditions.
    Spain's claims that it is enforcing its laws in a non-
discriminatory manner without regard to flag is also not a defense 
against an investigation of whether Spain has created unfavorable 
conditions in the foreign trade. Spain claims that it did not reject 
docking authorization to certain vessels on the basis of a vessel's 
flag or port of origin, but that it only rejected the transit of the 
individual containers. Spain, however, failed to support these claims 
in its response, and they are inconsistent with the evidence received 
in this investigation. The evidence shows that Spain rejected docking 
privileges for U.S. flag vessels and did not merely deny offloading or 
transshipment of specific containers. Moreover, the argument that it 
did not discriminate based on flag or port of origin does not rebut a 
finding that Spain's actions created unfavorable conditions. 
Selectively enforcing laws or regulations to deny docking privileges to 
specific vessels can create, and has created, unfavorable conditions 
even if Spain allows access to other vessels of the same flag or 
origin.
    Finally, it is true, as Spain contends, that a diplomatic solution 
may resolve this investigation. Spain is uniquely positioned to make 
that occur. But until that time, Spain continues to create unfavorable 
shipping conditions. The Commission is charged with meeting or 
adjusting those conditions while they persist. The Commission can 
rescind any final remedies once a diplomatic solution has been reached, 
but does not intend to delay the proposed remedies based on a 
hypothetical solution.\4\

III. Investigation Results

    The Commission's investigation provided conclusive evidence that 
Spain's actions have created unfavorable shipping conditions in the 
foreign trade resulting from its laws or regulations. Spain has 
publicly denied docking privileges to multiple vessels and publicly 
stated it will deny docking privileges to certain vessels in the 
future. Those vessels had to reroute to non-Spanish ports and omit 
Spanish ports from future voyages. Spain's policy of port denials is 
ongoing. Confidentially submitted evidence confirms that Spain's policy 
has created unfavorable conditions. The Commission concludes that 
Spain's actions create unfavorable shipping conditions in the foreign 
trade resulting from its laws or regulations.
    The Commission's investigation returned public and non-public 
evidence of Spain's actions. In May 2024, Spain publicly announced a 
policy of preventing vessels from docking at its ports and Spain's 
transport minister identified a vessel that had been refused docking 
privileges as the Marianne Danica.\14\ In November 2024, Spain's 
foreign ministry said two vessels participating in MSP, the Maersk 
Seletar and the Maersk Denver, ``will not stop in Spain.'' Those 
vessels did not dock in Spain. Another MSP vessel, the Maersk Hartford, 
with a stop planned at a Spanish port later that month also did not 
dock in Spain. Spain publicly reinforced and expanded this policy in 
September 2025.\15\

    Based on this record, the Commission concludes that Spain has 
created conditions unfavorable to shipping in the foreign trade through 
its laws or regulations. Spain has publicly announced and enforced a 
policy that has prevented MSP vessels from docking at Spanish ports. 
MSP vessels were turned away in at least three instances from Spanish 
ports. Publicly, Spain has only reinforced and expanded this public 
policy since those vessels were turned away in November 2024. Almost 
all MSP vessels no longer dock at Spanish ports. This policy prevents 
U.S. flag vessels from carrying goods destined for Spanish ports and 
prevents them from picking up Spanish goods for carriage to non-Spanish 
ports. This policy creates conditions that directly impact the purposes 
for which Congress created the Commission. The exclusion of MSP 
vessels, vessels necessary for the national defense and the domestic 
and foreign commerce of the United States,\20\ prevents these vessels 
from providing shipping services essential for maintaining waterborne 
domestic and foreign commerce, especially when called upon to serve as 
naval and military auxiliary vessels in a time of national 
emergency.\21\ 46 U.S.C. 42101, citing 46 U.S.C. 50101. Further, the 
Spanish policy impairs the efficient, competitive, and economical 
transportation of goods in the ocean commerce of the United States by 
preventing U.S. flag vessels from transporting goods destined for, or 
coming from, Spain. 46 U.S.C. 40101(2). The policy directly conflicts 
with the Commission's purpose of encouraging the development of an 
economically sound and efficient liner fleet of U.S.-flagged vessels 
capable of meeting national security needs and supporting commerce, as 
it prevents exactly those vessels from carrying goods to or from Spain. 
46 U.S.C. 40101(3). Finally, the policy undermines the Commission's 
ability to promote a competitive and efficient system for the carriage 
of goods by water in the foreign commerce of the United States since 
MSP vessels can no longer compete for cargos destined for, or coming 
from, Spain. 46 U.S.C. 40101(4). This record supports the Commission's 
conclusion that Spain's laws or regulations are creating conditions 
unfavorable to shipping in the foreign trade.

IV. Section 41108(d)

    The Commission commenced this investigation pursuant to its Chapter 
421 authority to remedy unfavorable conditions, but the evidence also 
demonstrates that Spain has prevented U.S. flag vessels from docking at 
its ports, and by doing so prevented these vessels from delivering 
goods to Spanish ports from other foreign ports and from loading 
Spanish goods for delivery in foreign ports. This supports the 
conclusion that Spain's actions have potentially impaired the access of 
U.S. flag vessels to ocean trade between foreign ports. As a result, 
the Commission, through this notice, is providing an opportunity for 
Spain to request a hearing pursuant to 46 U.S.C. 41108(d).
    The record shows that Spain denied access to its ports to three MSP 
vessels in November 2024. Spain denied docking privileges to the Maersk 
Seletar and the Maersk Denver in November 2024. Later in November 2024, 
Spain did not permit another MSP vessel, the Maersk Hartford, to dock 
at its ports. Spain's policy of denying docking privileges to these 
vessels, which are documented under the laws of the United States, 
still prevents them from exporting goods to Spain and from carrying 
Spanish goods to foreign ports. This continues to impair the ability of 
these vessels to engage in ocean trade between foreign ports. MSP 
vessels cannot compete fairly against non-MSP vessels for cargo 
destined for Spain or to carry Spanish exports.
    Section 41108(d) gives the Commission power to impose remedies 
authorized by sections 42304 and 42305 of Title 46 of the United States 
Code, but that section requires that the Commission do so only after 
notice and opportunity for a hearing. Because the Commission's 
investigation has focused on Chapter 421 violations to this point, the 
Commission has not yet provided Spain with an opportunity for a hearing 
concerning section 41108(d). Through this notice, the Commission 
informs Spain that it intends to consider, and potentially impose 
remedies for, Spain's actions that impair the ability of U.S. flag 
vessels to ocean trade between foreign ports. The Commission also, 
through this notice, provides Spain with the opportunity to request a 
hearing before the Commission. Should Spain request a hearing, its 
representatives should use the Further Contact

[[Page 65519]]

Information provided at the beginning of this notice to file such a 
request with the Commission.

V. Proposed Remedies

    The Commission proposes two sets of remedies to meet or adjust the 
unfavorable shipping conditions created by Spain in the foreign trade 
of the United States. First, the Commission proposes to levy fees on 
Spanish-flagged vessels entering U.S. ports. 46 U.S.C. 42106(4), 
42304(a)(4). Second, the Commission proposes to limit imports into the 
United States of some or all categories of Spanish goods. The 
Commission intends to exercise its authority to restrict the type of 
cargo carried to U.S. ports and work with relevant U.S. government 
agencies to enforce these restrictions. 46 U.S.C. 42106(4), 42109.

A. Per Voyage Fees

    First, the Commission proposes imposing fees on a per-voyage basis 
on Spanish-flagged vessels calling at U.S. ports. One of the 
Commission's prior uses of this authority came during an investigation 
into Japanese harbor services monopolies and Japan's licensing of 
terminal operators and stevedores.\22\ In that investigation, the 
Commission imposed a $100,000 fee per-voyage on vessels operated by 
Japanese carriers in a 1997 final rule. That investigation differed 
because there were many Japanese carriers and many Japanese-flagged 
vessels entering U.S. ports, whereas there are relatively few Spanish-
flagged vessels that call at U.S. ports. Still, the Commission now 
proposes similar remedies to those that led to a bilateral resolution 
in that investigation. The Commission proposes a per-voyage fee on 
Spanish-flagged vessels of $200,000, which is the approximate 
adjustment for inflation between 1997 and 2026. 46 U.S.C. 42106(4), 
42304(a)(4). This remedy alone will not fully meet or adjust the 
unfavorable conditions created by Spain, but it has been effective 
before and the Commission proposes to use it again.

B. Trade Category Import Restrictions

    Second, the Commission reviewed the Harmonized Tariff Schedule 
(HTS) to choose Spanish goods that could be limited from import to 
appropriately meet or adjust the unfavorable shipping conditions 
created by Spain. The HTS of the United States is a U.S. nomenclature 
system used to classify traded goods based on their material 
composition, product name, and/or intended function. The HTS is based 
on the international harmonized system, which is the global system of 
nomenclature applied to most world trade in goods. The international 
harmonized system maps exactly with the U.S. version to the 6-digit 
level while the HTS (U.S.) further stratifies traded goods into even 
more specific categories. The HTS is designed so that each article 
falls into only one category. It is divided into chapters, each of 
which has a 2-digit number. Each product category within the various 
chapters is designated by 4, 6, 8, or 10 digits. The 4-digit categories 
are called ``headings.'' The 6-, 8- and 10-digit classifications are 
called ``subheadings.''
    The Commission proposes limiting the import of trade categories 
from Spain to appropriately meet or adjust the unfavorable shipping 
conditions created by Spain's actions.\23\ The Commission emphasizes 
that this is a proposed list of trade categories that the Commission 
may limit, and that the Commission may choose a lesser remedy from 
these options. The Commission may choose to broadly limit imports, 
impose more targeted limitations, limit only certain of the trade 
categories described below, or some combination of these options. The 
Commission is soliciting public comment to help it decide the 
appropriate remedy to meet or adjust the unfavorable shipping 
conditions created by Spain. These unfavorable conditions have grave 
consequences for the efficiency and reliability of the international 
ocean shipping supply chain, as well as the ability of the Commission 
to promote the growth and development of U.S. exports, encourage the 
development of a liner fleet of U.S. vessels capable of meeting 
national security needs and supporting commerce, and supporting the 
U.S. merchant marine. These purposes are frustrated by Spain preventing 
U.S. vessels from importing U.S. goods into Spain and exporting Spanish 
goods to foreign ports. The unfavorable conditions created by Spain 
that deny U.S. vessels access to Spanish ports and Spanish goods 
require a significant mix of remedies to meet or adjust them. 
Therefore, the Commission has proposed to limit meaningful volumes of 
Spanish imports to approximate the damage done to U.S. carriers and the 
liner fleet of U.S. vessels capable of meeting national security needs.

1. Perfumery and Cosmetics
    The Commission proposes limiting imports of HTS chapter 33, 
Perfumery and Cosmetics. Perfumes and Toilet Waters (HTS 33.0300) 
imports were valued at nearly $700 million in 2025. At the Chapter 
level (2-digit HTS 33) Essential Oils Etc.; Perfumery, Cosmetic Etc. 
Preps, represents nearly $940 million in imports to the United States 
from Spain in 2025. The Commission proposes limiting imports of all 
goods falling under the HTS 33 chapter.
2. Oils and Fats
    The Commission proposes limiting imports of HTS chapter 15, Oils 
and Fats. United States imports from Spain under this category at the 
two-digit level in 2025 were $1.15 billion (Animal, Vegetable or 
Microbial Fats and Oils Etc.). At the four-digit level, the United 
States imports $836.9 million from Spain under HTS 15.09 (Olive Oil & 
Its Fractions, Not Chemically Modified). The Commission proposes 
limiting imports of all goods falling under the HTS 15 chapter.
3. Ceramics
    The Commission proposes limiting imports of HTS chapter 69, 
Ceramics. United States imports from Spain under this category in 2025 
at the two-digit level were $634.7 million (Ceramic Products). At the 
four-digit level, the United States imports $523.7 million under HTS 
69.07 (Ceramic Tiles and Mosaic Cubes) from Spain. The Commission 
proposes limiting imports of all goods falling under the HTS 69 
chapter.
4. Articles of Stone
    The Commission proposes limiting imports of HTS chapter 68, 
Articles of Stone. United States imports from Spain under this category 
in 2025 totaled $270.6 million (Articles of Stone, Plaster, Cement, 
Asbestos, Mica Etc.). At the 4-digit level, the United States imports 
$176.9 million under HTS 68.10 (Articles of stone, plaster, cement, and 
related products) from Spain. The Commission proposes limiting imports 
of all goods falling under the HTS 68 chapter.

5. Sugar and Confectionaries
    The Commission proposes limiting imports of HTS chapter 17, Sugar 
and Confectionaries. U.S. imports from Spain under this category in 
2025 sum to $138.8 million (sugar and confectionary products). The 
majority of this trade takes place under the category specific to a 
single 6-digit code, 17.0490 (sugar confection including white 
chocolate, no cocoa). The Commission proposes limiting imports of all 
goods falling under the HTS 17 chapter.

National Environmental Policy Act

    The Commission has determined that the National Environmental 
Policy Act (NEPA) does not apply to this action. NEPA only applies to 
``major federal actions.'' The terms ``major'' and ``Federal action'' 
each have independent force. NEPA applies only when both of these two 
criteria are met. The proposed rulemaking, if finalized, would be a 
``Federal action'' but not ``major'' and therefore not subject to NEPA. 
The Commission anticipates little to no environmental impact if the 
proposed changes are adopted. Proposed import restrictions merely 
restrict Spanish goods from import, but limited Spanish imports are 
expected to mostly be replaced by similar goods from other nations. 
Further, the fungibility of cargo containers means that carriers can 
easily replace lost Spanish cargo volume with volume of other cargo. 
Given this, the Commission has determined that there will be little to 
no impact if the proposed rule is adopted, and therefore NEPA does not 
apply to this action.

Sec.  551.2  Remedies to Meet or Adjust Conditions Unfavorable to 
Shipping in the Foreign Trade Created by the Government of Spain.

    (a) Conditions unfavorable to shipping in the foreign trade. The 
Federal Maritime Commission (Commission) after investigation has 
identified the following conditions unfavorable to shipping in the 
foreign trade of the United States created by the laws or regulations 
of the Government of Spain (Spain):
    (1) Spain announced a policy of preventing certain vessels from 
docking at Spanish ports.
    (2) Spain prevented U.S. flag vessels from docking at Spanish ports 
on at least three occasions.
    (3) Spain expanded and formalized its policy of preventing certain 
vessels from docking at Spanish ports.
    (b) Definitions--(1) Spanish vessel means a vessel registered under 
the Spanish flag, documented or registered under the laws of Spain.
    (2) Covered goods means goods falling within the trade categories 
listed below that are exported from Spain and intended for import into 
the United States.

(i) HTS 33--Perfumery and Cosmetics
(ii) HTS 15--Oils and Fats
(iii) HTS 69--Ceramics
(iv) HTS 68--Articles of Stone
(v) HTS 17--Sugar and Confectionaries

    (c) Assessment of fees. A fee of two hundred thousand dollars is 
assessed each time a Spanish vessel enters any port of the United 
States from any foreign port or place.

[[Page 65521]]

    (d) Refusal of clearance by the Secretary of the Department of 
Homeland Security. If any Spanish vessel subject to this section fails 
to pay any fee within the prescribed period, the Commission may request 
the Secretary of the Department of Homeland Security to refuse 
clearance required by 46 U.S.C. 60105 to that vessel or any other 
Spanish vessel. If any vessel, regardless of country of registration, 
transports any covered goods to a United States port and offloads those 
covered goods, the Commission may request the Secretary of Homeland 
Security to refuse clearance required by 46 U.S.C. 60105 to that vessel 
until the covered goods are reloaded onto that vessel.
    (e) Denial of entry to or detention at United States ports by the 
Secretary of the Department of Homeland Security. If any Spanish vessel 
fails to pay any fee required by this section within the prescribed 
period or if any vessel transports any covered goods destined for the 
United States, the Commission may request that the Secretary of the 
Department of Homeland Security:
    (1) Deny entry for purpose of oceanborne trade, of any designated 
Spanish vessel or any vessel carrying covered goods to any port or 
place in the United States or the navigable waters of the United 
States; or
    (2) Detain any designated Spanish vessel or any vessel carrying 
covered goods to any port or place in the United States or the 
navigable waters of the United States.
    (f) Adjustment in fees and goods to meet retaliatory measures. Upon 
a finding by the Commission that U.S. carriers or shippers have been 
subjected to discriminatory fees, restrictions, services disruptions, 
denial of access to ports, or other retaliatory measures by Spain, or 
any agency, organization, or person under the authority or control 
thereof, the level of fee set forth in paragraph (c) of this section 
shall be increased and additional chapters of HTS goods shall be added 
to the definition of covered goods. The increase and additions shall be 
based on the economic harm to U.S. carriers or shippers as a result of 
such retaliatory actions, provided that the total fee under this 
section shall not exceed the limits for Commission fees set by statute 
and regulation.
