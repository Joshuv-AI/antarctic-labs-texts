Is a Clinical Trial With a Non‐Bioequivalent Batch Necessary? The Critical Role of Intrasubject Variability in Olaparib Formulation Bridging by PBPK
Authors: Jin Dong, Andrea Moir, Xavier Pepin, Elizabeth Lowe, Sandy Hopkins, Weifeng Tang, Diansong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling is increasingly being used to support oral drug formulation bridging, the acceptance by regulatory agencies is low. One of the primary concerns is the absence of clinical pharmacokinetic (PK) data from a non‐bioequivalent (non‐BE) batch during model validation. Such data are strongly recommended by regulatory agencies to demonstrate that the models are sufficiently sensitive to detect formulation‐driven changes in drug exposure. Herein, we investigated whether using clinical non‐BE batch data is the most effective absorption model validation approach, using the bridging of adult and pediatric olaparib formulations as an example. Two olaparib PBPK models were developed: a “final” model and a “counterpart” model, each with comparable ability to predict the olaparib PK. Both models well predicted the clinical PK of a non‐BE batch of 250 mg olaparib—manufactured intentionally with a slower release profile—and its lack of bioequivalence relative to ten 25 mg tablets, formulated for faster release, as a conservative scenario. The final model closely captured both observed intra‐ and intersubject variabilities in C max and AUC, whereas the counterpart model was unable to reproduce the observed intrasubject variability. Our findings indicated that the clinical non‐BE data may not be necessary in all cases. In certain scenarios, validation against intrasubject variability may meet the dual objectives of capturing PK variability and confirming sensitivity within the absorption model. Using the final model, we demonstrated BE between adult and pediatric formulations of olaparib in adults and showed minimal food effect on its exposure in pediatrics.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling is being increasingly used in regulatory submissions to support oral drug product formulation bridging and assess food effects. In 2020, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) published a draft guidance document for PBPK applications in biopharmaceutics. 1 However, despite these guidelines, the rejection rate for PBPK submissions for biopharmaceutics applications remains high (almost two‐thirds). 2 , 3 One of the most common reasons for rejection is the absence of clinical data of non‐bioequivalent (non‐BE) batch for model validation. 3 It is strongly recommended to “challenge” the PBPK model with clinical data of non‐BE batches (may also be referred to as unacceptable bioavailability batches based on BE criteria) according to the FDA draft guidance. 1 This is to demonstrate whether the developed model possesses sufficient sensitivity to detect changes in drug exposure resulting from formulation and/or manufacturing modifications, thereby mitigating the risk of false positive predictions of BE. However, non‐BE batches are not routinely manufactured, and clinical trials with non‐BE batches solely for validating a PBPK model are not patient‐centric. Therefore, clinical pharmacokinetic (PK) data of non‐BE batches are commonly not available. The possibility of using clinical data from food or proton‐pump inhibitor (PPI) effect studies as a surrogate for validation of the model, rather than those from a clinical study with a non‐BE batch, has been proposed. 4

Incorporation of intrasubject variability is another critical regulatory assessment consideration of PBPK modeling for biopharmaceutics. Virtual bioequivalence (VBE) is commonly applied in PBPK modeling for biopharmaceutics to support formulation bridging and manufacturing changes. Intrasubject variability is a key factor that determines the confidence intervals and the number of subjects needed for clinical trials for bioequivalent (BE) assessment. 1 Likewise, incorporation of intrasubject variability in the PBPK model is a key consideration in the development and validation of a PBPK model for VBE. 5 PBPK platforms have recently incorporated intrasubject variability in VBE, enabling more realistic virtual assessment. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Olaparib is a poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor approved for treatment of certain ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers in eligible adult patients. 10 , 11 The commercially available tablet strengths of olaparib are 100 mg and 150 mg, with the recommended adult dosage being 300 mg taken orally twice daily (bid). 10 , 11 The plasma concentration (area under the curve [AUC]) of olaparib increases proportionally with dose over the dose range of 25 mg to 450 mg, whereas the maximal plasma concentration (C max) increases slightly less than dose proportionally. 10 A phase I study (study 6) was planned to evaluate the safety, tolerability, efficacy, and PK profile of olaparib in pediatric patients aged 6 months to ≤ 18 years with solid tumors (NCT04236414). In this study, tablet strengths of olaparib (25 mg and 100 mg) may be administered to patients aged 3–18 years. An age‐appropriate formulation (AAF) comprising enteric‐coated granules in sprinkle capsules (15 mg and 19.5 mg; see supplementary appendix for further details) was developed for patients aged < 6 years to overcome potential difficulties in swallowing the tablets.

Pediatric formulation assessments must be performed prior to the introduction of a new formulation, such as the granule AAF of olaparib, in the clinical program. 12 The most common bridging approach is conducting a relative bioavailability (rBA) study in adults, but the PBPK modeling approach with VBE has become an emerging approach to accelerate development to allow the use of the new formulation in pediatric patients without a rBA study in adults.

Olaparib can be taken with or without food by adults; findings from a clinical study indicated that food had no impact on the extent of absorption of olaparib following single dose of 300 mg (two 150 mg tablets) administration (AUC fed/fasted ratio: 1.08; 90% CI 1.01–1.16), whereas food may delay the rate of absorption. C max decreased by 21% under fed conditions (C max fed/fasted ratio: 0.79; 90% CI 0.72–0.86). 13 Furthermore, median T max was 4 hours (range 1 to 11.97 hours) with food, compared with median T max of 1.5 hours (range 0.5 to 5.85 hours) under fasted conditions. PBPK modeling is also a valuable tool for assessing the food effect in a pediatric population, given the potential physiological differences between adults and children.

A PBPK model of olaparib was updated to be qualified for regulatory assessment purposes to support bridging of the olaparib granule and tablet formulations to guide use of the olaparib AAF granules, and to predict the effect of food on the exposure of the AAF tablet and granule in pediatrics in lieu of clinical studies. The model was validated with the clinical data of a non‐BE batch manufactured intentionally with slower in vitro release than other tablets in the same study. Intra‐ and intersubject variabilities were considered in the VBE assessment for bridging adult and pediatric olaparib formulations. Furthermore, the olaparib PBPK model was used as a case study to determine the role of clinical PK data from a non‐BE batch for VBE purposes, and the possibility of utilizing intrasubject variability for validation of the model rather than the non‐BE batch approach.

METHODS

Software

All simulations were conducted using Simcyp™ v20 PBPK modeling software (Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA; released January 2021). 14 The results of the VBE simulations were analyzed using Phoenix® WinNonlin v8.3.3 (Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA; released June 2020).

Overall modeling strategy

The modeling strategy is shown in Figure 1 a . Briefly, the olaparib PBPK model was updated from a published model by Pilla Reddy et al. 15 and validated to support formulation bridging assessments and food effect predictions. The major updates (Figure 1 b ) are in the oral absorption component to qualify the model for VBE assessment: (1) implementing product‐particle size distribution (P‐PSD) estimated by fitting the corresponding in vitro dissolution data using Simcyp™ In Vitro Data Analysis Toolkit version 4 (SIVA4) to incorporate formulation/batch specific dissolution data to simulate olaparib in vivo dissolution 16 ; (2) incorporating regional permeability in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract with the intrinsic passive permeability estimated using SIVA4 from experimentally measured Caco‐2 permeability (region‐specific permeability more accurately reflects permeability under physiological conditions than the approaches that assume uniform/non‐region‐specific permeability). The importance of incorporating region‐specific permeability is further discussed in the Supplementary Appendix by comparison with the counterpart model with non‐region‐specific permeability; (3) applying the built‐in option of “new” anatomy for intestines in the GI tract in all the simulations (the “new” anatomy for intestines in Simcyp™ v20 is considered to more accurately reflect the intestinal physiology than the “original” anatomy). The model parameters are presented in Tables S1 , S2 , and S3 . Further details regarding model development and simulation settings including VBE assessments are reported in the Supplementary Appendix and Tables S4 and S5 .

Model validation with geometric mean pharmacokinetics and drug–drug interactions (DDI)

The updated model was validated by comparing observed and simulated PK parameters using data for 13 scenarios from six clinical trials including olaparib doses of 25 to 300 mg, and tablet strengths from 25 to 250 mg in adults and an adolescent cohort from study 6. The 250 mg tablet used in study 1 (crossover design) was manufactured intentionally with slower in vitro release relative to other tablet strengths in the same study (similarity factor (f2) < 50). The observed dosage normalized C max ratio for the 250 mg vs. 25 mg tablet in study 1 was 0.665.

In addition, the model was validated with observed clinical interaction of olaparib with a strong CYP3A inhibitor of itraconazole (NCT01900028) and a strong CYP3A inducer of rifampicin (NCT01929603). 17

Model validation with observed intra‐ and intersubject variabilities

Intrasubject variabilities were assigned to selected GI system parameters under fasted conditions (Table S6 ). The intra‐ and intersubject variabilities of C max and AUC of olaparib were simulated with the Simcyp VBE module. The virtual trials followed a crossover design using a 2‐treatment, 2‐phase, 2‐sequence (2T2P2S) with half of the total cohort allocated to each sequence (T1T2/T2T1). Ten virtual trials with 12 virtual subjects in each trial were generated for each simulation. The virtual subjects received an oral dose of 300 mg olaparib (administered as two 150 mg tablets from study 1) using the same formulation in both treatment sequences. The simulated intra‐ and intersubject variabilities of C max and AUC were compared with the observed values.

Virtual bioequivalence model validation with a non‐bioequivalent batch

The 250 mg tablet used in study 1 was considered as a non‐BE batch and compared with the 25 mg tablet in the same study. VBE in adult patients was performed to assess the bioequivalence between one 250 mg tablet and ten 25 mg tablets (ten virtual trials with 36 virtual subjects in each trial).

Model validation with clinical data of food effect in adults

The default Simcyp “High Fat Fed” physiology was used in the initial simulation for the validation of prediction of food effect on the PK of olaparib. In the final model, 4 hours, rather than the default value of 1 hour, was chosen as the stomach transit time for fed conditions based on published data. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 The remaining physiology parameters of the default Simcyp “High Fat Fed” were used without any change.

Model comparison between the final and counterpart models

A counterpart model with similar PK predictability was developed. The model parameters of the counterpart model are presented in Table S1 . A comparison of the components of the final and counterpart models is shown in Figure 1 b . The built‐in option of “original” anatomy for intestines in the GI tract was enabled for the counterpart model, compared to the “new” option enabled for the final model. The assumption of intestinal permeability was another major difference between the final and counterpart models. The region‐specific permeability of olaparib in the GI tract was incorporated in the final model, whereas the permeability across various regions of the GI tract was assumed to be the same as that in the jejunum in the counterpart model. The counterpart model was validated in the same way as the final model with the observed PK, food effect, and DDI data. The same intrasubject variabilities for selected GI system parameters in the fasted state were applied in the VBE simulations for both the final and counterpart models.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of physiology (bile salt concentration, initial volume of stomach fluid, stomach, small intestine, and colon transit time) and drug/dosing‐related parameters (effective permeability and fluid volume intake with dose) variations on the simulated C max and AUC of olaparib following a single dose of 300 mg (2 × 150 mg) olaparib tablets administration by both the final and counterpart models.

Model application for adult and pediatric formulation bridging

VBE in adult patients was conducted to assess BE of olaparib tablet and granule formulations (both 300 mg doses) using the final and counterpart models (ten virtual trials with 12, 24, or 36 virtual subjects in each trial).

Model application for prediction of food effect in pediatrics

The final model was used to predict food effects (under‐fed/fasted conditions) in pediatrics following tablet or granule administration. It was assumed that the stomach transit time was 4 hours (as for adults).

RESULTS

Final model validation with geometric mean pharmacokinetics and drug–drug interactions

For the final model, all ratios for simulated/observed C max and AUC were less than 2, and the majority were within the BE range of 0.8–1.25 (Figure 2 a and Table S7 ). It is also noteworthy that the 25 and 100 mg tablets are the tablets used in the pediatric study, and the 250 mg tablet—intentionally prepared with slower in vitro release—is the non‐BE tablet relative to the 25 mg tablet. The simulated/observed ratios were within 0.8–1.25 for all these tablets with the crossover design. The simulated/observed C max ratio was 1.57, and the AUC ratio was 0.90 in the adolescent cohort (aged 12–18 years) of study 6. The observed olaparib concentration–time profiles in adults after olaparib 300 mg twice daily (bid) at steady state were within the 90% prediction interval (Figure 2 b ).

For the final model, average fold error (AFE) values were 1.02 for C max and 0.96 for AUC, and absolute average fold error (AAFE) values were 1.21 for C max and 1.13 for AUC (Table S8 ). In addition, the average absolute prediction error (PE) for C max was 19.79% and for AUC was 11.37%. Together, these values indicate a good prediction capability for both C max and AUC.

Data from the DDI studies have been proposed as an alternative to intravenous PK data for building confidence in the developed model, aligning with best practices for PBPK modeling in biopharmaceutics applications. 5 The simulated/observed C max and AUC ratios for olaparib‐itraconazole and ‐rifampicin DDI were within twofold (Table S9 ). The validation using clinical DDI data of olaparib with itraconazole or rifampicin demonstrated that the model reasonably characterized CYP3A4‐mediated metabolism of olaparib, thereby increasing confidence in the model.

Virtual bioequivalence model validation with a non‐bioequivalent batch

The VBE assessment by the final model demonstrated bioinequivalence in virtual subjects administered one 250 mg tablet vs. ten 25 mg tablets from study 1. For the final model, all simulated C max ratios from the ten virtual trials failed to fall within the BE range, with the lowest ratio being 0.671 (90% CI, 0.614–0.732), whereas all the simulated AUC ratios were within the BE range.

Model comparison between the final and counterpart model

Similar to the final model, the counterpart model reproduced the geometric mean PK parameters well, including those from the study of the non‐BE batch, with similar accuracy (Figure 2 c and Table S10 ). The counterpart model also reasonably captured the observed olaparib concentration–time profiles in adults following administration of olaparib 300 mg twice daily (bid) at steady state (Figure 2 d ). The AFE, AAFE, and PE values of the counterpart model were similar to those of the final model (Table S8 ).

The same as the final model, the VBE assessment by the counterpart model demonstrated bioinequivalence in virtual subjects administered one 250 mg tablet vs. ten 25 mg tablets from study 1. For the counterpart model, the simulated ratio for Cmax from seven out of the ten virtual trials failed to fall within the BE range, with the lowest simulated ratio of 0.796 (90% CI, 0.775–0.818). All the simulated AUC ratios by the counterpart model were within the BE range.

Both the final and counterpart models underpredicted the food effect on the C max of olaparib with the Simcyp default stomach transit time of 1 hour for fed conditions (Table 2 ), whereas the performance of the counterpart model was slightly better than that of the final model. The results by the final and counterpart models were similar to each other with a stomach transit time of 4 hours (Table 2 ).

Additionally, both the final and counterpart models reasonably captured the DDI between olaparib and CYP3A precipitants including itraconazole and rifampicin (Table S9 ).

Conversely, the simulated intrasubject variabilities using the counterpart model failed to match the observed values as shown in Table 1 .

The sensitivity analyses demonstrated that variations in physiology‐ and drug/dosing‐related parameters had higher impact on the simulated C max rather than on AUC. It also showed that changes in physiology, such as bile salt concentration, stomach and small intestine transit time, resulted in a greater impact on the simulated C max with the final model compared to the counterpart model (Figure 3 ).

Model application – Comparison of tablet and granules in adult patients

BE between the tablet and AAF granule was established in the VBE assessment from all ten virtual trials including 36 adult patients in each using the final model (Figure 4 and Table S11 ). Half or one‐tenth of the virtual trials failed to demonstrate the bioequivalence between the two formulations when the virtual subject number in each trial was 12 or 24, respectively. Considering the relatively large intrasubject variability, a trial size of ≥ 36 is consistent with the estimated sample size to adequately power the study achieving BE between tablet and granule formulations. The result reaffirmed that the intrasubject variability is appropriately captured in the final model.

DISCUSSION

Use of clinical data of a non‐BE batch is strongly recommended by the US FDA as a critical component of model validation to increase confidence in oral absorption simulations. In this study, the 250 mg tablet in study 1 was used as a non‐BE batch to be compared with the 25 mg tablet from the same crossover study. This non‐BE batch was a batch manufactured intentionally with slower in vitro release than the other tablet strengths (similarity factor (f2) < 50) in the same study. The observed dosage normalized C max ratio for the 250 mg vs. 25 mg tablet in study 1 was 0.665. The simulated C max and AUC for both the final and counterpart models were within 0.8 to 1.25‐fold of the observed values from study 1, including the non‐BE batch. In addition, both models indicated a lack of BE between one 250 mg tablet and ten 25 mg tablets in study 1. The lowest simulated C max ratios by the final and counterpart models were 0.671 (90% CI, 0.614–0.732) and 0.796 (90% CI, 0.775–0.818), respectively. There are no observed data (1 × 250 mg tablet vs. 10 × 25 mg tablets) to directly validate these simulated ratios. However, the simulated dose normalized C max ratios by both models were within 0.8‐ to 1.25‐fold of the observed value of 0.665 for one 250 mg tablet relative to one 25 mg tablet.

The use of the 250 mg tablet in study 1 as a non‐BE batch for model validation is considered to be conservative. The slight non‐linearity in C max of olaparib may contribute—along with formulation differences—to the observed dosage normalized Cmax ratio of 0.665 for one 250 mg tablet versus one 25 mg tablet. The purpose of the non‐BE batch approach to validate the absorption model was to demonstrate whether the model is sufficiently sensitive to predict exposure differences arising from extrinsic factors such as formulation changes. In line with this principle, clinical data from food or PPI effect studies have been proposed as surrogates for model validation. 4 Similarly, predicting non‐proportional C max and/or AUC increases resulting from altered oral absorption at higher dose may also serve as an alternative validation approach. Most importantly, even if the 250 mg tablet was not a non‐BE batch to the 25 mg tablet when administered at the same dose level (1 × 250 mg tablet vs. 10 × 25 mg tablet)—although this outcome would be unexpected given their in vitro dissolution, clinical PK (crossover), and modeling data—treating the 250 mg tablet as a non‐BE batch is unlikely to cause a false positive of BE prediction in subsequent VBE assessment, since both models showed non‐BE between the two tablets at the same dose.

The simulation by the final model showed a larger difference between the non‐BE 250 mg tablet batch and the 25 mg tablet batch than the counterpart model. However, validation using the non‐BE batch clinical data alone may not disqualify the counterpart model, given the similar performance in this validation by both models as shown above. The final model was deemed superior because it well captured the observed intra‐ and intersubject variabilities, whereas the counterpart model was disqualified due to its substantial mismatch with the observed intrasubject variability (Table 1 ). Therefore, validation based on the non‐BE batch clinical data was not the most efficient result to distinguish the final and counterpart models for olaparib.

The key in the model validation for biopharmaceutics application is to demonstrate the model with sufficient sensitivity to detect the impact of formulation change on the exposure. In fact, the non‐BE batch approach may have limited utility for the purpose of avoiding false positives in VBE assessments. A model that can distinguish larger differences between non‐BE batches may not possess the sensitivity required to detect smaller differences. For example, consider a model validated using clinical data from a non‐BE batch B, which exhibits a slower release profile than reference batch A (and has an observed C max ratio of 0.5 for batch B vs. batch A). Such a model may be considered qualified for VBE assessments involving another non‐BE batch C, which has an even slower release than batch B and a true C max ratio of 0.4 vs. batch A, as the model has shown sufficient sensitivity to detect non‐BE for any C max ratio of 0.5 or lower. On the other hand, the model may yield a false positive BE when comparing batch D to batch A if the true C max ratio is 0.79, since identifying this smaller difference requires greater sensitivity. Therefore, we suggest that a non‐BE batch approach is generally more useful for extrapolating non‐BE outcomes, rather than interpolating within the VBE assessment context. Similarly, clinical data from a BE batch is more useful for interpolation to demonstrate BE. However, there would be no need to conduct VBE in either case.

Conversely, a model that can distinguish a small difference between batches is expected to possess sufficient sensitivity to detect larger differences. An effective absorption model should be able to quantitate the PK difference observed following the administration of two different formulations within the same subject, provided it can first characterize the PK variability of the same formulation given to the same subject under different occasions (i.e., intrasubject variability). To accurately capture intrasubject variability, the model must be sensitive enough to simulate the impact of changes in GI tract physiology alone on drug exposure. The level of sensitivity required to account for intrasubject variability is higher than that needed to simulate the combined effects of formulation and physiological changes. As a result, validation using observed intrasubject variability may serve as a more stringent criterion than validation based on the non‐BE batch approach in some, if not all cases.

In the case of olaparib, the validation with the clinical data of the non‐BE batch was not an efficient approach for distinguishing between the final and counterpart models. This is consistent with the inherent nature of this approach more suitable for extrapolation. On the other hand, the comparison of the sensitivity analysis of the two models showed that the sensitivity of the final model upon impacts by physiology changes in the GI tract is greater than that of the counterpart model (Figure 3 ). Our findings suggested that validation of intrasubject variability is essential not only for validating variability in VBE assessment but also as an alternative approach to validate the underlying absorption model. The VBE could be further improved by applying the concept of digital twin in quantitative systems pharmacology to more accurately and mechanistically account for the variabilities in physiology.

It was suggested that clinical data from food effect studies may be used to validate the absorption model. 4 However, the simulation of food effects did not distinguish between the final and counterpart models in the case of olaparib.

In conclusion, the VBE assessment by the final model of olaparib supported BE between olaparib tablet and granule formulations. The simulation results suggested that food is likely to have a minimal effect on the AUC of olaparib in pediatrics, which is similar to the observation in adults. Collecting clinical PK data of a non‐BE batch solely for model validation purposes is not patient centric. Comparison of the findings between the final and counterpart models suggests that the non‐BE batch approach may not always be the most effective and stringent method for model validation. In contrast, validation using intrasubject variability is critical not only for assessing variability in VBE evaluations but also for validating the absorption model with sufficient sensitivity to detect changes in drug exposure resulting from formulation changes. Incorporating intrasubject variability into PBPK modeling is an emerging and evolving area. Given the importance of model validation using intrasubject variability, further advances in PBPK modeling of intrasubject variability are warranted. Developing a general and robust workflow—grounded in multiple case studies—to integrate this key component into model development and validation is also needed.
