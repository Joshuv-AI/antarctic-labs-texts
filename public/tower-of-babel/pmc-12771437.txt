Development of a Lateral Flow Immunoassay Strips for Rapid Antigen Detection of Dabie bandavirus
Authors: Chao Huang, Bing-Yang Zheng, Zhi-Feng Li, Yuan-Fang Qin, Yong-Jun Jiao, Li Zhang, Lun-Biao Cui, Tian Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study reports the development of a point-of-care (POC) serological assay for the detection of the Dabie bandavirus (DBV) infection. Specifically, this assay is based on the principle of lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA) strips. Two monoclonal antibodies against DBV were generated. They showed an optimal pairing. One mAb, named NP-10, was immobilized on the strip surface as the capture antibody. The other mAb, named NP-20, was labeled with colloidal gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and used as the detector antibody. A series of parameters of this method were optimized during the AuNPs-mAb immune complex conjugating process, including the different pairing of antigens and antibodies, pH value, and the amount of coupled antibody. The whole detection process was completed within 15 min using 70 μL of the analyte solution, which consisted of 10 μL of sample and 60 μL of dilution buffer. The performance of the established assay was evaluated by using serum samples of DBV-infected individuals. It showed 90.9% clinical diagnostic sensitivity and 100% specificity with a κ coefficient of 0.929. The results demonstrated that our designed LFIA strips (LFIAs) used for DBV infection detection exhibited stable performance and reproducible outcomes. Consequently, this rapid and user-friendly LFIA can furnish physicians with preliminary test results. This enables them to make accurate diagnoses of DBV infections by integrating these results with other testing methods and clinical manifestations.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a tick-borne disease caused by DBV. It belongs to the genus Bandavirus within the order Bunyavirales and was formally classified by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV). This virus was first discovered in the central region of China in 2010 and is mainly transmitted through the bites of the long-horned blood tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), which can cause infections in humans. The clinical manifestations of SFTS patients include acute fever, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and multiorgan function impairment. Some patients may exhibit bleeding tendencies and neurological symptoms, with a fatality rate as high as 30%. Currently, the DBV has spread in more than 20 provinces in China, forming high-incidence areas in five provinces, including Jiangsu, Anhui, Henan, Hubei, and Guangdong. In recent years, the number of reported cases in East Asian countries has become increasingly reported. In China, there have been 27,447 cases and 1,326 deaths (case-fatality rate, CFR 4.83%) across 27 provinces by 2023, mainly in central mountainous areas. Japan averages 60–100 new cases yearly (CFR 7.8%), while South Korea has reported 1,203 cases with a high CFR of 18.54%. In addition, studies have demonstrated the potential for human-to-human transmission of DBV. Close contact with the blood or body fluids of patients can lead to clustered infections. This dual transmission mode greatly increases the potential risk of epidemic outbreaks. Therefore, this severe infectious disease requires close monitoring. At present, there is neither a clinically verified specific therapeutic drug for SFTS nor a vaccine that can be used on a large scale, which poses great difficulties for the implementation of precise prevention and control as well as clinical treatment. Timely, rapid, and accurate diagnosis of the pathogen is important for effective prevention and control of SFTS. Traditional detection methods mainly include virus isolation and culture, nucleic acid amplification techniques (such as PCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Although these testing techniques show relatively high accuracy in identifying the disease, they have disadvantages such as the need for professional equipment and technical personnel, long detection cycle, and high cost. − Lateral flow immunoassay, using colloidal gold nanomaterials as immunolabels, with its characteristics of simplicity, rapidity (results within 10–15 min), and low experimental preparation requirements, has shown great potential in the rapid screening of pathogen infection. − This study aims to explore and optimize the application of LFIAs based on the immunodetection applications of AuNPs − for DBV detection. The research is of great significance for the early diagnosis of DBV, timely treatment of infected patients, and the effective prevention and control of DBV epidemics. In order to develop the test strips, recombinant nucleocapsid protein (N protein, NP) was expressed and purified, and six strains of mAbs were screened out from an antibody library of DBV infection patients according to the process similar to that mentioned in previous literature. The rapid test strips were then developed based on the best pairing antibodies by LFIAs. These strips demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity in serological experiments, indicating their suitability for the detection of DBV infection.

Results and Discussion

In this study, we focused on developing an LFIA based on the double-antibody sandwich format for detecting NP to confirm DBV infection, with AuNPs serving as a visually detectable signal tracer. As shown in Scheme , DBV NP and its monoclonal antibodies were expressed and purified, from which suitable detector antibody and capture antibody were paired and selected. Next, in order to prepare the sample pad of the LFIA strips, the optimal conjugation conditions for forming AuNPs-mAb immune complexes were determined, including the optimal pH environment and the quantity of the labeled antibody. After the LFIAs were assembled, analyses of detection sensitivity, specificity, and stability were conducted. Furthermore, by detecting DBV-infected samples and comparing the results with those obtained by other classical methods such as PCR, the performance of this immunochromatographic method was evaluated.

To prepare the DBV NP for panning the antibody library, an Escherichia coli­(E. coli) expression system was employed. As depicted in Figure , DBV NP was expressed in E. coli BL21. Generation and Identification of mAbs against DBV NP

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) pooled from convalescent patients infected with DBV were used for the total RNA extraction. Phage-display technology was then employed to generate mAbs against the DBV NP. As depicted in the result of biolayer interferometry (BLI) (shown in Figure S2), the NP10 + NP20 group showed a rapid initial increase and continued to rise after close to 200 s mark. By around 250 s, the response value reached a plateau. This result indicated that the combination of NP10 and NP20 exhibited a stronger binding interaction and was suitable for the subsequent development of lateral flow immunoassays.

Appearance, Distribution, and Particle Size of AuNPs

The colloidal gold solution exhibited a homogeneous wine-red color, demonstrating clarity and translucency without visible floating particulates on the surface. The AuNPs solution showed no signs of precipitation or agglomeration, indicating a stable colloidal state. We then scanned the solution at 400–800 nm with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer. Generally, a narrow absorption peak located at around 525 nm indicates the uniformity and stability of colloidal gold nanoparticles. As shown in Figure , based on the particle size statistics from the TEM images, the size of the AuNPs is 22.1 ± 2.7 nm, which is also consistent with the conclusions from the trisodium citrate reduction method established by Frens. In addition, we measured the Zeta potential of the AuNPs and the result was determined to be −34.8 ± 2.8 mV, which was calculated as the average of three parallel measurements. This value indicates that the particles possess a strong negative surface charge, suggesting that the colloidal suspension is highly stable due to sufficient electrostatic repulsion between the particles. Combining the TEM images and Zeta potential analysis results of the AuNPs, we can conclude that the synthesized colloidal gold nanoparticles exhibited uniform morphology and monodispersity.

Pairwise Analysis

The six purified mAbs with strong binding to DBV NP were selected for pairing on the test strip. The detector antibodies were determined by the NaCl destabilization assay (salt stability test), which is a classic method to evaluate the stability of colloidal nanoparticles in physiological environments. In detail, to the 200 μL system of AuNPs conjugated with antibodies was added 45 μL of a 10% (w/v) NaCl solution, resulting in a final NaCl concentration of approximately 0.3 M. When the conjugation environment of AuNPs-mAb is acidic, AuNPs aggregate and turn purple in the disruption assay. The core reason for this is that the acidic environment disrupts the charge balance on the surface of colloidal gold and impairs the conjugation stability between antibodies and colloidal gold, thereby exacerbating the salt-induced aggregation of particles. To be specific, as shown in Figure , for each mAb, a relatively low pH value (such as 6.5 and 6.8) causes the aggregation of AuNPs and the color of the conjugation system changes from the wine red of the stable colloidal gold solution to purple, making it unsuitable for the conjugation reaction to occur. On the other hand, a relatively high pH may induce the self-aggregation of antibodies, thereby indirectly promoting the aggregation of colloidal gold. NP-11 and NP-21, for instance, as also demonstrated in Figure , when pH was greater than 7.4, AuNPs in the conjugation system undergo severe aggregation, the color even turns blue-black, and precipitates occur. For the other four mAbs, NP-1, NP-10, NP-20, and NP-27, although they all remained relatively stable under weakly alkaline conditions with a pH greater than 7.4 (a commonly used conjugation condition for antibodies and nanoparticles), a careful observation of their color changes revealed that the colors of NP-1, NP-10, and NP-27 all showed slight changes compared with pure AuNPs (tending to become lighter and pale pink), which indicates that AuNPs have undergone slight aggregation. Meanwhile, NP-20 remained a stable and clear wine red during the entire experiment conducted under weakly alkaline conditions, showing almost no difference from the original AuNPs solution, and this demonstrates that NP-20 is a suitable candidate for coupling with AuNPs. Hence, NP-20 mAb with the best conjugation effect was selected as a detector antibody, while the other five as candidates for capture antibodies, constituting a total of 5 antibody pairings in the candidate lateral flow assays.

With pH ≥ 7.4, NP-11 and NP-21: the color turns blue-black and precipitates occur, indicating the severe aggregation of AuNPs; NP-1, NP-10, and NP-27: the color turns lighter and pale pink, demonstrating the severe aggregation of AuNPs; NP-20: remained a stable and clear wine red in the whole experiment, which proved that NP-20 is a suitable candidate for serving as the detector antibody conjugated with AuNPs.

When NP-20 was used as the detector antibody, the other five mAbs were coated as capture antibodies on the nitrocellulose (NC) membrane of the LFIA strip to form the test line (T-line). All of these configurations successfully detected DBV NP, with PBS buffer serving as the blank control.

In the subsequent preparation of double-antibody sandwich immunochromatographic test strips, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the influence of different paired antibody combinations on the detection sensitivity and the color intensity of the T-line and control line (C-line) on the strips. As can be clearly observed with the naked eye from Figure A, when the paired combination of NP-10 and NP-20 was used, the LFIA could detect a red band signal at the T-line even when the DBV NP sample was at a much lower concentration. Meanwhile, we used ImageJ, an image processing software, to perform signal conversion on the LFIA image results to minimize the inaccuracies and lack of objectivity in experimental results caused by subjective judgment based on visual observations and to achieve quantitative analysis. As shown in Figure B, when the concentration of DBV NP was relatively high, the optical density (OD) signal values of T-lines generated by the five candidate capture antibodies were all basically identical. However, when the concentration of DBV NP was reduced to the nanogram-per-milliliter level, the optical signal value generated by the NP-10 was significantly higher than those of the other four candidate antibodies. On the basis of the aforementioned results, the antibody pair consisting of NP-10 and NP-20 was chosen as the capture antibody immobilized at the T-line and the detector antibody labeled AuNPs, respectively, which formed a sandwich immunoassay structure.

Optimization of AuNP-mAb Conjugates

In consonance with the fundamental principle of colloidal gold labeling, the synthesis of the (AuNPs-detector antibody) bioconjugates by physical electrostatic adsorption is strictly contingent upon a specific and meticulously controlled pH range. When the pH value of colloidal gold is close to or slightly higher than the isoelectric point (PI) of the protein, the antibody shows the greatest adsorption capacity on the surface of gold particles. A series of pH values (6.8, 7.1, 7.4, 7.7, 8.0, 8.3) was investigated by adding different volumes of 0.1 M potassium carbonate solution (K2CO3) to the AuNPs suspension at the existence of excessive mAb. Under the NaCl destructive effect mentioned above, the color of the AuNPs-detector antibody solution with pH 6.8 and 7.1 altered from red to blue because of the aggregation. As shown in Figure A, 7.7 is the threshold of pH value for determining whether AuNPs aggregate, leading to instability of immune complexes and a color change from wine red to bluish purple. Hence, the optimal pH value for conjugation was ascertained at around 7.7. Meanwhile, determining the minimum amount of protein that can form a stable immunoprobe is another important factor for the success of labeling. An inappropriate amount of protein could induce the aggregation of probes and reduce the activity of immune complexes. For antibodies of excessive amount, free protein in the solution of the immune-gold colloidal complex is easy to react with the active binding site first, which plays a “blocking role”, and leads to the failure of protein coupling. On the other hand, an inadequate protein amount will lead to insufficient synthesis of effective colloidal gold immunoprobes, which reduces the sensitivity of the test and gives rise to a false-negative phenomenon. To obtain optimal antibody concentrations for immune complex binding, different amounts of mAbs (0, 0.65, 1.3, 1.95, 2.6, 3.25, 3.9, 4.55, 5.2 μg) were added into the AuNPs solution (200 μL, pH 7.7) and the same kind of NaCl tolerance test was carried out. As shown in Figure B, 0.3 M NaCl directly induced accumulation or color change, which could be observed by a color shift from red to blue at 0.65–3.25 μg of mAbs. However, the AuNPs solution (200 μL) containing more than 3.9 μg of mAbs could remain red after introducing the NaCl solution. In practice, the quantity of mAbs added should typically be increased by 20% compared to this amount according to previous literatures. Therefore, a concentration of 23.4 μg/mL was ultimately selected as the optimal concentration for the chosen detector antibody.

Optimization of AuNPs-(DBV detector antibody) conjugates under (A) graduated pH values (from 6.8 to 8.3, with 0.3 as the interval); (B) different mAb amounts (0.65, 1.3, 1.95, 2.6, 3.25, 3.9, 4.55, and 5.2 μg).

An important characteristic of nanoparticles including AuNPs, the peroxidase mimetic activity, , was used to quantitatively verify the qualitative conclusions drawn from the aforementioned visual observations. First, AuNP–mAb conjugates obtained under different coupling conditions were centrifuged and resuspended. Then the product was utilized to catalyze the chromogenic reaction, in which hydrogen peroxide oxidizes 3,3‘,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. Subsequently, a microplate reader was employed to measure the absorbance values (Abs) with quantification. As illustrated in Figure S3A, when the pH is greater than 7.7 in the experiment on the optimal pH conjugation conditions for immune complexes, the absorbance generated by the substrate catalyzed by AuNPs is significantly higher than the values obtained under other conjugation conditions with lower pH. Similarly, as shown in Figure S3B, in the experiment to determine the optimal antibody dosage for mAb-nanoparticle conjugates, when the detector antibody exceeds 3.9 μg, the absorbance generated by the oxidation of the substrate in the presence of AuNPs is significantly higher than that under conjugation conditions with a lower amount of antibody. The core reason for this phenomenon lies in the fact that optimal conditions can simultaneously meet two key requirements. First, they maintain the optimal dispersion stability of AuNPs during the coupling process, preventing the enzyme-like active sites from being shielded due to agglomeration. Second, they maximize the retention of the inherent enzyme-like active center structure of AuNPs, while ensuring that the coupling of mAbs does not cause significant inhibition of this activity.

The Assembly Process of LFIAs and the Detection Principle

In this experiment, antigen–antibody interactions with sandwich mode were demonstrated to form an (AuNPs-detector antibody–antigen-capture antibody) immune complex for DBV detection (shown as Figure S6 in Supporting Information). An appropriate amount of sample is added to the sample well on the test card, and the sample will move forward along the strip under capillary force. If the sample is DBV-positive, the target antigen will bind to the gold-labeled antibody present in the conjugation pad, and the mixed liquid will migrate to the membrane-bound capture antibody on the T-line, indicating a positive result. Simultaneously, unbound conjugates continued to flow forward and bound to the antihuman polyclonal antibody on the C-line, resulting in the appearance of red bands. Therefore, the positive or negative result of this LFIA depends on the presence or absence of a red band on the T-line. Also, it is worth noting that if no red appears on the C-line, the test result is invalid regardless of the color on the T-line.

Sensitivity of LFIAs Test

To assess the sensitivity of our established LFIA, we tested a series of 10-fold dilutions of the DBV NP, starting at a concentration of 10 μg/mL. As the concentration of DBV NP decreased, the red band at the T-line became increasingly faint. When DBV NP reached 0.05 ng/mL, the color of the T-line gradually became indistinguishable from that of the negative control by the naked eye (Figure A). With optical density quantitative analysis as a supplement, the OD value of DBV NP at a concentration of 0.025 ng/mL was still higher than the average value of the negative control, plus 2.1 times its standard deviation. Based on the above analysis, our LFIA was able to detect DBV NP at a minimum concentration of 0.025 ng/mL, as shown in Figure B.

Specificity of LFIAs Test

To verify the specificity of LFIAs designed by our group, positive samples of DBV, monkeypox virus (MPXV), dengue fever virus (DFV) and influenza A (FluA) infections were simultaneously tested with healthy humans’ serum as a negative control. Specimens infected with DBV showed two distinct red bands on both the T-line and C-line. For the remaining samples, only the C-line showed a visible distinct band, while no signal appeared on the T-line (Figure ). Notably, the LFIA that we designed serves as a rapid and simple auxiliary detection method, enabling the distinction between obvious negative and positive results through naked-eye observation. This result showed that this unique LFIA has high specificity for DBV detection.

Analysis of DBV in Human Serum by LFIAs

To evaluate the clinical practicability, LFIAs were applied to detect serum samples from several DBV-infected individuals, where normal human serum was assessed as the negative control. Photos (Figure S5) and statistical data of clinical sample analysis are presented in Supporting Information. LFIA results are summarized in Table , which are identical with those of the molecular assay. Using the established LFIA method, 11 serum samples previously identified as positive for DBV infection by PCR were tested. Notably, only one sample yielded a negative result. Significantly, this particular sample exhibited a high cycle threshold (Ct value = 37.7) in the PCR test (described in Table S1), indicating a relatively low viral load. Meanwhile, 21 samples from healthy individuals were subjected to both PCR and LFIA strip testing, and showed consistently negative outcomes. Through a comprehensive analysis of the test results from molecular detection methods and this designed LFIA, our strips demonstrated satisfactory consistency, as evidenced by the Kappa test, which yielded a κ coefficient of 0.929. Also, the LFIA exhibited a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 100%, with a Youden index of 0.909. According to the above conclusion, when the κ coefficient is greater than 0.8, it indicates that this AuNPs-LFIA established by our research group and the PCR method, as the gold standard have good agreement.

Conclusions

In summary, we have described the development, optimization, and validation of a point-of-care test based on the specific binding between the antigen and the antibody, which were expressed and purified by our laboratory. Six strains of DBV-specific mAbs with high affinity to the target antigen were screened by the prokaryotic expression of DBV NP protein and paired via biolayer interferometry technology. These mAbs were paired and selected on an LFIA platform. The mAbs NP-20 and NP-10 paired well and have a detection limit of 0.025 ng/mL for DBV recombinant NP. In addition, the results of the LFIAs showed that the selected mAbs only bind to DBV NP protein specifically and did not cross-react with other virus. Serological study, such as diagnosis by detecting DBV antigen in the serum of infected individuals, is an important complement to the imperfect diagnostic strategy by molecular assays. , It may serve as a valuable tool for differentiating DBV infections in patients with acute fever from similar symptoms induced by other pathogens at an early stage. Moreover, it has the potential to offer physicians a preliminary test result. This designed LFIA, in combination with alternative testing methods and clinical manifestations, could make an accurate diagnosis and provide initial appropriate treatment to patients. Our LFIA does not require professional instruments and the whole test process is faster and easier than other methods such as virus isolation, PCR, and ELISA − especially in most public health communities as well as remote countryside and low-income countries or regions. Concerning the potential global risk of DBV infection, most countries have an urgent demand for large-scale screening with simple and rapid methods. In this instance, our AuNPs-LFIA is supposed to be a more efficient and effective solution to deal with the situation.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Chemical Reagent

The NP-specific mAbs were harvested and the binding specificity against NP was evaluated by our laboratory as reported previously. DBV-infected serum samples were provided by the Jiangsu Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Sample pads, conjugate pads, NC membranes, absorbent pads, and poly­(vinyl chloride) (PVC) baseboards were supplied by Shanghai JieYi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Other chemicals are shown in the Supporting Information.

Instruments

All photos of AuNPs and paper-based LFIAs were taken with digital cameras. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements were carried out by a JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd.). The NGC Quest 10 Plus system (BioRad, USA) was applied for antibody purification. Specific antibody pairing was performed by an Octet R8 system (ForteBio, Molecular Devices). In addition, other instruments are shown in the Supporting Information.

Cloning and Expression of DBV NP

The nucleoprotein gene of DBV (GenBank accession number: ATW63020.1) was synthesized in the pET-28a vector. Recombinant NP was expressed in E. coli and purified by nickel column. Briefly, the recombinant plasmid of pET28a-NP was transformed into E. coli BL21 competent cells and coated onto a Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin and incubated overnight. The correct clone was verified by sequencing. Prokaryotic expression was induced for 16–20 h at 20 °C after introducing a final concentration of 0.5 mM IPTG. The broth was collected and sonicated for 40 min at 200W. The supernatant was extracted after centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the target protein was purified by a Ni-chelation affinity column. Then NP was eluted with a two-step washing strategy. First, 5 column volumes (CV) of wash buffer containing 5 mM imidazole were applied to remove weakly bound contaminants without disrupting specific interactions between the His-tagged NP and Ni2+; Second, 10 CV of wash buffer containing 10–15 mM imidazole was used for further elimination of residual impurities with stronger nonspecific binding. Finally, DBV NP was eluted at around 250 mM imidazole concentration. The purified protein was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the protein concentrations were determined using an ultramicro UV–vis spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Preparation of Monoclonal Antibodies

Peripheral blood samples were collected from DBV-convalescing patients. PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll-Paque Plus (GE Healthcare, Sweden) density gradient media according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The human scFv phage display library used in this study was constructed previously. Three rounds of panning were performed using recombinant NP of DBV. A total of 6 clones of scFv genes were obtained. The variable region genes of heavy and light chains were cloned into the PTT5 vector containing the constant regions of human IgG1, respectively. Eukaryotic expression vectors of heavy and light chains were cotransfected into HEK-293F cells by Poly­(ether imide) (PEI, Polysciences). The culture supernatant of 293F was harvested by centrifuging at 10,000 g for 10 min and filtered through a 0.22 mm filter membrane. Protein A column (GE Healthcare, Sweden) was used for antibody purification by an NGC Quest 10 Plus system (Bio-Rad, USA). The purified antibodies were aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C for further use.

Binning Assays by Biolayer Interferometry (BLI)

BLI is a label-free biosensing technique that monitors biomolecular interactions in real time and has been widely used in research on the specific pairing of antigens and antibodies. Binning antibodies into competition groups was performed by BLI using the Octet R8 system (ForteBio, Molecular Devices). Recombinant NP was loaded on Anti-Penta-HIS (HIS1K) biosensors for 0.5 nm at 30 μg/mL. The first mAb was allowed to associate onto NP until saturation at 15 μg/mL. When the binding curve turned to be nearly straight, the second mAb at the same concentration was loaded onto the biosensors. The area under the curve (AUC) was quantified to characterize the binding kinetics of the secondary mAb under competitive (in the presence of the binning mAb) and noncompetitive (in the absence of the binning mAb) conditions. The AUC for the second mAb plus binning mAb was normalized and expressed as a percentage of the second mAb alone. Antibodies were classified as competitive when the binding ratio was less than 30%, partially competitive when the binding ratio ranged from 30% to 70%, and noncompetitive when the binding ratio was 70% or higher in both directions.

Synthesis of Colloidal AuNPs

The synthesis process of AuNPs was carried out according to the reported methods with appropriate modifications. Briefly, 1 mL of gold suspension HAuCl4·3H2O (1%, w/w) was diluted with 100 mL of distilled water and heated to 120 °C in a round-bottom flask. Freshly prepared 1% sodium citrate (1 mL) was then added rapidly with constant stirring. Subsequently, the color of the solution changed gradually from pale yellow to wine red. When the color of the solution changed to bright wine red, the solution was kept boiling for another 10 min. Move the flask from heating mantle and continue stirring for 15 min until it cools down. Ultrapure water was then added to a constant volume of 100 mL. The solutions were kept at 4 °C in the dark until use. The morphology, monodispersity, and average particle diameter of AuNPs were characterized by TEM, UV–vis, and Zeta potential.

Preparation of AuNP-mAb Bioconjugates

There are currently several kinds of strategies for conjugating antibodies to colloidal gold nanoparticles to form immune complexes. Based on a comprehensive consideration of antibody activity preservation, operational feasibility, experimental cost, and adaptability for subsequent applications, detector antibodies were immobilized to AuNPs by physical electrostatic adsorption under the conditions of optimal pH values and coupling antibody concentration. In our work, AuNP bioconjugates were prepared as previously reported with proper adaptations. Briefly, the pH of the AuNPs solution was adjusted to 7.7 with 0.1 M K2CO3 solution. For conjugation, 23.4 μg of mAbs was added into 1 mL of AuNPs solution. After being stirred slightly for another 20 min at room temperature, the final solution was mixed with BSA to a final blocking concentration of 1% and stirred for another 20 min. Then, the products were collected by centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 20 min to remove large-particle impurity, followed by a second-step centrifuging at 15,000 rpm for another 20 min. Finally, the sediment was resuspended into one-fifth of the original volume of Tris-HCl buffer (0.01 M, pH 8.0) supplemented with 1% BSA (w/v), 1% PEG-20000 (w/v), 10% sucrose (w/v), and 0.5% Tween-20 (v/v) and then stored under 4 °C in the dark for subsequent use.

Development of LFIAs

The schematic diagram of paper-based LFIAs is presented in Scheme . The immunochromatographic test strip was assembled according to well-established protocols and composed of four sections, including a sample pad (10 × 4 mm), a conjugate pad (8 × 4 mm), an NC membrane (20 × 4 mm), and an absorbent pad (10 × 4 mm). Sample pads were used for dripping samples that might contain DBV NP. After the pretreatment, the conjugate pad was immobilized with AuNPs-(DBV detector antibody) conjugates, followed by freezing at −60 °C for 4 h and subsequent vacuum freeze-drying overnight. Using a double-headed marker pen equipped with an extremely small nib (1.97 × 34 mm), T and C lines on NC membranes were prepared by dispensing 1.5 mg/mL of DBV capture antibody and 2.0 mg/mL of goat antihuman IgG, respectively. The interval between the two lines was set at 5 mm. NC membranes were then blocked with 1% (w/v) BSA and dried at room temperature in the dark for 2 h. All of the components mentioned above were attached to a PVC backing card. Before assembly, the pretreatments of the components such as conjugate pad are presented in Supporting Information (Figure S1). Finally, our prepared paper-based LFIAs, which were cut into a 4 mm width with scissors, were packed into aluminum foil pouches, vacuum-sealed, and stored at 4 °C for future use. The developed immunochromatographic strip could be easily fabricated in batch.

Test Procedures

When 70 μL of the sample to be tested was added onto the sample pad of paper-based LFIA strips, it transferred toward the absorbent pad under the action of capillary force. The positive and negative results can be determined by the specific reddish-purple tracer of the AuNPs. The red C-line indicates the validity of the test strip. If the C-line is not visible, the test result should be deemed invalid regardless of whether a red T-line appears or not (Scheme ). The test results were visible via a naked-eye observation within 15 min.
