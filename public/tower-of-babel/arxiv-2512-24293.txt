Quasi Neighborhood Balanced Coloring of Graphs


Abstract
For a simple graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) , a coloring of vertices of G G using two colors, say red and blue, is called a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring if, for every vertex of the graph, the number of red neighbors and the number of blue neighbors differ by at most one. In addition, there must be at least one vertex in G G for which this difference is exactly one. If a graph G G admits such a coloring, then G G is said to be a quasi Neighborhood balanced colored graph . We also define variants of such a coloring, like uniform quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, positive quasi neighborhood balanced coloring and negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring based on the color of the extra neighbor of every vertex of odd degree of the graph G G . We present several examples of graph classes that admit the various variants of quasi neighborhood balanced coloring. We also discuss various graph operations involving such graphs. Furthermore, we prove that there is no forbidden subgraph characterization for the class of quasi neighborhood balanced coloring and show that the problem of determining whether a given graph has such a coloring is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete.

Let

For any vertex

The members of

of

is called the

\deg(v)
.
Further, if we add the vertex
v
to its open neighborhood

, then we get the closed neighborhood of

, denoted by
N[v]
.
For graph-theoretic notation, we refer to Chartrand and Lesniak
[
4
]
.

Freyberg et al.
[
6
]
introduced the concept ‘neighborhood balanced coloring’.
A
neighborhood balanced coloring
of a graph
G
is a vertex coloring of
G
using two colors, say red and blue, such that each vertex has an equal number of neighbors of both colors.
This notion of coloring is somewhat similar to
cordial labeling

[
3
]
.
In
[
6
]
, the authors presented several necessary/sufficient conditions for a graph to admit neighborhood balanced coloring. They further presented several graph classes that admit neighborhood balanced coloring.

Minyard et al.
[
7
]
introduced the concept of ‘neighborhood balanced
3
-coloring’ of a graph and gave a characterization of several classes of graphs that admit such a coloring.
A graph is said to admit a
neighborhood balanced
3
-coloring
if the vertices of
G
can be colored using exactly three colors such that each vertex has an equal number of neighbors of all three colors.

Almeida et al.
[
1
]
generalized this concept by introducing the notion of neighborhood balanced
k
-coloring of graphs, for any positive integer
k\geq 2
. A coloring of the vertices of
G
using
k
colors such that every vertex has an equal number of vertices of each color in its neighborhood is called neighborhood balanced
k
-coloring, and the graph is called neighborhood balanced
k
-colored graph. They presented several classes of graphs that admit such a coloring and also studied neighborhood balanced
k
-colored graphs under various graph operations. They also proved that the problem of checking if a given graph

admits neighborhood balanced

-coloring is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. Moreover, they also showed that the class of neighborhood balanced
k
-colored graphs is not hereditary.

Motivated by the notion of neighborhood balanced colorings of graphs,
Collins et al.
[
5
]
introduced the concept of

closed neighborhood balanced coloring
.
In this setting, the vertices of a graph are colored with two colors, say red and blue, such that in the closed neighborhood of every vertex, the number of red vertices equals the number of blue vertices.
Collins et al.
[
5
]
established several necessary/sufficient conditions for a graph to admit a closed neighborhood balanced coloring.
They identified various classes of graphs that admit such a coloring and further investigated the behavior of closed neighborhood balanced colored
graphs under different graph operations. Moreover, they demonstrated that the class of closed neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not hereditary. Almeida et al.
[
2
]
further generalized this concept by introducing the notion of closed neighborhood balanced
k
-coloring of graphs, for any positive integer
k\geq 2
.

After examining these concepts, a natural question arises:
What if a graph can be colored with two colors—red and blue—such that, for every vertex, the difference between the number of red and blue neighbors is at most one?
This question was earlier raised in
[
6
]
.

In this article, we explore this question in detail and introduce a new vertex-coloring framework in which each vertex is assigned one of two colors, red or blue, under the condition that the absolute difference between the number of red and blue neighbors of every vertex is at most one. A formal definition of this coloring is presented below.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
G
be a graph. We color the vertices of
G
using two colors, red and blue. Such a coloring is called a
quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
if, for every vertex
v
in the graph, the number of red neighbors and the number of blue neighbors differ by at most one. In addition, there must be at least one vertex in
G
for which this difference is exactly one.
If a graph
G
admits such a coloring, then
G
is said to be a
quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph
.

Note that for a quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph the vertices of even degree will have an equal number of neighbors of both colors.

Based on the color of the additional neighbor that some vertices have under a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, we introduce several variants of the quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Definition 1.2
.

A quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of a graph
G
is said to be a
uniform quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
if, for every vertex of odd degree, the number of red neighbors is exactly one more than the number of blue neighbors. If a graph
G
admits uniform quasi neighborhood balanced coloring then it is called uniform quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph. The color red is called
dominating color
of the quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Note that in Definition
1.2
, the role of colors red and blue can be interchanged.

Definition 1.3
.

A quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of a graph
G
is said to be
positive
if every vertex of odd degree has exactly one more neighbor of its own color than of the opposite color.

Similarly, a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of graph
G
is said to be
negative
if every vertex of odd degree has exactly one more neighbor of the opposite color than of its own color.

Note that a negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of a graph having all vertices of odd degree is also its closed neighborhood balanced coloring.

In Section
2
, we present several examples of graphs that admit the various variants of quasi neighborhood balanced coloring. We also study such graphs under various graph operations. In Section
3
, we prove that the decision problem:
Given a graph
G
, does it admit a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring?
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

At the beginning, we fix the following notation, which we shall use throughout the article.
If
c
is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
G
, then denote by
V_{G}^{c}(R)
and
V_{G}^{c}(B)
the number of vertices of
G
colored red and blue respectively by the coloring
c
. When the coloring or the graph under consideration is clear from the context, we shall omit the subscript or the superscript from the above notations.

2.1
Counting Results

Let
G
be a graph that admits a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
c
.
Let
V(R)
and
V(B)
denote the sets of vertices of
G
colored red and blue, respectively by
c
.
Each of these sets is further partitioned according to the neighborhood imbalance:

•

V(R_{1})
: red vertices having more red than blue neighbors;

•

V(R_{2})
: red vertices having more blue than red neighbors;

•

V(B_{1})
: blue vertices having more blue than red neighbors;

•

V(B_{2})
: blue vertices having more red than blue neighbors.

Consider the bipartite subgraph
H
induced by the edges between
V(R)
and
V(B)
.
The number of edges of
H
satisfies

|E(H)|=\sum_{v\in V(R)}\deg_{H}(v)=\sum_{v\in V(B)}\deg_{H}(v).

Using the quasi neighborhood balanced coloring condition, we obtain the following expression for
|E(H)|
:

\displaystyle|E(H)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(R_{1})}\frac{\deg_{G}(v)-1}{2}+\sum_{v\in V(R_{2})}\frac{\deg_{G}(v)+1}{2}

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(B_{1})}\frac{\deg_{G}(v)-1}{2}+\sum_{v\in V(B_{2})}\frac{\deg_{G}(v)+1}{2}.

Rearranging (
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(R)}\deg_{G}(v)+|V(R_{2})|-|V(R_{1})|=\sum_{v\in V(B)}\deg_{G}(v)+|V(B_{2})|-|V(B_{1})|.

(2)

Since

2|E(G)|=\sum_{v\in V(R)}\deg_{G}(v)+\sum_{v\in V(B)}\deg_{G}(v),

substituting (
2
) yields

\displaystyle 2|E(G)|

\displaystyle=2\sum_{v\in V(B)}\deg_{G}(v)+|V(B_{2})|-|V(B_{1})|+|V(R_{1})|-|V(R_{2})|.

Equation (
1
) also gives

2|E(H)|-|V(B_{2})|+|V(B_{1})|=\sum_{v\in V(B)}\deg_{G}(v).

Substituting this expression into the previous equality, we obtain

\displaystyle 2|E(G)|

\displaystyle=4|E(H)|-|V(B_{2})|+|V(B_{1})|-|V(R_{2})|+|V(R_{1})|

\displaystyle=4|E(H)|-|V(B_{2})\cup V(R_{2})|+|V(B_{1})\cup V(R_{1})|.

Thus,

|E(H)|=\frac{2|E(G)|-r+s}{4},

where
r=|V(R_{2})\cup V(B_{2})|
and
s=|V(R_{1})\cup V(B_{1})|
.

Observe that:

•

For a
positive
quasi neighborhood balanced coloring,
r=0
and
s
equals the number of odd-degree vertices.

•

For a
negative
quasi neighborhood balanced coloring,
s=0
and
r
equals the number of odd-degree vertices.

These calculations yield the following results.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph admitting a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

1.

If
G
admits a positive quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, then

2|E(G)|\equiv-k\ (\bmod\ {4}),

2.

If
G
admits a negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, then

2|E(G)|\equiv k\ (\bmod\ {4}),

where
k
denotes the number of vertices of odd degree.

Corollary 2.3
.

For a (odd) regular graph
G
admitting a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring,

1.

If
G
admits a positive quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, then

2|E(G)|\equiv-|V(G)|\ (\bmod\ {4}),

2.

If
G
admits a negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring, then

2|E(G)|\equiv|V(G)|\ (\bmod\ {4}).

Some quasi neighborhood Balanced Colored Graphs

Theorem 2.4
.

The complete graph
K_{n}
is negative quasi neighborhood balanced colored if and only if
n
is even.

Proof.

When
n
is even, color exactly
\frac{n}{2}
vertices of
K_{n}
red and the remaining
\frac{n}{2}
vertices blue. Then every vertex has
\frac{n}{2}-1
neighbors of its own color and
\frac{n}{2}
neighbors of the opposite color, so the difference between the two counts is exactly
1
. Hence this coloring is a negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
K_{n}
.

For
n
odd, every vertex of
K_{n}
has even degree. By Lemma
2.1
, no such graph admits a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring. Therefore
K_{n}
is not a quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph when
n
is odd.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

The graph

admits positive quasi neighborhood balanced coloring for all positive integers

and

Proof.

Color the vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}

Under this coloring, each vertex

2.3
Operations with quasi neighborhood Balanced Colored Graphs

For any of the graph products and a fixed vertex
u
of
G
, the set of vertices
\{(u,v):\ v\in V(H)\}
is called an
H
-layer, and we denote it by
H_{u}
. Similarly, if
v\in V(H)
is fixed, then the set of vertices
\{(u,v):\ u\in V(G)\}
is called a
G
-layer and we denote it by
G_{v}
.
If one constructs
V(G)\times V(H)
in a natural way, the
H
-layers are represented by rows and the
G
-layers are represented by columns.

The
lexicographic product
of
G
and
H
, denoted by
G[H]
, is a graph with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and two vertices
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if either
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
or
u=u^{\prime}
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.
It may be instructive to instead construct
G[H]
by replacing every vertex of
G
with a copy of
H
and then replacing each edge of
G
with a complete bipartite graph between the corresponding copies of
H
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
G
and
H
be two graphs. If
H
admits quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
c
with
|V^{c}_{H}(R)|=|V^{c}_{H}(B)|
, then
G[H]
admits quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Proof.

Color vertices of
G[H]
as follows.
Apply the quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
c
of
H
to every
H
-layer in the lexicographic product
G[H]
. We claim that this induces a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
G[H]
.

Consider any vertex
(u,v)\in G[H]
. Since
c
is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
H
, the difference between the number of red neighbors and blue neighbors of
(u,v)
within its own layer
H_{u}
is at most one.

Now look at the neighbors of
(u,v)
outside the
H
-layer
H_{u}
. For each neighbor
u^{\prime}
of
u
in
G
, the vertex
(u,v)
is adjacent to every vertex in the layer
H_{u^{\prime}}
. Hence, outside its layer,
(u,v)
has

\deg_{G}(u)\cdot|V_{H}^{c}(R)|
red neighbors and

\deg_{G}(u)\cdot|V_{H}^{c}(B)|
blue neighbors.
Further, as
|V_{H}^{c}(R)|=|V_{H}^{c}(B)|
, the contributions of red and blue neighbors from all other layers is equal.

Combining these two observations, the total difference between the number of red and blue neighbors of
(u,v)
in
G[H]
is at most one. Thus the coloring obtained is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
G[H]
, as claimed.
∎

Using the same approach as in the above theorem, we can show that if the graph

H
admits a uniform/positive/negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring with

|V_{H}^{c}(R)|=|V_{H}^{c}(B)|
, then the lexicographic product
G[H]

also admits a uniform/positive/negative quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Theorem 2.11
.

If
G
is neighborhood balanced colored graph and
H
is quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph, then
G[H]
is quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph.

Proof.

Let
g
be a neighborhood balanced coloring of
G
and let
h
be a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
H
. We define a coloring of the lexicographic product
G[H]
as follows.

Fix a vertex
u\in V(G)
and consider any vertex
(u,v)\in V(G[H])
. If
g(u)
is red, then we color the entire
H
-layer
H_{u}
using the coloring
h
. If
g(u)
is blue, then we color the entire
H
-layer
H_{u}
using the complementary coloring
\bar{h}
. In this way, all the vertices of
G[H]
are colored. We claim that this is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
G[H]
.

Consider an arbitrary vertex
(u,v)\in V(G[H])
. Since
G
is a neighborhood balanced coloring graph, we may assume that
u
has exactly
k
red neighbors and
k
blue neighbors in
G
for some integer
k
. Because
h
(and therefore
\bar{h}
) is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
H
, the difference between the number of red and blue neighbors of
(u,v)
within its own layer
H_{u}
is at most one.

It remains to analyze the neighbors of
(u,v)
lying outside
H_{u}
. Each red neighbor of
u
in
G
contributes a full copy of
H
, colored according to
h
, and each blue neighbor contributes a full copy of
H
, colored according to
\bar{h}
. Hence the number of red neighbors of
(u,v)
outside
H_{u}
is

k\bigl(|V_{H}^{h}(R)|+|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(R)|\bigr)
,
and the number of blue neighbors outside
H_{u}
is

k\bigl(|V_{H}^{h}(B)|+|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(B)|\bigr)
.

Since
h
and
\bar{h}
are complementary colorings, we have

|V_{H}^{h}(R)|=|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(B)|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|V_{H}^{h}(B)|=|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(R)|.

Therefore,

|V_{H}^{h}(R)|+|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(R)|=|V_{H}^{h}(B)|+|V_{H}^{\bar{h}}(B)|,

and thus the red and blue contributions from all other layers are equal.

Combining this with the fact that the difference within
H_{u}
is at most one, it follows that
(u,v)
satisfies the quasi neighborhood balanced condition in
G[H]
.

Hence the defined coloring is a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring of
G[H]
, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.12

If
G
is quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph and
H
is neighborhood balanced colored graph, then
G\times H
is neighborhood balanced colored graph.

Proof.

Let
G
admit a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring
g
, and let
H
admit a neighborhood balanced coloring
h
. We define a coloring on the direct product
G\times H
as follows.

Fix a vertex
v\in V(H)
and consider any vertex
(u,v)\in V(G\times H)
. If
v
is colored red under
h
, then we color the entire
G
-layer
G_{v}
using
g
. If
v
is colored blue, we color the layer
G_{v}
using the complementary coloring
\overline{g}
. In this way, every vertex of
G\times H
receives a color.

We now show that this coloring is a neighborhood balanced coloring of
G\times H
.

Let
(u,v)
be any vertex in
G\times H
. Suppose that, under the coloring
g
of
G
, the vertex
u
has exactly one more red neighbor than blue. Then in the product
G\times H
, the vertex
(u,v)
will have one more red neighbor than blue in precisely those layers
G_{v^{\prime}}
where
v^{\prime}
is a neighbor of
v
in
H
that is colored red (under
h
). Likewise,
(u,v)
will have one more blue neighbor than red in the layers corresponding to neighbors
v^{\prime}
of
v
in
H
that are colored blue.

Since
H
is a neighborhood balanced graph, the vertex
v
has an equal number of red and blue neighbors under the coloring
h
. Consequently, every vertex
(u,v)
in
G\times H
has exactly the same number of red and blue neighbors and thus the defined coloring is a neighborhood balanced coloring of
G\times H
, completing the proof.
∎

2.4
Non-hereditary property of the class of quasi neighborhood balanced colored graphs

A family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs is hereditary if each induced subgraph of every graph in
\mathcal{F}
is also in
\mathcal{F}
.
Further, it is known that the hereditary classes can be characterized by providing a list of forbidden induced subgraphs.
Next, we show that the class of quasi neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not hereditary.

Theorem 2.20
.

The class of quasi neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not hereditary.

Proof.

To prove the theorem, it is enough to show that every graph is an induced subgraph of a quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph.

Let
G
be a graph with
V(G)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
. Construct a graph
H
from
G
with the vertex set
V(H)=\cup_{j=1}^{3}\{v_{i}^{j};\ 1\leq i\leq n\}
and the edge set

E(G)=\big(\cup_{p=1}^{2}\{v_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p};\ v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\}\big)\cup\{v_{i}^{1}v_{j}^{2};\ v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\}\cup\{v_{i}^{2}v_{i}^{3};\ 1\leq i\leq n\}.

It is easy to see that
G
is an induced subgraph of
H
. In
H
for
1\leq i\leq n
, color the vertices
v_{i}^{1}
using color red, the vertices
v_{i}^{2}
using color blue and the vertices
v_{i}^{3}
again using color blue.

Note that the vertices
v_{i}^{1}
have an equal number of neighbors of both colors. The vertices
v_{i}^{2}
have one more blue neighbor compared to red, while the vertices
v_{i}^{3}
also have one more blue neighbor compared to red. Hence, the graph
H
is a quasi neighborhood balanced colored graph.
∎

3
Hardness Results

We formally state the decision problem whether a given graph
G
admits a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Quasi Neighborhood Balanced Coloring

Input:
A graph
G
.

Question:
Is there a vertex coloring of
G
using colors red and blue, such that for any vertex, the difference between the number of red and blue neighbors is at most one, and there is at least one vertex where this difference is exactly one.

Our reduction relies on the neighborhood balanced coloring problem, whose
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness is established in
[
1
]
.

Neighborhood Balanced Coloring Problem

Input:
A graph
G
.

Question:
Is there a coloring of
G
using colors red and blue, such that for any vertex, there are an equal number of neighbors of both colors.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Quasi Neighborhood Balanced Coloring problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

We give a reduction from the neighborhood balanced coloring problem.
Let
G
be a graph that admits a neighborhood balanced coloring. We construct from
G
a graph
G^{\prime}
that admits a quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.

Let
e
be any red–blue edge of
G
whose endpoints are
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
.
Introduce a new vertex
v_{e}
and make it adjacent to both
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
.
Assign the color red to
v_{e}
.
Denote the resulting graph by
G^{\prime}
.

In
G^{\prime}
, every vertex of
G
other than
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
still has an equal number of red and blue neighbors, exactly as in the original neighborhood balanced coloring of
G
.
However, the vertices
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
each acquire one additional red neighbor, namely
v_{e}
.
Thus each of them has red–blue neighbor difference exactly one, while all other vertices have difference zero.
Hence
G^{\prime}
is a nearly neighborhood balanced colorable graph.

Conversely, suppose that
G^{\prime}
is quasi neighborhood balanced coloring.
Observe that the only vertices of odd degree in
G^{\prime}
are
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
, and both are adjacent solely to the added vertex
v_{e}
through which this imbalance occurs.
Since
a_{e}
and
b_{e}
each differ by exactly one in their counts of red and blue neighbors, and both obtain this imbalance from the same vertex
v_{e}
, deleting
v_{e}
restores balance at both vertices.

Remove
v_{e}
from
G^{\prime}
to obtain the graph
G
.
All vertices of
G
then have equal numbers of red and blue neighbors, establishing that
G
admits a neighborhood balanced coloring.
∎
