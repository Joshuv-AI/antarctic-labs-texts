Integration of lung function in allostatic load scoring and its impact on mortality prediction
Authors: Mohammad Azizzadeh, Emiel F. M. Wouters, Ahmad Karimi, Daisy J. A. Janssen, Bart Spaetgens, Sylvia Hartl, Robab Breyer-Kohansal, Marie-Kathrin Breyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Allostatic load refers to the physiological "wear and tear" that results from adaptation to stressors over the lifespan. In this study, we integrated lung function (LF) parameters into the calculation of the allostatic load score (ALS) to evaluate changes in its performance for predicting all-cause mortality. Data from 8,775 participants (aged 25–82 years; 52% female) who participated in the first wave of the Austrian LEAD cohort were used. “ALS without LF” was calculated using 12 parameters, including cardiovascular, metabolic, body composition, and bone mineral density measures. Z-scores of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were integrated with the aforementioned parameters for the calculation of “ALS with LF”. Participants scored 1 point for each at-risk marker and 1 point for medication use for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia. The total points constituted the ALS. Participants were followed-up for death for an average of 7.7 years. The association of ALS, with and without LF, with mortality was investigated using Cox proportional hazards models. ALS increased with age and was higher in males compared to females across all age categories. ALS (as a continuous variable) with LF [hazard ratio (HR): 1.19 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.14–1.24)] and without LF [HR: 1.16 (95% CI 1.11–1.21)] showed a significant association with mortality in sex- and age-adjusted models. The adjusted models incorporating ALS as a categorical variable showed that individuals with high ALS, with [HR: 2.44 (95% CI 1.41–4.20)] and without [HR: 2.47 (95% CI 1.46–4.80)] LF parameters, had a higher risk of mortality compared to those in the low ALS group. Cox models incorporating “ALS with LF” parameters exhibited higher concordance index and R2 values, along with a lower Akaike’s information criterion indicating superior predictive power compared to models that included “ALS without LF”. ALS is strongly associated with mortality, with higher ALS linked to an increased risk of mortality across both continuous and categorical analyses. Models that incorporate ALS with LF parameters demonstrated superior predictive performance and greater robustness, underscoring the added value of including LF in the models.

Introduction

The allostatic load (AL), which is quantified by the allostatic load score (ALS), is a measure of cumulative biological dysregulation representing physiological `wear and tear’ that the body sustains throughout the life course1,2. The term allostasis was originated from the view that healthy functioning requires continual adjustments of the internal physiological milieu. When environmental challenges and stressors exceed the individual’s ability to cope, the allostatic system becomes dysregulated and allostatic load may ensues3. ALS is most commonly calculated using biomarker measures from various systems including the neuroendocrine, metabolic, cardiovascular and inflammatory systems4,5. As an aggregate measure of these biomarkers, ALS reflects the severity of physiological dysregulation across multiple systems in response to chronic stressors, which ultimately contribute to disease outcomes over time6. There is no consensus approach for calculation of ALS. The number of biomarkers used for calculation of ALS in published studies have ranged from 6 to 247. Systematic reviews addressing variations in AL measurement have reported that only a few studies have incorporated lung function (LF) parameters as components of AL5,8.

ALS may be such a predictor. Indeed, AL has been shown to predict the risk of various physical and mental health outcomes, as well as mortality. However, most studies in this area have included populations with a limited age range, typically focusing on middle-aged or older adults, or individuals with specific disorders9–14. Furthermore, although lung function has been demonstrated to be associated with mortality risk15,16, none of the studies examining the relationship between AL and mortality have included lung function parameters in the calculation of AL17.

The Austrian LEAD (Lung, hEart, sociAl, boDy) study is a longitudinal, observational, population-based cohort study, that recruited around 15,000 participants, ranging in age from 6 to 82 years. In the present study, a subset of adult participants aged ≥ 25 was investigated: i) to explore ALS in the Austrian general adult population; ii) to investigate the relationship between ALS and subsequent all-cause mortality risk; and iii) to evaluate whether integrating lung function parameters into the calculation of ALS could improve the ability to predict mortality risk.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data from the first wave of Austrian LEAD study (NCT01727518; http://clinicaltrials.gov), an ongoing longitudinal, observational, population-based cohort, was used. In this cohort, participants were randomly selected from general population of Vienna (urban population) and six villages in Lower Austria (rural population). A comprehensive overview of the study protocol and the sociodemographic characteristics of participation has been published previously18. In summary, the LEAD study examines the respiratory, cardiovascular and mental health, including a blood parameters profile. Moreover, sociodemographic parameters and body composition profile are recorded. Participants who attended the baseline visit are invited to take part in follow-up visits every four years.

Lung function parameters, including pre- and post-bronchodilation spirometry, are measured in accordance with international recommendations from the European Respiratory Society (ERS)19. Reference values (Z-scores) for spirometry indices are calculated using the global lung function initiative (GLI) reference equations20 with ethnicity set as Caucasian for all participants. Brachial blood pressure (BP) is measured in the sitting position using the auscultatory method according to international standards21. Body composition and bone density are assessed using a Lunar Prodigy TM (GE Healthcare©, USA) DXA scanner. Reference values (Z-scores) for body composition measures are derived from reference equations generated within the LEAD study22. Blood parameters are analyzed using standard laboratory methods with the Siemens Dimension™ VISTA 1500 system.

14,966 participants, aged 6–82, were recruited in the LEAD study between 2012–2022 and completed the measurements of the first visit. 5,052 participants were excluded from our analysis, including those younger than 25 years at the time of the visit (n = 4887) and those with invalid spirometry data (n = 166). Additionally, 1,168 individuals with missing data for at least one parameter required for the calculation of ALS were excluded. Ultimately, 8,775 participants with complete data for ALS calculation were included in the analysis.

All the measurements were performed at the LEAD study centre of the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for Lung Health located at the Clinic Penzing, Vienna, Austria by trained technicians following standard operation procedures. The LEAD study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the City of Vienna (Ethikkommission der Stadt Wien, Vienna, Austria; protocol number EK-11-117-0711). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Allostatic load scoring

ALS without LF parameters

“ALS without LF” was calculated based on 12 parameters, including cardiovascular [systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP)]; metabolic [hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR, computed using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation)]; inflammatory [high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)]; and body composition and bone mineral density parameters [Z-scores of appendicular lean mass index (ALMI, defined as appendicular lean mass (kg)/(height (m))2), fat mass index (FMI, defined as fat mass (kg)/(height (m))2), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), and total body bone mineral density (BMD)].

Using data which was available in the first visit for the study population, the 25th and 75th percentiles for each parameter, were determined. The at-risk range was defined as above the 75th percentile for SBP, DBP, HbA1c, TC, TG, hs-CRP, z-score of FMI, z-score of VAT, and below the 25th percentile for HDL, GFR, z-score of total body BMD, and z-score of ALMI. For each biomarker, participants were assigned a score of 1 if the biomarker fell within the at-risk range and 0 if it did not. Furthermore, participants received 1 point for medication for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, each. The total points constituted the allostatic load score.

ALS with LF parameters

The same approach used for the calculation of "ALS without LF" was applied to calculate "ALS with LF." The only difference was the inclusion of two additional lung function parameters, the z-scores of pre-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and pre-bronchodilator forced vital capacity (FVC), in the calculation of "ALS with LF." For these parameters, values below the 25th percentile were considered to represent the at-risk range.

All-cause mortality

Mortality data for all LEAD participants, including the date and cause of death, was provided by Austria’s Federal Statistical Office (Statistik Austria), the agency responsible for collecting and publishing official statistics for the country, up to the end of December 2022. Since information on deaths was obtained from Statistik Austria, data was available even for individuals lost to follow-up in the cohort.

Among the 8,775 participants included in the analysis, 327 died between their participation date and December 31, 2022. Follow-up time (time to event) was calculated from the date of the visit to the date of death for participants who died before the end of 2022, and from the date of the visit to December 31, 2022, for survivors.

Statistical analysis

Selected characteristics of the non-survivor and survivor participants described as counts and percentages, for categorical covariates and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Distribution of ALS across age and sex categories were also described. ALS was compared between males and females using Mann–Whitney U test and between different age categories using Kruskal–Wallis test. Unadjusted and adjusted (for sex and age) Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between ALS with and without LF (as continuous variable) and mortality. Participants were also categorized into three groups based on ALS percentiles: those with ALS ≤ 10th percentile were considered low risk (0–1), those with 10th < ALS < 90th percentile were categorized as intermediate risk (2–7), and participants with ALS ≥ 90th percentile were classified as high risk (≥ 8). This categorization was applied to both ALS with and without LF parameters. Unadjusted and sex- and age-adjusted Cox models were then used to compute HRs for participants in the intermediate- and high-risk groups compared to those in the low-risk ALS group. Additionally, survival curves based on the Cox model were constructed to illustrate survival probabilities for participants in the low, intermediate, and high ALS groups. To evaluate whether integrating LF parameters into the calculation of ALS improves the ability to predict mortality risk, the performance of prediction models containing ALS with and without LF parameters was assessed using the concordance index, explained variation (R2), and Akaike’s information criterion (AIC). An AIC difference of ≥ 10 between two models was considered significant. The model with the lower AIC value was regarded as the better fit23. Furthermore, the association of each individual component of ALS with mortality risk was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumptions for the models were tested using Schoenfeld residuals. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R v3.4.1 within the RStudio environment v1.0.143.

Cox models incorporating ALS as categorical variable

ALS without LF

The hazard ratio for mortality increased with increasing level of the allostatic load from low as the reference over mid to high. The unadjusted Cox proportional hazard model showed that participants with intermediate [HR (95% CI): 4.75 (2.98–7.59), P < 0.001] and high [HR (95% CI): 16.34 (9.91–26.96), P < 0.001] ALS had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to the low ALS group. In the adjusted model, only the high ALS group [HR (95% CI): 2.47 (1.46–4.80)] exhibited a significantly greater risk of mortality compared to the low ALS group (Table 3b).

ALS with LF

The unadjusted Cox proportional hazard model revealed that participants with intermediate [HR (95% CI): 3.70 (2.22–6.17); P < 0.001] and high [HR (95% CI): 13.94 (8.26–23.52); P < 0.001] ALS had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to those in the low ALS group. However, in the adjusted model, only the high ALS group [HR (95% CI): 2.44 (1.41–4.20); P < 0.001] showed a significantly greater risk of mortality compared to the low ALS group (Table 3b).

Assessment of model performance

Models incorporating “ALS with LF” demonstrated better performance compared to those similar models incorporating “ALS without LF”, as indicated by higher explained variation (R2) and concordance values, as well as lower AIC (Delta AIC = 15.38 for adjusted models). Additionally, based on R2, concordance index, and AIC, the adjusted models showed superior performance over the unadjusted models (Table 4b).

Association of age and sex with mortality risk

In all adjusted models, increasing age was associated with a higher risk of death, and women had a lower risk of death compared to men, regardless of whether ALS with or without LF was included (Table 3a, b).

Association of individual components of ALS with mortality risk

Cox proportional hazard regression analysis assessing the association between individual biomarkers of ALS and all-cause mortality revealed that being in the at-risk range for all ALS components was significantly associated with mortality risk (P < 0.05) in unadjusted models, except for TC. Discussion

The present study provides comprehensive insights into the distribution of ALS in a European population. The findings demonstrate that ALS serves as a valuable single measure for predicting all-cause mortality. Controlling for the effects of age and sex, individuals with a high versus low total ALS had about 2.5 times the mortality risk. Furthermore, our analyses revealed that being in the at-risk range for FEV₁ or FVC was significantly associated with increased mortality, underscoring the critical role of respiratory function in healthy aging. By incorporating these lung function measures into ALS, we capture an additional physiological domain that is not reflected by traditional cardiovascular, metabolic, or inflammatory biomarkers. Including LF parameters in ALS provided a more holistic assessment of physiological health and enhanced its predictive ability for all-cause mortality.

The average ALS, calculated using 10 biomarkers in the adult Scottish population and 9 biomarkers in the U.S. population aged ≥ 25, was 2.52 and 1.95, respectively24,25. As the construction of AL indices used for calculation of ALS are different across the studies5,26, comparing the average of ALS between studies is not insightful. However, our findings regarding increasing ALS with age and its higher average in male than female across all age categories are in line with the results of other studies12,25,27. Biological and somatic aging are linked to natural age-related changes in various physiological biomarkers28. Additionally, as people age, they are likely to encounter a greater number of stressful events compared to younger individuals. Consequently, positive associations between age and allostatic load are commonly observed across different populations. Furthermore, some health condition affect females differently such as heart attack, stroke, and alzheimer’s disease which might contribute to lower allostatic load scores in women29–31.

Our findings on the significant association of ALS and mortality risk was in line with the results of other studies in European32–34 and non-European countries10,24. In a Scottish population, Robertson et al. reported that ALS, comprising eight markers from the cardiovascular, metabolic, and inflammatory systems, was significantly associated with a 10-year mortality risk (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16) in an adjusted model32. Bruun-Rasmussen et al. showed that individuals with high ALS had approximately 2.5 times the mortality risk compared to those with low ALS (HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.68–3.59)34. Additionally, Levine and Crimmins reported that individuals in the highest quintile of allostatic load had a 2.75-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality compared to those in the lowest quintile35.

High explained variation (R2) and concordance index especially for adjusted models indicate very good fit of prediction models developed in the present study. The performance of predictive models has not been reported in the studies focused on the association of ALS and mortality. Most studies have included body mass index (BMI) and/or waist-to-hip ratio in the calculation of ALS. However, evidence suggests that the distribution of soft tissue within the body significantly influences cardiometabolic and mortality risk36. Thus, incorporating body composition profiles instead of BMI in the calculation of ALS in the present study is a notable advantage. Moreover, utilising standardised values (z-score) for body composition parameters (standardised for age and sex) and lung function (standardised for age, sex and height), as well as accounting for medication use in the calculation of ALS, likely contributed to the high performance of the models37.

Our findings demonstrated that models incorporating ALS with LF parameters performed better than those without LF in predicting mortality, as indicated by higher explained variation (R2), higher concordance index, and lower AIC. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to evaluate the association between “ALS with LF” and mortality risk. The improved performance of ALS with LF parameters may reflect the unique physiological information captured by FEV1 and FVC. These measures are not only indicators of respiratory health but also integrative markers of cumulative exposures and systemic processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, environmental hazards, and aging-related morbidities. Reduced FEV1 and FVC can therefore reflect multisystem dysregulation rather than isolated pulmonary impairment. The relationship between lung function and mortality, as well as some health conditions, has been well-established in previous studies. Individuals with low lung function have been shown to exhibit a higher prevalence of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, along with an increased risk of all-cause mortality38–40. Furthermore, evaluation of the association between individual biomarkers of ALS and mortality in the present study revealed that being in the at-risk range for the Z-scores of FEV1 and FVC was significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality. Lung function parameters showed a strong association with mortality, highlighting their crucial role in longevity. Therefore, incorporating LF parameters into the list of biomarkers for calculating ALS, an aggregated marker of multiple system dysregulation, would be beneficial, particularly in studies focused on the concept of healthy (aging).

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of our study were the size of the cohort with wide age range in terms of the large number of individuals recruited from a general adult Austrian population, and the complete follow-up for death by linkage with the Statistik Austria. Another strength of the present study was availability of comprehensive data on standardised value (Z-score) for body composition and bone density as well as lung function parameters. Our study also had some limitations. The AL theory emphasises the importance of measuring dysregulation across different physiological, including neuroendocrine system. Biomarkers from the neuroendocrine system were not available for our study. Furthermore, all biomarkers were weighted equally in the score, which may not reflect their relative contribution to health outcomes. Similarly, dichotomizing biomarker values does not capture the magnitude of deviation from the reference range, which is an inherent limitation of traditional allostatic load indices.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that an elevated physiological burden, as measured by ALS, is a strong predictor of mortality risk among adults aged 25 years or older. Incorporating lung function parameters into the components used to calculate ALS enhances its predictive capacity for all-cause mortality. Therefore, allostatic load is identified as a potential modifiable risk factor for all-cause mortality, with significant potential as a prognostic indicator.
