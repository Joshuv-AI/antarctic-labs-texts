Gluteal Calcifying Aponeurotic Fibroma: An Exceptional Location of a Rare Fibroblastic Tumour: A Case Report
Authors: Xiaolan You, Erqiong Leng, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcifying aponeurotic fibroma (CAF) is a rare, benign but locally aggressive fibroblastic tumour classically involving the palms and soles of children and adolescents. Occurrence in atypical locations presents substantial diagnostic challenges. Gluteal involvement of CAF is exceedingly rare, we report a case of CAF arising in the gluteal region of a man in his twenties who presented with a four-year history of a slow-growing mass and recent pulling pain. Physical examination revealed a firm, ill-defined, deeply adherent mass with a striking gritty texture on palpation and scattered white spots on the overlying skin. Ultrasonography demonstrated a hypoechoic subcutaneous nodule containing internal hyperechoic foci consistent with calcifications. Complete surgical excision was performed, and histopathology revealed the diagnostic triad of CAF. At the 9-month follow-up (prior to submission), no recurrence or complications were observed, and the patient had returned to normal activities. This case highlights that CAF can occur in non‑acral sites such as the buttock, expanding the differential diagnosis of calcified subcutaneous nodules in young adults. The characteristic gritty texture may provide a valuable bedside diagnostic clue. Complete surgical excision with clear margins remains the mainstay of treatment.Keywords: calcifying aponeurotic fibroma, soft tissue neoplasms, buttock, diagnosis, differential, calcinosis

Introduction

Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma (CAF) is a rare, benign but locally aggressive fibroblastic tumour first described by Keasbey in 1953.1 It typically occurs on the palms and soles of children and adolescents, with pathogenesis hypothesised to involve repetitive microtrauma to aponeurotic tissues.1 However, CAF can exceptionally arise in atypical locations such as the trunk, head and neck, and proximal extremities.2–4 Gluteal involvement remains exceedingly rare, with only one previously reported case of intramuscular CAF in the gluteus maximus.5 Owing to its non-specific clinical presentation and extreme rarity, CAF occurring in unusual sites represents a significant diagnostic pitfall. We report a case of gluteal CAF and discuss its clinical, radiological and histopathological features, with emphasis on differential diagnosis and practical implications for clinical practice.

Case Report

A previously healthy man in his twenties presented with a slowly enlarging mass on the left buttock, which had recently become associated with a pulling sensation. He first noted the lesion several years prior as a small, painless nodule measuring approximately 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm, with no identifiable precipitating factors. Personal, social and family history were unremarkable.

Physical examination revealed a firm, ill-defined, deeply adherent mass measuring approximately 3 cm × 3 cm in the upper outer quadrant of the left buttock. Palpation elicited a striking gritty texture, and the overlying skin exhibited scattered white spots. Ultrasonography demonstrated a hypoechoic subcutaneous nodule measuring 2.2×0.9 × 0.7 cm containing internal hyperechoic foci consistent with calcifications (Figure 1). The lesion was completely excised under local anaesthesia. Gross examination revealed a firm, gray-white nodule with serous exudate on the surface (Figure 2). Histopathological examination showed an infiltrative, fascicular proliferation of bland spindle cells interspersed with chondroid and calcified nodules, some of which were surrounded by palisading epithelioid cells (Figure 3a–c). These features established the diagnosis of calcifying aponeurotic fibroma. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. A follow-up evaluation was performed via telephone immediately before manuscript submission, corresponding to 9 months postoperatively. At this time point, no evidence of local recurrence or procedure-associated complications was documented. The patient continued to be asymptomatic and had fully resumed all routine daily activities, without any functional impairment.

Discussion

We report a rare case of calcifying aponeurotic fibroma occurring in the gluteal region, an extremely unusual location for this tumour. While CAF is most commonly encountered on the palms and feet, the gluteal region — along with sites such as the elbow, upper arm, scalp, and trunk — represents a rare but recognised location.2–4 To our knowledge, only one previous case of CAF involving the gluteus maximus muscle has been reported, making this the second documented case in the gluteal region and the first to present as a subcutaneous lesion with such a pronounced gritty texture.5

The clinical significance of this case lies in two key observations. First, the distinct gritty texture appreciated on palpation served as a valuable bedside diagnostic clue. This finding, attributable to intralesional calcifications, may be more perceptible in the buttock due to the relatively thinner overlying subcutaneous tissue compared to the palm or sole. Second, the occurrence of CAF in a non-acral site challenges the traditional theory that pathogenesis is strictly linked to repetitive microtrauma in palmar and plantar aponeurotic tissues, and supports evolving hypotheses that CAF may arise from primitive mesenchymal cells with multidifferentiation potential in response to aberrant local microenvironmental signals.6

For clinicians, when encountering a firm, slow-growing subcutaneous nodule with calcification in a young adult, the differential diagnosis includes several entities. Nodular fasciitis typically presents with rapid growth and histologically shows tissue culture-like fibroblasts in a myxoid stroma, lacking the calcified chondroid islands characteristic of CAF.7 Soft tissue chondroma usually occurs in middle-aged to older adults, affects the hands and feet, and consists of well-circumscribed mature hyaline cartilage lobules without an infiltrative fibroblastic background.8 Fibrous hamartoma of infancy is almost exclusively seen in infants and exhibits a characteristic triphasic organoid histology.9 Imaging may reveal calcifications, but findings remain non-specific. Therefore, definitive diagnosis relies entirely on histopathological identification of the classic triad: infiltrative spindle cell proliferation, chondroid differentiation, and pathognomonic calcified foci with palisading epithelioid cells.1

Management consists of complete surgical excision with clear margins where feasible.10 Due to the tumour’s locally infiltrative growth pattern, recurrence rates of up to 50% have been reported, particularly following incomplete excision. Long-term clinical follow-up is therefore essential.11

We acknowledge the inherent limitations of this single-case report. First, MRI was not performed, which could have provided valuable preoperative information regarding the lesion’s extent and its relationship to adjacent anatomical structures, such as the gluteal muscles and sciatic nerve. Second, the original pathology report did not explicitly document surgical margin status; although gross total excision was achieved intraoperatively, the lack of formal microscopic margin assessment constitutes a notable limitation. Third, no ancillary pathological studies—including immunohistochemistry or molecular analysis—were undertaken, which might have further reinforced the diagnosis or excluded histopathological differentials. Finally, the follow-up duration of 9 months is relatively short, and thus cannot definitively rule out future relapse, despite the patient remaining asymptomatic to date. Long-term clinical surveillance remains essential to confirm sustained tumour control.

Conclusion

Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma should be included in the differential diagnosis of calcified subcutaneous nodules even in non-acral sites such as the buttock. The characteristic gritty texture may provide a valuable clinical clue, particularly in locations where the overlying soft tissue is thin. Enhanced awareness among clinicians can facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate management. Histopathological confirmation remains mandatory, and long-term follow-up is recommended due to the potential for local recurrence.
