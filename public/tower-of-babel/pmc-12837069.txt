Comprehensive analysis of phycobilisome linker proteins in Neopyropia yezoensis: classification, structure, evolution and function
Authors: Tian Gao, Yahui Yu, Xinyu Zhu, Hongye Xin, Chengzhen Meng, Wenlong Hu, Xianghai Tang, Yunxiang Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLinker proteins are essential components of phycobilisome (PBS), playing critical roles in phycobilisome assembly, energy transfer, and structural stability. However, these proteins remain poorly characterized in the economically important red macroalga Neopyropia yezoensis.ResultsIn this study, we identified 24 PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis and systematically investigated their physicochemical properties, structural features, chromosomal localizations, cis-acting elements, evolutionary relationships, and functional roles. These PBS linker proteins were classified into four groups (LR, LRC, LC, and LCM), with the majority encoded by nuclear genes. Notably, the number of genes within the LR group has markedly increased in Ny. yezoensis, which may have contributed to the species’ adaptation to diverse light environments. Expression analysis revealed that PBS linker genes exhibited distinct stage-specific expression patterns, with many highly expressed during the sporophyte stage, suggesting their crucial roles in adapting to the distinct environmental conditions associated with the two life-history stages of Ny. yezoensis. Furthermore, we found that the expression levels of PBS linker genes were correlated with nitrogen availability, indicating that these PBS linker proteins may regulate phycobilisome assembly in response to nitrogen fluctuations.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive analysis of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. Our findings offer valuable insights into the evolution and function of PBS linker proteins and advance our understanding of the adaptation mechanisms of Ny. yezoensis to complex and dynamic environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-08003-6.Keywords: Linker proteins, Neopyropia yezoensis, Expression analysis, Nitrogen availability, Adaptation mechanisms

Background

Photosynthesis is one of the most important biological processes and provides essential energy for most living organisms on earth. Light absorption constitutes the initial step in photosynthesis. Among photosynthetic organisms, various light-harvesting systems have evolved to efficiently capture light energy under different living environments [1]. Cyanobacteria and red algae have evolved sophisticated and highly efficient light-harvesting systems known as the phycobilisomes (PBSs) to adapt to complex and variable light environments [2, 3]. As the oldest and most prominent light-harvesting complexes, phycobilisomes exhibit exceptional energy transfer efficiency and possesses unique characteristics that distinguish them from antenna complexes found in other organisms, such as phototrophic bacteria, green algae, and higher plants [4]. Phycobilisomes capture light energy across a broad range of wavelengths in aquatic environments and transmit it efficiently to the reaction centers. With advancements in microscopy and spectroscopy, the structures of increasingly complex phycobilisomes and their energy transfer processes have been elucidated [1, 5, 6]. Phycobilisomes are exceptionally large and elaborate chromophore-protein complexes organized in a highly ordered structure on the stromal side of the thylakoid membrane in cyanobacteria and red algae [1].

Phycobilisomes are composed of phycobiliproteins (PBPs) and linker proteins, exhibiting remarkable diversity in structure and pigment composition in cyanobacteria and red algae [7]. Phycobiliproteins are water-soluble, brilliantly colored, disk-shaped protein complexes with covalently attached linear chain tetrapyrrole chromophores called phycobilins [8]. Phycobilins are categorized into four types: phycoerythrobilin (PEB), phycocyanobilin (PCB), phycoviolobilin (PVB) and phycourobilin (PUB), collectively determining the color and light absorption properties of phycobiliproteins [9]. Based on spectral characteristics and chromophore composition, phycobiliproteins can be classified into four groups: allophycocyanin (APC), phycocyanin (PC), phycoerythrin (PE) and phycoerythrocyanin (PEC) [10]. Notably, only the first three types (APC, PC, and PE) are found in red algae [11]. The basic structural unit of phycobiliproteins is a monomer consisting of α and β subunits with molecular weights of 16–18 kDa [3]. These monomers oligomerize into (αβ)3 trimer in a face-to-face arrangement and further assemble into (αβ)6 hexamer with three-fold symmetry [12]. PE contains an additional γ subunit, forming a more stable (αβ)6γ-hexameric structure [12]. Within phycobilisome architectures, phycobiliproteins efficiently capture light energy across a broad wavelength range (450–650 nm) and transfer it to photosystems to drive photosynthesis [8].

Linker proteins, another critical component of phycobilisomes, assemble phycobiliproteins into highly ordered structures within these antenna complexes [11]. Each trimeric or hexameric subassembly of the phycobilisome contains at least one specific linker protein. Based on their function and location, linker proteins can be classified into four types: rod linkers (LR), rod-core linkers (LRC), core linkers (LC), and core-membrane linkers (LCM) [7, 11]. LR stabilize and terminate rods, LRC connect rods to cores, LC stabilize and terminate core cylinders, and LCM facilitate the attachment of cores to the thylakoid membrane [3]. Linker proteins perform two critical functions in phycobilisomes: (1) structural organization: they connect adjacent phycobiliproteins, determine the positions of phycobiliproteins within the phycobilisome structure, and stabilize the overall phycobilisome assembly; (2) energy transfer modulation: they fine-tune absorption properties to facilitate or directly participate in the energy transfer from the peripheral rod to the core and ultimately to the photosynthetic reaction center [10, 13]. Previous studies suggest that the evolutionary origin of linker proteins in algae likely played a crucial role in their adaptation to diverse light environments [13, 14].

Neopyropia yezoensis (Bangiales, Rhodophyta), a marine red alga formerly known as Pyropia yezoensis or Porphyra yezoensis, is a highly nutritious species that represents one of the most economically significant marine crops globally, particularly in China, Japan, and Korea [15]. Notably, Ny. yezoensis has emerged as an excellent model organism for studying algal developmental biology and stress responses [16]. This species exhibits a distinctive heteromorphic life cycle, alternating between a microscopic diploid sporophyte (Fig. 1A) and a macroscopic haploid gametophyte (Fig. 1B) [17]. These two generations occupy markedly different ecological niches and display distinct morphological and physiological characteristics. The filamentous sporophyte (conchocelis) typically grows within dead calcareous shells submerged in seawater, where environmental conditions are relatively stable but light availability is limited (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the leafy gametophyte (blade) inhabits the upper intertidal zone of exposed coasts, a highly stressful environment characterized by constantly fluctuating light conditions (Fig. 1D). To adapt to these complex and variable light environments, Ny. yezoensis has developed highly efficient light-harvesting systems. The species possesses three phycobiliproteins: allophycocyanin, phycocyanin, and phycoerythrin [18]. Importantly, Ny. yezoensis can modulate both the composition and properties of phycobiliproteins within its phycobilisomes to acclimate to different light conditions [19]. As essential components of phycobilisomes, linker proteins likely play a critical role in this adaptive process. However, the current understanding of these mechanisms remains limited. To date, no high-resolution structure of Ny. yezoensis phycobilisome has been resolved, and research on phycobilisome in this species remains relatively scarce. Furthermore, systematic and comprehensive analyses of linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis are lacking.

In recent years, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of phycobilisomes from red algae have been increasingly resolved [5, 6, 20], providing valuable insights for the study of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. Furthermore, the availability of a high-quality assembled genome of Ny. yezoensis has facilitated research on PBS linker proteins in this species by providing reliable genomic information and enabling genome-wide analysis. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive and systematic characterization of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. Using bioinformatic approaches, we identified PBS linker proteins and analyzed their classification, phylogenetic relationships, structural features, chromosomal locations, conserved domains, interspecies collinearity, and cis-regulatory elements. Additionally, we systematically investigated the expression patterns of PBS linker genes across two heteromorphic life-history stages of Ny. yezoensis, as well as under nitrogen deficiency and resupply conditions using transcriptome data. This study provides valuable insights into the roles of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis and lays a foundation for further functional studies of these proteins.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification and characterization of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis

Two approaches were employed to identify PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. The first approach involved a BLAST search against the Ny. yezoensis proteome [16] using known PBS linker protein sequences from two red algal species, Griffithsia pacifica [5] and Porphyridium purpureum [6], as queries, with a default E-value threshold of 1e-5. The second approach was to retrieve Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles for all PBS linker protein domains (PF00427 and PF01383) from Pfam (https://pfam.sanger.ac.uk/) and use HMMER software version 3.0 [21] to identify PBS linker proteins with the default parameter. All candidate proteins were further validated for the presence of complete PBS linker domains using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). Proteins containing intact domains were designated as PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis and were named based on their orthologs in Gi. pacifica and Pi. purpureum. Physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, instability index, and theoretical isoelectric point (pI), were predicted using the ProtParam tool on ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of PBS linker genes, multiple sequence alignments were performed using the MEGA X-ClustalW program with default parameters for candidate PBS linker proteins from Ny. yezoensis and eight other red algal species: Neoporphyra haitanensis, Porphyra umbilicalis, Chondrus crispus, Gracilariopsis chorda, Cyanidioschyzon merolae, Galdieria sulphuraria, Gri. pacifica and Pi. purpureum. The PBS linker protein sequences of No. haitanensis were obtained from genome annotation data [22] based on their homology with Ny. yezoensis PBS linker proteins. Sequences for Pa. umbilicalis, Ch. crispus, Gra. chorda, Cy. merolae, and Ga. Sulphuraria were sourced from a previous study by Lee et al. (2019). A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE [23], with the best-fit substitution model automatically selected via ModelFinder [24] implemented in IQ-TREE. Branch support was assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation with 1,000 replicates along with the SH-aLRT branch test [25]. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using EvolView [26], and has been deposited in figshare (doi: 10.6084/m9.figshare.30464693).

Chromosomal location and gene structure analysis

The chromosomal location and gene structures of PBS linker genes were extracted from the Ny. yezoensis genome annotation file (GFF3 format). These features were visualized using TBtools-Gene Location Visualize from GTF/GFF and TBtools-Gene Structure View (Advanced), respectively [27].

Structural analysis of PBS linker proteins

The secondary structure of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis was analyzed using the online tool PSIPRED (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred). Structures of PBS linker proteins from Gri. pacifica and Pi. Purpureum served as structural references. Results from the CDD and InterPro were integrated to characterize the structural features of the PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. Finally, the protein structure were visualized using IBS (Illustrator for Biological Sequences) software [28].

Collinearity analysis

Interspecies collinearity analysis of PBS linker genes was performed between Ny. yezoensis and four other red algal species (No. haitanensis, Pa. umbilicalis, Gri. pacifica, and Pi. purpureum) using TBtools - One Step MCScanX - Super Fast software with default parameters [27]. The genome and annotation files for Ny. yezoensis and No. haitanensis were obtained from previous studies in our laboratory [16, 22], while those for Pa. umbilicalis, Gri. pacifica and Pi. Purpureum were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Collinearity results and orthologous PBS linker genes were visualized using TBtools - Dual Systeny Plot for MCscanX [27].

cis-acting element analysis

To identify potential cis-acting elements, 2-kb sequences upstream of the initiation codon of each PBS linker gene were extracted and submitted to PlantCARE [29]. Detected cis-acting elements were categorized based on their functional annotations, and their frequencies were visualized as a heatmap.

Expression analysis of PBS linker genes in two life cycle stages of Ny. yezoensis

The expression profiles of PBS linker genes were analyzed in two heteromorphic life-history stages (leafy gametophytes and filamentous sporophytes) using Illumina RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from our previous study [16]. The raw RNA-seq data are available in the NCBI database under BioProject accession number PRJNA589917. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) values. A gene expression heatmap was generated using TBtools software [27] based on log2 transformed FPKM values.

Analysis of phycobiliprotein composition in two life cycle stages of Ny. yezoensis

Linker proteins play a crucial role in phycobiliprotein assembly [1]. To compare the phycobiliprotein composition between the two life cycle stages of Ny. yezoensis, we analyzed the phycobiliprotein content and their respective ratios. The phycoerythrin (PE), phycocyanin (PC) and allophycocyanin (APC) were extracted according to the method described by Gao et al. (2025) [30]. The contents of these phycobiliproteins were calculated using the equations established by Kursar et al. (1983) [31].

Expression profiling of PBS linker genes under nitrogen deficiency and resupply conditions in Ny. yezoensis

Phycobilisomes function not only as light-harvesting antennae but also as nitrogen storage in red algae [11, 32]. To further understand their role in response to nitrogen fluctuations, we investigated the expression profiles of PBS linker genes in Ny. yezoensis under nitrogen deficiency and resupply conditions. Briefly, gametophytic blades (approximately 8 cm in length) were subjected to two nitrogen conditions: (1) normal nitrogen (NN) with 500 µM NaNO₃ and (2) low nitrogen (LN) with 10 µM NaNO₃ in Provasoli’s enrichment solution medium. After 8 days of nitrogen deficiency, the cultures were resupplied with nitrogen in the form of different sources: 500 µM NaNO₃ (nitrate), 500 µM NH₄Cl (ammonium), or 250 µM CH₄N₂O (urea), and maintained for an additional 4 days. All cultures were continuously aerated with filter-sterilized air, and the medium was replaced every two days. Growth conditions were maintained at 10 °C under 40 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ with a 12:12 h light: dark (L: D) photoperiod. Three biological replicates were included for each treatment group. All samples were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent RNA-seq analysis. RNA-seq analysis was performed as previously described [33]. A gene expression heatmap was generated using TBtools software [27] based on log2 transformed FPKM values. The raw RNA-seq data are available in the NCBI database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1169676 and PRJNA1307449.

Results

Identification and characterization of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis

A total of 24 PBS linker proteins were identified in Ny. yezoensis, and named based on their homology with PBS linker proteins from Gi. pacifica and Pi. Purpureum. The amino acid sequences of these proteins are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and their physicochemical properties are summarized in Table 1. The molecular weights of Ny. yezoensis PBS linker proteins ranged from 16.76 to 100.27 kDa, and their lengths ranged from 159 to 886 amino acids. Among them, LCM exhibited the longest sequence and highest molecular weight, whereas LC was the shortest and smallest. The predicted theoretical pI values ranged from 5.04 to 10.6, and instability indices ranged from 25.07 to 58.13. Furthermore, most genes encoding PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis are nuclear genes (91.67%), with a minority localized in the plastid genome (8.33%).

Sequence characteristics of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of PBS linker proteins

To investigate the evolutionary relationships and classification of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis, we constructed an unrooted maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree using full-length PBS linker protein sequences from Ny. yezoensis and eight other red algal species (Fig. 2). The classification of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis followed the schemes established for Gi. pacifica and Pi. purpureum in previous studies [5, 6]. Combining our phylogenetic analysis with prior studies [7, 11], the PBS linker proteins of Ny. yezoensis were categorized into four groups: rod linkers (LR), rod-core linkers (LRC), core linkers (LC), and core-membrane linkers (LCM). Among these, LR was the largest group and was further subdivided into LR1, LR2, LR3, LRγ4, LRγ5, LR6, LRγ6, LRγ8 and LR9. Notably, LRγ7 was absent in Ny. yezoensis. The LRC group was further classified into LRC1-LRC6. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PBS linker proteins from different species clustered more closely with those of the same class than with other linker proteins from the same species, suggesting that ancestral PBS linker genes diverged prior to species differentiation. Additionally, PBS linker proteins from Ny. yezoensis, No. haitanensis, and Pa. umbilicalis consistently grouped together. Strikingly, Ny. yezoensis possesses a significantly higher number of PBS linker proteins compared to Gi. pacifica and Pi. purpureum, indicating that extensive gene duplication events occurred after the speciation of Ny. yezoensis.

Chromosomal localization and gene structure of PBS linker genes

Using the genomic annotation information of Ny. yezoensis and TBtools [16, 27], we visualized the chromosomal distribution of the PBS linker genes. As shown in Fig. 3A, the majority of these genes (22 genes) were located in the nuclear genome, while the remaining two were identified in the plastid genome. Additionally, the number of genes on each chromosome correlated with the chromosome size. For instance, the largest chromosome (PY_1) harbored nine PBS linker genes, whereas the smallest chromosome (PY_3) contained six.

Chromosomal localization and gene structure of PBS linker genes in Ny. yezoensis. A Chromosomal location. Chromosome numbers are indicated at the centromeric position of each chromosome. The scale is in megabases (Mb). B Gene structure. Boxes filled with blue represent exons, and solid black lines represent introns

To characterize the gene structure, we analyzed the exon-intron composition of each PBS linker gene in Ny. yezoensis. Our results revealed that these genes either lacked introns or contained only a single intron, with no cases of multiple introns observed (Fig. 3B). Genes with few or no introns are often associated with rapid activation in response to various stress conditions [34].

Structural characterization of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis

Protein structure is intrinsically linked to biological function. To elucidate the functional diversity of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis, we performed comprehensive structural analyses. The LR proteins were classified into three groups based on their domain architectures (Fig. 4A): Class I (LR1-LR3 and LR6), containing a Pfam00427 domain; Class II (LRγ4-LRγ6 and LRγ8), possessing a conserved chromophore-binding domain (CBD); and Class III (LR9), featuring a FAS1 domain implicated in cell adhesion [35]. The LCM protein contained an αAPC-like domain followed by three tandem Pfam00427 domains, whereas LC proteins exhibited a simpler architecture with a single Pfam01383 domain (Fig. 4B). Similarly, the LRC proteins were categorized into three classes (Fig. 4C): Class I (LRC1), containing only a Pfam00427 domain; Class II (LRC2 and LRC3), combining a Pfam00427 domain and a coiled-coil motif; and Class III (LRC4-LRC6), exhibiting unique structural features, including extended α-helical regions in LRC4 and LRC5 and a FAS1 domain in LRC6.

Collinearity analysis of PBS linker genes

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of PBS linker genes, we performed interspecies collinearity analysis between Ny. yezoensis and four other red algae: No. haitanensis, Pa. umbilicalis, Gri. Pacifica, and Pi. Purpureum (Fig. 5). The analysis revealed 22 collinear gene pairs between Ny. yezoensis and No. haitanensis, indicating their close evolutionary relationship. In contrast, only four collinear pairs were identified between Ny. yezoensis and Pa. umbilicalis. Additionally, no collinear PBS linker gene pairs were detected between Ny. yezoensis and either Gri. pacifica or Pi. purpureum.

Cis-acting element analysis of PBS linker genes in Ny. yezoensis

To further elucidate the potential molecular functions and regulatory mechanisms of PBS linker proteins, we analyzed the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of all Ny. yezoensis PBS linker genes using the PlantCARE database to identify cis-acting regulatory elements. Four categories of cis-elements were identified: phytohormone-responsive, stress-responsive, light-responsive and development-related (Fig. 6). Within the phytohormone-responsive category, abscisic acid-responsive (ABRE) and MeJA-responsive (TGACG-motif and CGTCA-motif) were the most abundant. Among stress-responsive elements, the GC-motif (an enhancer-like element involved in anoxic-specific inducibility) predominated. Multiple light-responsive elements, such as G-Box and Sp1, were also detected. In contrast, the development-related category contained only one element, the CAT-box, which is associated with meristem expression. These results suggest that PBS linker proteins not only play a crucial role in PBS assembly but may also be involved in regulating plant growth, development, stress responses, light signaling, and other physiological processes.

Stage-specific expression patterns of PBS linker genes in Ny. yezoensis

Ny. yezoensis exhibits a heteromorphic life cycle, alternating between a macroscopic leafy gametophyte and a microscopic filamentous sporophyte. The expression patterns of PBS linker genes in these two heteromorphic life-history stages are presented in Fig. 7. A total of 14 PBS linker genes exhibited significant differential expression between the two life-history stages. Among these, ten genes showed higher expression in the sporophyte stage than in the gametophyte stage. Conversely, four genes showed higher expression in the gametophyte stage than in the sporophyte stage. Most strikingly, LRγ8 − 2 and LRγ8 − 3 were exclusively expressed in the sporophyte stage with no detectable expression in the gametophyte stage. Moreover, LRγ8 − 1 and LR1-3 displayed high expression in the sporophyte stage but minimal expression in the gametophyte stage. These results indicate that PBS linker genes play essential roles in both life stages, with their stage-specific expression patterns strongly suggesting crucial roles in light adaptation during generational transitions.

Comparison of phycobiliprotein composition between the two life cycle stages of Ny. yezoensis

Changes in linker proteins could modulate the phycobiliprotein composition within the phycobilisome. We compared the phycobiliprotein contents between conchocelis (sporophyte stage) and blades (gametophyte stage) by measuring the levels of phycoerythrin (PE), phycocyanin (PC), and allophycocyanin (APC). The results are shown in Fig. 8. The contents of PC and PE were higher in conchocelis, whereas APC was more abundant in blades (Fig. 8A). The ratios of these phycobiliproteins also differed between the two stages (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest that the distinct phycobilisome composition in conchocelis and blades represents an adaptive strategy to their different environmental conditions.

Expression profiles of PBS linker genes under nitrogen deficiency and resupply conditions in Ny. yezoensis

Phycobilisome function not only as light-harvesting antennae but also serve as nitrogen storage. To investigate PBS linker gene expression in response to changes in nitrogen availability, the gametophytic blades of Ny. yezoensis were subjected to nitrogen deficiency and resupply treatments. Overall, the expression of most PBS linker genes exhibited dynamic changes under these conditions (Fig. 9). The results showed that 18 PBS linker genes exhibited a consistent expression pattern: their expression levels decreased significantly under nitrogen deficiency and increased after nitrogen resupply with three nitrogen sources (nitrate, ammonium, and urea). Notably, these 18 genes represented all classes of PBS linker genes, except for two plastid-encoded genes. In summary, PBS linker gene expression levels correlated with nitrogen availability, suggesting that these linker proteins regulate phycobilisome assembly in response to nitrogen fluctuations.

Discussion

PBS linker proteins are ubiquitous in cyanobacteria and red algae, where they play crucial roles in phycobilisome assembly, energy transfer, and structural stability [10]. To date, research on PBS linker proteins has predominantly focused on cyanobacteria, whereas studies in red algae remain limited. This might be due to insufficient genomic data and a lack of structural insights into red algal phycobilisome. Recent advances in cryo-electron microscopy techniques have enabled the resolution of high-resolution phycobilisome structures from the red algae Gri. pacifica and Pi. purpureum, providing critical insights into phycobilisome architecture and the structural and functional roles of linker proteins [5, 6]. Furthermore, the chromosome-level genome assembly of Ny. yezoensis [16] serves as an essential genomic resource, facilitating the systematic identification and functional characterization of PBS linker proteins in this species.

Using two complementary approaches, we systematically identified PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis, and a total of 24 PBS linker proteins were successfully identified. Among these, the genes encoding LCM and LRC1 are plastid-encoded, while the remaining PBS linker genes are nuclear-encoded, consistent with previous reports in mesophilic red algae [14]. Notably, the number of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis is significantly greater than the numbers reported in Gri. pacifica and Pi. Purpureum [5, 6]. This difference is primarily due to an expansion of the LR group proteins. The number of proteins in the other groups remains comparable between Ny. yezoensis, Gri. pacifica, and Pi. purpureum. Intriguingly, we found that both LR6 and LRγ6 are present in Ny. yezoensis, whereas only one of these genes was detected in Gri. pacifica and Pi. purpureum. These findings demonstrate that the number of genes within the LR group has markedly increased in Ny. yezoensis. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PBS linker genes of the same class from different species are more closely related to each other than to genes of different classes from the same species. This strongly supports the evolutionary conservation of PBS linker genes, indicating that they share a common ancestral gene predating species divergence. Furthermore, PBS linker genes appear to have undergone extensive expansion and diversification during speciation, which may have facilitated their adaptation to diverse light environments.

PBS linker proteins play critical roles in photoprotection, photoadaptation, and complementary chromatic adaptation (CCA) in algae [1, 11, 36–40]. In this study, we examined the expression profiles of PBS linker genes in two life-history stages of Ny. yezoensis (Fig. 7). These stages inhabit significantly different environments and exhibit distinct morphological and physiological characteristics [17]. The results revealed differential expression of 14 PBS linker genes between the sporophyte and gametophyte stages, with a significantly greater number of genes highly expressed in the sporophyte (10 genes) than in the gametophyte (4 genes). Notably, LRγ8 − 2 and LRγ8 − 3 were exclusively expressed in the sporophyte, with no detectable expression in the gametophyte. Similarly, LRγ8 − 1 and LR1-3 exhibited high expression in the sporophyte but negligible expression in the gametophyte. These results indicate distinct phycobilisome content and composition between the two life-history stages of Ny. yezoensis. As the sporophyte grows within dead shells and inhabits subtidal zones with reduced light availability, the presumed higher phycobilisome content in the sporophyte may enhance its light-harvesting capacity, thereby adapting to its low-light environment. Furthermore, phycobiliprotein composition analysis revealed variations in phycobiliprotein content and ratios between the two stages (Fig. 8), further supporting differences in phycobilisome composition between the two stages. A previous study reported distinct composition and organization of the photosynthetic apparatus in the thylakoid membranes of sporophyte and gametophyte of Ny. yezoensis [41]. Collectively, these findings suggest that Ny. yezoensis regulates phycobilisome content and composition via linker proteins to adapt to the distinct environmental conditions associated with its two life-history stages.

Phycobilisomes function not only as light-harvesting antennae but also serve as nitrogen storage in cyanobacteria and red algae [11, 32, 42]. In this study, we further investigated the expression profiles of PBS linker genes under nitrogen deficiency and resupply conditions in Ny. yezoensis (Fig. 9). To ensure the reliability and robustness of our findings, three distinct nitrogen sources (nitrate, ammonium, and urea) were used for nitrogen resupply. Our results showed that almost all PBS linker genes were widely downregulated under nitrogen deficiency and subsequently upregulated upon nitrogen resupply. We propose that nitrogen deficiency triggers phycobilisome degradation, leading to reduced phycobilisome abundance and consequently downregulated expression of PBS linker genes. Conversely, nitrogen resupply increased phycobilisome abundance, resulting in the upregulation of these genes. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have demonstrated that phycobilisomes are degraded under nitrogen deficiency, while nitrogen resupply promotes phycobilisome reassembly and increased their abundance on thylakoid membranes in cyanobacteria and red algae [32, 42, 43]. These findings suggest that PBS linker proteins regulate phycobilisome assembly in response to nitrogen fluctuations.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted the first comprehensive analysis of PBS linker proteins in Ny. yezoensis. A total of 24 PBS linker proteins were identified and categorized into four groups (LR, LRC, LC, and LCM). Of these, two were plastid-encoded, while the others were nuclear-encoded. We analyzed their physicochemical properties, structural features, chromosomal localization, phylogenetic relationships, interspecies collinearity, and cis-acting elements, providing insights into the functional and evolutionary characteristics of PBS linker proteins. Notably, the number of genes within the LR group has markedly increased in Ny. yezoensis, which may have contributed to the species’ adaptation to diverse light environments. Expression analysis revealed stage-specific expression patterns of PBS linker genes between the two life-history stages of Ny. yezoensis, implicating their role in light adaptation during these distinct phases. Furthermore, most PBS linker genes were significantly downregulated under nitrogen deficiency and upregulated upon nitrogen resupply, indicating their involvement in regulating phycobilisome assembly in response to nitrogen fluctuations. Our findings provide valuable insights into the evolution and function of PBS linker proteins and advance our understanding of the adaptive strategies employed by Ny. yezoensis in complex and dynamic environments.
