Plasma albumin and other indicators as risk factors for delayed neurocognitive recovery in elderly patients undergoing orthopedic surgery
Authors: Guanxu Zhao, Huiqun Fu, Fangyan Liu, Mei Duan, Tianlong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe risk factors for neurocognitive impairment outcomes vary among different types of surgeries. The risk factors for delayed neurocognitive recovery (DNR) in elderly patients following orthopedic surgery are unclear. The objective of this study was to identify the risk factors for early postoperative delayed neurocognitive recovery in elderly patients who underwent elective orthopedic surgery.MethodsThe medical data of 166 elderly patients underwent elective orthopedic procedures were analyzed retrospectively. The cognitive scores on the day before the surgery and on the 7th postoperative day were assessed using the MoCA scale, and Z-scores were utilized to account for learning effects. The study population was divided into two groups according to whether DNR occurred by the 7th postoperative day or prior to discharge. Risk factors associated with postoperative DNR in elderly orthopedic patients were determined using logistic regression analysis.ResultsThere were 166 elderly surgical patients, with an incidence rate of 28.9% DNR. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that a history of drinking (OR = 6.853 [1.825–25.734], P = 0.004), cerebrovascular stenosis (mild: OR = 7.646 [1.432–40.833], P = 0.017; moderate or severe: OR = 5.593 [1.222–25.607], P = 0.027), a preoperative PaO2/FiO2 ratio of < 350 (OR = 2.549 [1.045–6.219], P = 0.040), and postoperative albumin levels of < 35 g/L (OR = 4.214 [1.489–11.923], P = 0.007) were independently associated with DNR on postoperative day 7. Furthermore, plasma albumin levels on postoperative day 1 showed a negative correlation with plasma interleukin 6 levels on postoperative day 3 (r = − 0.251, P = 0.006).ConclusionEarly DNR in elderly orthopedic patients appears to be associated with multiple factors, including a history of drinking, cerebrovascular stenosis, a preoperative PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio < 350, and postoperative albumin < 35 g/L.Keywords: Delayed neurocognitive recovery, Orthopedic surgery, Risk factors, Elderly patients, Plasma albumin

Introduction

Cognitive impairment occurring within the first month after surgery is defined as delayed neurocognitive recovery (DNR), which is characterized by objective deficits in attention, memory, and information processing abilities [1]. DNR can occur in individuals of any age [2], with 30%–75% of patients exhibiting DNR at the time of hospital discharge [3–5]. Older patients are at a higher risk of DNR due to age-related degenerative changes in brain function and structure [6]. Studies have demonstrated that individuals aged 60 years and above have a significantly higher incidence of DNR than younger populations [2]. DNR is associated with poor postoperative functional recovery, prolonged hospital stays, increased complications, and higher mortality rates, imposing a substantial burden on patients, families, and society [7]. Therefore, while no definitive personalised interventions have yet been proven to reduce the incidence of DNR, increased awareness and targeted monitoring of high-risk individuals during the perioperative period may still be beneficial.

Previous studies have shown that the preoperative risk factors for DNR include advanced age, a low education level, and the burden of comorbidities [8]. Intraoperative risk factors for DNR may be related to surgery-induced disturbances in the internal environment [8]. These disturbances activate innate immune cells, triggering a systemic inflammatory response that promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines and subsequent neuroinflammation, leading to nerve damage and cognitive decline [9, 10]. Additionally, the incidence of DNR varies significantly by the type of surgery. For example, the incidence of DNR in cardiac surgery has been reported to be as high as 60%, compared to 41.4% in non-cardiac surgery [2, 11]. Furthermore, the incidence of DNR among cardiac procedures was found to be the highest after open aortic surgery, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, and coronary artery bypass grafting [5]. These studies suggest that different types of surgery may cause varying degrees of disruption to the body’s internal environment, and thus varying rates of DNR. Although multiple risk factors for DNR have been reported in various studies [8, 12], the underlying risk factors for the development of DNR associated with major orthopedic surgery, such as joint replacement or lumbar interbody fusion, remain poorly understood. These surgeries pose significant risks to patients, highlighting the need for early recognition of high-risk individuals and the use of targeted mitigation strategies to improve patient outcomes.

As the prevalence of age-related degenerative changes in the joints and lumbar spine increase, a growing number of elderly patients are undergoing major orthopedic surgery [13]. Studies have reported that the incidence of DNR in the early postoperative period in elderly patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery can reach 25%–50% [14–16]. However, there is limited research on the risk factors for DNR in such patients. This study aimed to examine the factors contributing to early postoperative DNR in elderly individuals aged 65 years or above who underwent major orthopedic procedures. The identification of these potential risk factors may help raise clinical awareness of individuals at higher risk of DNR and serve as a foundation for future studies exploring targeted perioperative strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted retrospectively. The clinical data of elderly patients aged 65 years and older who underwent major orthopedic surgery at Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University from September 2018 to September 2020 were analyzed retrospectively. Data utilized in this study did not include identifiable patient information and were obtained by our team during prior research [17] with approval from the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University (ID: 2018[048]). As this was a retrospective study, informed consent was deemed unnecessary. Inclusion criteria were: (1) joint replacement or lumbar interbody surgery, (2) elective surgery. Exclusion criteria included: (1) second operation during same hospitalization, (2) patient experienced postoperative infections or other unexpected complications leading to prolonged hospital stays, (3) incomplete preoperative or postoperative cognitive scale scores, and (4) missing key clinical data (such as preoperative PaO2, blood loss, postoperative blood tests, etc.).

Definition of delayed neurocognitive recovery

Cognitive tests were performed before surgery and 7 days postoperatively or before hospital discharge if the hospital stay was shorter than 7 days. The cognitive score was obtained from the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment Basic (MoCA-B). The MoCA test was used to assess the level of cognitive decline [16]. Multiple studies have shown that frequent neuropsychological assessments within short intervals may lead to learning effects [2, 18]. To address possible learning effects, we introduced the concept of Z-scores to assess the presence of DNR following surgery. Based on our team’s previous study [16], the average difference between MoCA scores of age-matched healthy community volunteers on day 1 and day 8 was used to quantify the learning effect and calculate the standard deviation of day 1 scores. For the patients in this study, we determined the difference between their postoperative and preoperative MoCA scores, subtracted the learning effect, and then divided the result by the standard deviation of the healthy volunteers’ day 1 scores to obtain a Z-score. If the Z-score was ≥ 1.96, the patient was classified as having DNR.

Data collection

Demographic data were collected from the electronic medical record system, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), years of education, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, smoking history, drinking history, and major comorbidities. Cerebrovascular stenosis was categorized as mild stenosis (≤ 50%) or moderate-to-severe stenosis (> 50%).

Based on clinical experience and previous research [19, 20], we also collected clinical data, including preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), and inflammatory cytokine levels; preoperative lactic acid (Lac) levels; preoperative PaO2/FiO2 ratio (P/F, abnormal if P/F < 350); type of anesthesia and surgery; intraoperative hypotension (defined as MAP < 65 mmHg) [21]; intraoperative Lac increase (> 1.6 mmol/L); operation time; anesthesia time; intraoperative fluid infusion (including blood products); blood transfusion volume; blood loss; urine output; low postoperative albumin levels (< 35 g/L); and postoperative hospital stay. If not indicated, postoperative blood tests were applied to samples taken on the morning of the first day after surgery. The postoperative albumin level specifically refers to the plasma albumin measured on the morning of postoperative day 1. In healthy adults, the P/F should be greater than 400; considering that the target population of this study was elderly people over 65 years old, a P/F of < 350 was considered abnormal [22, 23]. Blood Lac levels of > 1.6 mmol/L and blood albumin levels of < 35 g/L were considered abnormal based on the reference ranges provided by the hospital laboratory department. All patients included in this study had normal preoperative laboratory results.

Statistical method

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 29.0. A P-value below 0.05 was regarded as indicating statistical significance. Continuous variables are represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using a T-test for normal distributions or are represented by median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using a Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages and were analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed for factors that were statistically significant between the two groups. Factors with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis (use the way of Forward: LR) to identify independent risk factors for early postoperative DNR. The results of univariate and multivariate analyses are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). The Hosmer–Lemeshow Test was used to evaluate the model fit. The plasma inflammatory cytokine concentrations were log transformed and analyzed using ANOVA. Correlations were analyzed by Pearson’s analysis (normal distribution) or Spearman’s analysis (non-normal distribution).

Results

Subject characteristics

Among the 170 patients initially included in this study, 4 were excluded: 1 with postoperative incision infection and 3 due to incomplete data. Finally, 166 patients were included (Fig. 1): 52 males (31.3%) and 114 females (68.7%). On postoperative day 7, DNR was observed in 48 of 166 patients (28.9%). Patients were divided into DNR and non-DNR groups based on the presence or absence of DNR on the 7th postoperative day.

The average age of all participants was 70.22 ± 4.40 years, with an age range of 65 to 79 years. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of demographic or clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, educational background, ASA grade, NYHA class, or comorbidities such as hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke, and hyperlipidemia. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified the following as potential risk factors for DNR on postoperative day 7: smoking history (OR = 5.890 [2.179–15.919], P < 0.001), drinking history (OR = 6.719 [2.191–20.601], P < 0.001), diabetes (OR = 2.090 [1.038–4.209], P = 0.039), cerebrovascular stenosis (mild OR = 11.879 [2.354–59.947], P = 0.003; moderate and severe (OR = 6.788 [1.923–23.965], P = 0.003), preoperative P/F of < 350 (OR = 2.971 [1.348–6.546], P = 0.007), and postoperative albumin levels of < 35 g/L (OR = 3.194 [1.373–7.432], P = 0.007) (Table 4).

When included in the stepwise multivariable logistic regression model, these factors remained independently associated with DNR on postoperative day 7: drinking history (OR = 6.853 [1.825–25.734], P = 0.004), cerebrovascular stenosis (mild OR = 7.646 [1.432–40.833], P = 0.017; moderate and severe OR = 5.593 [1.222–25.607], P = 0.027), preoperative P/F of < 350 (OR = 2.549 [1.045–6.219], P = 0.040), and postoperative albumin levels of < 35 g/L (OR = 4.214 [1.489–11.923], P = 0.007) (Table 4). Correlation between postoperative plasma albumin levels and inflammatory cytokine concentrations

Recent studies have shown that albumin plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory cytokines such as C-reactive protein, interleukin 6 (IL-6), and interleukin 10 [24, 25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammatory cytokine levels increase in patients following surgery [26]. Consequently, we conducted a comparison of plasma inflammatory cytokine levels before surgery and on postoperative days 1 and 3 (Table 5). Additionally, we evaluated the correlation between plasma albumin levels on postoperative day 1 and inflammatory cytokines levels on postoperative days 1 and 3. There was a negative correlation between albumin levels on postoperative day 1 and plasma IL-6 levels on postoperative day 3 (r = − 0.251, P = 0.006) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, the incidence of DNR among elderly patients undergoing orthopedic surgery was 28.9%. Preoperative factors associated with early DNR in this population included a history of drinking, cerebrovascular stenosis, and a preoperative P/F ratio < 350, while a postoperative albumin level < 35 g/L was also associated with an increased risk. Moreover, there was a negative correlation between albumin levels on postoperative day 1 and plasma IL-6 levels on postoperative day 3.

Different stress responses are triggered by different types of orthopedic surgeries [26]. Intense stress responses to trauma and invasive surgery can cause cognitive impairment [26], a result of systemic inflammation [27], and excessively elevated cortisol, which is attributed to ongoing central activation by the inflammatory reactions [28, 29]. Among the commonly used orthopedic procedures, joint replacement and lumbar interbody fusion are the most traumatic, with DNR incidence rates reaching as high as 43% and 50%, respectively [14, 16]. These conditions pose significant risks to patient safety, hinder postoperative recovery, and adversely affect patients’ long-term quality of life.

Smoking impairs working memory, reasoning abilities, and problem-solving performance, with the severity of these deficits increasing proportionally with daily smoking levels [30]. It was observed a statistical difference in smoking history between the DNR and non-DNR groups, which shows consistency with previous studies. However, further analysis indicated that a history of smoking did not independently contribute as a risk factor for DNR. This discrepancy may be attributed to confounding factors influencing the observed association between smoking and DNR in the univariate analysis. Smoking, a known risk factor for cerebrovascular stenosis, often co-occurs with other adverse habits like alcohol consumption [31]. Hence, its association may have been attenuated after adjusting for confounders due to these complex correlations. Recently, a clinical study demonstrated that a connection was found between past smoking and postoperative delirium (POD) in ICU patients, whereas current smoking showed no such association [32]. Smoking could result in alterations in brain structure, possibly increasing susceptibility to POD. However, the study conducted by Runjia Wang et al., indicates that nicotine might lower the risk of postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) in elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery through activation of the cholinergic pathway [33]. Due to the intricate neuropharmacological effects of nicotine, further investigation into its influence on postoperative cognitive function are needed in the future.

Alcohol induces neuroinflammation and brain damage by activating Toll-like receptor 4 – an immune-response protein that facilitates the production of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor – in glial cells [34, 35], aligning with the current neuroinflammation hypothesis for perioperative neurocognitive dysfunction [36]. Earlier research has indicated a connection between alcohol consumption and cognitive decline following surgery in older individuals, particularly affecting their visual, language, memory, and executive functions [37, 38]. Consistent with previous findings, drinking was associated with an increased risk of DNR in our cohort, with patients reporting alcohol consumption showing 6.853-fold higher odds compared with non-drinkers.

Individuals with diabetes face a heightened likelihood of experiencing cognitive impairment following surgery [39], and multiple earlier studies have identified a notable correlation between diabetes and DNR [40, 41]. An increasing number of observational studies are suggesting that microvascular complications linked to diabetes are related to an elevated risk of cognitive deficits [42]. Microvascular dysfunction caused by diabetes might result in heightened blood–brain-barrier permeability, enhanced inflammatory and immune reactions, decreased cerebral perfusion, and impaired neurovascular coupling [43–45]. These effects could directly compromise neuronal function and lead to cognitive decline. In this study, univariate analysis indicated that diabetes might contribute to the incidence of DNR, consistent with prior research findings, consistent with previous studies; however, the multivariate analysis did not find diabetes to be an independent risk factor for DNR. This may be attributed to the short postoperative assessment period for cognitive function. In a study of DNR risk factors in thoracic surgery patients after surgery, univariate analysis conducted on postoperative day 4 showed that diabetes was not a risk factor for DNR, whereas multivariate analysis conducted on postoperative day 30 showed that diabetes was an independent risk factor for DNR [19]. This was also confirmed in another study that found diabetes was linked to POD within 4 postoperative days and POCD 10 or more days postoperatively [46]. Diabetes was not an independent risk factor in our study may also be related to the unclear degree of microvascular dysfunction in diabetic patients undergoing surgery. Therefore, studying the relationship between the pathological changes of diabetes and brain function will help us to explore the diabetes-related risk mechanisms for DNR in surgical patients. For diabetic patients, active perioperative glycemic control may help improve microvascular function, thereby reducing the risk of DNR.

In this study, preoperative cerebrovascular stenosis was also associated with a higher risk of early postoperative DNR. Both mild and moderate-to-severe stenosis increased the risk of early postoperative DNR in patients, which aligns with findings from previous studies [47, 48]. Specifically, mild stenosis was associated with a 7.646-fold increase in odds, whereas moderate-to-severe stenosis was associated with a 5.593-fold increase in odds. The brain is an organ with one of the highest blood perfusion levels in the body and the only one whose functions depend heavily on the ability of the blood flow to regulate itself [49]. Increasing data from studies suggest that cerebrovascular stenosis damages the autoregulation of the cerebral blood flow, making the brain more prone to hypoxia and cerebral hypoperfusion during surgery, which may cause postoperative cognitive function changes [50, 51]. Often, patients with preoperative cerebrovascular stenosis have other risk factors, such as diabetes and alcohol use, and the cause of postoperative cognitive impairment in such patients may be multifactorial. Therefore, preoperative evaluation and preparation of these patients are crucial, with a particular emphasis on perioperative brain function monitoring. Of note, the results of the subgroup analysis for cerebrovascular stenosis, characterized by wide confidence intervals, should be interpreted with caution due to limited precision. This is likely attributable to the relatively small sample size of patients with cerebrovascular stenosis (DNR 15 vs. Non-DNR 6).

Pulmonary function is associated with cognitive decline [52]. Impaired pulmonary function contributes to cognitive decline by accelerating brain atrophy and microvascular damage [53]. Studies have shown that a preoperative 6-minute walking distance (a measure of preoperative cardiopulmonary function) is associated with POCD [54]. As the P/F ratio is a relatively accurate and independent indicator of lung function [54], we used it as an objective indicator of preoperative lung function and explored its relationship to DNR. A normal P/F should be greater than 400, but considering that the patients in this study were elderly, we defined 350 as the threshold for abnormal pulmonary function [22]. After multivariate regression analysis, a preoperative P/F ratio < 350 was associated with an increased risk of DNR on postoperative day 7, consistent with prior observations. In other words, patients with a P/F of < 350 had 2.549 times higher odds of developing DNR postoperatively compared with those with P/F of ≥ 350. We concluded that preoperative lung function preparation and intraoperative lung function protection could potentially enhance the postoperative outcomes of these patients.

Preoperative hypoalbuminemia is associated with complications after major surgery [55, 56]. In our clinical work, we have observed that a significant proportion of patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery display plasma albumin levels of < 35 g/L within the first 24 postoperative hours; therefore, we included this indicator in our analysis. The results showed that postoperative albumin level < 35 g/L within 24 h was associated with a higher risk of DNR on postoperative day 7, with a 4.214-fold increase in odds compared with patients with normal albumin levels. Further analysis showed that albumin levels on day 1 after surgery were negatively correlated with plasma IL-6 levels on day 3 after surgery. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. A potential explanation for this association may relate to the immunomodulatory and antioxidative properties of albumin. Previous studies have shown that surgically induced trauma or cell damage causes the release of endogenous factors that activate the innate immune system and produce inflammatory mediators, including key mediators such as cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) [9, 57, 58], leading to systemic inflammatory responses and oxidative stress. Studies have shown that, among the various inflammatory factors that increase in concentration due to stress, IL-6 has been reported to increase early, show a marked response, and remain elevated for a prolonged period [59]. It has been associated with systemic inflammatory responses and may be involved in neuroinflammation, possibly through pathways such as NF-κB signaling, which has been linked to cognitive dysfunction [9, 60]. Albumin is a multidomain protein characterized by the presence of 17 disulfide bonds and a free mercaptan at Cys-34 [61]. It has been suggested to possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, with evidence indicating that albumin may help mitigate oxidative stress by binding free radicals such as ROS and RNS [24], and may also modulate inflammatory responses, potentially through pathways involving intracellular glutathione and NF-κB regulation [62]. These mechanisms remain speculative in the context of the present study and require further investigation. In this study, however, postoperative plasma IL-6 levels showed no significant variation between the DNR group and the non-DNR group. It has been confirmed that IL-6 exerts a pro-inflammatory effect by binding to the soluble form of the IL-6 receptor and glycoprotein 130 (gp130), and IL-6 also produces an anti-inflammatory effect via membrane-bound IL-6 receptor and gp130 binding [63]. The complex role of IL-6 in the inflammatory process may partly explain the observed discrepancy in this study, where no significant difference in IL-6 levels was found between the two groups, despite differences in cognitive scores. Therefore, our findings do not provide direct evidence supporting an association between IL-6 and the occurrence of DNR, further analyses of the intensity of the anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory effects of plasma IL-6 are required to determine the association between postoperative IL-6 elevation and the occurrence of DNR. In brief, our findings suggest that early postoperative monitoring of albumin levels may be associated with systemic inflammatory status and cognitive outcomes. While albumin is weakly correlated with IL-6, which calls for caution in the interpretation of its clinical significance, and causality cannot be established, these observations warrant further investigation to clarify its clinical utility as a potentially modifiable risk factor. Future studies exploring the specific signaling pathways of IL-6 may help clarify its potential role in postoperative cognitive function.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective, single-center study may be subject to selection bias and limited generalizability. The small sample size (EPV < 10) and use of stepwise regression increase the risk of overfitting and model instability. Moderate class imbalance could have influenced model performance, and no resampling or class-weighting methods were applied, as the primary aim was to identify clinically interpretable predictors. In addition, the retrospective design precludes causal inference and is susceptible to unmeasured confounding.

Second, as this study focused on DNR at postoperative day 7, the findings are limited to early postoperative outcomes and may not reflect longer-term cognitive outcomes. Third, although the MoCA-B was used, the absence of comprehensive neuropsychological assessment may have limited the detection of subtle cognitive changes. Finally, due to limitations of the electronic medical record system, postoperative day 1 VAS scores were only available for a subset of patients. Although pain levels were generally low and comparable between groups in this subgroup, the potential confounding effect of postoperative pain cannot be fully excluded.

Future well-designed prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and more robust modeling strategies are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In general, early DNR in elderly patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery is likely influenced by multiple factors. In this study, drinking, cerebrovascular stenosis, a preoperative P/F ratio < 350, and postoperative albumin levels < 35 g/L were associated with an increased risk of early DNR. These findings provide preliminary insights into potential risk profiles in this population. However, given the observational nature of the study and the possibility of residual confounding, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Clinically, patients presenting with these characteristics may warrant closer monitoring and perioperative management.
