Limited incremental response to routine phytase supplementation in Hy-Line Brown laying hens fed mineral-adequate diets
Authors: Hatem M. Eltahan, Nasser S.S. Ben-Qasem, Gehad Abdeelgayed, In Ho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated whether routine moderate phytase supplementation in adequate-phosphorus diets provides additional measurable benefits in 40-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens, based on laying performance, egg quality, nutrient digestibility, excreta traits, gas emission, and apparent ileal amino acid (AA) digestibility. A total of 180 hens were assigned to three dietary treatments for 8 weeks: (TRT1) a basal diet, (TRT2) the basal diet supplemented with 500 FTU/kg phytase, and (TRT3) the basal diet supplemented with 1000 FTU/kg phytase. Each treatment included five replicates with 12 hens per replicate. Body weight and laying performance were recorded throughout the experimental period, while egg quality was assessed at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8. Nutrient digestibility, fecal score, excreta gas emission, volatile fatty acids (VFAs), and apparent ileal AA digestibility were also evaluated. Repeatedly measured variables were analyzed using a mixed model including treatment, time, and treatment × time. Phytase supplementation produced no significant overall treatment effect on body weight or laying performance (P>0.05). Body weight and egg production changed significantly over time (P = 0.008 and 0.004, respectively), whereas no significant treatment × time interactions were detected. Egg quality traits were not affected by treatment, time, or their interaction (P>0.05). Nutrient digestibility and apparent ileal digestibility of essential, nonessential, and total AAs also showed no significant treatment effects. Similarly, no treatment effects were detected for fecal score, ammonia or hydrogen sulfide emissions, or VFA concentrations; however, fecal score and butyric, valeric, and isovaleric acids changed significantly over time. In conclusion, supplementation with 500 or 1000 FTU/kg phytase did not produce statistically detectable incremental improvements in productive, nutritional, or excreta-related responses under the adequate-calcium and phosphorus conditions tested. These findings are specific to the phytase doses and nutritional conditions evaluated.

Introduction

Phosphorus is an essential mineral in laying-hen nutrition because it contributes to skeletal integrity, eggshell formation, energy metabolism, cellular signaling, and sustained egg production. In corn–soybean meal-based diets, however, a considerable proportion of phosphorus is present as phytate phosphorus, which poultry cannot efficiently utilize because of limited endogenous phytase activity. Therefore, inorganic phosphorus sources are commonly included in layer diets to meet nutritional requirements, although excessive safety margins may increase feed cost and phosphorus excretion. Exogenous phytase is widely used to hydrolyze phytate, release available phosphorus, improve calcium and phosphorus utilization, and reduce phosphorus losses in manure (Um and Paik, 1999; Jalal and Scheideler, 2001; Selle and Ravindran, 2007; Dersjant-Li et al., 2015).

The nutritional context of the diet strongly influences the response of laying hens to phytase. Clearer productive, mineral, and eggshell responses are generally reported when phytase is supplemented to diets with reduced phosphorus, available phosphorus, or calcium, where phytate hydrolysis can compensate for limited mineral supply (Jalal and Scheideler, 2001; Lim et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2007; Bello and Korver, 2019; Pongmanee and Korver, 2020). Moreover, in aging or nutritionally challenged hens, phytase may also support egg production, eggshell traits, calcium and phosphorus digestibility, and bone-related responses (Bello and Korver, 2019, 2020; Eltahan et al., 2023; Hervo et al., 2023). These findings support the role of phytase, but they also indicate that its benefits are conditional rather than universal.

In commercial laying-hen production, phytase may be included routinely even when the basal diet already meets calcium, total phosphorus, and available phosphorus requirements. Under such adequate-phosphorus conditions, the additional phosphorus released by phytase may maintain normal performance without producing measurable improvements. This distinction is important because routine enzyme supplementation should be justified by detectable productive, nutritional, or environmental benefits, not only by its theoretical capacity to hydrolyze phytate. Recent studies have emphasized that phytase responses depend on dietary phosphorus level, calcium availability, limestone particle size, calcium solubility, phytase dose, age, and digestive tract dynamics (Dersjant-Li et al., 2015; Hervo et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024; Lima et al., 2024). Furthermore, Salehi et al. (2025) reported that phytase efficacy in laying hens appears to depend not only on enzyme inclusion level but also on calcium particle size, mineral balance, and feeding strategy. Therefore, evaluating phytase under nutritionally adequate conditions is necessary to define its practical value and inform cost-effective supplementation strategies.

Beyond phosphorus release, phytase may influence nutrient utilization through the degradation of phytate, which can interact with minerals, proteins, amino acids, and digestive enzymes. These interactions may reduce protein solubility, impair amino acid availability, and affect energy and mineral utilization. However, amino acid and nutrient digestibility responses to phytase are inconsistent and appear to depend on diet composition, phytate concentration, calcium and phosphorus levels, ingredient source, and the degree of nutritional challenge (Cowieson et al., 2006; Selle and Ravindran, 2007; Dersjant-Li et al., 2015). Snow et al. (2003) reported limited effects of phytase on amino acid digestibility in molted laying hens, whereas Liu et al. (2007) observed improved amino acid digestibility when phytase was added to a negative-control diet. These contrasting findings suggest that phytase-related amino acid responses should be interpreted according to dietary adequacy rather than assumed under all feeding conditions.

Environmental outcomes are also relevant when evaluating phytase in laying-hen diets. Improved nutrient utilization may reduce nutrient excretion and contribute to lower environmental loading; however, excreta gas emissions such as ammonia and hydrogen sulfide are influenced mainly by nitrogen metabolism, uric acid degradation, manure moisture, microbial activity, sulfur-containing compounds, and fermentation of undigested nutrients (Ritz et al., 2004; Rothrock et al., 2010; Sharma et al., 2017; Heo et al., 2023). Therefore, phytase may not markedly reduce ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, or volatile fatty acid (VFA) production unless it also changes nutrient excretion or microbial fermentation. Park et al. (2024) reported that dietary phosphorus levels and phytase influenced certain odour-related responses in pullets and laying hens, suggesting that environmental responses to phytase may also depend on dietary phosphorus manipulation and physiological stage.

Accordingly, evaluating phytase only through laying performance or egg quality may not fully describe its practical value. A broader assessment including nutrient digestibility, apparent ileal amino acid digestibility, fecal score, ammonia and hydrogen sulfide emissions, and VFAs profile can provide a clearer interpretation of whether routine phytase supplementation offers measurable benefits under adequate-phosphorus conditions. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether routine moderate phytase supplementation at 500 and 1000 FTU/kg provides additional productive, nutritional, digestive, and environmental benefits in 40-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens fed nutritionally adequate diets.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Dankook University Animal Care Committee, Cheonan, Republic of Korea, under approval number DK-1-2521.

Birds, experimental design, and management

A total of 180 Hy-Line Brown laying hens in 40-week-old with an average initial body weight of approximately 2.0 kg were used in an 8-week feeding trial. Birds were randomly assigned to three dietary treatments, with five independent experimental replicates per treatment. Each experimental replicate comprised four adjacent cages, with three hens per cage, resulting in 12 hens per replicate. The dietary treatments were: TRT1, basal diet without phytase supplementation; TRT2, basal diet with phytase supplemented at 500 FTU/kg diet; and TRT3, basal diet supplemented with phytase at 1000 FTU/kg diet. Feed and water were ad libitum afforded during the study. Hens were managed under standard commercial laying-hen conditions, and birds were checked daily for general health and welfare. The hens were housed in double-sided battery cages measuring 45 × 35 × 37 cm (length × width × height), with three hens per cage, providing approximately 525 cm² of floor space per hen. Four cages assigned to the same dietary treatment were considered one experimental replicate, resulting in 12 hens per replicate. A commercial phytase Quantum Blue® 5 G (AB Vista; 5,000 FTU/g phytase activity), supplied by Daehan Feed Co., Ltd. (Republic of Korea), was incorporated into the diets at 10 and 20 g/100 kg diet to provide the intended supplementation levels of 500 and 1,000 FTU/kg diet, respectively. The phytase concentrations represent the formulated supplementation levels, as post-mixing phytase activity was not analytically determined. The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional recommendations of the Hy-Line International (2025) management guide. Laying performance

Laying performance measurements were based on five experimental replicates per treatment (n = 5). Body weight was recorded at the beginning and end of the trial. Egg production and downgraded eggs were recorded daily by experimental replicate throughout the 8 weeks. Feed intake was measured weekly by subtracting feed refusals from the amount of feed offered. Egg quality measurements

Egg quality was evaluated at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8. At each sampling time, six eggs were collected from each of five experimental replicates per treatment (30 eggs per treatment) after excluding cracked, broken, or abnormal eggs. Individual eggs were treated as subsamples within experimental replicate (n = 5). The measured egg quality traits included eggshell color, yolk color, Haugh unit, eggshell strength, and eggshell thickness. Eggshell strength was expressed as kg/cm², and eggshell thickness was expressed as mm. Eggshell thickness was measured at three shell regions: the blunt end, equator, and sharp end, and the mean value was used as the final eggshell thickness. Eggshell color, yolk color, Haugh unit, eggshell strength, and eggshell thickness were measured using a fully DET 6000 automatic egg tester (Kyoto, Japan).

Apparent nutrient digestibility and fecal score

Apparent nutrient digestibility was determined at week 8 using four independent experimental replicates per treatment (n = 4). Excreta collected within each experimental replicate were pooled to obtain one composite sample representing that experimental unit. The evaluated nutrients included dry matter, nitrogen, energy, crude fat, calcium, and phosphorus. Feed and excreta samples were dried in a forced-air oven at 70 °C for 72 h, ground to a fine powder, and passed through a 1-mm sieve before chemical analysis. Dry matter and nitrogen were analyzed according to AOAC methods 930.15 and 987.13, respectively (AOAC, 2007), while gross energy was determined using a Parr 6100 bomb calorimeter (Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA). Calcium and phosphorus digestibility were evaluated according to Huang and Schulte (1985). Chromium was measured by UV-visible spectrophotometry (Shimadzu UV-1201, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and apparent total tract digestibility was measured using the marker-ratio equation reported by Khan et al. (2024), Digestibility (%) = 100 − [(% marker in feed / % marker in excreta) × (% nutrient in excreta / % nutrient in feed) × 100].

Fecal score was evaluated using five-experimental replicates per treatment (n = 5) at the initial period, week 4, and week 8. Fresh excreta were visually scored at the replicate level using a 1–5 scale, where 1 represented normal feces and 5 represented liquid diarrhea. The mean fecal score of each replicate was used for statistical analysis.

Excreta gas emission and volatile fatty acids

Excreta gas emissions and VFAs concentrations were measured at the initial period, week 4, and week 8 as written elsewhere in Ali et al. (2025). Fresh excreta samples were collected from four independent experimental replicates per treatment (n = 4). Samples collected within an experimental replicate were combined to form one representative sample, which constituted one experimental observation for statistical analysis. Samples were placed in 2.6-L plastic sealed containers for 48 h fermentation under controlled conditions. Ammonia and hydrogen sulfide emissions were measured from the container headspace and expressed as ppm using a GV-100 gas sampling pump (Gastec Corp., Kanagawa, Japan). VFA concentrations were determined according to Kim et al. (2018) with slight modification. Fresh excreta samples were collected while avoiding cecal droppings and uric acid residues and immediately transferred into sterile tubes at two-week intervals and processed on the same day. Approximately 1 g of excreta was homogenized with 9 mL chilled distilled water using an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer (Digital Ultra-Turrax T25, IKA, Staufen, Germany). The homogenate was mixed with 0.05 mL saturated Hg Cl₂, 1 mL 25% H₃PO₄, and 0.2 mL 2% pivalic acid, then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully transferred to sterile tubes and stored at −20 °C until VFA determination by gas chromatography using a 6890 Series GC System (HP, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The VFAs measured included acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, and isovaleric acid, and values were expressed as mmol/L.

Apparent ileal AA digestibility

For apparent ileal amino acid digestibility, one hen was randomly selected from each of four different experimental replicates per treatment, resulting in four independent observations per treatment (n = 4). Ileal digesta collected from each sampled hen represented one independent replicate observation and were processed separately for analysis. The hens were euthanized by isoflurane (Mylan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) according to the approved animal care committee for ileal digesta collection. The ileal digesta were collected from the distal ileum, defined as the section between Meckel’s diverticulum and approximately 2 cm anterior to the ileo-cecal junction. Digesta samples were pooled by replicate, immediately frozen at −80 °C, freeze-dried, ground, and stored until AA analysis. Amino acid concentrations in diets and ileal digesta were determined by HPLC following appropriate sample preparation. Most amino acids were determined after acid hydrolysis, whereas sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine and cystine) were subjected to performic acid oxidation before acid hydrolysis. Tryptophan was determined separately following alkaline hydrolysis prior to HPLC analysis. Essential AAs included threonine, valine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, histidine, lysine, arginine, methionine, and tryptophan. Non-essential AAs included aspartic acid, serine, glutamic acid, proline, glycine, alanine, tyrosine, and cystine. Total essential AAs, total non-essential AAs, and total AAs were then calculated. Chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃) was included in the experimental diets at 0.2% as an indigestible marker during the digestibility collection period. Chromium concentrations in the diets and ileal digesta were determined by UV-visible spectrophotometry (UV-1201, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Apparent ileal AA digestibility was calculated using the marker-ratio method as follows: AID (%) = 100 − [100 × (Cr₂O₃ diet / Cr₂O₃ digesta) × (AA digesta / AA diet)].

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2013, Cary, NC, USA), with the experimental replicate considered the experimental unit. Body weight, laying performance, egg quality, and fecal score were based on five experimental replicates per treatment (n = 5); for egg quality, six eggs per cage were collected at each sampling time and averaged to obtain one cage observation. Repeatedly measured variables were analyzed using a mixed model including treatment, time, and treatment × time as fixed effects, with experimental replicate as the repeated subject. Excreta gas emissions and volatile fatty acids were analyzed from four replicate units per treatment (n = 4). Apparent nutrient digestibility and ileal AA digestibility were based on four independent observations per treatment (n = 4). One-way ANOVA was used for apparent nutrient digestibility and ileal AA digestibility. Results are presented as means ± SEM, with significance declared at P < 0.05.

Results

Growth performance

Dietary phytase supplementation did not significantly affect body weight or laying performance over the 8-week experimental period (Table 2). No overall treatment effects were detected for body weight, downgraded egg percentage, egg production, feed conversion ratio (FCR), average daily feed intake (ADFI), egg weight, or egg mass (P > 0.05). Initial body weight ranged from 1998 to 2001 g across treatments and remained similar at week 8 (2003–2004 g). A significant effect of time was observed for body weight (P = 0.008) and egg production (P = 0.004), whereas egg mass showed a tendency to change over time (P = 0.085). In contrast, time did not significantly affect downgraded egg percentage, FCR, or ADFI (P > 0.05). No significant treatment × time interactions were detected for any of the evaluated performance variables (P > 0.05), indicating that the temporal pattern of response was comparable among dietary treatments. Discussion

The present study evaluated the incremental response to moderate phytase supplementation in 40-week-old Hy-Line Brown hens fed a diet that already supplied adequate mineral concentrations (approximately 4.08% Ca, 0.68% total P, and 0.45% available P). Overall, supplementation with 500 or 1000 FTU/kg phytase did not produce statistically detectable improvements in laying performance, egg quality, nutrient digestibility, apparent ileal amino acid digestibility, excreta characteristics, gas emissions, or VFA concentrations. These findings suggested that the opportunity for a phytase additional response was limited under the mineral-adequate conditions used in the present experiment.

Productive performance and egg quality

The absence of a detectable treatment effect on egg production, feed intake, FCR, egg mass, body weight, or egg-quality traits is consistent with the strong dependence of phytase efficacy on dietary mineral status. Phytase responses are generally more pronounced when available or non-phytate phosphorus, calcium, or both are reduced, because phytate hydrolysis then contributes directly to correcting a nutritional limitation (Jalal and Scheideler, 2001; Lim et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2007; Bello and Korver, 2019; Pongmanee and Korver, 2020; Lim et al., 2020). In contrast, when mineral requirements are already adequately supplied, further release of phytate-bound phosphorus may provide little additional benefit for egg production or shell formation (Javadi et al., 2021; Saleh et al., 2021; Hervo et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024).

The limited response to phytase observed in the present study can be interpreted within the combined context of dietary calcium and phosphorus supply, limestone characteristics, phytase dose, and physiological stage. The basal diet already provided approximately 4.08% Ca, 0.68% total P, and 0.45% available P, thereby reducing the hens’ dependence on phytase-mediated release of phytate-bound phosphorus. Under such mineral-adequate conditions, the additional phosphorus released by 500 or 1000 FTU/kg phytase may have provided little marginal nutritional benefit. Moreover, calcium concentration and limestone solubility and particle size can influence mineral availability and the extent of the phytase response within the gastrointestinal tract (Selle and Ravindran, 2007; Hervo et al., 2023; Salehi et al., 2025). Consequently, the adequate Ca and available P supply, together with the mineral characteristics of the basal diet and the moderate phytase doses used, may collectively explain the absence of statistically detectable improvements in laying performance, eggshell quality, and nutrient digestibility. Improvements in shell quality and bone-related traits are more frequently reported under reduced-Ca or reduced-P conditions or in older hens with greater mineral demand (Bello and Korver, 2019, 2020; Eltahan et al., 2023). Moreover, mineral utilization in laying hens is also regulated within a broader nutritional and physiological framework. Vitamin D status plays a central role in intestinal Ca and P absorption, mineral homeostasis, and skeletal mineralization, and responses may vary with physiological stage and dietary mineral supply (Adhikari et al., 2020; Garcia-Mejia et al., 2024). Other dietary factors may also modify these responses; for example, zinc can influence eggshell formation through its involvement in carbonic anhydrase activity and Ca deposition (Zhang et al., 2017), whereas antioxidant and phytogenic feed additives may affect physiological resilience and nutrient-utilization pathways (Liu et al., 2019; Reyer et al., 2026). Although these factors were not specifically manipulated in the present study, they highlight that mineral utilization and the response to phytase should be interpreted within the broader nutritional and physiological context of the laying hen.

Although body weight and egg production changed significantly over time, no treatment × time interaction was detected, indicating that these temporal changes occurred similarly among dietary treatments. Therefore, they are more appropriately interpreted as age- or production-stage-related changes rather than responses to phytase supplementation.

Nutrient and amino acid digestibility

Phytase supplementation also failed to produce a statistically detectable improvement in dry matter, nitrogen, energy, fat, calcium, or phosphorus digestibility. Phytase-mediated improvements in mineral utilization are more consistently observed when the diet provides reduced available phosphorus or calcium, thereby increasing the dependence of the bird on phytate hydrolysis (Liu et al., 2007; Jang et al., 2008; Bello and Korver, 2020; Jlali et al., 2023). Under the present adequate mineral conditions, the nutritional ceiling for an additional digestibility response was likely relatively low.

A similar interpretation applies to apparent ileal amino acid digestibility. Phytate can bind proteins, amino acids, minerals, and digestive enzymes and thereby reduce nutrient availability; however, the magnitude of this antinutritive effect depends strongly on diet composition and nutritional challenge (Cowieson et al., 2006; Selle and Ravindran, 2007; Dersjant-Li et al., 2015). Snow et al. (2003) likewise reported limited and inconsistent effects of phytase on amino acid digestibility in laying hens, whereas Liu et al. (2007) observed greater responses when hens received a nutritionally reduced negative-control diet. Phytase-related improvements in ileal AA digestibility have also been reported in broilers exposed to lower phosphorus availability or greater phytate challenge (Ravindran et al., 2001; Rutherfurd et al., 2004). Therefore, the lack of a detectable AA digestibility response in the present study is consistent with the absence of a substantial dietary nutrient challenge.

Fecal characteristics, gas emission, and VFAs

Phytase supplementation did not significantly affect fecal score, NH₃ or H₂S emissions, or VFA concentrations. This outcome is biologically plausible because ammonia production is primarily associated with microbial degradation of uric acid and other nitrogenous compounds, whereas H₂S and VFAs are influenced by sulfur-containing substrates, undigested nutrients, excreta moisture, pH, and microbial fermentation (Ritz et al., 2004; Rothrock et al., 2010; Zhang and Kim, 2013; Sharma et al., 2017; Park et al., 2018). Because nitrogen and amino acid digestibility also showed no statistically detectable treatment effects, a marked phytase-related reduction in nitrogen-derived gas emissions would not necessarily be expected.

The significant effects of time on fecal score and some VFAs, including butyric, valeric, and isovaleric acids, occurred without significant treatment × time interactions. These changes therefore appear to represent temporal alterations in excreta characteristics or microbial fermentation that were independent of phytase supplementation. This interpretation is more conservative than attributing isolated numerical differences to phytase, particularly given the number of response variables evaluated. Previous work has similarly shown that excreta odor and fermentation responses are more evident when phytase is combined with broader dietary manipulation of phosphorus, protein, or amino acid supply (Sun and Kim, 2019; Heo et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024).

Practical interpretation and study limitations

The practical implication of the present study is not that phytase lacks value in laying-hen nutrition, but that adding moderate phytase doses on top of an already adequate Ca and P formulation may provide little detectable incremental benefit. This distinction is important because the economic value of phytase is often realized through nutrient matrix application and the ability to reduce inorganic phosphorus or calcium inputs while maintaining performance, rather than by simply adding phytase to a diet that already meets mineral requirements.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The experiment did not include a phosphorus-reduced negative control or a phytase nutrient matrix; therefore, it cannot determine whether phytase could allow a reduction in inorganic phosphorus while maintaining productive performance. The relatively small number of independent experimental replicates may also have limited the ability to produce statistically detectable improvements. In addition, phytase supplementation levels were based on the manufacturer’s declared enzyme activity and recommendations, and the actual phytase activity in the finished diets was not analytically verified. Future studies should evaluate phytase with graded reductions in available phosphorus and calcium, include direct analysis of phytase activity and phytate-P in the diets, and test a wider range of phytase doses. Higher doses may be relevant because responses can vary with dose and production stage (Lima et al., 2024).

Conclusion

Under the conditions of this 8-week study, no statistically detectable treatment effects were observed for the measured productive, nutritional, or excreta-related responses following supplementation with 500 or 1000 FTU/kg phytase. These findings are specific to the phytase doses, basal diet, mineral concentrations, hen age, and experimental conditions evaluated and should not be generalized to phosphorus-reduced diets or other production stages.
