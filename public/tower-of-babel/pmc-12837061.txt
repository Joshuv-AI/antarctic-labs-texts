The psychometric properties of the moral distress scale-revised among pediatric nurses
Authors: Bahareh Akbarzadeh, Tahereh Nasrabadi, Abbas Ebadi, Mozhdeh Tajari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMoral distress (MD) is a major problem among nurses in all clinical settings. Pediatric nurses are more at risk for MD because of their greater exposure to moral conflicts among their child clients, families, and colleagues. MD can negatively affect nurses’ physical and emotional well-being, job satisfaction, and job retention. The Moral Distress Scale-Revised (MDS-R) is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing morally distressful situations. This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian MDS-R among pediatric nurses.MethodsThis methodological study was carried out between 2024 and 2025. The MDS-R was translated into Persian via the forward‒backward method of the World Health Organization. Facial validity was evaluated through cognitive interviewing, content validity was evaluated on the basis of the comments of experts, and construct validity was evaluated through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. Data for factor analyses were collected from 447 pediatric nurses selected from a university children’s hospital in Tehran, Iran. Reliability was also evaluated via internal consistency and test-retest stability methods.ResultsThe content validity index (CVI) values of all the items were greater than 0.8, and the CVI of the whole scale was 0.92. Exploratory factor analysis revealed that the scale had four factors with an eigenvalue of more than 1. The factors were healthcare providers’ limited competency; care provisions under the pressure of personal, organizational, patient-related, and family-related factors; futile care; and lack of moral courage, which explained 53.22% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed the good fit of the four-factor model (Chi-square test value = 366.43 with a degree of freedom of 183 (P < 0.001); CMIN/DF = 2; NFI = 0.90; NNFI = 0.94; PNFI = 0.79; CFI = 0.95; IFI = 0.95; SRMR = 0.072; and AGFI = 0.82). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the test-retest intraclass correlation coefficient of the scale were 0.89 and 0.90, respectively.ConclusionThe Persian MDS-R has acceptable validity and reliability and can be used to assess pediatric nurses’ MD and develop strategies to reduce it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04256-6.Keywords: Pediatric nursing, Ethical dilemmas, Psychometrics, Iran

Background

Moral distress (MD) is a major problem among nurses [1]. Jameton defined MD in 1984 as a conflict between nurses’ practices and values [2]. MD refers to perceived distress when an individual cannot perform an action and is believed to be correct because of actual or potential barriers [3, 4]. In other words, MD is a state of mental imbalance in which an individual is prevented from doing what is correct or feels compelled to do what is incorrect [5].

Although all nurses often experience MD [6], pediatric nurses are more at risk for MD because of their greater exposure to moral conflicts among their child clients, families, and colleagues [7]. A study revealed that 25% of nurses in general pediatric wards and 41.2% of nurses in pediatric intensive care units suffer from moderate-intensity MD [8].

MD negatively affects not only nurses but also patients, their families [3, 9], and healthcare systems [10, 11]. It can cause problems such as job burnout, a lack of perceived support, job dissatisfaction, and job turnover [3, 12, 13]. The negative consequences of MD for patients include pain, suffering, prolonged hospital stay, reduced quality of life, altered dignity, false hope, and limited time to be with the family. Its negative consequences for families include grief, sorrow, guilt, a lack of preparedness to accept the patient’s conditions, and a lack of time to stay at the patient’s bedside [9]. These consequences reduce the quality of care and negatively affect healthcare systems [13, 14].

The serious consequences of MD for nurses, patients, and families highlight the importance of its effective prevention and management [15]. A basic step toward MD prevention and management is to identify morally distressful situations via valid and reliable instruments [16]. , such as the Moral Distress Scale-Revised (MDS-R). Hamric et al. developed and introduced three versions of the MDS-R for adult care nurses, physicians, and healthcare providers and three versions for pediatric care nurses, physicians, and healthcare providers [11]. Different versions of the MDS-R allow more specialized detection of moral distress in different settings, so different studies have evaluated the psychometric properties of the MDS-R in different working and cultural settings [16]. For example, some studies on adult care nurses in Iran reported that the Persian MDS-R has acceptable validity and reliability [17, 18]. However, there are few studies on the psychometric properties of the pediatric nurses’ version of the MDS-R. A study in this area evaluated only the face and content validity and reliability of the pediatric nurses’ version of the MDS-R but did not provide any data about its construct validity [1]. Thus, we conducted this study to narrow these gaps. The aim of this study was to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian MDS-R among pediatric nurses.

Methods

Design and participants

This methodological study was carried out between 2024 and 2025.

The samples used in this study were conveniently selected from a university children’s hospital in Tehran, Iran. The selection criteria were a bachelor’s degree or higher in nursing and at least six months of work experience in pediatric care wards. A rule of thumb states that a sample of size 200 is sufficient for factor analysis [19]. Accordingly, we recruited 237 pediatric nurses for exploratory factor analysis and 210 pediatric nurses for confirmatory factor analysis. The study instruments were sent to 470 pediatric nurses via WhatsApp, and ultimately, 447 participants completed and returned them (response rate = 95%).

Data collection instruments

The instruments used for data collection were a demographic characteristics questionnaire and the pediatric nurses’ version of the MDS-R. The items of the first questionnaire were related to age, sex, marital status, educational level, work experience, affiliated ward, and work shift.

The pediatric nurses’ version of the MDS-R, developed by Hamric, Borchers, and Epstein in 2012, has 21 items on the frequency and disturbance level of morally distressful situations. The items are scored on a 0–4 Likert scale. The total score of each item is calculated by multiplying the frequency score by the disturbance level score and is 0–16. The possible score of the scale is 0–84 for each of the frequency and disturbance level dimensions and 0–336 in total. This scale also has two open-ended questions at the end, which ask respondents to mention and score any other morally distressful situations and explain whether they have ever thought or are currently thinking about quitting their clinical settings. The developers of the scale reported that its Cronbach’s alpha was 0.89 [11].

Instrument translation

After permission was obtained from its developers, the MDS-R was translated into Persian via the forward-backward method of the World Health Organization [20]. To ensure that the translated version reflects the same meanings and concepts as the original version does, it is necessary to use a systematic and valid translation method [21]. Therefore, in this study, the World Health Organization translation guidelines, which have also been used in some other valid psychometric studies, were used [22, 23]. Accordingly, a nurse with a PhD degree and a general translator independently translated the MDS-R into Persian. They were native Persian speakers and were familiar with English-Persian translation and health and nursing concepts. None of the translators reported difficult-to-understand expressions on the scale. A nurse specialist compared and combined their translations. For backward translation, two English language specialists independently translated the Persian translation into English. We compared and combined their translations into one translation and sent it to the developers of the instrument, who approved it.

Evaluation of face and content validity

Facial validity was evaluated through cognitive interviewing, through which ten pediatric nurses were asked to comment on the difficulty, clarity, and relevance of the items. The items were then amended on the basis of their comments [24].

Content validity was qualitatively and quantitatively evaluated on the basis of the comments of ten nursing faculty members. In the qualitative evaluation of content validity, the faculty members were asked to comment on the wording, semantic convergence, comprehensibility, clarity, and difficulty of the items. In the quantitative evaluation of content validity, they were asked to rate the cultural relevance of the items via the following Likert scale: 1: “irrelevant”; 2: “relatively relevant”; 3: “relevant”; and 4: “completely relevant”. The content validity index (CVI) was subsequently calculated at the item level (ICVI) and scale level (SCVI). Items with a CVI of 0.80 or more were included in the scale [24].

Item analysis

Before the construct validity evaluation, the items were analyzed for any probable problems. Accordingly, thirty pediatric nurses were conveniently selected to complete the instrument. Then, SPSS software (v. 16.0) was employed to examine interitem correlations as well as the correlations between each item and the whole instrument [24].

Evaluation of construct validity

Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were performed to evaluate the construct validity of the MDS-R. In exploratory factor analysis, the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin test was performed to test sampling adequacy. A test value of more than 0.7 was interpreted as an adequate sample. Bartlett’s test was also performed to test the significance of the intervariable correlation matrix. Then, latent factors were extracted via principal component analysis with varimax orthogonal rotation and a scree plot. The variance of each factor and the cumulative variance of all factors were calculated. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, factors above the horizontal line in the scree plot, and factors that cumulatively explained at least 50% of the total variance in MD were considered for further evaluation. The minimum acceptable factor loading for each item was 0.3 [24]. The extracted factors were labeled according to the shared meaning of their items. Exploratory factor analysis was performed with SPSS software (v. 16.0).

Confirmatory factor analysis was performed through SPSS (v. 16.0) and LISREL (v. 8.8) software to evaluate the factor structure extracted through exploratory factor analysis. Maximum likelihood estimation was used to calculate the most common model fit indices. These indices were the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), minimum discrepancy function divided by degrees of freedom (CMIN/DF), normed fit index (NFI), nonnormed fit index (NNFI), parsimony normed fit index (PNFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), incremental fit index (IFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), and chi-square divided by the degree of freedom (χ2/df) [24].

Evaluation of reliability

The reliability of the MDS-R was evaluated via internal consistency and test-retest stability methods. For internal consistency evaluation, thirty pediatric nurses were randomly selected and asked to complete the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha value was subsequently calculated for each factor and for the whole scale, and values of 0.7–0.9 were considered acceptable [24]. For reliability evaluation through the test‒retest stability method, thirty participants were asked to complete the MDS-R twice with an interval of two weeks. The test‒retest intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was subsequently calculated.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran Islamic Azad University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (code: IR.IAU.TMU.REC.1400.342). The study aim was explained to the participants, and a numerical code was allocated to each of them to maintain confidentiality. Completion of the instruments was considered consent to participate in the study. We attempted to observe the Declaration of Helsinki throughout the study.

Results

Face and content validity

The items were amended on the basis of the comments of ten nurses and ten nursing faculty members to improve their clarity and comprehensibility. A quantitative evaluation of content validity also revealed that the ICVI values of all the items were greater than 0.8, and the SCVI of the scale was 0.92. Accordingly, all the items were included in the scale.

Item analysis

The highest correlation coefficient between items with the whole instrument was for item number 16 (follow the family’s wishes for the child’s care when I do not agree with them but do so because of fears of a lawsuit), and the lowest correlation coefficient was for item number 10 (Be required to care for patients I do not feel qualified to care for).

Construct validity

In total, 447 pediatric nurses completed the MDS-R for construct validity evaluation. Most of them were female (94%) and married (54.1%) and had a bachelor’s degree (81.4%). The means of their age and work experience were 34.84 ± 7.37 and 9.72 ± 6.95 years, respectively (Table 1).

Scree plot

Confirmatory factor analysis revealed a good fit of the extracted model (Fig. 2).

The factor structure of the persian MDS-R

The model fit indices were as follows: chi-square test value = 366.43 with a degree of freedom of 183 (P < 0.001); CMIN/DF = 2; NFI = 0.90; NNFI = 0.94; PNFI = 0.79; CFI = 0.95; IFI = 0.95; SRMR = 0.072; and AGFI = 0.82. According to Jaccard and Wan (1996), a model has a good fit when at least three model fit indices are acceptable [25]. Therefore, the extracted four-factor model had a good fit.

Reliability

The Cronbach’s alpha of the MDS-R was 0.890 for the whole scale, 0.847 for the first factor, 0.749 for the second factor, 0.769 for the third factor, and 0.672 for the fourth factor. The test-retest ICCs of the whole scale and its four factors were 0.904, 0.929, 0.778, 0.945, and 0.911, respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian MDS-R among pediatric nurses. The scale was translated from English into Persian via the method of the World Health Organization, after which its face, content, and construct validity and reliability were evaluated. The findings indicated the acceptable psychometric properties of the scale.

The CVI values of the MDS-R items ranged from 0.8 to 1, and the CVI of the whole scale was 0.91. In agreement with our findings, a previous study on pediatric nurses in Iran reported that the ICVI values of the items were 0.8–1 and that the SCVI of the scale was 0.96 [1]. These findings support the acceptable content validity of the Persian MDS-R among pediatric nurses.

The results of exploratory factor analysis for construct validity evaluation revealed that the Persian MDS-R consisted of four factors, namely, healthcare providers’ limited competency; care provision under the pressure of personal, organizational, patient-related, and family-related factors; futile care; and lack of moral courage. These factors explained 53.22% of the total variance in the MD. The developers of the MDS-R reported a single-factor structure for the scale [11]. Similarly, a study on critical care nurses reported a single-factor structure for the Turkish MDS-R [26]. Another study on the psychometric properties of the Greek version of the MDS-R among critical care nurses reported a three-factor structure that explained 52% of the total variance. That study also reported that most items were loaded on factor 1 and hence reported that the single-factor structure was more appropriate for the scale [27]. However, a study in Iran revealed that the Persian MDS-R among adult nurses consisted of five factors, namely, lack of professional competence, ignorance of legal issues and patient conditions, incomplete care, unquestioning obedience to medical orders and unsafe care, and care provision under personal and organizational pressures. These five factors explained 56.62% of the total variance [18]. These discrepancies among studies in terms of the factor structure of the MDS-R may be due to differences in their context and population. For example, some of these studies were conducted on general, pediatric, and critical nurses, whereas some of them were conducted only on critical care nurses.

Our findings also revealed that the most important contributing factor to MD among pediatric nurses was a lack of professional competence. This factor has six items. Nurses’ competence consists of knowledge, skills, and attitudes developed through university education and actual nursing practices and leads to increased care quality and patient and nurse satisfaction [28]. Clinical competence has a significant negative relationship with MD, meaning that lower clinical competence is associated with higher MD among nurses [29]. In line with our findings, a study in Iran revealed that low clinical competence was the most important contributing factor to MD among nurses [18]. Therefore, healthcare policymakers need to develop strategies to improve nurses’ clinical competence to reduce their MD.

Care provision under the pressure of personal, organizational, patient-related, and family-related factors was the second factor of the MDS-R and had eight items. A systematic review reported that personal factors (such as age, gender, educational level, and work experience) and organizational and workplace-related factors (such as work shift, ethical climate, the nurse–patient ratio, job satisfaction, job burnout, and organizational support) are among the factors influencing MD among pediatric nurses [30]. A study also reported the contradictory expectations of families and organizations as a contributing factor to moral distress among nurses in pediatric and neonatal intensive care units [12].

The third main factor of the MDS-R is futile care, which has four items. Futile care refers to care that does not bring a patient back to life and does not improve their quality of life [31]. The results of a qualitative study [32] and a systematic review [33] revealed the significant role of futile care in causing MD for critical care nurses. These findings indicate that futile care can contribute to MD among both pediatric and adult care nurses.

Lack of moral courage, with three items, was the fourth factor of the Persian MDS-R among pediatric nurses. Moral courage is a virtue that helps individuals overcome their fear and limitations, make good decisions and show good performance in every situation [34]. Individuals with moral courage make and implement decisions with confidence, awareness, and intention without paying much attention to their potential negative consequences [35]. Moral courage is among the factors influencing nurses’ MD [34]. A study reported a significant negative relationship between MD and moral courage, implying that nurses with lower moral courage are more likely to experience MD [36].

The results of the reliability evaluation revealed that the Cronbach’s alpha of the MDS-R was 0.89. Hamric et al. reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for the original MDS-R [11]. Moreover, the test-retest ICC of the Persian MDS-R was 0.90 in the present study. The ICC of the Persian MDS-R in a previous study was 0.94 [1]. These findings highlight the acceptable reliability of the Persian MDS-R for MD evaluation among pediatric nurses.

Limitations

We need a rather large sample of pediatric nurses for construct validity evaluation through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. However, pediatric nurses in the study setting had a heavy workload and had limited time for responding to the study instruments. Therefore, we performed a lengthy process of data collection. Moreover, this study was conducted on nurses from critical care and general pediatric care wards of a special pediatric hospital in Tehran, Iran. Further studies in other geographical areas are recommended to produce more reliable data about the psychometric properties of the Persian MDS-R.

Conclusion

This methodological study revealed that the Persian MDS-R has acceptable validity and reliability for MD evaluation among pediatric nurses. Therefore, it can be used to collect the necessary data for developing plans and strategies to reduce pediatric nurses’ MD.
