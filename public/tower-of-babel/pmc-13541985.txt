Effectiveness of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy in Reducing Depression and Anxiety Among University Students: An Experimental Study
Authors: Bhawna Gurnani, Shubhagata Awasthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression and anxiety are among the most prevalent mental health problems affecting university students and are associated with impaired academic performance, emotional distress and reduced quality of life. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is an evidence-based intervention that has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety; however, intervention-based studies among Indian university students remain limited.PurposeThe present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of CBT in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among university students from medical, paramedical and non-medical academic streams.MethodsA total of 600 university students were screened using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). Of these, 180 students meeting the inclusion criteria and exhibiting mild to moderate symptoms of depression and/or anxiety were selected for the study. Participants were randomly assigned to a CBT intervention group (n = 90) or a control group (n = 90). The intervention group received an eight-session structured CBT programme, while the control group continued routine academic activities. Depression and anxiety levels were assessed before and after the intervention using the DASS-21. Data were analysed using repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA).ResultsThe CBT group demonstrated significant reductions in both depression and anxiety scores from pre-test to post-test, whereas the control group showed minimal change. RM-ANOVA revealed significant main effects of time and significant time × group interaction effects for both depression and anxiety (p < .001), indicating that improvements were significantly greater among participants who received CBT.ConclusionThe findings provide empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among university students. The results highlight the potential value of integrating structured CBT-based mental health interventions within higher education institutions to promote psychological well-being and academic functioning.Keywords: Cognitive behavioural therapy, depression, anxiety, university students, Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21, mental health

Introduction

Mental health concerns among university students have emerged as a significant public health issue worldwide. The transition to higher education is often accompanied by academic demands, performance expectations, social adjustments and uncertainty regarding future career opportunities. These challenges can contribute to psychological distress, with depression and anxiety being among the most commonly reported mental health problems in student populations. Elevated levels of emotional distress not only affect psychological well-being but also interfere with academic performance, interpersonal relationships and overall quality of life.

Depression is characterised by persistent sadness, reduced interest in activities, feelings of hopelessness and impaired functioning, whereas anxiety involves excessive worry, nervousness, physiological arousal and difficulty managing uncertainty. Studies conducted across different countries have consistently demonstrated high rates of depression and anxiety among university students, suggesting that young adults pursuing higher education constitute a particularly vulnerable population. Within the Indian context, increasing academic competition, professional expectations and employment-related concerns further contribute to students’ psychological burden.

Given the growing prevalence of mental health difficulties among students, there is an increasing need for evidence-based interventions that can be implemented within educational settings. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is one of the most extensively researched psychological interventions for depression and anxiety. The approach is based on the premise that maladaptive cognitive patterns influence emotional experiences and behavioural responses. By identifying and modifying dysfunctional thoughts and promoting adaptive coping behaviours, CBT aims to reduce psychological distress and improve overall functioning.

Substantial evidence from randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses supports the effectiveness of CBT in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety across clinical and non-clinical populations. Previous research has demonstrated that CBT contributes to improvements in emotional regulation, coping skills, self-efficacy and psychological resilience. The structured and time-limited nature of CBT also makes it particularly suitable for implementation within university counselling and mental health services.

Despite the growing body of international evidence, intervention-based studies examining the effectiveness of CBT among Indian university students remain relatively limited. Much of the available literature has focused on prevalence estimates and correlational analyses rather than experimentally evaluating therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, few studies have examined students from different academic streams, despite evidence suggesting that academic environments vary considerably in terms of workload, performance pressure and stress exposure.

The present study was undertaken to address this gap by evaluating the effectiveness of a structured CBT programme in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among university students. Using an experimental pre-test–post-test control group design, the study examined changes in psychological outcomes following the CBT intervention and compared these outcomes with a control group. The findings are expected to contribute to the growing literature on student mental health and provide evidence regarding the applicability of CBT as a practical intervention within higher education settings.

Methodology

Study Design

The present investigation employed an experimental pre-test–post-test control group design to examine the effectiveness of CBT in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among university students. This design enabled the comparison of psychological outcomes before and after the intervention while also allowing differences between the intervention and control groups to be evaluated.

Participants and Sampling

The study was conducted among university students enrolled in medical, paramedical and non-medical academic programmes across different cities of Madhya Pradesh, India. An initial screening was carried out with 600 students using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). Students who exhibited mild to moderate levels of depression and/or anxiety and met the eligibility criteria were considered for inclusion in the study.

A total of 180 students were selected for the final sample. Participants were equally distributed across the three academic streams, with 60 students representing each stream.

To ensure balanced group allocation, participants were randomly assigned to either the CBT intervention group (n = 90) or the control group (n = 90). Each academic stream contributed 30 participants to the intervention group and 30 participants to the control group.

Assessment Measure

Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the DASS-21, a widely used self-report instrument designed to measure emotional distress. The scale consists of 21 items rated on a four-point response format. For this study, the Depression and Anxiety subscales were used to evaluate participants’ psychological status before and after the intervention.

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy Intervention

Participants assigned to the intervention group received a structured CBT programme consisting of eight sessions. The intervention was designed to address maladaptive thinking patterns, emotional difficulties and ineffective coping behaviours associated with depression and anxiety.

The programme included psychoeducation, identification of automatic thoughts, cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation, anxiety management strategies, problem-solving skills training and relapse prevention techniques. Participants were encouraged to complete structured homework assignments between sessions to facilitate the application of therapeutic skills in daily life.

The control group did not receive any psychological intervention during the study period and continued their routine academic activities.

Procedure

Following institutional permission and participant consent, eligible students completed the baseline assessment using the DASS-21. After the pre-test assessment, participants were allocated to either the intervention or control group.

The CBT programme was administered over eight structured sessions. Upon completion of the intervention, participants from both groups were reassessed using the same instrument under comparable conditions. Pre-test and post-test scores were subsequently compared to determine changes in depression and anxiety levels.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). Time (pre-test and post-test) was treated as the within-subject factor, whereas group (CBT intervention and control) was treated as the between-subject factor. The interaction between time and group served as the primary indicator of intervention effectiveness. Statistical significance was established at p < .05.

As shown in Table 1, students in the CBT group demonstrated a substantial reduction in depression and anxiety scores from pre-test to post-test. In contrast, students in the control group exhibited minimal change in both depression and anxiety scores across the assessment period. Baseline scores were comparable between the two groups, indicating that post-intervention differences were attributable to the CBT intervention.

An RM-ANOVA was conducted to examine the effect of CBT on depression scores across time and between groups. The analysis revealed a statistically significant main effect of time, F(1, 178) = 1,467.00, p < .001, indicating a significant change in depression scores from pre-test to post-test. A significant main effect of group was also observed, F(1, 178) = 15.60, p < .001, suggesting overall differences between the CBT and control groups. Importantly, a statistically significant time × group interaction effect was found, F(1, 178) = 1,139.00, p < .001, demonstrating that the reduction in depression scores over time was significantly greater in the CBT group compared with the control group.

An RM-ANOVA was conducted to examine the effect of CBT on anxiety scores across time and between groups. A significant main effect of group was also observed, F(1, 178) = 15.00, p < .001. Most importantly, a statistically significant time × group interaction effect was found, F(1, 178) = 1,302.00, p < .001, demonstrating that the reduction in anxiety scores over time was significantly greater among students who received CBT compared with those in the control group.

Discussion

The findings of the present study align with existing literature demonstrating the efficacy of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety by targeting maladaptive cognitions, behavioural avoidance and emotional dysregulation.1–3 The present study examined the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among university students using an experimental pre-test–post-test control group design. The findings provide strong empirical support for the effectiveness of CBT as a structured psychological intervention within higher education settings.

Results from the descriptive analysis (Table 1) demonstrated that students in the CBT intervention group showed a substantial reduction in both depression and anxiety scores from pre-test to post-test, whereas students in the control group exhibited minimal change over the same period. Baseline scores for depression and anxiety were comparable across groups, indicating that post-intervention differences were attributable to the CBT intervention rather than to pre-existing group differences.

Inferential analysis further strengthened these findings. The repeated measures ANOVA revealed statistically significant main effects of time for both depression and anxiety, indicating overall improvement in psychological symptoms across assessment points. More importantly, significant time × group interaction effects were observed for depression (Table 2) and anxiety (Table 3). These interaction effects confirm that reductions in depression and anxiety over time were significantly greater among students who received CBT compared to those in the control group, providing clear evidence of the intervention’s effectiveness.

The observed reductions in depression scores following CBT are consistent with the core principles of cognitive behavioural theory, which emphasise the modification of maladaptive thought patterns, behavioural activation and emotional regulation. By targeting negative automatic thoughts and dysfunctional beliefs, CBT likely enabled students to reinterpret academic stressors in a more adaptive manner, thereby reducing depressive symptomatology. The large interaction effect observed for depression suggests that CBT had a meaningful and clinically relevant impact on students’ emotional well-being.

Similarly, the significant reduction in anxiety scores among students who received CBT highlights the effectiveness of cognitive and behavioural strategies in managing excessive worry, physiological arousal and fear-based responses. Techniques such as cognitive restructuring, relaxation and problem-solving may have helped students develop greater tolerance for academic uncertainty and performance-related stress. The absence of comparable improvement in the control group reinforces the conclusion that reductions in anxiety were attributable to the intervention rather than to spontaneous recovery or external academic factors.

The findings of the present study are in line with previous international research demonstrating the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among student populations. Numerous studies have reported that CBT produces significant improvements in emotional functioning by equipping individuals with practical coping skills that can be applied beyond the therapeutic context. The present study extends this evidence by demonstrating the applicability of CBT within the Indian higher education context using an experimental design and standardised assessment measures.

From an applied perspective, the results underscore the importance of integrating evidence-based psychological interventions within educational institutions. University students often experience mild to moderate levels of psychological distress that may not meet diagnostic thresholds but interfere with academic engagement, motivation and quality of life. The present findings suggest that structured, time-limited CBT interventions can be effectively implemented to address these concerns and promote emotional resilience among students.

Despite the strengths of the study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study focused on short-term outcomes, and long-term follow-up assessments were not conducted to examine the sustainability of treatment effects. Additionally, the use of self-report measures may have introduced response bias. Future research may benefit from incorporating follow-up assessments, qualitative evaluations and multi-method approaches to further examine the long-term impact of CBT interventions in academic settings.

In conclusion, the present study provides robust empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among university students. The significant interaction effects observed for both psychological outcomes highlight CBT as a valuable, evidence-based intervention that can be feasibly integrated into higher education mental health services. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature advocating for preventive and early intervention approaches to promote student mental health and well-being.

Conclusion

The present study investigated the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among university students using an experimental pre-test–post-test control group design. The findings clearly demonstrate that CBT is an effective psychological intervention for alleviating symptoms of depression and anxiety within higher education settings.

Students who received the CBT intervention showed substantial reductions in both depression and anxiety scores from pre-test to post-test, whereas students in the control group exhibited minimal or no improvement. The significant time × group interaction effects observed for both outcome variables provide strong statistical evidence that the observed improvements were directly attributable to the CBT intervention rather than to the passage of time or external academic factors.

These findings highlight the relevance of CBT as a structured, evidence-based approach that addresses maladaptive cognitive patterns, emotional dysregulation and ineffective coping strategies commonly experienced by university students. By equipping students with practical cognitive and behavioural skills, CBT appears to promote adaptive coping, emotional resilience and improved psychological functioning in the face of academic and personal stressors.

The results of the study contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the integration of mental health interventions within educational institutions. Given the high prevalence of depression and anxiety among university students and the barriers associated with accessing specialised mental health care, the implementation of time-limited and skill-focused interventions such as CBT may represent a practical and effective strategy for addressing student mental health needs.

In conclusion, the present study provides robust empirical support for the effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety among university students. The findings underscore the importance of incorporating evidence-based psychological interventions into higher education mental health services to promote student well-being, academic engagement and long-term psychological well-being.

Implications of the Study

Practical Implications

The findings of the present study have important practical implications for higher education institutions, mental health professionals and student support services. The demonstrated effectiveness of CBT in reducing depression and anxiety suggests that it can be feasibly implemented as a structured intervention within university settings to address student mental health concerns.

Universities and colleges may consider integrating CBT-based programmes into existing counselling and wellness services. Given the time-limited and skill-oriented nature of CBT, such interventions can be delivered in individual or group formats, making them suitable for institutions with limited mental health resources. Group-based CBT programmes, in particular, may allow institutions to reach a larger number of students while maintaining therapeutic effectiveness.

The findings also underscore the value of early intervention for students experiencing mild to moderate psychological distress. Implementing CBT interventions at an early stage may help prevent the escalation of depression and anxiety into more severe psychological disorders, thereby supporting students’ academic engagement, motivation and overall well-being. Academic counsellors, psychologists and student welfare officers may utilise CBT techniques as part of routine mental health screening and intervention programmes.

Additionally, the structured and skills-based components of CBT, such as cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation and problem-solving, can be incorporated into psychoeducational workshops and mental health awareness programmes. Such initiatives may empower students with practical coping strategies that enhance emotional regulation and resilience in academic environments.

Policy Implications

From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the need for greater emphasis on mental health promotion within higher education systems. Educational policymakers and institutional administrators may consider formulating policies that support the inclusion of evidence-based psychological interventions, such as CBT, within university mental health frameworks.

The results provide empirical justification for allocating resources towards the development of structured counselling services and training programmes for mental health professionals within educational institutions. Policies that encourage the recruitment and training of counsellors in CBT-based approaches may enhance the quality and effectiveness of student mental health services.

Furthermore, the study supports the incorporation of mental health screening and intervention strategies as a routine component of student welfare policies. Establishing standardised protocols for early identification and intervention may reduce stigma, improve help-seeking behaviour and promote a culture of psychological well-being within academic institutions. At a broader level, the findings may inform national and institutional mental health policies aimed at addressing the growing burden of depression and anxiety among young adults in higher education.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Despite the strengths of the present study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study focused on short-term outcomes, and follow-up assessments were not conducted to evaluate the long-term sustainability of treatment effects. Future research may include longitudinal designs to examine whether the observed reductions in depression and anxiety are maintained over extended periods.

Second, the reliance on self-report measures may have introduced response bias, as participants’ responses could have been influenced by social desirability or subjective interpretation of questionnaire items. Incorporating clinician-administered assessments or mixed-method approaches may enhance the robustness of future investigations.

Third, although the experimental design strengthens causal inference, the generalisability of the findings may be limited to similar university populations. Future studies may examine the effectiveness of CBT across diverse educational contexts, cultural backgrounds and age groups to enhance external validity.

Future research may also explore the comparative effectiveness of different modes of CBT delivery, such as group-based, online or blended interventions, to identify the most efficient and accessible formats for university settings. Additionally, examining mediating factors such as cognitive change, coping skills and emotional regulation may provide deeper insight into the mechanisms through which CBT exerts its therapeutic effects.
