Adoption of new treatment options in non-systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) in clinical practice
Authors: Hedda C Lamers, Erika Van Nieuwenhove, Sebastiaan J Vastert, Marc H A Jansen, Annet van Royen-Kerkhof, Joost F Swart, Sytze de Roock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe recent development of biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) have expanded the possibilities of pharmacological treatment for patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). The objective of this study is to evaluate trends in initial prescriptions for these targeted therapies in patients aged 0–18 years with non-systemic JIA in clinical practice. We also identified the approval dates and key regulatory changes for each medication.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using prescription records from the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital, the largest pediatric rheumatology center in the Netherlands, analyzing data from January 2012 to December 2023. The market introduction of targeted therapies for JIA were extracted from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) database.ResultsOver an 11-year period, first prescriptions dates of 11 bDMARDs and tsDMARDs (N = 672) and 3 conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) (N = 720) were analysed among 696 patients. TNF-alpha inhibitors accounted for the largest proportion of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs, comprising 79.3%. The prescription of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs increased, from a relative proportion of 32.2% in 2012 to 62.7% in 2023, while the prescription of csDMARDs declined from 67.8% in 2012 to 34.3% in 2023. The prescription of JAK inhibitors rose from 1.6% in 2020 to 5.9% in 2023.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive overview of the implementation of targeted therapies (bDMARDs and tsDMARDs) in non-systemic JIA over the past decade, showing a rise in targeted therapies alongside a decrease in csDMARD prescriptions. While TNF-alpha inhibitors remain the most commonly prescribed, there has been a significant rise in the prescription of JAK inhibitors and other new therapies. Factors such as route of administration, patient comfort, costs and treatment efficacy may influence the adoption of these DMARDs. The findings highlight the need for continued research to identify factors for successful implementation of these therapies in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-025-01173-5.Keywords: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Targeted therapies, bDMARD, Biologicals, TsDMARDs, CsDMARDs, JAK inhibitors, TNF- alpha inhibitors

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a complex inflammatory disease with a multifactorial immune pathogenesis [1]. JIA is not a single disease, but an umbrella term. It encompasses all forms of arthritis that begin before a patient is aged 16 years, persist for more than 6 weeks and is of unknown origin. It is the most common chronic rheumatic disease and a significant cause of short- and long-term disability [2, 3]. The incidence ranges from 1.6 to 23 and prevalence from 3.8 to 400 per 100.000 in the population of Europe in 2010 [4]. JIA is categorized into systemic JIA (sJIA) and non-systemic JIA, with the latter further subdivided into different subtypes [5].

The cornerstone of treatment in non-systemic JIA is pharmacological treatment. In accordance with the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), particularly methotrexate, have served as the first-line therapy for over 30 years [6–8]. Advancements in the understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying JIA, have led to the development of new therapeutic options. These targeted therapies specifically target certain cytokines or cellular interactions, interfering with the activation and regulation of the immune system. Biological disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) constitute a key category of targeted therapies [9].

The first bDMARDs approved for non-systemic JIA were TNF-alpha inhibitors, with etanercept being the first, followed by adalimumab [10, 11]. These agents continue to be the preferred initial bDMARD due to their established efficacy and clinical experience [12, 13]. Subsequently, abatacept, a T-lymphocyte activation blocker, and tocilizumab, an IL-6 inhibitor, were approved for non-systemic JIA treatment [14, 15]. Later, other interleukin pathway inhibitors, including IL-17 inhibitors and IL-12/IL-23 inhibitors, were introduced. In recent years, Janus Kinase inhibitors (JAK inhibitors), have been authorized as treatment options. This new class of targeted therapies is called targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) [16]. Numerous additional bDMARDs and tsDMARDs are still undergoing clinical trials. The introduction of therapy with targeted therapies has led to substantial improvements in prognosis compared to treatment with csDMARDs, marking a significant evolution in treatment of JIA [8].

The increasing range of treatment options poses challenges for pediatric rheumatologists[17]. While each targeted therapy offers benefits like improved route of administration and disease control, these must be considered alongside concerns over long-term safety and high costs [18]. Therefore, understanding prescription trends in children with JIA is crucial for evaluating the factors that shape these trends and ensuring optimal treatment [13]. A few studies have analyzed medication utilization in non-systemic JIA, but focus remained on the initial diagnosis period or did not involve recent targeted therapies as JAK inhibitors [19, 20]. To our knowledge, no study has comprehensively evaluated prescription trends for a wide range of targeted therapies, or examined how the introduction of these new treatments affects previous therapies. Therefore, this study investigated trends in initial prescription for targeted therapies in patients aged 0–18 years with non-systemic Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) in clinical practice. We also identified the approval dates and key regulatory changes for each medication.

Methods

This is a single center retrospective cohort study using data from the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) of the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital in Utrecht, The Netherlands. JIA patients were selected based on the presence of at least one filled-out Ritchie Articular Index. Data collected between April 2011 and September 2024 was extracted from the EMR and anonymized. This quality of care study was exempted from the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act and was conducted according to good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE Statement for observational studies.

Market introductions

The market introduction of targeted therapies for JIA were extracted from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) database. Reports for each medication were analyzed, focusing on changes such as modifications in administration routes, extensions for new indications, biosimilars or adjustments in the approved age range.

Patient data

Data from patients was included when (1) patients had non-systemic JIA and (2) patients were younger than 18 years at the time of their first prescription date. Excluded were duplicate records, data from before 2012 (transition to EMR in April 2011), and data after 2023 (as data collection for 2024 was incomplete). Medication data not relevant for this study were excluded, including corticosteroids (systemic and intra-articular, typically used only as short-term anti-inflammatory therapy), immunosuppressives without a primary indication for non-systemic JIA (e.g., ciclosporin, azathioprine, tacrolimus, mycophenolic acid, hydroxychloroquine), and targeted therapies currently in clinical trial for non-systemic JIA treatment. Medication variables collected included medication name, dosage, prescribing clinician, prescription date, and date of initial prescription for all DMARDs prescribed in the hospital for JIA (arthritis and/or uveitis). Additional patient characteristics included biological sex, age at first prescription date, and JIA ILAR classification. Missing classifications were listed as unknown. Included medications were conventional synthetic DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide), bDMARDs (abatacept, adalimumab, etanercept, golimumab, tocilizumab, ustekinumab, secukinumab, rituximab and infliximab) and tsDMARDs (tofacitinib and baricitinib).

Dataset and prescription sequence

This study was based on real-world data derived from retrospective pharmacy records from a single center in the Netherlands. Start and stop dates were updated at each new order or dose adjustment. Only the very first prescription date of each systemic DMARD could be determined with certainty. Treatment duration and reasons for switching therapies (e.g., inefficacy or intolerance) could not be reliably identified. To provide additional context, the frequency of concomitant csDMARD use at the initiation of targeted therapy was quantified, duration of this co-medication could however not be determined. To avoid misclassification, analyses were restricted to the first prescription date of each systemic DMARD. Prescription sequence was assessed by assigning a sequential integer value to each newly prescribed systemic DMARD. When two medications were initiated on the same date, both were coded as first starts and assigned the same sequence number. For example, if methotrexate and adalimumab were prescribed on the same day, both were coded as sequence number 1; if methotrexate was prescribed first and adalimumab one month later, methotrexate was coded as sequence number 1 and adalimumab as sequence number 2.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and medication types were summarized and assessed using descriptive statistics. The proportion of patients with each medication was calculated and changes in prescription trends over time evaluated. The mean prescription order was calculated by assigning a sequential integer value to each prescribed medication, and then computing the mean number per year. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29).

Definitions

In this study, first prescription date refers to the first date a specific systemic DMARD was prescribed to an individual patient for the treatment of non-systemic JIA. Restart of the same medication or concomitant medication is not included. The prescription sequence is calculated by assigning a sequential integer value to each prescribed systemic DMARD.

Cohort analysis

This study included 696 patients, accounting for 1392 unique first prescriptions. Inclusion and exclusion decisions are depicted in Supplemental Fig. 1. The majority of patients were female (65.9%), and the mean age at first prescription was 10.9 ± 5.0 years. The most common non-systemic JIA category was oligoarticular JIA (48.3%), followed by RF-negative polyarthritis (24.1%), undifferentiated JIA (10.8), ERA (6.8%), RF-positive polyarthritis (5.4%), and PsA (4.5%). JIA category was unknown for 17.8% of the patients. The distribution of JIA categories per medication group (csDMARDs and targeted therapy) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Prescription trends

A total of 720, (51.7%) of first prescription dates consisted of csDMARDs, and 672 (48.3%) of targeted therapies (Table 2). Within the csDMARDs, methotrexate was the most commonly prescribed medication, accounting for 85.1% of csDMARD prescriptions (n = 613) (Table 2A). Among the targeted therapies (Table 2B), adalimumab was the most frequently prescribed, representing 49.0% of all prescriptions in this category, followed by etanercept (18.2%), tocilizumab (11.9%), and golimumab (8.2%). TNF-alpha inhibitors accounted for the largest proportion of targeted therapies, comprising 79.3% of all prescriptions. Within the TNF-alpha inhibitor group, adalimumab was the most commonly prescribed, making up 61.7% of the prescriptions, followed by etanercept (22.9%). JAK inhibitors represented 3.3% of all prescriptions, with tofacitinib accounting for 95.4% of JAK inhibitors prescriptions.

Prescription trends by year

The proportion of first prescription dates of csDMARDs declined from 67.8% in 2012 to 34.3% in 2023, while the proportion of targeted therapies (bDMARDs + tsDMARDs) increased from 32.2% in 2012 to 62.7% in 2023 (Fig. 1A). Among all targeted therapy starts, 33.6% were initiated concomitantly with a csDMARD, with no significant variation across years (data not shown). Among the targeted therapies groups, TNF-alpha inhibitors remained the most prescribed group (Fig. 1B). This proportion declined since 2020, coinciding with an increase in the prescription of JAK inhibitors, which rose from 1.6% in 2020 to 5.9% in 2023. Tocilizumab also contributed to the decline in TNF-alpha inhibitors but displayed a fluctuating pattern. Adalimumab represented the largest proportion of TNF-alpha inhibitors, reaching 81.3% in 2020 (Fig. 1C). Etanercept accounted for a large proportion between 2012 and 2015, but declined after that period. The proportion of golimumab increased from 2018 onwards, reaching 22.5% of the TNF-alpha prescriptions by 2023. The proportion of infliximab fluctuated throughout the years without a clear trend.

Prescription sequence

CsDMARDs were commonly the first prescribed group of DMARDs per individual patient, with a mean prescription sequence of 1.15 (Fig. 2A). Adalimumab (mean 1.89) and etanercept (mean 1.97) were comparable in prescription sequence between 2013 and 2018. After 2019 etanercept was prescribed later in the prescription sequence then adalimumab, reaching 2.5 in 2023. Tocilizumab remained around the third prescribed medicine for most years. Golimumab, first prescribed in 2017 (mean 3.3), decreased to 2.3 by 2023. Tofacitinib had a mean prescription sequence of 3.2. Excluding the years 2013, the total number of prescriptions per year remained stable (Fig. 2B). The number of first prescriptions of csDMARDs and etanercept declined over the years, and the number of prescriptions of golimumab, adalimumab, tocilizumab and tofacitinib increased.

Discussion

This study investigated trends in initial prescription for targeted therapies in patients aged 0–18 years with non-systemic JIA in clinical practice, using prescription records from the largest pediatric rheumatology center in the Netherlands. The findings indicate an increase in the use of targeted therapies over time, while the use of csDMARDs has declined. Where some therapies have become more commonly prescribed, others have either seen a decline or failed to gain substantial market share for JIA treatment.

Previous research demonstrates that the implementation of new medications in healthcare is often slow, our findings show that the pace of adoption varies depending on the type of therapy [21–23]. We found a gradual increase in targeted therapy prescriptions, aligning with findings from research conducted in various countries [19, 24, 25]. Our results show that TNF-alpha inhibitors are the preferred targeted therapies throughout the study period, consistent with findings from similar cohorts [19, 20]. A particularly noteworthy finding is the rapid increase in the use of JAK inhibitors following their EMA approval in 2021. This suggests that the route of administration plays an important role in the adoption of treatments by pediatric rheumatologists, as JAK inhibitors are the only oral targeted DMARDs. Similarly, tocilizumab and adalimumab prescriptions increased following changes in their administration routes. Tocilizumab transitioned from intravenous to subcutaneous administration, and adalimumab changed to a citrate-free formulation in 2017, which significantly reduced injection discomfort [26].

Our results show that adalimumab became the preferential TNF inhibitor after 2016, thereby replacing etanercept. These findings are consistent with an analysis of initial treatments for non-systemic JIA between 2009 and 2018 [19]. The preference for adalimumab compared to etanercept after 2016 may be because of the introduction of its citrate-free formulation in 2017 and the superior efficacy in treating JIA-associated uveitis, a significant comorbidity [27, 28]. Another contributing factor could be the introduction of a biosimilar of adalimumab in 2017, which helped reduce medication costs [29]. A similar trend was observed with tocilizumab after the introduction of its biosimilar in 2023 [30]. In contrast therapies such as ustekinumab, secukinumab, and abatacept have not yet achieved significant market share for JIA in this cohort.

Our results show that the prescriptions of csDMARDs decline, however csDMARDs continue to be prescribed first in the treatment sequence for JIA. This reflects that csDMARDs consistently serve as the initial therapy, prescribed either as monotherapy or concomitantly with a bDMARD, but not as second-line treatment. This pattern is consistent with prior research as well as the guidelines of the Dutch Association for Pediatrics (NVK) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR). [6, 7, 31]

This study has limitations to be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study based on pharmacy records, we were limited to evaluate prescription data. Consequently, we were unable to assess the effectiveness, combination or continuation of therapies. The reasons for discontinuation are also not recorded in this dataset and could therefore not be analysed. Second, due to the transition to the Electronic Medical Record system in 2011, some medications may have been inaccurately labeled as first prescription dates. To minimize this, data prior to January 2012 were excluded. Lastly, treatment guidelines and decision-making in JIA vary across countries and healthcare systems, so the results of this study should be interpreted within the context of the Dutch healthcare system. The applicability of these findings may be limited to systems with comparable guidelines for csDMARDs and targeted therapies. The reimbursement status also differs by country and was not included in this study, which could lead to delays between the EMA approval date and the actual implementation of these therapies.

This study offers a comprehensive overview of prescription trends for targeted therapies over a period of more than a decade, providing insight into how these medications are being integrated into clinical practice. The availability of extensive patient data and prescription records from a large cohort is a key strength of this study. While this analysis does not directly assess treatment efficacy, it provides valuable information on how these therapies are being utilized in routine care. These findings offer important guidance for pediatric rheumatologists, who are increasingly challenged by the growing array of therapeutic options [17]. Brunner et al. reported that 15–19% of JIA patients are exposed to off-label bDMARDs, reflecting the need for further approvals in pediatric care [32]. Trials that are currently ongoing for upadacitinib, ixekizumab, and sarilumab are crucial to address the unmet therapeutic needs for JIA patients. With the expanding availability of targeted therapies, identifying the factors contributing to successful implementation is important. Incorporating these insights into the development of new therapies could be valuable, as factors such as the route of administration, costs and patient comfort may influence therapy adoption. Further research should focus on balancing the cost-effectiveness and efficacy of these therapies.

This report provides a comprehensive overview of JIA treatment over the past decade, highlighting the complexities and variability in clinical practice. It demonstrates the gradual implementation of targeted therapies, emphasizing differences in type of therapy. While TNF-alpha inhibitors remain the most prescribed, the proportion of other targeted therapies such as JAK inhibitors is gradually increasing. Factors such as route of administration, patient comfort, costs and treatment efficacy may influence the adoption of targeted therapies. The findings highlight the need for continued research to identify factors for successful implementation of these therapies in clinical practice.
