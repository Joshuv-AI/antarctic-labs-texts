Evaluation of methods for early detection of Tropilaelaps mites in European honey bee (Apis mellifera) colonies
Authors: Cooper Nat Schouten, Kelly Lees, John M K Roberts, Emma Thompson Brewster, Flemin Gabriel, Koi Tonny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTropilaelaps mites are emerging ectoparasitic parasites of European honey bees (Apis mellifera) that pose a serious threat to apiculture and pollination security globally. There is a paucity of evidence to support current detection methods used in biosecurity programs. We conducted field trials in Papua New Guinea using 27 infested A. mellifera colonies across six apiaries to evaluate six Tropilaelaps detection methods: bump test, alcohol wash, modified rapid brood uncapping, worker brood uncapping, environmental DNA (eDNA) swabs, and sticky mats with an acaricide. eDNA swabs and acaricides with sticky mats had the highest sensitivity detecting Tropilaelaps in 100% and 92% of the tested infested colonies respectively, while the alcohol wash and bump test had a sensitivity of 41% and 33% in the tested hives. Cost analysis identified rapid brood uncapping as the most cost-effective method (A$6.87 per test), while eDNA swabs and acaricide-treated sticky mats, despite their high sensitivity, were substantially more expensive (A$28.75 and A$57.08 per test, respectively). This study provides critical evidence to strengthen current honey bee biosecurity surveillance protocols and underscores the need for adoption of the most sensitive, standardized, cost-effective Tropilaelaps detection methods to improve early detection and thereby strengthen international honey bee biosecurity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52776-1.Keywords: Tropilaelaps mercedesae, Honey bee biosecurity, Surveillance methods, Beekeeping, Apiculture, Environmental DNA (eDNA)Subject terms: Biological techniques, Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology, Zoology

Introduction

The ectoparasitic mite Tropilaelaps spp. (Anderson & Morgan) has shifted host from the Giant Asian Honey bee (Apis dorsata F.) to managed European honey bees (Apis mellifera L.) and are threat to apiculture and pollination-dependent horticultural industries1–3. Tropilaelaps spp. are identified by the World Organization for Animal Health as a reportable disease of honey bees and are considered an extreme threat to honey and pollination industries based on the species’ ability to spread and reproduce, and the difficulty to detect and predicted costs to eradicate and/or control3. Four species are currently recognized in this genus, of which two species (Tropilaelaps clareae and T. mercedesae) are particularly harmful to A. mellifera4. In particular, T. mercedesae continues to expand its geographic range with recent reports in the European countries of Georgia5, and Russia, including the Krasnodar region of Western Russia6 Western Siberia and Central Asian countries of Uzbekistan7 and Kazakhstan8. A. mellifera are especially vulnerable to being parasitized by Tropilaelaps with their potential impacts being arguably greater than Varroa destructor (Anderson & Trueman), which is the leading cause of colony losses globally3,9. In comparison to V. destructor, Tropilaelaps spp. reproduce faster, have higher fecundity and reproduction10,11, inflict more injuries to developing larvae and prepupae,12,13, and are a significant vector of honey bee viruses14.

Globally beekeepers are already managing V. destructor, and these control efforts will contribute to reducing the impacts of Tropilaelaps spp. However, research has shown that Tropilaelaps spp. have more genes dedicated to detoxification of pesticides11, thus resistance to currently adopted miticides among beekeeping businesses are likely to be a significant challenge15. Further, globally adopted control options for V. destructor are significantly less effective for Tropilaelaps spp. with formic acid and brood breaks currently being the most efficacious control options16–18. Due to their small size19, rapid locomotion20,21, their colour providing camouflage with capped brood, and their restricted period of phoresy22, they are difficult to detect using current surveillance methods. The reliability and sensitivity of surveillance methods are critical to international honey bee biosecurity efforts for their early detection23–25. Despite being an increasing and significant threat to honey and pollination dependent industries globally, there continues to be relatively little known about Tropilaelaps spp. life history, genetic diversity, biological life cycle, feeding behaviours, sex determination, dispersal mechanisms, monitoring and control options21. This situation is exacerbated by the fact that the distribution of Tropilaelaps spp. is primarily in low- and middle-income countries where there are limited bee biosecurity investments and conducting research on the species is challenging6,26.

Since the standard methods for Tropilaelaps research was published19, efforts have been made to investigate novel methods for Tropilaelaps spp. detection. Thirteen different methods have been developed and those that report statistical sensitivity of detection data range from 5.2 ± 2.3%23 to 100%25(Table 1, Supplementary Information Table S1). Importantly, there are methods being utilised in honey bee biosecurity programs globally, for example the use of the bump test, sugar shake and acaricides with sticky mats, which have limited statistically validated sensitivity data. The cost-effectiveness of detection methods is critical to surveillance program viability and design, yet comparative analyses of detection method costs remain limited.

This research was conducted to (1) review the sensitivity of detection methods for T. mercedesae; (2) conduct field trials to evaluate the sensitivity and cost effectiveness of T. mercedesae detection methods, and (3) provide recommendations for standard operating procedures (SOPs) and future T. mercedesae surveillance policy and research.

Methods

Field Trial

This research was conducted in the Eastern Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea between 4-21st May 2025. Twenty-seven colonies were used for evaluating T. mercedesae detection methods across six apiaries with a minimum of three colonies and a maximum of seven colonies per apiary. All colonies were queen right, eight frame Langstroth deeps with a minimum of seven frames of bees and a maximum of 11 frames of bees using cluster count methods36. Six Tropilaelaps detection methods were evaluated including the bump test, alcohol washing, modified rapid brood uncapping, uncapping worker brood cells, eDNA swabs and 24 h mite fall using amitraz (Apitraz) and sticky mats. The eDNA swab was conducted first, followed by identification of suitable frames for the bump test, sampling worker bees for the alcohol wash and brood uncapping. We inserted the sticky mats and acaricides last to avoid interactions between tests. The following protocols were adopted for each method.

Bump Test

The bump test was implemented as described by Pettis et al., 2013, where all bees were removed from a brood frame and the frame was bumped over a pan by hitting one end of the frame on the side of the pan, turning the frame, rebumping the frame, and repeating the process once more for a total of four bumps. T. mercedesae numbers were then recorded.

Alcohol Wash

Adult A. mellifera (n = 300) were collected using a ½ measuring cup from one brood frame per colony. The sample of bees were washed in 250 mL of 75% alcohol for 30 s37,38. The wash of the bee sample was conducted three times in three separate Varroa EasyCheck containers37,38. Varroa wash containers were cleaned, dried and thoroughly examined for mites in between tests. T. mercedesae numbers were recorded individually for each wash.

Modified Rapid Brood Uncapping

Rapid brood uncapping was carried out as recommended and described by Uzunov et al. (2025) using Veet Expert Hair Removal Wax Strips. Uzunov et al. (2025) recommended a 30 s observation period rather than a 60 s observation period because observers in that study reported the definitive number of T. mercedesae in an average of 19.8 s. For analysis we modified the method and used in field observations rather than video recorded data because rewatching videos is time consuming and therefore adds additional expenses to biosecurity surveillance efforts. We conducted replicates of three rapid uncapping strips per colony.

Uncapping Brood Cells

Brood frames were taken from the apiary for later inspection of worker brood cells using standard methods described by Anderson and Roberts (2013). Due to time limitations, we were only able to uncap an average 126 cells per colony with a minimum of 100. Total T. mercedesae cells which were infested were recorded for each frame.

eDNA swab

Two sterile eDNA FLOQ swabs per colony were moistened with sterilised water, inserted into the hive entrance to their maximum depth (approximately 10 cm) and rolled for 30 s across the bottom board to collect DNA from hive debris. Swabs were then snapped and placed in sterile vials with 100% ethanol and transported to CSIRO (Canberra, Australia) for analysis. DNA was extracted from one swab per colony using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Extracted DNA was tested by qPCR using T. mercedesae-specific primers/probe designed using Geneious Prime 2023 from accessioned sequences of mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase (coxI) gene (Tm-F_227: 5’-AGATTACCAGTCTTAGCAG CAGC-3’, Tm-R_326: 5’-AAAGGATTGGGTCTCCTCCAC-3’, Tm-P_284FAM-5’-ACCTCATTTTTCGACCCTGCA-3’-QSY) and A. mellifera ribosomal 18 S primers/probe (Ward et al., 2007) for confirmation of the DNA extraction. The specificity of the T. mercedesae qPCR assay was confirmed in silico using NCBI Primer BLAST and in the laboratory against reference samples of other bee mites (T. clareae, V. jacobsoni, V. destructor and Acarapis woodi (Rennie) and A. mellifera. Assay sensitivity was tested using serial dilution of synthetic DNA for the T. mercedesae cox1 gene to show robust detection to 103 copies/µL. Duplicate multiplex qPCR reactions were done on a CFX96 Touch Real-time PCR machine (BioRad) for each sample using GoTaq Enviro qPCR reagent (Promega), 400 nM per primer and 100 nM per probe. Cycling conditions were 95℃ (2 min), followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ (15 s) and 59℃ (1 min).

Sticky Mats with 24 h miticide

Commercial Mann Lake eight frame sticky mats with numbered counting grids were inserted on bottom boards of each hive and labelled. Because screened bottom boards were not available, screened wire mesh was placed on top of sticky mats to reduce bees interfering with mite counts. Two strips of Apitraz (amitraz) with the active ingredient amitraz were applied per hive for 24 h after which time, mats were removed and total T. mercedesae numbers per mat were recorded for each mat using Iluco Headlight and Flip-Up Dental Surgical Loupes with 3.0x magnification. To the authors knowledge, amitraz had not previously been used in Papua New Guinea and so we did not assume implications of resistance of T. mercedesae to amitraz within this experiment15,39.

Statistical analysis

Data location and spread

Boxplots were used to visualise the location and spread of T. mercedesae for each test except eDNA. eDNA was excluded as this detection method reports on the prevalence of DNA copies, rather than mite numbers. Uncertainty estimates for the means are shown as 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analysis was done using Microsoft Excel and MATLAB (Version 9.12.0, R2022a, Natick, Massachusetts: The MathWork Inc. https://www.mathworks.com/) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows 2020 (Version 30.0.0 https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

Sensitivity of detection methods

Our analysis assumes “universal” infestation (all hives infested) as described by Pettis et al. (2013) as all colonies were found to be positive for T. mercedesae using at least one detection method. The tests that detected T. mercedesae were divided by the total number of hives studied to compare all six methods to evaluate the sensitivity percentage (p). The total number of tests used was 27 for all tests except sticky mat (n = 26) because one mat was not amendable for analysis. We classified infestation levels into low (< 10), medium (11–100) and high (> 100) based on 24 h acaricide sticky mat mite counts. This is not an ideal approach as it introduces circularity due to using sticky mat with acaricide as both a detection method to investigate and a baseline. However, in the absence of established definitions for low, medium and high levels of infestation it is an attempt at classification based on the most sensitive method of which the response variable is mite count.

A generalised linear mixed effects models was used to compare the sensitivity of test types using a binomial logistic regression response and a Logit link function. Apiary site was included as a random effect to account for the multiple observations per apiary.

Cost effectiveness

The cost effectiveness of T. mercedesae detection methods was evaluated for labour and material costs including the time to prepare tests, manipulate hives, implementation and analysis. We also included depreciation for field-based materials and evaluated total costs per test and cost per 100 tests. In addition to the six methods evaluated in this study, analysis was also evaluated sugar shake, CO2, drone brood uncapping and sticky mat natural mite fall to allow for comparisons of commonly used detection methods. Detailed data and test assumptions used in the analysis are provided in the Supplementary Information S2.

Results

T. mercedesae counts

T. mercedesae were detected in 100% of hives (n = 27) in 100% of apiaries (n = 6). The total number of T. mercedesae detected in this study was 2,401. The average worker brood infestation of T. mercedesae was 6 ± 3%. All tests had right skewed distributions and rapid brood uncapping, worker brood uncapping and sticky mat tests had outliers at high values (Fig. 1). Sticky mats with acaricides found the highest average number of T. mercedesae (80 ± 50), followed by worker brood uncapping (7 ± 3), rapid brood uncapping (2.7 ± 1.3) and the bump test (0.7 ± 0.4), with the lowest number of mites found using the alcohol wash (0.63 ± 0.34).

Sensitivity of detection methods

The sensitivity of detection in this study varied from 33% to 100% with eDNA being the most sensitive and the alcohol and bump tests having the lowest sensitivity and requiring higher repetition to achieve high levels of sensitivity (Table 2). The generalised linear mixed effects model showed that the test type had an effect on the binomial sensitivity response (detected or not detected) (p < 0.001) and that the apiary sites as a random effect did not (p = 0.219). The overall pseudo-R2 marginal measure of this test was 83.3%.

Sensitivity at different levels of infestation

Only eDNA swabbing was able to detect T. mercedesae at low levels of infestation with sensitivity of 100% (Table 3). At medium levels of infestation eDNA swabbing and 24 h sticky mats with acaricides had good sensitivity (> 90%) while worker brood uncapping and rapid brood uncapping had lower sensitivity levels (50–90%). All methods were found to have high (> 99%) sensitivity at high levels of infestation, except for the bump and alcohol wash methods. This illustrates there will likely be varying detection thresholds across tests, with the lowest being eDNA and sticky mat with acaracide and the highest being alcohol wash and bump test.

Cost effectiveness

Analysis of the material costs showed the lowest cost methods were worker brood uncapping, drone brood uncapping and the bump test which all had negligible (< A$0.01) material costs to implement (Table 4). The highest materials costs were for eDNA swab tests ($20.00) followed by sticky mats with acaricides (A$13.75) and CO2 (A$8.16). When labour costs are included the most cost-effective methods were rapid brood uncapping (A$6.87 per test), alcohol wash (A$7.72 per test) and sugar shake (A$8.04 per test), while the least cost-effective tests were sticky mats with acaricides (A$57.08), natural mite fall with sticky mats (A$38.85) and eDNA swab (A$28.75).

Discussion

Sensitivity of detection methods

Tropilaelaps spp. are an increasing threat to global apiculture and pollination dependent industries3 and surveillance methods for early detection are therefore critical to safeguarding global food security24,25. Despite this, standardised, sensitive and statistically supported methods for T. mercedesae detection have been critically lacking. For example, research by Pettis et al. (2013) examined 236 hives from 10 apiaries, 100% of these apiaries were found to be positive for T. mercedesae when evaluating methods including the bump test, alcohol wash, worker and drone brood uncapping and natural mite fall onto sticky mats. Despite analysis applying universal infestation and high sampling effort and diversity of tests, these methods only detected the presence of T. mercedesae in 74.6% (n = 176) of hives evaluated23. Uzunov et al. (2025) examined 30 brood frames from three apiaries, and each apiary was determined to be positive for T. mercedesae when evaluating the rapid brood uncapping method. Of the 30 colonies tested, only 36.6% (n = 11) were found to be positive for T. mercedesae. Similarly, only 80% of colonies (n = 48) were found to be positive for T. mercedesae across six apiaries which were positive in a recent study evaluating the bump test, sugar shake, CO2, worker brood uncapping and alcohol wash24. Our study detected T. mercedesae infestations in each apiaries and all hives using at least one method, despite a low number of hives being assessed. Recent research by Tokach et al. (2026) using natural mite fall onto sticky mats also detected T. mercedesae in 100% of colonies. Our findings corroborate with that of Tokach et al. (2026) suggesting that where an apiary is positive for Tropilaelaps spp., all colonies could be considered infested. There is paucity of information regarding Tropilaelaps spp. immigration, emigration and drift within and between colonies and apiaries17 and further research is needed. Additionally, because low infestation detection is extremely difficult, targeted work is needed to investigate sensitivities of detection methods at very low levels of infestation, which is critical to early detection.

The sensitivity data for sticky mats with acaricides fills an important knowledge gap for this test, which forms part of the contingency response of several government agencies globally. While this test had a high level of sensitivity, it should be noted that research is increasingly demonstrating evidence for chemical resistance to synthetic miticides, including amitraz, coumaphos, flumethrin and fluvalinate10,15,16,21,39,40. It is plausible that as Tropilaelaps spp. spread to countries with more intensive beekeeping industries, they may be exposed to more synthetic miticides and this may influence the sensitivity of methods that employ acaricides to detect Tropilaelaps spp. in future surveillance programs.

In this study, all detection methods had high (> 90%) sensitivity at high levels of infestation, except for the bump test (63%) and alcohol wash method (63%). Comparable sensitivities for these tests were also recently reported by Tokach et al. (2026) at 73.1% and 38.5% for the bump test and alcohol wash respectively. Pettis et al. (2013) highlighted that the reduced levels of sensitivities for the bump test can be overcome by increasing the number of colonies tested. However, in comparison eDNA swabs and sticky mats with acaricides, the bump test and alcohol wash required 5x (n = 12) and 3.5x (n = 9) more hives to be tested to achieve 99% confidence of detecting at least one T. mercedesae in an apiary which has an infestation based on the sensitivities calculated in this study. At low levels of infestation, using a single method and sample effort, only eDNA swabbing and 24 h sticky mats with acaricides were able to detect T. mercedesae, with high or good sensitivity, respectively. The high eDNA detection rate may be overestimated as the persistence of T. mercedesae DNA from past infestations is untested. However, another study detecting T. mercedesae DNA in hive debris found similarly high sensitivity (Kim et al. 2019). It is also important to highlight that DNA detection is not confirmation of mite presence, although eDNA methods for V. destructor have shown a good correlation with mite infestation levels (Roberts et al. 2025). Nonetheless, eight hives in this study were made from newly constructed materials with clean bottom boards and yet T. mercedesae detection was still consistently validated.

Cost effectiveness

Effective honey bee biosecurity programs require that the most economically damaging pests be evaluated, and that methods employed to detect priority pests are sensitive and cost effective. Trade-offs exist between maximum sensitivity and cost effectiveness, particularly in the context of programs sampling for multiple species simultaneously. Early detection is vital to reduce the impacts and spread of Tropilaelaps spp. and our results show that sticky mats with acaricides or eDNA swabs are highly sensitive and appropriate components for inclusion in honey bee biosecurity SOPs. These methods are also amendable to changing environmental contexts which may make brood less available for sampling during surveillance. While the sticky mat with acaricide was highly sensitive, it was also the most expensive at A$57.08 per test. The increased cost of this test was primarily due to labour costs associated with having to return to hives 24 h after sticky board insertion. This was also noted by Pettis et al. (2013) who highlighted additional apiary visits as a limitation to this method for large-scale screening. The eDNA swab method was also more expensive than other tests in this study (A$28.75), however, the cost per test is reduced substantially depending on the capacity and infrastructure of laboratories for high throughput analysis. Further, eDNA swabbing may be able to simultaneously detect multiple honey bee pathogens which could further enhance the cost effectiveness of this method33–35. In countries which do not have formal honey bee biosecurity programs and where cost effectiveness is a priority, the rapid uncapping method may provide useful insights for early detection and evaluation of the spread of Tropilaelaps spp. in the event of an incursion. Rapid uncapping also provides a low-cost method that could be developed further as a monitoring tool for beekeepers and could be utilised in community engagement biosecurity approaches41, particularly where AI models are used to validate rapid brood uncapping videos42.

Recommendations

Tropilaelaps mites represent a significant threat to managed A. mellifera, making early detection critical for honey bee biosecurity25. This study demonstrated that eDNA swabs and acaricides with sticky mats were the most sensitive tools, both detecting T. mercedesae in > 90% of infested colonies. The data obtained and literature review conducted (Table 1, Supplementary Information Table S1) revealed improved surveillance methods than some approached that currently employed in bee biosecurity programs, such as the bump test and sugar shake methods. For example, the rapid brood uncapping method provided good levels of sensitivity and was cost effective highlighting the potential value of incorporating this method in surveillance efforts. Trade-offs exist between maximum sensitivity and cost effectiveness, and further research is needed to evaluate these under country specific biosecurity program designs and scenarios. We recommend integrating the most effective techniques into standard Tropilaelaps spp. surveillance and design of bee biosecurity programs. Further investments are needed to optimize field tests and eDNA diagnostics to improve affordability and broaden their scope for simultaneous detection of multiple pathogens. It is recommended that future work evaluates minimum detection thresholds (the approximate level of infestation before the method will be likely to detect at least one mite) by applying the few most promising methods to a wider sample size. Beekeeping practices and hive structures vary globally so future work is also needed to evaluate the suitability and effectiveness of the detection methods in different beekeeping systems. Strengthening the evidence for the sensitivity of surveillance methods can guide disease intervention methods and policy, hypothesis driven research efforts and importantly, measure the impact this knowledge has on improving bee health and the resilience of honey and pollination dependent industries.
