Understanding enablers and barriers to implementing the Safer Baby Bundle: a mixed-methods study with site leads
Authors: Christine Andrews, Fran Boyle, Ashley Pade, Ann Lancaster, Philippa Middleton, David Ellwood, Adrienne Gordon, Michael Nicholl, Kirstine Sketcher-Baker, Tanya Farrell, Ida Stevia Diget, Vicki Flenady
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Australian Safer Baby Bundle (SBB) consists of five elements (smoking cessation, fetal growth restriction, decreased fetal movements, maternal side sleeping and decision making around timing of birth) which aim to reduce stillbirth and improve antenatal care. Queensland (QLD), New South Wales (NSW), and Victoria (VIC) were the first states to implement the SBB, laying the foundation for its national rollout. This study explores site leads’ experiences integrating the SBB into routine care.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted using surveys and interviews with site leads following SBB implementation across maternity services in QLD, NSW and VIC. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively, qualitative data underwent inductive and deductive thematic analysis, guided by Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) to identify implementation barriers and enablers.ResultsIn 2022, 45 site leads completed surveys, and 20 were interviewed. From surveys, most reported strong endorsement of the SBB from hospital leadership (81%) and opinion leaders (73%). However, only 32% felt their service was adequately resourced, and just 17% believed teams had enough time for Quality Improvement (QI) activities. Only two of five SBB elements, decreased fetal movements (93%) and maternal side sleeping (82%), were reliably implemented across sites. Other elements showed moderate uptake, with smoking cessation (47%) achieving the lowest fidelity. From interviews, five themes were identified as barriers and enablers to implementing and sustaining the SBB in routine practice: (1) nationally consistent resources and training; (2) navigating QI readiness and capacity; (3) collaborative change; (4) service capacity to make recommendations operational; and (5) capability to access data and track progress. Mapping to the four NPT constructs revealed key drivers of SBB implementation: coherence (understanding the approach), cognitive participation (engagement), collective action (operationalising change through relationships) and reflexive monitoring (assessing and adapting).ConclusionsSBB implementation was supported by strong leadership endorsement but challenged by limited resources, time for QI, and the exacerbating effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. Reliable integration of key elements varied across sites, shaped by consistent training, service capacity, collaborative approaches, and access to data. These findings underscore the need for tailored support and resilient infrastructure to embed and sustain evidence-based antenatal care practices at scale.Trial registrationThe Safer Baby Bundle Study was retrospectively registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry database, ACTRN12619001777189, date assigned 16/12/2019.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13839-7.

Background

The Safer Baby Bundle (SBB), developed in 2018 by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) Centre of Research Excellence in Stillbirth (Stillbirth CRE), is a core component of the Australian Stillbirth National Action and Implementation Plan (SNAIP [1], 2020). Designed to address the Australian stillbirth rate (8.0 stillbirths per 1,000 births in 2022 [2]), which had seen minimal improvement over the previous two decades [3], the SBB presents a structured bundle of evidence-based change ideas aimed at improving practice in five key areas; (Element 1) smoking cessation support, (Element 2) monitoring for fetal growth restriction (FGR), (Element 3) management of decreased fetal movements (DFM), (Element 4) maternal side sleeping and (Element 5) shared decision making around timing of birth (TOB) for women with risk factors for stillbirth [4].

Unwarranted or unexplained variation in maternity care has a significant impact on outcomes for mothers and babies [5]. Although there is widespread recognition that high-quality clinical care should be informed by evidence-based best practice, this standard is not always followed. Systematic reviews highlight that a range of implementation strategies can effectively improve uptake of recommended clinical practices [6]. Among these, multicomponent interventions, particularly those combining interprofessional educational opportunities, local opinion leaders, audit, and feedback, have shown the greatest impact across diverse healthcare settings [6, 7]. However, the effectiveness of individual strategies and/or combinations of interventions varies depending on setting and implementation fidelity [8]. The use of ‘care bundles’ is recognised as an effective approach to reduce practice variation, improve health care quality and associated outcomes [9, 10]. Internationally, Quality Improvement Collaboratives (QICs) have become a widely used method for implementing such interventions, despite little empirical evidence for their effectiveness over other strategies [8, 11]. Given the substantial investment in Quality Improvement (QI) initiatives, understanding the ‘how’ and ‘why’ of their success is paramount, alongside evaluating their effectiveness.

The Saving Babies Lives Care Bundle, a stillbirth prevention strategy implemented by the National Health Service in England and Scotland (2015), demonstrated improved process outcomes and a decline in stillbirth rates in early adopter units compared to national averages [12]. However, their study did not explore the reasons behind observed site-level variation in implementation, leaving a critical gap in understanding the factors that contribute to successful uptake. A proposed stillbirth prevention bundle in the United States reflects growing international interest in identifying effective ways to implement structured interventions to reduce stillbirths and associated inequities [13].

Safer Baby Bundle implementation

The Stillbirth CRE implemented the SBB in close partnership with the Departments of Health of Queensland (QLD), New South Wales (NSW) and Victoria (VIC). As the first states in Australia to implement the SBB, they have provided a critical foundation for national rollout. State-led QI program strategies differed across jurisdictions and were influenced by QI capability, resources and local systems. Each state had a dedicated QI project/steering team providing leadership and maintaining momentum and engagement. VIC obtained dedicated funding to collaborate with the Institute for Healthcare Improvement to implement the SBB through a formal QIC, named the Safer Baby Collaborative [14]. This well-resourced QIC supported multiple VIC maternity services to come together to learn, apply and share improvement methods [8, 9]. The approach for QLD and NSW was a pared-back version of a formal QIC, with NSW supporting maternity services in four of its 15 Local Health Districts, while QLD implemented a state-wide program across all 16 Hospital and Health Services providing antenatal care.

Normalisation Process Theory

Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) is an analytical framework used to explore how complex health interventions are implemented, embedded, and sustained in routine practice. It focuses on four key mechanisms which support the incorporation of new practices into everyday clinical routines [15]; coherence – the practice is clearly understood, cognitive participation – people are willing to engage, collective action – people work together to implement it, and reflexive monitoring – people evaluate and reflect on its impact.

Although the SBB does not introduce entirely new clinical practices, it brings together five evidence-based components into a care bundle designed to standardise care and reduce practice variation [9]. NPT was selected to guide our analysis as its mechanisms offer valuable insights into how the SBB can be effectively embedded and sustained as part of routine care, particularly in the context of a large-scale QI initiative.

Aim

The primary aim of this paper is to present findings from a mixed-methods evaluation exploring the views and experiences of site leads involved in embedding the SBB into practice across maternity services. The study used surveys to identify key implementation factors. Qualitative interviews underpinned by NPT, were then used to explore these findings in greater depth. This approach was used to address the following research questions:What factors (enablers and barriers) influenced the implementation of SBB in participating services?How do the design and conceptual framing of the care bundle approach (as applied in the SBB), along with the QI strategies used, influence its integration into routine maternity care?

Methods

Setting and design

This mixed-methods study was conducted across three Australian states (NSW, QLD and VIC) from September to December 2022. It forms part of a broader project evaluating the effectiveness of the SBB for reducing late gestation stillbirth in Australia. The SBB study protocol [16], which outlines the initiative and its evaluation, is briefly summarised here. Progressive implementation of the SBB over 3 years commenced from mid-2019. The commencement dates and implementation strategies varied across the three jurisdictions. The common goal was to integrate the SBB over a 12–24-month period following its launch within each jurisdiction. However, the ability to undertake QI work was significantly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to disruptions and delays in the planned implementation periods.

Surveys (closed-ended questions, quantitative data) and semi-structured interviews (open-ended questions, qualitative data) with SBB site leads were undertaken post-implementation of the SBB.

Participants and recruitment

A list of site leads from 84 maternity services across QLD, NSW and VIC was provided by the project teams of each respective state to the SBB Study Team. Site leads were defined as individuals responsible for overseeing and coordinating the implementation of the SBB within their respective maternity services, either independently or as part of a local project team. All site leads were invited to participate in the quantitative and/or qualitative component of the study via email, alongside a description of the study and a link to the online survey. Each service was asked to submit a single survey response to avoid duplication of fidelity and service usage data. Where services had large implementation teams or multiple site leads, collaborative completion was encouraged to ensure the response reflected the broader implementation experience. If collaboration was not feasible, the individual most involved in leading the SBB implementation was asked to respond. Consent to participate was implied by submitting a completed survey. Site leads and/or members of implementation project teams who wanted to participate in the qualitative component of the study were asked to submit their interest to the SBB Study Team via email, who then contacted them to schedule an interview time. All interview participants received a participant information sheet that outlined the purpose of the study, that participation was voluntary and withdrawal from the study would not affect their workplace relations. Verbal consent to record and transcribe each interview was obtained.

Data collection, analysis and management

Quantitative data (surveys)

Quantitative data were collected between August and December 2022 via a self-administered online survey using Checkbox (Checkbox Survey INC., Watertown, MA, USA). The survey was developed by a multi-disciplinary team of researchers and clinicians and was informed by: (i) findings from surveys conducted before the implementation of the SBB [17, 18], and (ii) evaluations undertaken as part of the UK Saving Babies Lives Care bundle [19]. The survey was designed to understand the fidelity of SBB implementation at each respective site and to uncover what helped and hindered the implementation process. It was comprised of five sections: (1) maternity service and site lead demographics, (2) SBB implementation fidelity, (3) service provision and training, (4) leadership and governance, and (5) quality improvement activities and approaches. The survey tool is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Responses were checked for duplicates and incompletes. Responses were included for analysis if demographic information was provided and more than 75% of the survey items were completed. IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyse descriptive statistics. Responses from Likert Scales were dichotomised to simplify analysis and enhance the interpretability of the data. Specifically, self-reported delivery of best practice recommendations was categorised as ‘reliably delivered’ (responses of 81–99% [Usually] and 100% [Every time]) versus ‘not reliably delivered’ (responses ranging from 0% [Never] to 80% [Frequently]). This approach allowed an overall implementation fidelity measure to be generated for clearer identification of recommended practices consistently embedded in care versus those requiring further implementation support. An element was reliably “implemented” if more than 80% ( > 0.8) of its recommendations were reliably delivered. Moderate implementation (between 0.5 and 0.8) suggested that reliable delivery was improving, while low implementation ( < 0.5) indicated that further improvement was needed. Due to restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic on carbon monoxide (CO) monitor roll out (as part of Element 1, smoking cessation support), delivery of best practice for this recommendation was excluded from the overall fidelity measure for Element 1. All missing values are reported within tables and figures.

Qualitative data (semi-structured interviews)

The study team developed an interview guide comprised of 14 open-ended questions designed to explore the enablers and barriers influencing SBB implementation (see Supplementary Material 2). The interview guide development was informed by survey findings and aimed to examine key topics in greater depth, including the interviewee’s role, characteristics of their service and project team, and factors affecting sustainable practice change. It also addressed the impact of COVID-19 on antenatal care delivery and SBB implementation. Additional questions explored perceived successes, challenges, and strategies for scaling up the initiative.

Interviews were conducted one-on-one following a semi-structured format to allow flexibility in capturing site leads’ experiences, in contrast to the more structured nature of the survey. Interviews were conducted by authors AL, CA and AP via videoconferencing platforms (Zoom or Microsoft Teams) or telephone. The median length of an interview was 42 minutes. Interviews were recorded with informed verbal consent, transcribed verbatim, de-identified, and exported into NVivo 12 for analysis.

Data saturation, defined by the recurrence of themes and lack of new data, guided the completion of recruitment. Researcher reflexivity was considered throughout the study (see Supplementary Material 3). Qualitative analysis followed a two-step process. In step one, to address our research questions and capture themes reflective of the site leads’ authentic experience, we utilised reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) [20]. Two coders (CA, AP) familiarised themselves with the data and developed initial codes. Themes and sub-themes were developed from the codes through iterative discussion among the research team. These themes were then mapped to NPT using a framework method approach in step two [21]. The four NPT constructs were given definitions that provided context for their role in SBB implementation. The themes identified in step one were then analysed against these within an analysis table (in Microsoft Excel). This combined approach enhanced analytical rigour by ensuring findings were both grounded in participant data and informed by theory.

Data management and consent

Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were assured their information would remain confidential. Study data was securely stored on password-protected drives on the Mater Research Institute-UQ network with access restricted to the research team.

Ethics

This study received human research ethics approval from the Metro North Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) in June 2019 (approval number: HREC/2019/MNHA/47709). This HREC and their review process of multi-centre research proposals have received certification from the NHMRC.

Understanding the SBB approach and purpose: sense making (coherence)

The NPT construct, coherence, was primarily reflected in Themes 1 and 2. This suggests that the process of ‘making sense’ of the SBB was a key mechanism for supporting the cohesive application of knowledge to practice.

Theme 1: nationally consistent resources and training

Sub-theme: training supported the cohesive application of knowledge to practice

Strong alignment with the NPT construct of commitment was evident across all themes except Theme 5, underscoring the importance of engagement in building shared ownership of SBB implementation across diverse service contexts.

Discussion

This study explored the views and experiences of site leads involved in implementing the SBB across maternity services in QLD, VIC, and NSW. Using a mixed-methods approach underpinned by NPT, the evaluation identified key factors that influenced the embedding of the SBB into routine antenatal care. These included leadership support, service capacity, interdisciplinary collaboration, and access to project-specific data. By examining how these factors influenced the uptake and sustainability of the SBBs five elements, the study offers practical insights into enablers of large-scale improvement and strengths within health services that can support sustained change.

Interdisciplinary collaboration was identified as a critical enabler; however, site leads consistently reported challenges in engaging key professional groups. While midwives were generally involved, engagement from obstetricians and general practitioners was varied, creating barriers to cohesive and standardised care, especially for elements such as FGR and TOB, where clinical decision-making is more complex and typically led by obstetricians. When obstetricians were actively engaged, they strengthened team cohesion and facilitated updates to clinical guidelines. These findings reflect broader challenges identified in the literature, where inconsistent stakeholder engagement and communication have been shown to hinder the adoption of clinical practice guidelines in maternal and neonatal settings [23]. Survey data from frontline healthcare professionals implementing the SBB also highlighted difficulties in discussing birth planning based on individualised stillbirth risk [18], reinforcing the need for stronger interdisciplinary collaboration.

This study’s theme of collaborative change, particularly fostering and sustaining buy-in, illustrates how consensus within and across teams is critical for embedding complex practices. These dynamics reflect the collective action construct of NPT, where operationalising change depends on the relational and organisational work required to enact new practices. The study demonstrated that when obstetricians were engaged and consensus was built, implementation was more consistent and sustainable. Conversely, inconsistent engagement created fragmentation, particularly in areas requiring nuanced clinical judgement. Site leads emphasised that personalised approaches, such as one-on-one meetings, targeted workshops, and customised communication materials, were essential for building support. Integrating SBB principles into obstetric registrar training programs may further strengthen engagement and promote sustained involvement from obstetric teams.

Resource limitations were a significant barrier to implementation. While a few services were able to secure partially funded roles to support project coordination, most leads reported being expected to take on implementation responsibilities alongside existing workloads, without additional time or financial support. This lack of dedicated resources led to role strain and reduced implementation fidelity, an issue identified from survey responses where only 32% of leads felt their service was adequately resourced, and just 17% believed their teams had sufficient time for QI activities. These findings align with qualitative accounts of staff struggling to balance competing demands, often resorting to ad-hoc solutions to enable participation in essential learning sessions. Consistent with Chapman et al.‘s findings in healthcare settings [24], these results underscore the need for structured support and ongoing education to successfully embed bundled care initiatives like the SBB.

Our findings have important implications for strengthening QI approaches in maternity care. While this study did not assess clinical outcomes, no clear advantage was observed between those participating in a full QIC and those using a pared-back approach. Both approaches faced similar barriers and enablers related to time, resources, and staff capacity. This suggests that the success of QI efforts may depend less on the specific model and more on the underlying readiness, engagement and support within services. Notably, an increase in ultrasound capacity was reported following implementation of the SBB, in relation to FGR and DFM elements. This aligns with findings from the UKs bundle, where staff reported increased demand for ultrasound scans, and service data showed a measurable rise in the number of scans per pregnancy [12]. These findings reinforce the importance of anticipating and planning for increased service demands when implementing stillbirth reduction initiatives and highlight how targeted QI efforts can accelerate existing service improvements when they are aligned with clinical priorities and supported by adequate resourcing.

While the utility of ultrasound in improving perinatal outcomes was not the focus of this study and was not raised within participant interviews, we acknowledge the broader implications of increased ultrasound use, such as resource strain and potential over-intervention, are important to consider. For example, recent comparative evaluation of risk stratification pathways for predicting small-for-gestational-age (SGA) found that while the SBB pathway achieved higher detection rates, it also resulted in higher false positive rates, increasing demand for follow-up ultrasounds and potentially contributing to increased intervention rates [25].The SBB rollout acted as a catalyst for broader system-level improvements. Clinical dashboards were developed and refined across QLD and NSW, providing facility-level data for benchmarking and performance monitoring. The Quality Improvement Data System (QIDS) Maternity Intelligence System (MatIQ), developed in NSW, now provides near real-time maternal and newborn data insights to clinicians across all public maternity hospitals. Drawing weekly birth data from eMaternity and Cerner systems, MatIQ enables customised dashboards and trend visualisations that support local practice improvement and clinical decision-making [26]. While MatIQ has significantly enhanced post-implementation monitoring and sustainability, it was developed during the rollout and was not available to implementing sites at the time of this study, so its benefits are not reflected in site lead interviews. At the national level, the launch of the SBB Dashboard in 2025 through the Women’s Healthcare Australasia (WHA) platform marked an important step towards broader benchmarking and performance monitoring (for WHA-member hospitals, > 155) [27]. However, existing QI platforms are not integrated with electronic medical records and rely on manual data entry, limiting their efficiency and scalability. NSWs approach demonstrates the potential of embedding automated, interpretable data systems into clinical workflows. Extending similar infrastructure across other states would be a valuable direction for health departments aiming to strengthen QI capacity and sustain improvements in stillbirth prevention.

The COVID-19 pandemic had a multifaceted impact on implementation, as reflected in the data collected during this study. COVID-related challenges were interwoven across all aspects of implementation, influencing each stage of the process. Direct disruptions included delayed timelines, loss of momentum, and reduced in-person attendance at maternity services, which limited opportunities for education and engagement with women. Indirectly, staff redeployment, burnout, and widespread change fatigue constrained capacity for QI and staff training. These challenges undermined key mechanisms of normalisation. While shared understanding and commitment (coherence and cognitive participation) were evident among teams, the ability to enact and sustain change (collective action) was compromised by resource limitations and service instability. Similarly, access to data for tracking progress (reflexive monitoring) was hindered, affecting teams’ capacity to appraise and adapt QI efforts. The pandemic exposed limitations in health service infrastructure [28] and underscored the need for context-responsive implementation approaches that can withstand shifting priorities and operational instability.

Building on our earlier research with women and healthcare professionals [18, 29], this study adds a vital health systems perspective, highlighting how leadership, QI capacity, and access to data enabled the successful implementation of SBB recommendations, even amid unprecedented challenges. It also reveals persistent barriers and underscores the value of shared understanding, consistent messaging, and readiness for change.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its systems-level perspective, which highlights practical enablers that can inform future QI initiatives. The integration of qualitative and quantitative data enhances the depth of analysis and provides a nuanced understanding of implementation fidelity across diverse service contexts.

However, several limitations should be noted. The study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, particularly in assessing implementation fidelity. The small sample size and purposive sampling limit the generalisability beyond participating sites. Limiting survey responses to one per site helped ensure accurate reporting of fidelity and service usage data but reduced the diversity of perspectives from implementation team members. Additionally, the study focused on the three jurisdictions (QLD, NSW, VIC) involved in the initial SBB rollout, so findings may not fully reflect experiences in other regions. Finally, while the study explored implementation processes, it did not assess clinical outcomes, which constrains conclusions about the direct impact of the SBB on maternal and perinatal health.

Conclusion

This study offers practical insights into the implementation of the SBB across maternity services in QLD, VIC, and NSW, highlighting how its five elements were embedded into routine antenatal care. While health leadership endorsement and collaborative efforts supported uptake, limited resources, time constraints, inconsistent engagement across professional groups and variable access to data for tracking progress posed significant challenges. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted implementation efforts, delaying timelines, reducing staff capacity, and limiting engagement, highlighting the need for flexible strategies that can adapt to service instability and evolving priorities. Despite these barriers, the SBB fostered a strong sense of shared purpose and acted as a catalyst for broader system improvements, including the development of clinical dashboards and national benchmarking tools. These findings offer valuable learnings for improving maternity care and guiding the implementation of other complex health system improvement initiatives. To build on these gains, future efforts should prioritise sustained investment in QI infrastructure, tailored support for interdisciplinary collaboration, and mechanisms for ongoing education and data-driven monitoring. These factors will be essential to embed and scale evidence-based practices that reduce stillbirth and improve maternity care outcomes.
