Reporting of Rurality, Deprivation, and Socioeconomic Characteristics in Cancer Clinical Trials
Authors: Joseph M. Unger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow frequently are geographic, socioeconomic, and core demographic characteristics reported in randomized clinical trial primary publications in oncology?FindingsIn this systematic review of 441 US-based randomized clinical trial primary reports, reporting of age and sex was common, and reporting of race and ethnicity has increased substantially over time. In contrast, reporting of rurality, income, education, insurance, and area-level deprivation was rare or wholly absent.MeaningGeographic and socioeconomic characteristics are rarely reported in primary cancer clinical publications, limiting the ability to assess generalizability and interpret findings across populations.

Introduction

Cancer outcomes in the US are determined in part by both geography and socioeconomic context. Patients with cancer from rural areas have demonstrably worse outcomes due to factors such as poor access to prevention and screening, limitations in availability of oncology care providers and clinics, and travel burden.1,2,3 Area-level measures of socioeconomic deprivation are associated with worse cancer outcomes in population studies and even among patients receiving protocol-directed care.4,5 In a large 2021 study that examined 55 phase II and III trials,5 residence in the most deprived areas was associated with meaningfully worse overall, progression-free, and cancer-specific survival despite access to standardized, protocol-guided therapy in a trial.

Randomized cancer clinical trials provide the primary evidence for identifying effective new cancer treatments. The conduct of cancer clinical research serves as a bridge between the extant cancer population and the impact and diffusion of new cancer treatments (Figure 1). Cancer care discovery begins in the early drug discovery and development phase, in which new agents are identified and tested in phase I trials. Promising agents and therapies advance to comparative testing in randomized trials. The design, monitoring, and analysis of randomized clinical trials have developed over decades.6,7,8,9,10 In recent years, the representation of trial cohorts has been recognized as a vital aspect of trial interpretation.11,12,13,14,15 Representation in trials informs interpretation, which, in turn, influences public and private coverage decisions, physician and patient treatment choices, and, ultimately, the extent to which newly proven treatments are adopted.16,17,18 Thus, the conduct of cancer clinical trials serves as a mediating process between individuals with cancer and access to newly proven treatments, and understanding trial cohort representation is central to this process. This framework informed the design of the present review by identifying key domains of patient characteristics relevant to trial representation, including geographic, socioeconomic, and core demographic factors, and guided variable selection. Adequate characterization of trial populations is essential for both generalizability (ie, applicability to the source population) and transportability (ie, applicability to external populations and settings).19,20,21

Conceptual Model

Regulatory and advocacy guidance has recognized the importance of characterizing clinical trial cohorts to aid interpretation. In 2022, the US FDA issued draft guidance recommending sponsor action plans to better ensure that trial cohorts reflect intended target populations.17 Researchers and policymakers called for improved representation in trials, arguing that diversity is vital for interpreting benefits and risks in across clinical populations.22 Importantly, this call to action extended beyond race and ethnicity to broader social and structural factors, such as rurality and socioeconomic deprivation, which influence access, delivery, and outcomes.16,17,23 These associations are well documented in US and international settings.24,25,26,27

Primary clinical trial publications are a widely disseminated source of evidence informing regulatory decisions, clinical guidelines, and standards of care. However, prior work has indicated that reporting of key social factors, most notably race and ethnicity, is poor.13 Comparatively little attention has been paid to geographic and socioeconomic characteristics. This study examined reporting of geographic, socioeconomic, and core demographic variables in primary publications of randomized cancer clinical trials.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Framework

This systematic review evaluated reporting of geographic (rurality, area-level deprivation), socioeconomic (income, education, insurance), and core demographic (age, sex, race, ethnicity) variables in primary randomized cancer clinical trial reports. The study followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines. A prespecified conceptual framework guided identification, abstraction, and synthesis, and study selection was documented with a PRISMA flow diagram. This study used study-level data from published studies and was not considered human participants research; therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required under the Common Rule (45 CFR 46.102).

Protocol and Registration

This review was not prospectively registered, and a formal protocol was not prepared. The study was designed as a descriptive systematic review of reporting practices rather than a meta-analysis of treatment effects. Eligibility criteria and abstraction procedures were defined prior to screening and applied consistently throughout the review.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A systematic PubMed search identified eligible cancer clinical trial reports published in high-impact general medical and oncology journals (The New England Journal of Medicine, Journal of Clinical Oncology, JAMA, JAMA Oncology, The Lancet, The Lancet Oncology, and Annals of Oncology). Searches were limited to 2020 through 2025 (inclusive) and used a comprehensive set of cancer-related keywords while excluding nonprimary publication types (eg, reviews, editorials, case reports). All cancer types were included. The full query is provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they reported primary results of randomized cancer clinical treatment trials, included enrollments from at least 1 US site, and were published as a full peer-reviewed article in 1 of the 7 journals. Studies were excluded if they were nonrandomized, were not primary trial reports (eg, secondary analyses or follow-up publications), were conducted entirely outside the US, or represented duplicate or flashback publications.

Study Selection

Study selection followed a prespecified 2-stage process: title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review. Records identified via PubMed were retrieved and screened using a predefined algorithm. Initial exclusion of clearly ineligible publications was performed by the investigator. Large language model–based tools (ChatGPT and Claude) were used only for triage and consistency checks using bibliographic information (eg, titles and abstracts); final inclusion decisions were made by the investigator, consistent with PRISMA guidance on automation tools. No full-text articles, figures, tables, or other copyrighted materials were entered into these tools.

Remaining articles underwent manual abstract and full-text review to confirm eligibility. Reasons for full-text exclusion were prospectively documented, and uncertainties were resolved by iterative review using the prespecified criteria.

Data Extraction and Classification

Data were extracted using a standardized abstraction form developed prior to full-text review. For each included trial, information was manually abstracted on trial characteristics (publication year, journal, cancer type, intervention modality, trial phase, and sponsor type) and the reporting of 15 geographic, socioeconomic, and core sociodemographic variables. Race and ethnicity were considered both as a binary (ie, whether a trial reported any race or ethnicity categories) and by category (American Indian and Alaskan Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, White, multiple races, or other). Both full-text articles and associated supplemental materials were reviewed. Reporting was coded as present or absent based on explicit cohort characterization in the main text, tables, figures, or appendices of the primary publication. Abstraction was guided by prespecified definitions and applied consistently across studies.

To assess abstraction reliability, a random sample of 20 included studies was independently reabstracted across all 15 primary characteristics, yielding 300 item-level abstractions. Concordance was calculated at the item level, and discrepancies were reviewed and resolved.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was the proportion of trials reporting the presence of a given geographic, socioeconomic, or core demographic variable. For each characteristic, observed reporting proportions and corresponding 95% Wilson binomial confidence intervals were calculated. To assess temporal trends, logistic regression models were used. Because outcomes were trial-level binary indicators with no within-study variance, meta-analytic pooling was not performed. Secondary analyses used negative binomial regression to assess the number of reported race and ethnicity categories by sponsor type, time period, and their interaction, with results reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% CIs. Because this review characterized reporting practices rather than treatment effects, conventional assessments of study-level risk of bias and reporting bias were not applicable, and no formal bias assessment was performed.

Results

Study Selection

The PubMed search identified 6681 unique records; 5233 records were excluded at initial screening, leaving 1447 articles for full-text review (Figure 2). Following full-text assessment, 1006 trials were excluded, most commonly because they were conducted wholly outside the US (447 trials), not randomized (297 trials), or not primary trial reports (178 trials). Additional exclusions included nontreatment trials (77 trials) and flashback publications (7 trials).

PRISMA Flow Diagram of Study Identification and Selection

Flashback publications were articles that rereported previously published primary trial results without new randomized outcomes and were excluded to avoid double counting. US-based trials were defined as trials that included enrollment from at least 1 US-based site or institution.

The final analytic cohort comprised 441 randomized cancer clinical trial primary article publications included in the quantitative analyses of sociodemographic reporting (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). In a validation sample of 20 randomly selected studies, concordance was 99.7% (299 of 300 data items).

Sample Characteristics

Annual study publications varied between 61 to 85 per year (Table). Most trials were published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology (153 [34.7%]) or the New England Journal of Medicine (101 [22.9%]). One-third of trials were masked (142 [32.2%]), and 322 (73.0%) were Phase 3. The most common disease categories were breast (78 [17.7%]), hematologic (65 [14.7%]), and lung (62 [14.1%]). Most trials reported on systemic therapy interventions (351 [79.6%]), with 71 (16.1%) reporting on multimodality therapies. The majority of trials were industry sponsored (309 [70.1%]) and were conducted internationally (360 [81.6%]). Among international trials, 25% had less than 10% US participation, 31% had 10% to 25%, and 43% had more than 25% participation.

Reporting of Geographic and Socioeconomic Variables

Among the 441 eligible primary cancer clinical trial reports, reporting of age and sex was virtually universal, with age reported in 100% of trials and sex in 99.8% (440 trials) (Figure 3; eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Reporting of race and/or ethnicity was common, with 365 trials (82.8%) reporting at least 1 race or ethnicity category. However, reporting of race and ethnicity varied substantially across specific categories. White, Asian, and Black races were reported in 356 (80.7%), 330 (74.8%), and 320 (72.6%) trials, respectively, while Hispanic ethnicity (140 [31.8%]), American Indian or Alaskan Native (129 [29.3%]), Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander (75 [17.0%]), and multiple race groups (68 [15.4%]) were reported in a minority of trial reports.

In contrast to the common reporting of core demographic variables, the reporting of geographic and socioeconomic variables was almost nonexistent. No trials reported participant rurality, area-level deprivation, or income. Two trials (0.5%) reported education28,29 and 1 trial (0.2%) reported insurance status.30 The 3 trials that reported education or insurance status were all federally sponsored cooperative group trials; beyond that, no subgroup demonstrated routine or systematic reporting of geographic or socioeconomic characteristics.

Reporting of Race and Ethnicity Categories Over Time and by Sponsor

Reporting of individual race and ethnicity categories increased over the study period (Figure 4). Reporting of any race or ethnicity increased from 62.3% in 2020 (38 of 62 trials) to 93.9% in 2025 (77 of 82 trials) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). This observation is consistent with the emphasis on race and ethnicity reporting highlighted by the FDA’s 2022 draft guidance.11 To explore this further, race and ethnicity reporting was grouped into 2 periods: 2020-2021 vs 2022-2024. For nearly all individual race and ethnicity categories, reporting frequencies were statistically significantly higher in the 2022 to 2025 period than in 2021 to 2022 (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). The proportion of trials reporting any race or ethnicity category increased from 65.7% (94 of 143 trials) before 2022 to 90.9% (271 of 298 trials) during or after 2022, representing more than a 5-fold increase in the odds (odds ratio, 5.23; 95% CI, 3.10-8.85; P < .001).

Patterns of race and ethnicity reporting differed by sponsor. Across 441 primary cancer clinical trial reports, federally or academically sponsored trials reported more race and ethnicity categories than industry-sponsored trials (mean [SD], 4.07 [2.14] vs 3.51 [2.04]; P = .01). In negative binomial models, this corresponded to a 19% higher number of reported categories (IRR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.07-1.32; P = .001). Reporting also increased over time, with trials published during or after 2022 reporting 64% more categories than earlier trials (IRR, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.46-1.84; P < .001). The interaction between sponsor type and time period was not statistically significant (IRR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.65-1.04; P = .11), indicating that increases over time occurred across both sponsor categories.

Discussion

In this framework-guided systematic review of 441 randomized cancer clinical trial primary reports published between 2020 and 2025, geographic and socioeconomic characteristics were rarely reported. No trial publications reported participant rurality, income, or area-level deprivation, and few reported education and insurance. In contrast, age and sex were nearly universally reported, and race and ethnicity reporting improved markedly, especially after 2022. Although academic or federally sponsored trials reported more race and ethnicity categories, increases in race or ethnicity reporting after 2022 were observed for both sponsor categories. These findings highlight substantial progress in race and ethnicity reporting, while demonstrating the near-complete omission of geographic and socioeconomic characteristics in trial publications.

This study examined reporting of geographic, socioeconomic, and core demographic variables in randomized clinical trial primary publications rather than the representation of these groups. Although reporting does not ensure representative enrollment, it is necessary to evaluate whether trial cohorts reflect target populations. Our findings align with recent regulatory and consensus guidance emphasizing that adequate characterization of enrolled populations is essential for interpreting trial results and assessing their applicability to real-world settings.11,16,17 Moreover, expanded reporting may help broaden prevailing conceptions of representation beyond age, sex, and race to encompass geographic and socioeconomic context, advancing a more inclusive conceptualization of the social, economic, and structural drivers of disparities and cancer risk and helping to reduce barriers to trial participation for all patient populations. CONSORT guidelines have traditionally emphasized core demographic characteristics such as age, sex, and race and/or ethnicity,31 whereas 2025 updates32 recognized broader baseline characteristics, including place of residence, education, and socioeconomic status. Despite this, reporting of geographic and socioeconomic factors remains uncommon. Efforts to improve measurement and reporting may include regulatory guidance, trial registry standards, journal requirements, and consensus recommendations from professional societies and advocacy organizations.

In the US, patients residing in rural or socioeconomically deprived areas experience consistently worse cancer survival than their urban and less deprived counterparts. Population-based studies have shown higher cancer mortality rates, later-stage diagnosis, and lower receipt of guideline-concordant therapy among individuals living in rural or high-deprivation regions, even after adjustment for individual-level clinical factors.2,33,34 These disparities are driven by structural barriers, including travel distance, limited oncology workforce availability, and insurance instability, that directly affect treatment across the care continuum.35,36,37,38 Despite these patterns, geographic and area-level socioeconomic characteristics are rarely incorporated into clinical trial reporting, representing a critical gap in trial design and interpretation, particularly as geographic disparities continue to widen in the US and internationally.2,39,40

The socioeconomic factors insurance status, income, and educational attainment are strong and consistent predictors of cancer outcomes in the US, influencing stage at diagnosis, treatment adherence, and survival. Patients who are uninsured, publicly insured, or have insurance disruptions experience higher cancer mortality and lower access to definitive therapy compared with privately insured patients.41,42,43 Lower income and educational attainment have been independently associated with later-stage presentation, reduced participation in cancer screening, and poorer survival across multiple tumor types.44,45 These socioeconomic gradients persist even after accounting for race and ethnicity, reflecting their additive contributions to cancer disparities.46 The absence of these measures in trial reporting limits the ability to interpret trial findings across socioeconomic strata, both within the US and internationally.39,47,48

In contrast to the near absence of geographic and socioeconomic reporting, age and sex were reported in nearly all cancer trial publications, and race and ethnicity reporting was common and increased substantially after 2021. Routine reporting of age and sex reflects their central role in trial eligibility, treatment tolerance, and interpretation of outcomes.31,49 Race and ethnicity reporting has received increasing emphasis given persistent disparities in cancer incidence and survival.29,50 White, Asian, and Black race categories were reported in most trials, while other categories were less consistently reported; however, all improved over time, especially after 2021, across sponsor types. This increase likely reflects recent regulatory guidance and advocacy efforts to improve representation.11,51 Taken together, these patterns suggest a shift in reporting norms for race and ethnicity. However, despite broader recognition of social and structural determinants of cancer risk, geographic and socioeconomic characteristics remain largely unreported. Framed within the conceptual model of cancer care discovery and diffusion, this omission limits the ability to interpret trial findings in geographic and socioeconomic contexts, which complicates coverage and treatment decisions and ultimately slows the adoption of new treatments (Figure 1).

Limitations

This systematic review has limitations. The review focused on primary trial publications from high-impact journals, which have an outsized influence on how trial evidence is translated into practice. However, these findings may not generalize fully to reporting norms in the broader oncology research. The focus on reporting practices in primary trial publications does not preclude the possibility that some variables, even if absent from trial reports, may have been collected and reported elsewhere, such as trial registries or regulatory submissions. For characteristics that are not routinely collected, additional efforts will be required to incorporate these variables into trial design and data collection. Also, this review was conducted by a single investigator, which may introduce the potential for misclassification or omission despite the use of prespecified inclusion criteria, standardized abstraction procedures, and manual verification; however, a validation exercise demonstrated high item-level concordance, mitigating concern about item-level misclassification. Furthermore, initial screening was AI-assisted, which may introduce study-level misclassification; however, this approach is consistent with PRISMA guidance on the use of automation tools in study selection, and evaluations from 2024 have demonstrated acceptable performance when such tools are used to assist human review.52 Additionally, temporal analyses reflect associations with publication year and cannot establish causal links between regulatory guidance or advocacy efforts and changes in reporting practices. Finally, the analysis was restricted to US-based randomized cancer treatment trials published in high-impact journals, which may limit generalizability to nonrandomized studies, trials conducted exclusively outside the US, or publications in lower-impact venues. Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across trial subgroups and analytic approaches supports the robustness of the observed reporting patterns.

Conclusions

In this systematic review, a persistent absence of reporting of geographic and socioeconomic characteristics was found for randomized clinical trial findings published in high-impact journals. These findings demonstrate a disconnect between the routine reporting of core demographic characteristics and the near-complete omission of geographic and socioeconomic context in primary cancer clinical trial publications. Although reporting of race and ethnicity improved in recent years, these advances have not extended to variables such as rurality, area-level deprivation, insurance status, income, or education, despite their well-established relevance to cancer outcomes and access to care. Because primary randomized trial reports serve as the evidentiary foundation for regulatory approvals, clinical guidelines, and treatment decision-making, the omission of key geographic and socioeconomic variables may propagate downstream limitations in the interpretation, generalizability, and application of trial findings. Improving the transparency and consistency of reporting in primary trial publications is essential to ensuring that advances in cancer therapeutics are evaluated and implemented in ways that reflect the populations most affected by cancer disparities, ultimately enhancing the adoption of new treatments into clinical practice for patients across all geographic, socioeconomic, and demographic backgrounds.
