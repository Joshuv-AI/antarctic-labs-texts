Abstract
We consider the ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}_{2} -optimal feedback control problem, for the case in which the plant is passive with bounded 𝕃 2 \mathbb{L}_{2} gain, and the feedback law is constrained to be output-strictly passive. In this circumstance, we show that this problem distills to a convex optimal control problem, in which the optimization domain is the associated Youla parameter for the closed-loop system. This enables the globally-optimal controller to be solved as an infinite-dimensional but convex optimization. Near-optimal solutions may be found through the finite-dimensional convex truncation of this infinite-dimensional domain. The idea is demonstrated on a simple vibration suppression example.



I
Introduction

We consider a particular class of linear feedback control problems, illustrated in Figure
1
.
The plant
\mathcal{P}
constitutes the linear time-invariant (LTI) mapping
\{w,u\}\mapsto\{z,y\}
and is assumed to be in
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, the channel
u\mapsto y
is assumed to be passive.
We seek the feedback controller
\mathcal{K}
that minimizes the standard
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance objective for closed-loop mapping
w\mapsto z
, over the set
\mathbb{K}
of all linear output-strictly-passive (OSP) mappings.

This problem is motivated by technological design issues that arise in several areas of engineering, including vibration suppression and robotics.
A vast array of technologies exist for imposition of feedback in mechanical systems, for the purpose of response suppression.
Some of the simplest technologies are comprised of entirely of passive components, for which the imposed “feedback” is physically realized directly by the constitutive laws of these components.
In many circumstances passive control technology is the most practical and preferred, as it may be implemented without the need of a power supply or electronics of any kind.
Furthermore, the closed-loop negative feedback connection of two systems is bounded-input-bounded-output (BIBO) stable, if the plant has finite
\mathbb{L}_{2}
gain (i.e., is in
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
) and is passive, and if the feedback law is OSP
[
1
]
.
As such, even if considerable uncertainty exists in
\mathcal{P}
, if
u\mapsto y
is known to be passive with finite gain, then an OSP-constrained
\mathcal{K}
is guaranteed to be stability-robust.

Passive control technology has been widely applied in mechanical and structural engineering for over a century
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Passive electrical networks have also been embedded into mechanical vibratory systems for the purpose of response suppression, as electrical shunt circuits connected to electromechanical transducers
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
Passive networks can be optimized via network synthesis, both for electrical
[
10
]
as well as mechanical realizations
[
11
]
.
It is a classical result
[
12
]
that a muliport LTI network is passive if and only if the matrix transfer function characterizing its driving-point admittance (i.e., the transfer function for channel
u\mapsto y
) is Positive Real (PR).
Furthermore, any rational PR admittance can be realized via a finite network of elementary linear passive components (i.e., inductors, capacitors, resistors, and transformers for electrical networks, and springs, dashpots, inerters, and levers for mechanical networks).
The classical passive network synthesis methods of Brune
[
13
]
, Bott and Duffin
[
14
]
, and Darlington
[
15
]
can be used to find such a network realization
[
10
]
.
Further restriction to networks of dissipative passive devices implies further restriction of the feasibility domain to Strict Positive Real (SPR) rational transfer functions
[
16
]
.

On the other technological extreme, the use of active (i.e., externally-powered) sensors and actuators allows for more sophisticated control laws, nonlocal measurement feedback, real-time adaptation, and higher control authority.
In choosing a feedback technology (e.g., active vs passive) for a given application, the increased financial and logistical demands associated with active systems must be justified by a tangible improvement in the performance they achieve relative to passive systems.
In order to make this justification convincing, the feedback law designed for each candidate technology should be optimized to render the best performance achievable, constrained to that particular technology’s physical limitations.
The optimal feedback laws for active and passive technologies, when respectively optimized, may turn out to be rather dissimilar.

Block diagram for feedback system under consideration

Early work related to this idea emerged in the early 1990s in the area of vibration suppression of aerospace structures, by MacMartin and Hall
[
17
]
. In that work, the motivation was to suppress vibrations in large-scale and lightweight space structures due to exogenous disturbances.
The underlying heuristic strategy was to achieve favorable vibration suppression performance by optimizing (i.e., maximizing) worst-case net power absorption at the control actuators. evaluated over an uncertainty domain of possible disturbance spectra.
(Because the resultant optimal
\mathcal{K}
is energy-absorptive at all frequencies, its transfer function
\hat{K}(s)
is guaranteed to be PR.)
Starting from this objective, the authors exploited passivity of the plant (i.e., the vibrating structure) to frame the problem equivalently as an unconstrained
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
optimal control problem.
They published a companion paper with an experimental validation
[
18
]
, but ultimately this line of inquiry appears to have been subsequently abandoned.
One possible reason for this is that the heuristic motivation can lead to unfavorable results, and can in some circumstances result in a closed-loop response which has worse disturbance rejection performance than the uncontrolled system.
This is due to the fact that the feedback controller not only influences the power absorbed by the actuators, but also indirectly influences the power injected into the plant by the disturbance.
As such, a controller designed to maximize energy absorption may achieve optimality primarily by inducing the disturbance to inject more power into the plant than in the open-loop case.

In contrast to this early work, the vast majority of the research on this subject has treated the passivity of
\mathcal{K}
as a constraint, to be imposed on the optimization of some other objective.
Particular attention has focused on the optimization of
\mathcal{K}
for an
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance measure, with associated Laplace-domain transfer function
\hat{K}(s)
constrained to be strictly positive-real (SPR).
The short but seminal paper by Lozano-Leal and Joshi
[
19
]
introduced a technique which, by appropriate choice of the weights in the
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance measure, guarantees to produce a SPR feedback law without the need to impose the SPR constraint explicitly.
The technique has been advanced and leveraged in numerous other studies, including
[
20
]
, which extends it to mixed
\mathbb{H}_{2}
/
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
problems.
However, the problem type in
[
19
]
arises only in very special circumstances, beyond which the SPR-constrained
\mathbb{H}_{2}
problem has no (known) closed-form solution.
Indeed, it is even unclear under which circumstances the optimal transfer function is rational
[
21
]
.

Studies by Gapski and Geromel
[
22
]
and Shimomura
[
23
,
24
]
and more recently Forbes
[
25
]
, used Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI) techniques to develop computationally-efficient design approaches for SPR-constrained

\mathbb{H}_{2}
optimal control problems.
In all these approaches, the problem is made tractable by exploiting the Certainty-Equivalence principle, and assuming either the Kalman-Bucy filter (in the case of
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
) or the certainty-equivalent full-state feedback gain (as in
[
25
]
) is the same as for the unconstrained
\mathbb{H}_{2}
problem.
This results in a significant reduction in the size and complexity of the optimization, but also introduces conservatism, as it is not clear that the original problem exhibits certainty-equivalence, or indeed separation of any kind.
And even with this added conservatism, the optimization problem is still nonconvex, although convexity may be recovered through the introduction of further conservatism through the use of a common Lyapunov variable
[
22
]
.
Alternatively, the reduced nonconvex problem can be solved for a local optimum, e.g., via the use of iterative convex over-bounding
[
23
,
24
]
.

In the general case the SPR-constrained optimization of
\mathcal{K}
under the
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance measure can be framed as a bilinear matrix inequality (BMI) problem.
This class of nonconvex optimization problems is computationally expensive but can be solved using various techniques.
In
[
26
]
, for example, the BMI problem is solved in the widely-available YALMIP semidefinite programming platform, with application to the design of passive suspensions systems.
In
[
27
]
, the same basic problem is approached, in the context of general vibrating structural systems.
In that study it is noted that the BMI formulation has a further disadvantage, beyond being computationally expensive.
Specifically, for a given
\hat{K}
its optimization domain contains an infinite number of equivalent realizations, all of which yield the same performance.
A methodology is used in
[
27
]
to eliminate equivalent realizations, with the resultant technique involving products of five matrix variables.
This nonconvex optimization problem is then solved using iterative convex overbounding techniques.
Other studies have considered the problem of PR and SPR-constrained feedback design, over a domain that is restricted to transfer functions with a certain number of, or configuration of, components.
For example, in
[
28
]
, PR-constrained feedback laws of restricted complexity are optimized, and it is shown that the necessary and sufficient conditions for satisfaction of the PR constraint can be simplified over this restricted domain.

The present paper revisits the
\mathbb{H}_{2}
optimal control problem with a passivity constraint on
\mathcal{K}
.
We show that if
\mathcal{P}
is in
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
and channel
u\mapsto y
is passive, then the problem may be solved without conservatism as an infinite-dimensional convex optimization, through a change of variables.
Specifically, the domain for the convex optimization is the closed-loop Youla parameter
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
This approach enables the globally-optimal
\mathcal{K}
to be approached to arbitrary accuracy as the limit of a sequence of finite-dimensional optimizations, with each term in the sequence optimized over a successively larger-dimensional parametric domain.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
II
develops the precise optimization problem to be solved.
This includes a summary of the assumptions and conventions made, and the original statement of the problem in continuous-time.
Section
III
presents one computationally-simple approach to obtain a sub-optimal solution to the optimization problem, which is based on certainty-equivalence.
Section
IV
provides the main result, which is the reformulation of the problem as a convex optimization over the infinite-dimensional domain of the closed-loop Youla parameter.
Section
V
adapts the result from the previous section to a finite-dimensional optimization methodology that asymptotically approaches the global optimum as the dimension of the domain is increased.
Section
VI
provides two examples in the context of vibration suppression.
Section
VII
discusses various ways in which the ideas presented here can be extended to other related problems. Finally, Section
VIII
contains some brief conclusions.

II-A

Plant Model

Assumption 1

We presume that plant
\mathcal{P}:\{w,u\}\mapsto\{z,y\}
is characterized by the
n_{P}
-order linear time-invariant transfer functions, i.e.,

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{P}_{wz}&\hat{P}_{uz}\\
\hat{P}_{wy}&\hat{P}_{uy}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{l|ll}A&B_{w}&B_{u}\\
\hline\cr C_{z}&0&D_{uz}\\
C_{y}&0&D_{uy}\end{array}\right]

where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{P}\times n_{P}}
,
B_{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{P}\times n_{w}}
,
B_{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{P}\times n_{u}}
,
C_{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{z}\times n_{P}}
,
C_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{u}\times n_{P}}
,
D_{uz}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{z}\times n_{u}}
, and

Remark 1

Note that we assume the mapping

is strictly proper.
This is in contrast, for example, to the standard assumptions of the
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
optimal control problem, for which one must assume

Assumption 2

Plant

has bounded

such that for all

\|y\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}^{2}+\|z\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}^{2}\leqslant\gamma^{2}\left(\|w\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}^{2}+\|u\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}^{2}\right).

(10)

Remark 2

It is a standard result that Assumption
2
is equivalent to the statement that
\hat{P}_{ij}\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
for
\{i,j\}\in\{w,u\}\times\{z,y\}
.

Assumption 3

Plant
\mathcal{P}
is such that mapping

is passive, i.e., there exists a function
\beta:\mathbb{L}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for all
\{w,u\}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}\times\mathbb{L}_{2}
,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[u^{H}(t)y(t)+y^{H}(t)u(t)\right]dt\geqslant-\beta(w).

(11)

Remark 3

It is a standard result that, together with Assumption
2
, Assumption
3
is equivalent to the statement that
\hat{P}_{uy}
is PR, i.e., it is analytic in
\mathbb{C}_{>0}
and

\hat{P}^{H}_{uy}(s)+\hat{P}_{uy}(s)\succeq 0,\ \ \forall s\in\mathbb{C}_{>0}.

(12)

Furthermore, this is true if and only if
\hat{P}_{uy}(s)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}_{>0}
and

\hat{P}_{uy}^{H}(j\omega)+\hat{P}_{uy}(j\omega)\succeq 0,\ \ \forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)

Definition 1

We denote

as the set of all plants

Feedback Domain

Definition 2

\hat{K}^{H}(s)+\hat{K}(s)-\epsilon\hat{K}^{H}(s)\hat{K}(s)\succeq 0,\ \forall s\in\mathbb{C}_{>0}.

Furthermore, this is true if and only if
\hat{K}(s)
is analytic and uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{C}_{>0}
and

\hat{K}^{H}(j\omega)+\hat{K}(j\omega)-\epsilon\hat{K}^{H}(j\omega)\hat{K}(j\omega)\succeq 0,\ \forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

We note that constraint

[
32
]

We note that

.
(Specifically the

Consequently criterion

II-C

Problem Statement

The closed-loop system mapping
\mathcal{T}_{wz}:w\mapsto z
is characterized by transfer function
\hat{T}_{wz}
, as

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{wz}

\displaystyle=\hat{P}_{wz}-\hat{P}_{uz}\hat{K}[I+\hat{P}_{uy}\hat{K}]^{-1}\hat{P}_{wy}.

(17)

The feedback-passivity-constrained
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
optimal control problem can be framed as the following optimization:

\text{OP1}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
\text{Minimize:}&J\triangleq\|\hat{T}_{wz}\|_{\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}^{2}\\
\text{Domain:}&\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(18)

III
A Suboptimal Solution

This section provides a straightforward methodology to find a suboptimal solution to OP1.
Although it does not achieve the optimal performance, this technique may achieve acceptable performance with considerably less design effort.
Additionally, its solution will be used later in the paper as a “reference” solution for the determination of the true optimum.

The design approach we consider here exploits certainty-equivalence, and in particular, the reduced-order Kalman-Bucy filter. This topic is is not new (see, e.g., the classical work of Friedland
[
33
,
34
]
), but the variant of the result we use here relaxes the assumptions than these prior results, and consequently a proof is provided for completeness. Following this, the design of the certainty-equivalent full-state feedback law is described.

To formulate the problem, we frame it in the stochastic context, in which
w(t)
is considered to be white noise with spectral intensity
\Phi_{w}=I
. The objective of OP1 in (
18
) is then equivalent to the minimization of
\mathcal{E}\{z^{T}z\}
, where
\mathcal{E}\{\cdot\}
denotes the expectation in stationarity.

III-A

The Reduced-Order Kalman-Bucy Filter

For the realization of
\mathcal{P}
in (
9
), let the internal state of the system be denoted
x
, and presume that at time
t
, the information

\mathbb{Y}(t)\triangleq\left\{y(\tau):\tau<t\right\}

(19)

is available.
Let
\xi(t)
be the expectation of
x(t)
, given
\mathbb{Y}(t)
, i.e.,

\xi(t)=\mathcal{E}\left\{x(t)|\mathbb{Y}(t)\right\}

(20)

where
\mathcal{E}\{\cdot|\cdot\}
denotes the conditional expectation. Furthermore, let
\Sigma
denote the covariance matrix for
x(t)-\xi(t)
in stationarity, i.e.,

\Sigma=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}\left\{[x(t)-\xi(t)][x(t)-\xi(t)]^{H}|\mathbb{Y}(t)\right\}.

(21)

We seek to find
\xi(t)
and
\Sigma
, which is the standard Kalman-Bucy filtering problem, with the caveat that
y
is measured without noise. It is a classical result that if
(A,C_{y})
is detectable and
(A,B_{w})
has no uncontrollable modes on the imaginary axis then
\Sigma
has a well-defined solution, although the dynamic mapping
v\mapsto\xi
in general constitutes an improper transfer function, thus requiring the evaluation of ideal derivatives of
v
to determine
\xi
. However, under certain conditions this transfer function is proper, although not strictly so.

Define

\displaystyle y_{d}(t)\triangleq

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}\left(y(t)-D_{uy}u(t)\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{y}Ax(t)+C_{y}B_{u}u(t)+C_{y}B_{w}w(t)

(23)

If
C_{y}B_{w}
is full row rank, i.e., if
C_{y}B_{w}B_{w}^{H}C_{y}^{H}\succ 0
, then we may equivalently treat
\{y_{d}(\tau):\tau<t\}
as the information available at time
t
, which results in the Kalman-Bucy filter being solvable, with

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{H}+B_{w}B_{w}^{H}

\displaystyle-[\Sigma A^{H}+B_{w}B_{w}^{H}]C_{y}^{H}[C_{y}B_{w}B_{w}^{H}C_{y}]^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times C_{y}[A\Sigma+B_{w}B_{w}^{H}]

(24)

and

\tfrac{d}{dt}\xi(t)=[I+LC_{y}]A\xi(t)+B_{u}u(t)-L[y_{d}(t)-C_{y}B_{u}u(t)]

(25)

where the Kalman-Bucy gain
L
is

L=-[\Sigma A^{H}+B_{w}B_{w}^{H}]C_{y}^{H}[C_{y}B_{w}B_{w}^{H}C_{y}^{H}]^{-1}.

(26)

However, we note that the stabilizing solution to Riccati equation (
24
) may not be unique.
The theorem below fully characterizes the set of solutions.

Theorem 1

Assume
(A,C_{y})
is observable,
(A,B_{w})
has no uncontrollable modes on the imaginary axis, and
C_{y}B_{w}
has full row rank. Then:

1.

There exists a solution
\Sigma\succeq 0
to (
24
), with
\mathcal{R}\left\{C_{y}^{H}\right\}=\mathcal{N}\left\{\Sigma\right\}
,
C_{y}L=-I
, and for which
[I+LC_{y}]A
is stable.

2.

For all such solutions, the subspace
\mathcal{R}\left\{\Sigma\right\}
is
[I+LC_{y}]A
-invariant and is asymptotically stable.

3.

The solution
\Sigma
is unique if and only if the transfer function
C_{y}[sI-A]^{-1}B_{w}
has no transmission zeros on the imaginary axis and no poles at the origin.

Proof:

To prove statement 1, first change coordinates to

\begin{bmatrix}y\\
\chi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{y}\\

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}C_{y}\\

Then (

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\

The (1,1) and (1,2) terms, together, require

One solution is

As such,

This Riccati equation has a unique stabilizing solution

To prove statement 2, we note that in the partitioned coordinates,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}C_{y}\\

This shows that the subspace

It is asymptotically stable because
\Sigma_{22}\succ 0
.

To prove statement 3, we have already shown that if
A
has no eigenvalues at the origin then
\Sigma_{11}=0
and

are the unique solutions. Furthermore,
\Sigma_{22}
has a unique stabilizing solution if and only if the pair

has no uncontrollable modes on the imaginary axis. If this caveat fails then there exists a left eigenvector
\eta

such that

, which corresponds to an imaginary eigenvalue. This implies that
\eta^{H}EB_{w}B_{w}^{H}E^{H}\eta=0

(35)

which implies that
C_{y}[sI-A]^{-1}B_{w}
has a transmission zero on the imaginary axis.
∎

Choosing the minimal solution
\Sigma
to (
24
), we have equivalently that if

\tfrac{d}{dt}\xi(t)=A\xi(t)+B_{u}u(t)-Le(t)

where

(37)

is the innovations process, which is zero-mean white noise, independent of
\mathbb{Y}(t)
, with spectral intensity

\Phi_{e}=C_{y}B_{w}B_{w}^{H}C_{y}^{H}.

(38)

As such, for the output vector
z(t)=C_{z}x(t)+D_{uz}u(t)
, we have that

\mathcal{E}\left\{z(t)z^{H}(t)\right\}=\mathcal{E}\left\{\zeta(t)\zeta^{H}(t)\right\}+C_{z}\Sigma C_{z}^{H}

(39)

where

\zeta(t)\triangleq C_{z}\xi(t)+D_{uz}u(t).

(40)

Therefore the optimal mapping
\mathbb{Y}(t)\mapsto u(t)
for OP1 is equivalent to the mapping that minimizes the performance measure evaluated with
\zeta(t)
in exchange for
z(t)
.

This motivates the formulation of
u(t)
as a static feedback law operating on the full observed state, i.e.,
u(t)=F\xi(t)
.
Assuming this to be the case, define

\xi^{\prime}(t)=\xi(t)+L[y(t)-D_{uy}u(t)]

(41)

and we have that (
25
) is equivalent to

\tfrac{d}{dt}\xi^{\prime}(t)=[I+LC_{y}]\left[A\xi^{\prime}(t)+(ALD_{uy}+B_{u})u(t)-ALy(t)\right]

(42)

Furthermore,
u(t)=F\xi(t)
is equivalently formulated as

u(t)=[I-FLD_{uy}]^{-1}F\left[\xi^{\prime}(t)-Ly(t)\right]

(43)

Combining these two equations we have that

\tfrac{d}{dt}\xi^{\prime}(t)=[I+LC_{y}]A^{\prime}\left[\xi^{\prime}(t)-Ly(t)\right]

(44)

where

A^{\prime}=A+[ALD_{uy}+B_{u}][I-FLD_{uy}]^{-1}F.

(45)

Clearly, because
C_{y}L=-I
, we have that
\tfrac{d}{dt}C_{y}\xi^{\prime}(t)=0
, so assuming
C_{y}\xi^{\prime}(0)=0
, it follows that
C_{y}\xi^{\prime}(t)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let

x_{K}(t)\triangleq L_{\perp}\xi^{\prime}(t)

(46)

where
L_{\perp}
is a full row-rank matrix with
\mathcal{N}\left\{L_{\perp}\right\}=\mathcal{R}\left\{L\right\}
.
Then we have that

\tfrac{d}{dt}x_{K}(t)=L_{\perp}A^{\prime}\left[\xi^{\prime}(t)-Ly(t)\right]

(47)

But

\xi^{\prime}(t)=C_{y\perp}x_{K}(t)

(48)

where
C_{y\perp}
is a matrix with full column rank, such that
\mathcal{R}\left\{C_{y\perp}\right\}=\mathcal{N}\left\{C_{y}\right\}
and such that
L_{\perp}C_{y\perp}=I
.
So at last, we have the reduced-order Kalman-Bucy filter and feedback law as

\displaystyle-\hat{K}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}A_{K}&B_{K}\\
\hline\cr C_{K}&D_{K}\end{array}\right]

where

\displaystyle A_{K}=

\displaystyle L_{\perp}A^{\prime}C_{y\perp}

(51)

\displaystyle B_{K}=

\displaystyle-L_{\perp}A^{\prime}L

(52)

\displaystyle C_{K}=

\displaystyle[I-FLD_{uy}]^{-1}FC_{y\perp}

(53)

\displaystyle D_{K}=

\displaystyle-[I-FLD_{uy}]^{-1}FL

(54)

III-B

Optimal Regeneratively-Constrained Control

Consider the relaxation of the passivity constraint on
\mathcal{K}
, from Definition
2
, to an stationary expectation, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}\left\{u^{H}y+y^{H}u+\epsilon u^{H}u\right\}\leqslant 0.

(55)

In this sub-section, we show that OP1 with this relaxed constraint in place of
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}
results in a convex solution, which makes use of the certainty-equivalence result in the previous subsection.

We first restate one of the many variants of the Kalman-Yakubovic-Popov (KYP) Lemma, which will be instrumental to the solution to the problem. The proof is standard
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2

If
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
then there exists a
W=W^{T}\succeq 0
such that

A^{T}W+WA+C_{p}^{H}R^{-1}C_{p}=0.

(56)

where
C_{p}\triangleq C_{y}^{H}-B_{u}^{T}W
and
R\triangleq\epsilon I+D_{uy}+D_{uy}^{H}
.

With
u(t)=F\xi(t)
as described in the previous subsection, we have that the stationary covariance
\Sigma_{\xi}=\mathcal{E}\{\xi(t)\xi^{H}(t)\}
is the solution to Lyapunov equation

[A+B_{u}F]\Sigma_{\xi}+\Sigma_{\xi}[A+B_{u}F]^{H}+L\Phi_{e}L^{T}=0.

(57)

Meanwhile, the performance
J
is

J=\tr\left\{[C_{z}+D_{uz}F]\Sigma_{\xi}[C_{z}+D_{uz}F]^{H}\right\}

(58)

and the regenerative constraint is

\tr\left\{F\Sigma_{\xi}C_{y}^{H}+C_{y}\Sigma_{\xi}F^{H}+RF\Sigma_{\xi}F^{H}\right\}\leqslant 0

(59)

The regenerative constraint can be written equivalently as

\tr\left\{S_{e}L^{H}XL\right\}\leqslant 0

(60)

where
X
obeys dual Lyapunov equation

[A+B_{u}F]^{H}X+X[A+B_{u}F]+F^{H}C_{y}+C_{y}^{H}F\\
+F^{H}RF=0

(61)

Adding the passivity-type Riccati equation from the KYP Lemma gives

[A+B_{u}F]^{H}[X+W]+[X+W][A+B_{u}F]\\
+\left[C_{p}^{H}+F^{H}R\right]R^{-1}\left[C_{p}+RF\right]=0

(62)

As such, the regenerative constraint can again be converted to the the condition

\tr\{[C_{p}^{H}+F^{H}R]R^{-1}[C_{p}+RF]\Sigma_{\xi}\}\leqslant\tr\{S_{e}L^{H}WL\}

(63)

where
\Sigma_{\xi}
is the solution to (
57
).

Letting
G=F\Sigma_{\xi}
, we therefore have that there exists a
F
rendering performance
J<\gamma
if and only if the following linear matrix inequalities (LMIs) have a feasible solution
\{S,G,Q,V\}
with
S=S^{H}
,
Q=Q^{H}
and
V=V^{H}

\displaystyle AS+SA^{H}+B_{u}G+G^{H}B_{u}^{H}+LS_{e}L^{H}\prec 0

(64)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}Q&C_{z}S+D_{uz}G\\
SC_{z}^{H}+G^{H}D_{uz}^{H}&S\end{bmatrix}\succ 0

(65)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}V&C_{p}S-RG\\
SC_{p}^{H}-G^{H}R&S\end{bmatrix}\succ 0

(66)

\displaystyle\tr\left\{R^{-1}V\right\}\leqslant\tr\{S_{e}L^{H}WL\}

(67)

for which
\gamma=\tr\{Q\}+\tr\{S_{e}L^{H}\Sigma L\}
.
As such,
F
can be optimized for minimal
J
as the convex optimization below.

\text{OP2}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
\text{Minimize:}&\tr\{Q\}\\
\text{Domain:}&S=S^{H},Q=Q^{H},V=V^{H},G\\
\text{Constraints:}&\eqref{regen_constr_1},\eqref{regen_constr_2},\eqref{regen_constr_3},\eqref{regen_constr_4}\end{array}\right.

(68)

The resultant optimal regenerative controller
\mathcal{K}
is formulated as in (
III-A
).

III-C

Projection onto
\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}

In some special circumstances the optimal regenerative controller will lie in
\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}
, but in general this will not be the case.
Therefore, it is necessary to adjust the solution
F
obtained via the optimization formulated in the previous subsection, so that the adjusted controller (
III-A
) adheres to this constraint.
For clarity, we denote the optimum regeneratively-constrained
F
as
F_{r}
.

First it is necessary to determine the constraint that
F
must satisfy to assure that the controller (
III-A
) lies in
\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}
.
It is equivalent to find conditions on
F
such that the non-reduced-order mapping
y_{d}\mapsto\{y,u\}
, which as

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{Z}_{1}\\
\hat{Z}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{l|l}[A_{o}+B_{o}F]&-L\\
\hline\cr C_{y}+D_{uy}F&0\\
F&0\end{array}\right]

(69)

where
A_{o}=[I+LC_{y}]A
and
B_{o}=[I+LC_{y}]B_{u}
,
satisfies

\hat{Z}_{1}^{H}(j\omega)\hat{Z}_{2}(j\omega)+\hat{Z}_{2}^{H}(j\omega)\hat{Z}_{1}(j\omega)\\
+\epsilon\hat{Z}_{2}^{H}(j\omega)\hat{Z}_{2}(j\omega)\preceq 0,\ \ \forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(70)

Via the generalized Kalman-Yakubovic-Popov Lemma
[
35
]
, this condition holds if and only if there exists a matrix
X=X^{H}\succeq 0
such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}[A_{o}+B_{o}F]^{H}X+X[A_{o}+B_{o}F]+\Xi&-XL\\
-L^{H}X&0\end{bmatrix}\preceq 0

(71)

where

\Xi\triangleq C_{y}^{H}F+F^{H}C_{y}+F^{H}RF.

(72)

Clearly this requires that
XL=0
and that

[A+B_{u}F]^{H}X+X[A+B_{u}F]+\Xi\preceq 0

(73)

Adding this to the passivity-type Riccati equation from Lemma
2
, we equivalently require that

[A+B_{u}F]^{H}[W+X]+[W+X][A+B_{u}F]\\
+[C_{p}^{H}+F^{H}R]R^{-1}[C_{p}+RF]\preceq 0.

(74)

Equivalently, setting
Y=[W+X]^{-1}
, we have that we require

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}YA^{H}+AY+G^{H}B_{u}^{H}+B_{u}G&YC_{p}^{H}+G^{H}R\\
C_{p}Y+RG&-R\end{bmatrix}\preceq 0

(75)

\displaystyle[YW-I]L=0

(76)

where
G\triangleq FY
.
It is known that these conditions are feasible (i.e., with
F=0
) and our goal is to find the feasible
F
which is “closest” to
F_{r}
.

Ideally, the measure of closeness would be directly related to performance
J
, but there does not appear to be an obvious way to do this which is also convex.
A compromise is to choose
F
as the solution to the optimization:

\text{OP3}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
\text{Minimize:}&\tr\left\{[F-F_{r}]Y[F-F_{r}]^{H}\right\}\\
\text{Constraints:}&\eqref{passivity_projection_1},\eqref{passivity_projection_2}\\
\text{Domain:}&G=FY,Y=Y^{H}\end{array}\right.

(77)

This optimization has the advantage of being convex in
G
and
Y
.
However, there is no guarantee on the degree to which the performance achieved with this solution differs from true optimality.

Despite being suboptimal, the design approach described in this section has the advantage of being computationally straightforward, as it is comprised of two sequential, small-scale, convex optimizations (i.e., OP2 and OP3).
It also has the distinct advantage of producing a control design
\mathcal{K}
as in (
III-A
), which has a state dimension lower than that of the plant.
For these reasons, it may be the preferred approach despite its suboptimality.

It should be noted that the assumptions made in Theorem
1
can be relaxed.
If
(A,C_{y})
fails to be observable, a solution
\Sigma
still exists for Riccati equation (
24
), although
\mathcal{R}\left\{C_{y}^{H}\right\}\subset\mathcal{N}\left\{\Sigma\right\}
rather than the equality holding, and
\mathcal{R}\left\{\Sigma\right\}
is not
[I+LC_{y}]A
-invariant. However, these conditions are not necessary for the ensuing analysis.
If
C_{y}B_{w}
fails to have full row rank, supplemental (fictitious) process noise may be introduced so that it does.
If
A
has an eigenvalue on the imaginary axis (controllable or otherwise), this violates Assumption
2
, and therefore is a contingency outside the scope of this paper.
Finally, if the solution
\Sigma
is non-unique due to
C_{y}[sI-A]^{-1}B_{w}
having transmission zeros on the imaginary axis, then any valid solution may be used.

IV
Optimal Solution

This section develops a convex approach to solving OP1 without the introduction of conservatism.
First we convert the problem to an equivalent discrete-time system optimization via a bilinear transformation.
Then we establish certain spectral factorization results that play a central role in the solution.
Following this, we establish the associated Youla parameter for the problem.
Finally, we recast OP1 in terms of the Youla parameter and show that the resultant optimization is convex.

IV-A

Bilinear Transformation

In order to solve OP1, it is convenient to first convert the problem to one in which the norms and domain constraints are in
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
rather than
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
and
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
. Toward this end, we employ the bilinear transformation

q\triangleq\frac{1+2\tau s}{1-2\tau s}

(78)

for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
It is straightforward to verify that the above relationship maps
\mathbb{C}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{O}_{>1}
.
The inverse mapping is

s=\frac{1}{2\tau}\frac{q-1}{q+1}

(79)

which is analytic in
q
for all
q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1}
.
Consequently, for
\{i,j\}\in\{w,u\}\times\{z,y\}
,
\hat{P}_{ij}(s)
is analytic and uniformly bounded for all
s\in\mathbb{C}_{>0}
if and only if

\tilde{P}_{ij}(q)\triangleq\hat{P}_{ij}\left(\frac{1}{2\tau}\frac{q-1}{q+1}\right)

(80)

is analytic and uniformly bounded for all
q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1}
.
It is also the case that for
\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
,

\tilde{P}_{ij}(e^{j\Omega})=\hat{P}_{ij}(j\omega(\Omega))

(81)

where

\omega(\Omega)=\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\tanh(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega)

(82)

and we note that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\Omega}\omega(\Omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{1}{|1+e^{j\Omega}|^{2}}.

(83)

As such,
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
implies that
\tilde{P}_{ij}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
and that

\tilde{P}_{uy}^{H}(q)+\tilde{P}_{uy}(q)\succeq 0,\ \ \forall q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1},

(84)

which holds if and only if

\tilde{P}_{uy}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})+\tilde{P}_{uy}(e^{j\Omega})\succeq 0,\ \ \forall\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi].

(85)

IV-B

Spectral Factorization

We have that
\tilde{P}_{ij}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Phi&\Gamma_{i}\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{j}&\Delta_{ij}\end{array}\right]

where

\displaystyle\Phi\triangleq

\displaystyle(I+2\tau A)(I-2\tau A)^{-1}

(88)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}\triangleq

\displaystyle 2\tau(I-2\tau A)^{-1}B_{i}

(89)

\displaystyle\Pi_{j}\triangleq

\displaystyle 2C_{j}(I-2\tau A)^{-1}

(90)

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij}\triangleq

\displaystyle D_{ij}+2\tau C_{j}(I-2\tau A)^{-1}B_{i}.

(91)

In terms of these matrices, we have the following theorem, which is another variant of the KYP Lemma.

Theorem 3

Let
\epsilon,\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and assume
\mathcal{P}
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
. Then
\mathcal{P}
satisfies Assumption
3
if and only if there exists a matrix
\Sigma_{P}=\Sigma_{P}^{H}\succeq 0
, and compatible matrices
\Pi_{S}
and
\Delta_{S}
, such that

\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{H}\Sigma_{P}\Phi-\Sigma_{P}&\Phi^{H}\Sigma_{P}\Gamma_{u}-\Pi_{y}^{H}\\
\Gamma_{u}^{H}\Sigma_{P}\Phi-\Pi_{y}&\Gamma_{u}^{H}\Sigma_{P}\Gamma_{u}-\Delta_{uy}-\Delta_{uy}^{H}-\epsilon I\end{bmatrix}\\
=-\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{S}^{H}\\
\Delta_{S}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{S}&\Delta_{S}\end{bmatrix}.

(92)

Furthermore, for

\tilde{S}\triangleq\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Phi_{S}&\Gamma_{S}\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{S}&\Delta_{S}\end{array}\right]

(93)

with
\Gamma_{S}=\Gamma_{u}
and
\Phi_{S}=\Phi
, it follows that
\tilde{S},\tilde{S}^{-1}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
and

\tilde{P}_{uy}(q)+\tilde{P}_{uy}^{H}(q)+\epsilon I=\tilde{S}^{H}(q)\tilde{S}(q),\ \ \forall q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1}.

(94)

Our analysis also employs the following related factorization result, which is straight-forward to verify.

Corollary 1

For the conditions of Theorem
3
we have that there exists a
\tilde{F}
such that
\tilde{F},\tilde{F}^{-1}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
, and

[\tilde{P}_{uy}(q)+\tilde{P}_{uy}^{H}(q)+\epsilon I]^{-1}=\tilde{F}^{H}(q)+\tilde{F}(q),\ \ \forall q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1}.

(95)

Specifically,

\tilde{F}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Phi_{F}&\Gamma_{F}\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{F}&\Delta_{F}\end{array}\right]

(96)

with

\displaystyle\Phi_{F}=

\displaystyle\Phi_{S}-\Gamma_{S}\Delta_{S}^{-1}\Pi_{S}

(97)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{F}=

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{S}\Delta_{S}^{-1}-\Phi_{F}\Sigma_{F}\Pi_{S}^{H}\right]\Delta_{S}^{-H}

(98)

\displaystyle\Pi_{F}=

\displaystyle-\Delta_{S}^{-1}\Pi_{S}

(99)

\displaystyle\Delta_{F}=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{S}^{-1}\left[I+\Pi_{S}\Sigma_{F}\Pi_{S}^{H}\right]\Delta_{S}^{-H}

(100)

and where
\Sigma_{F}=\Sigma_{F}^{H}
is the solution to Stein equation

\Sigma_{F}=\Phi_{F}\Sigma_{F}\Phi_{F}^{H}+\Gamma_{S}\Delta_{S}^{-1}\Delta_{S}^{-H}\Gamma_{S}^{H}.

(101)

IV-C

Youla Parametric Domain

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{wz}(q)\triangleq

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{wz}\left(\frac{1}{2\tau}\frac{1-q}{1+q}\right)

(102)

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(q)\triangleq

\displaystyle\hat{K}\left(\frac{1}{2\tau}\frac{1-q}{1+q}\right).

(103)

Similarly to the observations above, we have that
\tilde{T}_{wq},\tilde{K}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
if and only if
\hat{T}_{wz},\hat{K}\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, we have that

\tilde{T}_{wz}=\tilde{P}_{wz}-\tilde{P}_{uz}\tilde{K}[I+\tilde{P}_{uy}\tilde{K}]^{-1}\tilde{P}_{wy}.

(104)

We have that for
\hat{T}_{wz}\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
, the objective of OP1 is

\displaystyle J=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\trace\{\hat{T}_{wz}^{H}(j\omega)\hat{T}_{wz}(j\omega)\}d\omega

(105)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\trace\{\tilde{T}_{wz}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\tilde{T}_{wz}(e^{j\Omega})\}\frac{d\omega(\Omega)}{d\Omega}d\Omega

(106)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\|\tilde{T}_{fz}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}^{2}

(107)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{fz}=

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{fz}-\tilde{P}_{uz}\tilde{K}[I+\tilde{P}_{uy}\tilde{K}]^{-1}\tilde{P}_{fy}

(108)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{fz}(q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+q}\tilde{P}_{wz}(q)

(109)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{fy}(q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+q}\tilde{P}_{wy}(q).

IV-D

Convex Reformulation of OP1 in Youla Domain

We parametrize
\tilde{Q}(q)
via its infinite set of Markov parameters

,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, as

\tilde{Q}(q)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\bar{Q}(k)q^{-k}

(130)

where we note that coefficients
\bar{Q}(k)
for
k<0
must be zero in order for
\tilde{Q}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
.

Constraint
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}
can also be written directly in terms of the Markov parameters
\{\bar{Q}(k),k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\}
.
Specifically we have that constraint (
119
) is evaluated with

\tilde{U}(q)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\tilde{F}(q)\end{bmatrix}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{-k}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Q}(k)&\bar{Q}(k)\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(131)

Using the Youla parametrization from the previous subsection and the definitions above, we can reformulate OP1 as OP4, below.

\text{OP4}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon,\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\\
\text{Minimize:}&J=\tfrac{1}{\tau}\|\tilde{T}_{fz}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}^{2}\\
\text{Domain:}&\bar{Q}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}},\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\\
\text{Constraint:}&\eqref{Q_constraint}\end{array}\right.

(132)

As such, OP4 has a countably-infinite-dimensional domain, it has a convex quadratic objective on this domain, and is subjected to an uncountably-infinite number of convex constraints, each of which is a linear matrix inequality parametrized by
\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
.

V
Finite-Dimensional Solutions

V-A

Incremental Solutions

In principle, OP4 provides a convex equivalent to OP1, the solution of which is the stated goal of this paper.
However, OP4 requires optimization over a countably-infinite domain, and is therefore impractical.
To be implemented, the number of nonzero Markov parameters must be truncated to a finite number,
n
, resulting in a sequence of suboptimal solutions that asymptotically approaches optimality as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Practically, evaluation of this sequence is ended when
n
becomes large enough to exceed the allowable computational time or available memory. There is consequently advantageous to employ techniques that reduce the value of
n
that is necessary to achieve acceptable asymptotic behavior.

One means of accomplishing this is to solve for the optimal solution as an incremental improvement relative to the suboptimal design from Section
III
. For clarity, we refer to the bilinear-transformed suboptimal controller described in this section as
\tilde{K}_{0}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{L}
and
\mathcal{G}_{R}

Denote

.
Then we have that
\mathcal{K}_{0}
,
\mathcal{G}_{L}
and
\mathcal{G}_{R}
can be subsumed into the plant model, resulting in a mapping

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{V}_{fz}&\tilde{V}_{u_{1}z}\\

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{P}_{fz}-\tilde{P}_{uz}\tilde{Q}_{0}\tilde{P}_{fy}&\tilde{P}_{uz}\tilde{M}_{0}\tilde{G}_{L}\\

(138)

V-C

Finite-Dimensional Constraint

Constraint (
119
) is infinite-dimensional, but may be replaced by an equivalent finite-dimensional constraint through the use of auxiliary variables.
To present this result, we first note that for the simplifying assumptions made here regarding
\mathcal{G}_{L}
and
\mathcal{G}_{R}
,
\tilde{U}
has a state space realization

\tilde{U}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Phi_{U}&\Gamma_{U}\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{U}&\Delta_{U}\end{array}\right]

(158)

where only
\Pi_{U2}
and
\Delta_{U}
depend on parameters
\{\bar{Q}_{1}(0),...,\bar{Q}_{1}(n)\}
, with this dependence being affine.
Consequently, via the KYP Lemma, we have that (
119
) holds if and only if there exists a matrix
\Sigma_{U}=\Sigma_{U}^{H}\succ 0
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Phi_{U}-\Sigma_{U}&\Phi_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Gamma_{U}-\Pi_{U}^{H}\\
\Gamma_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Phi_{U}-\Pi_{U}&\Gamma_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Gamma_{U}-\Delta_{U}-\Delta_{U}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\preceq 0.

(159)

We therefore have the following convex, finite-dimensional optimization as equivalent to OP5.

\text{OP6}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\\
\text{Minimize:}&J\text{ as in }\eqref{474393947585}\\
\text{Domain:}&\bar{Q}_{1}(0),...,\bar{Q}_{1}(n),\\
&\Sigma_{U}=\Sigma_{U}^{H}\succ 0\\
\text{Constraint:}&\eqref{convex_pr_lmi}\end{array}\right.

(160)

The objective in OP6 is convex and quadratic, while the finite-dimensional constraint is a matrix inequality that is affine in the domain parameters.
Due to its finite-dimensionality and convexity, OP6 can be efficiently solved.

V-D

Existence and Uniqueness

Clearly OP6 is guaranteed to be feasible, because (
159
) is satisfied with
\bar{Q}_{1}(k)=0
for all
k\in\{0,..,n\}
.
Convexity of the objective and constraint, together with the fact that the problem has guaranteed feasibility, ensure that OP6 has a global minimum, which we denote
J^{\star}(\tau,n)
.
However, these facts do not assure that the problem has unique corresponding minimizers,
\{\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(0),...,\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(n)\}
. However, this can be assured by the imposition of extra assumptions on
\mathcal{P}
, as illustrated by the following theorem.

Theorem 5

For OP6, let
\mathbb{W}\subseteq[-\pi,\pi]
be defined as

\mathbb{W}=\big\{\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi]\ :\ \tilde{P}_{uz}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\tilde{P}_{uz}(e^{j\Omega})\succ 0,\\
\land\ \tilde{P}_{fy}(e^{j\Omega})\tilde{P}_{fy}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\succ 0\big\}

(161)

and let
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
be its complement.
Then
\{\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(0),...,\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(n)\}
are unique if
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
has zero measure.

Proof:

Suppose
\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{a}
and
\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{b}
are two minimizers, each rendering performance
J^{\star}(\tau,n)
.
Because OP6 is convex, it follows that all interpolated points
\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{\alpha}=\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{a}+\alpha(\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{b}-\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{a})
for
\alpha\in[0,1]
are also minimizers achieving the same performance
J^{\star}(\tau,n)
.
Let
\tilde{T}_{fz\alpha}^{\star}
be the closed-loop transfer function corresponding to Youla parameter
\tilde{Q}^{\star}_{\alpha}
.
Then

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\|\tilde{T}_{fz\alpha}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}^{2}

(162)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\tr\Big\{[\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})-\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega}]\tilde{P}_{fy}(e^{j\Omega})\tilde{P}_{fy}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times[\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})-\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega}]^{H}\tilde{P}_{uz}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\tilde{P}_{uz}(e^{j\Omega})\Big\}d\Omega

(163)

Now recall that
\tilde{P}_{fy}
and
\tilde{P}_{uz}
are uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{O}_{>1}
, and also that constraint (
119
) requires
\sup_{\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi]}\|\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})\|\leqslant\tfrac{2}{\epsilon}
, and similarly for
\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}
.
As such, each multiplicative component in the above integral is individually bounded.
Furthermore, the integrand itself is positive-semidefinite in
\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})-\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})
, and is zero if and only if
\Omega\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
.
It follows that if
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
has zero measure, then

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\tr\left\{[\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})-\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})][\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})-\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})]^{H}\right\}d\Omega

\displaystyle=0

(164)

implying that
\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}=\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}
except on a set of zero measure.
If this is the case, then it implies that the same is true for incremental Youla parameters
\tilde{Q}_{1b}^{\star}
and
\tilde{Q}_{1a}^{\star}
.
Via the Parseval theorem, this implies that
\bar{Q}_{1a}^{\star}(k)=\bar{Q}_{1b}^{\star}(k)
for all
k\in\{0,...,n\}
.
∎

V-E

Asymptotic Behavior

We consider the asymptotic behavior of
J^{\star}(\tau,n)
and minimizers
\{\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(0),...,\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(n)\}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and examine the influence of the choice of
\tau
on this behavior.
To begin, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 6

J^{\star}(\tau,n)
is monotonically nonincreasing in
n
, and converges to a well-defined limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, if the conditions of Theorem
5
hold then
\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(k)
converges to a well-defined limit with

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\|\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(k)\|^{2}<\infty.

(165)

Proof:

That
J^{\star}(\tau,n)
is monotonically nonincreasing in
n
is verified by observing that if
\{\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(0),....,\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(n)\}
are the optimal Markov parameters for order
n
, then
\{\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(0),....,\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(n),0\}
is feasible for
n\leftarrow n+1
.
The uniqueness of the limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau,n)
then follows from this result, together with the positive semidefiniteness
J
.
Let the limit be denoted
J^{\star}_{\infty}(\tau)
.
To show that each
\bar{Q}_{1}^{\star}(k)
converges uniquely as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we note that the proof to Theorem
5
applies even if
\tilde{Q}_{a}^{\star}
and
\tilde{Q}_{b}^{\star}
have infinitely many nonzero Markov parameters.
As such, the conditions of Theorem
5
require that performance
J_{\infty}^{\star}(\tau)
is achieved by an infinite sequence
\{\bar{Q}_{1}(0),\bar{Q}_{1}(1),....\}
which is unique under the
\bar{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
measure.
Finally, to prove bound (
165
), note that from (
141
) we have via the Triangle Inequality that

\|\tilde{Q}_{1}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}\leqslant\|\tilde{N}_{0}^{-1}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}}\|\tilde{Q}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}+\|\tilde{K}_{0}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}

(166)

The
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
norm on the right-hand side must be finite because
\|\tilde{K}_{0}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}}<\tfrac{2}{\epsilon}
for all
\mathcal{K}_{0}\in\mathbb{K}_{\epsilon}
, and
\|\tilde{P}_{uy}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}}
is bounded for all
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}
.
Meanwhile, the
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}
norms are uniformly bounded for all finite
n
because feasibility constraint (
119
) requires that for all
\Omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
,

\tilde{Q}(e^{j\Omega})+\tilde{Q}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\\
-\tilde{Q}(e^{j\Omega})\left[\tilde{P}_{uy}(e^{j\Omega})+\tilde{P}_{uy}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})+\epsilon I\right]\tilde{Q}^{H}(e^{j\Omega})\succeq 0

(167)

which, because
\tilde{P}_{uy}
is PR, implies that
\|\tilde{Q}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}}<\tfrac{2}{\epsilon}
.
It follows that
\|\tilde{Q}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}
is therefore uniformly bounded on the feasibility domain.
Since
\tilde{K}_{0}
is feasible, its norm is similarly bounded. Consequently
\|\tilde{Q}_{1}^{\star}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}
is uniformly bounded as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Bound (
165
) then follows directly from Parseval’s theorem.
∎

Regarding the choice of
\tau
, we note that all choices are asymptotically equivalent, as illustrated by the following theorem.

Theorem 7

Let
\tau_{a},\tau_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Then

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau_{a},n)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau_{b},n).

(168)

Furthermore, assume the conditions of Theorem
5
hold and let
\hat{Q}_{i\infty}^{\star}
be the (unique) asymptotic limit of
\hat{Q}_{i}^{\star}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
with
\tau=\tau_{i}
, for
i=a,b
.
Then
\|\hat{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}-\hat{Q}_{b\infty}^{\star}\|_{\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}=0
.

Proof:

Let
\tilde{Q}_{i}^{\star}(q;,n)
be the optimal solution to
\tilde{Q}
with
\tau=\tau_{i}
and with dimension
n
, and define

\tilde{Q}_{i\infty}^{\star}(q)\triangleq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{Q}_{i}^{\star}(q;n)

(169)

Then this asymptotic solution for
\tau_{a}
is associated with the continuous-time transfer function

\hat{Q}_{a}(s)^{\star}\triangleq\tilde{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}\left(\frac{1+2\tau_{a}s}{1-2\tau_{a}s}\right)

(170)

Then define the
\tilde{Q}_{ab}(q)
as the bilinear transformation of
\hat{Q}_{a}^{\star}
with parameter
\tau_{b}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{ab}(q)\triangleq

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{a}^{\star}\left(\frac{1}{2\tau_{b}}\frac{q-1}{q+1}\right)

(171)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}\left(\frac{1+\frac{\tau_{a}}{\tau_{b}}\frac{q-1}{q+1}}{1-\frac{\tau_{a}}{\tau_{b}}\frac{q-1}{q+1}}\right)

(172)

which is analytic and uniformly bounded for all
q\in\mathbb{O}_{>1}
if
\tilde{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}(q)
is.
Furthermore, noting that
\tilde{Q}_{ab}(e^{j\Omega_{b}})=\tilde{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega_{a}})
where

\Omega_{b}=2\tan^{-1}\left[\frac{\tau_{b}}{\tau_{a}}\tan(\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_{a})\right]

(173)

we conclude that
\tilde{Q}_{ab}
is feasible if and only if
\tilde{Q}_{a\infty}^{\star}
is.
Now suppose
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau_{a},n)<\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau_{b},n)
.
Then this results in a contradiction because the value of
J
obtained with
\tilde{Q}_{ab}
is lower than
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J^{\star}(\tau_{b},n)
, and is feasibly attained by Markov parameters

\bar{Q}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{j\Omega k}\tilde{Q}_{ab}(e^{j\Omega})d\Omega.

(174)

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
.
This proves (
168
).

To prove that
\|\hat{Q}_{a}^{\star}-\hat{Q}_{b}^{\star}\|_{\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}=0
, assume it is not the case.
Then from the reasoning above, this implies that there exists a
\tilde{Q}_{ab}
such that
\tilde{Q}_{ab}(e^{j\Omega})\neq\tilde{Q}_{b\infty}^{\star}(e^{j\Omega})
for all
\Omega
in some set
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
of finite measure, which also attains the optimal asymptotic performance.
This implies that the asymptotic limit
\tilde{Q}_{b\infty}^{\star}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
is nonunique.
If the assumptions of Theorem
5
hold, this leads to a contradiction.
∎

V-F

Dual Formulation

As
n
is increased, the optimization domain for OP6 increases like
n^{2}
due to the usage of matrix variable
\Sigma_{U}
.
There is a considerable advantage to the reformulation of OP6 in its dual domain, for which the dimensionality increases linearly with
n
rather than quadratically.

Introduce Lagrange multiplier matrix
\Upsilon=\Upsilon^{H}\succeq 0
, partitioned with the same dimensions as the partitions in LMI (
159
), i.e.,

\Upsilon=\begin{bmatrix}\Upsilon_{11}&\Upsilon_{12}\\
\Upsilon_{12}^{H}&\Upsilon_{22}\end{bmatrix}

(175)

For concision of presentation, let
J_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
g\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)n_{p}^{2}}
, and
H=H^{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)n_{p}^{2}\times(n+1)n_{p}^{2}}
be appropriately defined such that

J=J_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(g^{H}x+x^{H}g+x^{H}Hx\right)

(176)

where
x\triangleq\textrm{vec}\{\bar{Q}_{1}(n),...,\bar{Q}_{1}(0)\}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)n_{p}^{2}}
, and where we note that
H\succ 0
.
Then OP6 is expressed equivalently as

\min\limits_{x,\Sigma_{U}=\Sigma_{U}^{H}}\max\limits_{\Upsilon=\Upsilon^{H}\succeq 0}J_{pd}(x,\Sigma_{U},\Upsilon)

(177)

where
\Upsilon
is a Lagrange multiplier matrix used to enforce constraint (
159
), and
J_{pd}
is the primal-dual objective, i.e.,

\displaystyle J_{pd}=

\displaystyle J_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(g^{H}x+x^{H}g+x^{H}Hx\right)

\displaystyle+\tr\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\Upsilon_{11}&\Upsilon_{12}\\
\Upsilon_{12}^{H}&\Upsilon_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Phi_{U}-\Sigma_{U}&\Phi_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Gamma_{U}\\
\Gamma_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Phi_{U}&\Gamma_{U}^{H}\Sigma_{U}\Gamma_{U}\end{bmatrix}\right\}

\displaystyle-\tr\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\Upsilon_{11}&\Upsilon_{12}\\
\Upsilon_{12}^{H}&\Upsilon_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&\Pi_{U}^{H}(x)\\
\Pi_{U}(x)&\Delta_{U}(x)+\Delta_{U}^{H}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right\}

(178)

In order for
J_{pd}
to be extremal in
\Sigma_{U}
,
\Upsilon
must satisfy

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{11}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{U}&\Gamma_{U}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Upsilon_{11}&\Upsilon_{12}\\
\Upsilon_{12}^{H}&\Upsilon_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{U}^{H}\\
\Gamma_{U}^{H}\end{bmatrix}.

(179)

Because
\tilde{U}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
it follows that the moduli of the eigenvalues of
\Phi_{U}
are strictly less than
1
, and consequently the above equation determines
\Upsilon_{11}
uniquely and linearly from
\Upsilon_{12}
and
\Upsilon_{22}
.
Meanwhile, there exist a vector
h
and matrix
E
such that

\textrm{vec}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-\Pi_{U}(x)&-\Delta_{U}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right\}=h+Ex

(180)

Letting

\psi=\textrm{vec}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\Upsilon_{12}^{H}&\Upsilon_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right\}

(181)

we then have that

\displaystyle J_{pd}=

\displaystyle J_{0}+\psi^{H}\left(h+Ex\right)+\left(h+Ex\right)^{H}\psi

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(g^{H}x+x^{H}g+x^{H}Hx\right)

(182)

The dual objective
J_{d}
is then the minimization of
J_{pd}
as above, over
x
and
\Sigma_{U}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle J_{d}=

\displaystyle J_{0}+\psi^{H}h+h^{H}\psi

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}(2E^{H}\psi+g)^{H}H^{-1}(2E^{H}\psi+g)

(183)

We thus arrive at the dual formulation of OP6, as

\text{OP7}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Given:}&\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\\
\text{Maximize:}&J_{d}\text{ as in }\eqref{57686978695858}\\
\text{Domain:}&\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{(2n_{p})^{2}(n+1)}\\
\text{Constraint:}&\Upsilon(\psi)\succeq 0\end{array}\right.

(184)

where
\Upsilon(\psi)
is characterized uniquely by (
181
) and (
179
), and where the corresponding primal optimal parameters is obtained from the dual solution via

x=-H^{-1}\left(2E^{H}\psi+g\right).

(185)

VII
Some Extensions

VII-A

Generalization to Other Performance Measures

The scope of this paper was restricted to
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance objectives, but the methodology employed here applies more broadly, often with only minor modifications.

For example, extension to generalized-
\mathbb{H}_{2}
performance objectives straightforward.
Such objectives take the form

J=\max\limits_{i\in\{1,...,n_{J}\}}\|\hat{T}_{w_{i}z_{i}}\|_{\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{2}}^{2}

(199)

where for each
i
,
w_{i}
and
z_{i}
are predefined subspaces of the overall input and output vectors respectively.
Extension of the methodology to objectives of this form requires no substantive modifications the optimization methodology, with OP6 being modified from (
160
) only in the evaluation of
J
, which becomes
\max\{J_{1},...,J_{J}\}
where each
J_{i}
has the form of (
156
).
The dual version of the optimization, OP7 as in (
184
), can also be formulated, through standard use of a Lagrange multipliers.
We do note that the sub-optimal control design methodology from Section
III
cannot be applied in the generalized
\mathbb{H}_{2}
case, unless
w_{i}
is the same for all
i\in\{1,...,n_{J}\}
because otherwise certainty-equivalence does not hold.

Extension to
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
performance objectives is also straightforward, requiring only minor changes in the methodology.
One important change is in the relation of the performance measure to the discrete-time Youla parameter
\tilde{Q}
, which for the case considered in the paper, is equation (
107
) with
\tilde{T}_{fz}
evaluated as in (
117
).
In an
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
context,

J=\|\tilde{T}_{wz}\|_{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}}

(200)

with it being unnecessary to introduce the new discrete-time input
f
at all.
Another important change is that
J
must be evaluated using the KYP Lemma and an auxiliary Hermitian multiplier matrix, rather than being evaluated directly as a quadratic function of the control variables, as described in Section
V-B
.
This in turn results in some differences in the optimization domains of OP6 and OP7.

VII-B

Generalization to Non-Passive Plants

It is possible to relax Assumption 3, regarding the passivity of the mapping
u\mapsto y
for plant
\mathcal{P}
, to instead only require that this mapping be asymptotically stable.
To see this, let
\mathcal{G}
be a LTI system with a transfer function
\hat{G}
that is strongly positive real, i.e., a transfer function in
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
for which there exists a
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

\hat{G}(j\omega)+\hat{G}^{H}(j\omega)\succeq\gamma I,\ \ \forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(201)

Then if
\mathcal{P}
is asymptotically stable and
\hat{P}_{uy}\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
then there exists a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\hat{P}_{uy}+\alpha\hat{G}
is PR and lies in
\hat{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
.
Let
y^{\prime}=y+\alpha\mathcal{G}u
and let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be the mapping
\{w,u\}\mapsto\{z,y^{\prime}\}
.
Then we can design a feedback law
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:y^{\prime}\mapsto u
which is constrained to passivity, using the methodology proposed here.
Then we can find the equivalent feedback law
\mathcal{K}:y\mapsto-u
as

\mathcal{K}=\left(I+\alpha\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\mathcal{G}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

(202)

where we note that
\mathcal{K}
is guaranteed to be passive if
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{G}
are.
As such, through this approach we are able to design passivity-constrained feedback laws for non-passive plants.
However, it is important to note that feedback laws designed via this methodology are not optimal.
This approach may nonetheless be useful for situations in which the actuators exhibit dynamics, such as time delay, which result in minor passivity violations for the mapping
u\mapsto y
.

VIII
Conclusions

Feedback passivity affords certain advantages in many control applications.
It allows for a feedback controller to be implemented in a manner which, theoretically, requires no power in order to operate perpetually.
Moreover, when the plant is itself passive, such controllers have unconditional guarantees on stability-robustness.
These observations provide a clear motivation to determine, with some precision, the best performance that is achievable with a passive feedback law.
This paper has presented a technique for accomplishing this, in the context of
\mathbb{H}_{2}
-optimal control.

The key contribution of this work is the recognition that feedback passivity may be imposed as a constraint on an optimal control problem, in a manner that is convex.
This is done by explicit optimization of the Youla parameter associated with the feedback controller.
We have shown that in the context of
\mathbb{H}_{2}
-optimal control, this leads to an efficient and convex algorithm to solve for a controller which is optimal, given a prescribed number
n
of nonzero Markov coefficients for the Youla parameter.
We have, furthermore, shown that the optimization domain for this algorithm grows linearly with
n
.

Although we have touched on a few extensions of these ideas to other performance objectives, as well as to non-passive plants, many questions remain.
The most important is the extension of the techniques considered here to guarantee bounds on robust performance, rather than just robust stability.
To the author’s knowledge, such an extension remains an open problem.
Additionally, there are some technical questions raised in the course of this analysis which were left open.
Most notable among these is the optimal choice of filters
\mathcal{G}_{L}
and
\mathcal{G}_{R}
in the formulation of the incremental Youla parameter in Section
V-A
.
Additionally, although heuristic techniques were employed in the examples presented here, regarding the optimal choice of the time constant
\tau
used in the bilinear transformation, it may be that a more systematic approach exists to identify the best choice for this parameter, based on problem data.

Finally, we note the important role that control complexity often plays in problems involving feedback-passivity constraints. Our analysis here does not account for the favorability of controllers with low order, compared to those with higher order. Additionally, the techniques proposed here do not allow for feedback laws to be decentralized (without sacrificing convexity).
Extensions to accommodate such constraints would greatly enhance the domain of problems for which the proposed techniques have relevance.
