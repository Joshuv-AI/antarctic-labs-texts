Robust Reinforcement Learning under Diffusion Models for Data with Jumps
Chenyang Jiang cjiang77@wisc.edu, Donggyu Kim donggyu.kim@ucr.edu, Alejandra Quintos alejandra.quintos@wisc.edu, Yazhen Wang yzwang@stat.wisc.edu

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning (RL) has proven effective in solving complex decision-making tasks across various domains, but challenges remain in continuous-time settings, particularly when state dynamics are governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with jump components. In this paper, we address this challenge by introducing the Mean-Square Bipower Variation Error (MSBVE) algorithm, which enhances robustness and convergence in scenarios involving significant stochastic noise and jumps. We first revisit the Mean-Square TD Error (MSTDE) algorithm, commonly used in continuous-time RL, and highlight its limitations in handling jumps in state dynamics. The proposed MSBVE algorithm minimizes the mean-square quadratic variation error, offering improved performance over MSTDE in environments characterized by SDEs with jumps. Simulations and formal proofs demonstrate that the MSBVE algorithm reliably estimates the value function in complex settings, surpassing MSTDE’s performance when faced with jump processes. These findings underscore the importance of alternative error metrics to improve the resilience and effectiveness of RL algorithms in continuous-time frameworks.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms have garnered substantial attention for their efficacy in addressing intricate decision-making challenges across diverse domains, spanning robotics, finance, and healthcare. Within RL frameworks, agents iteratively learn to make sequential decisions through interactions with an environment, aiming to maximize a cumulative reward signal. At the heart of RL lies the fundamental challenge of accurately estimating the value function, which provides insights into the expected cumulative reward under a given policy.

In this paper, we delve into the task of estimating the value function within RL frameworks operating in continuous-time settings, where state dynamics are delineated by stochastic differential equations (SDEs) potentially incorporating jump components. Prior works have explored various algorithms to tackle this challenge. For instance,
Doya 2000
introduced the temporal-difference (TD) algorithm tailored for the continuous-time deterministic case, while
Jia and Zhou 2021
elucidated the convergence properties of the Mean Square TD Error (MSTDE) algorithm within continuous-time stochastic settings. These algorithms endeavor to estimate the value function by iteratively updating a parameter vector using distinct error metrics, thereby shedding light on their convergence behavior under diverse noise conditions. The optimal parameter derived from these algorithms ensures that the estimated value function closely approximates the true value function.

Our investigation commences with a thorough review of the MSTDE algorithm, which endeavors to minimize the mean-square TD error to estimate the value function. Nonetheless, we underscore its limitations when confronted with scenarios characterized by state dynamics featuring jump components. To mitigate this challenge, we introduce the Mean Square Bipower Variation Error (MSBVE) algorithm. By focusing on minimizing the mean-square quadratic variation error, the MSBVE algorithm exhibits enhanced robustness in coping with stochastic noise and jump processes within state dynamics.

Through extensive simulation experiments and formal proof, we validate the efficacy of the MSBVE algorithm in accurately estimating the value function under demanding conditions marked by significant variability with jumps. Our findings unequivocally demonstrate that the MSBVE algorithm surpasses the performance of the MSTDE algorithm, especially in scenarios characterized by substantial stochastic noise with jumps, where the MSTDE algorithm struggles to converge. These results underscore the paramount importance of considering alternative error metrics, such as the mean-square quadratic variation error, to bolster the performance and resilience of RL algorithms operating in continuous-time settings.

The key distinction of our work lies in its focus on robustness: while recent studies have primarily aimed to find optimal controls in jump-diffusion models, our approach seeks to develop an algorithm that ensures the estimated value function remains unaffected by jump noise, offering a more resilient solution for general SDEs with jumps. In contrast,
Gao et al. 2024
and
Bo et al. 2024
employ randomized control methods alongside q-learning to derive the optimal policy distribution for general SDEs with jumps, differing mainly in their choice of entropy terms.
Bender and Thuan 2023
narrows its focus to the mean-variance portfolio selection problem, providing theoretical optimal solutions but not addressing general SDEs with jumps.
Guo et al. 2023
introduces the Greedy Least-Square algorithm and derives sublinear regret bounds, though its model assumes that only the drift term depends on the state and control, excluding the diffusion and jump components. Lastly,
Denkert et al. 2024
replaces control with a random point process, applying an actor-critic algorithm to solve a randomized problem that ultimately leads to the optimal value functions for the original system. Unlike these works, which concentrate on finding the optimal control under jump diffusions, our research addresses the challenge of value function estimation under jump dynamics, emphasizing robustness against jump noise.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the setup for the RL problem within continuous-time settings. In Section 3, we delve into the detailed descriptions of the MSTDE and MSBVE algorithms. The simulation results are presented in Section 4, where we analyze and discuss the performance of these algorithms. We draw conclusions in Section 5, summarizing the key findings and implications of our study. Finally, in Section 6, we provide detailed proofs to support the theoretical underpinnings of our proposed algorithms and their convergence properties.

Setup

In a general RL problem, the state space and action space are denoted by
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
, respectively. At time
t
, the environment provides the current state
X_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
. The agent then selects an action
A_{t}=\pi(X_{t})\in\mathcal{A}
according to policy
\pi
. A reward
R_{t}=r(t,X_{t},A_{t})
is calculated based on this action. Subsequently, the environment generates the next state
X_{t+1}
based on the previous state
X_{t}
and action
A_{t}
. Here, the state
X_{t}
can be described by the following SDE, defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0})
:

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t},A_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t},A_{t})dW_{t}+L(t,X_{t},A_{t})dN_{t}.

In real-world scenarios, practical constraints often limit the ability to frequently change policies. For instance, in financial trading, risk management policies may dictate specific trading strategies that cannot be altered frequently. Therefore, this paper focuses on a RL problem with a fixed policy under the continuous-time settings. Since the policy
\pi
is fixed,
A_{t}=\pi(X_{t})
is a function of
X_{t}
, we use the following SDE without the action
A_{t}
to describe the state
X_{t}
:

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}+L(t,X_{t})dN_{t},

(1)

where
W_{t}
is a
m
-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted Brownian motion,
N_{t}
is a
n
-dimensional
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted counting process independent of
W_{t}
, and
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, and
L:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
are the drift coefficient, diffusion coefficient, and jump size, respectively.
The value function
J:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined as:

\displaystyle J(t,x)=E\left[\int_{t}^{T}r(s,X_{s})ds+h(X_{T})|X_{t}=x\right],

(2)

where
r:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a reward function based on the current time and state, and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a reward function applied at the end of
T
.

We make the following standard assumptions:

Assumption 1
. The following conditions hold true:

(i)
b,\sigma,L,r,h
are all continuous functions in their respective arguments;

(ii)
b,\sigma
are uniformly Lipschitz in
x
, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|b(t,x)-b(t,x^{{}^{\prime}})|+|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,x^{{}^{\prime}})|\leq C|x-x^{{}^{\prime}}|,\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ x,x^{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d};

(iii)
b
and
\sigma
both have linear growth in
x
, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|b(t,x)|^{2}+|\sigma(t,x)|^{2}\leq C^{2}(1+|x|^{2}),\ \forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};

(iv)
r
,
h
and
L
have polynomial growth in
x
, i.e., there exist constants
C>0
and
\nu\geq 1
such that

|r(t,x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{\nu}),\quad|h(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{\nu}),\quad|L(t,x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{\nu}),\ \forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(v) The total number of jumps
N_{T}
is finite with probability 1, i.e.
P(N_{T}<\infty)=1
. Moreover, for all
i
from 0 to
n-1
, for any time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
with width
\Delta t=t_{i+1}-t_{i}>0
, the probability of there existing a jump within this interval tends to zero as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, i.e.
P(N_{t_{i+1}}-N_{t_{i}}=1)\rightarrow 0
as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
.

Assumption 1(i)-(iii) guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the SDE (
1
)(See Theorem 5.2.9 in
Karatzas and Shreve 2014
). Assumption 1(iv) ensures that
J(t,x)
is finite for any
(t,x)
. Assumption 1(v) ensures that the jump process has finite jumps in finite time.

Recall that J can be characterized by the following PDE based on the Feynman-Kac formula (
Karatzas and Shreve 2014
):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}J(t,x)+r(t,x)=0,\ (t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
J(T,x)=h(x),\end{array}\right.

where

\mathcal{L}J(t,x):=\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}(t,x)+b(t,x)\cdot\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t,x)\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x).

Assumption 2
. The above PDE admits a classical solution
J\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the polynomial growth condition, i.e., there exist constants
C>0
and
\mu\geq 1
such that

|J(t,x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{\mu}),\ \forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Assumption 2 guarantees the existence of a solution to the PDE (
2
).

To analyze this RL problem, we discretize the time interval
[0,T]
into
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
with equal time step
\Delta t
, where
i=0,1,2,...,n-1
and
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}=T
. The goal is to estimate the value function
J
using a parametric family of functions
J^{\theta}
with
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{l},l\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
(which could be reduced in complexity if we have some prior knowledge about the value function or the underlying dynamics) based on the observed states
X_{t_{i}}
and rewards
R_{t_{i}}=r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})
, without the knowledge of the explicit functional form of
b,\sigma,L,r,h
. We need the following assumption for
J^{\theta}
.

First, introduce

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}([0,T])=

\displaystyle\Big\{\kappa=\{\kappa_{t},0\leq t\leq T\}\text{ is real-valued and }\mathcal{F}_{t}\text{-progressively measurable:}

\displaystyle E\int_{0}^{T}|\kappa_{t}|^{2}dt<\infty\Big\}.

Define the
L^{2}
-norm
\|\kappa\|_{L^{2}}=(E\int_{0}^{T}|\kappa_{t}|^{2}dt)^{1/2}
on the space
L_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}([0,T])
.

Assumption 3
.
J^{\theta}(t,x)
is a smooth function of
(t,x,\theta)
with all the derivatives existing.
E|J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})|^{2}
is a continuous function of
\theta
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\theta\in\Theta
. Moreover, for all
\theta\in\Theta
,
\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(\cdot,X_{.}),|\frac{\partial J^{\theta}}{\partial x}(\cdot,X_{.})^{\dagger}\sigma(\cdot,X_{.})|\in L_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}([0,T])
, and their
L^{2}
-norms are continuous functions of
\theta
.

Assumption 3 ensures that
E|J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})|^{2}
,
\|\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(\cdot,X_{\cdot})\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|\frac{\partial J^{\theta}}{\partial x}(\cdot,X_{.})^{\dagger}\sigma(\cdot,X_{.})\|_{L^{2}}
are finite in any given compact set
\Theta
.

3
Algorithms

We commence our exploration by revisiting the Temporal-Difference (TD) Error proposed by
Doya 2000
and the Mean-Square TD Error (MSTDE) algorithm, along with its convergence behavior described by
Jia and Zhou 2021
.

3.1
Deterministic Setting

In the deterministic setting, the state
X_{t}
follows an ODE:

dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t})dt.

Since the states
X_{t}
are deterministic, the expectation equals itself, and equation (
2
) becomes:

J(t,X_{t})=\int_{t}^{T}r(s,X_{s})ds+h(X_{T})=J(t+\Delta t,X_{t+\Delta t})+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}r(s,X_{s})ds.

Moving
J(t,X_{t})
to the right side and dividing both sides by
\Delta t
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{J(t+\Delta t,X_{t+\Delta t})-J(t,X_{t})}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}r(s,X_{s})ds=0.

(5)

As
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, the left side of (
5
) becomes:

\displaystyle\delta_{t}:=\frac{d}{dt}J(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t}),

which is the the well-known TD Error. When estimating the value function
J
using
J^{\theta}
with parameter
\theta
, the TD Error becomes:

\delta_{t}^{\theta}:=\frac{d}{dt}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t}).

Building upon the above TD Error,
Doya 2000
introduced the mean-square TD error (MSTDE):

\text{MSTDE}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\delta_{t}^{\theta}\right|^{2}dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{d}{dt}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t})\right|^{2}dt.

To solve the RL problem using the observed discretized data
X_{t_{i}}
and
r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})
,
Jia and Zhou 2021
defined the
\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)
which is an approximation of
\text{MSTDE}(\theta)
:

\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left[\frac{J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}+r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})\right]^{2}\Delta t,

and proposed an algorithm to update
\theta
by minimizing the
\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)
using the gradient descent method.

3.2
Stochastic Setting

In scenarios where the state
X_{t}
follows a continuous SDE:

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}.

(6)

We first recall the findings of
El Karoui et al. 1997
, the PDE (
2
) is connected to the following forward-backward stochastic differential equation (FBSDE):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dX_{s}=b(s,X_{s})ds+\sigma(s,X_{s})dW_{s},\ s\in[t,T];\ X_{t}=x,\\
dY_{s}=-r(s,X_{s})ds+Z_{s}dW_{s},\ s\in[t,T];\ Y_{T}=h(X_{T}).\end{array}\right.

The value function
J(s,X_{s})
and the solution
\{(X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s}),\ t\leq s\leq T\}
of the above FBSDE have the following relationship:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{s}=J(s,X_{s}),\ s\in[t,T],\\
Z_{s}=\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}(s,X_{s})^{\dagger}\sigma(s,X_{s}),\ s\in[t,T].\end{array}\right.

For any fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{X_{s},\ t\leq s\leq T\}
solving the first equation of (
3.2
), define

\displaystyle M_{s}:=J(s,X_{s})+\int_{t}^{s}r(u,X_{u})du,\ s\in[t,T].

(11)

By combining (
3.2
) and (
3.2
), we observe the following result:

\displaystyle dM_{s}

\displaystyle=dJ(s,X_{s})+r(s,X_{s})ds

\displaystyle=dY_{s}+r(s,X_{s})ds

\displaystyle=Z_{s}dW_{s}.

Assumption 1 guarantees that
E[\int_{0}^{T}Z_{s}^{2}ds]<\infty
, which implies
M_{s}
is a martingale. The martingale property implies that for any
\Delta t>0
:

\displaystyle E[M_{t+\Delta t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=M_{t},

plug in the formula for
M
, we have:

\displaystyle E\left[J(t+\Delta t,X_{t+\Delta t})+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}r(s,X_{s})ds\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=J(t,X_{t}).

Similar to (
5
), we obtain:

E\left[\frac{J(t+\Delta t,X_{t+\Delta t})-J(t,X_{t})}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}r(s,X_{s})ds\right]=0.

As
\Delta t
goes to zero,
\frac{d}{dt}J(t,X_{t})
does not exist due to the non-differentiability of Brownian motion. Although MSTDE does not theoretically exist in this setting, we can still define the
\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)
similar to the deterministic case:

\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}E\left[\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}+r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})\right)^{2}\Delta t\right].

Similar to (
11
), we can define:

M^{\theta}_{s}:=J^{\theta}(s,X_{s})+\int_{t}^{s}r(u,X_{u})du.

Then,

\displaystyle\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}E\left[\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})+r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})\Delta t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2\Delta t}E\left[\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}r(s,X_{s})ds)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}E\left[\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(M^{\theta}_{t_{i+1}}-M^{\theta}_{t_{i}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{1}{2\Delta t}E\langle M^{\theta}\rangle_{T}.

The above limit holds as the size of the partition goes to 0. This provides the intuition that minimizing the
\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)
will guide the optimal parameter
\theta^{*}
towards minimizing the expected quadratic variation of
M^{\theta}
. By Itô’s formula, we have:

\displaystyle dM_{t}^{\theta}=\left[\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t})\right]dt+\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}.

Hence,

\displaystyle d\langle M^{\theta}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\equiv(dM_{t}^{\theta})^{2}=\left\{\left[\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t})\right]dt+\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})\right]^{2}(dW_{t})^{2}+\left[\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t})\right]^{2}(dt)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left[\mathcal{L}J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})+r(t,X_{t})\right]dt\cdot\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})\right]^{2}dt+\text{ high-order small term},

where the last line is due to
(dW_{t})^{2}=dt
and the fact that the terms involving
(dt)^{2}
and
dW_{t}dt
are of higher order than
dt
. Therefore, minimizing the expected quadratic variation of
M^{\theta}
is equivalent to minimizing
E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})\right|^{2}dt\right]
. Since the reward function
r(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})
actually does not contribute to the quadratic variation of
M^{\theta}
as seen before, we use the following error term instead of
\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}(\theta)
:

\displaystyle\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}^{*}(\theta):=E\left[\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})\right)^{2}\right].

(12)

Jia and Zhou 2021
proved the following result (Theorem 2 in
Jia and Zhou 2021
):

Suppose the state
X_{t}
follows the equation (
6
) and assumptions 1-3 hold. Let
\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}(\Delta t)\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}^{*}(\theta)
, and assume that
\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}(\Delta t)
exists. Then, we have:

\theta_{\text{MSTDE}}^{\star}\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t})\right|^{2}dt\right].

3.3
Stochastic setting with jumps

To extend the MSTDE algorithm to the scenarios where the state
X_{t}
has a jump part added, i.e.,
X_{t}
follows SDE (
1
).
We would like the algorithm to converge to a quantity associated with the continuous part rather than with the jump process. For example, if
X_{t}
represents the stock price, we would like the algorithm to converge to the rate of the return, represented by
\int_{0}^{T}b(t,X_{t})dt
, and to the random fluctuations due to the market volatility, represented by
\int_{0}^{T}\sigma(t,X_{t})dW_{t}
, rather than to the jump components, represented by
\int_{0}^{T}L(t,X_{t})dN_{t}
. However the MSTDE algorithm, as explained in the previous section, converges to the expected quadratic variation of
M^{\theta}
, which is now linked to the behavior of the jump process.

Theorem 1
. Suppose the state
X_{t}
follows the equation (
1
) and assumptions 1-3 hold. Let

\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}(\Delta t)\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\text{MSTDE}_{\Delta t}^{*}(\theta),

and assume that
\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}(\Delta t)
exists. Then

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{MSTDE}^{*}}^{\star}\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t-})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t-})\right|^{2}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\left[J^{\theta}(t,X_{t})-J^{\theta}(t,X_{t-})\right]^{2}dN_{t}\right],

(13)

where
X_{t-}:=\lim_{\Delta t\searrow 0}X_{t\Delta t}
is the left limit of
X_{t}
.

To address the issue posed by the presence of jump components in the state dynamics, we introduce the Mean-Square Bipower Variation Error (MSBVE) as an alternative error term. The MSBVE is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{MSBVE}_{\Delta t}(\theta):=E\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|J^{\theta}(t_{i+1},X_{t_{i+1}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})\right|\left|J^{\theta}(t_{i},X_{t_{i}})-J^{\theta}(t_{i-1},X_{t_{i-1}})\right|\right].

(14)

Unlike the MSTDE, which considers the squared difference between consecutive value function estimates, the MSBVE uses the absolute difference. This modification eliminates the influence of jump components, enabling the algorithm to focus on the continuous part of the state dynamics.

Theorem 2
. Suppose the state
X_{t}
follows the equation (
1
) and assumptions 1-3 hold. Let

\theta_{\text{MSBVE}}^{\star}(\Delta t)\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\text{MSBVE}_{\Delta t}(\theta),

and assume that
\theta_{\text{MSBVE}}^{\star}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\theta_{\text{MSBVE}}^{\star}(\Delta t)
exists. Then

\theta_{\text{MSBVE}}^{\star}\in\arg\min\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\left(\frac{\partial J^{\theta}(t,X_{t-})}{\partial x}\right)^{\dagger}\sigma(t,X_{t-})\right|^{2}dt\right].

By employing the MSBVE algorithm, we expect that the optimal parameter vector
\theta^{\star}
will converge to the same quantity associated solely with the continuous part as in the setting presented in section 3.2, even in scenarios where the state exhibits jumps. The effectiveness of this approach will be demonstrated through numerical simulations and examples in the subsequent section.

4
Simulation

In our simulation set up, we consider the state
X_{t}
, defined on the interval
t\in[0,1]
, follows
dX_{t}=dW_{t}+X_{t-}dN_{t}
where
W_{t}
is the standard Brownian Motion,
N_{t}
is the Poisson process independent of
W_{t}
. We only select those Poisson process paths where exactly one jump occurs in
[0,1]
, which implies the jump occurs uniformly within the interval
[0,1]
. The value function
J
is approximated by
J^{\theta}(t,x)=(\theta(1-t)+1)x
. Under this setting,

These findings suggest that the MSBVE algorithm is more robust in the presence of stochastic process with jumps compared to MSTDE, meaning it converges to a quantity related only to the continuous part of the process even when jump components are added. The robustness enables efficient convergence to the desired parameter values even in challenging scenarios with significant variability.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have explored the problem of estimating the value function in continuous-time reinforcement learning under stochastic dynamics with potential jump components. To address this problem by updating a parameter vector to minimize different error metrics, we revisited the Mean-Square TD Error (MSTDE) algorithm and introduced the Mean-Square Bipower Variation Error (MSBVE) algorithm. Our algorithm converges to an optimal parameter which when plugging into the estimated value function, provides a good approximation of the true value function.

We have demonstrated that the MSBVE algorithm offers improved robustness and convergence behavior compared to the MSTDE algorithm. It’s important to note that if the dynamics follow a SDE with no jumps, both algorithms are equivalent. In scenarios where the dynamics follow a SDE with jumps, MSBVE converges to a quantity related only to the continuous part while MSTDE converges to the quantity related to both the continuous part and the jump part. Our findings suggest that considering alternative error metrics, such as the mean-square quadratic variation error, can enhance the performance and reliability of RL algorithms in continuous-time settings, particularly when having a diffusion with jumps.

Moving forward, future research could explore extensions and refinements of the MSBVE algorithm, as well as investigate its application to real-world RL problems with complex dynamics and noise conditions. Additionally, further theoretical analysis and empirical studies could provide deeper insights into the convergence properties and computational efficiency of the proposed algorithm, paving the way for advancements in continuous-time reinforcement learning methodologies.
