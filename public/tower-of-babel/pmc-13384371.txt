Meta-Analysis of the Prognostic Value of Skin Perfusion Parameters in Sepsis Patients: Evidence Integration Based on the Mottling Score, Capillary Refill Time, and Peripheral Perfusion Index
Authors: Li Juan Shi, Shu Fen Ke, Li Sang Fu, Li Mei Zhu, Liang Ying Chen, Yao Ning Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Despite advances in intensive care, sepsis and septic shock still have high morbidity and mortality. Microcirculatory disturbance is a key issue in sepsis, leading to hypoperfusion, hypoxia, and organ failure. The skin is a sensitive indicator of microcirculatory changes and often shows early systemic changes. Recently, bedside skin perfusion indicators such as the mottling score (MS), capillary refill time (CRT), and peripheral perfusion index (PPI) have become common in practice. However, their value in predicting outcomes lacks strong evidence.Methods:A systematic search was conducted across databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, EBSCO, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, and VIP, with a search period spanning from the inception of the databases to January 2026. Cohort studies or case–control studies investigating the correlation between skin perfusion parameters and the prognosis of sepsis patients (primary outcomes: 28-day mortality, 14-day mortality, and in-hospital mortality) were included. Two researchers independently performed literature screening, data extraction, and assessment of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) bias risk. A meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4.1 software.Results:This study included a total of 22 articles, encompassing 2,727 study subjects. The meta-analysis results demonstrated that: 1) MS was significantly associated with 28-day mortality [odds ratio (OR) = 2.27, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.79–2.87, P < 0.001] and 14-day mortality (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.32–2.91, P < 0.001); 2) prolonged CRT duration was significantly associated with 28-day mortality (OR = 3.29, 95% CI: 2.08–5.21, P < 0.001) and in-hospital mortality (OR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.18–5.12, P < 0.001); 3) reduced PPI was significantly associated with 28-day mortality (OR = 5.05, 95% CI: 3.65–6.98, P < 0.001) and in-hospital mortality (OR = 4.56, 95% CI: 3.32–6.26, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis confirmed robust results with no significant publication bias.Conclusion:The MS, CRT, and PPI, as bedside and noninvasive skin perfusion parameters, demonstrate significant predictive value for mortality risk in sepsis patients. Integrating these indicators facilitates early clinical identification of high-risk patients, guiding risk stratification, and resuscitation therapy. Future large-sample prospective studies are required to further standardize their assessment protocols and intervention thresholds.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated immune response to infection and is one of the most important public health problems worldwide (1). Approximately 50 million cases of sepsis occur globally each year, with around 11 million deaths, resulting in high mortality rates, substantial consumption of medical resources, and imposing a heavy burden on healthcare systems (2). Despite the continuous improvement in medical standards and the application of new technologies,it remains a highly lethal disease. Even under modern intensive care conditions, the ICU mortality rate for sepsis patients remains as high as 25% - 35%,posing a serious threat to patient survival (3). Therefore, early identification of high-risk patients and timely intervention are critical steps in improving sepsis prognosis (4).

Traditional prognostic assessment methods for sepsis predominantly rely on macroscopic clinical indicators, such as serum lactate levels, the Sepsis-Related Organ Failure Assessment score, and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score. These indicators primarily reflect systemic hemodynamic and organ function changes, yet fail to promptly reflect microcirculatory perfusion status at the tissue level (5). Studies have shown that microcirculatory dysfunction is one of the core pathological mechanisms in the pathogenesis of sepsis, characterized by reduced capillary blood flow, uneven perfusion, and endothelial dysfunction, among others (6,7). Multiple research findings have demonstrated that these alterations can lead to tissue hypoxia and cellular metabolic disturbances, ultimately resulting in organ failure (8,9). Early studies found that microcirculatory abnormalities may persist in sepsis patients even after macrohemodynamic parameters normalize, significantly increasing the risk of death (10). Furthermore, microcirculatory perfusion abnormalities are closely associated with inadequate oxygen delivery and organ dysfunction, constituting an independent risk factor for the prognosis of sepsis patients (11). Recent studies have further revealed that microcirculation-related biomarkers and perfusion indicators can more accurately reflect tissue hypoperfusion earlier, providing a new direction for the early identification of sepsis (12). Therefore, real-time monitoring of microcirculation status is considered a critical strategy for optimizing sepsis treatment and improving prognosis (13).

As the largest organ of the human body, the skin’s microcirculatory changes can directly reflect the overall perfusion status of the organism, and are regarded as an important “window” for observing microcirculatory function (14). In recent years, variou bedside skin perfusion assessment indicators have been gradually applied in clinical practice, including the mottling score (MS), capillary refill time (CRT), and peripheral perfusion index (PPI). These indicators offer advantages such as being noninvasive, simple, reproducible, and capable of real-time dynamic monitoring, enabling rapid reflection of peripheral tissue perfusion status (15,16). Studies have confirmed that elevated MS, prolonged CRT, and decreased PPI are significantly associated with mortality risk in sepsis patients, serving as important indicators for assessing disease severity and predicting clinical outcomes (17,18). Compared with traditional macroscopic indicators, these skin perfusion parameters can identify occult tissue hypoperfusion earlier, providing an important basis for early clinical intervention (19,20).

However, although existing studies have suggested that skin perfusion parameters possess good prognostic predictive value, most current research consists of single-center observational studies with small sample sizes, inconsistent measurement methods, and threshold criteria, leading to certain heterogeneity in results. There is still a lack of high-quality, evidence-based systematic evaluation of their predictive efficacy (21). Therefore, this study conducted a meta-analysis to integrate existing research evidence, systematically evaluating the predictive value of three skin perfusion parameters—MS, CRT, and PPI—in septic patients, aiming to provide higher-quality evidence-based support for early clinical risk stratification and precision therapy (22).

METHODS

The literature retrieval strategy was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (23) guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Aim

The primary aim of this study was to systematically evaluate and integrate the existing evidence on the prognostic value of bedside skin perfusion parameters—specifically the MS, CRT, and PPI—in predicting mortality among adult patients with sepsis or septic shock.

Study design and ethics

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple international and Chinese databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, EBSCO, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, and VIP, from their inception to January 15, 2026.

The study included cohort studies (prospective or retrospective) and case–control studies that investigated the association between skin perfusion parameters and prognostic outcomes (e.g. 28-day, 14-day, or in-hospital mortality) in adult patients diagnosed with sepsis or septic shock. Two reviewers independently carried out the literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

As this study is a meta-analysis based on published data, ethical approval and patient consent were not required.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: 1) study subjects were patients with sepsis/septic shock (age ≥18 years); 2) the study content included clinical research on the prognostic prediction of sepsis patients using skin perfusion parameters (MS, CRT, and PPI); 3) the study design was a case–control study, prospective or retrospective cohort study; 4) data needed to be complete and comparable, with outcome indicators including but not limited to 28-day mortality rate, and 14-day in-hospital mortality rate.

Exclusion criteria: 1) duplicate publications; 2) non-Chinese/English literature; 3) inability to obtain full-text.

Population intervention comparator outcome

Population: Adult patients diagnosed with sepsis or septic shock.

Index prognostic factors: Skin perfusion parameters, including MS, CRT, and PPI.

Comparator: Patients with normal versus abnormal skin perfusion parameter values.

Outcomes: Primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality and 14-day mortality.

Retrieval strategy

The system searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, EBSCO, China, Wanfang Database, and VIP Database; the search terms combined subject terms and free words. Chinese search terms included “sepsis/septicemia/septic shock,” “skin perfusion parameters/mottling score/capillary refill time/peripheral perfusion index,” “mortality, prognosis, prediction, adverse outcomes,” “observational studies/cohort studies.” English search terms included “Sepsis/Septic Shock sepsis, Bloodstream/Severe Sepsis/Sepsis, Severe/Pyemia/Pyemias/Shock, Septic/Bacteremia,” “Skin perfusion indicators, mottling score/mottling capillary refill time/CRT, peripheral perfusion index/PPI/perfusion index,” “Prognosis/prognosis/mortality/survival/outcome.” The search time frame was from the database establishment to January 15, 2026. Literature screening and data extraction

The literature was independently screened, data extracted, and cross-verified by two researchers. Data extraction from the included literature covered the first author, publication date, population, sample size, skin perfusion parameter grouping, and prognostic indicators.

Literature quality evaluation

The NOS was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies through a nonrandomized study bias risk assessment method (24). The NOS implemented a semi-quantitative star-rating system for literature quality evaluation, comprising three dimensions: selection criteria (four items, four points), inter-group comparability (one item, two points), and exposure criteria (three items, three points), all scored out of nine. A minimum score of six was required to meet the quality reliability threshold. The assessment was conducted independently by two researchers, with disagreements resolved through discussion and consultation with a third party to reach a consensus.

Statistical method

Statistical analysis of the included literature was performed using RevMan 5.4.1 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). To investigate the prognostic value of skin perfusion parameters in sepsis patients, subgroup analyses were conducted based on the classification of skin perfusion parameters in the original studies. For continuous variables, the effect size was expressed as mean difference; for binary variables, the effect size was expressed as odds ratio (OR). All effective measures were reported as 95% confidence intervals (CI). Heterogeneity analysis among the included studies was performed using the default Q test method in RevMan (α = 0.05), with the I2 value calculated.

When I2 ≤ 50% and P ≥ 0.05, a fixed-effects model was used to analyze the data, indicating homogeneity between studies. When I2 > 50% and P < 0.05, heterogeneity was considered, and further analysis was conducted to identify the source of heterogeneity. After excluding significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to analyze the data, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the source of heterogeneity by sequentially excluding any individual study from the included analysis and then combining the remaining studies for analysis.

RESULTS

Literature search results

Through initial database screening, 1,247 articles were obtained, including 112 in Chinese and 1,135 in English. Using Zotero 6.0, 752 duplicates were removed. After reviewing titles and abstracts, 443 articles that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, leaving 52 articles initially selected. Full-text access was obtained for 3 articles, while in-depth reading and further screening excluded 27 articles that did not meet the criteria. Ultimately, 22 articles (25–46) were included. Among the 22 included articles (25–46), 21 were cohort studies and one was a case–control study, enrolling a total of 2,727 patients. According to the NOS, 11 studies were of high quality (NOS score ≥ 8 points) and 11 were of moderate quality (NOS score = 6–7 points). Meta-analysis results

For studies with multiple time points or anatomic sites, only one primary dataset was included per outcome to avoid unit-of-analysis bias. Two of these studies measured at two different time points: 40 minutes after admission and 6 hours after resuscitation (H0, H6) (30,38). One study conducted two measurements at different sites: the knee joint and the fingertip (28). One study simultaneously reported the effects of two different skin perfusion parameters on prognosis: the impact of MS and CRT on mortality in sepsis patients (33). Therefore, the analyzed parameters totaled (17).

28-day mortality rate

A meta-analysis was conducted on (25–46) articles from the 22 included studies. Based on the classification of skin perfusion parameters in the original research, subgroups were formed to compare the effects of different skin perfusion parameters (MS group, CRT group, PPI group) on 28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, 14-day mortality, and 24-hour mortality in sepsis patients. Five articles (30,32–34,46) reported the prognostic value of the MS in sepsis patients, with low heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 12%, P = 0.34). Fixed-effect model analysis showed statistically significant differences [OR = 2.27, 95% CI (1.79–2.87), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 3(A)). Four articles (43,47–49) reported the prognostic value of CRT in sepsis patients, with heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 68%, P = 0.01). Random-effects model analysis showed statistically significant differences [OR = 3.29, 95% CI (2.08–5.21), P < 0.00001]. After excluding the study by Morocho et al. (38), the heterogeneity decreased to 0%, with the difference remaining statistically significant [OR = 4.25, 95% CI (3.21–5.62), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 3(B)).

Forest plot of comprehensive analysis results (A) mottling score; (B) capillary refill time; (C) peripheral perfusion index. CI, confidence interval; IV, inverse variance; SD, standard deviation.

In-hospital mortality rate

Two studies (28,46) reported the prognostic value of CRT in sepsis patients, showing moderate heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 32%, P = 0.23). Analysis using a random-effects model revealed statistically significant differences [OR = 2.46, 95% CI (1.18–5.12), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 3(B)). Three studies (27,43,45) reported the prognostic value of PPI in sepsis patients, demonstrating homogeneity among studies (I2 = 0%, P = 0.67). Analysis using a fixed-effects model showed statistically significant differences [OR = 4.56, 95% CI (3.32–6.26), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 3(C)).

14-day mortality rate

Four studies (25,26,29,35) reported the prognostic value of the MS in sepsis patients, with significant heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 81%, P = 0.002). Analysis using a fixed-effects model revealed statistically significant differences [OR = 1.96, 95% CI (1.32–2.91), P < 0.0009]. After excluding the study by Ait-Oufella et al. (25), the heterogeneity decreased to 0%, with the difference remaining statistically significant [OR = 2.32, 95% CI (1.85–2.91), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 3(A)).

Skin perfusion parameters are significantly associated with the prognosis of sepsis patients

This study integrated recent evidence on the relationship between skin perfusion parameters and the prognosis of sepsis patients. The results consistently demonstrated that a high MS, prolonged CRT, and reduced PPI were all closely associated with 28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, and 14-day mortality. The combined effect size of these three indicators reached moderate to high levels of association, suggesting their significant value in early risk stratification and outcome prediction.

Subgroup analysis of the 28-day mortality rate and in-hospital mortality rate in the MS showed (Chi2 = 0.58, P = 0.45) (Fig. 3(A)), indicating no significant difference in the predictive efficacy of the MS for 28-day mortality and 14-day mortality. Subgroup analysis of the 28-day mortality rate and in-hospital mortality rate for CRT and PPI showed (Chi2 = 0.43, P = 0.51) (Fig. 3(B)) and (Chi2 = 0.20, P = 0.66) (Fig. 3(C)), indicating no significant difference in the predictive efficacy of PPI for 28-day mortality and in-hospital mortality.

In terms of effect size, PPI reduction demonstrated the strongest predictive power for mortality risk, followed by CRT prolongation and elevated MS. All three showed good directional consistency and statistical stability. The heterogeneity among studies was generally low in outcome analyses, with minimal changes in combined results after sensitivity analysis and sequential study exclusion, indicating the robustness and reliability of the conclusions.

Subgroup analyses across different outcome types (28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, and 14-day mortality), study populations (sepsis and septic shock patients), and measurement time points consistently demonstrated favorable trends, indicating that skin perfusion parameters exhibit broad applicability and clinical relevance in predicting mortality risk. The publication bias assessment revealed no significant bias, further reinforcing the reliability of the study’s conclusions. As a rapid bedside microcirculatory perfusion indicator, skin perfusion parameters provide critical evidence for early condition assessment, risk stratification, and prognosis evaluation in sepsis patients, offering essential guidance for clinical decision-makers in developing resuscitation strategies and monitoring treatment responses.

The funnel plot analysis of publication bias revealed a generally symmetrical distribution of study points, suggesting a low likelihood of publication bias. Sensitivity analysis conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies demonstrated no change in the direction of the pooled effect size, indicating stable results from the meta-analysis (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

This study conducted a systematic meta-analysis to integrate and quantitatively compare the prognostic value of three bedside skin perfusion parameters—MS, CRT, and PPI—in sepsis patients. All three indicators effectively predict short-term mortality, with PPI showing the strongest predictive power (OR = 5.05 for 28-day mortality), followed by CRT (OR = 3.29) and MS (OR = 2.27).

Clinical value of mottled score

The results of this study indicate that elevated MSs are significantly associated with mortality at 14 and 28 days in sepsis patients. Its predictive value stems from its pathophysiological basis: cutaneous mottling is an intuitive sign of septic microcirculatory disturbance, caused by sympathetic excitation-induced heterogeneous peripheral vasoconstriction, uneven blood flow distribution, and endothelial dysfunction, directly reflecting the severity of tissue hypoperfusion (24,36–39). Our findings are highly consistent with the pioneering work of Ait-Oufella et al. (25,35) and Kazune et al. (34), further consolidating the status of the MS as an early warning tool.

However, the clinical application of the MS has significant limitations. Its evaluation is highly dependent on the subjective judgment of the observer and is susceptible to interference from factors such as patient skin pigmentation, ambient light, and temperature. This may lead to discrepancies in assessment results among different researchers or even within the same researcher at different time points. Although studies have attempted to improve consistency through standardized training (15), its inherent subjectivity remains a barrier to widespread adoption.

Clinical application of capillary refill time

CRT prolongation has been demonstrated to be closely associated with 28-day mortality and in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients, which aligns with the position of the Save Sepsis Campaign Guidelines that recommend it as an adjunct resuscitation indicator (47). The predictive value of CRT lies in its ability to sensitively reflect the early redistribution of peripheral blood flow during shock—when the body prioritizes perfusion to core organs such as the heart and brain, perfusion to peripheral tissues (e.g., fingertips) is initially reduced, leading to an extension of CRT (48).

In the early stage of shock, due to sympathetic nervous system excitation, peripheral and visceral organ perfusion decreases, allowing blood redistribution.

Blood flows from peripheral tissues and organs to vital organs (lungs, heart, brain). Consequently, insufficient blood flow to peripheral tissues manifests earlier than macrocirculatory hemodynamic disturbances. As a blood flow sensitive index, the normalization of CRT is parallel to the increase of local blood flow (49). Therefore, compared with urine output, lactate, and mean arterial pressure, CRT may be more sensitive in guiding the early phase of fluid resuscitation, making it a microcirculatory parameter that should not be overlooked. Mrganm et al. found that CRT before hospital admission was a predictor of 1-day and 7-day all-cause mortality in emergency patients (50,51). Lara et al. (52) demonstrated that CRT prolongation is strongly associated with tissue hypoperfusion in sepsis patients (17). As a biomarker reflecting microcirculatory status, CRT exhibits significant correlations with lactate levels and mortality rates, providing critical support for early clinical warning.

However, the gold-standard method for CRT measurement has yet to be standardized. The traditional manual compression technique is highly susceptible to variations in the operator’s applied pressure magnitude, duration, and ambient temperature. This variability is the primary cause of significant differences in cutoff values across studies (ranging from 3.25 seconds to 4.85 seconds) and may also account for the high heterogeneity observed in the CRT subgroup analysis of this study (I2 = 68%). To overcome this challenge, researchers have begun exploring objective measurement methods, such as digital CRT monitors, automated pneumatic devices, and integrated fiber optic sensors (53), among others. This variability is the primary cause of significant differences in cutoff values across studies (ranging from 3.25 seconds to 4.85 seconds) and may also account for the moderate heterogeneity observed in the CRT subgroup analysis of this meta-analysis. This lack of standardization currently limits the direct comparability of findings across different clinical settings.

Predictive role of peripheral perfusion index

The most prominent finding of this study is that PPI demonstrates the strongest predictive efficacy for mortality risk among the three. Pulse oximetry (PPI) quantifies pulsatile blood flow components non-invasively and continuously, with its values reflecting peripheral vasoconstriction and insufficient microcirculatory perfusion (54). Compared with the subjective MS and the easily influenced CRT, the advantage of PPI lies in its objectivity and dynamic monitorability, making it particularly suitable for scenarios requiring precise titration of resuscitation therapy (28,55).

The study by Yu Chao et al. (56) demonstrated that the skin exhibits a correlation with the microcirculation of vital organs. By monitoring changes in hemodynamic parameters of skin tissue perfusion, the blood flow status of critical organs can be synchronously reflected. During septic shock, adequate circulating blood volume is maintained through sufficient blood volume, stroke volume, and peripheral vascular resistance. In response to shock, peripheral tissue vasodilation occurs, reducing perfusion. Multicenter observational studies indicate that, unlike macrocirculatory targets (e.g., lactate), microcirculatory parameters such as PPIs provide a more direct reflection of peripheral tissue perfusion status and are associated with mortality (57).

It is crucial to emphasize that the pooled ORs from our meta-analysis robustly support a significant prognostic association between abnormal skin perfusion parameters (MS, CRT, and PPI) and increased mortality in sepsis patients. However, these pooled estimates do not establish universal, clinically applicable cutoff values for CRT or PPI. As shown in our results (Table 1), the cutoff values varied widely across the included studies (e.g., CRT: 3.25–4.85 seconds; PPI: 0.2–1.86). This heterogeneity underscores the current lack of standardization and highlights that definitive diagnostic or intervention thresholds for these parameters must be derived from future, large-scale prospective studies with standardized measurement protocols.

Despite the promising prospects of PPI, its clinical application still faces challenges. The greatest obstacle lies in the lack of a unified intervention threshold, as evidenced by the wide range of cutoff values (0.2–1.86) in the included studies. This heterogeneity in thresholds, likely stemming from variations in device models, probe placement sites, and measurement timing, represents a significant limitation for cross-center comparison and clinical implementation. It underscores that our pooled odds ratios, while robustly supporting a prognostic association, do not establish universal cutoff values.

Synergistic effects among predictive indicators and clinical integration pathways

When evaluated individually, the three indicators each have their strengths and weaknesses: the MS is the most intuitive but relatively subjective; CRT is user-friendly but prone to interference; PPI is the most objective and continuous but device-dependent with unclear thresholds. Therefore, a combined application may be the optimal strategy. For example, integrating the intuitive MS with the objective PPI can both rapidly identify high-risk patients and assess resuscitation efficacy through continuous PPI monitoring. The ANDROMEDA-SHOCK trial by Hernandez et al. demonstrated that a resuscitation strategy targeting CRT normalization outperforms a strategy solely focused on lactic acid reduction in improving organ function, suggesting that a comprehensive evaluation system based on skin perfusion holds significant clinical value. The pooled ORs strongly support prognostic associations between abnormal skin perfusion parameters and mortality, but do not define universal clinical cutoffs for CRT or PPI. Cutoff values varied widely across included studies (CRT: 3.25–4.85 seconds; PPI: 0.2–1.86), reflecting differences in measurement protocols, devices, and populations. Standardized thresholds are still needed for clinical implementation. Therefore, the proposed strategy of combining MS, CRT, and PPI for integrated risk assessment remains hypothesis-generating and warrants validation in well-designed prospective clinical trials.

MS, CRT, and PPI are simple, noninvasive, bedside tools that strongly predict short-term mortality in sepsis patients. PPI shows the strongest prognostic performance. These parameters support early risk stratification, but standardized measurement protocols and universal clinical cutoffs require further large-scale prospective studies.

CONCLUSIONS

This study conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the prognostic predictive value of different skin perfusion parameters (MS, CRT, and PPI) in sepsis patients, assessing the predictive efficacy of these bedside noninvasive indicators. The results demonstrated that elevated MS, prolonged CRT, and decreased PPI were significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, and 14-day mortality in sepsis patients. These findings provide valuable insights for clinical practice.

Through a comprehensive analysis of existing literature, we found that these skin perfusion parameters possess significant predictive value, particularly in clinical practice. As bedside rapid assessment tools, they can provide real-time reflection of patients’ microcirculatory status and offer evidence for early intervention. Notably, PPI, as a continuous, noninvasive, and easily monitorable parameter, demonstrates high clinical application potential in the prognostic evaluation of sepsis patients.

This study still has certain limitations. The included studies were primarily observational, posing potential bias risks; the measurement methods and criteria for the MS, CRT, and PPI varied across different studies, which may affect the comparability of effect sizes. Further large-sample, multicenter prospective studies are still needed to validate the standardized use and thresholds of these cutaneous perfusion parameters in the assessment of sepsis prognosis.

Limitations

Despite the comprehensive and systematic approach employed in this meta-analysis, First, as all included studies were observational in design, they establish association but not causation between abnormal skin perfusion parameters and mortality. Second, our analysis was restricted to short-term mortality outcomes (e.g., 14-day, 28-day, in-hospital); the prognostic value of these parameters for long-term survival and functional recovery remains unexplored. Finally, although not unique to our study, the general lack of standardized measurement protocols for these bedside parameters across the literature must be acknowledged as a constraint on the immediate clinical translation of our pooled estimates.

Future directions

Based on the findings and limitations of this meta-analysis, several important directions for future research are proposed.

First, there is an urgent need for large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies with standardized protocols for measuring skin perfusion parameters. Such studies should employ uniform measurement techniques, anatomical sites, and timing of assessments to establish reliable cutoff values and enhance the comparability of findings across different clinical settings and populations.

Second, the development and validation of standardized measurement methods are critical priorities. For CRT, the exploration of objective measurement tools—such as digital CRT monitors, automated pneumatic devices, and fiber optic sensor-integrated systems—may help overcome the variability associated with manual compression techniques. For PPI, efforts should focus on establishing consensus regarding optimal device placement, measurement timing, and intervention thresholds through collaborative international initiatives.

Third, randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate whether resuscitation strategies guided by skin perfusion parameters, either alone or in combination, can improve patient-centered outcomes compared to conventional approaches. Building upon the foundation of trials like ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, future studies should investigate whether incorporating multiple perfusion parameters into treatment algorithms yields synergistic benefits.

Fourth, research should explore the integration of skin perfusion parameters with other prognostic tools, including traditional severity scores (Sepsis-Related Organ Failure Assessment, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II), biomarkers (lactate, procalcitonin), and emerging microcirculatory assessment technologies. Developing composite prediction models that combine multiple indicators may enhance prognostic accuracy and provide more comprehensive guidance for clinical decision-making.

Fifth, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the predictive value of these parameters for long-term outcomes, including 90-day and 1-year mortality, as well as functional recovery, organ dysfunction sequelae, and health-related quality of life in sepsis survivors.

Sixth, investigation of the dynamic changes in skin perfusion parameters over time, rather than isolated measurements, may provide richer prognostic information. Future studies should examine trajectories of these parameters during resuscitation and their association with outcomes, potentially identifying optimal timing for intervention and targets for resuscitation.

Seventh, research should extend to diverse populations and healthcare settings to validate the findings across different geographic regions, ethnic groups, and resource-limited environments where advanced monitoring technologies may not be available. Eighth, mechanistic studies exploring the pathophysiological basis linking skin perfusion abnormalities to organ dysfunction and mortality could provide deeper insights. Investigations into the relationships between these clinical signs and underlying microcirculatory alterations, endothelial dysfunction, and cellular metabolic disturbances may reveal novel therapeutic targets.

Such guidelines would facilitate the standardized implementation of these simple, noninvasive tools in routine clinical care, potentially improving early identification of high-risk patients and guiding resuscitation therapy in sepsis management.
