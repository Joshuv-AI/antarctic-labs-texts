Biomechanical comparison of double mini-locking plate versus lateral locking plate for Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures
Authors: Xiao Wang, Yongchang Chen, Li Dong, Gadisa Musa Wako, Yidong Xu, Fei Xiao, Haibo Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLateral locking plate (LLP) is a successful standard treatment for Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures. However, its uniplanar nature can be a limitation when insufficient bone is available at the distal fragment for secure screw fixation, particularly in low or osteoporotic fractures. This study evaluated the biomechanical performance of a novel biplanar double mini-locking plate (DMLP) system designed to address this challenge.MethodsTwenty left fibula Sawbones models were used to simulate Danis-Weber B fractures. The samples were divided into two groups, Group A (DMLP) and Group B (LLP), with 10 samples in each group. Biomechanical testing included quasistatic axial compression, torsional loading, and torsional failure tests to assess the stiffness, displacement, failure mode, and failure torque.ResultsBiomechanical testing revealed that Group A exhibited superior structural properties compared with Group B. In the axial compression test, the compression stiffness of Group A was 31% greater than that of Group B (402.8 ± 100.4 N/mm vs. 308.4 ± 60.51 N/mm, p = 0.0202). In the torsion tests, Group A demonstrated significantly greater torsional stiffness within the 0.25–0.5 N·m range (0.42 ± 0.05 N·m/° vs. 0.29 ± 0.17 N·m/°, p = 0.0172) and the 0.5–1.0 N·m range (0.39 ± 0.03 N·m/° vs. 0.27 ± 0.16 N·m/°, p = 0.0191). Additionally, the failure torque in Group A was approximately 26% greater (14.21 ± 2.01 N·m vs. 11.24 ± 1.47 N·m, p = 0.0036). Digital image correlation (DIC) analysis revealed that Group A had significantly reduced relative displacement at the fracture site under axial compression (-0.12 ± 0.06 mm vs. -0.32 ± 0.15 mm, p = 0.0011) and at 10° of torsion (0.68 ± 0.14 mm vs. 1.23 ± 0.21 mm, p < 0.0001). All fractures occurred at the proximal end, with no significant difference in failure location between the two groups (p = 1.000).ConclusionIn this in vitro biomechanical study using Sawbones models, the DMLP demonstrated superior performance compared to the LLP for the fixation of simulated Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures. Under the quasistatic loading conditions tested, the DMLP construct exhibited significantly greater axial stiffness, torsional stability, and failure torque. These findings provide a strong biomechanical rationale, based on this standardized experimental model, warranting further investigation for its potential clinical application.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Distal fibula fracture, Danis-Weber b, Double mini-locking plate, Lateral locking plate, Biomechanics, Sawbones

Introduction

Distal fibula fractures are among the most common ankle injuries, accounting for approximately 90% or more of all ankle fractures [1, 2]. Danis-Weber B fractures, which occur at the level of the syndesmosis, are the most common type of distal fibula fracture, representing 60–70% of cases [3]. These fractures are typically caused by external rotational forces on the ankle joint and are often associated with ligamentous injuries. If not properly treated, they can lead to complications such as ankle instability and posttraumatic arthritis [4].

Surgical intervention is the primary treatment for Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures [4, 5]. Lateral locking plate (LLP) is a widely accepted and successful treatment option that is valued for its simplicity and reliable fixation [4–6]. However, in specific, biomechanically demanding scenarios such as fractures with a low fracture line, severe comminution, or poor bone quality, where limited bone is available at the distal end for screw fixation, achieving adequate stability can remain a challenge [7]. While various strategies exist to augment fixation, such as adding a posterior plate, they can introduce issues such as screw interference or peroneal tendon irritation [6, 8].

Therefore, an ideal fixation strategy would enhance mechanical stability while simultaneously minimizing implant-related complications. With this goal of optimization in mind, our research team has developed a novel double mini-locking plate (DMLP) system (patent number: ZL201620358709.3) (Fig. 1). This system represents a significant departure from improvised dual-plating techniques. It is an integrated, purpose-built construct featuring two low-profile, mini-locking plates arranged in a vertical cross configuration with pre-engineered, collision-free screw trajectories. The posterior-lateral plate is anatomically contoured to minimize interference with the fibular tendons. The purpose of this study was to conduct the first biomechanical comparison of the initial stability provided by this novel DMLP system against a standard LLP in a simulated Danis-Weber B fracture model. We hypothesized that the biplanar DMLP construct would provide greater axial and torsional stiffness than the uniplanar LLP.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used fibula models and did not involve human or animal subjects; therefore, ethical approval was not required. A total of 20 Sawbones left fibula models (Absolute™ 4th Gen., #3427-1, Pacific Research Laboratories, USA) were used, all of which were standardized according to the Danis-Weber B distal fibula fracture model. The models were randomly divided into two groups, with 10 samples in each group. Group A was fixed using the DMLP, which was independently designed by the authors and manufactured by Double Medical Technology Inc., whereas Group B was fixed using an LLP from the same manufacturer (model #020302003). The detailed specifications of the plates and screws used in each group are provided in Figs. 1 and 2. The internal fixation material for both groups was titanium alloy. The biomechanical performance of the two fixation methods was compared under quasistatic axial compression and torsional loading. Fracture displacement and failure modes were recorded using a digital image correlation (DIC) system, which also captured failure images during the tests.

Surgical simulation

Following Rachel et al., Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures were created on fourth-generation Sawbones models using a 1.0 mm oscillating saw [9]. A 30° oblique osteotomy was performed anteroinferior to posterosuperior, starting 2.5 cm from the fibular tip, with a 2.0 mm gap. The fracture ends were held with a reduction clamp during fixation.

In Group A, the DMLP was used. The lateral plate was positioned 1.5 cm, and the posterolateral plate was positioned 1.3 cm proximal to the fibular tip, both of which were aligned with their respective bone ridges. All screws used in this group were 2.7 mm titanium alloy locking screws, except for one 2.7 mm non-locking cortical screw. Consistent with a bridging plate technique appropriate for the comminuted fracture model, a standardized screw insertion sequence was used to ensure stability at the proximal and distal ends of the construct [10–13]. For the lateral plate, screws were inserted into holes 2, 5, then 1. For the posterolateral plate, the non-locking cortical screw was first placed in hole 3 to bring the plate close to the bone surface, followed by locking screws at holes 4, 5, and 1.

In Group B, the LLP was placed according to the manufacturer’s recommendation, with its distal end aligned with the fibular tip. The plate used in this group was made of titanium alloy, and the screws were 3.5 mm in diameter and made of titanium alloy; all screws were of the locking type. By adopting a similar bridging strategy, a standardized screw insertion sequence was implemented to achieve stable fixation. The screw insertion sequence was as follows: holes 3, 4, 5, 6, and 1 (Figs. 1 and 2).

All surgical simulations were performed with rigorous standardization. In both groups, all screws distal to the fracture were unicortical to avoid breaching the distal tibiofibular joint. All locking screws were inserted using dedicated guides to ensure consistency. To ensure reproducible fixation, a strict protocol was followed: all screws were inserted by a single surgeon, and a calibrated torque-limiting driver was used for final tightening to ensure consistent fixation. Using this pre-set instrument, the 2.7 mm locking screws in Group A were tightened to a target torque of 1.2 N·m, with the exception of the single non-locking screw which was tightened to 0.8 N·m to achieve compression, and the 3.5 mm screws in Group B were tightened to a target torque of 2.5 N·m. The procedures were performed by the same surgeon with over 5 years of experience, under the supervision of a senior consultant. Postoperative radiographs confirmed accurate and consistent implant placement (Fig. 2).

Biomechanical evaluation

The biomechanical performance of the two fixation constructs in distal fibula fracture repair was assessed using a servo-hydraulic material testing system (Bionix Servo-hydraulic Test System; MTS Systems, Eden Prairie, MN, USA) in combination with an XTDIC three-dimensional full-field strain measurement system (XTDIC-FLEX-PL, XTOP 3D Technology (Xian) Co., Ltd., China) equipped with dual RICOH cameras.

Prior to testing, the fibula models were encapsulated with denture base resin—3 cm proximally and distally below the fracture line. Clay was prefilled in the distal plate, screw holes, and fracture gap to avoid resin interference and ensure that load transmission occurred solely through the bone–implant system, providing a more accurate clinical simulation [7]. The embedded specimens were mounted on the testing system, and the load was reset to eliminate gravitational influence.

To enable accurate DIC measurement, the proximal tibia and implant surfaces were spray-coated with matte white primer and overlaid with irregular black speckles as tracking markers [14]. Prior to each test, the system was calibrated using a manufacturer-supplied, high-precision calibration target to ensure sub-pixel measurement accuracy. After calibration, the DIC system was placed 91 cm from the sample, capturing data at 5 Hz for real-time surface deformation monitoring (Fig. 3).

For axial stiffness testing, a quasistatic axial load was applied in displacement control mode at 0.01 mm/s for three preconditioning cycles, ramping up to 100 N, following the protocol of Marianne et al. [7]. The maximum load of 100 N was deliberately chosen to simulate the axial force transmitted through the fibula (10–15% of body weight) for a 70 kg individual during a static bipedal stance [15, 16]. This step is critical as it simulates physiological compression, creating the necessary precondition to evaluate rotational stability. The first two cycles allowed for settling, and the third cycle was used for measurement. The axial stiffness was calculated as the slope between 30% and 70% of the maximum load on the linear load–displacement curve.

To ensure that torsional deformation was concentrated in the distal fibula, the bone was osteotomized, and the proximal 15 cm of the model was preserved and re-embedded [7]. Torsional loading was applied according to Rachel et al. [9]: the system rotated the specimen counterclockwise at 1°/s in displacement control mode, simulating external rotational stress. A constant 100 N axial preload was maintained using the hydraulic system during torsion. This constant preload serves a dual role: it simulates the physiological condition of static standing [15, 16], while ensuring a consistent bone-implant interface, which is critical for reliable and repeatable torsional measurements, a methodology consistent with prior biomechanical studies [7, 9]. Torque and angular displacement were recorded at 100 Hz. The torque was measured at 10° of external rotation, and the initial torsional stiffness was calculated from the torque–rotation curve between 0.25 and 0.5 N·m.

The failure criterion was defined as the occurrence of additional fractures or screw pullout. If no failure was observed, the torsional failure test was repeated. The DIC system continuously monitored bone surface deformation near the fracture line, and the high-speed camera captured the dynamic failure progression. All DIC data were analyzed using XTDIC software.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). The stiffness, failure load, and bone fragment displacement of the two groups were compared using an independent t-test. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied if the data did not follow a normal distribution. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the magnitude and precision of the observed differences. A post hoc power analysis based on the observed difference in failure torque yielded a Cohen’s d of 1.69. With 10 samples per group and α = 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 0.95, confirming that the sample size was sufficient to detect statistically and clinically meaningful differences.

Following normality assessment with the Shapiro-Wilk test, torsional stiffness data were analyzed with the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, while all other primary outcomes were normally distributed and compared using independent t-tests. Fisher’s exact test was used for the analysis of failure location.

All the samples successfully completed the failure test. Fractures occurred at the proximal end of all the samples, though the exact location varied. Most fractures were located near the proximal screws, while a few occurred at the embedding junction. No significant differences were found between the two groups (p = 1.000) (Table 3). Additionally, the failure torque in Group A was approximately 26% greater than that in Group B (p = 0.0036) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study compared the biomechanical performance of the DMLP and LLP for fixing simulated Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures within a controlled laboratory setting, utilizing standardized Sawbones models and quasistatic loading protocols. Our results demonstrated that the DMLP construct yielded superior axial stiffness, torsional stiffness, and failure load compared to the LLP. These findings provide a preliminary biomechanical rationale derived from this specific in vitro model, and may inform the design of future studies aimed at optimizing clinical treatment strategies for these challenging fractures.

Regarding axial stability, the DMLP demonstrated significantly greater axial stiffness than the LLP (402.84 ± 128.4 vs. 308.4 ± 60.51 N/mm, p = 0.0202). This advantage is likely due to the cross-fixation design of the DMLP, which increases the screw-bone contact area and provides multidirectional support, thus improving the overall stability of the construct [17, 18]. DIC measurements during axial compression revealed that the displacement at the fracture site in the DMLP group was significantly smaller than in the LLP group (− 0.12 ± 0.06 vs. −0.32 ± 0.15 mm, p = 0.0011). The negative displacement values indicate that the fracture fragments moved closer together, and the smaller displacement in the DMLP group suggests better control of micromotion, which positively impacts fracture healing [19–21].

The results also revealed that the DMLP exhibited significantly greater torsional stiffness, higher torque at 10° of external rotation, and less relative displacement at the same angle measured by DIC (p = 0.0172–0.0191, p = 0.0493, and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Tables 1 and 2). This indicates that the DMLP provides enhanced rotational resistance. This finding is highly relevant, as a consistent body of biomechanical literature has identified rotational instability as a critical concern in ankle fractures, arising from both fracture patterns and associated ligamentous injuries [7, 22–24]. To address this challenge, multiplanar fixation has been explored. For example, the addition of a posterior plate has been shown to significantly increase torsional stiffness in cadaveric models [25]. However, other cadaveric analyses suggest that a simple dual-plating construct with non-locked plates may not offer a significant biomechanical advantage over a single locked plate [9]. The DMLP, as a single, integrated implant providing locked, multiplanar fixation, was developed as one potential strategy to address this biomechanical challenge. Our finding of significantly increased torsional stiffness suggests that this integrated design may be an effective approach to enhancing rotational control in fibular fixation.

All the specimens successfully completed the predefined biomechanical loading protocols before failure testing. During the failure tests, fractures consistently occurred at the proximal end of the constructs, with the majority initiating around the proximal screw holes and a minority at the proximal embedding junction. A comparison between groups revealed no statistically significant difference in failure location (p = 1.000), indicating that the proximal region around the screws serves as a common stress concentration area in both the DMLP and LLP constructs under torsional loading. Post-failure inspection showed localized bone cracking near the proximal screws in both groups, further supporting the interpretation that failure resulted from stress concentration within the bone itself, manifesting as new fractures, rather than from mechanical failure at the implant–bone interface, such as screw loosening or pullout [26, 27].

The consistent failure location at the proximal end may be partly due to the homogeneous density and simplified anatomy of the Sawbones models [28, 29]. This finding is also consistent with another biomechanical study on synthetic bone that attributed similar proximal failures to stress concentrations at the implant-bone interface [30]. In clinical settings, particularly in osteoporotic bone, failure modes may be more complex, including screw loosening, pullout, or plate deformation under cyclic loading [7, 31, 32]. Although such complex features were not observed in this study, the findings underscore the biomechanical vulnerability of the proximal fixation zone. This highlights the importance of selecting an implant of appropriate length and, when feasible, ensuring timely implant removal to reduce long–term stress accumulation and minimize the risk of secondary fractures [26, 30, 33–35].

The DMLP group demonstrated significantly higher failure torque than the LLP group (14.21 ± 2.01 vs. 11.24 ± 1.47 N·m, p = 0.0036), indicating superior torsional resistance [17, 18, 36]. While greater rigidity enhances construct stability, it may also reduce energy dissipation through deformation, potentially increasing local stress transmission in specific regions [26, 27]. However, on the basis of current observations, there is no evidence of a generalized increase in stress concentration or failure severity in the DMLP group compared with the LLP group.

The role of the DMLP can be compared with that of other common fixation options for Danis-Weber B fractures. While posterior antiglide plating, which is commonly used for oblique fractures, offers strong resistance to posterior shear forces under loading conditions, it often necessitates the use of larger 3.5 mm implants and extensive posterior exposure, thereby increasing the risk of peroneal tendon irritation and other soft tissue complications [8]. Intramedullary fibular nails, on the other hand, offer a minimally invasive alternative that preserves surrounding soft tissues but are reported to have reduced torsional and bending stiffness compared with plate-based constructs, especially in osteoporotic bone or comminuted fractures [23, 37]. These nails may also face concerns, such as insufficient rotational control or the risk of implant toggling in certain clinical settings [33].

In contrast, the DMLP system employs two low-profile 2.7 mm locking plates placed on the lateral and posterolateral surfaces of the fibula. Compared with LLP, this dual-column construct demonstrated superior torsional resistance in our testing. This enhanced stability is complemented by the design’s capacity to accommodate an increased number of distal screws, which improves the total screw-bone contact area and offers a potential advantage in terms of poor bone stock. However, while the system’s design offers several theoretical advantages, it is crucial to clarify that any soft tissue benefits are purely hypothetical at this stage. Features such as the low-profile plates and the tapered distal edge of the posterior component are intended to reduce implant prominence and potential irritation after healing. Consequently, any clinical benefits such as improved comfort and reduced complications remain hypothetical and require further clinical evaluation for confirmation [38–40].

Despite its promising design, this study has several important limitations. First, the use of synthetic Sawbones models, while providing excellent consistency, means our findings are based on a material that is fundamentally homogeneous. This simplification does not capture the stress concentrations that can occur in real bone, which might influence fracture patterns and construct failure modes. Second, our ‘pure-bone’ model excluded soft tissues and ligaments, which are key contributors to ankle stability in vivo. The absence of these secondary stabilizers may lead to an overestimation of the loads borne by the fixation plates alone. Third, our loading protocol was limited to quasistatic conditions and did not include cyclic or fatigue testing. Therefore, while our results indicate high initial stability, they do not predict the long-term endurance of the constructs under repetitive physiological loads, such as screw loosening. Finally, the small sample size and absence of clinical data mean these results should be interpreted as a preliminary biomechanical proof-of-concept.

Nevertheless, this study successfully serves as a crucial biomechanical proof-of-concept. Its primary value lies in establishing the initial stability and feasibility of the DMLP system within a highly controlled laboratory environment, specifically on Sawbones fibulae under static test conditions. By demonstrating these advantages, it provides the necessary scientific rationale to justify more complex and clinically relevant investigations, such as cadaveric and, ultimately, prospective clinical trials.

Conclusion

Through in vitro biomechanical testing on simulated fractures in Sawbones fibulae, this study demonstrated that the DMLP system significantly outperforms the LLP in the fixation of simulated Danis-Weber B distal fibula fractures. The cross-fixation design of the DMLP system provides multidirectional support, enhancing axial stability and rotational resistance. The increased resistance to rotation is a critical factor in preventing common failure modes associated with distal fibula fractures in this type of biomechanical model. Despite the study’s inherent limitations related to its use of Sawbones models instead of human cadaveric tissue and its application of static loading, the results underscore the biomechanical potential of the DMLP system to improve the stability of distal fibula fracture fixation, offering a promising concept that warrants further clinical investigation.
