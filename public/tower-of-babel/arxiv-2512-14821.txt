Symmetric Dicke States as Optimal Probes for Wave-Like Dark Matter
Ping He, Jing Shu, Bin Xu, Jincheng Xu

Abstract
We identify symmetric Dicke states as the optimal quantum probes for distributed sensing of wave-like dark-matter fields. Within an ensemble-averaged quantum-metrological framework that incorporates the field’s random phases and finite coherence, they maximize the Fisher information for short-baseline arrays with N d \displaystyle N_{d} sensors and realize a robust N d 2 \displaystyle N_{d}^{2} enhancement. They also retain this collective advantage under amplitude-damping noise, whereas GHZ-type probes are highly fragile and rapidly lose their sensitivity once such noise is included. For two sensors at separations comparable to the dark-matter coherence length, the optimal entangled state acquires an additional spatial-correlation phase and outperforms both Dicke and independent probes. Our framework applies broadly to stochastic bosonic fields, including gravitational waves, and can be implemented with superconducting qubits, atomic ensembles, and NV centers.

Dicke states.

Dicke states arise from the fully symmetric subspace of
\displaystyle N_{d}
two-level sensors, which take

\displaystyle|g\rangle\ /\ |e\rangle
as the sensor ground / excited state.
R. H. Dicke originally introduced these fully symmetric collective spin states to model cooperative spontaneous emission (i.e., superradiance) in ensembles of closely spaced two-level atoms
Dicke 1954
.
In Dicke’s picture of superradiance, an ensemble of
\displaystyle N_{d}
atoms behaves as a single collective spin.
In the small-sample regime, all atoms couple to a common field mode and occupy a Dicke state: the collective emission rate (
\displaystyle\propto N_{d}
) is enhanced by the excitation number of order
\displaystyle N_{d}
, resulting radiated intensity scaled as
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
.
Dicke states
\displaystyle|j,j_{z}\rangle
are eigenstates with definite total spin
\displaystyle\sqrt{j(j+1)}
and magnetization
\displaystyle j_{z}
, equivalently represented as equal-weight superpositions of all configurations with a fixed excitation number
\displaystyle k=j+j_{z}
,

|D^{(k)}_{N_{d}}\rangle=\binom{N_{d}}{k}^{-1/2}\!\sum_{\pi}P_{\pi}\!\left(|e\rangle^{\otimes k}|g\rangle^{\otimes(N_{d}-k)}\right),

(1)

where
\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}
runs over all distinct permutations of the
\displaystyle N_{d}
tensor factors, and
\displaystyle P_{\pi}
is the permutation operator that reorders those factors.
The symmetric Dicke state (
\displaystyle j=N_{d}/2
and
\displaystyle j_{z}=0
for even
\displaystyle N_{d}
or
\displaystyle\pm 1/2
for odd
\displaystyle N_{d}
) distributes excitations uniformly across all sensors, producing a stable collective response.
In each experimental realization, the DM field carries an effectively random phase due to its stochastic momentum distribution.
Since the detection of the DM cares only about the field amplitude encoded in the transition probability rather than the relative phase, the appropriate basis is the Dicke manifold, whose symmetric states are intrinsically phase-insensitive.
The symmetric Dicke mode collects all excitation pathways that add constructively in amplitude, thereby maximizing the DM-induced transition rate even under random phases and local amplitude-damping noise.

corresponds to the Dicke state at the south pole of the Bloch sphere, as shown in Fig.

Quantum Metrology for Dark Matter Detection.

We model each detector as a two-level sensor with ground state
\displaystyle\ket{g_{I}}
, excited state
\displaystyle\ket{e_{I}}
, and transition frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{I}
, where
\displaystyle I=1,2,\dots,N_{d}
labels the detectors. The interaction between a scalar DM field
\displaystyle a(t,\mathbf{x})
and the
\displaystyle I
-th detector is described by

H_{I}=\eta\,\Omega_{I}\,a(t,\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\sigma}_{I}^{x},

(2)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{I}=\ket{g_{I}}\bra{e_{I}}+\ket{e_{I}}\bra{g_{I}}
,
\displaystyle\eta
denotes the DM-detector coupling and
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}
characterizes the detector response. Vector/tensor DM candidates are accommodated by replacing
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}a\!\to\!\Omega_{I}^{\mu}a_{\mu}
or
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}^{\mu\nu}a_{\mu\nu}
, with identical logic for what follows. The stochastic field is represented as a superposition of
\displaystyle N_{b}
non-interacting modes
Derevianko 2018
;
Guo et al. 2019
;
Dror et al. 2021

a(t,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{N_{b}\bar{\omega}\,\omega_{j}}}\,\cos\!\big[\omega_{j}t-\mathbf{p}_{j}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{j}\big],

(3)

where each mode with momentum
\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{j}
follows a probability distribution
\displaystyle f(\mathbf{p}_{j})
,
\displaystyle m_{a}
is the DM mass,
\displaystyle\omega_{j}=\sqrt{m_{a}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{j}^{2}}
is its energy, and
\displaystyle\bar{\omega}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}\,f(\mathbf{p})\,\omega
is the average. The local DM density is taken as
\displaystyle\rho=0.4~\mathrm{GeV/cm^{3}}
, and random phases
\displaystyle\alpha_{j}\!\sim\!\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi)
reflecting the Galactic ensemble. Moving to the interaction picture and applying the rotating-wave approximation,

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{I,\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\eta\,\Omega_{I}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{2N_{b}\bar{\omega}\,\omega_{j}}}\,e^{-i[(\omega_{j}-\omega_{I})t-\mathbf{p}_{j}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x}_{I}+\alpha_{j}]}\,\hat{\sigma}_{I}^{+}+\mathrm{h.c.},

(4)

with
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{I}^{+}=\ket{e_{I}}\bra{g_{I}}
.
This Hamiltonian explicitly captures the resonant exchange between the detector and individual DM field modes near the detector frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{I}
.

Starting from the initial detector state
\displaystyle\rho_{0}
at
\displaystyle t=0
, the coupling
\displaystyle\eta
is encoded by

\rho_{\eta}(t)=U_{I}(t)\rho_{0}U_{I}^{\dagger}(t),

(5)

with the time-evolution operator

U_{I}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\Big[-i\!\int_{0}^{t}\!H_{I,\mathrm{int}}dt^{\prime}\Big],

(6)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering.
For weak encoding we write
\displaystyle U_{I}(t)=e^{-i\eta M_{I}(t)}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
with

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{I}(t)=\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{t}\!H_{I,\mathrm{int}}dt^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Phi_{I}(t)\\
\Phi_{I}^{\ast}(t)&0\end{pmatrix},

(7)

where the matrix element
\displaystyle\Phi_{I}(t)
is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi_{I}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv|\Phi_{I}(t)|\,e^{i\Theta_{I}(t)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Omega_{I}\sum_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{2N_{b}\bar{\omega}\omega_{j}}}e^{i(\vec{p}_{j}\cdot\vec{x}_{I}-\alpha_{j})}\frac{e^{-i(\omega_{j}-\omega_{I})t}-1}{-i(\omega_{j}-\omega_{I})}.

(8)

The operator
\displaystyle M_{I}
has eigenvalues
\displaystyle\pm|\Phi_{I}|
with corresponding eigenvectors

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{\pm}_{I}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\pm e^{i\Theta_{I}}\\[2.0pt]
1\end{pmatrix},

(9)

so
\displaystyle\eta
imprints a phase-dependent rotation in the
\displaystyle\{\ket{\pm}_{I}\}
subspace.

In this work, we quantify detection sensitivity using the classical and quantum Fisher information.
For a parameter-dependent state
\displaystyle\rho_{\eta}
and a POVM

\displaystyle\{\Pi_{X}\}
with outcome probabilities
\displaystyle p(X|\eta)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\eta}\Pi_{X}]
, the
classical Fisher information (CFI) is

F_{C}(\eta)=\sum_{X}\frac{[\partial_{\eta}p(X|\eta)]^{2}}{p(X|\eta)},

(10)

which measures how strongly the outcome distribution responds to the parameter
\displaystyle\eta
and sets the Cramér–Rao bound on the estimator variance
\displaystyle n\,\mathrm{Var}(\eta)\geq F_{C}^{-1}
.
The quantum Fisher information (QFI),

F_{Q}(\eta)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{\eta}L_{\eta}^{2}],\qquad L_{\eta}\rho_{\eta}+\rho_{\eta}L_{\eta}=2\,\partial_{\eta}\rho_{\eta},

(11)

is the maximum attainable CFI over all quantum measurements and determines the ultimate precision limit
\displaystyle F_{C}\leq F_{Q}
.
For a pure initial state
\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}
, the QFI simplifies
to
Paris 2009
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011

\displaystyle F_{Q}=4\,\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{0}}(M_{I})
, and is maximized by choosing
\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{0}}
as an equal superposition of the two
eigenstates of
\displaystyle M_{I}
in Eq. (
9
). A more detailed summary of the quantum-metrological framework is provided in Supplementary Material A.

Because
\displaystyle\alpha_{j}
(and thus the global phase
\displaystyle\Theta_{I}
) is random across different measurement realizations, performance must be assessed on the ensemble. For any observable
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
we define the ensemble average as

\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\prod_{m,n=1}^{N_{b}}\!\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{m}\,f(\mathbf{p}_{m})\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{d\alpha_{n}}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{A}.

(12)

For a general initial state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{0}}=\alpha\ket{g_{I}}+\beta\ket{e_{I}}
one finds

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{Q}(\rho_{\eta})=4|\Phi_{I}|^{2}(1-2|\alpha\beta|^{2})-8\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(\Phi_{I}^{2}\alpha^{2}\beta^{*2}\right).

(13)

Averaging over realizations gives
\displaystyle\overline{\Phi_{I}^{2}}=0
, and

\overline{|\Phi_{I}|^{2}}=\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\rho}{2\bar{\omega}}\!\int\!\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}\,f(\mathbf{p})}{\omega}\,\Big[\frac{\sin[(\omega-\omega_{I})t/2]}{(\omega-\omega_{I})/2}\Big]^{2}\equiv C_{II}(t),

(14)

where
\displaystyle C_{II}(t)
is the detector’s autocorrelation function, increasing monotonically with integration time
\displaystyle t
.
Thus, the maximum QFI under ensemble averaging is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{F_{Q}(\rho_{\eta})}=4C_{II}(t),

(15)

achieved by preparing
\displaystyle\ket{g_{I}}
or
\displaystyle\ket{e_{I}}
as the optimal states, corresponding to
\displaystyle\beta=0
or
\displaystyle\alpha=0
, respectively.
Eq. (
14
) exhibits the familiar short- and long-time regimes: for
\displaystyle t\ll\tau_{c}
(DM coherence time),
\displaystyle C_{II}(t)\propto t^{2}
; for
\displaystyle t\gg\tau_{c}
,
\displaystyle C_{II}(t)\propto t
via the narrowband
\displaystyle\delta
-approximation.

A projective readout in the energy basis saturates both bounds in
\displaystyle F_{C}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})\leq F_{Q}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})\leq\overline{F_{Q}(\rho_{\eta})}
to order
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
, where the second inequality follows from the convexity of the QFI.
For an initial
\displaystyle\ket{g_{I}}
the excitation probability is
\displaystyle p_{e_{I}}(t)=\eta^{2}C_{II}(t)
, yielding

F_{C}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})=F_{Q}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})=\overline{F_{Q}(\rho_{\eta})}=4\,C_{II}(t).

(16)

For the
\displaystyle N_{d}
-independent-detector network, when both the initial state preparation and the final projective measurement are performed in the energy basis, the total Fisher information adds,

(F_{C})^{(\mathrm{ind})}=(\overline{F_{Q}})^{(\mathrm{ind})}=\sum_{I=1}^{N_{d}}4\,C_{II}(t),

(17)

representing the standard quantum limit (SQL) of the baseline sensitivity which scales linearly with the number of detectors
\displaystyle N_{d}
.

Optimal protocol for quantum sensor networks.

For
\displaystyle N_{d}
-detector networks capable of quantum enhancement, we consider
\displaystyle N_{d}
detectors interrogated collectively

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\text{int}}=\sum_{I}I_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{N_{d}}.

(18)

Writing
\displaystyle U(t)\approx e^{-i\eta M}
with

\displaystyle\displaystyle M=\sum_{I}I_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{N_{d}},

(19)

and averaging over the ensemble, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{F_{Q}}=4\,\overline{\braket{(\Delta M)^{2}}}=4\,\overline{\braket{M^{2}}}-4\,\overline{\braket{M}^{2}}.

(20)

The optimization reduces to maximizing the variance of
\displaystyle M
.

Introducing cross-correlators

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{IJ}(t)=\frac{\Omega_{I}\Omega_{J}\rho}{2\bar{\omega}}\!\int\!\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}\,f(\mathbf{p})}{\omega}\,e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{I}-\mathbf{x}_{J})}\Big[\frac{\sin[(\omega-\omega_{d})t/2]}{(\omega-\omega_{d})/2}\Big]^{2},

(21)

the averaged quadratic form is

\overline{M^{2}}=\sum_{I}C_{II}+2\sum_{I\neq J}C_{IJ}\,\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{I}\otimes\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{J}.

(22)

For an array of
\displaystyle N_{d}
detectors within a single coherence patch, assuming all detectors are identical (
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}=\Omega_{d}
,
\displaystyle\omega_{I}=\omega_{d}
) and arranged in a short-baseline configuration
where the separations are much smaller than the coherence length, all correlations become equal
\displaystyle C_{IJ}=C_{d}
. We find

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{M^{2}}=2C_{d}(\mathcal{J}^{2}-\mathcal{J}_{z}^{2})

(23)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=(\mathcal{J}_{x},\mathcal{J}_{y},\mathcal{J}_{z})
denotes the collective spin, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{2}=\mathcal{J}_{x}^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{y}^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{z}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{z}
its
\displaystyle z
component.
Thus, maximizing
\displaystyle\overline{M^{2}}
reduces to maximizing an appropriate algebraic combination of collective spin operators.

The collective spin operators
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{z}
admit the Dicke states
\displaystyle\ket{j,j_{z}}
as their common eigenbasis.
For
\displaystyle\overline{M^{2}}
, the maximal eigenvalue occurs via maximizing
\displaystyle j=N_{d}/2
and minimizing
\displaystyle|j_{z}|=0
for even
\displaystyle N_{d}
or
\displaystyle 1/2
for odd
\displaystyle N_{d}
, giving the ensemble-averaged QFI

(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}=2\big(N_{d}^{2}+2N_{d}-2|j_{z}|\big)\,C_{d},

(24)

i.e. a genuine
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
enhancement over the SQL for large
\displaystyle N_{d}
. This optimum is operational: projective measurements in the energy basis saturate the Cramér–Rao bound up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
.
For
\displaystyle N_{d}>2
, the ensemble-averaged QFI of GHZ states is
\displaystyle 2N_{d}(N_{d}+1)C_{d}
, which is strictly lower than that of the symmetric Dicke states. This comparison highlights that incorporating the stochastic nature of the DM field through ensemble averaging is essential for identifying the truly optimal quantum probe in realistic, coherent sensor networks.

Impact of noise.

We assess the robustness of distributed sensing under realistic decoherence channels, focusing on phase damping (pure dephasing) and amplitude damping (energy relaxation).
As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the DM-induced signal changes the collective excitation number by one, driving transitions of the form
\displaystyle\ket{j,j_{z}}\!\to\!\ket{j,j_{z}\pm 1}
.

Phase damping acts horizontally on the collective Bloch sphere and therefore preserves excitation number.
Since the leading-order DM signal is encoded in transitions between Dicke states with different
\displaystyle j_{z}
, dephasing leaves the transition probabilities, and thus the CFI, unchanged up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
.

Amplitude damping, in contrast, reduces the excitation number and can contaminate the DM-induced transition.
For symmetric Dicke probes
\displaystyle\ket{j=N_{d}/2,j_{z}}
, the signal branch
\displaystyle\ket{j,j_{z}+1}
is unaffected by amplitude damping, while the opposite branch acquires a suppression factor.
The resulting CFI takes the form

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}^{(\mathrm{Dicke})}=(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})^{N_{d}/2+j_{z}+1}\big[N_{d}^{2}+2N_{d}-4j_{z}(j_{z}+1)\big]\,C_{d},

(25)

showing that the characteristic
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
scaling of the Dicke response survives under moderate dissipation.

In sharp contrast, GHZ probes are extremely fragile.
A GHZ state expands entirely into Dicke components with even excitation numbers, while the DM interaction drives them into a superposition of odd-excitation Dicke states.
Amplitude damping produces exactly the same odd-excitation manifold even in the absence of DM, causing the DM-induced transitions to become indistinguishable from relaxation noise.
Consequently, the CFI collapses to

\displaystyle(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}^{(\mathrm{GHZ})}=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
.

A full quantitative analysis is provided in Supplementary Material C.
These results demonstrate that symmetric Dicke probes preserve their collective enhancement and maintain quantum advantage even in the presence of realistic decoherence, while GHZ probes are unsuitable for distributed sensing in realistic environments.

Discussion.

We identify symmetric Dicke states as the optimal probes for distributed sensing of wave-like dark-matter fields.
They saturate the ensemble-averaged quantum limit despite the stochastic DM phase and provide a robust
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
enhancement with simple energy-basis measurements.
Our noise analysis shows that Dicke probes are intrinsically resilient to dephasing and retain collective advantage under moderate amplitude damping, establishing clear operational thresholds for experimental implementation.

The required ingredients such as preparation of symmetric Dicke excitations and population-resolving measurements have been demonstrated in several quantum platforms,
including
trapped ions
Linington and Vitanov 2008
;
Hume et al. 2009
;
Noguchi et al. 2012
;
Ivanov et al. 2013

Cold atoms
Zou et al. 2018
;
Pennetta et al. 2022

superconducting qubits
Wu et al. 2017
;
Stojanovic and Nauth 2023
;
Chen et al. 2025

and NV centers
Wang and Terhal 2021
;
Ji et al. 2017
;
Ren et al. 2022
.
These implementations naturally provide access to the symmetric manifold and to the energy-basis measurement required by the protocol.
Taken together, these considerations indicate that the essential components of our Dicke-based sensing framework are compatible with existing experimental technologies and point toward a realistic path for noise-robust quantum-enhanced searches for wave-like dark matter and other spatially coherent fields with random phases.

Appendix S1
A. Quantum metrological framework

The general pipeline of quantum estimation consists of: probe preparation, parameter encoding (state evolution), measurement, and estimator construction. Let
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
denote unknown parameters of interest (e.g. the Dark Matter (DM) coupling
\displaystyle\eta
and possibly multi-parameter extensions). The covariance matrix obeys the Cramér–Rao inequality

n\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\ \geq\ F_{C}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\ ,

(S1)

where
\displaystyle n
is the number of independent repetitions,
\displaystyle F_{C}
is the classical Fisher information (CFI) matrix associated with a specific positive operator-value measurement (POVM). For a POVM
\displaystyle\{\Pi_{i}\}
with
\displaystyle p(i|\widehat{\mathbf{x}})=\mathrm{Tr}\,[\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}\Pi_{i}]
, the CFI matrix reads

[F_{C}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})]_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p(i|\widehat{\mathbf{x}})}\,\partial_{x_{\mu}}p(i|\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\,\partial_{x_{\nu}}p(i|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}).

(S2)

In the asymptotic limit, maximum-likelihood estimators saturate the Cramér–Rao bound
\displaystyle n\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\ =\ F_{C}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\
.

Quantum Cramér–Rao inequalities further constrains the covariance matrix

n\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\ \geq\ F_{C}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\ \geq\ F_{Q}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}),

(S3)

where
\displaystyle F_{Q}
is the quantum Fisher information (QFI) matrix, which sets the ultimate bound on estimation precision allowed by quantum mechanics, irrespective of the specific measurement strategy.
The QFI matrix is defined via the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLDs)
\displaystyle L_{\mu}
(
\displaystyle\partial_{x_{\mu}}\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(L_{\mu}\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}+\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}L_{\mu}\big)
):

[F_{Q}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})]_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{Tr}\,[\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}\tfrac{1}{2}(L_{\mu}L_{\nu}+L_{\nu}L_{\mu})].

(S4)

A sufficient condition for the quantum Cramér–Rao boundary to be saturable is
\displaystyle[\rho_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}},\,L_{\mu}]=0
and
\displaystyle[L_{\mu},L_{\nu}]=0
.
Since the CFI quantifies the information about parameter extractable from a specific measurement scheme, the inequality
\displaystyle F_{C}\leq F_{Q}
reflects that the QFI provides the maximum attainable information across all possible measurements.

In the single-parameter case,
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\equiv\eta
, with unitary encoding
\displaystyle\rho_{\eta}=U_{\eta}\rho_{0}U_{\eta}^{\dagger}
where
\displaystyle U_{\eta}=\exp(-i\eta\hat{\mathcal{G}})
, and a pure probe state
\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}
, the ultimate precision bound is given by
Paris 2009
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011

F_{Q}(\eta)=4\,\mathrm{Var}_{\psi_{0}}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}).

(S5)

If
\displaystyle N_{d}
sensors are
independent
, the generator is additive
\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}=\sum_{I}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{I}
, and
\displaystyle F_{Q}
is additive:
\displaystyle F_{Q}^{(\mathrm{ind})}=\sum_{I}4\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{I})\propto N_{d}
, i.e. the standard quantum limit (SQL). Entanglement can boost collective fluctuations to
\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{I}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{I})\propto N_{d}^{2}
, yielding Heisenberg scaling.

For stochastic wave-like fields with a random global phase
\displaystyle\varphi
, the figure of merit is the
ensemble-averaged
QFI

\overline{F_{Q}}\ \equiv\ \mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\!\left[F_{Q}\right].

(S6)

For the coupling parameter
\displaystyle\eta
, there is
\displaystyle\overline{\rho_{\eta}(t)}=\overline{U_{I}(t)\rho_{0}U_{I}^{\dagger}(t)}
, and the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{C}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})\leq F_{Q}(\overline{\rho_{\eta}})\leq\overline{F_{Q}(\rho_{\eta})}.

(S7)

An appropriate measurement scheme can achieve equality in Eq. (
S7
), thereby reaching the quantum-limited precision.

Appendix S2
B. Two-detector optimization

With short baseline configuration, we identify the symmetric Dicke state as the optimal detection state.
For situations where detector baseline is equivalent to the coherence length, our framework still provides effective conclusions.
For example, considering two detectors located at
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{2}
, the ensemble-averaged QFI is given by

\overline{F_{Q}}=4\,\overline{\braket{(\Delta M)^{2}}}=4\,\overline{\braket{M^{2}}}-4\,\overline{\braket{M}^{2}},

(S8)

where
\displaystyle M=M_{1}\otimes I_{2}+I_{1}\otimes M_{2}
. Using the single-detector definitions (cf. main text Eq. (
7
)), we find

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\braket{M^{2}}}=\braket{\overline{M^{2}}}=\overline{|\Phi_{1}|^{2}}+\overline{|\Phi_{2}|^{2}}+2\,\braket{\overline{M_{1}\otimes M_{2}}},

(S9)

where the third term represents the detector–detector contribution governed by the cross-correlation
\displaystyle C_{12}(\mathbf{x}_{12},t)
. The expectation is

\overline{M_{1}\otimes M_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&\\
&0&C_{12}(\mathbf{x}_{12},t)&\\
&C_{12}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x}_{12},t)&0&\\
&&&0\end{pmatrix}

(S10)

with the maximal eigenvalue
\displaystyle|C_{12}|
and eigenstate

\ket{\psi}^{(\max)}_{\mathrm{2-det}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(e^{i\phi_{12}}\ket{e_{1}g_{2}}+\ket{g_{1}e_{2}}\Big),\qquad\phi_{12}=\arg(C_{12}).

(S11)

This optimal state maximizes Eq. (
S8
), as it minimizes
\displaystyle\overline{\braket{M}}
(in fact,
\displaystyle\braket{\psi_{\max}|M|\psi_{\max}}=0
). The ensemble-averaged QFI is

(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}=4\Big(C_{11}+C_{22}+2|C_{12}|\Big).

(S12)

Equation (
S11
) provides the optimal initial state for two detectors beyond the coherence distance. Compared to the symmetric Dicke state for two detectors
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{\mathrm{(Dicke)}}_{\mathrm{2-det}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\ket{e_{1}g_{2}}+\ket{g_{1}e_{2}}\Big)
, the key difference is the extra phase factor
\displaystyle e^{i\phi_{12}}
, which arises from the cross-correlation function at different detector positions, with
\displaystyle\phi_{12}=\arg(C_{12})
.

In Fig.
2
, we present the QFI for three representative initial states: the optimal state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{(\mathrm{max})}_{\mathrm{2-det}}
, the symmetric Dicke state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{(\mathrm{Dicke})}_{\mathrm{2-det}}
, and the product state of two independent detectors
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{(\mathrm{ind})}_{\mathrm{2-det}}
.
The results are evaluated at
\displaystyle t=\tau_{c}\simeq 10^{6}/m_{a}
, assuming two identical detectors (
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{2}
,
\displaystyle\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
) separated by a baseline
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{12}
oriented parallel to the Earth’s velocity
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{g}
in the Galactic frame.
We adopt the standard halo model of cold dark matter, in which the velocity distribution follows a Maxwellian profile,

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(\vec{v})=(\pi v_{\mathrm{vir}}^{2})^{-3/2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{g})^{2}}{v_{\mathrm{vir}}^{2}}\right],

(S13)

with virial velocity
\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{vir}}\simeq 220~\mathrm{km/s}
and Galactic velocity
\displaystyle v_{g}\simeq 232~\mathrm{km/s}
.
The phase
\displaystyle\phi_{12}
associated with the cross-correlation
\displaystyle C_{12}
plays a crucial role in realizing the full quantum enhancement from entanglement.
When this phase is neglected, the QFI for the symmetric Dicke state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{(\mathrm{Dicke})}_{\mathrm{2-det}}
remains smaller than that of the optimal state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}^{(\mathrm{max})}_{\mathrm{2-det}}
that includes
\displaystyle\phi_{12}
.
Notably, near separations
\displaystyle d\sim 2\lambda_{c}
, destructive interference arising from
\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[C_{12}]<0
causes the Dicke-state QFI to drop even below that of two independent detectors.

A simple projective measurement in the energy basis

\displaystyle\{\ket{g_{1}g_{2}},\ket{g_{1}e_{2}},\ket{e_{1}g_{2}},\ket{e_{1}e_{2}}\}

saturates the bound to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})

\begin{cases}p_{g_{1}g_{2}}=p_{e_{1}e_{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\big(C_{11}+C_{22}+2|C_{12}|\big),\\[4.0pt]
p_{g_{1}e_{2}}=p_{e_{1}g_{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).\end{cases}

(S14)

Appendix S3
C. Noise analysis

We analyze robustness against phase damping (pure dephasing) and amplitude damping (energy relaxation).
For clarity we present a Kraus picture and keep terms to leading order in
\displaystyle\eta^{2}
and small error probabilities.
In the energy basis
\displaystyle\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}
, a standard amplitude-damping (AD) channel with error probability
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}
has Kraus operators

E_{0}^{\mathrm{AD}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-p_{\mathrm{AD}}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{1}^{\mathrm{AD}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{AD}}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sum_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}=I.

(S15)

Pure dephasing (PD) with probability
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{PD}}
can be expressed as

E_{0}^{\mathrm{PD}}=\sqrt{1-p_{\mathrm{PD}}}\,I,\qquad E_{1}^{\mathrm{PD}}=\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{PD}}}\,\ket{g}\bra{g},\qquad E_{2}^{\mathrm{PD}}=\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{PD}}}\,\ket{e}\bra{e},

(S16)

which damps coherences while leaving populations unchanged.

Two detectors.

For the two-detector configuration, we retain the noiseless optimal probe
\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\max}}
in Eq. (
S11
), derived under noiseless conditions (
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}=p_{\mathrm{PD}}=0
), without performing noise-adaptive re-optimization.
After coherent evolution under
\displaystyle U_{1}\otimes U_{2}
, we apply either the amplitude- or phase-damping channel independently via

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho_{\eta})=\sum_{k}E_{k}\rho_{\eta}E_{k}^{\dagger}
, and evaluate the CFI associated with projective measurements in the energy basis.

Dephasing.

Since dephasing preserves diagonal populations in the measurement basis, and the leading signal resides in the small transition probabilities
\displaystyle p_{g_{1}g_{2}}
and
\displaystyle p_{e_{1}e_{2}}
[Eq. (
S14
)], the CFI is unchanged to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
:

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{PD}}=(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

(S17)

Formally, off-diagonal elements
\displaystyle\rho_{g_{1}e_{2},e_{1}g_{2}}
are damped, but they do not contribute at leading order to
\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}p_{X}
for
\displaystyle X\in\{g_{1}g_{2},e_{1}e_{2}\}
.

Amplitude damping.

Amplitude damping transfers weight from the single-excitation subspace
\displaystyle\{\ket{e_{1}g_{2}},\ket{g_{1}e_{2}}\}
into
\displaystyle\ket{g_{1}g_{2}}
, thereby contaminating the small signal in
\displaystyle p_{g_{1}g_{2}}
, and symmetrically from
\displaystyle\ket{e_{1}e_{2}}
into single excitations. To quantify degradation, compare the AD-induced background in
\displaystyle p_{g_{1}g_{2}}
to the signal in Eq. (
S14
). Defining
\displaystyle\lambda\ \equiv\ p_{\mathrm{AD}}/p_{g_{1}g_{2}}\ \sim\ p_{\mathrm{AD}}/[\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\big(C_{11}+C_{22}+2|C_{12}|\big)]
, a first-order calculation in
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}
and
\displaystyle\eta^{2}
yields three regimes:

1.

Signal-dominated (
\displaystyle\lambda\ll 1
):
\displaystyle(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}\ \simeq\ \Big(1-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big)\,(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}.

2.

Dissipation-dominated (
\displaystyle\lambda\gg 1
):
\displaystyle(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}\ \simeq\ \frac{(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})^{2}}{2}\,(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}\ <\ (\overline{F_{C\ \mathrm{or}\ Q}})^{(\mathrm{ind})},
so the quantum advantage is lost.

3.

Crossover (
\displaystyle\lambda=\mathcal{O}(1)
):
\displaystyle(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}=\frac{(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

A sufficient condition for retaining advantage is

\lambda\ \leq\ \frac{4|C_{12}|}{C_{11}+C_{22}-2|C_{12}|},

(S18)

which implies an operational threshold on the coupling

\eta\ \geq\ \sqrt{\frac{p_{\mathrm{AD}}\big(C_{11}+C_{22}-2|C_{12}|\big)}{2|C_{12}|\big(C_{11}+C_{22}+2|C_{12}|\big)}}.

(S19)

In the Lindblad parametrization one may set
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}=1-e^{-\gamma_{1}T}
and choose
\displaystyle T
at the optimum determined by the signal filter (main text).

\displaystyle N_{d}
identical detectors.

We now extend the noise analysis to an array of
\displaystyle N_{d}
identical sensors, assuming that each detector experiences independent local noise channels acting after coherent evolution.
In the noiseless case, the optimal probe remains the symmetric Dicke state
\displaystyle\ket{j=N_{d}/2,m}
, which maximizes the ensemble-averaged sensitivity.
We examine the impact of two dominant decoherence mechanisms: phase damping and amplitude damping.

Phase damping.

As in the two-detector case, phase damping acts transversely on the collective Bloch sphere, leaving the diagonal populations in the energy basis unchanged.
Because the DM signal is encoded in small transition probabilities among diagonal elements, phase damping does not affect the leading-order measurement statistics.
Consequently, the corresponding CFI remains unchanged up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
:

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{PD}}=(\overline{F_{Q}})_{\max}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

(S20)

Amplitude damping.

Amplitude damping transfers excitations from
\displaystyle\ket{e}
to
\displaystyle\ket{g}
with probability
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}
, thereby reducing the collective excitation number and contaminating the small signal components that encode the DM response.

•

For
\displaystyle N_{d}

independent
detectors initialized in
\displaystyle\ket{g}^{\otimes N_{d}}
, the CFI scales linearly with
\displaystyle N_{d}
and is reduced by a factor of
\displaystyle(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})
:

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}^{(\mathrm{ind})}=(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})\,4N_{d}C_{d}.

(S21)

•

For the symmetric Dicke probe
\displaystyle\ket{N_{d}/2,j_{z}}
, amplitude damping primarily affects the transition
\displaystyle\ket{N_{d}/2,j_{z}}\to\ket{N_{d}/2,j_{z}-1}
, while the complementary state
\displaystyle\ket{N_{d}/2,j_{z}+1}
is suppressed by
\displaystyle(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})^{N_{d}/2+j_{z}+1}
.
The resulting CFI under independent amplitude-damping channels becomes

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}^{(\mathrm{Dicke})}=(1-p_{\mathrm{AD}})^{N_{d}/2+j_{z}+1}\big[N_{d}^{2}+2N_{d}-4j_{z}(j_{z}+1)\big]\,C_{d}.

(S22)

The leading
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
scaling persists for small
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{AD}}
, indicating that collective coherence survives moderate dissipation.
The quantum advantage is preserved when

p_{\mathrm{AD}}<1-\left[\frac{4N_{d}}{N_{d}^{2}+2N_{d}-4j_{z}(j_{z}+1)}\right]^{1/(N_{d}/2+j_{z})}.

(S23)

•

The X-GHZ state, being a superposition of Dicke states with alternating excitation parity, suffers decoherence across all signal components.
Each constituent Dicke component is independently degraded by amplitude damping, leading to

(\overline{F_{C}})_{\mathrm{AD}}^{(\mathrm{GHZ})}=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}),

(S24)

which severely limits its detection sensitivity.

Hence, Dicke states remain substantially more robust to both amplitude and phase damping, preserving their
\displaystyle N_{d}^{2}
collective enhancement even in the presence of realistic noise.
