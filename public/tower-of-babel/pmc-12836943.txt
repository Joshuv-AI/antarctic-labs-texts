Association between parental oral health literacy and child oral health: findings from a cross-sectional study using the TOHLAT-P scale
Authors: Nevra Karamüftüoğlu, Kadir Serdar Yılmaz, Seçil Özkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of parents’ sociodemographic characteristics, oral health-related attitudes and behaviors, and oral health literacy (OHL) levels on the oral health outcomes of their children aged 6–12 years who attended a pediatric dentistry clinic.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used the TOHLAT-P scale as a data collection tool and analyzed data from 238 parent-child pairs. The Pearson Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables.ResultsA significant association was found between children’s oral hygiene status and parental education level (college/university graduate) (p = 0.002), employment status (employed) (p = 0.029), history of receiving oral health education (p = 0.045), regular toothbrushing (p = 0.020), routine dental check-ups (p < 0.001), and high TOHLAT-P scores (p < 0.001). Participants with a TOHLAT-P score of 30 or below were found to be 5.366 times more likely to have poor-to-moderate oral hygiene levels (based on OHI-S scores) compared to those with scores of 31 or above (p < 0.001).ConclusionsParental OHL level has a positive impact on children’s oral hygiene, and sociodemographic and behavioral factors may influence this literacy level. In this context, it is recommended to develop family-based intervention models and literacy-focused health education strategies.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Child oral health, Health behaviors, Oral health literacy, Parental attitudes, TOHLAT-P scale

Background

Oral health (OH) is a key component of general health and well-being, especially during childhood and adolescence. One of the major challenges in pediatric dentistry is ensuring equitable access to preventive and therapeutic oral health services for all children [1]. While clinical-based interventions remain central to managing individual cases, they are insufficient to address widespread disparities in oral health status, especially in low-resource settings such as Türkiye [2].

Dental caries, the most prevalent chronic disease of childhood, is often the result of an imbalance between cariogenic microorganisms and host factors including saliva, dietary habits, and oral hygiene practices. This multifactorial condition can negatively impact children’s chewing function, nutrition, speech development, and overall quality of life. First permanent molars, which erupt early and have complex occlusal morphology, are particularly susceptible to caries. If left untreated, lesions in these teeth may progress to pulpal involvement, abscesses, or early extraction—leading to orthodontic complications and reduced masticatory efficiency [3].

Parental influence is widely recognized as a determinant of children’s oral health behaviors. In this regard, oral health literacy (OHL)—defined as an individual’s ability to obtain, process, and understand oral health information to make informed decisions—has garnered significant attention [4]. Studies show that low OHL in parents is associated with higher caries risk, less frequent dental visits, and poor oral hygiene in their children [5, 6]. In addition, parental OHL has been shown to influence children’s daily oral health behaviors such as toothbrushing frequency, sugar consumption, and dental attendance patterns. Parents with higher literacy levels are more likely to supervise their children’s brushing, provide fluoride toothpaste, and restrict cariogenic snacks, resulting in improved oral hygiene and reduced caries experience [4–6].

More recently, oral health literacy has been associated not only with clinical outcomes such as caries indices, but also with psychosocial constructs including dental anxiety, oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), and trust in dental professionals. The Turkish Oral Health Literacy Adult Form for Parents (TOHLAT-P), in particular, has been integrated into conceptual frameworks exploring how parental literacy indirectly affects children’s dental trust and cooperation via behavioral mediators [7]. Additionally, Buldur and Oguz [8] emphasized that TOHLAT-P not only measures comprehension and numeracy skills but also evaluates parents’ listening and decision-making abilities regarding oral health practices. This multidimensional structure makes it a robust tool for capturing functional oral health literacy within the sociocultural context of Turkish families. These findings suggest that TOHLAT-P captures more than just knowledge—it reflects communication, behavior, and relational dynamics within pediatric dental care.

Despite increasing awareness of OHL, there is a scarcity of studies in Türkiye evaluating its direct relationship with children’s clinical dental status. TOHLAT-P, a culturally validated instrument, allows for systematic assessment of OHL in caregivers. This study aims to evaluate parental oral health literacy levels using TOHLAT-P and examine their association with clinical indicators such as DMFT/dmft, PUFA/pufa, and OHI-S scores in children. Understanding these relationships may support the development of public health strategies focused on improving pediatric oral health through caregiver education and community-based interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at a private dental health center located in Ankara, Türkiye, between March 1, 2024, and March 30, 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Gazi University (Approval No: E.366953, Date: 25/05/2022). The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participating parents or legal guardians. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Participants

A total of 238 parent–child pairs participated in the study. Inclusion criteria included children aged 6–12 years and parents who could read and understand Turkish. Exclusion criteria involved children with systemic diseases or special healthcare needs and parents who declined to provide informed consent.

Data collection instruments

The study utilized a structured questionnaire comprising two main sections:

Sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics of the parents (age, education level, employment status, number of children, income perception, regular medication use).

Sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics of the parents (age, education level, employment status, number of children, income perception, regular medication use).

The Turkish Oral Health Literacy Adult Form for Parents (TOHLAT-P), developed and validated by Buldur and Oguz (2023), was used to assess parental oral health literacy [8]. The TOHLAT-P consists of 27 items across four dimensions (reading comprehension, numeracy, listening, and decision-making), with higher scores indicating better OHL. According to the scoring system proposed by Buldur and Oguz (2023), total TOHLAT-P scores are categorized as follows: 0–30 = inadequate, 31–44 = marginal, and 45–54 = adequate oral health literacy level [8]. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89) and strong test–retest reliability (> 0.85), confirming its suitability for use among Turkish parents [8]. These psychometric strengths validate its utility in epidemiological and clinical studies assessing caregiver literacy.

Clinical examination

All children underwent intraoral examinations performed by a calibrated pediatric dentist in a standardized clinical setting using disposable mouth mirrors and WHO periodontal probes under optimal lighting. The following indices were recorded:

DMFT/dmft Index: Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth for permanent and primary dentition.

DMFT/dmft Index: Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth for permanent and primary dentition.

PUFA/pufa Index: Pulpal involvement, Ulceration, Fistula, and Abscess.

PUFA/pufa Index: Pulpal involvement, Ulceration, Fistula, and Abscess.

OHI-S Index: Simplified Oral Hygiene Index [9].

OHI-S Index: Simplified Oral Hygiene Index [9].

Calibration was performed prior to data collection to ensure consistency in diagnostic criteria and recording. The pediatric dentist responsible for examinations underwent a structured training session using standardized case photographs and example radiographs based on WHO criteria for DMFT/dmft, PUFA/pufa, and OHI-S indices. A pilot assessment was carried out on approximately 10% of the study sample (n ≈ 24) two weeks before data collection, followed by a second evaluation of the same participants after a 10-day interval. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.87), indicating excellent agreement across all indices. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus prior to the main data collection phase.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Descriptive statistics were reported as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Normality of distribution was assessed via Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed, and since they did not follow a normal distribution, the Spearman correlation test was applied to evaluate relationships between continuous variables. The Pearson chi-square test was used for the comparison of categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the chi-square analysis were considered for inclusion in the regression model where appropriate. Power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 software determined that a minimum sample size of 210 was required to detect medium effect size with 95% confidence level and 80% power.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of parents

A total of 238 parents participated in the study, consisting of 148 mothers (62.2%) and 90 fathers (37.8%). All eligible families who were invited agreed to participate voluntarily, and no explicit refusals were reported during recruitment. The participation rate was therefore considered to be close to complete. The mean age of the parents was 35.48 ± 6.14 years. Most participants had at least a high school education. Nearly half of the families (46.2%) had two children, while 33.6% had three or more. Among the participants, 21.8% reported using medication regularly.

40.3% of parents reported having previously received oral and dental health education, while 59.7% stated they had not received such education. 68.1% of parents indicated that they brushed their teeth regularly. Regarding daily tooth brushing frequency, 64% reported brushing twice a day, 33.5% once a day, and 2.4% stated they brushed rarely. 59.7% of parents reported using fluoride-containing toothpaste, whereas 40.3% used non-fluoridated toothpaste. Consumption of sugary foods or beverages was present in 87.4% of parents, while 12.6% stated they did not consume such products. 52.1% of parents reported attending regular dental check-ups. Among them, 51.6% visited the dentist once a year, 35.5% every six months, 9.7% every three months, and 3.2% monthly.

Oral health status of children

The children examined had a mean age of 8.63 ± 1.42 years, with an almost equal gender distribution. The mean DMFT score was 1.21 ± 1.40, while the mean dmft score was 2.23 ± 2.44. The PUFA/pufa index revealed that 36.6% of the children had at least one untreated carious lesion with complications. According to the OHI-S scores, 52.1% of children had good oral hygiene, while 28.6% and 19.3% had moderate and poor hygiene, respectively.

Parental oral health literacy levels

The mean TOHLAT-P score was 46.84 ± 7.52, with 36.6% of parents classified as having inadequate OHL, 33.2% as marginal, and 30.3% as adequate. OHL levels significantly differed based on parents’ education level (p < 0.001), presence of health insurance (p = 0.012), and economic perception (p = 0.008).

No statistically significant difference was found in children’s OHI-S levels based on parents’ age, marital status, perceived economic status, regular medication use, number of household members, or number of children. However, notable differences were observed between some subgroups. The frequency of good oral hygiene among children was 66.7% in the 18–29 age group, 77.3% in the 30–39 age group, 83.1% in the 40–49 age group, and 69.2% among those aged 50 and above. Among parents who regularly used medication, the frequency of good oral hygiene in their children was 69.2%, compared to 81.7% in those who did not use medication. A statistically significant association was found between parental education level and children’s OHI-S levels (p = 0.002). The frequency of good oral hygiene was 87.1% among children of high school graduate parents, 82.3% among those whose parents had a university degree, and 61.5% among those whose parents had a secondary school education or below. A significant difference was also found based on parental employment status (p = 0.029). The frequency of good oral hygiene was 87.5% among children of retired parents, 81.5% among those of employed parents, 76.7% among those of stay-at-home mothers, and 33.3% among those of unemployed parents (Table 1).

A statistically significant difference was found in children’s OHI-S levels between parents who reported regular tooth brushing and those who did not (p = 0.020). The frequency of good oral hygiene among children was 83.2% when parents brushed their teeth regularly, compared to 70.1% when they did not. Similarly, a significant difference was observed based on the use of fluoride-containing toothpaste (p = 0.011). The frequency of good oral hygiene was 84.5% among children whose parents used fluoride toothpaste and 70.8% among those whose parents did not. In addition, the frequency of dental check-ups was significantly associated with children’s OHI-S levels (p < 0.001). Good oral hygiene was reported in 90.6% of children whose parents visited the dentist once a year, 86.4% of those who visited every six months, and 50.0% of those who visited every three months or less frequently. Although not statistically significant, regular dental check-up attendance showed a near-significant trend (p = 0.054). The frequency of good oral hygiene was 83.9% among children whose parents attended regular check-ups, compared to 73.7% among those who did not. Additionally, among children whose parents replaced their toothbrush only when bristles were deformed, the frequency of good oral hygiene was 88.2%, whereas this frequency dropped to 57.1% among those whose parents were unaware of their toothbrush replacement frequency. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.110). No statistically significant differences were found between children’s OHI-S levels and parents’ daily tooth brushing frequency, consumption of sugary foods and beverages, or the frequency of such consumption (Table 2).

No statistically significant difference was found between parents’ OHI-S levels and the time elapsed since their child’s last dental check-up. The frequency of good oral hygiene was 77.2% among parents who had taken their child to the dentist within the last 6 months, and 88.9% among those whose child had last visited the dentist one year ago or earlier. Similarly, there was no statistically significant association between parents’ OHI-S levels and the type of toothbrush used by their child. The frequency of good oral hygiene was 74.4% among parents of children using manual toothbrushes, 88.2% among those using electric toothbrushes, and 87.5% among those whose children used both types. Furthermore, no statistically significant difference was observed based on whether the child had previously received a fluoride application. The frequency of good oral hygiene was 83.6% among parents of children who had received fluoride and 74.1% among those who had not (Table 2).

A statistically significant difference was found in children’s OHI-S levels based on parental oral health literacy (TOHLAT-P) scores. The frequency of good oral hygiene was 89.2% among children whose parents scored 31 points or higher, and 66.7% among those whose parents scored 30 points or lower. No statistically significant differences were found in children’s OHI-S levels and DMFT, dmft, or PUFA scores. However, variations were observed between subgroups. The frequency of good oral hygiene was 85.3% among children with a DMFT score of 0, 78.4% in those with scores between 1 and 3, and 75.0% in those with scores of 4 or more. Among children with a dmft score of 0, the frequency was 87.5%, compared to 85.2% for those with scores of 1–2, 70.6% for scores of 3–5, and 76.5% for scores greater than 5. Regarding PUFA scores, 80.2% of children with a score of 0 had good oral hygiene, compared to 69.2% of those with a score of 1 or more (Table 2).

A statistically significant weak negative correlation was found between parental TOHLAT-P scores and both the number of children (r = − 0.314, p < 0.01) and household size (r = − 0.272, p < 0.01). In addition, TOHLAT-P scores were negatively correlated with children’s OHI-S scores (r = − 0.300, p < 0.01), suggesting a potential association between lower parental oral health literacy and poorer oral hygiene in children (Fig. 1). Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the relationship between parental OHL and the OH status of their children, using validated clinical indices including DMFT/dmft, PUFA/pufa, and OHI-S. The findings highlight a significant and multifactorial association between parental literacy levels and children’s oral health outcomes, underlining the pivotal role that caregivers play in shaping oral hygiene habits, treatment-seeking behavior, and preventive practices from an early age.

The average TOHLAT-P score in this study population was 46.84, suggesting a moderate level of OHL among Turkish parents attending a private dental setting. However, a substantial portion—approximately one-third—were classified as having inadequate OHL. This mirrors findings from international research indicating that limited literacy remains a major public health issue even in urban populations [10]. Lower literacy scores in this study were significantly associated with lower educational attainment, lack of health insurance, and perceived financial hardship, confirming prior studies that describe a close interplay between health literacy and socioeconomic status [11–14].

In terms of clinical relevance, the study revealed that children of parents with inadequate OHL had significantly worse oral health outcomes. Their mean DMFT/dmft scores, PUFA/pufa values, and OHI-S indices were notably higher than those of children whose parents had marginal or adequate OHL. This suggests that oral health education and promotion efforts should not only target children but must also consider parental literacy levels as a core determinant of pediatric oral health. Prior studies support this finding by demonstrating that parents with higher OHL are more likely to adopt effective oral hygiene routines, schedule timely dental visits, and provide healthier dietary options [4, 8, 12]. Interestingly, although parental dental attendance frequency was significantly associated with better OHI-S scores in children, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Regular visits may reflect a higher awareness and prioritization of oral health rather than direct causal protection. Some studies have reported similar patterns, suggesting that parents who attend dental check-ups regularly are also more likely to model positive behaviors and supervise their children’s oral hygiene practices [5, 6, 11]. However, other research indicates that frequent visits may occur in response to pre-existing dental problems rather than preventive intent [12]. Therefore, the observed association in our study may represent both preventive and reactive behaviors, highlighting the need for longitudinal data to clarify this relationship.

Importantly, Buldur and Oguz [8] developed and validated a structural path model that positioned TOHLAT-P as an exogenous variable influencing children’s trust in dental professionals through intermediary pathways such as parental and child oral health behaviors, anxiety, and OHRQoL. Their findings demonstrate that improvements in parental OHL, as measured by TOHLAT-P, can cascade through multiple behavioral and psychological dimensions, culminating in higher dental trust and cooperation among children.

These results, together with the current study, emphasize that TOHLAT-P is not only a measure of oral health knowledge but a valuable tool for understanding broader relational and behavioral outcomes in pediatric dentistry. According to Buldur and Oguz [8], the TOHLAT-P’s four-domain framework enables a holistic appraisal of caregivers’ capacity to navigate the dental care system, make informed choices, and interact effectively with dental providers. This conceptual breadth is particularly important in pediatric settings, where caregiver behavior significantly influences child cooperation and treatment adherence. The integration of TOHLAT-P into public health models could therefore strengthen intervention strategies aimed at improving both clinical and psychosocial oral health indicators.

Despite the significance of the findings, this study is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it remains unclear whether low OHL causes worse oral health or vice versa. The study sample was drawn from patients attending a dental faculty clinic, which may not fully represent families who do not seek dental care due to limited access or lack of awareness. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to the wider population should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, while TOHLAT-P is a validated tool, it may not fully capture contextual factors such as cultural beliefs, oral health myths, or health system navigation difficulties, which are known to influence health behaviors. Further qualitative studies could illuminate these dimensions.

Moreover, the study did not include behavioral determinants such as brushing frequency, dietary sugar intake, or access to fluoridated toothpaste—all of which are crucial intermediaries in the literacy–health outcome pathway. Future studies would benefit from a more comprehensive approach, incorporating behavioral, environmental, and policy-level factors into explanatory models. Multivariate analyses, longitudinal follow-up, and interventional study designs are also needed to strengthen the evidence base and support the development of tailored oral health promotion strategies.

In light of these findings, oral health policymakers and public health professionals should prioritize the integration of health literacy frameworks into routine dental care and school-based oral health programs. Literacy-sensitive communication strategies, culturally adapted educational materials, and caregiver-focused outreach initiatives can serve as key instruments in addressing health disparities and promoting equity in pediatric oral health.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a clear association between parental OHL and the oral health status of children. Lower OHL levels were significantly related to higher caries experience, more advanced untreated lesions, and poorer oral hygiene in children, underscoring the importance of caregivers’ understanding and engagement in oral health practices. Sociodemographic factors such as parental education, income perception, and access to health insurance were found to significantly influence OHL levels.

Given these findings, improving parental OHL through culturally appropriate, accessible, and community-based education initiatives could serve as a key strategy in preventing dental diseases in children and reducing health disparities. Policymakers and public health practitioners should consider integrating oral health literacy assessments and interventions into broader preventive programs targeting families with young children.

Further longitudinal and interventional studies are recommended to explore the long-term impact of OHL enhancement on pediatric oral health outcomes and to develop effective strategies tailored to the needs of low-literacy populations.
