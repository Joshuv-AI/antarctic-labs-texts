From polyetheretherketone to 3D‑printed composites: hydrothermal stability and cement‑dependent resin bonding of high‑performance polymers to pediatric restorations
Authors: Szu-I Lin, Chien-Fu Tseng, Chi-Fei Hsieh, Yuichi Mine, Yao-Chang Chiang, Chiang-Wen Lee, Tzu-Yu Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the water sorption/solubility, surface characteristics, and bonding performance of different high‑performance polymers intended for restorations and appliances used in pediatric dentistry, emphasizing their hydrothermal stability and cementation protocols for durable bonding.MethodsDisc‑shaped specimens of milled polyetheretherketone (PEEK), ceramic‑filled 3D‑printed composite resin, and milled polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA)‑based acrylic polymer were prepared. Water sorption and solubility were determined according to ISO 4049 after storage in distilled water and artificial saliva. Surface treatments included no treatment, airborne‑particle abrasion, and abrasion followed by application of a dimethacrylate/methyl methacrylate‑based primer. Surface microstructure, roughness, and wettability were evaluated. Shear bond strength was tested using either resin cement or glass ionomer cement under three aging conditions (24‑h water storage, thermocycling, and highly accelerated aging), and failure modes were classified.ResultsAll materials showed very low water sorption (Wsp) and solubility (Wsl) below ISO 4049 limits (Wsp < 5 µg/mm³ and Wsl ~ 1 and 2 µg/mm³), with minor medium‑ and time‑dependent changes, indicating similar hydrolytic stability among the polymers. Airborne‑particle abrasion markedly increased surface roughness (from ~ 0.65 μm to 2.75–5.35 μm), whereas primer application reduced roughness while maintaining microroughness and significantly improved wettability; however, final roughness and contact angles remained material‑dependent. With resin cement, shear bond strength ranged from 17.07 to 23.26 MPa after 24‑h storage, decreased to 10.18–14.28 MPa after thermocycling, and increased up to 25.72 MPa after highly accelerated aging for the 3D‑printed and milled composites, with predominantly mixed or cohesive failures. Glass ionomer cement produced substantially lower bond strengths (0–8.70 MPa) and predominantly adhesive failures under all conditions.ConclusionsHigh‑performance polymers exhibited excellent resistance to water sorption and solubility and maintained favorable surface characteristics after clinically relevant pretreatments. Under the present in vitro conditions, resin cement combined with airborne‑particle abrasion and primer achieved more durable bonding than glass ionomer cement and may help guide material selection for pediatric‑oriented restorations and appliances.Keywords: 3D printing, Polyetheretherketone, Hydrothermal stability, Bonding, Pediatric dentistry

Background

The concept of biomimetic restorations refers not to a specific material but to a design philosophy that imitates the hierarchical structure, optical appearance, and mechanical behavior of natural teeth [1]. The goal is to achieve elastic and fracture responses that are close to those of enamel and dentin. In this study, materials possessing tunable mechanical properties, suitable for digital manufacturing, and exhibiting elastic modulus and fracture behavior similar to dental and osseous tissues are collectively defined as high‑performance polymers for analytical purposes. Among them, polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is the most representative high‑performance polymer [1, 2], as it exhibits high strength, fatigue and corrosion resistance, and low water sorption and solubility, while its elastic modulus approximates that of cortical bone, making it a potential substitute for metal and zirconia frameworks; however, PEEK exhibits an opaque grayish appearance and limited translucency, which may compromise anterior esthetics and often requires veneering with a composite or ceramic layer in clinical situations [2–4]. Meanwhile, advances in digital technologies have enabled the development of new high‑performance polymers such as ceramic‑filled 3D‑printed composite resins, which allow rapid and customized production of crowns and prostheses with mechanical performance and marginal fit comparable to conventionally milled composites [5, 6].

Primary and young permanent teeth in pediatric patients present distinct biomechanical requirements compared with those of adults [7]. They are subjected to lower masticatory loads, and their supporting periodontal and alveolar structures are still developing. Rigid restorations made of metal or ceramics may therefore cause stress shielding or excessive wear of opposing teeth [8, 9]. Hence, high‑performance polymers with intermediate elasticity, stress‑absorbing capacity, and favorable biocompatibility may serve as promising alternatives for restorations and appliances in pediatric populations [10–12]. Nevertheless, such materials are not yet routinely used for primary teeth, and data on their hydrothermal stability and bonding performance in pediatric‑oriented applications remain limited, which underscores the novelty and motivation of the present laboratory study.

However, most existing studies have focused on the mechanical and biocompatibility properties of individual high‑performance polymers. Comparisons among different high‑performance polymers in terms of their physical and clinically relevant surface characteristics remain limited [13, 14]. Moreover, for materials intended for crowns or pediatric appliances, bonding performance critically affects long‑term outcomes. Clinically, glass ionomer cements (GICs) are still widely used in pediatric dentistry due to their chemical adhesion to tooth structure and sustained fluoride release, which benefit patients with high caries risk [15–17]. Nevertheless, their compressive and bond strengths are generally lower than those of resin cements. Resin cements (RCs) provide stronger bonding and mechanical performance but are more technique‑sensitive and susceptible to moisture‑dependent conditions [18–21]. To date, the bonding behavior and failure patterns of high‑performance polymers with these two types of cements are not well understood.

This study aimed to evaluate the stability and bonding potential of different high‑performance polymers under simulated wet conditions. Specifically, it sought to compare the water sorption and solubility of various high‑performance polymers to assess their long‑term stability. In addition, the study investigated surface roughness, contact angle, and morphology following clinically relevant surface treatments. Finally, it evaluated the shear bond strength and failure modes of high‑performance polymers bonded to dental substrates using resin or glass ionomer cements. These in vitro data are intended to provide comparative information that may assist material selection for restorations and appliances in pediatric patients, while recognizing that clinical studies are still required. Based on current literature and clinical considerations, the study hypothesized that significant differences exist in water sorption and solubility among high‑performance polymers. It further proposed that similar surface roughness and wettability can be achieved after identical surface treatments, and that under the same surface condition, the bond strengths of resin and glass ionomer cements to high‑performance polymers would show no significant difference.

Methods

Sample size calculation

The sample size (n = 10 per test) was determined a priori using G*Power v3.1.9.6 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and 1 − β = 0.80, confirming adequate power to detect intergroup differences.

Specimen preparation

Three high-performance polymers were evaluated: milled polyetheretherketone (BHP; breCAM BioHPP, bredent, Germany), ceramic-filled 3D-printed composite resin (PCR; FREEPRINT crown, DETAX, Germany), and milled polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA)-based acrylic polymer (MCR; PMMA DISK, Yamahachi, Japan). PMMA DISK is a conventional acrylic polymer rather than a composite and may exhibit different behavior from filler-containing materials. Ceramic fillers in BioHPP may slightly modify the properties of unfilled PEEK, including stiffness, water sorption, and surface characteristics. The materials used are summarized in Table 1.

Disc‑shaped specimens (10 mm diameter, 3 mm thickness) were designed using 3D modeling software (SolidWorks Corp., Dassault Systèmes, Waltham, MA, USA) and fabricated by either 3D printing (FREEPRINT crown, DETAX GmbH & Co. KG, Ettlingen, Germany) or milling (DGSHAPE DWX‑52DCI; Roland DG Corp., Shizuoka, Japan). For PCR specimens, printed disks were cleaned in isopropyl alcohol using ultrasonic agitation for 1 min, air‑dried, and post‑cured in a xenon flash curing unit (Otoflash G171, NK‑Optik GmbH, Germany) for 2 × 2000 flashes according to the manufacturer’s instructions before finishing. All specimens were finished with #600 silicon‑carbide paper, ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water (DW), and air‑dried.

Water sorption and solubility analysis

Specimens were oven‑dried at 37 ± 2 °C for 22 h, stored in a desiccator with silica gel for 2 h, and repeatedly weighed until a constant mass (m1; change < 1.0 mg over 24 h) was achieved. Diameter and central thickness were measured to calculate specimen volume (V). Half of the specimens were immersed in DW and the remainder in artificial saliva (AS; BZ 109, Biochemazone, Alberta, Canada) at 37 ± 2 °C for 7, 14, or 30 days. After removal, surface moisture was gently blotted with filter paper, and specimens were weighed to obtain the wet mass (m2). Surface treatment and surface characterization

Specimens finished with #600 silicon‑carbide paper served as control (NT group). Additional specimens were air‑abraded with 50‑µm Al₂O₃ particles (AA treatment) and then coated with a urethane dimethacrylate / methyl methacrylate‑based primer (HC primer; SHOFU Inc., Kyoto, Japan), followed by 10 s of light curing with a UV curing unit (Phrozen Tech Co. Ltd., Hsinchu City, Taiwan) to form the primer‑treated group (PR treatment). Thus, three surface conditions were prepared: NT (no additional treatment after polishing), AA (airborne‑particle abrasion only), and AA + PR (airborne‑particle abrasion followed by primer application).

Surface morphology and microstructure were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU3500, Hitachi High Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan) in secondary electron mode at 15.0 kV and magnifications of 500× and 5000×. Surface roughness (Ra) was measured using a profilometer (Surftest SJ‑210, Mitutoyo, Tokyo, Japan) with a 6.4 mm tracing length, 0.8 mm cutoff, and scanning speed of 0.5 mm/s; the mean of three readings per specimen was recorded. Surface wettability was determined with a contact‑angle goniometer (Phoenix Mini, Surface Electro Optics Co., Ltd., Suwon, Korea) using DW as the probe liquid.

Shear bond strength testing

A 5-mm-diameter resin mold was placed on the specimen surfaces and filled with either RC (G‑CEM ONE, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) or GIC (Fuji II, conventional, non‑reinforced glass ionomer cement; both GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Shear bond strength was evaluated on AA + PR‑treated surfaces for all materials, unless otherwise specified. A vertical load of 4.9 N was applied to standardize the cement thickness, excess cement was removed, and light curing was performed using an LED unit (LITEX 696 TURBO; Dentamerica Inc., City of Industry, CA, USA) at 1,200 mW/cm² (460–510 nm) for 180 s with the tip positioned 1 mm from the surface, followed by storage at 37 °C for 1 h.

Specimens were randomly assigned to one of three aging conditions: (1) storage in DW at 37 °C for 24 h (CON condition), (2) 10,000 thermocycles between 5 °C and 55 °C (THC condition), or (3) highly accelerated aging at 134 °C and 2 bar for 5 h (HAS condition). These conditions were selected to represent short‑term storage, clinically relevant thermal cycling, and an exaggerated hydrothermal stress scenario, respectively, recognizing that HAS does not reproduce actual intraoral temperatures and pressures.

Shear bond strength (SBS) was evaluated on AA + PR‑treated surfaces for all materials, unless otherwise specified. SBS was measured using a universal testing machine (JSV‑H1000, Japan Instrumentation System Co., Ltd., Nara, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min, applying a shear load to the adhesive interface until failure. SBS values were calculated from the failure load divided by the bonded area. Failure modes were examined under an optical microscope (BX40, Olympus Optical Co., Ltd.) and classified as adhesive, cohesive, or mixed.

Statistical analysis

All data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Depending on the data distribution, one‑way or two‑way analysis of variance with Tukey’s honestly significant difference post‑hoc tests was applied to compare materials, surface treatments, and aging conditions. Briefly, water sorption and solubility were analyzed by two‑way ANOVA (material × storage time) within each medium, surface roughness and contact angle by two‑way ANOVA (material × surface treatment), and shear bond strength by two‑way ANOVA (material × aging condition) for each cement type.

Results

Water sorption and solubility

Figure 1 presents the Wsp and Wsl values of the three materials stored in DW or AS for 7, 14, and 30 days. Across all groups, Wsp values remained close to zero at each time point and were well below the ISO 4049 limit (40 µg/mm³), indicating minimal water uptake. Similarly, Wsl values were either slightly negative or near zero in both media and did not exceed the ISO 4049 threshold (7.5 µg/mm³), showing only minor variations over time.

Surface characterization

SEM images (Fig. 2) showed that the NT surfaces of all materials were relatively smooth, displaying only marks left by the silicon‑carbide paper. In contrast, the AA surface exhibited pronounced micro‑irregularities and particle‑like debris, whereas the AA + PR surface partially filled or masked these features, resulting in a more homogeneous surface morphology. Regarding wettability (Fig. 3A; Table 2), AA treatment slightly reduced the contact angle of BHP but increased it for PCR and MCR compared with NT. AA + PR treatment significantly decreased contact angles (P < 0.05). Quantitatively (Fig. 3B; Table 2), AA treatment significantly increased Ra (2.75–5.35 μm) relative to NT (~ 0.65 μm) in all materials (P < 0.05). Although AA + PR treatment decreased Ra (2.01–4.76 μm) compared with AA treatment, Ra remained higher than NT, with PCR consistently exhibiting the significantly greatest roughness (P < 0.05).

Results of SBS and failure mode classification are summarized in Fig. 4; Tables 2 and 3. THC condition significant reduced SBS in all materials (10.18–14.28 MPa) (P < 0.05), whereas HAS condition increased SBS compared with CON and THC conditions in PCR (25.72 MPa) and MCR (23.44 MPa) conditions. Failure‑mode analysis revealed that, in the RC groups, BHP showed purely mixed failures in the CON condition (100%) but predominantly adhesive failures after the THC (80%) and HAS (60%) conditions; PCR exhibited exclusively cohesive failures under all aging conditions (100%); and MCR showed mainly mixed failures in the CON (60%) and THC (100%) conditions but predominantly adhesive failures in the HAS condition (90%). In the GIC groups, BHP failed mainly in a mixed mode in the CON (70%) and HAS (60%) conditions but almost entirely adhesively after the THC condition (100%); PCR showed predominantly mixed failures in all conditions; and MCR exhibited mainly mixed failures in the CON condition (90%), predominantly adhesive failures in the THC condition (90%), and a higher proportion of adhesive (50%) and cohesive (30%) failures with fewer mixed failures (20%) in the HAS condition.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the three high‑performance polymers, despite their different compositions and microstructures, exhibited water sorption and solubility values in both distilled water and artificial saliva that were close to zero, far below the ISO 4049 limits [22], and only minimally affected by storage time and medium, thereby failing to support the first null hypothesis. These findings agree with previous reports on PEEK and various CAD‑CAM or 3D‑printed resin materials characterized by low water sorption and solubility [23–26]. Note that, water sorption and solubility were evaluated according to ISO 4049, which provides a standardized protocol for resin‑based restorative materials. For polymer‑based crown and veneering materials such as PEEK‑based and 3D‑printed polymers, ISO 10477 is also relevant, and its requirements were considered when interpreting the present water sorption, solubility, and bonding results. When water sorption and solubility are controlled within ISO standards, the risks of dimensional instability, time‑dependent deterioration of mechanical properties, and progressive degradation of the bonded interface can be reduced [27, 28], which is particularly meaningful for pediatric patients requiring long‑term appliances under frequent sugar exposure, pH fluctuations, and a humid oral environment; however, this implication remains hypothetical because the present data were obtained in vitro [28, 29]. The negligible differences between DW and AS align with earlier observations that highly crosslinked, relatively hydrophobic CAD/CAM polymers and 3D‑printed resins show generally low water sorption and solubility with limited variation among different aqueous media, likely due to their dense crosslinked networks and lower content of hydrophilic monomers [25, 28, 30]. Slightly negative solubility values are more plausibly attributed to cumulative methodological errors and minor bias in volume and mass estimation caused by water uptake, suggesting that ISO‑based solubility values should be interpreted with caution [22, 28, 31]. The consistently low water sorption and solubility observed for BHP and the 3D‑printed composite may also be influenced by their ceramic‑ or glass‑filler reinforcement and dense crosslinked networks, although this relationship was not directly analyzed in the present study.

Regarding surface properties, all three high‑performance polymers showed a similar trend after identical surface treatment, but their final roughness and wettability did not converge, so the second null hypothesis was partially rejected. AA treatment significantly increased surface roughness in all materials, and subsequent primer application AA + PR slightly reduced Ra compared with AA treatment alone while keeping it higher than the NT group, concurrently decreasing the contact angle and improving wettability. From a materials perspective, differences between BHP and the two composite resins PCR, MCR in filler content, matrix composition, hardness, and processing method (milling versus 3D printing) likely influenced the extent and pattern of surface etching and microcrack formation during AA treatment, resulting in distinct surface morphologies [5, 27, 32, 33]. SEM observations indicated that primer partially filled or covered AA‑induced depressions and sharp irregularities (AA + PR treatment), producing a smoother macrosurface but retaining microscale porosities and undulations that may favor mechanical interlocking and improved wetting through increased surface energy [13, 14, 30, 34]. Even under the same AA and AA + PR protocols, BHP, PCR, and MCR maintained material‑specific surface characteristics, implying that optimal surface treatment strategies for high‑performance polymers should be tailored to substrate composition and microstructure rather than relying on a single universal protocol. It should be noted that the present design does not isolate the effect of the primer alone, because AA + PR was always applied after air abrasion; thus, the results should be interpreted as comparisons between untreated, air‑abraded, and air‑abraded‑plus‑primed surfaces rather than three independent surface treatments.

The bonding results clearly showed that, under identical surface conditions, RC and GIC produced significantly different bond strengths to the high‑performance polymers, thus rejecting the third null hypothesis. RC consistently yielded the highest SBS values across all materials and aging conditions, with mainly mixed and cohesive failures, in line with evidence that higher SBS typically correlates with such failure patterns and stronger interfacial adhesion [18, 19, 24]. In contrast, GIC demonstrated markedly lower SBS values and predominantly adhesive failures, consistent with reports that conventional and resin‑modified GICs often show low SBS and adhesive failure modes on various substrates [35–37]. These differences reflect distinct bonding mechanisms: RC depends on low‑viscosity monomers to wet, infiltrate, and polymerize within the roughened, high‑energy surface created by AA and AA + PR treatments, forming resin tags and micromechanical interlocking, whereas GIC relies mainly on ionic exchange and acid–base interactions with glass and mineral phases, which are scarce on hydrophobic polymeric surfaces such as BHP, PCR, and MCR [1, 13–15, 19, 23, 24]. From a methodological perspective, the use of a 5‑mm‑diameter bonded cylinder and a conventional shear bond strength setup may generate non‑uniform stress distribution at the interface, so the SBS values should be interpreted primarily for comparative purposes rather than as absolute indicators of clinical performance. In addition, the same surface pretreatment was applied to both RC and GIC to standardize the substrate conditions and isolate the influence of cement type, but the fundamentally different bonding mechanisms mean that their direct numerical comparison must be interpreted with caution and mainly in terms of relative trends rather than as evidence that the two systems are equivalent or interchangeable in clinical practice [34–36]. Within the RC groups, thermocycling generally reduced SBS, likely due to thermal expansion mismatch, water ingress, and interfacial fatigue, whereas the highly accelerated aging condition sometimes increased SBS for PCR and MCR [27, 29]; this counterintuitive finding may be related to additional post‑polymerization or stress relaxation within the resin network but remains hypothetical and was not directly demonstrated by the present methods [27, 28]. Given these uncertainties, the increased bond strengths after HAS should be regarded as an experimental observation within this specific model rather than evidence of superior clinical durability. GIC, however, showed no comparable reinforcing effect under any aging condition, with SBS remaining low and dominated by adhesive failures, indicating high environmental sensitivity and insufficient interfacial toughness on high‑performance polymer surfaces [15, 18, 19].

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted entirely in vitro and did not simulate clinical conditions such as saliva, occlusal loading, and patient behavior in pediatric patients; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as laboratory data rather than directly extrapolated clinical outcomes. Second, only three high‑performance polymers were tested, the surface treatment and adhesive protocols were restricted to a single airborne‑particle abrasion condition, one primer, and selected cements, and bonding was assessed only by shear bond strength, which does not fully capture the complex, multidirectional intraoral stresses. In particular, the highly accelerated aging protocol represents an artificial hydrothermal stress rather than a clinically realistic aging pathway, and the observed changes in bond strength under this condition should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, failure modes were analyzed descriptively without inferential statistical comparison of their distributions, so these data should be regarded as supportive qualitative information rather than definitive evidence of fracture‑pattern differences among groups. Future studies including a broader range of high‑performance polymers, surface treatments, complementary mechanical tests, and clinically relevant aging conditions are warranted. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as comparative in vitro data on hydrothermal stability and bonding behavior of high‑performance polymers, which may inform but cannot determine clinical protocols for pediatric patients.

Conclusions

Different high‑performance polymers exhibited similarly stable water sorption behavior and bonding potential after appropriate surface treatments. Overall, they showed excellent resistance to water sorption and solubility, as well as favorable bonding performance when properly conditioned under the present in vitro conditions. Among the tested combinations, the resin‑cement protocol with airborne‑particle abrasion and primer produced more durable and stable bonding than glass ionomer cement within this experimental model. These findings suggest that such resin‑cement protocols may be advantageous for high‑performance polymers in pediatric‑oriented restorative and appliance applications; however, they should be considered as laboratory evidence that may guide material and protocol selection rather than definitive recommendations for long‑term clinical use. Further in vivo and clinical studies with a broader range of materials, surface treatments, and cement systems are required to confirm these trends.
