Nonlinear relationship between diabetes mellitus duration and diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Daxue Zhang, Yongli Zhang, Jian Kang, Xuchun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the non-linear relationship between diabetes mellitus (DM) duration and the development of diabetic retinopathy (DR). By investigating the association between these variables, our goal is to contribute to the existing knowledge regarding the impact of DM duration on the development and severity of DR. A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on 420 patients in the Department of Endocrinology at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, who had undergone ophthalmic consultations from December 2017 to November 2018. The analysis of DM duration and DR utilized a generalized additive model to identify both linear and non-linear connections. The threshold effect was determined using a two-piece regression model. The study included a total of 420 patients, with a mean age of 58.7 years. Of these, 56.9% (239/420) were male. The prevalence of DR was 38.33% (161/420). After adjusting for confounding factors, a nonlinear relationship between DM duration and DR was observed, with a turning point at 8 years. On the left side of the turning point, the prevalence increased by 24% per 1-year increase in DM duration (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.11–1.38; P<0.0001). However, no statistically significant differences were found on the right side of the turning point (OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.97–1.08; P = 0.4987). Our study identified a non-linear relationship between DM duration and DR in patients. When the DM duration is less than 8 years, a positive correlation exists between DM duration and DR. However, once the DM duration exceeds 8 years, the effect reaches saturation, and no significant correlation is observed.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus duration, Diabetic retinopathy, Nonlinear relationship, Saturation effectSubject terms: Diseases, Risk factors

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a prevalent chronic complication of diabetes, characterized by progressive damage to the retinal microvasculature due to chronic and advancing diabetes1. This results in a cascade of retinal lesions, including microaneurysms, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, neovascularization, vitreous hemorrhage, macular edema, and, in severe instances, retinal detachment2. DR stands out as a leading cause of adult blindness, posing a significant threat to the quality of life for individuals with diabetes3. Additionally, it imposes a substantial economic burden on society4.

The prevalence of DR varies among countries, regions, and races, with higher rates observed in developing countries compared to developed countries5. A meta-analysis encompassing 22,896 people with diabetes from 35 worldwide studies revealed a DR prevalence of 34.6%, with proliferative DR at 6.96% and diabetic macular edema at 6.81%. DR with vision-threatening complications accounted for 10.2%6. Approximately 1 in every 3 people with diabetes are affected by DR7.

The primary risk factors for DR include elevated blood glucose levels or significant glycemic fluctuations, hypertension, dyslipidemia, prolonged diabetes duration, diabetic kidney disease, pregnancy, obesity, and susceptibility genes8–11. Studies have emphasized that diabetes mellitus (DM) duration is the most crucial risk factor for DR and is an inevitable aspect12. A longer duration of diabetes augments the likelihood of developing DR, with the risk increasing by 8% for every additional year of diabetes history13. Prolonged exposure to hyperglycemia explains this association, potentially elevating the risk of vascular damage and other consequences such as DR14,15. It has been established that the duration of diabetes is the most significant risk factor for diabetic retinopathy. Early screening of diabetic patients proves beneficial for treatment and delaying disease progression16. However, uncertainty remains regarding the relationship between diabetes duration and retinopathy risk, and the optimal time for early screening has yet to be determined.

In this study, our objective is to investigate the inflection point effect of DM duration on DR risk through a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study. The aim is to ascertain the optimal timing for early screening of DR risk, offering guidance for clinical practice to identify the most opportune moment for early screening.

Methods

Data source

This study is a secondary analysis of a retrospective cross-sectional study. The data were obtained from the “Dryad” database (https://datadryad.org). This website allows users to download the original data from published literature for free. In accordance with Dryad’s terms of service, we have referenced the relevant Dryad data package in this article: Zhuang, Xuenan et al. (2019)17, Data from: Association of diabetic retinopathy and diabetic macular edema with renal function in southern Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a single-center observational study, Dryad, Dataset, 10.5061/dryad.6kg1sd7.

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study involved 420 patients in the Department of Endocrinology at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital who had undergone ophthalmic consultations from December 2017 to November 2018. The study included patients aged ≥ 18 years with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and records of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) 35-degree 7-standard fields color retinal photographs (Topcon TRC; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan). Inclusion criteria encompassed patients without any other ocular illnesses that could potentially affect ocular circulation (e.g., glaucoma, endophthalmitis, retinal vascular occlusion, age-related macular degeneration, refractive error > 3 diopters, eye trauma). Additionally, cases with severe systemic diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, connective tissue disorders) or a history of previous intravitreal injection or dialysis were excluded. Women who were pregnant or menstruating were also excluded. The original study provides additional details regarding the research. The authors explicitly state that the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (registration number: gdrec2016232A). All clinical information was obtained through electronic medical records. As this study is retrospective, the Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital waived the need for informed consent to participate.

Definition of DR

The primary outcome variable in this study was whether patients had diabetic retinopathy (DR) by examination at admission. DR diagnosis was conducted by two trained graders utilizing both clinical ophthalmic examination (slit-lamp examination) and image assessment (fundus photography). These graders were kept unaware of the patient’s medical condition, and in cases where there were discrepancies in grading opinions, an expert in fundus evaluation further confirmed the diagnosis. Based on the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Disease Severity Scale18, cases were categorized into different stages: DR1/no DR (no diabetic retinopathy), DR2/mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), DR3/moderate NPDR, DR4/severe NPDR, and DR5/proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). Among these stages, DR patients were defined as those falling within the DR2-5 categories.

Exposure variable

The DM duration is defined as the period of time (in years) from the initial diagnosis of diabetes to the current date for each patient. It is recorded as a continuous variable.

Covariate variables

The covariates considered in this study include patients baseline information such as age, sex, medical history (hypertension and diabetes), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Laboratory tests encompass glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), renal function tests (including serum creatinine (Scr), urine creatinine (Ucr), uric, serum urea, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine protein, and urine albumin), blood lipids (including non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TRIG), total cholesterol (CHOL), lipoprotein A (Lpa), apolipoprotein A (APOA), and apolipoprotein B (APOB)), as well as other blood biochemistry parameters (including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), acetylcholinesterase (AchE), serum albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), D-dimer, and vitamin (Vit) B12). All laboratory tests were conducted on an empty stomach before 08:00 AM daily.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (for normal distribution) or median (quartile) (for skewed distribution), while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies or percentages. Statistical differences between the means and proportions of the groups were determined using One-way Analysis of Variance (for normal distribution), Kruskal–Wallis H test (for skewed distribution), and chi-square tests (for categorical variables).

To consider the effect of DM duration on the prevalence of DR, we used univariate and multivariate logistic regression models; model I is the unadjusted model, model II adjusted for age and sex and model III is the full adjusted model. In Model III, variables considered for inclusion were age, sex, hypertension, Scr, Ucr, uric, serum urea, eGFR, urinary albumin, NEFA, HDL, LDL, CHOL, Lpa, APOB, ALT, AST, ALB, D-dimer, and VitB12. In model III, the covariates were adjusted based on the principle that when added to the model, the matched odds ratio should change by at least 10%19. Account for non-linear correlation between DM duration and prevalence of DR, we also used generalized additive model (GAM) and a fitted smoothing curve (penalized spline method) to address nonlinearity. Besides, in cases where a nonlinear correlation was observed, a two-piecewise logistic regression model was utilized to calculate the saturation effect of DM duration on DR through a smoothing plot. The inflection point, at which the maximum likelihood model was applied, was automatically calculated using a recursive method when the ratio between DR and DM duration distinctly appeared in the smoothed curve20. Finally, considering that the association between the DM duration and prevalence of DR may vary in some populations, the researchers conducted exploratory stratified analysis in some subgroups through the logistic regression model and used the likelihood ratio test to check the hierarchical differences to determine whether there was interaction.

All analyses were performed using statistical software packages R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA). P < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

The clinical characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. We analyzed a total of 420 patients, with a mean age of (58.7 ± 13.7)years. Among them, males accounted for 56.9% (239/420) of the population. The prevalence of DR was 38.33% (161/420), with an average duration of 9.7 years for patients diagnosed with T2DM. Based on the patients’ DM duration, we divided them into quartiles (Q1-Q4) and observed the distribution trends among the different groups. Compared to the Q4 group, the Q1-Q3 groups showed lower levels of age, SBP, Scr, serum urea, urinary albumin, urinary protein, and D-dimer, as well as a lower proportion of females (all P < 0.05). On the other hand, the Q1-Q3 groups had higher levels of DBP, Ucr, eGFR, NEFA, APOB, ALT, and ALB (all P < 0.05).

The results of relationship between DM duration and DR

Three models were used to evaluate the linear association between DM duration and DR (Table 3). In the model I, each 1-year increase in DM duration was associated with a 9% increase in the prevalence of DR (OR: 1.09; 95% CI: 1.06–1.12; P < 0.0001). In Model II, with each additional year of DM duration, the prevalence of DR increased by 10% (OR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.06–1.14; P < 0.0001). In Model III, adjusting for additional factors, each additional year of DM duration was associated with an 8% increase in the prevalence of DR (OR: 1.08; 95%CI: 1.03–1.12; P = 0.0002).

For sensitivity analysis purposes, we transformed the four quartiles of DM duration into categorical variables and calculated the trend test p-value. The results indicated that the findings remained consistent whether DM duration was analyzed as a continuous or categorical variable.

Results of nonlinear association between DM duration and DR

As shown in the smoothing spline, the DM duration was found to have a nonlinear relationship with DR (Fig. 1). The two-piecewise regression model showed that the inflection point was 8.00 years after adjusting for covariates(age, sex, hypertension, Scr, Ucr, uric, serum urea, eGFR, urinary albumin, NEFA, HDL, LDL, CHOL, Lpa, APOB, ALT, AST, ALB, D-dimer, VitB12). On the left side of the inflection point, the prevalence increased by 24% per 1 year increase in DM duration (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.11–1.38; P<0.0001 ) (Table 4).

The results of subgroup analyses

We examined age, sex, Scr, eGFR, NEFA, LDL, CHOL, APOB, ALT, and AST as potential effect modifiers to assess the stability of the relationship between DM duration and DR across various subgroups. The results (Table 5) indicated significant variations in the association between DM duration and DR among different subgroups when considering NEFA, LDL, CHOL, APOB, and ALT as potential effect modifiers (all P for interaction < 0.05).

Discussion

Our study investigated the association between the DM duration and the prevalence of DR. After adjusting for relevant confounding variables, our results revealed that for each additional year of DM duration, the odds of developing DR increased by 8% (OR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.03–1.12; P = 0.0002). The utilization of a smoothed curve fitting graph identified a nonlinear relationship between DM duration and the prevalence of DR, with an inflection point at 8 years. To the left of the inflection point, for every 1-year increase in DM duration, the odds of DR increased by 24% (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.11–1.38; P < 0.0001). However, once the DM duration exceeded 8 years, the odds of DR reached saturation, and no significant correlation was observed (OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.97–1.08; P = 0.4987).

DM duration plays a crucial role as the most important risk factor in the occurrence and development of DR21. Our study findings suggest that within the first 8 years of DM duration, early intervention is beneficial in delaying the onset of DR. However, beyond 8 years, timely measures have a limited impact on the occurrence of DR. These results are consistent with previous studies22,23. An observational study encompassing 4,513 patients with T2DM and a 28-year follow-up found that the prevalence of DR increased with longer disease duration. Among patients with a disease duration of 0–5 years, the prevalence of DR was 6.6%. This rate increased to 24.0% among those with a disease duration of 10–15 years, further climbing to 52.7% among those with a disease duration of 20–25 years, and reaching 63.0% among those with a disease duration of over 30 years24. Our study observed that the average DM duration among patients was 9.7 years, and the overall prevalence of DR was 38.33%. The stratified analysis revealed the following rates: 0–5 years (19.35%), 6–10 years (46.02%), 11–15 years (47.54%), 16–20 years (52.17%), and over 20 years (63.64%). These findings are consistent with previous studies. Similarly, another study25 found that the prevalence of any form of DR significantly increased with longer DM duration. The latest report from the epidemiological study on DR in Wisconsin26 found that the main risk factors for proliferative DR were the duration of diabetes and glycemic control, and uncontrolled hypertension and males may further increase the risk. However, our subgroup analysis results showed that the risk of developing DR was higher in females than in males.

Diabetic patients are exposed to a high-glucose environment for a long period of time, which can lead to early damage to the blood-retinal barrier. This damage results in increased permeability, microvascular leakage, and the formation of neovascularization and fibrosis in the retina. Ultimately, there can be structural changes in retinal microvascular cells27, even leading to complete loss of capillaries28–30. The level of vascular endothelial growth factor may be associated with the severity of DR31,32. However, studies have also found that some diabetic patients with longer DM duration do not develop DR, and it is currently unclear why some individuals, despite having a longer duration of diabetes, do not experience DR. There has been questioning of the notion that DM duration is a strong predictive factor for DR, and it has been suggested that a considerable proportion of diabetic patients may be protected from diabetic vascular complications33. Cabrera et al. hypothesized that these protected individuals represent an “extreme phenotype” of having no DR and that “protective factors” (possibly genetic or environmental) may contribute to slowing down or preventing retinal pathology34. Therefore, considering a patient’s DM duration, specific targeted strategies should be developed for the prevention of DR35. Patients who had previously received intravitreal injections were excluded from our study. Intravitreal injections have been widely used to treat DR36. This treatment may affect retinal structure and function, including possible complications such as retinal fibrosis and geographic atrophy37,38. This treatment may alter the natural course of DR, including the assessment of macular edema39. Therefore, to minimize the interference of treatment effects on lesion assessment, we chose to exclude these patients. This exclusion may limit the generalizability of our findings because it excluded a subset of patients with more severe diseases. Future studies could consider including these treated patients to more fully assess the impact of diabetic retinopathy and to explore the effects of different treatment strategies on disease progression40.

The findings of our study have significant clinical implications. The main mechanism by which DM duration leads to DR is prolonged exposure to high glucose levels, with fluctuations in blood glucose and episodes of hypoglycemia potentially exacerbating retinal changes27. Optimal glycemic control can help prevent or delay the onset and progression of DR, underscoring the importance of early management of blood glucose levels to postpone the development of DR41,42. Upon a diagnosis of T2DM, it becomes crucial to take prompt measures for preventing the occurrence of DR, with earlier intervention yielding better outcomes43. Early retinal screening for lesion signs and implementation of preventive measures can reduce the incidence of DR44.

However, there remains uncertainty regarding the optimal timing for early intervention and the extent to which early interventions can reduce the occurrence of DR. With the inflection point identified in our study at 8 years, we have determined the latest time point for early prevention of DR. Patients with a DM duration of fewer than 8 years can implement appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic measures to delay or even prevent the onset of DR. However, in those with a diabetes duration of longer than 8 years, there was no significant association between DR and diabetes duration, suggesting that implementing relevant measures for DR prevention after 8 years of disease duration may have limited clinical significance. Although there was no significant association between DM duration and DR prevalence in those with a diabetes duration of greater than eight years, the prevalence of DR was higher in this subgroup and ongoing routine DR screening is still required to prevent vision loss.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations as it is based on a secondary analysis of retrospective cross-sectional studies. The study primarily focused on individuals with T2DM, and it remains uncertain whether the findings can be generalized to individuals with type 1 diabetes. While this study established a relationship between DM duration and DR risk, it is important to note that DM duration itself cannot be directly intervened upon; interventions can only target factors such as blood glucose levels and urea levels. Thirdly, we did not conduct a detailed analysis of the severity of DR, we merely assessed the presence of DR in diabetic patients without evaluating the various stages of severity (DR2-5) in depth. This implies that we are unable to provide specific clinical characteristics and prognostic information for patients at different stages of DR. Future research could consider incorporating an assessment of DR severity to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the retinal health status of diabetic patients. Finally, as the data on the patients’ duration of diabetes are derived from their medical records, this method of data collection may result in an underestimation of the time since diagnosis, which could, in turn, affect the accuracy of the recorded DM duration. This potential bias might influence the assessment of DR. Nonetheless, the study provides valuable evidence for identifying a specific time point for early intervention, which can guide the development of effective strategies for early intervention45.

Conclusion

Our study found a non-linear relationship between the DM duration and DR in patients. When the DM duration is less than 8 years, there is a positive correlation between DM duration and DR. However, once the DM duration exceeds 8 years, the effect reaches saturation, and no significant correlation is observed.
