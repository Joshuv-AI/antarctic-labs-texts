Circulatory pro‐CTSD binds brain endothelial LRP1 to trigger its lysosomal degradation leading to amyloid beta clearance deficit in Alzheimer's disease mice
Authors: Zhi‐Jun Li, Zhao Yang, Dan Zhao, Yi‐Hua Qiu, Wen‐Yu Wan, Zeng‐Kang Huang, Li Tian, Rui‐Qun Qi, Hong‐Guang Liu, Yu‐Hua Chen, Dong‐Yu Min, Xiao‐Qian Xu, Wei‐Dong Zhao

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONClearance of cerebral Aβ was primarily mediated by the brain endothelial transporters including LRP1. The regulatory mechanism of LRP1 expression remained unclear.METHODSLRP1 in brain endothelial cells treated with pro‐CTSD were analyzed by western blot. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is associated with the decline in cognitive function, memory and mobility among individuals aged 65 years and older. 1 The global prevalence of dementia is expected to reach 150 million by 2050, imposing a heavy burden. 1 , 2 Amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition in the brain is widely acknowledged as the primary pathological changes in AD, 3 causing neuroinflammation, neurotoxicity and ultimately leading to neuronal loss. 3 , 4 Aβ accumulation results from the imbalance between Aβ production and Aβ clearance, and dysregulated Aβ clearance contributes to increased Aβ deposition and exacerbates AD progression. 5 , 6 , 7 Understanding the mechanisms of Aβ clearance may provide new therapeutic approaches to AD.

The blood‐brain barrier (BBB) is considered as the major pathway for Aβ clearance from brain parenchyma to the peripheral blood. 8 , 9 , 10 As the primary constituent of BBB, brain microvascular endothelial cells bearing Aβ receptors are the main cellular component mediating brain‐to‐blood Aβ transport. 11 The extracellular Aβ binds with brain endothelial receptors and is internalized with endocytic vesicles, which can be transported to peripheral circulation via transcytosis pathway. 11 Previous studies identified low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) 12 , 13 and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) 9 as the transporters responsible for Aβ efflux. LRP1 is the most well‐studied receptor facilitating Aβ clearance. Studies from in vitro Aβ efflux model have demonstrated that Aβ is internalized and transcytosed through LRP1. 14 , 15 Downregulation of LRP1 in the brain microvessels of mice by the LRP1 antisense oligonucleotide leads to impaired Aβ42 efflux. 16 Conditional knockout of LRP1 in the brain endothelium inhibited Aβ clearance in transgenic AD mice. 17 , 18 The reduction of LRP1 in brain endothelial cells of AD patients has been reported. 19 , 20 However, the regulatory mechanisms of brain endothelial LRP1 levels remain to be elucidated.

Cathepsin D (CTSD) is a lysosomal protease composed of disulfide‐linked 34 kDa heavy chain and 14 kDa light chain. The lysosomal CTSD can cleave Aβ into fragments at a low pH of 4.0‐4.5. 21 , 22 , 23 Genetic deletion of CTSD induced intracellular accumulation of Aβ in mice overexpressing human amyloid precursor protein. 24 The secreted prototype form of CTSD, pro‐CTSD has been identified in human plasma. 25 , 26 Our previous study found pro‐CTSD could interact with LRP1 in the brain endothelium. 25 Whether the circulatory pro‐CTSD is involved in LRP1 homeostasis, Aβ clearance, and AD progression remained unknown.

In this study, we found that circulatory pro‐CTSD downregulates LRP1 expression in brain microvascular endothelial cells. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that circulatory pro‐CTSD binds brain endothelial LRP1 to promote its internalization into lysosomes for degradation, thereby reducing LRP1‐mediated brain‐to‐blood Aβ efflux. Using AD mouse models, we found increased circulatory pro‐CTSD is associated with cerebral Aβ deposition and exacerbates the cognitive impairment.

METHODS

Animals and genotyping of transgenic mice

The use and care of animals are following the guidelines of local animal ethics committee, SPF Laboratory animal Center of China Medical University: Laboratory Animal Use License Number (SYXK (Liao) 2022‐0007). Approval number: CMU20241720. Wide type (WT) mice, the 5×FAD mice and the hCTSDhi transgenic mice were constructed and obtained from Cyagen Biosciences. 25 The APP/PS1 transgenic mice in the C57BL/6 genetic background were purchased from Nanjing Biomedical Research Institute of Nanjing University. The hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice were generated by crossing hCTSDhi transgenic mice with APP/PS1 transgenic mice. Mice were housed under a 12 h light‐dark cycle. Food and water were freely available. Both male and female mice were used in this research.

We used PCR analysis of tail DNA to confirm the genotypes of transgenic mice. Tail biopsies were acquired, and genomic DNA was prepared using Mouse Direct PCR Kit (Selleck).

Cell culture and stable THP‐1 cell line construction

Human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs) as a gift from Dr. K.S.Kim (Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD). Human monocytic leukemia cell line (THP‐1), Bend.3 cell line and HEK293T cell line were obtained from ATCC. Cells were cultured as described previously. 25 To construct human CTSD (NM_001909) overexpressing THP‐1 cell line, inserting the CTSD gene into the vector element to produce pLenti‐EF1a‐EGFP‐P2A‐Puro‐CMV‐CTSD‐3×Flag and conducting lentivirus infection with Lipofectamine 2000. Puromycin selection was applied at 1 µg/ml for a week. We successfully achieved stable overexpression of CTSD (CTSDOE) in monocyte cell lines THP‐1, along with the establishment of stable expression of the empty vector (Vector) in THP‐1 for use as a control. 25

Synthesis of the near‐infrared II (NIR‐II) fluorescent probe

The NIR‐II probe was synthesized by conjugating indocyanine green–N‐hydroxy succinimide ester (ICG‐NHS) with Aβ (1‐42) peptide. Briefly, 55 µL of ICG‐NHS (10 mg/mL in DMSO), 1 mg of Aβ (1‐42), and 4 µL of N‐ethyl‐diisopropylamine (DIPEA) were mixed in 1 mL of DMSO and reacted at room temperature for 4–6 h in the dark. The NHS ester group of ICG‐NHS covalently bound to the ε‐amino groups of lysine residues in Aβ (1‐42), forming stable amide bonds. The product was purified using 4 kDa ultrafiltration with repeated PBS washing. The purified conjugate ICG‐Aβ (1‐42) showed absorbance and emission peaks at 780 and 987 nm, respectively, and distinct HPLC retention times compared with free ICG‐NHS, confirming successful conjugation.

Immunofluorescence

For brain slices, mice were subjected to cardiac perfusion and isolated the whole brain tissue. The brain sections of 80 µm thickness were obtained using Vibratomes (Leica) after 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation for at least 24 h. After adequate rinsing in 0.1 M PBS, the slices were blocked with 5% donkey serum containing 0.5% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Incubation with the primary antibodies for overnight at 4°C, and the fluorescent secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:200) avoid from the light at room temperature for 3 h. Nucleus staining was applied with DAPI for 15 min and stored covered at 4°C until imaging.

For cell slices, cells were seeded on the sterile coverslips and fixed using 4% PFA for 30 min. The cell slices were blocked with 2%‐3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS and incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C for overnight. Followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:200) at room temperature for 1 h and DAPI for 15 min. Images were captured by Stereo fluorescence microscope (Leica), CLSM (Nikon), or TIRFM (Olympus).

Stereotaxic injection

Male and female WT and hCTSDhi mice at 6‐ and 9‐month‐old age were performed for stereotaxic injection of HiLyte™ Fluor‐555 labeled Aβ (1‐42) peptide (Anaspec, AS‐60480‐1) into the prefrontal cortex. Anesthetized the mice with 2% isoflurane (RWD) during the surgery. The mice were fixed keep the ear and the skull in the same horizontal line. Expose the skull with sterilization surgery scissors and adjust the level of the anterior and posterior fontanels using the stereotaxic apparatus (RWD). Drill a hole on the skull at +1.0 mm in the anterior‐posterior (AP) axis and ‐1.0 mm in the medial‐lateral (ML) axis relative to bregma. A 10 µL Hamilton syringe was placed at ‐1.5 mm in the dorsal‐ventral (DV) axis relative to bregma, 2 µL (20 µM) of HiLyteTM Fluor 555‐labeled Aβ (1‐42) peptide or 200 ng ICG‐ Aβ at 100 ng/µL was injected into the prefrontal cortex at a rate of 0.3 µL/min. After the injection, the syringe was kept in place for another 5 min before removing. The skin of the scalp was glued (3M™ Vetbond™ Tissue Adhesive), and the mice were allowed to recover on a heat pad. Then placed the mice back to the cage for further rearing or live imaging under the NIR‐II SAIS system.

Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) imaging

The brain slices or cell coverslips were captured on the CLSM system (Nikon A1R HD25) using the NIS‐Elements software. Images were captured by using a 20 × objective lens and a 60 × objective oil lens.

For cerebrum slices, micro vessels were labeled using CD31 antibody (R&D, AF3628, 1:400) and imaged continuously of 10‐20 µm thickness with 1 µm per step using Z series. To observe LRP1 distribution on the single micro vessel using LRP1 antibody (Santa Cruz, sc‐57353, 1:200) and CD31 antibody for immunostaining, and the images were captured using single stack. Also, beta amyloid (Cell Signaling Technology, D54D2, 1:500) and the neuron marker NeuN (Proteintech, 26975‐1‐AP) were observed through staining of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus area. At least 6 mice of WT or transgenic mice were used for imaging and no less than 5 fields of view were captured per mouse. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and area were statistically analyzed using ImageJ software, and the vessel density was analyzed using AngioTool software.

For cell coverslips, cells were performed for immunofluorescence followed by multichannel imaging with GFP, TRITC, and 647 nm channels. Each group was carried out in three independent replications and no less than 10 fields were captured in each shot, with a final count of over 30 cells. The images were processed and analyzed by ImageJ software.

Total internal reflection fluorescent microscope (TIRFM) imaging

TIRF microscopy images were performed using Olympus cellSens Dimension software on a microscope (IX83 P2ZF, Olympus) using a camera (Photometrics Prime BSI) and an UPLAPO OHR 100×/1.5 NA objective oil lens. Cells were subjected to immunostaining using LRP1 antibody (Abcam, ab92544, 1:200) or HA antibody (Proteintech, 51064‐2‐AP, 1:50) without permeability and then placed under the microscope for observation. A 488 nm or 561 nm laser is used to exite the green or red fluorescent signal with the penetration depth at 100–150 nm. Images were applied for deconvolution using the cellSens Dimension 3.1 software. Each group was carried out in at least three independent replications, with a final count of over 30 cells, or over 150 puncta. The puncta and mean intensity were analyzed utilizing ImageJ software.

Stereo fluorescence microscope imaging

After brain stereotactic injection of HiLyte™ Fluor‐555 labeled Aβ (1‐42) peptide (Anaspec, AS‐60480‐01) and cerebrum collection, the brain slices were performed immunostaining using S100β antibody (Proteintech, 15146‐1‐AP, 1:200). The images were captured on Leica microscope (M165 FC) using Leica Application Suite X software and a camera (DFC310FX‐745521015). For the assessment of brain fluorescent peptide efflux, 7 representative brain sections were imaged and the mean area fraction of Aβ (1‐42) were calculated by the ratio of red / green fluorescence area using ImageJ software.

Near‐infrared II (NIR‐II) live imaging

After shaving the scalp, mice were injected with 200 ng of the near‐infrared‐II (NIR‐II) fluorescent probe ICG‐Aβ via stereotaxic surgery. The mice were then anesthetized in an isoflurane chamber and positioned on the stage of the NIR‐II SAIS imaging system, with the head securely fixed. Imaging parameters were optimized to avoid excessive laser power or signal saturation. The fluorescence images were acquired hourly for 6 h postinjection. The MFI of each image was quantified under consistent exposure settings. The MFI at 30 min postinjection was defined as the peak value, representing the maximal exogenous Aβ load in the mice brain. The relative Aβ retention percentage at each time point was calculated and plotted over time to generate kinetic curves.

Western blot

Total protein was isolated, and the concentration was determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the technical protocol. The protein samples were supplemented with Dual Color SDS‐PAGE Protein Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime) and boiled, subjected to SDS‐PAGE using an Easy PAGE Gel Fast Preparation Kit (Seven Biotech). The samples were separated using a vertical electrophoresis system and transferred to the PVDF membrane on the ice. Next, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated with the primary antibodies (Supplementary methods) for overnight at 4°C. Then the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:10000) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with 1×TBST, tSignals were developed with SuperSignal West Pico Plus (Thermo Fisher) and Tanon 5200 imaging system (Tanon) and were quantified by ImageJ software.

Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)

Co‐immunoprecipitation was performed using Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime) from HEK293T lysates 48 h after transfection. Lysates (NP‐40 buffer with protease inhibitors) were incubated with control IgG or the indicated antibodies (anti‐HA or anti‐pro‐CTSD) overnight at 4°C, followed by binding to Protein A/G beads. After washing, bound complexes were eluted in SDS sample buffer and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies.

Single‐cell RNA‐seq data analysis

Relative mRNA expression of Lrp1, P‐gp, and Rage in the brain microvascular endothelial cells from WT and hCTSDhi mice were obtained from our single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset and analyzed by differential gene expression as previously described. 25

in vitro model of amyloid beta transport

For structural modeling, the human LRP1 cluster II sequence (NP_002332.3, residues 190‐1190) was extracted, and the pro‐CTSD structure was predicted using AlphaFold2. To simulate the transport of amyloid beta from brain parenchyma across the BBB, we firstly utilized the HBMECs at the density of 2×105 cells per well seeded on the filter membrane with a pore size of 0.4 µm in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning, 3413). The HBMECs were cultured to reach confluence before incubating with the CTSDOE CM or rhCTSD (1 µg/ml) for 48 h in the lower chamber. Subsequently, the HiLyte™ Fluor‐555 labeled Aβ (1‐42) peptide (200 ng/ml) was added, and the incubation was continued for another 2 ∼ 4 h at 37°C in a CO2 incubator. The fluid in the upper chamber was collected and the fluorescence intensity (FI) at Abs/Em = 551 nm/567 nm was detected using a SpectraMax M5 (Molecular Devices). The group without the addition of Aβ was used as the control, and the control value was subtracted from the actual value. The results were analyzed comparatively to obtain the relative multiplicity of change using Prism software.

Cloning

Standard procedures were applied as described below. HA‐LRP1‐C1, HA‐LRP1‐C2, HA‐LRP1‐C3 and HA‐LRP1‐C4 plasmids were gifts from Kenneth S. Then cloned the mutation sequences into the original vector to generate the HA‐LRP1‐C2‐mutant plasmids. This work was entrusted to Shanghai Sangon Biotech.

Molecular docking model of interaction between pro‐CTSD (blue) and LRP1‐C2 structure (pink) using Protein‐Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP).

Behavioral assessments

Novel object recognition (NOR) were conducted as previously described. 28 Mice were acclimated to the testing room for 2 h before testing. Then the mice were allowed to move freely in the empty open‐field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) for 10 min to adapt to the environment. 24 h after habituation, the mouse was placed in the same box containing two familiar objects and allowed to explore for 10 min. The mice were given an intersession interval of 2 h. Before the test phase, one of the two familiar objects in the box was replaced with a novel one and the mouse was allowed to explore the box for 10 min. Exploration was defined as the mouse directing its snout toward an object at close range or making physical contact, time spent climbing on or sitting against the object was not counted. Locomotor trajectories were automatically tracked and recorded using video‐tracking software. Memory performance was quantified as a preference index calculated by the ratio of the time spent exploring the novel object to the total time spent exploring both the novel and the familiar objects. To minimize olfactory cues, the box and objects were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials and allowed to dry before the next test.

The Y‐maze test was performed as described previously. 29 The Y‐maze consists of three symmetrical arms measuring 50 cm × 9 cm with an angle of 120° between the arms. Mice were acclimated to the testing room for 2 h before testing. In the training phase, the mouse was allowed to shuttle between the start arm and the family arm, while the novel arm was turned off. Then the mice were given an intersession interval of 2 h. In the test phase, the novel arm was opened, and the mouse was shuttled freely among the three arms for 10 min. The time, ambulation, and number of visits in both the novel and familiar arm were recorded by the investigator, and the percentage of ambulation and time spent by mice in the novel arm were quantified. The maze was cleaned thoroughly with 70% ethanol and dried between trials to minimize olfactory cues, and scoring was performed by an investigator blinded to genotype and treatment.

Data statistical analysis

For data with n ≥ 6, the normality of data distribution was assessed by Shapiro‐Wilk test and all data conform to normal distribution. Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed by Student's t‐test. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two‐way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. The data are presented as mean ± SD for group variability, whereas imaging quantifications are reported as mean ± SEM for comparisons of estimated means. p value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. RESULTS

Circulatory pro‐CTSD downregulates brain endothelial LRP1

To analyze the potential involvement of pro‐CTSD in AD pathogenesis, we measured the levels of pro‐CTSD in the plasma of 5×FAD transgenic mouse model of AD (Figure S1). Western blot analysis showed that plasma pro‐CTSD levels were increased in 3‐month‐old 5×FAD mice compared with the wild‐type (WT) littermates control mice (Figure 1A and 1B). To identify the potential source of pro‐CTSD, we collected the paired carotid arterial and jugular venous plasma 30 , 31 and measured the CTSD concentrations by ELISA assays. The results showed that the CTSD were at similar levels between arterial and venous plasma in 5×FAD mice (Figure 1C), indicating a systemic elevation of circulatory CTSD rather than a brain‐restricted origin in AD mice.

Then, the hCTSDhi transgenic mice with high‐level of plasma pro‐CTSD 25 were used for further experiments. The single‐cell RNA sequencing data of the brain endothelial cells of hCTSDhi transgenic mice were utilized to analyze the expressional changes of Aβ transporters including LRP1, P‐gp (also known as ABCB1) and RAGE. 11 The results showed no significant difference between hCTSDhi and WT mice (Figure 1D). In addition, the effect of pro‐CTSD on Aβ transporters were detected in cultured human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs) in vitro. HBMECs were treated with different concentration of recombinant human pro‐CTSD (rhCTSD) for 48 h followed by western blot analysis. The results showed that LRP1α and LRP1β protein levels were reduced in HBMECs by rhCTSD treatment in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 1E and 1F). In contrast, the expression of P‐gp and RAGE, two additional Aβ transporters besides LRP1, remained unchanged (Figure 1E and 1F). Furthermore, LRP1 levels in the HBMECs were assessed after treatment with conditional media (CM) collected from THP‐1 monocytic cells overexpressing human CTSD (CTSDOE), which has been demonstrated 25 to contain secreted pro‐CTSD. The HBMECs treated with the CM from THP‐1 monocytic cells expressing empty vector were used as control (Vector). Western blot analysis revealed that LRP1α and LRP1β protein levels were significantly reduced in HBMECs after 96 h of CTSDOE CM treatment compared with control CM (Figure 1G and 1H).

To verify above findings, we further evaluated LRP1 expression in vivo by performing immunofluorescence staining using brain slices from hCTSDhi mice, with brain slices from WT mice as control. The slices were stained with antibodies recognizing LRP1 as well as CD31, a vascular endothelium marker (Figure 1I). Consistently, hCTSDhi mice exhibited markedly lower LRP1 expression in cortical and hippocampal microvessels compared with WT mice (Figure 1I and 1J). These data illustrated that the protein levels of LRP1 in the brain endothelium is downregulated by circulatory pro‐CTSD. In addition, no significant changes were observed in endothelial proliferation, migration, or tube formation after CTSDOE CM treatment compared with Vector CM treatment (Figure S2), demonstrating that the pro‐CTSD had no obvious effect on general endothelial cell function.

Aβ efflux from the brain is inhibited by circulatory pro‐CTSD

LRP1 is one of the main transporters responsible for the clearance of brain‐derived Aβ. 12 , 18 Given that circulatory pro‐CTSD downregulates cerebral microvascular LRP1 expression (Figure 1), we hypothesized that pro‐CTSD may affect the Aβ clearance from brain. To test this, we firstly constructed near‐infrared‐II (NIR‐II) fluorescent dye Indocyanine Green‐N‐Hydroxy Succinimide Ester (ICG‐NHS) labeled Aβ (1‐42), denoted as ICG‐Aβ. ICG‐NHS exhibits advantages such as strong penetration and low biotoxicity, with recent studies demonstrating its applicability for in vivo experiments. 32 The ICG‐Aβ showed an absorption peak at 780 nm, and its emission wavelength was detected at 987 nm using a spectrometer (Figure 2A). High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of ICG and ICG‐Aβ at 780 nm revealed distinct peaks under the same mobile phase, indicating their different properties (Figure 2B). Moreover, we performed a comparative analysis using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy to determine whether ICG conjugation alters the aggregation propensity of Aβ42. 33 The CD spectra of ICG‐Aβ42 and native Aβ42 in dissolved states displayed nearly overlapping curves, indicative of a predominantly random coil conformation (Figure S3). Following aggregation in vitro, both samples exhibited a strong negative peak around 220 nm, which is a well‐established spectral signature for β‐sheet formation (Figure S3). These data showed that ICG conjugation did not alter the in vitro aggregation properties of Aβ (1‐42).

Then, the ICG‐Aβ peptides were injected into the cerebral cortex of 6‐month‐old hCTSDhi mice by brain stereotaxic injection (AP = + 1.0 mm, ML = ‐1.0 mm, DV = ‐1.5 mm), with littermate WT mice as controls. The mouse head was immobilized on the stage of the NIR‐II imaging system under continuous anesthesia with 1% isoflurane. Initial imaging was performed at 30 min postinjection, and repeated hourly up to 6 h (Figure 2C). The statistical analysis showed impaired Aβ clearance rate in the brains of hCTSDhi mice compared to WT control mice (Figure 2D). Furthermore, 6‐month‐old WT mice were received intravenous injection with recombinant pro‐CTSD proteins (rhCTSD) at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg for 3 consecutive days (Figure S4A and S4B), and we found the vascular LRP1 in brain sections was reduced compared with the PBS controls (Figure S4C and S4D). Consistently, compared with PBS controls, the rhCTSD‐injected mice showed a significantly slower clearance rate for the cerebral Aβ (Figure S4E and S4F). These data provide the causal demonstration that high levels of circulatory pro‐CTSD acts as a driver to downregulate endothelial LRP1 and to impair brain Aβ clearance.

To test age‐dependent differences in Aβ clearance between hCTSDhi and WT mice, we induced Aβ aggregates in the mouse cerebral cortex using the fluorescent Aβ (1‐42) peptides. The Aβ (1‐42) peptides were injected into the cerebral cortex of 6‐ and 9‐month‐old hCTSDhi mice using the same stereotaxic coordinates with age‐matched littermate WT mice as controls. Then the successive transverse brain slices were obtained 5 d after injection. The brain Aβ deposition was analyzed by immunostaining with antibody against S100β, an astrocytic marker, to detect the slice profile (Figure 2E). 7 successive brain sections were prepared and the Aβ deposition area in the slice was measured. The results showed that the residual Aβ deposition area in the brain of 6‐ and 9‐month‐old hCTSDhi mice were significantly larger than that in WT control mice (Figure 2F and 2G), suggesting that the cerebral Aβ clearance was attenuated by circulatory pro‐CTSD.

Then we established in vitro model of brain endothelial cell barrier to assess the effect of pro‐CTSD on endothelial Aβ efflux. The HBMECs were seeded on the filter membrane in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert for 2‐3 d until forming confluent monolayer. Then, the fluorescent Aβ (1‐42) were added to the lower chamber and the Aβ in the upper chamber was measured 4 h after rhCTSD treatment to assess Aβ efflux from the basal (the lower chamber) to the luminal (the upper chamber) side of the endothelial barrier (Figure 2H). The results showed that pro‐CTSD treatment inhibits the Aβ efflux in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 2I). Consistent results were obtained using the conditional media (CM) from THP‐1 cells overexpressing human CTSD (CTSDOE) (Figure 2J). These results indicated that circulatory pro‐CTSD inhibits Aβ clearance from the brain.

Pro‐CTSD induces lysosomal degradation of LRP1 in brain endothelial cells

LRP1 is a well‐studied endocytic receptor that are routed to endosomes after internalization. Thus, it could be speculated that the LRP1 protein reduction in brain endothelial cells induced by pro‐CTSD may be caused by lysosomal degradation. To verify this hypothesis, stable THP‐1 cells overexpressing Flag‐tagged CTSD was established by lentivirus‐mediated transfection followed by puromycin selection, and the expression of Flag‐tagged CTSD was verified by western blot (Figure 3A). Then the HBMECs were incubated with CTSDOE CM from THP‐1 cells overexpressing CTSD followed by immunostaining to analyze the lysosomal localization of LRP1. The CM‐treated HBMECs were stained with antibodies recognizing LRP1 and lysosomal marker Lamp1, respectively. The results showed that the co‐localization of LRP1 with Lamp1 was significantly increased in the brain endothelial cells treated with CTSDOE CM compared to control (Figure 3B and 3C), consistent with enhanced lysosomal trafficking of LRP1. To determine the intracellular localization of LRP1‐pro‐CTSD complex, we performed immunostaining with antibodies recognizing Rab11 and Rab7, the markers for recycling endosomes and late endosomes, respectively. The Vector CM or CTSDOE CM‐treated HBMECs were used for immunostaining, and the co‐localization of LRP1 with Rab7 or Rab11 were quantified as Manders’ coefficients. The results showed that CTSDOE CM treatment increased the co‐localization of LRP1 with Rab7 compared with Vector CM, whereas the LRP1 co‐localization with Rab11 remained unchanged (Figure S5A and S5B), supporting preferential sorting of LRP1‐pro‐CTSD complex toward the late endosome–lysosome pathway rather than recycling endosomal route.

To verify the lysosomal degradation of LRP1 by pro‐CTSD, the CTSDOE CM‐treated HBMECs were pre‐incubated with V‐ATPase inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) to inhibit lysosomal acidification followed by western blot analysis of LRP1. The results showed that the CTSDOE CM‐induced reduction of LRP1α were prevented by BafA1 treatment (Figure 3D). Then the localization of LRP1 on the cell membrane was resolved by immunostaining followed by total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM). We observed numerous individual LRP1‐positive puncta on the plasma membrane of brain endothelial cells (Figure 3E). We further found that the treatment with CTSDOE CM induced a prominent reduction in the number and FI of LRP1‐positive puncta compared to control, and this reduction was significantly attenuated by BafA1 treatment (Figure 3E and 3F).

Given the binding of pro‐CTSD with LRP1, 25 we hypothesized that the extracellular pro‐CTSD per se could be internalized into brain endothelial cells. To test this, the Bend.3 brain endothelial cells were treated with CTSDOE CM and the localization of Flag‐tagged CTSD within cells were analyzed by immunostaining. Interestingly, we observed the co‐localization of Flag with LRP1 in the Lamp1‐positive region in brain endothelial cells incubated with CTSDOE CM (Figure 3G), suggesting lysosomal delivery of extracellular Flag‐tagged pro‐CTSD. Manders’ analysis of triple co‐localization (Flag/LRP1/LAMP1) in individual cells yielded 15.6% ± 1.4% (Mean ± SEM, n = 6). These results collectively indicated that the extracellular pro‐CTSD bound with LRP1 and was then co‐internalized into the lysosomes, leading to degradation of LRP1 in brain endothelial cells.

Pro‐CTSD binds cluster 2 domain of LRP1 to facilitate its internalization

Studies have shown that the extracellular domain of LRP1 contains cluster 2 (C2), C3 and C4 subdomains for ligand binding. 34 , 35 , 36 To determine the cluster specifically bound with pro‐CTSD, the constructs containing HA‐tagged C2, C3 and C4 of LRP1 ectodomain, respectively, were prepared (Figure 4A). The constructs were then co‐transfected with His‐tagged pro‐CTSD into HEK293 cells for immunoprecipitation assay. The results showed that C2, but not C3 and C4, domain of LRP1 could be associated with pro‐CTSD (Figure 4B). Then we performed molecular docking analysis to identify the amino acid residues on LRP1 C2 domain that most likely bind with pro‐CTSD. The binding free energy of pro‐CTSD/LRP1‐C2 complex was ‐36.91 kcal/mol. According to the results of molecular docking analysis, three amino acid residues involved in formation of salt bridges with lysine and histidine residues of pro‐CTSD are identified, that is Asp‐810 (D810), Glu‐819 (E819), and Asp‐853 (D853) (Figure 4C). Then we generated LRP1‐C2 mutant construct with these three amino acid residues mutated to glycine (top panel, Figure 4D). The constructs were then co‐transfected with His‐tagged pro‐CTSD into HEK293 cells for immunoprecipitation analysis. The results showed that the LRP1‐C2‐mutant failed to bind with pro‐CTSD (bottom panel, Figure 4D). Subsequently, TIRFM was employed to investigate the effect of recombinant pro‐CTSD on the membrane distribution of LRP1‐C2 using LRP1‐C2‐transfected HEK293T cells. The results revealed a significant reduction of membrane surface LRP1‐C2 upon exposure to recombinant human pro‐CTSD (rhCTSD) treatment (Figure 4E and 4F). In contrast, the distribution of LRP1‐C2‐mutant on the plasma membrane remained unchanged by rhCTSD treatment (Figure 4E and 4F). These data demonstrated that pro‐CTSD binds with the amino acid residues D810/E819/D853 in the cluster 2 of the extracellular domain of LRP1 to facilitate its internalization.

Circulatory pro‐CTSD exacerbates Aβ deposition in the brain of AD mice

To further investigate this association, we generated the hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice by crossing hCTSDhi mice with APP/PS1 mice, the latter serving as an AD pathological model exhibiting significant cerebral Aβ deposition. We first determined whether pro‐CTSD may affect the brain vasculature which is critical for Aβ clearance. 37 The brain slices of hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice were obtained and subjected to immunostaining using antibody against CD31, a vascular endothelial marker. The results showed that the vascular density in the hippocampal and cortical regions of 6‐, 9‐, and 12‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice was similar to that in the age‐matched APP/PS1 mice (Figure 5A–C). These suggested that the brain vasculature remained unaffected by the increased circulatory pro‐CTSD in plasma.

We then assessed Aβ deposition by immunostained with Aβ antibody (Figure 5D). The Aβ‐positive plaque area was measured for quantification analysis (Figure 5E–G). We found that the Aβ plaques in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus regions of 6‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice were at similar levels with that in APP/PS1 mice (Figure 5E–G). To validate Aβ deposition biochemically, we performed ELISA assays to quantify Aβ40 and Aβ42 in 6‐month‐old 5×FAD and hCTSDhi/5×FAD brain homogenates, measuring both PBS‐soluble and 5 M GuHCl‐extracted insoluble fractions. The results showed that both soluble and insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels were significantly increased in hCTSDhi/5×FAD mice compared with 5×FAD controls (Figure 5F). Interestingly, we found that the Aβ deposition in the cortical and hippocampal regions were significantly increased in 9‐ and 12‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice compared to that in age‐matched APP/PS1 mice (Figure 5E‐G). Together, these results indicated that circulatory pro‐CTSD exacerbated Aβ deposition in the brain of AD mice without affecting brain vasculature.

hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice exhibit exacerbated memory impairment

Neuronal loss in the brain has been well established in AD. 38 Thus, we attempt to examine the neuronal damage in the brain of hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice. The brain slices of hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice were obtained and subjected to immunostaining using antibody against NeuN, a neuronal nuclear marker. The results indicated that, compared with the APP/PS1 mice, the neurons in the CA1 region were significantly reduced in 6‐, 9‐ and 12‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice (left, Figure 6B). The number of neurons in the dentate gyrus region (DG) was decreased in the 12‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice compared to age‐matched APP/PS1 mice (middle, Figure 6B), whereas no significant change was observed in the neurons at the cortex region (right, Figure 6B). These findings suggested that circulatory pro‐CTSD significantly exacerbate neuronal loss in the hippocampal region of AD mice.

Then, we evaluated the cognitive performance of the hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice. In the NOR test, hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice showed diminished exploratory ability towards novel objects at 9 and 12 months of age compared to age‐matched APP/PS1 mice (Figure 6C). The new object exploration ability did not show any significant differences in the 6‐month‐old hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice compared to the age‐matched APP/PS1 mice (Figure 6C). Consistently, the results from Y‐maze test showed that hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice spent significantly less time in the novel arm than APP/PS1 mice at 9 and 12 months of age (Figure 6D). These indicated that circulatory pro‐CTSD enhances neuronal loss and exacerbates cognitive impairment in AD mice.

DISCUSSION

The association of plasma CTSD levels with AD

Previous studies showed the association between plasma CTSD levels and AD progression. Results from ELISA assays showed that the plasma CTSD concentration of patients with mild AD and severe AD were significantly elevated compared to age‐matched controls. 39 A clinical study involving 109 subjects corroborates these findings, revealing that serum CTSD levels in patients with AD were higher than those without cognitive impairment and those exhibiting mild cognitive impairment. 40 In line with these findings, in this study, we found the elevated levels of plasma pro‐CTSD in 5×FAD mice (Figure 1A‐C), and revealed the contribution of pro‐CTSD to cerebral Aβ clearance and Aβ deposition in the brain (Figure 2B and 2C). It is noteworthy that a few studies reported that the peripheral CTSD is decreased, rather than increased, in AD patients. 41 Kim et al. performed immunoblotting and ELISA in a 56‐participant cohort and observed a negative association between plasma CTSD and amyloid pathology. 42 Considering the well‐established defective endo‐lysosomal degradation pathway in AD 43 and the evidence showing loss of CTSD gene causing lysosomal dysfunction, 44 it could be deduced that the decreased CTSD 45 might be the mature form of CTSD derived from the lysosomal pathway. To our knowledge, studies that specifically measure the pro‐CTSD levels in the plasma of AD patients are still lacking. It is thus interesting to determine the changes of circulatory pro‐CTSD in AD patients in future studies.

Our findings presented circulatory pro‐CTSD as a potential biomarker and a mechanistic mediator linking peripheral alterations to impaired cerebral Aβ clearance via the BBB pathway. Recent studies reported Aβ clearance also involves the glymphatic CSF‐ISF exchange pathway 46 and meningeal lymphatic drainage. 47 Given that pro‐CTSD is a circulatory factor that acts on the endothelial interface, it is an interesting issue to investigate whether the Aβ clearance by glymphatic–lymphatic pathway is affected by circulatory pro‐CTSD.

High‐level plasma pro‐CTSD exacerbates AD progression in an age‐dependent manner

Previous studies show that in APP/PS1 mice, Aβ deposition begins around 4–6 months and increases markedly by 9–12 months, accompanied by progressive synaptic loss and neuroinflammatory responses. 48 , 49 In line with this disease progression, we found that CTSD overexpression did not significantly exacerbate cognitive deficits at 6 months but markedly aggravated cognitive impairment at 9 and 12 months (Figure 6C and 6D). Consistently, Aβ staining revealed an age‐dependent increase in amyloid deposition in hCTSDhi/APP/PS1 mice (Figure 5C–F), indicating that the behavioral worsening is associated with enhanced Aβ burden. These findings suggested that circulatory CTSD may act as an age‐dependent amplifier of amyloid pathology.

Circulatory pro‐CTSD represents a potential risk factor linking diabetes with AD

Epidemiology studies suggested that the prevalence of AD is increased in type 2 diabetes. 50 Aβ deposition in the brain is enhanced under diabetic condition in rodents 51 , 52 and nonhuman primates. 53 Regarding the mechanism, studies showed that Aβ production in the brain was enhanced by insulin resistance, 54 hyperglycemia 55 and dyslipidemia. 56 In terms of Aβ clearance in the brain under diabetic condition, though the diabetes‐associated cerebrovascular dysfunction was assumed to impair Aβ clearance, 57 the exact molecular mechanism of disturbed Aβ clearance remained unclear. Here we found the cerebral Aβ clearance was inhibited by the circulatory pro‐CTSD (Figure 2C–G), the level of which is increased in the plasma of type 2 diabetes patients. 25 Mechanistically, we found pro‐CTSD specifically triggered the degradation of cerebral vascular LRP1 (Figure 1F–K), a major transporter carrying Aβ across the BBB. Thus, it could be deduced that the circulatory pro‐CTSD might serve as a risk factor to link type 2 diabetes with Aβ pathology in AD. A combined T2DM‐AD model with pro‐CTSD neutralization may clarify whether pro‐CTSD indeed mediates the diabetes‐associated impairment of Aβ clearance and cognitive decline.

Extracellular pro‐CTSD is internalized and activated in brain endothelial cells

The internalization and activation of zymogen within cells represent a general mechanism in eukaryotic cells. Regarding the mechanism of internalization, the non‐activated zymogen is primarily internalized through phagocytic pathway. For example, the trypsinogen was internalized via phagocytosis and then converted to active trypsin within murine macrophages. 58 Occasionally, zymogen could be endocytosed into the cells via receptor‐mediated pathway. Studies showed the mannose 6‐phosphate (M6P) receptor‐mediated protein internalization into rat cardiac myocytes 59 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). 60 In this study, we found that the extracellular non‐enzymatic pro‐CTSD could be internalized into brain endothelial cells (Figure 3G), which is in line with the reported internalization of recombinant pro‐CTSD protein into H4 neuroglioma cells. 61 The lysosomal localization of the internalized pro‐CTSD (arrows, Figure 3G) indicated that the circulating pro‐CTSD can be activated within the acidic environment of lysosomes in endothelial cells, similar to the maturation and activation of endogenously produced pro‐CTSD. 62 Regarding the internalization mechanism of pro‐CTSD, we identified that the extracellular pro‐CTSD bound with the cluster 2 domain of LRP1 receptor, a well‐known receptor controlling receptor‐mediated endocytosis, 63 on the brain endothelial cells to facilitate its internalization (Figure 4B, E and F). Thus, our study presents pro‐CTSD as an additional non‐activated zymogen that could be transported to endothelial cells via receptor‐mediated endocytosis leading to its activation.

The luminal and abluminal LRP1 at brain endothelium

Membrane receptors for growth factors and cytokines display a polarized distribution in endothelial cells. 64 , 65 , 66 Given the findings that brain endothelial LRP1 is required for Aβ clearance, 18 , 19 it has been thought that LRP1 is located at the abluminal, brain‐facing side of polarized endothelial cells, to bind with extracellular Aβ for its clearance. 67 in vitro study reported that LRP1 may transport the abluminally‐endocytosed Aβ to the luminal expressed P‐gp for efflux into the blood. 9 In our study, the circulatory pro‐CTSD binds with brain endothelial LRP1 (Figure 4B) to promote its degradation (Figure 3D–F), suggesting the localization of LRP1 at the luminal side of brain microvessels. The expression of LRP1 at the luminal side of brain endothelium has been implicated by a study showing that LRP1 can be utilized for delivery of therapeutic angiopeps to the brain. 68 The regulatory mechanism underlying the distribution of luminal and abluminal LRP1 at the BBB is presently unexplored.

We revealed that circulatory pro‐CTSD binds endothelial LRP1 to suppress the Aβ clearance from the brain (Figure 2), indicating the binding of pro‐CTSD with luminal LRP1 suppressed the Aβ transport mediated by the abluminal LRP1. These results implied that the abluminal pool of LRP1 may be regulated by the luminal LRP1 and the exact mechanism remained to be determined in future study.

Pro‐CTSD binds LRP1 to activate endocytosis as well as signal transduction

Ligand‐dependent endocytosis and transmembrane signal transduction have generally been regarded as independent processes. In this study, we found that soluble pro‐CTSD acts as a ligand of LRP1 to promote the endocytosis of LRP1 into the lysosomal compartment (Figure 3B–G). Interestingly, our previous research has revealed that pro‐CTSD could bind LRP1 to activate the transcription of caveolin‐1 in brain endothelial cells. 25 These two seemingly separate processes could be reconciled with the findings showing that LRP1 receptor is directly involved in the transduction of signaling events. Studies reported the direct interaction of LRP1 with signaling adaptor protein including SHC‐adaptor protein (Shc) 69 and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95). 70 , 71 Thus, pro‐CTSD bind LRP1 to activate independent processes including LRP1 endocytosis as well as transmembrane signal transduction in brain endothelial cells.
