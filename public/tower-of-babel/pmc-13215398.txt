Adolescent predictors of post-traumatic growth during the COVID-19 pandemic: a 28-year longitudinal study
Authors: Ingrid Elise Oldereid Andersen, Gertrud Sofie Hafstad, Ole Martin Solberg, Tilmann von Soest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Despite growing interest in the positive psychological consequences of trauma, such as post-traumatic growth (PTG), most existing studies rely on cross-sectional designs, limiting our understanding of how and when growth occurs. Longitudinal research is needed to identify early-life antecedents of PTG and clarify its developmental pathways.Objective: This study examined whether social, psychological, and sociodemographic factors in adolescence predict PTG in midlife in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.Method: We used data from a 28-year population-based longitudinal study (N = 2,215). Adolescent predictors from 1992 included social relationships, mental health, and sociodemographic characteristics. PTG in response to the COVID-19 pandemic was measured in 2020 using the three subscales relating to others, personal strength, and appreciation of life from a short version of the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory. We conducted univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses and tested for possible moderation effects of gender.Results: Having close friendships and caring parents in adolescence consistently predicted higher PTG in midlife. Social acceptance was related to increased PTG in univariate analyses, but not in adjusted models. Mental health problems were univariately associated with greater scores on the appreciation of life subscale. Immigrant status predicted greater appreciation of life in both univariate and multivariate models, whereas higher parental education was associated with lower scores on the personal strength subscale. No significant moderation effects of gender were observed.Conclusions: This is the first study to investigate adolescent predictors of PTG in midlife. Findings highlight the lasting importance of social and familial support during adolescence for fostering positive psychological outcomes later in life.KEYWORDS: Post-traumatic growth, adolescence, longitudinal study, social relationships, COVID-19

Introduction

The positive psychological consequences of traumatic and highly stressful events, such as post-traumatic growth (PTG), have received growing attention in research, particularly with respect to identifying factors that promote such growth. However, most existing studies rely on cross-sectional designs or assess predictors after individuals have already experienced a stressful event. This makes it difficult to differentiate true antecedents of PTG from characteristics that emerge as result of the traumatic event. To gain further insights into predispositions for PTG, more longitudinal research is needed (Lowe et al., 2013; Su & Chen, 2015). To address this gap, this study investigates whether social, psychological, and sociodemographic factors measured in adolescence predict PTG in midlife, specifically in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Drawing on 28 years of prospective data, this study offers a unique opportunity to examine long-term longitudinal relationships between these factors and positive psychological adaptation following adversity.

Post-traumatic growth and the COVID-19 pandemic

PTG refers to positive psychological changes that can arise from the struggle with highly challenging life circumstances (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 2004). These circumstances are often referred to as ‘seismic events’ in the PTG literature, an analogy to earthquakes used to illustrate how these experiences can disrupt individuals’ fundamental assumptions about themselves and the world (Tedeschi et al., 1998). These disruptions can substantially challenge existing cognitive structures and initiate a process of cognitive reprocessing, involving problem solving, making sense of, and finding meaning in the event. Through this cognitive work, new schematic structures about the world and the self are developed, a process which is referred to as ‘growth’ (Janoff-Bulman, 2006).

The COVID-19 pandemic represents a seismic event that likely disrupted basic assumptions for many people. It challenged perceptions of personal and collective vulnerability (Maaravi & Heller, 2020) and altered daily routines, personal roles, and experiences of personal freedom (Masiero et al., 2020; Seto, 2024). For many, the pandemic represented a source of psychological distress, with studies documenting significant increases in anxiety, depression, and stress during this period (Vindegaard & Benros, 2020). Despite this stress, research indicates that a substantial share of individuals experienced some degree of PTG during the pandemic (Lau et al., 2021; Ulset & von Soest, 2022).

Predictors of post-traumatic growth

Although previous research has examined proximal predictors of PTG in adulthood, no study has yet explored how experiences and characteristics in adolescence may shape one’s later capacity for growth. Existing reviews consistently highlight social connectedness, mental health, and sociodemographic characteristics as correlates of PTG (Henson et al., 2021; Kinsella et al., 2015). Investigating whether these factors, when present earlier in life, predict PTG decades later offers a valuable developmental perspective – one that helps understand not only how growth emerges after trauma, but also how early life experiences may lay the foundation for resilience across the life span.

Calhoun and Tedeschi (2006), argue that individuals cope with seismic events in the context of their social relationships. Supportive relationships may facilitate the construction of effective mental frameworks, offer empathic acceptance of self-disclosure, and provide new schemata when processing a seismic event. Cross-sectional studies have indeed found positive associations between PTG and social factors such as relationship quality, close friendship, self-disclosure, and social support (Copley & Daniels, 2023; Hearn et al., 2021; Nenova et al., 2013). However, because of their cross-sectional nature, it remains unclear whether strong social relationships promote PTG or whether individuals who experience growth are more likely to build supportive relationships. Only one longitudinal study has examined pre-trauma social relationships as predictors of later PTG (Sawyer et al., 2012), finding no significant link between social support during pregnancy and PTG following childbirth. However, the study’s small sample size (N = 96) and the generally low PTG levels limited its statistical power. While social relationships in midlife may directly influence coping with major stressors, those formed in adolescence may shape later capacities for support and coping by fostering key social and emotional competencies.

Adolescent mental health may also influence an individual’s later capacity for PTG. Coping efforts are thought to be crucial to cognitively process seismic events and handle the resulting emotional distress (Calhoun & Tedeschi, 2006). Because mental health problems are often negatively associated with effective coping (e.g. Krattenmacher et al., 2013), mental health problems may hinder PTG. Cross-sectional studies have consistently shown a positive relationship between post-traumatic stress symptoms and PTG (Shakespeare-Finch & Lurie-Beck, 2014). However, findings on other forms of psychopathology, such as anxiety and depression, have been mixed, with studies reporting positive, negative or no relationships (Collicutt McGrath & Linley, 2006; Schroevers & Teo, 2008; Urcuyo et al., 2005). Moreover, the limited number of longitudinal studies have not found evidence that pre-trauma mental health problems predict later PTG (Lowe et al., 2013; Sawyer et al., 2012). Given the small number of studies, with sample sizes varying from under 100 to fewer than 400 participants, more research based on larger samples is needed to clarify the relationship between adolescent mental health and subsequent PTG.

In addition to social and psychological factors, sociodemographic characteristics may shape an individual's capacity for post-traumatic growth. Low childhood socioeconomic status (SES) is related to a higher risk of encountering adversity (Assari, 2020), which may, in turn, provide individuals with opportunities to develop coping mechanisms that foster later growth (Schaefer & Moos, 1998). At the same time, lower SES might limit access to social, emotional, and material resources to cope with seismic events, thereby reducing the likelihood of growth. Empirical studies reflect this complexity: some studies report a positive relationship between SES and PTG (Harding et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2014), others report a negative association (Koutrouli et al., 2012), and still others find no significant relationship (Brandão et al., 2020; Halldorsdottir et al., 2022). Consequently the direction and strength of the association between adolescent SES and adult PTG remains unclear.

Family structure during adolescence may also influence PTG. Adolescents not living with both biological parents may have reduced access to familial support and resources, potentially diminishing their capacity for growth later in life. However, this hypothesis has not been previously examined, and it remains unclear whether growing up outside the traditional two-parent household is linked to PTG. Immigration background might likewise shape the predisposition to experience PTG. Calhoun and Tedeschi (2006) argue that individuals from different cultural backgrounds may process and respond to seismic events differently, shaped by varying worldviews, narratives, and modes of thinking. Som studies support an association between a migration background and PTG (Helgeson et al., 2006; Maguen et al., 2006), suggesting that cultural background may play an important role in shaping responses to adversity.

The role of gender in post-traumatic growth

Research suggests that women report higher levels of PTG than men (Vishnevsky et al., 2010), and gender may also moderate associations between predictors and PTG. For instance, because women are more likely to seek and receive social support (Taylor et al., 2002), social relationships may play a stronger role in fostering PTG among women. Gender may also shape the link between mental health and PTG, as women more often engage in constructive rumination and emotion-focused coping – processes associated with growth (Swickert & Hittner, 2009; Vishnevsky et al., 2010). A meta-analysis by Prati and Pietrantoni (2009), which examined 103 studies on the relationships between social support, optimism, spirituality, coping and PTG, found that gender significantly moderated only the effect of religious coping. We will examine gender as a potential moderator of longitudinal associations with PTG, including for predictors such as social relationships and mental health.

The present study

The aim of this study is to gain novel knowledge on the prospective relationships between social, mental health, and sociodemographic factors in adolescence and adult PTG, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic in Norway. PTG was measured by a composite score of the three subscales relating to others, personal strength, and appreciation of life from a short version of the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI-SF). Based on previous research and theory, we hypothesize adolescent social relationships to be positively related to PTG in midlife. Due to conflicting results, it remains open how mental health problems and PTG are related. Similarly, prior studies provide no clear basis for predicting the relationship between SES and PTG. We further hypothesize that growing up not living together with both biological parents is related to less PTG. In contrast, we expect immigrant background to be positively related with adult PTG. Finally, it is unclear if we can expect a moderating role of gender on the predictor variables in this study.

Methods

Procedure and participants

We used data from the Young in Norway Study, a longitudinal survey conducted across five waves between 1992 and 2020. As we examined adolescent predictors of PTG during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, only data from the first data collection in 1992 and the last data collection in 2020 were used. The initial sample in 1992 consisted of students recruited from 67 representative junior and senior high schools. We collected questionnaire data in 1992 at school with a response rate of 97%. In 2020, 2,215 participants completed the electronic questionnaire, yielding an overall response rate of 52%. For more information about the study procedure, see Fluit et al. (2023). The study was approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (reference: 25462).

Measures

Post-traumatic growth

PTG was assessed in 2020 by the PTGI-SF, adapted to the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. The PTGI-SF consists of 10 items, and demonstrates good psychometric properties (Cann et al., 2010). The PTGI-SF assesses five domains: relating to others, personal strength, appreciation of life, spiritual change, and new possibilities. In the present study, six out of the 10 items in the PTGI-SF were used for the domains relating to others, personal strength and appreciation of life. The domains spiritual change and new possibilities were excluded, consistent with previously research conducted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic in Norway (Ulset & von Soest, 2022). Spiritual change was omitted due to its limited applicability in the Norwegian context, which is characterized by high levels of secularity (Lemos & Puga-Gonzalez, 2021). New possibilities was excluded because the pandemic was ongoing at the time of the assessment, likely limiting participants’ opportunities to explore and pursue new directions. Response options ranged from does not correspond (1) to corresponds to a very great degree (6). Mean scores were computed for each of the three subscales and for an overall PTG composite score. The composite scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .88). Spearman-Brown coefficients for the subscales were ρ = .81 for appreciation of life, ρ = .88 for personal strength and ρ = .73 for relating to others.

Social relationships

Parental care was measured in 1992 by a 5-item short version of the Parental Bonding Instrument (Pedersen, 1994). Respondents were asked to rate statements about their parents on a response scale ranging from very unlike (1) to very like (4), with higher scores indicating higher levels of parental care (α = .70).

Adolescent social self-concept was measured by the Close Friendship and Social Acceptance subscales from a revised version of The Self-Perception Profile for Adolescents (Wichstrøm, 1995). The Social Acceptance subscale assesses perceived peer acceptance, whereas the Close Friendship subscale measures perceptions of having close, supporting relationships. Response options ranged from corresponds very poorly (1) to corresponds very well (4). Internal consistency was α = .77 for social acceptance and α = .79 for close friendship.

Mental health problems

Symptoms of anxiety and depression in 1992 was measured using a 12-item short version of the widely used Hopkins Symptoms Checklist (Derogatis et al., 1974). Respondents were asked to report symptoms during the past week with response options ranging from not distressed (1) to extremely distressed (4). Internal consistency was α = .83.

Sociodemographic variables

We assessed whether participants in 1992 lived together with both biological parents (1) or lived in other family constellations (0). Parental SES was assessed in 1992 by categorizing the education level of the parent with the highest education into primary school or less (1), upper secondary school (2), lower university or college education (3) and higher university education (4). Immigrant status was assessed by whether participants were born in Norway (0), or in another country (1). Gender (females coded as 1), and age in 1992 were also recorded.

Analyses

Regression analyses were used to investigate the relationship between adolescent predictors and PTG in midlife. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). The dplyr package (Wickham et al., 2025) was used for data manipulation and the package psych (Revelle, 2025) was used to obtain descriptive statistics. Univariate linear regressions were first used to investigate the relationship between each of the predictors and PTG. Subsequently, all predictors were included simultaneously in multiple regression models. To examine a possible moderating effect of gender, interaction analyses were performed. A significance level of .05 was applied. Missing data were handled by using multiple imputation with the mice package, generating 10 imputed datasets.

Discussion

This study examined how psychosocial and sociodemographic factors in adolescence predicted PTG in adulthood, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Drawing on data from a large-scale, population-based longitudinal study spanning 28 years, our findings offer novel insights into long-term developmental precursors of PTG. In multivariate analyses, close friendships and parental care during adolescence emerged as consistent predictors of higher PTG nearly three decades later. Participants with an immigrant background reported greater appreciation of life in midlife, while higher parental education was associated with lower levels of personal strength. Some associations observed in univariate analyses, such as those involving adolescent mental health problems or social acceptance, did not remain significant when considered alongside other predictors. Although women reported higher levels of PTG, gender did not moderate the associations between adolescent predictors and adult PTG.

Social relationships

Perhaps most notable finding from this study is that two key indicators of social relationships in adolescence, close friendship and parental care, showed significant associations with posttraumatic growth nearly three decades later, during the COVID-19 pandemic. The lasting imprint of relational experiences highlights the central role of adolescent social environments in shaping long-term adaptive capacities. These findings align with social information processing theory (Crick & Dodge, 1994), which posits that early social experiences shape relational schemata that influence cognitions, emotions, and behaviour across the life course. Adolescents who experience warm, supportive, and trusting relationships may therefore be more likely to seek and benefit from social support in adulthood. Calhoun and Tedeschi (2006) argue that such support is an important facilitator for PTG by providing emotional reassurance and helping individuals reframe their worldviews after disruptive events.

The association between parental care in adolescence and midlife PTG is also consistent with attachment theory (Bowlby, 1979), which suggests that secure, nurturing caregiver relationships promote emotion regulation, social competence, and confidence. Such traits that may help individuals to adapt positively to major life disruptions (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970; Delgado et al., 2022). The results are also consistent with cross-sectional studies linking both close friendships (Copley & Daniels, 2023) and secure attachment styles (Gleeson et al., 2021) to higher PTG. Importantly, our study extends this literature by establishing temporal precedence, supporting that social relationships are antecedents, and not just correlates, of later PTG. This is the first robust longitudinal evidence demonstrating that supportive social relationships in adolescence predict PTG in midlife following a potentially seismic event.

Mental health

In our study, adolescent mental health problems were related to greater appreciation of life in univariate analyses; however, this relationship diminished into non-significance in multivariate models. This pattern suggests a more complex relationship between early mental health problems and PTG, with potential influences from confounding factors. For instance, gender was correlated with both mental health problems and PTG in our data and may be partially accounted for the initial univariate association.

The absence of a significant multivariate association is consistent several cross-sectional and longitudinal studies that have similarly reported no robust relationships between mental health and PTG (Barskova & Oesterreich, 2009; Long et al., 2021; Lowe et al., 2013; Sawyer et al., 2012). However, these findings are not aligned with theoretical perspectives suggesting that early psychological problems reduce the likelihood of growth. For example, Janoff-Bulman (2006) argues that individuals who experience the world as threatening and unfair, due to struggles earlier in life, may develop negative cognitive schemas that are less conductive of PTG, interpreting seismic events as a confirmation of a hostile world.

Overall, our findings support the notion that mental health problems do not play a straightforward role in predicting PTG. One possibility is that the relationship is non-linear, with PTG most likely to emerge among individuals with moderate, rather than low or high, levels of psychological functioning (Janoff-Bulman, 2006). Another possibility is that mental health problems both constrain and foster PTG: they may reduce coping capacity in midlife, while also providing prior experiences in managing adversity, potentially strengthening their ability to respond adaptively to further stressors (Schaefer & Moos, 1998). Such opposing influences may cancel each other out, masking underlying dynamics that may warrant further investigation.

Sociodemographic factors

Few of the sociodemographic variables examined showed prospective associations with PTG, a finding that was unexpected, given both theoretical arguments for their relevance and their relative stability across the life course.

Neither living arrangements nor parental education during adolescence were clearly linked to overall PTG in midlife. However, some noteworthy patterns appeared. Lower parental education was linked to higher personal strength. This finding is consistent with earlier research suggesting that facing difficulties early in life may help people develop coping skills that support growth later on (Ulset & von Soest, 2022; Wen et al., 2020). Growing up with limited resources may also cultivate a mindset of strength and perseverance that becomes particularly salient during stressful events.

Participants with an immigrant background reported higher scores on the appreciation of life subscale, although immigrant status was not related to the composite PTG score or other subscales. Previous studies on ethnic minority status and PTG have produced mixed results (Helgeson et al., 2006; Maguen et al., 2006). Our findings suggest that immigrant status may relate specifically to certain facets of growth, such as appreciating life, potentially because individuals with immigrant backgrounds may draw on cultural values, prior challenges, or alternative frames of reference that foster gratitude or heightened appreciation of life in the face of adversity. The low proportion of participants with immigrant background (2.74%) may also have reduced the statistical power to detect broader associations.

Finally, being female was associated with higher PTG across the composite score and all subscales. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that women tend to report more PTG than men (Vishnevsky et al., 2010) and with theories suggesting that women may experience stronger emotional reactions and perceive seismic events as more stressful (Olff et al., 2007). However, we found no evidence that gender moderated the associations between predictor variables and PTG, suggesting that although women report higher levels of growth, the developmental pathways leading to PTG may be similar across genders.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its use of a 28-year longitudinal dataset, which allowed to examine adolescent factors predicting PTG in midlife. The sample is broadly representative of the general Norwegian population, increasing the generalizability of the findings and enabling stronger inferences about the directionality of associations.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, PTG was measured using the short version of the Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory, consisting of only two items per subscale. This likely reduced the reliability of the subscales (Cann et al., 2010), even though the composite score had acceptable internal consistency in our study. Furthermore, only three of the five subscales of PTG were included. Consequently, the composite score represented only selected aspects of growth rather than the full construct. For example. spiritual change might have provided additional information related to existential or meaning-making processes, while new possibilities may have captured aspects of adaptive reorientation and life change. Including these domains could therefore have altered both the overall level of PTG and the magnitude or pattern of associations observed, particularly for predictors related to coping, life transitions, or broader worldview changes. Future studies should investigate whether the inclusion of all PTG domains yields different estimates of PTG and modifies the associations between early-life factors and later growth.

Second, floor effects may have been present, as indicated by mean PTG scores around 3 for both the composite measure and the subscales on a scale ranging from 1 to 6. This suggests generally low levels of reported growth, with a substantial proportion of participants clustered toward the lower and of the scale. One possible explanation is that many participants may not have experienced the COVID-19 pandemic as a sufficiently severe or personally salient crisis to elicit PTG. The restricted variability may, in turn, have attenuated associations with predictors and reduced statistical power, potentially leading to an underestimation of effects.

Third, the study experienced notable attrition over time. Previous studies using the Young in Norway dataset have found that characteristics such as male gender and parental immigrant background are related to higher dropout rates (Rognli et al., 2022). As such, the final sample may differ systematically from those who left the study, potentially limiting the generalizability the results.

Finally, although several prospective associations were statistically significant, effect sizes were small. This might limit the practical significance of the findings. However, given the 28-year follow-up period, small sized associations are not unexpected. Individuals are influenced by numerous intervening life experiences over such a long timeframe, and even modest associations suggest a degree of stability in associations of adolescent predictors over time.

Conclusion and implications for future research

Despite its limitations, the present study offers important insight into long-term predispositions for PTG. Most notably, social relationships in adolescence, specifically close friendship and caring parents, were related to higher levels of PTG in midlife during the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, immigrant background predicted greater appreciation of life, while low parental education was associated with greater personal strength. By using a long-term longitudinal design, this study advances previous research by providing evidence for the directionality of these associations, rather than relying solely on cross-sectional findings.

Understanding the developmental origins of PTG can inform interventions aimed at strengthening protective factors early in life. Supporting adolescents in building strong social relationships may enhance their capacity for positive adaptation when facing future adversity. The findings highlight the need for further longitudinal research on predispositions for PTG. Future studies should investigate the possibility for non-linear relationship between pre-trauma mental health and PTG and examine whether these patterns hold in in different cultural contexts and in response to types of traumatic events other than the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, research investigating the causal mechanisms linking early-life factors to later PTG would provide valuable insight into the developmental foundations of growth and how resilience processes that promote PTG can best be supported across the life course.
