Understanding the determinants influencing snake meat consumption intention in Ghana using the extended theory of planned behavior
Authors: Maxwell Kwame Boakye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman consumption of snakes is listed as one of the main threats to their conservation status. Consumer behavior change interventions are identified as the most effective means to conserve species threatened by human consumption. However, knowing which behavioral drivers of consumption intention to focus on is necessary to guide change interventions effectively. This study determined the factors influencing behavioral intentions to consume the Vulnerable (VU) snake species Bitis gabonica in Ghana. Data were collected from 296 households in the Avatime traditional area in October and November 2024 using in-person interviews with a structured questionnaire. Analysis was done using the partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) technique in SmartPLS 3.2.8. The findings revealed that the paths between attitude, perceived behavioral control, and social norm towards consumption intention of Bitis gabonica were positive and significant. Attitude was the most important predictor of Bitis gabonica consumption, followed by perceived behavioral control and subjective norm. The study recommends behavior change campaigns that emphasize changes in attitude toward human consumption of Bitis gabonica. Persuasive messages aimed at changing beliefs and emotions towards snakes should be the primary focus of attitudinal change efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23464-3.Keywords: Attitude, Behavioral, Conservation, Gaboon viper, Ophidiophobia, Wildlife value, Mutualistic orientationSubject terms: Biodiversity, Conservation biology, Ecosystem services

Introduction

Snakes are widely consumed as a source of animal protein in various cultures worldwide1–5. In many sub-Saharan African countries, snake meat has been a staple source of protein and consumed as a traditional delicacy1,4,6,7. Bitis spp. is one species of snake that is consumed for dietary purposes in sub-Sahara Africa5,6,8 and is among reptiles eaten in Ghana9. Gaboon viper (Bitis gabonica) has been recorded to be sold in bushmeat markets for dietary purposes in several countries, including DR Congo7, Cameroon10, Nigeria11,12, and the Republic of Guinea13. The Gaboon viper is listed as Vulnerable (VU) in the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species14, and dietary consumption has been identified as one of the main threats to the species conservation15.

Human behavior change is identified as key to attenuating global biodiversity loss, as human actions are driving the extinction crisis16–18. Exploitation for consumption is identified as one human action that affects the status and threat of wildlife species16,18. Consumer behavior change has been suggested as the most effective means for conservation interventions aimed at reducing bushmeat consumption16. Previous studies have shown that the behavioral aspects that impact the intention to consume bushmeat presents significant potential for interventions aimed at conservating wildlife species19–23. However, studies on snake consumption have generally focused on the cultural aspect, with limited attention to the behavioral determinants influencing consumption1–5.

Snake meat is consumed for dietary purposes in Ghana24–26 and the cultural specificity of meat socialization is identified to influencing the consumption of snake meat, with different cultures socialized into liking or disliking its consumption25. The Avatime people of the Volta Region in Ghana are noted for their consumption of B. gabonica27. The people’s culture has been socialized to accept the consumption of the Gaboon viper meat. Although the cultural specificity and acceptance for the consumption of the Gaboon viper among the Avatime people are well-known, the behavioral determinants underlying its consumption have yet to be determined. Given the Vulnerable (VU) conservation status of B. gabonica, there is a need to understand the determinants fueling consumption intentions to develop conservation management strategies that address conservation needs resulting from human exploitation for bushmeat purposes. When a person’s values, attitudes, and social norms towards a behavior intention are known, conservation strategies and messages are better tailored to promote actions that preserve biodiversity16,17. This study aims to find the behavioral determinants associated with the consumption intention of Gaboon viper meat using the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), along with the extended construct of Wildlife Value Orientations (WVOs), and Perceived Susceptibility (PS) to zoonotic diseases resulting from consuming this meat.

Theoretical underpinning of study

Theory of planed behavior (TPB)

A social-psychological model that has been the most influential for explaining human behavior is the TPB28. According to TPB, an individual’s intentions to perform the behavior are the most powerful determinant of the actual behavior29. Behavioral intention is a function of the following three factors: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control toward the behavior30,31. Attitudes toward the behavior deal with the positive or negative evaluations about performing the target behavior, (2) subjective norms in the context of a given behavior is the influence of the opinions of others, such as family and friends into performing the target behavior, and (3) perceived behavioral control which to perceptions of ease or difficulty of performing the target behavior30,31.

The TPB has been used to identify antecedents of meat consumption behavior and design effective interventions32–35 and has demonstrated its capacity to predict bushmeat consumption behavior20,22,23,36. The TPB was therefore selected as the theoretical framework for this study as it has demonstrated its capability in meat consumption studies. The study hypothesized as follows:

H1

Attitude positively influences consumption intention towards the Gaboon viper.

H2

Subjective norm positively influences consumption intention towards the Gaboon viper.

H3

Perceived behavioral control (ease of access) positively influences consumption intention towards the Gaboon viper.

Ajzen29 stated that the addition of other predictors besides those in the TPB framework is allowed if they enhance the predictive power of the TPB model. Various metal-analytical studies have established the usefulness of additional variables in enhancing the predictive power of intention in the TPB framework28,37,38. Bushmeat consumption studies have incorporated additional constructs of the Norm Activation Model (NAM)20, attitudinal characteristics and perceived susceptibility22, food neophobia/neophiliac23, and experience36 into the original TPB framework to increase the model’s predictive potential. Wildlife Value Orientations (WVOs) and Perceived Susceptibility (PS) were included in this study model due to their influence on intention towards bushmeat consumption.

Wildlife value orientations (WVOs)

The Wildlife Value Orientations (WVOs) are individual’s conceptualization of human-wildlife interactions39. They are important for predicting the behavioral intention underlying human wildlife relationships in conservation management decisions39–41. WVO has been utilized in behavioral intention studies to understand beliefs about wildmeat consumption42–45. However, WVO application in the TPB framework is limited, although both can assist in predicting behavior intentions.

WVOs affect relationships with wildlife, and two main classes of domination and mutualism are identified39,46,47. The domination orientation advocates for a clear separation of groups (i.e., animals from humans), the maintenance of beliefs that reinforce justification for cultural practices, the subordination of the role of animals to that of humans46. Domination-oriented values emphasize the rational exploitation of wildlife. The mutualism orientation emphasizes on wildlife as an integral part of the social community and as life forms having similar rights to humans, worthy of care and compassion, capable of forming a trusting relationship with humans39,46,47. Mutualism-oriented values view wildlife in human terms and rationalize welfare-enhancing behaviors as opposed to actions resulting in death or harm to wildlife.

H4

Domination WVO (animal use beliefs) will positively influence consumption intention towards the Gaboon viper.

H5

Mutualism WVO (social affiliation beliefs) will negatively influence consumption intention towards the Gaboon viper.

Perceived susceptibility (PS)

Perceived susceptibility (PS) is the likelihood that a person believes they are vulnerable to contracting sickness from an undertaking or behaviour48,49. Snakes are intermediate hosts to most diseases-causing pathogens, and their consumption may serve as a driver for the transmission of foodborne zoonoses, leading to public health risk50–52. In recent times, the COVID-19 outbreak has highlighted the increased risk of zoonotic diseases associated with bushmeat consumption53–55. In Ghana, Abukari and Kankam19 identified that the perceived risk of zoonosis influences the consumption of bushmeat. Weariness about zoonosis is predicted to influence the consumption of the Gaboon viper. Therefore, the study hypothesized that:

H6

Perceived susceptibility will negatively influence the consumption intention of the Gaboon viper.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Avatimes people with a human population of about 35, 00056 live in the western portion of the Volta Region, mainly in the villages and towns of Amedzofe, Vane, Gbadzeme, Dzokpe, Biakpe, Dzogbefeme, and Fume57,58. The study area is dominated by two main vegetation types: the moist semideciduous forest vegetation that dominates in the mountains, and the savannah woodland areas in the valley59. Farming is the main occupation in the study area, and crops produced include cassava, yams, beans, cocoyams, rice, and plantain56–59. Tea, coffee, and cocoa are cultivated as cash crops in the area56,57. For the Avatime, maintaining traditions, customs, and practices is a safeguard for their ethnic identity60. The consumption of the Gaboon viper, known locally in the local language as Ligalε, is one such unique traditional practice that has been maintained in the Avatime area27. It was expected that people in the study area would be ideal for determining the factors underlying the consumption of the Gaboon viper.

Determination of sample size

Within the seven towns and villages in the Avatime area, 35 individuals were conveniently sampled to determine their knowledge about Gaboon viper consumption. An interview with the sampled population revealed that 30 individuals (p = 0.86) were aware of the consumption of the Gaboon viper (Ligalε). Sampling procedure and data collection

The study population consisted of households, with each household serving as the unit of analysis. Similar to previous bushmeat studies by Abukari and Kankam19, this study used the Expanded Program of Immunization (EPI) sampling method63. With the EPI approach, the location middle of the selected study community was identified, and through the spinning of a bottle or pen, a random direction was determined. A household was chosen as the starting point, from the center to the edge of the community along the randomly chosen direction, using either a spined bottle or pen. Within the chosen households, individuals who had confirmed the consumption of Gaboon viper were sought and interviewed. Neighboring households were chosen if no suitable respondents were found in previous households who could provide the answers for the study. This process was repeated until the required number of respondents was achieved for the study.

Data on the determinants influencing Gaboon viper consumption intention were collected from respondents between October and November 2024, using structured questionnaires and in-person interviews. The study assistants who handed out the interview questions helped participants complete the questionnaire by explaining the measurement items to them. Ewe is the predominant language in the area, and Avatime speakers are also proficient in it58. Every research assistant involved in this study was proficient in both Ewe and English language. The research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The ethics committee of the Ho Technical University approved the study (HTU/DRI/EC2025-004). An informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Instruments used for data collection

The questions used to measure the latent variables were selected after a meticulous review of the relevant literature on meat consumption behavior intentions. The TPB questions were largely based on related studies on bushmeat consumption20,22,23,32,33,53. The Wildlife Value Orientation (WVO) questions were adopted from Ehrhart et al.40, and Keener-Eck et al.41. The perceived susceptibility instruments were adapted from Abukari and Kankam19. The interview questions provided in the Supplementary Information file were structured into two sections. The first section dealt with demographic questions relating to gender, age, educational qualifications, monthly household income, and marital status. The second section of the questionnaire dealt with the components of TPB (attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control) and its extended constructs of WVO (domination and mutualism), and perceived susceptibility (supplementary material). A five-point Likert scale was used to rank respondents’ degree of agreement with measuring items in each of the constructs. The statements were anchored on either side with strongly agree (5) to strongly disagree (1). The study’s selected questionnaire was piloted with 20 respondents to assess its applicability regarding ease of understanding, clarity, and timing for individual interviews. The consistency and reliability test were performed for the piloted study, and all the constructs had appropriate Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70.

Data analysis

The characteristics of respondents were analyzed using SPSS version 29. The proposed relationships between the theoretical constructs were assessed through the partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) technique. The PLS algorithm was used to evaluate the outer model fitness, including convergent reliability and validity, discriminant validity, as well as the inner model fitness, including model’s fit indices, the coefficient of determination (R2), predictive relevance (Q2), and the hypothesis testing (path coefficient)64,65. Path significance was estimated via the bootstrapping method with 5000 resamples at a 5% significance level. Data analysis was performed using SmartPLS 3.2.8.

Results

Demographics, descriptive, and inferential statistics

The majority of the 296 participants were males (n = 177; 59.80%) and females (n = 119; 40.20%). The age group of participants revealed that most were between the ages of 31–40 (n = 81; 27.36%), followed by ages 21–30 (n = 69; 23.31%), ages 41–50 (n = 50; 16.89%), ages 18–20 and ages 51–60 (n = 41; 13.85%; each), ages and those above 60 years (n = 14; 4.73%). The level of education of the participants revealed that most had attained Senior High School/Vocational School (n = 139; 46.96%), followed by tertiary education (n = 70; 23.65%), Junior High School level (n = 35; 11.82%), Middle School Leaving Certificate (n = 34; 11.49%), basic/primary (n = 16; 5.41%), and below primary/no formal education (n = 2; 0.68%). The self-reported average monthly income revealed that most of the participants earned between 500 and 999 Ghana Cedis as monthly income (n = 151; 51.01%), followed by less than 500 Ghana Cedis (n = 101; 34.12%), 1000–1499 Ghana Cedis (n = 42; 14.19%), between 1500 and 2000 and above 2000 Ghana cedis (n = 1; 0.34%; each), and. Based on marital status, the majority of participants were single (n = 158; 53.38%), followed by married (n = 116; 39.19%), widowed (n = 17; 5.74%), and divorced (n = 5; 1.69%).

Also, Hair et al.67 suggested removing indicators with loadings below 0.7 if their removal leads to an increase in composite reliability. The removal of SAB2 improved the Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) of the social affiliation beliefs (SAB) construct. Measurement model

The standardized root mean residual (SRMR) values of 0.072 and 0.064 for the original and extended TPB frameworks were within the recommended range of 0.01 to 0.08, indicating a good fit64. The model structure analyses indicate a Normed Fit Index (NFI) of 0.827 and 0.811 for the original and extended TPB frameworks were within the acceptable ranges of between 0 and 1 but below the recommended ideal value of at least 0.966. Regarding the model’s explanatory power, R2 values of 0.438 and 0.509 indicate that 43.8% and 50.9% of the variance in the dependent variable is explained by the independent variables in the original and extended TPB framework models, respectively. The model predictive relevance (Stone–Geisser criterion, Q2) values of 0.348 and 0.403 indicate that the model can predict the endogenous latent components with an accuracy of 40.3% for the extended TPB and 34.8% for the original TPB framework.

Structural models

Table 3 presents the results of the structural model used to test the study hypotheses for both the original and extended TPB frameworks. The results revealed that attitude (β = 0.491, t = 11.284, p < 0.001), perceived behavioral control (β = 0.216, t = 4.476, p < 0.001) and subjective norms (β = 0.111, t = 2.503, p < 0.012) all had a positive significant relationship with consumption intention. H1, H2, and H3 were therefore confirmed for the original TPB framework. The path between attitude, perceived behavioral control, and social norm towards consumption intention Gaboon viper for the extended TPB framework were positive and significant (β = 0.445, t = 8.302, p < 0.001), (β = 0.164, t = 3.705, p < 0.001), and (β = 0.114, t = 2.472, p < 0.001), respectively. Therefore, H1, H2, and H3 were confirmed for the extended TPB framework. The relation between consumption intention of Gaboon viper and animal use beliefs (β = − 0.238, t = 4.615, p < 0.001), and social affiliation beliefs (β = 0.204, t = 3.569, p < 0.001), were significant. The study hypothesis proposed a positive and negative path relationship between consumption intention and animal use beliefs, as well as social affiliation beliefs, respectively. Therefore, H4 and H5 were not confirmed as the paths were negative for animal use beliefs and positive for social affiliation beliefs, contrary to the hypothesized paths. The intention to consume the Gaboon viper and perceived susceptibility path was not significant (β = 0.010, t = 0.181, p < 0.856); therefore H6 is not confirmed.

Discussion

Bushmeat consumption remains a significant threat to biodiversity conservation of vulnerable species such as B. gabonica. Behavioral change interventions are identified as a means through which tailored strategies for effective biodiversity conservation can be accomplished16,17. However, knowing which behavioral drivers of consumption intention to focus on is crucial to guide effective change interventions. This study determined the behavioral intentions influencing the consumption of the Gaboon viper for bushmeat purposes. The study’s findings revealed that attitude was the most important predictor of Gaboon viper consumption, followed by perceived behavioral control, and subjective norm. The original TPB and extended TPB model’s explanatory power of 43.8% and 50.9% respectively, were above the 39% suggested by Armitage and Conner28 to account for the variance in intention to act using the TPB framework. The study’s applied model, therefore, satisfactorily explains the intention to consume the Gaboon viper meat. The predictive advantage of the extended model was supported by findings from metal-analytical studies28,37,38.

Attitude as the main determinant of bushmeat consumption is well-established in literature21,70–72 and is consistent with the findings of this study. Attitudinal motives, such as taste, healthiness, and cultural acceptability, are the primary drivers of behavioral intention towards bushmeat consumption70–75. A positive attitude dimension is a better predictor of behavioral intention76. According to Glasman and Albarracín77, the attitudes people hold with confidence and emanating from direct experience are better predictors of behavior than their doubtful ones, and those from indirect experience. The favorable attitude towards the taste, healthy nature, cultural acceptability, and safety attributes of Gaboon viper bushmeat may have influenced the attitude construct as the strongest predictor of behavior intention for consumption in this study.

Previous studies have shown that highly accessible attitudes are stronger predictors of behavioral intention76,78,79. Accessible attitudes are therefore more likely to be acted upon. Perceived behavioral control, measured as ease of access in this study, was the second most significant predictor of intention based on path relationships, an indication that access to the Gaboon viper was not difficult. In Ghana, encounters with snakes are highest among people who engage in farming activities80,81. The studied communities were predominantly farmers56,82, and their day-to-day activities make them more prone to encountering snakes. With-low level mechanized agricultural practices, exposure of farmers to snakes concealed in vegetation, soil and crops are high in Ghana81. Accessibility through frequent encounters with snakes may have influenced attitude as the strongest predictor of behavior intention in this study.

The consumption of snake meat varies across different cultures, with some socialized into liking it, whiles others disliking it6,7,25. In Ghana, the consumption of snake meat is influenced by meat socialisation25. When the consumption of meat is a socially accepted and expected behavior, there is a positive subjective norm towards the intention to perform such behavior83–85. The measuring items for subjective norms all had high factor loadings, indicating high approval within the community. The positive significant path association between subjective norm and intention in this study can be attributed to the approval of Gaboon viper meat consumption in the studied communities.

Value orientation and emotions vary based on a specific wildlife species43. Value orientations associated with fear and anger may result in reduced sensitivity to biocentric values43. Wildlife species that elucidate fear and anger usually attract approval for persecution43–45. Globally, snakes tend to generate a feeling of fear, disgust, or hatred86–88. In Ghana, there is a high prevalence of ophidiophobia, and snakes are generally persecuted among local people25,81,89,90. Generally, people are wary of snakes and believe killing them is acceptable. The strong negative feeling towards snakes might have led to reduced mutualistic-oriented value towards their killing and consumption for bushmeat purposes. This may have resulted in a positive instead of a negative path of association between the path of social affiliation beliefs and consumption intentions. The negative association between domination-oriented value and consumption intention can be attributed to the fear of snakes. The irrational degree of anxiety in seeing a snake far exceeds the interest in hunting them, which may have resulted in the negative rather than the expected positive path for animal use beliefs and intention to consume the Gaboon viper.

The risk of zoonotic diseases from consuming of bushmeat is insignificant in influencing the intention of consumers due to their reduced weariness22,53,54,73. The perception of risk from consuming snakes was found to be low among wild animals consumed for bushmeat purposes in Kenya and Tanzania91. Similarly, risk perception regarding zoonotic infections from consuming reptiles, including snakes, was observed to be low in Nigeria55. Reduced wariness of contraction of zoonotic diseases may have accounted for the lack of significance in the relationship between perceived susceptibility and intention to consume Gaboon viper meat in this study. The findings of this study contradicted a previous study in Ghana by Abukari and Kankam19 which found zoonotic awareness reduced bushmeat consumption. Abukari and Kankam19 study was conducted against the backdrop of public health campaigns on COVID-19 and acknowledge that reduced bushmeat consumption was not limited to concerns about contracting zoonotic diseases.

Conclusion

This study assessed the determinants of the consumption intention of the Gaboon viper in Ghana using the TPB, WVOs, and perceived susceptibility to zoonotic risk. Among the factors examined, attitude was found to be the strongest determinant of intention, followed by perceived behavioral control, and subjective norm for the hypothesized association paths with intention. Perceived susceptibility to zoonotic ailments had no significant influence on the consumption intention of the Gaboon viper. Behavior change campaigns aimed at reducing human consumption of the Gaboon viper should emphasize a change in attitude towards consumption. Attitudinal change in this case requires changing beliefs and emotions towards snakes. This will require the use of persuasive massages that emphasizes on ecological importance of snakes to convince people to become mutualistic oriented towards snakes.

The study had limitations worth mentioning. This study only assessed the consumption intention of the Gaboon viper; hence, the results cannot be generalized to all snakes consumed. The study was cross-sectional in design and does not establish causation. Future studies should use a longitudinal approach to assess the pattern of change and the extent to which behavior intentions alter, particularly following attitudinal change campaigns. Although this study used the extended TPB farmwork, which enhanced the predictive power of the model, future studies, should include emotional disposition as a possible mediator or moderator given its role in the lethal management of wildlife.
