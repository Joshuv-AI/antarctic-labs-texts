When bone bridges become barriers—a case of DISH-related dysphagia
Authors: Diomidis C Ioannidis, Eleana Ntatsaki
Published: 2025

Editorial referring to case: Progressive dysphagia secondary to cervical osteophytosis due to Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH) by Artem Kuptsov, Inge J M H Caelers, Andreas K Demetriades.

William Mayo.

Although new-onset dysphagia typically raises concern for an underlying neoplastic process, a number of rarer benign and potentially reversible aetiologies must also be considered. Uncommon causes of extrinsic oesophageal compression include massive renal hydronephrosis, marked enlargement of the left atrium or a tortuous aorta [1–3]. Accurate identification of the underlying pathology is essential, as recognition of these reversible conditions may permit targeted interventions that can alleviate symptoms.

In this issue of the Journal, the case of a 58-year-old male with a three-year history of severe, progressive dysphagia is presented [4]. The swallowing impairment was of sufficient severity to result in clinically significant weight loss. Diagnostic evaluation revealed a 9 mm bony outgrowth on the anterolateral aspect of the cervical spine spanning levels C3–C6, which was exerting compressive effects on the posterior oesophageal wall.

The patient was diagnosed with Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH), a systemic rheumatological disorder characterised by abnormal new bone formation. DISH most frequently involves the anterolateral aspects of the spinal entheses, although peripheral joints may also be affected, and the condition demonstrates a higher prevalence among males [5]. As noted by the authors, the majority of cases remain asymptomatic; however, in certain instances, the development of large osteophytes can result in clinically significant complications, including oesophageal compression leading to dysphagia, tracheal compression causing dyspnoea, and, in rare cases, airway obstruction.

Although initial management of DISH-related osteophytes is typically conservative, interventional approaches should be considered especially when the osteophytes reach a size sufficient to produce significant symptoms [6]. In the case described, following radiological confirmation of the osteophyte and exclusion of alternative diagnoses such as ankylosing spondylitis, Kuptsov et al. instituted conservative therapy. This consisted of dietary modification to a soft and blended diet with adequate fluid intake over a six-month period, in conjunction with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and proton pump inhibitors. However, these measures failed to alleviate the patient’s dysphagia.

The neurosurgical team proceeded with an anterolateral cervical spine approach, performing drilling of the anterior osteophyte at the C2–C3 disc level. The patient experienced complete resolution of dysphagia within one month. Surgical interventions have demonstrated similarly favourable short-term outcomes; however, recurrence of osteophyte formation and consequent external oesophageal compression has been reported, even up to a decade postoperatively [7].

An important question arises as to whether DISH can be prevented. While advancing age and male sex are well-established non-modifiable risk factors, several potentially reversible associations have been identified, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and metabolic syndrome [8, 9]. Accordingly, strategies aimed at mitigating these metabolic risk factors may be of value. Weight reduction, optimisation of glycaemic and lipid control, and the management of metabolic syndrome, complemented by regular physical activity to preserve spinal and joint mobility as well as to improve overall metabolic health, could play a role in reducing the likelihood or severity of DISH.

Although in medicine we often strive to avoid or delay surgical intervention in favour of less invasive therapies, including targeted pharmacotherapy, physiotherapy and lifestyle adjustments, it needs to be recognised that there are cases where a mechanical modification of the pathological anatomy is necessary. In such situations, a timely surgical intervention may make all the difference in the patient’s care.

The authors should be commended for managing the patient surgically following a period of conservative treatment, highlighting the observation of William Mayo (Fig. 1), one of the founders of the Mayo Clinic: ‘a chance to cut is a chance to cure’.
