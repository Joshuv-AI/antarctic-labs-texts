Impact of CCR5Δ32 on the risk of infection, Staphylococcus aureus carriage, and plasma concentrations of chemokines in Danish blood donors
Authors: Khoa Manh Dinh, Kathrine Agergård Kaspersen, Susan Mikkelsen, Bertram Dalskov Kjerulff, Jens Kjærgaard Boldsen, Mikkel Steen Petersen, Kristoffer Sølvsten Burgdorf, Erik Sørensen, Bitten Aagaard, Barbara Forman-Ankjær, Mie Topholm Bruun, Karina Banasik, Thomas Folkmann Hansen, Mette Nyegaard, Palle Duun Rohde, Søren Brunak, Henrik Hjalgrim, Sisse Rye Ostrowski, Ole Birger Pedersen, Henrik Ullum, Lise Tornvig Erikstrup, Christian Erikstrup
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe CC chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) is a suggested receptor for Staphylococcus aureus leukotoxin ED. Homozygosity for the Δ32 deletion (CCR5Δ32) protects against human immunodeficiency virus infection and possibly also against leukotoxin ED. We examined the impact of CCR5Δ32 on the susceptibility to S. aureus infection, all-cause infections, and S. aureus nasal carriage, respectively, and on the concentrations of circulating chemokines in blood donors.MethodsWe included 95,406 participants from the Danish Blood Donor Study (DBDS) genotyped for >650,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms. The CCR5Δ32 (rs333, MAF: 0.12) was imputed from a reference panel and validated. Infectious outcomes were identified by diagnosis codes and redeemed prescription of antibiotics in national health registers. Data on S. aureus nasal carriage and forty-seven inflammatory biomarkers were available for 6721 and 7811 participants, respectively. Cox, logistic, and linear regression models adjusted for relevant confounders were used to explore said associations.FindingsDuring more than 700,000 person-years of observation, we found that CCR5Δ32 was associated with neither an increased risk of redeemed dicloxacillin, hospital-treated S. aureus-associated infection (replicated in 345,996 Icelanders), redeemed antibiotics, all-cause infection, and nor with S. aureus nasal carriage. We discovered an association between CCR5Δ32 and elevated CCL4 concentrations, which were 1.26-fold higher in Δ32-heterozygotes (95%-CI: 1.23–1.30) and 2.64-fold higher in Δ32-homozygotes (95%-CI: 2.41–2.90) compared with wildtype homozygotes. Conversely, concentrations of CCL2, CXCL-10, and CCL11 were slightly lower among Δ32-heterozygotes.InterpretationResults from this CCR5Δ32 high-prevalent cohort do not support the idea that CCR5Δ32 affects the risk of S. aureus carriage or infection to any relevant degree, in this northern European context. CCL4 was the main chemokine affected by CCR5Δ32 and was observed in higher concentration among Δ32-carriers. This study cannot rule out that S. aureus is a previous driver of CCR5Δ32 selection.FundingThe Health Research Fund of Central Denmark Region, Aarhus University, Danish Administrative Regions, Bio- and Genome Bank Denmark, Danish Blood Donor Research Foundation, Aase & Ejnar Danielsens Foundation, Højmosegård Grant, National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, and A.P. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for published research articles using following search strategy without date or language restrictions: ((“Receptors, CCR5” [Mesh] OR (CCR5 delta 32) OR (CCR5 Δ32)) AND (“Staphylococcus aureus” [Mesh] OR (Staphylococcus aureus infection)). Our search identified 13 papers describing the link between the CCR5 receptor and S. aureus leukotoxins, including leukotoxin ED, in mice and human cells as well as future aspects of using CCR5 antagonists as S. aureus therapeutics. We then filtered for studies in humans and searched: ((“Receptors, CCR5” [Mesh] OR (CCR5 delta 32) OR (CCR5 Δ32)) AND (plasma chemokine concentrations. We found three relevant papers, however only in patients with HIV. The search for: ((“Receptors, CCR5” [Mesh] OR (CCR5 delta 32) OR (CCR5 Δ32)) AND (Staphylococcus aureus nasal colonisation/carriage) OR (infection all-cause) did not result in any papers. It is still unclear why the allele frequency of the CCR5Δ32 polymorphism is highest among individuals with North European ancestry and the frequency decreases from north to south. S. aureus was proposed as the driver of this selection, based on the finding that CCR5Δ32 blocks Leukotoxin ED produced by S. aureus. Thus, population-based studies investigating the role of CCR5 in S. aureus nasal colonisation, the risk of infection, and the impact of CCR5Δ32 on plasma concentrations of chemokines in otherwise healthy individuals are needed.

Added value of this study

We show no clinically relevant effect of the CCR5Δ32 on the risk of hospital-treated S. aureus-associated infection or all-cause infection. Similar finding was replicated among 345,996 Icelanders. Also, CCR5Δ32 was associated with neither redeemed antibiotics used for the treatment of S. aureus infection nor with S. aureus nasal carriage. Notably, we discovered an association between CCR5Δ32 and elevated CCL4 concentrations whereas the concentrations of CCL2, CXCL-10, and CCL11 were lowered among Δ32-heterozygotes.

Implications of all the available evidence

The CCR5Δ32 reduces susceptibility to HIV infection, but it is a risk factor for symptomatic West Nile virus infection and correlates with disease severity after tick-borne encephalitis virus infection. This large population-based study of a CCR5Δ32 high-prevalent cohort contributes to clarifying a possible protective effect of the Δ32-allele on the risk of S. aureus nasal carriage and infection that has arisen from previous studies. Our results do not support this association to any relevant degree in this northern European context. However, we cannot rule out S. aureus as a previous driver of CCR5Δ32 selection. Additional work is needed to address the impact of chemokine concentrations among Δ32-carriers in relation to infection risk and progression as well as large-scale population-based studies identifying genes and variants associated with S. aureus persistent carriage and invasive S. aureus infection are warranted.

Introduction

The CC chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) is expressed on the cell surface of a wide range of cells, including T lymphocytes, macrophages, endothelial cells, vascular smooth-muscle cells, immature dendritic cells, and fibroblasts.1 CCR5 is exploited by certain pathogens: for example, HIV-1 uses CCR5 as a co-receptor to enter immune cells and establish infection. A 32-base-pair deletion (CCR5Δ32) results in a truncated CCR5 protein that does not reach the cell surface; indeed, this deletion was originally identified because homozygous individuals are protected against HIV infection.2,3 CRISPR technology was recently used for controversial embryonal gene editing of CCR5 in two Chinese babies, in an attempt to confer resistance to HIV infection by CCR5Δ32 homozygosity.4 The allele frequency of the CCR5Δ32 polymorphism is highest among individuals with North European ancestry. In European populations, the frequency of the deletion decreases from north to south and is rare in African and Asian populations.5 The high prevalence in northern countries suggests positive selection of CCR5Δ32, possibly driven by the bubonic plague6 or by the smallpox epidemics.7 Other studies, however, did not support this hypothesis.8

Staphylococcus aureus has also been proposed as a the driver of this selection, based on the finding that CCR5Δ32 renders resistance against leukotoxin ED (LukED), produced by S. aureus.9 S. aureus is a commensal that colonizes the skin and mucous membrane in humans, mainly in the anterior nares.10 S. aureus nasal carriage prevalence of 30–41% is found in population-based studies.11, 12, 13 Although colonisation itself is harmless, S. aureus is a leading nosocomial pathogen that can cause a broad range of infectious diseases ranging from simple skin and soft-tissue infections (SSTI) to life-threatening, deep abscesses, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, endocarditis, and bacteremia.14 S. aureus accounts for 21% of all bacteremias in 2022 in Denmark; a condition with high morbidity and mortality.15,16 There has been an effort to fight antibiotic-resistant S. aureus, but despite a significant decrease in the methicillin-resistant S. aureus percentage in blood stream infections in Europe, the health burden of methicillin-susceptible S. aureus is increasing, highlighting the importance of monitoring antibiotic resistance trends as well as the incidence of infections.17 It remains to be elucidated whether CCR5Δ32 affects the risk of S. aureus infection, all-cause infection risk, and the ability of S. aureus to colonize the nostrils.

CCR5 belongs to a family of chemokine receptors that, upon activation by their preferential ligands (CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5), direct leukocytes towards specific sites of inflammation to combat infection. Like cytokines and growth factors, chemokines are relevant as phenotypical markers in a wide range of diseases.18 While an ex vivo stimulation of whole blood from blood donors found no altered production of CCR5 ligands,19 a genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified several genetic variants strongly associated with CCL4 concentration. The variants include rs113010081 and rs113341849 in the CCRL2 gene on chromosome 3 and they are in pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) with CCR5Δ32 (rs333) located approximately 42 kb downstream and 30 kb upstream apart, respectively.20 However, the impact of CCR5Δ32 on circulating chemokine concentrations is unknown.

Our aim was to examine the impact of CCR5Δ32 on susceptibility to S. aureus infection and S. aureus nasal carriage; and whether the deletion affects the concentrations of circulating chemokines in a large population-based cohort of Danish blood donors. Based on the observation that CCR5Δ32 blocks LukED from S. aureus, we hypothesized that CCR5Δ32 lowers the risk of S. aureus-associated infection and nasal carriage. Also, we expected higher concentrations of CCL4 based on the recent GWAS finding. Finally, we tested whether CCR5Δ32 is associated with an increased risk of all-cause infection.

Methods

Study participants

The Danish Blood Donor Study (DBDS) is an ongoing, nationwide, large-scale public-health study and biobank initiated March 2010. Blood donors aged 18–70 years from all blood centres in Denmark were invited to participate during a blood donation. At inclusion, participants completed a comprehensive questionnaire (including smoking behaviour and anthropometric measures) and whole blood and a plasma sample were stored in a biobank. Further detailed description of DBDS has been previously published.21 In 2014, we began collecting nasal swabs among DBDS participants for a sub-study called the Danish Blood Donor Staphylococcus aureus Carriage Study (DBDSaCS).13 Repeat donors were invited for a second nasal swab. To date, the DBDS comprises more than 170,000 participants and the DBDSaCS more than 9000 participants. For the current study, participants from the DBDS and DBDSaCS cohorts were recruited from March 2010 to July 2021.

Genotyping and imputation

DNA was purified from whole blood samples drawn at DBDS enrolment and stored at −20 °C. Genotyping of Danish samples was performed in two batches at deCODE genetics (Reykjavik, Iceland) using the Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA) (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) of more than 650,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Standard quality control was applied as previously described.22 Variants derived from whole genome sequencing (WGS) of 25,215 Scandinavian samples, of which 8360 are Danish, using NovaSeq Illumina technology where imputed into the chip genotyped samples that were phased using Eagle (version 2.4.1). High-quality variants, called using Grapthyper,23 were imputed into 270,627 genotyped Danes using haplotype sharing in a Hidden Markov Model based on a Li and Stephens model24 similar to the one used in IMPUTE2.25 The CCR5Δ32 (rs333, chr3: 46373452) variant was well imputed with an imputation info score of 0.982.

Identification and classification of infection

Infectious events were defined as the first relevant redeemed prescription of antibiotics or hospital-treated infection after inclusion. We collected individual follow-up data from Danish national health registers.

In the Danish National Patient Register, hospital in- and outpatient contacts have been recorded since 1977 with dates of admission and discharge. Available diagnostic information is based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) from 1995 to the present.26,27 We defined ICD-10 diagnosis codes related to S. aureus infections including abscesses, SSTIs, and invasive S. aureus infections. Primary outcome was S. aureus-associated infections. Secondary outcomes were all-cause infection categorized into subgroups: respiratory tract infections, gastrointestinal infections, and urinary tract/pelvic infections.28 The Danish National Prescription Register was established in 1995 and contains information on every redeemed prescription of drugs dispensed from Danish pharmacies: the dispensation date, the type of drug according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes, dosage, and pack size.29 We identified all redeemed prescriptions of antibiotics agents for oral treatment of bacterial (J01x) as a proxy for infections. Also, redeemed prescriptions of dicloxacillin/flucloxacillin and fusidic acid-containing topical drugs were used to assess S. aureus-associated infections (ICD-10 diagnosis and ATC codes in Supplementary Methods). The analysis of S. aureus-associated infections, including subgroup analyses (abscesses, SSTIs, S. aureus invasive infections) was replicated by lookup in an existing dataset from the Icelandic population using the same ICD-10 codes as in Denmark (Supplementary Methods).

Identification and classification of S. aureus carriage

The cultivation protocol for S. aureus in DBDSaCS has been described in detail previously13 and in Supplementary Methods. S. aureus nasal carriage was defined as a binary variable both in the analyses based on one nasal swab (carrier/non-carrier) and based on two nasal swabs (persistent carrier/others); persistent carriers (when colonized at either time point) while intermittent (when colonized at one of two time points) and non-carriers (not colonized at either time point) were categorized as other forms of carriage based on previous study.30

Biomarker measurements

A total of 47 inflammatory biomarkers were measured using the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) V-PLEX Human Biomarker 54-plex kit among a subgroup of DBDS participants termed ‘The 10K Inflammatory Biomarker Cohort’, including a chemokine panel of ten analytes (Supplementary Table S1). In brief, plasma samples from 9876 randomly chosen participants enrolled in the period March 2010 to March 2021 were selected for biomarker measurement. All samples were stored at −20 °C before thawing and aliquoting.31

Statistics

Characteristics of the cohort are presented as quantities, percentages, or medians with ranges. Groups were compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data, X2 test for categorical data, and Kruskal–Wallis test for non-Gaussian data. CCR5Δ32 genotype frequencies were tested for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

The association between CCR5Δ32 and the risk of infection was explored by Cox proportional hazards analysis with age (continuous) as underlying timescale and adjusted for sex, current smoking status (dichotomous), body mass index (BMI; continuous). Participants were followed from inclusion in the DBDS until first infectious event, emigration, death, or the end of follow-up (August 4, 2021), whichever occurred first. The proportional hazards assumption was checked for each analysis by inspecting the scaled Schoenfeld residuals and no violations were observed. The association between CCR5Δ32 and S. aureus nasal carriage was assessed by multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and current smoking status. The association between CCR5Δ32 and chemokine concentrations was determined by multivariable linear regression after log-transformation adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and current smoking status as well as sample age, and region of inclusion as described previously.31 Assumptions for linear regression analysis were checked by inspecting scatterplots and examination of Q–Q plots of residuals without any violations observed. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to assess comorbidity, calculated as a cumulated score from 15 years before inclusion. Only 2.8% of participants had a CCI above 0. Among the 19 major conditions included in the CCI,32 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was the most prevalent, affecting 61% of those with a CCI >0, or 1.7% of all participants, while the remaining conditions each only affected a few participants. Therefore, we chose to adjust all analyses for COPD only.

Risk estimates are presented as hazards ratios (HR) from Cox analyses, odds ratios (OR) from logistic analyses, or geometric mean ratios from log-transformed linear regression with 95% confidence interval (CI). The P-value significance level was 0.05 for the primary chemokines investigated (CCR5 ligands: CCL2, CCL3, and CCL4). For the additional chemokines, significance levels were Bonferroni adjusted by the number of chemokines in the panel to 0.005. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

CCR5Δ32 and redeemed antibiotics prescriptions

A total of 15,824 participants (17%) redeemed prescriptions of dicloxacillin and 23,867 (25%) redeemed prescriptions of fusidic acid agents during 719,248 person-years of observation. Neither heterozygosity nor homozygosity was associated with redeemed dicloxacillin when compared with wildtype CCR5 homozygotes (CCR5/Δccr5: HR 1.01, CI: 0.97–1.05 and Δccr5/Δccr5: HR 0.96, CI: 0.84–1.11) and fusidic acid agents (CCR5/Δccr5: HR 1.01, CI: 0.98–1.04 and Δccr5/Δccr5: HR 1.03, CI: 0.92–1.15). Furthermore, a total of 65,723 participants (69%) redeemed prescriptions of antibiotics during 384,206 person-years of observation. Neither heterozygosity nor homozygosity was associated with redeemed prescription of antibiotics in the crude or adjusted models (Table 2).

CCR5Δ32 and hospital-treated infections

During 731,135 person-years of observation, 3513 (4%) participants were hospital-treated for S. aureus infections and 10,876 (11%) participants were hospital-treated for all-cause infections. CCR5Δ32 heterozygosity and homozygosity were associated with neither hospital-treated S. aureus infection overall nor with subgroups of S. aureus infections (Table 3). This was corroborated by results from an existing Icelandic dataset with 131,432 S. aureus cases and 214,564 controls, where no associations were found (Table 4). Likewise, sub-analyses showed no association between CCR5Δ32 and risk of all-cause infection. With regard to more specific subgroups of infections, no effects of CCR5Δ32 on the risk of respiratory tract infection, gastrointestinal infection, urinary tract/pelvic infection were observed (Table 3).

Discussion

This hitherto largest population-based study of healthy Danes suggests no clinically relevant effect of the CCR5Δ32 on the risk of hospital-treated S. aureus-associated infection. Also, CCR5Δ32 was associated with neither redeemed antibiotics used for the treatment of S. aureus infection nor with S. aureus nasal carriage. These results do not support the idea that CCR5Δ32 affects the risk of S. aureus carriage or infection to any relevant degree, at least not in this northern European context. However, we cannot rule out S. aureus as a previous driver of CCR5Δ32 selection. Interestingly, we discovered an association between CCR5Δ32 and elevated CCL4 concentrations, with higher concentrations among Δ32-heterozygotes and Δ32-homozygotes, whereas concentrations of CCL2, CXCL-10, and CCL11 were slightly lower among Δ32-heterozygotes. Additionally, the risk of all-cause infection was not associated with CCR5Δ32. Sub analyses with specific infection groups showed that CCR5Δ32 did not associate with risk of respiratory tract infections, gastrointestinal infections, and urinary tract/pelvic infections.

The current study determines the infectious health impact of the CCR5Δ32 polymorphism in a large CCR5Δ32 high-prevalent population of primarily North European ancestry. A previous case–control study found that homozygosity for the CCR5Δ32 was reported to associate with increased risk of symptomatic West Nile virus infection among 1542 participants, including blood donors as controls33; an infection not endemic to Denmark. Also, findings among 514 children suggest that CCR5Δ32 predisposes to increased susceptibility to chronic Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections and a lower risk of asthma development.34 The testing of 171 respiratory samples from patients infected with influenza virus A (H1N1) yielded a higher fatality rate among patients with CCR5Δ32 than patients with wildtype.35 On the other hand, CCR5Δ32 did not associate with the susceptibility to Chlamydia trachomatis-induced arthritis and neither with osteomyelitis among 218 and 153 patients, respectively.36,37 Although we found no association between CCR5Δ32 and the susceptibility of bacterial and viral infections, particularly not respiratory tract infections or specifically Influenza virus infections, CCR5 could still affect disease progression after infection.

In recent years, much effort has been made to examine genetic risk factors for S. aureus nasal carriage and infections, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) in IL-4,38, 39, 40 mannose-binding lectin,41 TLR2,42 DEFB1 gene promotor,43 glucocorticoid receptor gene,44 C-reactive protein,38,40 and vitamin D receptor.45 In addition, four GWASs have investigated potential associations between common genetic variants and human susceptibility to S. aureus infection and nasal carriage. These studies revealed multiple variants in a candidate gene (GLS2), imputed SNPs (rs115231074 and rs35079132) located near HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB1 genes on chromosome 6 in the HLA class II region, and variants in genes with impact on cellular integrity and morphology.46, 47, 48, 49, 50 Notably, a Danish study of middle-aged and elderly twins reported a modest influence of host genetics factors on the S. aureus carriage state.51 Nevertheless, large-scale studies of the association between genetic variants and S. aureus carriage in the general population are limited. The current study addresses specifically the impact of CCR5Δ32 on the risk of S. aureus infection and nasal carriage in humans. Our findings serve to clarify the possibility of a reduced risk of S. aureus colonisation and subsequent lower risk of infections, as prompted by the findings by Alonzo et al. showing CCR5Δ32 conferring protection against S. aureus LukED in mouse studies.9

We here show a significant impact of CCR5Δ32 on the concentrations of chemokines CCL2, CCL4, CCL11, and CXCL-10. This is in contrast to a previous study of 399 blood donors that found no change in CC-chemokine production (CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5) between CCR5Δ32-heterozygotes and CCR5 wildtype individuals upon ex vivo stimulation of whole blood from. However, one GWAS by Ahola-Olli et al. identified five variants in the 17q12 locus that associated independently with CCL4 and collectively explained 34% of the variation in circulating CCL4. Two further variants in the 3p21.31 locus, rs113010081 and rs113341849, were also associated with CCL4 concentrations.20 Using the WGS data from the Dan-NICAD cohort,52 we found that rs113010081 and rs113341849 were in pairwise LD with rs333 and exerted similar effects as CCR5Δ32 on outcomes in all analyses, demonstrating that CCR5Δ32 also associate with higher CCL4 concentrations (Supplementary Tables S6–S9). A possible explanation is that receptors for inflammatory chemokines usually are regulated reciprocally to the ligands, i.e. progressively higher CCL4 concentrations are produced in CCR5Δ32 heterozygotes and homozygotes as a result of Δ32-deletion encoding truncated receptors not reaching the cell surface.53 However, contrary to previous studies, we observed that CCR5Δ32 was correlated to the concentrations of CCL2, CCL11, and CXCL-10. While mouse models blocking CCR3 and CCL11 showed decreased airway inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness, other studies reported inconsistent results of the association between CCR5Δ32 and reduced asthma risk in humans.54,55 However, the significance of these findings is unclear which imposes a need to clarify the role of specific chemokines in specific disorders to understand which molecules are the best targets for each disease.53

Danish blood donors are unremunerated and they represent a selected population healthier than the general population, since they must meet strict health criteria to donate. In particular, donors must be free of symptoms of infection, and they are deferred if they received oral antibiotics the four weeks prior to donation. Also, donors are permanently excluded if diagnosed with certain chronic diseases, such as diabetes, cancer, and untreated hypertension. Thus, it is a population with a limited proportion of individuals with comorbidities (2.8%) well suited for assessing the impact of CCR5Δ32 on the risk of infections in otherwise healthy individuals.

The genotyping of CCR5Δ32 was based on the Illumina GSA chip and has undergone a similar standard quality control as that described elsewhere.22 In the Danish cohort, the Δ32 allele frequency and genotype distribution are in full agreement with the Icelandic cohort (Table 4), the Dan-NICAD cohort,52 and previous Danish reports.56,57

Our analyses captured infectious outcomes of increasing severity by using information on both ATC codes for antibiotics agents and hospital discharge codes to identify and broad infectious endpoints. Using the Danish health registers is considered as a strength owing to the complete follow-up for outcome events, and the fact that infectious ICD-10 discharge codes have been validated with high positive predictive values.58 Even though the coverage of the Danish Prescription Register is nearly 100%, we cannot rule out the possibility that some antibiotics have been prescribed outside their proper indication. In our study, we may thus have underestimated the risk of infections, given that blood donors are healthier than the general population. However, when replicating our analysis of the association between CCR5Δ32 and S. aureus-associated infections in an independent population-based cohort of Icelanders, we had similar findings.

We observed a constant S. aureus nasal carriage rate of 40–41% in this cohort throughout the study period. This higher prevalence than previous reported in European populations can be attributed to our use of Tryptic Soy Broth enrichment in the S. aureus culture method, thereby lowering the detection limit of nasal carriage. Importantly, having two nasal swabs in a subgroup enabled us to distinguish between persistent, intermittent, and non-carriage.13 However, a limitation is the lack of information on whether S. aureus nasal isolates carry genes encoding LukED. The prevalence of LukED genes was reported to be 82% in blood isolates and 61% in nasal isolates among 429 patients in Germany.59 Nonetheless, this study is conducted without regard to whether S. aureus is carrying LukED or other virulence factors.

This study of 95,406 blood donors from the DBDS showed no increased risk of S. aureus-associated infection, which was replicated in 345,996 Icelanders, and no increased risk of all-cause infection associated with CCR5Δ32 whether measured by use of redeemed prescription of antibiotics or hospital-based treatment of infection. Moreover, S. aureus nasal carriage was not associated with CCR5Δ32. Lastly, CCL4 was the main chemokine affected by CCR5Δ32 with higher concentration observed among Δ32-carriers. Future large-scale population-based studies identifying genes and variants associated with S. aureus persistent carriage and infection in otherwise healthy individuals are warranted.
