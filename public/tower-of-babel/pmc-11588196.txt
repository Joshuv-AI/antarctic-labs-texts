A Study of Serum Ferritin and Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone Levels in Male Patients with Androgenetic Alopecia
Authors: Pallakshi Prasad, Preema Sinha, Nagendra Singh Beniwal, Rajesh Sahu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is a multifactorial disorder expressed by several genes and various environmental factors apart from nutritional and endocrinological factors. Although association of iron deficiency (ID) as well as thyroid disorder has been evaluated in females, this is still an untouched topic as far as male pattern hair loss (MPHL) is concerned. This study tries to establish if any association of MPHL with ID and thyroid dysfunction.Materials and Methods:This case–control study comprised 30 consecutive cases of MPHL and equal numbers of age-matched healthy controls attending dermatology outdoor department at a tertiary care hospital. Cases as well as controls were evaluated and screened for the levels of serum ferritin (SF) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Value of SF (8–388 ng/mL) and TSH (0.358–3.7 uIU/mL) was used as standardized reference.Results:In cases of AGA, SF level varied from 6.00 to 212.09 ng/mL (mean - 88.30 ng/mL). Whereas controls showed SF levels ranging between 23.67 and 185.05 ng/mL (mean 78.69 ng/mL). The range of TSH level in the case group was 0.379–5.078 uIU/L (mean 2.25 uIU/L), respectively, whereas control group showed serum TSH levels ranging between 0.30 and 4.30 uIU/L (mean - 1.78 uIU/L). Statistically considering two samples of equal variance P (2-tailed) for SF level was found to be 0.424, whereas 0.085 in case of TSH level depicting insignificant association. Spearman’s correlations (P = 0.244) between alopecia grade and SF level were found to be statistically insignificant (P = 0.193). Similarly, the P value calculated for the alopecia grade and serum TSH was 0.784, making the correlation between alopecia and serum TSH as insignificant.Conclusion:The study found no statistically significant difference in the SF level and TSH level of the cases as compared to controls.

INTRODUCTION

In the course of evolution, human scalp hairs appear to be vestigial with limited biological roles, yet these have immense psycho-social implication. Hairs are integral to personality and individual outlook. Williamson et al. illustrated the detrimental effect of hair loss on the quality of life such as loss of self-confidence, low self-esteem, and heightened self-consciousness.[1] Androgenetic alopecia (AGA), which is also known as patterned hair loss (PHL) is quite a common skin condition affecting both the genders. The term “AGA” was first proposed by Orentreich.[2] AGA is a genetically predetermined condition characterized by progressive conversion of terminal hairs to vellus hairs in patterned distribution. AGA is accompanied with a significant negative impact on the quality of life of the affected patient.[3]

AGA is the most common sort of hair loss condition encountered in the routine outpatient department (OPD). Although AGA is common across the races, the prevalence is the highest among Caucasians. Estimated prevalence of AGA in Caucasian population is 30% for men in 30s, 40% in 40s, and 50% in 50s.[4] Whereas a population-based study by Krupa Shankar et al. established that the prevalence of AGA is 58% of male population in 30–50 years’ age group.[5] However, overall prevalence of AGA is quite low (21.3%) in the Chinese population.[6] The incidence of AGA rises with age irrespective of gender.

The key to basic pathology of AGA is gradual and progressive miniaturization of hair follicles in genetically predisposed individuals finally leading to the conversion of terminal hairs to vellus hairs. In AGA duration of anagen phase is shortened; however, lengthening of telogen phase occurs.[7]

It is postulated that proliferation and differentiation nature of multipotent epithelial stem cells of dermal papillae are pivotal in the maintenance of hair growth. Testosterone and other androgens such as dihydrotestosterone (DHT)/androstenedione are also metabolized in cutaneous tissues. Enzyme 5-α reductase converts testosterone to more potent androgen DHT in certain organs such as prostate glands. DHT strongly binds to the androgen receptor (AR) which finally exert biological activity after translocation to the nucleus.[8] In alopecia, concentration of DHT, 5-α reductase, and ARs is increased. The higher the concentration of androgen and ARs; enhanced the effect on expression of gene which control follicular cycling.[9]

AGA is multifactorial disorder manifested by interplay of several gene as well as environmental factors. Polygenic inheritance from either of parents, genetic predisposition, and effect of androgen are the important key factors in the pathogenesis of AGA.[3] Kaufman recognized two significant characteristics of AGA: (a) Shortening of anagen phase and (b) progressive follicular miniaturization which in turn leading to the conversion of terminal hair to vellus or vellus like follicles.[8]

Micronutrients play a vital role in the growth of rapidly proliferating organs such as hair and iron is the most common micronutrient implicated in various forms of hair loss such as telogen effluvium, alopecia areata, and alopecia universalis; nevertheless, its direct association has not yet been established in PHL.[10]

The total iron in the body is distributed among the three compartments: (a) Storage iron-in the form of ferritin and hemosiderin, (b) transport iron in the form of transferrin, and (c) functional iron bound to hemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes, etc. Deficiency of iron generally occurs in a continuum of increasing severity which includes stage of iron depletion followed by the stages of iron deficient erythropoiesis and iron-deficient anemia.[11] Serum ferritin (SF) level is the most common and early marker to detect iron deficiency (ID) in clinical as well as public health settings. SF is the main iron-binding protein in nonerythroid cells which reflects total body iron stores. It decreases from very early stage of ID as iron reserves decrease. Because only ID can cause very low serum ferritin concentration (FC), a FC is very specific for ID.[12]

Thus, SF concentration is a routinely available indicator with well-described associations with iron status but also recognized limitations associated with distortions in the setting of concomitant inflammation and liver disease.[1213]

The limited available data suggest the commonly recommended SF cut off 15 mg/L is a specific but not sensitive threshold.[1415161718] Being a reactive protein, SF plays active role in inflammation, hence screening of ID with serum FC in patients with active infection or inflammation is not recommended.

Like iron, thyroid disorders are also evidently associated with hair ailments including varied types of hair loss.[13] Thyroid hormone is crucial for the development and maintenance of hair follicle.[14] Freinkel and Freinkel elucidated that deficiency of thyroid hormone was associated with increased percentage of telogen hair which was further restored to normal after replacement therapy with thyroid hormone.[15]

Association of male pattern hair loss (MPHL) with ID and thyroid disorder is still naïve in the medical literature and no studies are available on this subject as on date. This study intends to establish the association of MPHL with ID and thyroid dysfunction (if any).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present study is a case–control study conducted on patients attending dermatology OPD at a tertiary care hospital of Central India over a period of 9 months. Prior approval of Institutional Ethical Committee obtained.

The aim of this study is to study the levels of SF and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in male patients of AGA and to establish its association if any.

Cases included thirty consecutive male patients of age between 18 and 50 years diagnosed clinically and aided by dermoscopy as case of AGA attending dermatology outpatient clinic. Controls included thirty age and sex matched healthy individuals visiting dermatology outpatient clinic who did not complain of hair loss.

The exclusion criteria are as follows:-

(a)Patients aged <18 years or >50 years old, smokers(b)Patients with comorbidities, namely, thyroid disorders and chronic/acute corticosteroid therapy(c)Cutaneous lymphoma or any other malignancy(d)Patient on chemotherapy or exhibited to chemotherapy in the last 5 years(e)Patient with a history of prolonged illness or exposure to stressful event in the past 6 months(f)Patient unwilling to be part of the study population.

RESULTS

This was a case–control study comprising of thirty cases and equal number of age- and sex-matched healthy controls as selected by the inclusion and exclusion criteria depicted in preceding paragraphs. This study included cases as well as controls between 20 and 50 years with a mean age of 32.6 years.

In cases SF level varied from 6.00 to 212.09 ng/mL with a mean value of 88.30 ng/mL and standard deviation 49.92, whereas controls showed SF levels ranging between 23.67 and 185.05 ng/mL with mean 78.69 ng/mL and standard deviation of 42.24. The range of TSH level in the case group was 0.379–5.078 uIU/L with mean and standard deviation being 2.25 uIU/L and 1.13, respectively. Whereas control group showed serum TSH levels ranging between 0.30 and 4.30 uIU/L with the mean value of 1.78 uIU/L and standard deviation of 0.90 [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Although AGA is the most common presentation of hair loss encountered in clinical practice universally affecting both the genders, there are no data available regarding its association with SF and TSH, particularly in males. The relationship between ID and hair loss is still unclear. Same is the case with association of thyroid disorder and pattern hair loss.

This study was conducted to ascertain such correlation in MPHL, being a naïve topic till now. The present study describes male PHL as per Norwood-Hamilton Classification and to determine its correlation with ID and thyroid dysfunction.

A study in early 60s on ID and alopecia showed hair regrowth with iron supplementation and hair loss on discontinuation of iron therapy in women of ID anaemia.[16]

A prospective cohort study of 22 women with chronic telogen effluvium treated with iron and L-lysine demonstrated significant reduction in telogen (19.5% to 11.3%) and regained ferritin levels (33–89 ng/mL).[17] Kantor et al. conducted a case–control study comparing SF level in female PHL showed significantly lower SF level (37.3 ng/mL) than healthy controls (59.5 ng/mL).[18] Bregy and Trüeb determined no association between SF > 10 μg/L and hair loss in women.[19]

On the contrary, there are many studies which do not suggest any correlation between ID and hair loss. A cross-sectional study of 181 healthy women with telogen effluvium or female pattern hair loss; and SF as variable did not show any statistically significant association.[19] Sinclair R concluded no direct relationship between low SF and hair loss while studying correlation between low SF (≤20 μg/L) and chronic diffuse telogen hair loss in women.[20]

The reference range of test values for SF according to our laboratory values was taken as 8–388 ng/mL. 3.33% cases out of case group (of 30) found to have SF less than the normal reference value whereas none from the control group had SF values lesser than the normal reference range. Although 16.6% of patients reporting to the clinic with MPHL had SF levels on the lower side, i.e., <50 ng/mL, suggesting the importance of iron screening in patients of MPHL. However, this study could not put forward a statistically significant correlation between ID and male PHL (P > 0.05).

In early 70s, Freinkel and Frienkel demonstrated that the deficiency of thyroid hormone was associated with alopecia, particularly telogen effluvium.[15] Thyroid autoimmunity has been linked with alopecia areata. Baloch et al. showed possible correlation of hypophyseal hypothyroidism in female AGA.[21] However, there is no study available establishing the correlation of MPHL and thyroid dysfunction.

Lately, Vincent and Yogiraj established the association of multiple variables of alopecia with relation to thyroid disorder. They demonstrated that thyroid dysfunction associated alopecia including all types such as diffuse alopecia, AGA, alopecia areata increased with aging. In the age group of 0–20 years and 21–40 years, thyroid dysfunction was seemingly correlated with alopecia areata and diffuse alopecia with almost equal prevalence, whereas thyroid dysfunction was found to be more associated with alopecia areata and AGA (50%).[13]

The reference range of test values for TSH according to our laboratory values was taken as 0.358–3.7 mIU/mL. Out of thirty cases of MPHL, 6.66% cases had TSH above the normal reference range, suggestive of association with hypothyroidism. Moreover, none of these cases had TSH below the reference range. On the other hand, no one from the control group showed TSH more than the upper lab limit. Akin to case group, none of them had TSH less than the normal laboratory values. However, this study could not establish statistical correlation between the MPHL and thyroid function.

Limitation of study

The small sample size as well as sample being drawn from dermatology OPD of a tertiary care hospital, sample may not represent true population and inference drawn may also not be representative of general AGA prevalent in the community. This study may further be considered in the larger sample size to strengthen the result and to derive significant inference.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION

This study elucidated that there was no statistically significant difference in the ferritin levels and TSH levels between normal male individuals and patients with MPHL. It is also emphasized that there was no correlation between both the biochemical parameters and grade of alopecia.

In the present scenario, the role of these factors is still intriguing to conclude; however, screening for ID and thyroid dysfunction can be considered important screening tests while evaluating hair loss in male patients in the routine clinical practice.
