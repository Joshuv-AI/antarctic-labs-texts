MicroRNA-122 as a regulator and biomarker of liver disease
Authors: Sara Ahmadova, Zofia Wicik, Joanna Mucha, Jeff Palatini, Katarzyna Ziętal, Dagmara Mirowska-Guzel, Adam Przybylkowski, Ceren Eyileten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MicroRNA-122 (miR-122) is the most abundant liver-specific microRNA, comprising ~ 70% of the hepatic miRNA pool, and a central regulator of lipid metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, viral replication, and hepatocarcinogenesis. This review synthesizes experimental, clinical, and molecular evidence on the role of miR-122 across the spectrum of liver disease, including metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH), drug-induced acute liver injury, hepatitis B and C virus (HBV/HCV) infection, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and colorectal cancer liver metastasis. Mechanistically, miR-122 governs hepatic lipogenesis through the Sirt1/LKB1/AMPK axis, modulates inflammation via LPS/TLR-4/FoxO3 signaling, and exerts tumor-suppressive and antiviral effects through Cyclin G1/p53, HO-1, NDRG3, GALNT10, PEG10, and NEGR1. A recurring theme is the compartment- and stage-dependent behavior of miR-122: hepatic expression declines with disease progression, whereas circulating levels rise with hepatocyte injury, reconciling apparently contradictory reports and underscoring the importance of specimen source and disease stage in biomarker interpretation. We further contrast the etiology-specific regulation of miR-122 in HBV- versus HCV-associated disease, in which epigenetic silencing and interferon-linked mechanisms drive divergent expression. Finally, we critically appraise the failed clinical translation of anti-miR-122 therapeutics (miravirsen, RG-101), highlighting viral resistance, safety liabilities, and the tumor-suppressor paradox that constrains inhibition-based strategies. Collectively, miR-122 emerges as a minimally invasive biomarker and a biologically informative, though therapeutically challenging, target in liver disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00109-026-02718-1.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), a prevalent condition affecting 25–30% of the global population, progresses from hepatic steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), characterized by inflammation and fibrosis, and can ultimately lead to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, or acute liver failure marked by hepatocyte necrosis and systemic inflammation [1, 2].

MAFLD, HBV, and HCV are the leading global causes of chronic hepatitis, which can progress to fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). [3]. While HBV and HCV drive carcinogenesis through mechanisms like viral DNA integration and chronic inflammation [4], HCC can also arise from non-viral etiologies like MAFLD, often in non-cirrhotic livers, whereas secondary liver metastases, most commonly from colorectal cancer, exploit the liver's unique vasculature to colonize the organ [5, 6].

MicroRNAs are short, non-coding RNAs that critically regulate gene expression and cellular homeostasis, with dysregulation of the liver-specific miR-122, constituting ~ 70% of hepatic miRNAs, being a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis, progression, and diagnosis of liver diseases, as evidenced by its deficiency leading to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma in models [7, 8].

In this review paper, we sought to provide an in-depth examination of how miR-122 contributes to the pathogenesis, disease progression, diagnostic approaches, and treatment possibilities in a wide range of liver conditions, covering MAFLD, MASH, acute liver injury, HBV/HCV infections, and HCC with different origins. The literature search focused specifically on liver-related conditions, while maintaining inclusivity by considering all human studies regardless of the ethnic background of study participants (Supplemental file 1 Sect. "Mechanistic and clinical insights into miR-122 and SIRT1 signaling in the non-cancerous pathogenesis of MAFLD/MASH").

By summarizing findings from experimental models, clinical studies, and molecular investigations, our review highlights miR-122 as a central regulator of hepatic metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, viral replication, and tumorigenesis, and as a promising minimally invasive diagnostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in liver disease. Although several reviews have addressed miR-122 in individual liver diseases, the present review is distinguished by three features: (i) a systematic contrast of the etiology-specific regulation of miR-122 between HBV and HCV across both non-cancerous and cancerous contexts; (ii) integration of the metabolic miR-122/Sirt1/AMPK axis with the inflammatory FoxO3/TLR-4 and oncogenic Cyclin G1/p53, PEG10, GALNT10, and NDRG3 pathways across disease stages; and (iii) a critical appraisal of the failed clinical translation of anti-miR-122 therapy, including the tumor-suppressor paradox that limits its therapeutic window. As an illustrative example, miravirsen, the first anti-miRNA oligonucleotide to enter clinical trials, lowered circulating miR-122 and HCV RNA in early-phase studies, yet its development was ultimately discontinued after viral resistance emerged through 5′ UTR mutations permitting miR-122-independent replication and after direct-acting antivirals rendered host-targeted anti-HCV therapy largely redundant. By bringing together this experimental and clinical evidence, we aim to provide a broader understanding of the multifaceted role of miR-122 in liver disease.

Mechanistic and clinical insights into miR-122 and SIRT1 signaling in the non-cancerous pathogenesis of MAFLD/MASH

Multiple studies have investigated the role of miR-122 in the development and progression of MAFLD, with a particular focus on its regulatory effects on lipid metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis.

A previous study investigated the regulatory role of miR-122 in MAFLD and its role in lipid metabolism regulation through Sirt1 and the LKB1/AMPK pathway. To establish an MAFLD model, researchers administered a high-fat diet to C57BL/6 mice and treated hepatic cells with free fatty acids (FFA). Study findings showed that miR-122 expression was increased in MAFLD, leading to Sirt1 suppression and the activation of key lipogenic genes (SREBP1, FASN, ACC1, and SCD1), which increased lipid accumulation in liver cells. However, inhibiting miR-122 restored Sirt1 expression, activating the LKB1/AMPK pathway, which reduced hepatic fat accumulation. Results suggested that increased miR-122 promotes fat accumulation in hepatic cells by inhibiting Sirt1 [9].

Chen et al. found that adipocyte-derived exosomes (ADEs) carry high levels of miR-122, which is elevated in individuals with MAFLD and promotes disease progression by suppressing Sirt1. In hepatocytes, miR-122-regulated inhibition of Sirt1 increased gluconeogenic/lipogenic enzymes (G6Pc, PEPCK, FAS) and inflammation (TNF-α, IL-1β), while reducing fat oxidation (PPARα). In vitro, ADE-derived miR-122 worsened metabolic dysfunction, and it exacerbated liver injury (↑ALT/AST) and steatosis. Conversely, miR-122 inhibition or Sirt1 overexpression reversed these effects, restoring metabolic balance and reducing damage. The findings highlight the miR-122/Sirt1 axis as a key driver of MAFLD, offering a potential diagnostic marker and therapeutic target [10].

In addition to the in vitro analysis, human studies have also examined the significance of miR-122 in patients with MAFLD. One of the first studies from 2011 showed that in MAFLD patients, circulating miR-122, miR-34a, and miR-16 levels were substantially elevated compared to healthy controls, while miR-21 levels remained unchanged. Both miR-122 and miR-34a showed progressive increases with disease severity, the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis. Their serum concentrations correlated positively with liver enzyme activity, stage of fibrosis, and inflammatory markers. Additionally, miR-122 demonstrated a significant correlation with serum lipid levels [11].

In the Japanese population, miR-122 has been widely investigated in the context of MAFLD. Miyaaki et al. paired liver and serum samples collected from 67 individuals with MAFLD and revealed a considerable positive association between hepatic and circulating miR-122 levels. Notably, while miR-122 expression was increased in severe steatosis (> 33%) compared to mild steatosis (< 33%), it showed an inverse relationship with fibrosis progression, being higher in mild versus severe fibrosis. These findings demonstrate miR-122's stage-specific association with MAFLD progression and highlight serum miR-122 as a promising non-surgical biomarker for hepatic fibrosis assessment [12]. Furthermore, another study investigated the relation between circulating miR-122 levels as well as histopathological features of MAFLD in 36 Japanese patients without HCC who underwent two liver biopsies over a median follow-up of 4.6 years. The results showed that changes in circulating miR-122 were linked to key mechanisms of liver injury, including steatosis, ballooning degeneration, and fibrosis, and correlated with clinical parameters such as liver enzyme levels [13]. In Egyptian population, Samy et al. explored the potential of circulating blood-exosomal miRNAs (miR-122, miR-128, miR-200, miR-298, and miR-342) as non-invasive biomarkers for MAFLD diagnosis and its progressive form, MASH. The study also investigated the inflammatory LPS/TLR-4/FoxO3 pathway, which connects gut microbiota imbalance to liver inflammation and disease progression. Using serum samples from 50 MAFLD patients, 50 MASH patients, and 50 healthy controls, the researchers analyzed miRNA expression and measured key proteins (LPS, TLR-4, FoxO3, and adiponectin). The results revealed significant upregulation of miR-122, miR-128, FoxO3, TLR-4, and LPS in MAFLD/MASH patients compared to controls, while miR-200, miR-298, miR-342, and adiponectin showed downregulation. Authors suggested that miR-122 and miR-200 might function as biomarkers for tracking disease progression from MAFL to MASH and for assessing the role of dysbiotic gut microbiota in MAFLD development by activating the LPS/TLR-4/FoxO3 signaling axis [14]. More recent studies reinforce these observations and confirm the continued relevance of miR-122 as a MAFLD/MASH biomarker. A 2025 PRISMA-based systematic review of 1,149 studies identified miR-122 as the most frequently investigated circulating miRNA in MASLD (~ 36% of studies), consistently correlating with disease severity and fibrosis progression and reaching diagnostic AUROCs of 0.81–1.0 for MASH [15]. Consistent with this, circulating miR-122-5p was significantly elevated in MASLD patients and positively associated with steatosis, liver stiffness, and hepatic fat content in the FLiO cohort [16]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that miR-122 plays a central role in MAFLD progression by modulating lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and fibrosis, primarily through Sirt1 inhibition and disruption of the LKB1/AMPK pathway. Elevated miR-122 levels correlate with hepatic steatosis severity in both preclinical models and human patients, although its association with fibrosis appears to be stage-dependent, supporting its potential as a minimally invasive biomarker.

Clinical and experimental evidence for miR-122 in drug-induced acute liver injury

Recent research has established miR-122 as a diagnostic marker for both severe and chronic liver injury, where elevated blood concentration directly correlates with the degree of hepatocyte apoptosis from various causes, including viral, alcohol, chemical, and drug-induced damage [18].

A previous study examined miR-122's potential for early drug-induced liver injury detection, particularly in acetaminophen (Paracetamol)-induced injury. Animal model and in vitro analysis were performed by including four groups: as a control, acetaminophen, antagomir, acetaminophen + antagomir. Key methods included serum ALT/AST measurement, histopathology, luciferase assays confirming miR-122-5p's targeting of NDRG3, and cellular analyses showing miR-122-5p inhibition prevented APAP-driven injury through NDRG3 upregulation and reducing apoptosis. The results compared acetaminophen-treated animal groups against controls, showing that acetaminophen increased liver necrosis, inflammation, serum ALT/AST, and miR-122-5p expression, while miR-122-5p suppressor partially reversed these effects. In vitro analysis confirmed the animal model. Final comparisons showed NDRG3 overexpression rescued miR-122-5p mimic effects, while NDRG3 knockdown counteracted miR-122-5p inhibitor benefits. These findings establish that inhibiting miR-122-5p prevents acetaminophen-induced damage via modulating NDRG3 and apoptosis markers via increasing Bcl-w/Bcl-2, while decreasing Bax [1].

An important study investigated miR-122 as a biomarker for liver injury using a combination of in vitro models, murine models of acute toxic liver damage, and a clinical cohort of 223 critically ill patients. Serum miR-122 levels were substantially elevated in ICU patients versus healthy controls and were analyzed across key clinical parameters: (1) disease severity (APACHE II scores), (2) septic vs. non-septic causes, (3) mortality outcomes (ICU death and long-term survival), and (4) liver injury status based on enzyme activity. MiR-122 showed a strong correlation with traditional liver injury markers (ALT, AST, GLDH), with persistent associations observed at day 3, particularly with GLDH. In vitro results further supported these findings. Overall, the study highlights miR-122 as a reliable and independent indicator of hepatocyte injury in both animal and human models, regardless of the underlying condition [2] (Table 1).

In summary, miR-122 serves as a sensitive biomarker for acute liver injury, particularly in drug-induced hepatotoxicity and ischemia–reperfusion injury to liver cells. Besides, increased serum miR-122 levels consistently correlate with liver damage in critically ill patients, demonstrating its clinical utility for early detection and monitoring of acute hepatic injury.

The role of miR-122 in HBV/HCV replication, inflammation, and liver fibrosis

MiR-122 as a modulator of HBV hepatitis

Multiple studies have investigated the function of miR-122 in HBV infection on the regulatory impact on viral replication and liver inflammation [19, 20]. MiR-122 appears to suppress HBV replication through various molecular pathways, and its altered expression could play a role in viral persistence, disease progression, as well as therapeutic potential.

A previous study explored miR-122, a liver-specific miRNA essential for liver function and hepatitis C virus (HCV) regulation, focusing on its function in hepatitis B virus (HBV) replication and its mechanistic correlation with heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1, an antiviral enzyme). The study employed chemically synthesized miR-122 inhibitors/mimics and HO-1 siRNA (RiboBio) to transfect Huh-7 and HepG2 cells, followed using real-time PCR, immunoblotting along with ELISA analyses of HBV antigens (HBsAg/HBeAg). Compared to control cells, miR-122 inhibition significantly increased HBV protein levels (HBsAg and HBeAg), while miR-122 overexpression reduced HBsAg/HBeAg. Therefore, HO-1 induction suppressed HBV replication, whereas HO-1 knockdown enhanced viral protein production. Importantly, the antiviral effects were specifically mediated through the miR-122/HO-1 axis, with no observed impact on cell proliferation in either cell type [19].

Previous study collected samples from chronic asymptomatic carriers (ASC), Chronic HBV infection (CHB) patients, and patients with HBV-associated acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) and healthy controls (HC) for miRNA analysis. Compared to healthy controls, HBV-infected groups, including asymptomatic carriers (ASC), CHB, and ACLF, showed increased serum miRNA diversity and higher levels of liver-specific miRNAs, particularly miR-122 and miR-194. MiR-122 levels were notably higher in HBeAg-positive than in HBeAg-negative patients. In hepatoma cells, the inhibition of miR-122 in Huh7 cells doubled HBV mRNA levels, while its overexpression in HepG2 cells suppressed replication, confirming its antiviral role. Collectively, these results indicate that miR-122 holds promise not only as a diagnostic biomarker for HBV infection but also as a key regulator of viral replication and associated hepatic injury [21].

Previous study employed serum miRNA profiling in a group of 10 healthy controls and 10 chronic HBV individuals. Subsequently, validation was performed for 19 selected miRNAs via quantitative RT-PCR in a larger cohort. Additionally, miRNA levels were compared between serum and liver tissue using biopsy-obtained liver tissue. To investigate AGO2's role in HBV replication, we examined its intracellular co-localization with HBV core (HBcAg) and surface (HBsAg) antigens in a stable HepG2 transfection model. The functional impact of AGO2 depletion was assessed via siRNA-mediated knockdown. Study findings revealed significant upregulation of several miRNAs, such as miR-122, miR-22, and miR-99a, in patients with HBV. AGO2 was found to physically bind with HBcAg and co-localize in the endoplasmic reticulum. Similarly, HBsAg co-localized with AGO2 across multiple cellular regions. Notably, siRNA silencing of AGO2 significantly reduced extracellular HBV DNA and HBsAg levels. In conclusion, these findings highlight a potential regulatory role for AGO2 and HBV-specific miRNAs in the viral life cycle. On the other hand, no evidence was found for a direct miR-122-AGO2 interaction in the HBV model [4]. Chen et al. analyzed clinical samples obtained from 80 patients infected with HBV, alongside samples from healthy control subjects, and they showed significantly lower serum miR-122 levels in HBV patients versus healthy controls. Notably, miR-122 levels inversely correlated with viral load, which was consistent with in vitro findings that demonstrated miR-122 overexpression suppressed HBV replication, reducing HBsAg, HBeAg, and viral DNA by 50–70%. These results indicate that HBV downregulates miR-122, which serves as a natural antiviral factor by targeting a conserved site in the HBV polymerase gene [22] (Table 1).

Multiple studies demonstrate that miR-122 suppresses HBV replication by binding to viral RNA and host factors, including cyclin G1, HO-1, and disrupting pro-viral mechanisms. Clinically, reduced miR-122 in HBV patients correlates with higher viral load and liver injury, underscoring its antiviral and tumor-suppressive role in non-carcinoma HBV contexts. While miR-122 overexpression inhibits HBV antigens (HBsAg/HBeAg), its interaction with AGO2 in HBV remains unclear.

MiR-122 as a modulator of HCV hepatitis

In addition to HBV infection, recent studies highlight the potential of miR-122 in a dual capacity as both a biomarker and a treatment target for disease progression in liver disease associated with HCV. One of the first studies by Cermelli et al. analyzed MAFLD patients, in addition to investigating the role of miR-122 in chronic liver inflammation. Among patients with chronic HCV, circulating expression levels of miR-122, miR-34a, and miR-16 were significantly upregulated compared with healthy controls, while miR-21 remained unchanged. These alterations occurred independently of viral load, and both miR-122 and miR-34a showed a positive association with disease severity, including liver enzyme activity, fibrosis grade, and inflammatory response. Importantly, the findings were confirmed in an independent HCV cohort, reinforcing the value of miR-122 and miR-34a as promising non-invasive biomarkers of liver disease severity in hepatitis [11]. A recent study investigated circulating miR-122 levels in a group of Pakistani patients with chronic HCV-induced liver disease, with a predominant prevalence of HCV genotype 3 infections. The researchers analyzed serum samples from 222 patients, clinically categorized into mild, moderate, or severe disease stages. The study found that serum miR-122 levels were significantly elevated in patients with cirrhosis, with the most pronounced increase occurring in those with mild cirrhosis. Additionally, a positive association was observed linking elevated miR-122 expression to increased ALT activity in this subgroup. Notably, these findings from a genotype 3-dominant cohort were consistent with earlier reports from genotype 1-predominant populations, which suggest that miR-122 upregulation following HCV infection is not dependent on viral genotype [23]. A previous study searched novel compounds for HCV treatment via miR-122 link. Researchers have tested and proven reliable for large-scale drug screening. They screened over 336,000 chemical compounds to find ones that could block miR-122 and restore the light signal. Out of 1,023 compounds, researchers identified two different compounds that block the transcription factor HNF4α through a direct molecular interaction with its ligand-binding domain, thereby disrupting recognition within the miR-122 promoter. Structural studies revealed key hydrogen-bonding interactions, offering insights for future inhibitor design. Both compounds significantly reduced hepatitis C virus RNA replication (~ 90%) in human hepatic cells, highlighting their therapeutic potential. To our knowledge, this study is the first to validate a small molecule that inhibits a miRNA-regulating transcription factor. Importantly, these compounds substantially decrease HCV replication in human liver cells, highlighting their potential as antiviral agents and tools for studying miR-122 biology [24].

Previous research using sera from chronic HCV-infected patients and liver transplant candidates found that a significant upregulation of miR-122 was observed in HCV patients compared to healthy individuals. Additionally, the study examined IFN-λ3 genotypes and their association with variations in HCV progression and treatment response. The role of miR-122 in HCC

MiR-122 as a biomarker and regulator in HBV- and HCV-associated HCC

Serum miR-16 was substantially downregulated in HCC patients compared to those with chronic HCV, while miR-199a levels were notably lower in chronic HCV patients than in healthy controls. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that miR-16 could distinguish HCC from HCV patients with 57.5% sensitivity and 70% specificity. Importantly, combining serum miR-16 with alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) enhanced diagnostic sensitivity to 85% and improved overall accuracy to 87.5%. Furthermore, miR-199a and miR-16 levels were significantly associated with tumor characteristics, including size and lesion number. On the other hand, the study failed to find miR-122 as a differentiative biomarker for liver disease [26].

Another study by using human tumor tissue samples examined the impact of miR-122 expression for survival of Hepatitis B virus-associated hepatocellular carcinoma (HBV-HCC) patients. 142 primary liver tumor samples and an additional 20 matched HBV-HCC and non-tumor liver tissues confirmed that miR-122 was significantly lower in HBV-related tumors. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that low miR-122 expression correlated with larger tumor size, venous invasion, poor tumor differentiation, and reduced overall survival [27]. Apart from the tumour tissues, the previous study analyzed the blood samples in the Chinese population. MiR-122 levels were measured in serum from 14 HCC patients before and one month after surgical resection. Notably, serum miR-122 levels were significantly higher in HBV patients with HCC than in healthy controls and decreased post-operatively [28].

Qiao et al. examined the expression patterns of miR-122 and miR-22 in HBV-related HCC. Patients with HBV-associated HCC exhibited significantly lower levels of miR-122 and miR-22 compared to those with benign liver disease or non-HBV-related HCC. In contrast, no significant difference in serum miR-122 or miR-22 levels was detected between benign liver disease and non-HBV-related HCC cases. Furthermore, reduced expression of both miRNAs was inversely associated with key clinical and pathological features, including tumor size, lymph node metastasis, TNM stage, liver cirrhosis, AFP levels, and HBV DNA load, all of which emerged as independent risk factors [29].

A previous human study found that miR-122 expression in liver tissue was significantly lower in patients with HBV infection (without carcinoma) compared to healthy controls. Moreover, miR-122 levels were inversely correlated with intrahepatic viral load and hepatic necroinflammation, suggesting that reduced miR-122 contributes to enhanced HBV replication. The study also identified cyclin G1 as a direct target of miR-122, showing that miR-122 downregulates cyclin G1, thereby disrupting its interaction with p53. This disruption abrogates p53’s inhibitory effect on HBV replication, ultimately promoting viral persistence. These findings were confirmed through in vivo and ex vivo analyses of liver tissue samples, alongside cell transfection and protein interaction experiments. Although the study did not include patients with HCC, it highlighted the miR-122–Cyclin G1–p53 axis and suggested that loss of miR-122 may facilitate HBV replication and potentially contribute to hepatocarcinogenesis [20].

The previous study involved 101 patients with advanced primary HCC, 48 patients with chronic hepatitis B, and 89 healthy controls, with blood samples collected for miRNA analysis. It was found that serum miR-21, miR-122, and miR-223 were elevated in both HCC and chronic hepatitis patients, but miR-21 and miR-122 were higher in chronic hepatitis patients than in HCC patients. On the other hand, the study failed to find these miRNAs as biomarkers for differentiating between HCC and chronic hepatitis patients, indicating their potential to reflect liver injury rather than specifically diagnosing HCC [30].

Previous study employed both in vitro liver cancer cell models and in vivo experiments using HBV transgenic mice, as well as liver tissue samples from chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and HBV-associated HCC patients. PCR results showed markedly lower miR-122 levels in CHB and HCC tissues than in healthy controls. Notably, study found the direct interaction between miR-122 and PTTG1 gene binding factor (PBF), confirmed by luciferase assays. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed elevated PBF levels in liver samples from CHB and HCC patients. Importantly, an inverse relationship between miR-122 expression and PBF overexpression was observed in CHB patients. Overall, the study identifies a novel HBV-mediated miR-122/PBF regulatory axis that contributes to HCC development [31].

MiR-122 expression was found to be down-regulated in the HBV-related HCC cell line compared to the HCC cell line without HBV. NDRG3, a member of the N-myc downstream-regulated gene family, was up-regulated in HBV-related HCC cells and identified as a target of miR-122. The research highlighted the inverse correlation between miR-122 and NDRG3 expression in HBV-related HCC tissues. By transfecting miR-122 into the HBV-related HCC cells, the researchers demonstrated that miR-122 repressed NDRG3 expression, leading to a reversal of the malignant phenotype, and significantly inhibited HBV replication, viral antigen expression, and cell proliferation. The findings suggest that miR-122 and NDRG3 could serve as important diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for HBV-related HCC [32].

Previous study examined the role of the Hnf4α/miR-122 axis in HBV-HCC and its regulation of the oncogenic GALNT10 gene via using HBV-HCC cell lines. Results showed that Hnf4α and miR-122 were significantly downregulated in HBV-HCC, leading to the upregulation of GALNT10, which enhanced tumor growth and invasion compared to corresponding nontumor tissues. The study highlights the Hnf4α/miR-122/GALNT10 pathway as a potential therapeutic target, where restoring miR-122 expression could help inhibit GALNT10-driven tumor progression [27]. Previous advanced study investigated the role of the miR-122/PEG10 axis in HCC pathogenesis. Research combined analysis of 147 human HCC specimens, in vitro model (HepG2 and Hep3B cell lines), and in vivo model (miR-122 knockout mice) showed that miR-122 downregulation relieved the translational repression of PEG10, which leads to its overexpression and subsequent contribution to HCC progression. While PEG10 is not an ideal diagnostic marker, its elevated expression was a robust prognostic indicator of poor survival and advanced disease. These findings demonstrated that the reduction in miR-122 levels of miR-122 results in the elevation of PEG10, which in turn contributes to the promotion of HCC [33] (Table 1). Notably, studies investigating miR-122 in HCC have reported seemingly conflicting findings depending on the biological specimen analyzed. While most tissue-based studies consistently demonstrate reduced miR-122 expression in tumor tissue, supporting its tumor-suppressive role, several serum-based studies have reported elevated circulating miR-122 levels in patients with HCC. These differences may reflect distinct biological processes, as circulating miR-122 can be released from injured hepatocytes, whereas reduced intrahepatic miR-122 expression contributes to tumor initiation and progression. Furthermore, variations in underlying liver disease etiology (HBV, HCV, or MAFLD), tumor stage, and patient characteristics may additionally contribute to the heterogeneity observed across studies. Therefore, interpretation of miR-122 levels in HCC should consider both the biological sample source and disease context.

Differential regulation of miR-122 in HBV- and HCV-related HCC

To investigate whether miR-122 expression in tumor tissue depends on the underlying viral infection, a previous study analyzed paired tumor and non-tumor liver samples from Japanese patients with HBV or HCV using qRT-PCR. Results showed that miR-122 was significantly lower in HBV-related HCC compared to non-infected liver tissue, but this reduction was not observed in HCV-related HCC. Surprisingly, non-tumor tissue from HCV-infected patients (but not HBV-infected patients) also exhibited reduced miR-122 levels, which was also confirmed in the study in chimpanzees with chronic HCV or HBV [34]. Further analysis revealed that in a Japanese cohort affected by hepatitis C, lower miR-122 in the non-tumor liver parenchyma correlated with the IL28B rs8099917 TG genotype (linked to poor interferon therapy response) and inversely correlated with interferon-stimulated gene expression. This suggests that reduced miR-122 in chronic HCV may stem from the host’s interferon response, while the distinct miR-122 patterns in HBV- vs. the pathogenesis of HCV-related HCC is likely driven by distinct, virus-specific oncogenic pathways [34]. Besides, another study analysed using microarray analysis, the expression levels of 188 miRNAs were analyzed in HCC liver tissues obtained from a cohort of 26 patients, including 12 with HBV-associated HCC and 14 with HCV-associated HCC. 17 miRNA showed down-regulation in HCC, leading to the activation of cancer-related signaling pathways, for example, cell cycle regulation, cell adhesion, and protein degradation. However, miR-122 expression did not differ significantly between HBV- and HCV-related HCC. Although initial microarray analysis had identified miR-122 as being differentially expressed between chronic hepatitis and HCC patients, this finding was not validated in the ex vivo analysis. The study proposed JAB1, APEX, PARN, DDAH2, and clathrin heavy chain as target genes of miR-122, implicating it in the regulation of base-excision repair, translational initiation, and cellular respiration. Ultimately, a significant difference in miR-122 expression was only confirmed when comparing chronic hepatitis patients to HCC patients, not between the two viral etiology groups [35]. Previous study showed that miR-122 showed the most pronounced upregulation in HCC cells following treatment were conducted with the DNA hypomethylating agent 5-aza-2'-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-CdR) and the histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor 4-phenylbutyric acid (PBA). At the molecular level, study identified that a transcriptional complex consisting of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) as well as retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) binds specifically to the DR1 and DR2 response elements within the miR-122 regulatory region, thereby stimulating miR-122 expression. Treatment with 5-Aza-CdR, in addition to PBA, was found to enhance the binding of this PPARγ/RXRα activator complex, while also diminishing the recruitment of the transcriptional corepressors N-CoR and SMRT to these regulatory elements. A key finding is that the DNA–protein regulatory complex incorporates the histone methyltransferase SUV39H1. The function of this recruitment is to repress miR-122 expression through H3K9 methylation, a well-known repressive histone mark. The distinct ways HBV and HCV regulate miR-122 offer a mechanistic basis for their divergent effects: suppressed miR-122 in HBV-associated HCC versus maintained expression in HCV-related HCC. Consequently, the findings provide a mechanistic basis for the virus-specific downregulation of miR-122, clarifying why it is suppressed in HBV but not in HCV infection [36] (Table 1). In conclusion, the regulation of the tumor-suppressive miR-122 fundamentally showed distinct patterns in HBV-positive versus HCV-positive HCC. While its downregulation in HBV-associated HCC is driven by epigenetic silencing via a specific transcriptional complex, its reduction in chronic HCV infection appears linked to the host's interferon response.

MiR-122 in MAFLD-related HCC

Mice lacking miR-122 (Mir122a–/–) display dysregulated lipid metabolism, evidenced by reduced cholesterol, triglycerides, and MTTP expression, along with lipid accumulation and the development of steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. These mice also exhibit altered expression of cancer-related genes and activation of oncogenic pathways (AKT and MAPK), accompanied by loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN. Consequently, they develop spontaneous HCC with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) features. Reintroduction of miR-122 reversed metabolic defects, fibrosis, and tumor progression. Mechanistically, miR-122 directly targets KLF6, and the miR-122/KLF6 axis has been identified as a central regulator of liver fibrosis and cancer development [8].

In a longitudinal study, researchers monitored 81 Japanese MAFLD patients with repeated liver biopsies over nearly three years to investigate how shifts in serum miR-122 levels were linked to their future risk of cancer and death. While 56.8% had stable fibrosis, 21.0% progressed and 22.2% improved. In the high-risk group with persistent fibrosis (n = 64), those who developed liver cancer (n = 7) had significantly lower miR-122 levels than non-cancer patients (n = 54). An miR-122 ratio < 0.5 was linked to higher cancer incidence and poorer survival. This predictive value was confirmed in patients with advanced fibrosis (stage ≥ 2). The study concluded that monitoring circulating miR-122 dynamics can help predict prognosis in MAFLD patients with persistent severe fibrosis [6]. Supporting the relevance of miR-122 across the MAFLD-to-HCC continuum, a recent extracellular-vesicle (EV) study profiled 168 patients spanning steatosis, steatohepatitis, cirrhosis, and HCC. MiR-122 was differentially partitioned between serum and circulating vesicles across disease stages, being lower in EVs than in serum in MASLD, while EV-associated miR-4758 emerged as a candidate marker detected exclusively in the serum of HCC patients. These findings highlight how the analytical compartment and sample handling can influence miR-122-based biomarker performance along progression to MAFLD-related HCC [37] (Table 1).

Role of miR-122 in colorectal cancer progression

One of the latest studies identified the miR-122/NEGR1 axis as a novel therapeutic and predictive target in colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM). Bioinformatics and functional analyses confirmed the interaction between miR-122 and NEGR1, while in vitro experiments showed that this axis enhanced colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In vivo CRLM mouse models further demonstrated their role in promoting tumor metastasis and macrophage polarization. A significant increase in miR-122 and a concomitant decrease in NEGR1 were found in liver metastases compared to primary tumors. In summary, the miR-122/NEGR1 axis facilitates colorectal cancer-driven progression and liver metastasis through PI3K/AKT pathway activation and the induction of M2 macrophage polarization [38] (Table 1).

Together, it was demonstrated that miR-122 is a key regulator in metabolic regulation, fibrosis, and tumor progression in liver and cancer models. Its dysregulation not only drives hepatic carcinogenesis but also contributes to colorectal cancer liver metastasis (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Despite a strong preclinical rationale, the clinical translation of anti-miR-122 therapy has so far failed, and this experience is instructive. Miravirsen, an LNA-modified antisense oligonucleotide and the first anti-miRNA drug to reach humans, produced dose-dependent, sustained reductions in HCV RNA in phase 2a trials [39], but its development was discontinued after viral resistance emerged through 5′ UTR mutations permitting miR-122-independent replication [40] and, more decisively, after direct-acting antivirals achieved > 95% cure rates and rendered a host-targeted anti-HCV strategy largely redundant. The GalNAc-conjugated antagomir RG-101 achieved marked viral-load reductions, including sustained virological responses after a single dose [41], but was placed on FDA clinical hold in 2016 following cases of severe hyperbilirubinemia, attributed to inhibition of the MRP2 bilirubin transporter together with preferential hepatocyte uptake [42, 43]. Beyond these specific liabilities, a more fundamental concern arises from miR-122's own biology: because miR-122 is a tumor suppressor whose loss drives steatosis, fibrosis, and hepatocarcinogenesis (as in Mir122a–/– mice [8]), sustained systemic suppression carries a theoretical long-term oncogenic risk. This paradox, inhibiting a hepatoprotective, tumor-suppressive miRNA for short-term antiviral benefit, likely constrains the therapeutic window of anti-miR-122 approaches and argues for miR-122 restoration, rather than inhibition, in metabolic and neoplastic liver disease.

This same context-dependence is equally apparent at the level of biomarker data, where several studies have reported seemingly contradictory findings regarding miR-122 expression in MAFLD and MASH. While most studies demonstrate elevated circulating miR-122 levels associated with hepatocellular injury and steatosis severity [2, 11, 12, 14], reduced hepatic miR-122 expression has been observed in advanced fibrosis and progressive liver disease [8, 13]. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in the biological material analyzed, as circulating miR-122 reflects hepatocyte release into the bloodstream following liver injury [2, 18], whereas hepatic miR-122 expression may decline due to disease-associated transcriptional dysregulation [8, 36]. Furthermore, miR-122 expression appears to be influenced by disease stage, with distinct patterns observed across steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis [6, 12, 13]. Variability in patient populations, experimental models, and analytical methodologies may further contribute to the inconsistent findings reported across studies [23, 34].

Therefore, the interpretation of miR-122 expression should take into account both the biological specimen analyzed and the stage of disease progression. Importantly, elevated circulating miR-122 and reduced hepatic miR-122 should not necessarily be considered contradictory findings, as they may reflect distinct biological processes occurring during different stages of disease progression.

Conclusion

MiR-122, the most abundant liver-specific miRNA, is a central regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, viral replication, and tumor suppression, and its dysregulation is implicated across the spectrum of liver disease, from MAFLD/MASH and acute liver injury to HBV/HCV infection, HCC, and colorectal cancer liver metastasis. A unifying theme of this review is that the direction of miR-122 change is context-dependent: hepatic expression declines as disease progresses, whereas circulating levels rise with hepatocyte injury and vary with disease stage. Consideration of this compartment- and stage-dependent behavior reconciles much of the reported variability and is essential for the valid interpretation of miR-122 as a biomarker. Given its high sensitivity to hepatocellular injury but limited disease specificity, miR-122 is likely to achieve optimal diagnostic performance within multi-marker panels and longitudinal assessment rather than as a standalone marker. The etiology-specific regulation of miR-122 in HBV- versus HCV-associated disease further underscores its mechanistic complexity. Importantly, the failed clinical translation of anti-miR-122 inhibitors (miravirsen, RG-101), together with the tumor-suppressor paradox, argues that restoration rather than inhibition of miR-122 is the more rational therapeutic direction outside active HCV infection. Future work should prioritize standardized assays, longitudinal and multi-omics studies, and well-designed trials of miR-122–restoring strategies to fully realize its diagnostic and therapeutic potential.
