Interventional treatment of non-spontaneously healed spontaneous coronary artery dissection: a case report

Introduction

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) occurs when the coronary artery wall separates spontaneously, forming a false lumen that compresses the true lumen and impedes coronary blood flow. This condition arises independently of iatrogenic trauma or atherosclerosis. SCAD is a notable cause of non-atherosclerotic acute coronary syndrome, myocardial infarction, and sudden cardiac death, with approximately 90% of SCAD patients being women aged 47 to 53 years [1, 2]. Coronary angiography remains the primary diagnostic tool for SCAD. Intravascular imaging, recognized for its high spatial resolution, effectively identifies intimal tears, false lumens, and intramural hematomas, thereby facilitating straightforward SCAD diagnosis. In stable patients without high-risk features, conservative management is often advised [3, 4]. Conversely, immediate revascularization should be considered in patients with high-risk characteristics [5]. The primary approach for revascularization is percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) being a secondary option. Although PCI can be challenging and is associated with complications such as iatrogenic dissection and stent malposition [6], it generally improves coronary blood flow and yields favorable mid-term outcomes [5]. This article explores the case of a SCAD patient who experienced severe complications during their initial PCI procedure, highlighting the successful rescue efforts.

Case presentation

The patient is a 49-year-old woman who reported intermittent chest discomfort during physical exertion over the past three months, with symptoms resolving within minutes of rest. Prior evaluation at an external facility revealed moderate stenosis in the proximal segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) via coronary computed tomography angiography. She was referred to our outpatient clinic, where treatment with aspirin and rosuvastatin was initiated; however, her symptoms persisted. The patient had a one-year history of hypertension, managed with oral losartan and hydrochlorothiazide, achieving satisfactory blood pressure control. She also had a history of depression for over a year, though it remained untreated. There was no significant family history of cardiovascular disease. Upon admission, her vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.5 °C, pulse 84 bpm, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min, and blood pressure 125/70 mmHg. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Laboratory tests indicated normal results, with troponin T at 0.005 ng/mL, total cholesterol at 3.03 mmol/L, triglycerides at 1.36 mmol/L, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol at 1.62 mmol/L, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol at 1.13 mmol/L. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and chest computed tomography yielded unremarkable findings. Echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 64% with no segmental wall motion abnormalities. Preoperative medication included aspirin 100 mg daily, clopidogrel 75 mg daily, rosuvastatin 10 mg at bedtime, losartan potassium-hydrochlorothiazide 62.5 mg daily, and metoprolol succinate 47.5 mg daily. Scheduled coronary angiography revealed severe stenosis in the mid to proximal segments of the LAD, moderate to severe stenosis in the distal LAD, and severe stenosis in the distal segment of the first diagonal branch. Given the patient’s profile—middle-aged woman with no significant cardiovascular risk factors apart from hypertension—intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) was performed, revealing dissection, hematoma, and thrombus in the mid LAD, with the hematoma extending into the middle layer and a dissection length of approximately 20 mm, along with a minimum lumen area of 2.48 mm². Due to the instability of the coronary lesion, PCI was performed using a 2.5 × 20 mm semi-compliant balloon (Emerge) was used for dilation at 8 atm. Post-dilation angiography showed extension of the dissection distally and the development of slow flow. The patient experienced severe chest pain, bradycardia, and junctional escape rhythm. Immediate treatment included oxygen, fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, and pain management. Follow-up angiography revealed thrombus in the left main to the proximal LAD. A bolus of tirofiban was administered, followed by continuous peripheral intravenous infusion. A 2.75 × 38 mm stent (Helios) was implanted, and non-compliant balloon dilation was performed using 3.0 × 16 mm (GRIP) and 3.5 × 8 mm (GRIP) balloons. The patient’s blood pressure and heart rate gradually stabilized, and symptoms improved. Follow-up angiography demonstrated TIMI grade 3 flow with no thrombus or dissection (Fig. 1). The patient improved postoperatively and was discharged after a week, with clopidogrel switched to ticagrelor 90 mg bid. Two months later, the patient was readmitted with persistent chest discomfort unrelated to physical activity alleviated by deep breathing, along with low mood. Vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.5 °C, pulse 65 bpm, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min, and blood pressure 128/75 mmHg. Physical examination and laboratory tests, as well as ECG, chest X-ray, and echocardiography, were unremarkable. Suspecting ticagrelor-related chest discomfort, clopidogrel was reinstated at 75 mg daily. Thromboelastography showed an ADP inhibition rate of 70%. Scheduled coronary angiography revealed patency of the previously placed LAD stent, moderate to severe stenosis in the distal segment, and no significant change in the degree of stenosis in the distal diagonal branch compared to two months prior. Although IVUS was not performed, a 2.5 × 23 mm stent (NOYA) was placed in the distal LAD to prevent adverse events. The procedure was successful without the severe complications experienced previously (Fig. 2A and B). The patient was discharged three days later. Eight months later, the patient was readmitted again. Her emotional state had slightly improved, but she continued to experience intermittent chest discomfort. Follow-up angiography (Fig. 3) showed patent LAD stent and no significant changes in the remaining vessels. She was discharged two days later with a recommendation for psychological support. She transitioned to aspirin monotherapy one year after the initial coronary intervention and had not reported any episodes of angina.

Discussion

SCAD is characterized by the separation of coronary artery wall layers due to intramural hemorrhage, with or without associated intimal tear. This condition is unrelated to atherosclerosis, iatrogenic injury, or trauma [2]. Women account for 87–95% of SCAD patients, with an average age of onset ranging from 44 to 53 years [7], In women under 50, SCAD accounts for 25–33% of myocardial infarctions and it is responsible for approximately 15–20% of myocardial infarctions during pregnancy or the perinatal period [2]. Type 2 SCAD is the most common form, accounting for approximately 60–75% of cases diagnosed via coronary angiography, characterized by the absence of an intimal tear and presenting as diffuse arterial narrowing due to intramural hematoma-induced stenosis [2]. Treatment decisions for SCAD are influenced by key differences from atherosclerotic coronary artery disease. First, the underlying pathophysiology of SCAD involves intramural dissection rather than plaque rupture or erosion in an inflammatory and thrombotic milieu [8]. Second, PCI for SCAD is more challenging and associated with inferior short-term and long-term outcomes compared to PCI for atherosclerotic lesions. Most conservatively managed SCAD lesions demonstrate improved coronary flow and reduced stenosis over time, with approximately 95% showing vascular healing within 30 days post-SCAD [9]. Therefore, expert consensuses recommend conservative management for clinically stable SCAD patients over immediate revascularization [3, 10]. For high-risk SCAD patients, several factors must be considered in choosing between medical management and revascularization. High-risk clinical features include persistent chest pain with ongoing or worsening ischemia, hemodynamic instability, shock, or malignant arrhythmias. High-risk anatomical features encompass severe proximal dissection of multiple vessels or dissection involving the left main or LAD ostium [3, 10]. When high-risk features are present, immediate revascularization should be contemplated. A retrospective study categorized left main or proximal coronary artery involvement as high-risk SCAD, while mid, distal, or collateral artery involvement was classified as low-risk SCAD. PCI was predominantly performed in high-risk patients (68.4%), while conservative management was more prevalent in low-risk patients (62.8%). One-year follow-up indicated similar rates of major adverse cardiac events between the two groups; however, the healing rate was higher in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (86.4% vs. 33.3%, p < 0.05), suggesting that lesion anatomy plays a critical role in treatment decision-making [11]. Factors that can elevate the risk of PCI include longer lesions requiring multiple stents [12], T guidewire entry into the false lumen causing vessel occlusion, hematoma extension [13] and poor stent apposition due to hematoma absorption [14]. Techniques to mitigate iatrogenic complications during PCI include preferring femoral access [15], utilizing hydrophilic guidewires [16], initiating with soft guidewires and progressing to stiffer wires as necessary [16], employing intravascular imaging for diagnosis and stent optimization [17], using smaller diameter balloons, considering cutting balloons to create windows in the hematoma to reduce false lumen pressure, using multiple stents, starting with the distal segment, then proximal, and finally mid-segment to prevent hematoma expansion [18], considering longer stents with an additional 5–10 mm coverage at the proximal and distal edges of the dissection [3, 10, 18], avoiding a 1:1 vessel-to-stent size ratio, and opting for optimal apposition and self-expanding stents [18]. The European Society of Cardiology recommends drug-eluting stents for SCAD patients, cautioning against the use of bioresorbable vascular scaffolds due to potentially elevated event rates [3]. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is typically recommended for SCAD patients undergoing PCI for one year, followed by single antiplatelet therapy. The role of statins in SCAD remains inadequately supported by evidence [3, 10].

This case report first presents a patient with SCAD exhibiting a more severe clinical presentation than typical SCAD cases. The patient, a middle-aged woman with hypertension and depression, initially presented with chest discomfort and was diagnosed with proximal LAD stenosis via coronary computed tomography angiography. Three months later, coronary angiography revealed Type 2 SCAD-like lesions in the LAD and diagonal branch with no self-healing noted in the literature. The lesions were more severe than classic Type 2 SCAD, with IVUS showing intimal dissection and thrombus extending into the media. Despite high-risk features, the patient did not experience acute myocardial infarction. During the PCI procedure, although a soft guidewire was initially used, the insufficient understanding of SCAD lesions resulted in the absence of low-pressure dilation balloons or cutting balloons to relieve false lumen pressure. This led to the expansion of the dissection, causing no-reflow, which rapidly progressed to left main thrombosis and hemodynamic collapse. Rapid placement of a long stent, along with supportive medications, restored blood flow and improved the patient’s condition. Follow-up angiography showed no abnormalities in the stented area, though the diagonal branch remained stenosed. PCI for the distal lesion was successful, but without IVUS, atherosclerosis could not be completely excluded. At 8 months follow-up, coronary angiography showed stable stent placement and no new SCAD or complications. Although IVUS after the initial PCI indicated satisfactory stent expansion and apposition, the patient’s large coronary false lumen at baseline posed higher long-term risks of stent malapposition, in-stent thrombosis or in-stent restenosis. Regular follow-up with intravascular imaging would have been warranted to monitor this risk, however, the absence of such follow-up in this case represents a limitation. The patient received DAPT following the initial coronary intervention and transitioned to aspirin monotherapy after one year, which continues to date. The patient has since remained asymptomatic.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates a patient with non-healing Type 2 SCAD complicated by thrombosis, who experienced severe complications but ultimately underwent successful PCI. Large-scale population studies are necessary to further elucidate the mechanisms of SCAD and to refine treatment strategies.
