New insights into beech wood structure through photothermal AFM-IR
Authors: Akpan E. I., Pirro C., Wetzel B.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the polymeric architecture and distribution in wood cell walls is the key to developing efficient treatment and modification methods for both downstream conversion and advanced material development. This paper reports the spatial chemical mapping of aromatic structures of lignin and β-glycosidic linkages of polysaccharides in the middle lamella of beech wood cell walls using high resolution AFM-IR methodology. The study confirms that lignification in the cell walls is highly heterogeneous, with higher lignin concentration in the central middle lamella than the secondary cell walls. Contrary to previous findings, the S1/S2 transition layer may not be a region of depleted lignin content but it is possible that it is a region of complex and differing cellulose chain organisation, as indicated by the lack of difference in C–O–C vibration when IR polarisation was changed from 90° to 0°. Introduction

Wood stands out as the most versatile biological material that can form the basis for a wide range of sustainable products crucial to achieving the global bio-economy targets. In its natural form, wood is suitable for several applications including construction, furniture and everyday products.1 Moreover, through structural engineering, wood is adaptable into advanced materials with functional and multifunctional properties.1–6 Also, wood is an inexhaustible source of novel biopolymers, fuels and chemicals through the deconstruction of the wood cell wall biopolymers including lignin, hemicellulose and cellulose. However, these polymers are physically and chemically interwoven together within the wood cell walls, which makes it recalcitrant to separating or even modifying the wood into the desired new polymers or products. Developing efficient modification or separation techniques requires a very good nanoscale understanding of the wood structure. Specifically, a better understanding of the structural organisation, adhesion and spatial organization of the polymers will inform the development of modification techniques as well as pave the way for the use of biomimicry principles to achieve novel functional materials. Thus, a key challenge in the area of advanced wood processing is the development of methods that enable the in situ probing of the chemical, structural and mechanics of the plant cell wall at the nanoscale.

Although wood structural architecture differs according to type, species, climate and location, the general macrostructure of wood is built from a combination of fibres or tracheid, which are connected by a cementitious layer called the compound middle lamella. The fibres and tracheid are made of three cell wall layers including S1, S2 and S3, whereas the compound middle lamella is made of the middle lamella and a thin primary cell wall, which is mostly indistinguishable from the compound middle lamella. Theoretically, all the cell walls are distinct and different from each other in terms of fibre orientation, composition and concentration. Moreover, the structure and composition of lignin and hemicellulose in the cell walls are also different. The middle lamellar is an important part of the wood cell wall structure because it plays a crucial role in cell wall structural integrity and cell adhesion. Moreover, because it binds the adjacent cell walls together, it is critical to understanding the mechanical failure zone in wood. Furthermore, being the most lignified part of wood, lignin distribution in the middle lamella is a great source of insight in understanding lignin chemical and enzymatic modification and delignification mechanisms.

A variety of imaging techniques have been used to visualise and investigate the content, concentration and distribution of the cell wall biopolymers, including optical light microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, confocal Raman microscopy and atomic force microscopy. However, precise localisation of the cell wall biopolymers with high specificity and spatial resolution requires label free methods that are capable of providing compositional and structural information. Although, non-linear Raman microscopies have been used for chemical imaging of the cell wall biopolymers, it suffers from low resolution. The resolution problem is overcome by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), but chemical imaging in TEM depends on unreliable staining methods, which can be a source of artefacts. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is capable of achieving a spatial resolution of around 20 nm with infrared capability that can reveal chemical composition with high specificity in the nanoscale, making it different from TEM.

Although ample studies on the distribution of cell wall biopolymers in wood have been carried out over the years, key questions such as the nature and distribution of cellulose and lignin in the transition layers are still unanswered. A recent study by Kesari et al.7 using photo induced force microscopy successfully mapped lignin and cellulose in the cell walls of Spruce. However, the mapping suffered from low resolution making the identification of major features very difficult. Moreover, the study used the C–O IR vibrations at 1034, 1060 and 1096 cm−1 to infer that the S1/S2 transition layer is a region of high cellulose content, although these absorption bands are known to be significantly overlapped with lignin and hemicellulose.8 A more recent study by Lee et al.9 reported the use of AFM IR to understand the distribution of cell wall components and cellulose microfibril orientation in a two year old poplar. The study sheds light on the distribution of cell wall biopolymers and microfibril orientation of cellulose but suffered from low resolution and poor surface preparation. Previously, TEM studies by Reza et al.10 suggested that the transition zone is a region of low lignin content leading to the assertion that the low lignin content is the reason for the high propensity to failure. Since the transition zone is a zone of mixed cellulose orientation, the weak interface may be because of changes in cellulose orientation. Moreover, various studies have asserted that concentration of lignin gradually reduces at the transition from the compound middle lamella to S1 layer. This assertion has neither been visualised at a higher resolution nor substantiated. The use of point spectra at nanoscale is relatively unreliable in determining bulk lignin content in the transition layer because the nano tip may be directed at lignin or cellulose substructures giving rise to false localised information. In this paper, we report the spatial mapping of cellulose and lignin in beech wood cell walls at a high spatial resolution to probe the variation of lignin and cellulose in the compound middle lamella, S1, S2 and their transition layers using the AFM-IR methodology. Moving away from using conventional nano-point IR analysis, the paper explores surface mapping of cellulose and lignin vibrations to gain new insights into lignin gradient in the compound middle lamella and the structural difference between lignin in the compound middle lamella and the secondary cell walls. Combining the AFM Tapping Mode technology, which limits the presence of artefacts and cryo-microtome surface preparation technique, we realise compositional maps with limited surface artefacts thereby realising a more plausible mapping of the cell wall components. It goes further to ascertain the existence, size and structure of cellulose and lignin in the S1,2 transition layer by probing the effect of IR polarisation on the beta glycosidic linkages in cellulose and hemicellulose, which is known to be affected by polarisation.

Materials and preparation

Samples (5 × 5 mm2) were obtained from randomly selected beech wood veneer from a commercial wood provider (Atlasholz AG, Germany). The exact radial position was not provided by the manufacturer and could not be determined with certainty. The wood has a density of 0.781 g cm−2 and an average lignin content of 26.15% measured using the Klason lignin method. Sample surface preparation for AFM measurements was carried out by Diatome, Switzerland. The samples were sectioned using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome with a Diatome cryo imuno diamond knife.

Methodology

A Dimension IconIR AFM (Bruker) in AFM-IR Tapping Mode was used for topography and IR-absorption mapping at fixed wavenumbers. In AFM-IR Tapping Mode operation, a pulsed, tuneable and intensity changeable IR-laser (Quantum Cascade Laser) is focused on the sample directly underneath the AFM-tip. The cantilever, which is driven by a piezo actuator, oscillates at the second resonance frequency mode (f2) and intermittently contacts the sample surface. The oscillation amplitude is used as the control variable, which is kept constant during scanning by a feedback loop that immediately adjusts the z-position of the cantilever in response to differences in height. If the amplitude setpoint cannot be reached due to topographical changes, the deviation is recorded in the amplitude error image before the feedback loop adjusts the z-position. Topographical changes are thus recorded in the amplitude error image, while the recording of the adjustment of the z-position ultimately results in the topographical image. The repetition rate fP of the IR-laser is set to the difference between the second and first resonance mode (fP = f2 − f1) of the cantilever. When the wavenumber of the irradiated IR-light matches an absorption band of the sample, a rapid, pulsed thermal expansion occurs, and this photothermal expansion is proportional to the IR-absorption. Due to the nonlinear tip–sample interaction, modulating the cantilever at the driving frequency f2, while the sample expands at frequency fP, the cantilever is excited at the first resonance mode, which results in an amplitude increase.11,12 This detection scheme enables absorption mapping at one fixed wavenumber as well as point spectra recording in the wavenumber range of 1800–750 cm−1 with a spatial resolution of ca. 20 nm and a probe depth of ca. 50 nm.12 Point spectra were obtained as an average of 10 individual scans at each position. A gold-coated silicon cantilever (PR-UM-TNIR-D-10 from Bruker), with a spring constant of 40 N m−1 and a tip radius of 20 nm, was used. The first resonance frequency mode of the cantilever is near 250 kHz, while the second is near 1600 kHz. An amplitude setpoint of 85–150 mV was used as feedback signal for imaging. A laser power of 16% was used for all measurements except 1730 cm−1, where 50% power was used to compensate for the low IR absorption of this band. A conventional FTIR-ATR analysis was performed using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50) with ATR using a diamond crystal. All spectra were collected in absorption mode in the 4000–400 cm−1 wavelength range at 90° polarisation with 32 scans at 2 cm−1 resolution. Only the fingerprint region was analysed. Background correction was applied to all curves.

Results and discussion

Structural characteristics of the middle lamella

Wood is made of elongated hollow tracheid linked together by the middle lamella. The secondary cell walls are organized into distinct three regions including S1, S2 and S3, whereas the middle lamella is indistinguishably connected to the primary cell wall and are mostly identified together as the compound middle lamella (CML). The highly lignified CML serves as the connection between adjacent cell walls and for this must possess strong adhesion to the cell walls. Fig. 1 depicts typical topographic features of the beech cell wall near the CML showing the transition from the CML to the secondary cell walls. Contrary to a recent report13 that the S1 layer is very thin and therefore cannot be measured easily, the S1 layer in the current study is very distinct with a diameter of 300–600 nm. It is evident that the S1 does not show regularity in thickness, but they appear thinner at the intercellular edges and thicker at the cell corners (Fig. 1d and g). The fibre direction in the S1 layer is easily detectable in the topographic and amplitude error images (Fig. 1g) to be nearly parallel to the imaging plane confirming that the micro-fibril angle should be within 45–90° to the neutral axis.14,15 The primary cell wall is generally not noticeable because it is tightly connected to the middle lamella. A few cracks are noticeable from the topographic images. These cracks are mostly between the S1 and S2 layers and minimally between the CML and S1 (see Fig. 1e white arrows).

Mapping of cell wall composition

Several studies have been conducted to understand the chemical distribution of biomass using point IR spectra AFM at different locations of the wood structure.16 These studies offer a lot of insight into the chemical structure of wood but could not show the distribution of these components and their average abundance in each part of the cell structure. To gain more insight into the distribution and variation of the cell wall biopolymers, we employed the AFM-IR to map cellulose and lignin related vibrations within the middle lamella of beech wood cell walls. Firstly, a conventional FTIR-ATR of the beech wood was conducted to identify the approximate positions of the various functional groups related to lignin, hemicellulose and cellulose (see. Fig. 2a). The CC skeletal stretching of the aromatic ring at 1514 cm−1 was chosen as a standard band for mapping lignin in the cell wall. In addition, the CO stretching vibration in conjugated carbonyl of lignin at 1640 cm−1 was used to compare lignin structure at the different cell wall positions. The 1160 cm−1 band is attributed to the C–O–C asymmetric vibrations of the beta-glycosidic bonds of polysaccharides and was therefore used as a standard for the mapping cellulose related vibrations. It should be noted that this band has also contributions from hemicellulose and pectin and should not be treated exclusively as a cellulose marker. Because the 895 cm−1, attributed to the C–O–C bending vibration of cellulose beta glycosidic linkage did not show a well-defined absorption, it did not result in any credible vibrations and therefore was not used in the mapping of cellulose. Because cellulose is the dominant contributor to the 1160 cm−1 band, we choose it to represent cellulose, while keeping in mind that it can be affected by hemicellulose and pectin. The band at 1730 cm−1 was mapped to understand the variation of CO stretching vibration in hemicellulose. However, it should be noted that this band may contain contributions from lignin. Different positions in the cell wall were chosen to map the various vibrations mentioned above. Several scans were performed at various locations and the results compared before conclusions were made.

Fig. 2b and c are the topographic and amplitude error images of the position used to map the presence of lignin and beta-glycosidic linkages. Fig. 2d represent the mapping of 1160 cm−1 a polysaccharide-related (mostly cellulose and hemicellulose) vibration in the cell walls and middle lamella. It is evident that the CML possess less C–O–C asymmetric vibrations signals compared to the secondary cell walls. The absorption is higher in the S2 layer and comparatively less in the S1 layer. A concentric slit can be seen in the S2 layer towards the S3 layer. The 1160 cm−1 seem to have lower absorption towards the S3 layer (Fig. 2d lower region). The 1160 cm−1 originates from the C–O–C asymmetric vibrations of the β-glycosidic bonds of polysaccharides (cellulose and hemicellulose), indicating that cellulose and hemicellulose have higher concentrations in the secondary cell walls than in the CML. Contrary to previous findings that the CML is mostly made of lignin and pectin, our study shows that cellulose and hemicellulose may be present in the CML. This supports the findings of Hafrén et al.17 where a fibrous network was found in the middle lamella after delignification. Mapping at a different location (Fig. 2e) and higher resolution verifies that the β-glycosidic bonds are present in the CML but in a lower concentration compared to S1 and S2 layers. Fig. 2f and g show 1514 cm−1 aromatic stretching vibration of lignin in the cell wall confirming a stronger vibration of lignin in the CML than in the secondary cell wall layers. This is consistent with the findings of Takada et al.,18 where lignin absorption in the cell walls was found to be one third lower than in the CML. The average width of the structures showing the strong lignin vibration in Fig. 2f correlates to the width of the CML and that of the low cellulose content in Fig. 2e.

A higher resolution at a different position (Fig. 2g) confirms that the CML is highly lignified in relation to the secondary cell walls. An additional mapping for lignin was performed at 1640 cm−1, which is attributed to the CO band for lignin (Fig. 2h). The conjugated carbonyl vibration shows an overall lower absorption when compared to 1514 cm−1. This may be attributed to relative abundance of the CC bands in lignin compared to the CO, which is theoretically lower than the aromatic chains in native lignin. Moreover, the carbonyl vibration mapping (Fig. 2h) shows a marked difference from the CC mapping in terms of distribution especially within the secondary cell walls. Irrespective of the generally low absorption of the 1640 cm−1 band across the cell walls, its absorption in the secondary cell walls is relatively abundant than the absorption of 1514 cm−1. It should be carefully observed here that the absorption intensity between the two bands from two different scanning cannot be compared because it can be affected by topography and scanning conditions. The 1640 cm−1 absorption band arises mainly from conjugated carbonyl (CO) vibrations from lignin side chains and conjugated side-chain CC stretch originating from oxidized lignin structures such as coniferaldehyde,19 sinapaldehyde and quinone methide intermediates. It can also arise from the esters and ether linkages connecting the lignin carbohydrate complexes (LCC). Based on this spatial spectral difference, we suggest that the higher relative prevalence of 1640 cm−1 in the S2 layer compared to 1514 cm−1 may come from the fact that lignin in the S2 layer are more chemically modified with higher possibility of incorporation of syringyl units and oxidized structures.20,21 Moreover, the LCC are more prevalent in the S2 layer because of the extensive hemicellulose bonding, which gives rise to the presence of ester and ether linkages, hence the relative abundance of conjugated structures.

The point spectrum in Fig. 3 confirms the variation of lignin and C–O–C asymmetric vibrations across the cell walls. Spectrum 1, 2, 3, 11, 12 and 13 corresponding to the S2 layer show similar absorption across the finger print region. These spectra show high 1160 cm−1 and low lignin related (1506, 1595, and 1640 cm−1) absorption confirming that the S2 layer has high beta glycosidic linkages (cellulose realted) compared to lignin related vibrations. The C–O bands in the 1100–1020 cm−1 region are clustered with no distinct separation of the specific bands. The S1 layer comprising of spectrum 4, 8, 9 and 10 show a slight lower 1160 cm−1 absorption intensity compared to the S2 layer but similar lignin vibrations (1506, 1595, and 1640 cm−1). The middle lamella represented by spectra 6 shows higher lignin absorption at 1506, 1595 and 1640 cm−1 compared to the spectra in S1 and S2 layers. Notably, the stretching vibration of C–O (ether or phenolic OH) in syringyl (S) and guaiacyl (G) units at 1224 cm−1 is well resolved in this spectra contrary to the S1 and S2 regions where they appeared crowded and overlapped. The 1224 cm−1 vibration in the S1 and S2 layers are crowded because of the complex mixture of cellulose and hemicellulose with lignin. However, the absorption in the CML is less crowded because of the presence of a lower amount of polysaccharides making the band to be well resolved and distinct.22 The 1160 cm−1 absorption is significantly lower in the middle lamella. The intensity starts reducing with spectra 5 and almost disappears in spectra 6 but starts increasing again in spectra 7, corresponding to the findings in Fig. 2. Similarly, the C–O band cluster in the 1100–1020 cm−1 region becomes well resolved in the CML than in the S1 and S2 layers probably due to the absence of the complex composition of cellulose and hemicellulose.

Evidence of lignin gradient in the CML

To understand more about the distribution of lignin within the CML, IR scans at 1514, 1160 and 1640 cm−1 were performed at a higher resolution within the CML and the S1/S2 interface. A small portion (Fig. 4a white square) at the cell corners was chosen for the measurement. The topographic image shown in Fig. 4b confirms that the CML is not a layered fibrilla structure as the S1 and S2 layers. It is likely that the lignin within the CML coats the fibrous cellulose so that they do not appear visible in the topographic image. Mapping the 1160 cm−1 C–O–C vibration shows that the polysaccharide related chains are dominant in the cell walls but lower in the CML with a distinct transition zone at the S1/S2 (S1,2) and S1/CML interface. In Fig. 4c, the S1,2 zone is easily detectable in the upper part of the image but very small in the lower part showing heterogeneity in the structural arrangements and the size of the transition layer. The polysaccharide chain shows a gradient at the S1/CML interface. Fig. 4d shows that the aromatic lignin structure is very prominent in the CML and has a distinct gradient structure across the CML/S1 interface, which is also evident in Fig. 4e. The lignin gradient may be related to the fact that lignin is first polymerized at the cell corners and the CML creating a lignin sink in these areas before polymerisation in the cell walls. The early-polymerized deposits are essentially made of highly condensed lignin structures. We found in the previous section that the aromatic ring structure is the prevalent lignin structure in the middle lamella. The steep gradient of the lignin aromatic structure as it moves from the CML to the other part of the cell structures is evident in Fig. 4e. It should be noted that the conjugated carbonyl (Fig. 4f and g) vibration did not show a distinct gradient compared to the aromatic ring vibration although it shows a higher vibration in the CML than in the cell walls. This can be attributed to the relatively low concentration of the conjugated side chains in the cell walls making it difficult to capture a gradient. A closer look at the topography of the CML/S1 interface (Fig. 5a and d) shows that the S1 layer is made up of about 5–6 lamellae (following the white dotted line) that are 30–50 nm thick contrary to the CML, which shows no evidence of a lamella structure. Fig. 5b and c shows that the lignin content is relatively constant within the CML and within the S1 layer with a steep gradient at the CML/S1 transition layer. Fig. 5e shows that the C–O–C content in the CML is higher than at the transition, indicating either a low polysaccharide content or a difference in chain orientation. Because the 1160 cm−1 absorption band is parallel with the plane of polarisation of linearly polarised light, it is very sensitive to changes in chain orientation, making the later the more plausible reason for the observed reduced absorption. The transition layer is a region where changes in cellulose orientation is highest, which may partly account for irregularities and increased porosity within the region. However, as shown in the topographic image, there is a clear distinction between the CML and the S1 layer indicating that the binding at this interface is mostly from lignin. The C–O–C bands within the CML appear as discontinuously distributed spots different from the S1 layer, which shows a continuous pattern following the lamella structure. The red arrows show regions of scanning artefacts.

To examine the presence of a transition layer with lower lignin content at the S1/S2 transition zone, high resolution scanning was performed at the S1/S2 interface. Fig. 6 shows the variation of lignin and cellulose related vibrations at the S1/S2 interface. Topographically, there is a distinct transition zone from the S1 layer to the S2 layer (Fig. 6b upper) similar to the CML/S1 transition zone (Fig. 5d lower). However, the IR scan of the aromatic units of lignin in this region does not show any large difference at the transition region (Fig. 6c and d). The signal is relatively constant until 500 nm across the ROI (Fig. 6c white scale bar) and steadily increased between 500 and 850 nm but remained constant afterwards. We did not see any distinct difference in lignin content at the S1/S2 transition zone as proposed by some publications.10,23 However, looking at the C–O–C vibration of the β-1,4 glycosidic linkages (Fig. 6f–h), there is a distinct change in 1160 cm−1 absorption at the S1/S2 and S1/CML transition zones. This distinct change in absorption may be a direct change in β-glycosidic bonds concentration in these zones or a change in orientation or structure. Such distinct change in the IR absorption in this region could easily be dismissed as a result of topographic effect on the IR signals. However, a closer look at the topography – IR overlay (Fig. 5) of both 1514 cm−1 and 1160 cm−1 shows that the absorption did not follow a particular topographic pattern. For example, the regions marked with red circles in Fig. 6f are of widely differing topography but they both show similar IR absorption in Fig. 6g. Similarly, regions marked with white circles are regions of similar topography but they show very different IR absorption. Because topography influence is not the case, it is safe to say that the distinct change in absorption of the 1160 cm−1 band at the transition zone is either due to reduced polysaccharide content or local changes in conformation and arrangement.

This finding directly contradicts the notion that transition layers are spots of high cellulose contents.16,24,25 Some researchers have shown that the change in fibrils orientation between the S1 and S2 does not occur abruptly, but gradually,25 indicating that the region should be a region of both changes in orientation and concentration. As stated earlier, the 1160 cm−1 absorption band is parallel to plain polarised light making it very sensitive to changes in chain orientation and can be used to check changes in cellulose orientation.26 Also, studies have shown that fibril organisation in the secondary cell walls gradually changes from S-helix in the S1 to a Z-helix in the S2 layer,10 supporting the idea that the S1/S2 transition layer is a chemically complex environment rather than a uniformly low lignin or high cellulose content region. A further look at the 1514 cm−1 vibration away from the cell corners did not show any region of distinct difference in vibration at the S1/S2 transition zone (Fig. 7a–c), reinforcing the idea that the zone may not necessarily be made of depleted lignin. Since the studies supporting the existence of a transition zone with depleted lignin content was based entirely on contrast differences in stained TEM imaging of the cell walls, we assume that the inference may have resulted from topographic effects or staining artefacts rather than reflecting actual variations in lignin content. Mapping of 1160 cm−1 at the cell corners shows a distinct S1,2 transition layer with low absorption (Fig. 7d–f) similar to that shown from a different area in Fig. 6. Moving away from the cell corners, the transition layer is still distinct but smaller in size than at the cell corners (Fig. 7g–i). Fig. 5–7 confirms the existence of the transition layer with a reduced absorption of polysaccharides 1160 cm−1 but no variation in aromatic lignin structures from the 1514 cm−1 absorption.

To amplify these findings, point spectra were performed at a high resolution close to the S1/S2 transition layer as shown in Fig. 8. Spectral points were chosen to include S2 (15–17), S1,2 (12–14), S1 (8–11), S1,cml (2, 6) and CML (3–5) regions. A closer look at the C–O stretching vibration in the 1020–1100 cm−1 region shows the difference in the chemical nature of the various zones. The 1086 and the 1035 cm−1, which appeared as a cluster in spectrum 1 (S1 layer) gradually became resolved from spectrum 2 (S1,2) reaching the highest in spectrum 5 (CML) and then gradually loses the resolution until spectrum 10. It is apparent that these zones possess less amount of the beta glycosidic linkages as evident in the 1160 cm−1 C–O–C vibration so that the lignin-specific aromatic vibrations at 1035 and 1085 cm−1 become distinct and well-resolved peaks. A similar order is seen in the 1160 cm−1, where the CML (3–5) spectra show low absorption compared to those in the S1, S1,2 and S2. Noticeably, the 1160 cm−1 in the S1,2 transition zone shows a gradual but minimal difference in the absorption intensity supporting the idea that the transition zone may be a region of low cellulose concentration. Another notable difference is within the cluster of bands in the 1200–1280 cm−1. The band cluster usually include bands from lignin syringyl units (1232 cm−1) and guaiacyl (1270 cm−1) aromatic ring breathing, along with C–O stretching (1280 cm−1) of lignin, but overlapped with xylan pyranose ring vibrations (1254 cm−1). Spectra within the middle lamella and the S1,cml transition zone show well resolved lignin bands with marked differences in peak positions, indicating that the region has less overlapping hemicellulose bands resulting in the appearance of the lignin specific vibrations. Moreover, spectra within the transition zones differ slightly from those in the S1 and S2 layers. Obviously, the lignin aromatic ring vibrations at 1507 and 1596 cm−1 show higher absorption in spectra 4, 5, 6 and 7, which are within the middle lamella and the transition zone, confirming the results of the chemical maps presented in the previous section. It should be noted that the point spectra show slight difference between the C–O–C asymmetric vibrations absorption between the S1,2 transition zone and the S2 layer, supporting that the transition zone may be a layer of low polysaccharide content or difference in structure.

An attempt was made to understand the reason for the low cellulose absorption in the transition zone by performing an IR scanning at 0° polarisation. Since the 1160 cm−1 absorption band is sensitive to the polarization of incident linearly polarized IR light,24,27 changing the polarisation of the IR source should detect changes in cellulose conformation. For the measurement, a new calibration of the laser position was performed and it was noted that the 0° polarisation shows low IR signals across the wavelength and therefore could be sensitive to topographic changes. Fig. 9 shows the topography and IR maps of 1160 cm−1 in the 0° polarisation. A first glance at the results shows that the S1,cml transition layer does not present any noticeable difference in the 1160 cm−1 absorption intensity. This is a first indication that the low 1160 cm−1 absorption in the transition layer when mapped using the 90° polarisation is caused by the change in cellulose chain orientation along the transition zone. The maps also show high 1160 cm−1 absorption at the cell wall boundaries, which may be attributed to topographic changes and not necessarily a difference in absorption.

Hemicellulose and guaiacyl/syringyl lignin marker vibrations and suspected topographic effects

An attempt was made to map the 1315 cm−1, 1730 and 1260 cm−1 bands at the same position, under one calibration period and using the same scan parameters except for the band at 1730 cm−1 where the IR emission was increased to 50% compensate for the low signals of this band. The 1315 cm−1and 1260 cm−1 are frequently assigned to the aromatic-ring breathing of syringyl with overlapping cellulose vibration and guaiacyl-ring breathing coupled with C–O/CO stretching vibrations respectively, and the 1730 cm−1 is usually assigned to ester carbonyl CO band mostly from acetylated hemicellulose. The band may also contain vibrations from esterified pectin. Fig. 10 shows the mapping of these bands at the same position as 1160 and 1514 cm−1. The maps of 1160 and 1514 cm−1 remain the same as reported earlier with a clear distinction between the CML and the other cell wall layers. The lignin related 1514 cm−1 (Fig. 10b) is prominent in the CML than in the other cell wall layers. The gradient is easily detectable and the transition layers are very clear. Despite the S2 layer on the right and the S2 layer on the left having distinct topographic difference (see. Fig. 10a), a line section of the amplitude values did not show any significant difference in the IR absorption between both areas. Similarly, the 1160 cm−1 band relating to the C–O–C of polysaccharides in Fig. 10c show a clear distinction between the cell wall layers as reported in the earlier section. It can be clearly seen that despite the cutting lines run from left to right across the image, the IR absorption of the S1 layers distinctively follow the S1 lamella structures and the CML appear as localised but disconnected spots. A closer look at the S2 layers on both sides shows that despite the difference in topography the absorption map is not significantly different. It can be easily seen that the C–O–C units are more pronounced in the S2 layers compared to the S1 layers and the middle lamella.9 The 1730 cm−1 in Fig. 10d shows that the absorption is fairly higher in the CML and S1 layers than in the S2 layers. This could be as a result of the presence of partially esterified homogalacturonan in the middle lamella or that the middle lamella contains a higher amount of acetylated hemicellulose7 compared to the other cell wall layers.28 A few structural artefacts can be seen on the surface but the map did not show any extensive topographic effect. In the point spectra presented in the previous section, it is apparent that the 1260 cm−1 becomes separated from the cluster of bands within the middle lamella. It was therefore necessary to map the vibration across the cell walls. Fig. 10e shows the vibration map of the 1260 cm−1 band. Corroborating the point spectra, the scan shows high intensity of this band in the middle lamella and S1 layers but because it is largely affected by topography, it is difficult to conclude its behaviour in the S2 layer. We assume that the overlapping nature of the band in the S1 and S2 layers does not allow it to be properly mapped but as the concentration increases in the middle lamella it becomes well resolved in the CML, separating into a distinct band. This indicates that the fraction of the 1260 cm−1 component in the CML is higher than in the S1 and S2, where it has a relatively low concentration.

A careful comparison of the upper S2 layer of Fig. 10e and d shows that the topographic effect is a lot pronounced in the 1260 cm−1 band than the 1730 cm−1. Hence, the high absorption in the S2 layer following the cutting lines are truly topographic effects and may not be true IR absorption.

Fig. 11 illustrates this effect at other locations compared with 1514 cm−1 maps taken from the same ROI. Comparing Fig. 11b and c shows that the IR maps of the 1260 cm−1 are highly affected by the topography than the IR maps of the 1514 cm−1. Moving to a different location (Fig. 11e and f), the same effect can be seen. It is important to note that this effect occurs despite the higher IR response of this vibration (reaching 2.7 mV) compared to 1514 cm−1 (1.3 mV). Similar effect is also noted in the map of 1315 cm−1 (Fig. 12), which is typically assigned to aromatic vibration associated with syringyl ring breathing with C–O/CO-coupled stretching and guaiacyl ring vibrations substituted at C5.29–31 The band is also overlapped by polysaccharides and may not be a distinct G-lignin band. The reason for the high effect of topography on these bands are currently not known but we posit that, bands with overlapping vibrations are more prone to topographic effects than bands with distinct vibrations such as 1514 cm−1 aromatic CC vibrations. A more extensive study is required before a proper conclusion can be made. Despite the high topographic effect of the 1315 cm−1 band, it is clear from the maps that the functional group or groups occur more in the middle lamella than in the S1 and S2 layers. This is supported by the point spectra in Fig. 3 and 8 where the IR absorption of the band was higher in the CML than other regions. Fig. 12d–f shows a higher resolution of the IR maps. It is clear that this vibration is higher in the CML than in other regions and at the cell corners. The localisation at the cell corners was earlier reported using Immunogold labelling.32 The authors found that condensed lignin subunits were localized in the cell-corner in developing poplar xylem.

Conclusion

The results presented in this paper advances the use of AFM-IR to efficiently and correlatively map the chemical composition of wood at spatial resolutions of around 20 nm for a better understanding of the wood structure and offer insights and guidance towards functionalisation and modification of wood towards advanced applications. Our results suggest that surface mapping of functional groups offer better insights than point spectra because point spectra cannot account for local changes in the functional groups. Significantly, we have observed that the transition layers may not be regions of depleted lignin content as supposed by studies using TEM and Raman spectroscopy, but a chemically complex environment with mixed chain orientations. The paper also clearly illustrates the need to understand how the orientation of cellulose chains affects IR absorption and its application to understanding the cell wall composition. The presence of lignin gradient across the central middle lamella is ascertained and properly illustrated towards a better understanding of wood's central middle lamella. Mapping of different lignin functional groups indicates that the structure of lignin in the different cell walls may be significantly different, exposing the need for further experiments. As a sustainable resource, wood and wood waste should not remain a material for bio energy recovery and paper making, but a material of choice for the development of high-performance materials through biotechnological modification and functionalisation. The development of novel methods for such applications requires a good understanding of wood's chemical structure, where the presented solution has a key capability.
