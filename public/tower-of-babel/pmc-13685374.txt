High xCT expression indicates poor prognosis and chemoradiotherapy resistance in rectal cancer patients
Authors: Yingru Li, Heng LiuFu, Zhilong Yuan, Wenchang Gan, Shaoyong Peng, Jinhong Li, Haiyang Xin, Canfeng Cai, Bing Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundxCT/SLC7A11, an amino acid transporter, is implicated in various cancers. However, its role in rectal cancer remains unclear. This study aimed to explore its predictive value for prognosis and chemoradiotherapy response in rectal cancer patients.Methods and materialsxCT expression was assessed using immunohistochemistry and RT-qPCR, and its associations with clinicopathological features and prognosis were analyzed. Single-cell RNA sequencing data and the TIMER platform were used to examine xCT’s correlation with immune cell infiltration. A prognostic nomogram model was developed and evaluated with ROC, calibration, and decision curve analysis. Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity assays were conducted to assess xCT’s role in ferroptosis and chemoradiotherapy sensitivity.ResultsxCT expression was elevated in rectal cancer tissues and correlated with adverse clinicopathological features, including tumor regression grade (P < 0.001). Single-cell and immune infiltration analyses indicated that xCT was associated with the immune microenvironment. High xCT expression predicted worse overall and disease-free survival, and multivariate analysis confirmed it as an independent prognostic factor. The xCT-based nomogram accurately predicted 1- and 3-year survival rates. Additionally, xCT knockout inhibited cell proliferation and enhanced chemoradiotherapy sensitivity in vitro and in vivo by promoting ferroptosis.ConclusionHigh expression of xCT was associated with poor prognosis and chemoradiotherapy response in rectal cancer, representing a potential prognostic marker and therapeutic target.

Introduction

Rectal cancer remains a significant global health challenge, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1–4]. Standard treatment for rectal cancer typically includes surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. For locally advanced rectal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) is a widely used treatment. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines list nCRT as a standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer, as it has been proven to not only reduce tumor staging but also increase sphincter preservation rates and reduce the risk of local recurrence [5–8]. Despite these standard treatments, the prognosis for rectal cancer patients remains uncertain. Approximately 20% 30% of rectal cancer patients exhibit poor sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy, which leads to resistance, tumor progression, and metastasis during treatment and results in a poor prognosis [9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify new prognostic markers and therapeutic targets.

xCT/SLC7A11, a specific amino acid transporter, is a key component of the system xc-. Its function involves capturing and transporting extracellular cystine into cells and then converting it into glutathione (GSH). GSH participates in protein synthesis and maintains the cellular redox state, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress-induced cell death [10–13]. Accumulating evidence has suggested an increasingly important role for the cystine/glutamate transporter system xc- in cell growth, proliferation, metastasis, and multidrug resistance in multiple cancer types [14–17]. Additionally, xCT is a key regulatory protein in ferroptosis and is closely related to the process of ferroptosis. The downregulation of xCT expression, by inhibiting the cysteine metabolic pathway, leads to decreased intracellular cysteine levels and the depletion of GSH, causing the accumulation of lipid peroxides and eventually inducing ferroptosis [18]. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell death that is distinct from other forms of cell death, such as apoptosis and necrosis [19, 20]. Increasing evidence suggests that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in cancer biology, particularly in terms of cancer cell sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy [21, 22].

However, the role of xCT in the prognosis and chemoradiotherapy response of rectal cancer patients has not been fully elucidated. Our study focused on investigating the clinical role of xCT in the prognosis of rectal cancer patients. Additionally, we also evaluated the potential effect of xCT expression on chemoradiotherapy response. Therefore, the results of this study may provide a theoretical foundation for improving the prognosis of rectal cancer patients and developing more effective treatment strategies for rectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Patient eligibility and rectal cancer tissue samples

We established a cohort of 78 patients who underwent radical rectal cancer surgery. All patients were diagnosed with rectal adenocarcinoma pathologically, and no evidence of distant metastases was detected through computed tomography (CT) scans. The cancer tissue samples were collected before the patients received nCRT. MSI-H/dMMR patients were excluded. In addition, 34 histologically normal colorectal mucosal tissues obtained from separate surgical specimens were included as normal controls for immunohistochemical analysis. A comprehensive follow-up was conducted after surgery to assess treatment outcomes. The research protocol was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and the guidelines set forth by the Ethics Review Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (2022ZSLYEC-137).

Recurrence and metastasis were systematically monitored through colonoscopies and enhanced computed tomography scans. Data for follow-up was gathered through outpatient visit and telephone follow-up. Disease-free survival (DFS) is defined as the time span from the surgical procedure to either the point of cancer recurrence or the last recorded observation. Overall survival (OS) refers to the duration from the surgical resection date to either the survival endpoint or the final follow-up consultation [23].

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy regimen

The mFOLFOX6-radiotherapy treatment protocol was adopted [24]. Patients were administered preoperative chemotherapy consisting of five cycles. Each cycle included 400 mg/m² leucovorin intravenously, followed by 400 mg/m² fluorouracil intravenously and a 48-hour continuous infusion of 2.4 g/m² fluorouracil, with 85 mg/m² oxaliplatin intravenously administered on the first day of each cycle. Concurrent radiotherapy was delivered during cycles 2 to 4 (46.0–50.4 Gy). Postoperative treatment involved seven cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy, following the same regimen.

Tumor regression grade (TRG)

TRG is a histopathological assessment used to categorize the extent of tumor response to neoadjuvant treatment before surgical resection. In our study, the AJCC/CAP TRG category was assessed by two pathologists based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP) regression grading [25, 26]. The four categories of AJCC/CAP TRG were as follows: complete response (TRG 0), no viable cancer cells remaining; moderate response (TRG 1), only a small cluster or individual cancer cells remaining; minimal response (TRG 2), residual cancer remaining but predominantly fibrotic; poor response (TRG 3), minimal or no destruction of the tumor, with extensive residual cancer.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), the tissue microarray sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in ethanol. Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 10 mmol/L citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave. Then, the sections were incubated in 10% goat serum for 30 min, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with xCT/SLC7A11 Rabbit mAb (12691 S, CST, 1:100). The following day, the slides were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. Photomicrographs were taken after treating the sections with a DAB Detection Kit (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO).

The staining results were analyzed by two pathologists using a blinded coding system. The staining intensity of the sections was categorized as no staining (0), weak staining (1), moderate staining (2), or strong staining (3). The percentage of positive cells was scored as follows: 0, < 5%; 1, 5–25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; and 4, > 75%. The final IHC score (0–12) was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by the percentage of positive cells for each patient [27]. High expression was defined as an IHC score of 6 or higher.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data was obtained from the publicly available dataset GSE146771, generated using the Smart-seq2 platform. A standardized analysis pipeline was applied to the datasets, including quality control, clustering, cell type annotation, and data curation.

Differential gene expression and pathway analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted to evaluate xCT expression across identified clusters. Violin plots were generated to visualize expression patterns. Enrichment of the Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) pathway was assessed using the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) tool, mapped onto UMAP space to localize pathway activity across cell types.

Analysis of immune infiltration

TIMER platform was used for analysis of immune cell infiltration across various cancer types. It facilitates the comparison of gene expression levels between tumor and normal tissues, as well as the assessment of immune cell infiltration levels within tumors. In our study, we examined the relationship between xCT expression and the abundance of various infiltrating immune cells, including B cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages.

Screening of risk factors and establishment of a prognostic model

Univariate Cox regression was initially employed to screen clinical and pathological factors associated with prognosis in R software (version 4.4.3). Backward stepwise regression was utilized to refine the selection of factors for the final prognostic model construction. The selected factors were visualized using a nomogram created with the “regplot” package in R, allowing for a graphical representation of patients’ survival prognosis risk. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). A calibration curve was employed to compare the nomogram’s predicted probabilities with actual probabilities, assessing the model’s calibration. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was then conducted to determine the model’s clinical utility and its effectiveness in aiding clinical decision-making.

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher). The cDNA was synthesized using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (FSQ-201, TOYOBO). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Real-time PCR Master Mix (QPK-201B, TOYOBO) on a LightCycler 480 (Roche, Switzerland). The expression of xCT were standardized relative to internal control gene GAPDH expression through the application of the 2−ΔΔCT method [28]. The sequences of primers used for xCT were 5′- CAG CTT TTG TAC GAG TCT GGG-3′ (forward) and 5′- CGC AAG TTC AGG GAT TTC ACA-3′ (reverse). The sequences of primers used for GAPDH were 5′- GAG TCA ACG GAT TTG GTC GT-3′ (forward) and 5′- GAC AAG CTT CCC GTT CTC AG-3′ (reverse).

Cell culture

The HCT116 and SW837 cell lines used in this study were purchased from ETHEPHON (Shanghai, China). Both cell lines were cultured in DMEM (C11995500CP, ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% Vivacell Certified Fetal Bovine Serum (C04001-500, Vivacell) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Viral packaging and infection

To generate xCT/SLC7A11 knockout (KO) cell lines, we constructed the LentiCRISPRv2-sgxCT plasmid by inserting the sgRNA fragment into the lentiCRISPR vector (Addgene, 52961) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences for the sgRNAs targeting xCT were as follows: xCT-sgRNA#1 (Sense: CAC CGA AGT ATT ACG CGG TTG CCA C; Antisense: AAA CGT GGC AAC CGC GTA ATA CTT C) and xCT-sgRNA#2 (Sense: CAC CGG TGT CCA CCA TCT CCA AAG G; Antisense: AAA CGC CTT TGG AGA TGG TGG ACA C). The plasmid was co-transfected into HEK-293T cells along with psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene, 12259) and cultured for 48 h. The culture medium was then collected as virus, supplemented with polybrene (10 μg/ml), and incubated with HCT116 and SW837 cells for an additional 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were selected with puromycin to generate stable cell lines.

Cell cytotoxicity assay and clonogenic survival assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5,000 cells per well. After 24 h, the cells were exposed to a gradient of 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu) at concentrations ranging from 2 µmol/L (21 µM) to 256 µmol/L (28 µM). Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0037, Beyotime); absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The percentage of cell survival in the treated samples was normalized against that in the control group. IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (San Diego, California).

For colony formation assays, HCT116 or SW837 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After treatment with 5-Fu and/or exposure to ionizing radiation (IR), the cells were incubated for 2 weeks without disturbance to allow for colony formation. The cells were then fixed with methanol for 10 min and stained with crystal violet (C8470, Solarbio). Colonies were counted using ImageJ software, and the results were plotted to generate survival fractions using GraphPad Prism.

Xenograft tumor model

Male BALB/c nude mice (3–4 weeks) were purchased from BesTest (Zhuhai, China) for ectopic tumor model construction. To establish subcutaneous tumor models for evaluating the role of xCT knockout in tumor growth and treatment response, HCT116 wild-type (WT) cells and xCT knockout (xCT-KO) cells (1 × 106 cells/100uL) were injected into the flanks of the mice. The mice were divided into four groups (n = 4/group) based on the type of cells injected and whether irradiation (IR) was administered. Tumor size was measured every five days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume = (length × width2) / 2. Once the tumors reached a size of approximately 100 mm3, the IR groups were treated with 2 Gy X-rays focused on the tumor area for five consecutive days. When the experiment reached 30 days, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the xenograft tumors were excised and photographed for further analysis.

For nude mice-related experiments, all studies complied with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

Lipid peroxidation assay

The Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (BL904A, Biosharp) was used to analyze malondialdehyde (MDA) production according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HCT116 or SW837 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. After treatment with 5-Fu or IR, the cells were cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, samples were collected following the guidelines provided. The MDA-TBA adduct was quantified by colorimetry at 535 nm using a spectrophotometer. The MDA content in the treated groups was normalized to that in the control group and is expressed as a percentage. The results shown are representative of three independent experiments.

Lipid ROS assay

Lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using the C11-BODIPY 581/591 probe (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions as previously reported [29]. Briefly, cells were collected after the indicated treatments and washed with PBS. The cells were then incubated with 2 µM C11-BODIPY 581/591 at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS to remove unbound probe, detached with trypsin, collected, and resuspended in PBS. The fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry to evaluate lipid ROS levels, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells were collected and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS at 4 °C for 24 h. After washing with 0.1 M PBS, the samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, stained with uranyl acetate, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were then prepared and examined using a transmission electron microscope. Mitochondrial ultrastructural changes, including mitochondrial size, morphology, and cristae structure, were evaluated.

Measurement of glutathione content

The total glutathione content in the cells was determined using a Glutathione Assay Kit (S0052, Beyotime). HCT116 or SW837 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. Following treatment with 5-Fu or IR, the cells were cultured for 24 h. Samples were collected and processed following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the absorbance at 412 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer. The total glutathione content in the treated groups was normalized to that in the control group and is expressed as a percentage. The results shown are representative of three independent experiments.

Measurement of Fe2+ content

The Fe2+ content in the cells was determined using a Ferrous Iron Content Assay Kit (BC5410, Solarbio). HCT116 or SW837 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured in the appropriate medium. Following treatment with 5-Fu or IR, the cells were incubated for 24 h, then collected and lysed using an ultrasonic crusher. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and the absorbance was measured at 593 nm. The Fe2+ content in the treated groups was normalized to that in the control group and is expressed as a percentage. The results shown are representative of three independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0) and R software (version 4.4.3). The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test was used to compare the categorical data between groups. The independent-sample t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between groups. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to compare means among three or more groups. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess the interaction between two independent categorical variables on a continuous outcome. Post-hoc tests were performed when appropriate to identify significant differences between groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were applied using the “survival” package in R to identify factors influencing patient survival. Significant variables from the univariate analysis were further analyzed in a multivariate model to account for their combined effects. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated with the “survminer” package to visualize survival probabilities over time, and the log-rank test was used to compare differences between groups. For all the statistical tests, a significance level of p < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

xCT is overexpressed in rectal cancer and associated with TRG

A comprehensive pan-cancer analysis from TCGA database indicated that xCT expression levels were upregulated in most cancer types, including rectal cancer (Fig. 1A). Additionally, xCT promoter methylation levels were significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, suggesting a potential mechanism for the upregulation of xCT expression in rectal cancer (Fig. 1B). As shown in supplementary Fig. 2, analysis of the E-MTAB-12862 from ArrayExpress dataset revealed significantly higher xCT expression in rectal cancer tissues (n = 281) compared to normal tissues (n = 35). To validate these findings, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on rectal cancer samples from our cohort (Fig. 1C). Based on staining intensity, the samples were categorized as follows: no staining, weak staining, moderate staining, or strong staining. The results indicated enhanced xCT staining in rectal cancer samples (Fig. 1D), and the difference in staining intensity distribution was statistically significant (P = 0.0008; Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, we found that tumors with higher IHC scores were significantly related to lower TRG levels (Fig. 1E). We next conducted an RT-qPCR assay to measure xCT expression in 20 rectal cancer tissue samples and corresponding normal mucosal tissue samples and high expression of xCT in rectal cancer was confirmed (Fig. 1F). The Sankey diagram showed that patients with low scores had better TRG and prognosis (Fig. 1G). These findings suggested that high expression of xCT in rectal cancer tissues was associated with poor response to nCRT.

Relationship between xCT expression and clinicopathologic characteristics

To further explore the clinical relevance of xCT expression in rectal cancers, we divided the samples into a low-expression group (n = 37) and a high-expression group (n = 41) according to the IHC scores. The Chi-square test or Fisher exact probability test were used to analyze the correlation between xCT protein expression and the clinicopathological characteristics of the rectal cancer patients. The results showed a significant association between high xCT expression and higher TRG levels (P < 0.001). Additionally, tumor tissues with high expression of xCT were associated with high CEA levels (P = 0.009), advanced T stage (P = 0.024), advanced N stage (P < 0.001), positive venous invasion (P = 0.005), and poor differentiation (P = 0.007). However, there was no correlation between xCT expression and other clinicopathologic factors including sex, distance from the anus, tumor size, CA19-9 level, or Ki-67 level (Table 1).

xCT expression patterns and correlations with immune infiltration

Single-cell sequencing data from the GSE146771 dataset was analyzed to investigate the expression patterns of xCT (Fig. 2A-B). Analysis of the reactive oxygen species pathway indicated that xCT activation was localized to specific cell clusters, with a concentration of higher activity in distinct regions (Fig. 2C). This suggests a potential link between ROS pathway activation and xCT expression. The violin plot showed increased xCT expression across multiple cell types, particularly in malignant and stromal cells (Fig. 2D). Additional single-cell analyses from the GSE136394 and GSE166555 datasets further confirmed the association between xCT expression and oxidative phosphorylation pathway activation in specific cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1). The expression profile of immune checkpoint genes further highlighted the relationship between xCT and multiple immune checkpoints, showing heterogeneous expression among immune-related genes in the high xCT expression group (Fig. 2E). Correlation analysis demonstrated that xCT expression was significantly associated with several inferred immune infiltration parameters, including B cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8 + T cells (Fig. 2F-I). These findings suggest a potential link between xCT and immune microenvironment-related features, but should be interpreted cautiously because of the correlative nature of the analysis.

High xCT expression is associated with poor prognosis survival

To examine the relationship between xCT expression and the survival outcomes of rectal cancer patients, we initially analyzed data from GEO dataset (GSE17536 and GSE14333). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that higher xCT expression was linked to shorter OS (P = 0.003, Fig. 3C) and shorter DFS (P < 0.001, Fig. 3D). Our cohort further confirmed these findings, showing that high xCT expression was correlated with poor overall survival (P < 0.001, Fig. 3A) and disease-free survival (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B).

To identify prognostic clinical factors in rectal cancer patients, we conducted Cox regression analysis on the TCGA dataset. The result showed that xCT expression was an independent predictors of OS (Fig. 4A-B). Similarly, we conducted a Cox regression analysis of our cohort (Supplementary Table 1). Univariate analysis revealed that xCT expression, CEA level, venous invasion, N stage, and TRG were significantly associated with at least one survival endpoint (Fig. 4C-D). After adjusting for other clinical and pathological factors, multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that high xCT expression level was an independent predictor of poorer OS (HR, 2.805; 95% CI, 1.194–6.588; P = 0.018, Fig. 4E) and lower DFS (HR, 2.653; 95% CI, 1.107–6.36; P = 0.029, Fig. 4F).

Construction and evaluation of the prognostic model

We developed a nomogram of TCGA rectal cancer cohort based on significant prognostic factors, including xCT expression, age, and pN stage (Fig. 5A). The calibration plot for the nomogram confirmed its predictive accuracy in predicting survival outcomes for rectal cancer patients (Fig. 5B). To improve survival prediction in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, a separate nomogram prognostic model was constructed based on xCT scores and venous invasion status to assess survival risk in our cohort (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the calibration curve of this nomogram indicated high concordance between observed outcomes and predicted results (Fig. 5D). Moreover, we plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the performance of our nomogram model. The results demonstrated that our model could effectively predict 1-year (AUC = 0.728) and 3-year (AUC = 0.803) survival rates (Fig. 5E). And the decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed that our model was clinically effective, suggesting that the diagnostic nomogram could serve as a practical tool for predicting patient prognosis (Fig. 5F-G).

Knockout of xCT promotes the sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy in vitro

Stable xCT knockout (xCT-KO) clones were established in the HCT116 and SW837 cell lines. Next, the colony formation assay showed that xCT-KO clones exhibited more pronounced cellular cytotoxicity when exposed to IR and/or 5-Fu treatment (Fig. 6A-B). Clonogenic survival assay showed that xCT-KO cells displayed a marked decrease in survival when subjected to IR (Fig. 6C-D). Moreover, in the cytotoxicity assays to 5-Fu treatment, the IC50 values of 5-Fu were significantly lower in the xCT-KO cell lines compared with WT cells. Specifically, the IC50 for HCT116-xCT-KO was 10.91µM, while HCT116-WT cells was 18.95µM. Similarly, the IC50 for SW837-xCT-KO was 18.11µM, while SW837-WT was 30.32µM (Fig. 6E-F). These findings suggested that HCT116 and SW837 with xCT-KO demonstrated increased sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy.

Knockout of xCT promotes the sensitivity to radiotherapy in vivo

To investigate whether knockout of xCT promotes the radiotherapy sensitivity of cancer cells in vivo, subcutaneous tumor models were established in nude mice. The therapeutic modalities were demonstrated in Fig. 7A. The results showed that xCT-KO significantly inhibited growth and reduced the weight of tumors derived from HCT-116 cells. The IR group also showed a reduction in tumor volume and weight. Interestingly, the combination of xCT-KO and IR exhibited a greater tumor suppressive effect in vivo (Fig. 7B-D). These findings indicate that xCT-KO can inhibit tumor growth and enhance the effects of IR treatment, thereby improving survival.

xCT knockout enhances chemoradiotherapy sensitivity by regulating ferroptosis

Recent studies have highlighted ferroptosis as a critical mechanism influencing cancer progression and treatment outcomes [30]. To explore the significance of xCT in modulating ferroptosis and thereby affecting the efficacy of cancer treatments including IR and 5-Fu, we conducted a comparative analysis of xCT-KO and wild-type (WT) cells. Compared with WT cells, GSH levels were reduced more significantly in xCT-KO cells after IR or 5-Fu treatment (Fig. 8A-B). Additionally, MDA levels exhibited a more significant elevation in xCT-KO cells compared with wild-type (WT) cells following treatment with IR or 5-Fu (Fig. 8C-D). Fe2+ levels in HCT116 and SW837 cells of both xCT-KO and WT were elevated after IR or 5-Fu treatment, but there was no difference between the two groups (Fig. 8E-F). Clonogenic survival assays revealed that the percentage of surviving xCT-KO cells treated with the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was higher than that in the control group after radiation treatment (Fig. 8G-H). These results revealed that xCT knockout increased chemoradiotherapy sensitivity by promoting ferroptosis in cancer cells. To further validate ferroptosis involvement, lipid ROS detection and transmission electron microscopy were performed. xCT-KO increased lipid ROS levels, which were reduced by Fer-1 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Transmission electron microscopy showed ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial changes in xCT-KO cells, including smaller mitochondria and reduced or disappeared cristae, which were partially reversed by Fer-1 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These findings further support that xCT knockout enhances ferroptosis susceptibility.

Discussion

Despite advancements in chemoradiotherapy and targeted therapies, the prognosis for patients with advanced rectal cancer remains inadequate. The exploration of reliable prognostic markers and therapeutic targets is therefore essential in improving treatment outcomes. This study aims to discover prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets that can improve the efficacy of treatment modalities for rectal cancer. By identifying these novel markers, we seek to enhance the precision and effectiveness of treatments, paving the way for more accurate prognosis predictions and innovative therapeutic strategies.

xCT, a component of the system xc- cystine/glutamate antiporter, plays a crucial role in cancer by promoting glutathione synthesis, thereby contributing to the antioxidant capacity and survival of cancer cells under oxidative stress. Previous studies have shown that xCT is overexpressed in many types of cancer and impacts various facets of tumor biology [31, 32]. Besides, high xCT expression has been shown to be a poorer prognostic factor in various cancers such as lung adenocarcinoma [33], epithelial ovarian cancer [34], and hepatocellular carcinoma [35]. In addition, xCT is recognized as a critical factor in the development and progression of CRC and high xCT activity is also a predictive factor for disease recurrence in CRC patients [36–38]. However, studies focusing on the prognostic role of xCT specifically in rectal cancer remain unclear.

Recent studies in colorectal cancer have further implicated the xCT axis in ferroptosis regulation, tumor progression, and treatment resistance. For example, HTRA1 was reported to interact with xCT to modulate chemosensitivity through ferroptosis, while CHI3L1 mediated radiation resistance in colorectal cancer by inhibiting ferroptosis via the p53/xCT pathway. In addition, LPCAT2 was shown to suppress colorectal cancer progression through the PRMT1/xCT axis [39–41]. Importantly, the major observations derived from the public datasets in our study were further supported by our institutional cohort and functional experiments, which strengthens the robustness of the present conclusions.

Therefore, our studies aimed to explore the relationship between xCT expression and prognosis in rectal cancer patients. Differing from the research about CRC, our studies uniquely distinguish patients with rectal cancer from those with broader categories of CRC, conducting an independent analysis of survival prognosis. We observed the significantly higher xCT expression in rectal cancer tissues and survival analysis revealed that high xCT expression is correlated with poorer OS and DFS. Multivariate Cox analysis further confirmed that xCT is an independent adverse prognostic factor. A nomogram prognostic model was constructed based on xCT scores and venous invasion status. The model effectively predicted 1-year (AUC = 0.728) and 3-year (AUC = 0.803) survival rates. Our findings highlighted xCT as a potential prognostic marker for the survival outcomes of rectal cancer patients. Additionally, xCT expression was associated with several immune-related features in the bioinformatics analyses, including immune checkpoint-related expression patterns and inferred immune infiltration levels. However, these observations are correlative and should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not establish a direct causal role of xCT in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment in rectal cancer.

Previous research has shown that increased xCT expression can contribute to resistance against radiotherapy in breast cancer and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [42, 43]. Moreover, xCT has been shown to play a significant role in affecting the sensitivity to certain chemotherapy drugs in hepatocellular carcinoma and adrenocortical carcinoma [35, 44]. However, the effect of xCT on chemoradiotherapy in rectal cancer remains uncertain. In our study, TRG was used as a pathological indicator of response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and higher xCT expression was significantly associated with poorer TRG in both the IHC and clinical data analyses. These findings suggested that overexpression of xCT may reduce the sensitivity of rectal cancer cells to nCRT. Moreover, our further clonogenic survival assays showed that xCT-KO cells formed significantly fewer colonies after IR than those in the control group. Additionally, cytotoxicity experiments also showed that the sensitivity to 5-Fu was significantly increased after xCT knockout. Moreover, xenograft tumor model showed that xCT-KO can inhibit tumor growth and enhance radiotherapy sensitivity in vivo. These results suggest that xCT-KO increases the sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy of colorectal cancer cells.

As a crucial regulator of ferroptosis, the impact of xCT on resistance to therapy by regulating susceptibility to ferroptosis has been extensively studied. Research indicates that the role of xCT in inhibiting ferroptosis enhances the resistance to sorafenib in hepatocellular carcinoma [45]. Besides, recent studies have demonstrated that xCT significantly contributes to the inhibition of ferroptosis and thus inducing radiation resistance in both breast cancer [42] and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [43]. Furthermore, reduction of xCT in hepatocellular carcinoma cells can lead to ferroptosis and then increased radiosensitivity [46]. However, whether xCT regulation of ferroptosis in rectal cancer affects sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy remains to be clarified. Our subsequent experiments confirmed that the GSH levels in xCT-KO cells were markedly lower than those in the control groups, while the MDA levels had increased. Moreover, the number of colonies notably increased in the IR-treated xCT-KO clones after the application of the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1. Notably, Fe²⁺ levels did not differ significantly between xCT-KO and WT cells, suggesting that the enhanced ferroptotic tendency in xCT-deficient cells may be more closely related to impaired antioxidant defense and aggravated lipid peroxidation [47]. These findings implied that knockout of the xCT promotes the susceptibility of cancer cells to ferroptosis, thereby enhancing the responsiveness to chemoradiotherapy treatments. This interpretation was further supported by additional lipid ROS and transmission electron microscopy assays, which showed Fer-1-reversible lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial ultrastructural changes in xCT-KO cells.

Our study contributed to the initial understanding of the complex role of xCT in rectal cancer treatment, particularly its potential to modulate sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy through the inhibition of ferroptosis. We discovered the prognostic correlation of xCT expression, which was subsequently validated using GEO datasets. However, the complexities of xCT interactions with chemoradiation sensitivity underscore the need for a more comprehensive investigation. Future studies with larger clinical cohorts and additional in vivo experiments are needed to further validate these preliminary findings and enhance the generalizability of our results. Additionally, external validation of the cohort-derived prognostic model was limited by differences in variable availability and measurement between datasets, and further validation in more standardized cohorts is needed. Moreover, it’s crucial to conduct a thorough investigation into the molecular interactions of xCT, exploring its relationships with diverse signaling pathways and biomarkers. Such efforts will not only confirm the significance of xCT in the progression and treatment response of rectal cancer but also illuminate the development of innovative therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, our study showed that high expression of xCT was associated with poor prognosis in rectal cancer patients. Furthermore, xCT knockout increased chemoradiotherapy sensitivity by promoting ferroptosis in colorectal cancer cells. These findings suggest that xCT emerges as a promising target for augmenting the efficacy of rectal cancer treatment.
