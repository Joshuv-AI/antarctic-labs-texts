Climate change impact assessment on water resources in the mountainous Karaj Dam Basin using an integrated weather generator-machine learning framework
Authors: Mohammad Reza Khazaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assesses the impacts of climate change on water resources and droughts in the Karaj Dam basin, a key water source for Tehran, Iran. Sixteen climate projections from four GCMs under four SSPs were downscaled using the M-LARS-WG model. Monthly streamflow was simulated using the Random Forest (RF) model, with NSE of 0.96 (calibration) and 0.95 (validation). The models demonstrated strong performance in reproducing weather, streamflow, and drought characteristics, with droughts assessed by SPI, SPEI, and SDI indices. Under the median of future projections, annual streamflow and precipitation rise by ~ 4%, from 12.7 to 13.3 m3/s and 687 to 714 mm, while the probability of moderate or more intense droughts decreases by 3–4% (e.g., from 0.16 to 0.13 based on SPEI). Rising temperatures and increased snowmelt are expected to alter seasonal streamflow more than precipitation, leading to higher winter flows and lower spring flows. Based on the 12-month SDI, average drought duration and magnitude are projected to decrease from 23.4 months and 22.8 to 20.6 months and 20.2, respectively. These findings for the Karaj basin—due to differences in baseline climate conditions—contrast with projections for Tehran, alleviating concerns over severe future water shortages in the capital.Keywords: Drought, Rainfall-runoff model, Machine learning, Weather generator, M-LARS-WGSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

Water resources are essential for the development and sustainability of human societies. Climate change is changing the hydrological regimes of rivers1,2. It is expected that the frequency and severity of droughts will increase across many regions of the world, particularly in semi-arid areas already experiencing considerable water stress, due to climate change3–6. Therefore, understanding changes in streamflow regimes and water resources under future climate conditions is essential for effective water resources management and sustainable development1. This study aims to project changes in the water resources of the Karaj Dam basin under the influence of climate change. The Karaj Dam is a key water resource for Tehran, the capital of Iran, a city that hosts a significant portion of the country’s population and industries. While Tehran is already facing water stress, recent projections for the Tehran meteorological station indicate a significant increase in the frequency and severity of meteorological droughts in the future due to climate change6. Moreover, Iran, located in a dryland region, has experienced severe water shortages and prolonged droughts, significantly affecting socioeconomic conditions, groundwater, and agricultural production7. Over recent decades, most of the country’s major basins have shown a persistent decline in runoff and water storage2. About 25% of Tehran’s water demand, with a population of around 10 million, is supplied by Karaj Dam8, whose basin lies in a mountainous region. Therefore, assessing the impacts of climate change on the water resources 2of this basin using appropriate methods is of critical importance.

Climate change projections are primarily derived from General Circulation Models (GCMs), which simulate future climate variables under different emission scenarios at the global scale. However, the coarse spatial resolution of GCM outputs limits their direct applicability to basin-scale studies, making downscaling necessary. 1,9. Due to the difficulty and complexity of accurately downscaling GCM outputs, a wide variety of downscaling techniques have been created. Downscaling approaches are generally classified into dynamical methods, which rely on Regional Climate Models (RCMs), and statistical methods. 10,11. RCMs are not available in all regions12. Among statistical methods, weather generators (WGs) have been extensively applied to climate change impact studies on water resources and droughts19–22. WGs are stochastic models calibrated using historical observations to generate synthetic time series of meteorological variables such as precipitation, minimum temperature (Tmin) and maximum temperature (Tmax) with arbitrary lengths. By incorporating changes projected by GCMs into their parameters, WGs enable the simulation of future climate conditions at the local scale6,13. Key advantages of WGs include their ability to preserve inter-variable correlations14,15, transfer a wide range of GCM-projected changes to downscaled series14,16,17, and generate long synthetic records suitable for risk and drought analyses9,18. However, many conventional WGs tend to underestimate inter-annual variability12, which is a critical limitation for drought studies that rely on monthly or multi-monthly indices. Accurately reproducing inter-annual variability is therefore essential for reliable drought simulation6.

The M-LARS-WG model23, a modified version of LARS-WG, is a daily WG whose structure has been modified to accurately reproduce inter-annual variability. These modifications have improved the model’s ability to accurately reproduce key meteorological characteristics and to downscale GCM outputs6,23,24. Despite the expected capability of this model for drought studies, it is being used for the first time in this study to assess the impacts of climate change on streamflow-related droughts. Its performance is compared with the LARS-WG model, which has been widely used in numerous studies to evaluate the impacts of climate change on drought19–22.

By simulating the rainfall-runoff process for the basin and using downscaled future scenarios as inputs to the rainfall-runoff model, river flow scenarios for the future can be simulated, and the impacts of climate change on the basin’s surface water resources and hydrological droughts can be assessed.

Machine learning-based models are among the most powerful tools for simulating rainfall-runoff processes. The Random Forest (RF) method, one of the machine learning (ML) techniques, is well-suited for simulating rainfall-runoff processes. Some advantages of RF include its ability to mine relationships within nonlinear data, high predictive accuracy, acceptable tolerance to outliers and noise, and its capacity to avoid overfitting. Additionally, its simplicity stems from the smaller number of parameters that need to be tuned, as well as its ability to identify important predictor variables with minimal human intervention25,26. Therefore, RF has been applied in several studies for rainfall-runoff simulation27–30. Miller et al.31 applied RF to simulate monthly natural streamflow at nearly 2,000 stream-gages in the United States (U.S.). The mean Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) of 0.85 was achieved, indicating strong model performance. Shortridge et al.29 applied RF to simulate monthly rainfall-runoff in five basins in Ethiopia, achieving NSE values ranging from 0.71 to 0.87. Ojha et al.30 applied the RF method to simulate monthly rainfall-runoff in a basin in India and achieved an NSE value of 0.79, which was better than the five other machine learning models it was compared with.

In this study, to assess the impact of climate change on the surface water resources of the Karaj Dam basin, future scenarios from four GCM models under four new SSP scenarios (a total of 16 GCM projections) are downscaled using the M-LARS-WG model. The basin’s monthly streamflow is simulated using a Random Forest model, and the impacts of climate change on streamflow and meteorological variables are evaluated. Subsequently, the impacts of climate change on droughts are assessed using the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI), Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), and Streamflow Drought Index (SDI), which are among the most widely used tools for drought assessment. Identifying the characteristics of future water resources and droughts, considering the impacts of climate change, is crucial for effectively preparing for and mitigating the adverse effects of climate change3,32,33. Therefore, the results of this study can be directly applied to water resource planning in Tehran and the development of climate change adaptation strategies. Furthermore, the performance of the M-LARS-WG model is evaluated and compared with that of the LARS-WG model, a commonly used weather generator.

Study region and data

The study area is the Karaj Dam Basin, which serves as a crucial water source for Tehran. This basin, measured at the Sira hydrometric station at the inlet of the dam’s reservoir, covers an area of 720 km2 (Fig. 2a). Its elevation ranges from 1,790 to 4,300 m above sea level (Fig. 2b). The basin is located approximately between 51˚E–51.6˚E longitude and 35.8˚N–36.2˚N latitude. Observed daily precipitation, Tmin, and Tmax data for the 30-year period 1984–2013 were obtained from the Nesa meteorological station, located near the center of the basin (36.07° N, 51.32° E; elevation 2300 m a.s.l.) in a very humid ecoregion. The station has an average annual maximum temperature of 16.5 °C, an average annual minimum temperature of 0.3 °C, and a mean annual precipitation of 688 mm.

The study basin with its hydrometric and meteorological stations (a), and the hypsometric curve of the basin (b). Map generated in ArcGIS 10.4.1 (available at https://www.esri.com).

Additionally, daily streamflow data from the Sira station were used for a 21-year period (1984–2004), as they are considered to be of reliable quality. The mean river discharge during this period is 12.2 m3/s.

Following Khazaei6, daily precipitation, Tmax, and Tmin data from four GCMs (Table 1) under four SSP-RCP scenarios (a total of 16 projections), available at https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/, were used for the control (1984–2013) and future (2025–2054) periods to address uncertainties in GCM structure and emission scenarios.

Since raw GCM outputs usually contain substantial systematic biases, they were not used directly in this study. Instead, a statistical bias correction approach was applied. Specifically, changes in key statistical characteristics (e.g., means, standard deviations, and other relevant statistics) between control and future GCM simulations for the grid cell covering the study basin were transferred to the corresponding observed climate statistics. These bias-corrected statistics were then used to calibrate the weather generator and to generate future downscaled weather series for the study region. This procedure effectively minimizes the propagation of GCM-related biases into the final results and represents a commonly used approach in climate change impact studies using weather generators. Further details are provided in the ‘Downscaling Methods’ section.

The SSP-RCP scenarios used include SSP119, SSP370, SSP460, and SSP585. SSP119 presents minimal challenges in both adaptation and mitigation, with a radiative forcing of 1.9 W/m2 by 2100, aligning with the Paris Agreement goals. SSP460 requires substantial adaptation efforts but faces fewer mitigation challenges, leading to a radiative forcing of 6.0 W/m2 by 2100. SSP370 entails challenges for both adaptation and mitigation, with radiative forcing reaching 7.0 W/m2, representing a medium-to-high emissions pathway. SSP585, driven by extensive fossil fuel use, has low adaptation challenges but significant mitigation difficulties, resulting in the highest radiative forcing of 8.5 W/m2 by 2100.

M-LARS-WG for weather generation and downscaling

To generate data using the M-LARS-WG, the daily series of precipitation, Tmin, and Tmax are first produced by the LARS-WG34 model. Then, the inter-annual variability of these series is corrected using two monthly components of the M-LARS-WG model10,23.

The LARS-WG model is a stochastic daily WG that produces synthetic time series of precipitation, Tmax, Tmin, and solar radiation of a desired length for a specified location. The probability distributions, as well as the autocorrelation and cross-correlation parameters of each variable for each calendar month, are estimated from the observed weather data series. The data generation process begins by alternating wet and dry spells in sequence. The lengths of these spells are randomly and independently approximated by the corresponding semi-empirical probability distributions. Subsequently, for each wet day, the precipitation amount is randomly sampled from the semi-empirical distribution of precipitation values corresponding to that calendar month. To model the observed daily Tmax and Tmin series, the daily mean and standard deviation cycles of Tmax and Tmin are reduced separately for wet and dry days. A first-order multivariate autoregressive model (AR(1)) is then applied to the standardized residuals to capture the auto-correlation and cross-correlation between these variables. The daily AR(1) model is subsequently used to generate synthetic standardized Tmax and Tmin series, which are later de-standardized based on the mean and standard deviation cycles corresponding to rainfall occurrence. More information about the LARS-WG model can be found in Semenov and Stratonovitch (2010).

To correct for inter-annual variability in precipitation, the quantile perturbation method (QPM) is employed so that the generated monthly precipitation distribution aligns with the observed monthly precipitation distribution. In this method, the generated daily series are considered on a month-by-month basis, and the probability of occurrence of the total precipitation for each month is determined. Then, the precipitation values for all days within that month are multiplied by a correction factor. This factor is determined such that the total monthly precipitation produced for that month matches the observed precipitation with the same probability. The determination of the probability for monthly values is carried out separately for each calendar month of the year.

The correction of inter-annual variability in the Tmin and Tmax series is performed using a tri-variate AR(1) model, whose parameters are estimated by fitting to the observed monthly series. The monthly precipitation series is obtained by aggregating the daily precipitation series. Then, the monthly Tmin and Tmax series are generated using the monthly AR(1) model as dependent variables of the monthly precipitation series. Subsequently, the monthly means of Tmin and Tmax in the daily series are adjusted to match the corresponding values generated by the monthly model. This approach ensures that the statistical characteristics at the monthly timescale, derived from the outputs of the daily model, are consistent with those of the monthly model, thereby correcting inter-annual temperature variability. A detailed description of the M-LARS-WG model is provided in Khazaei et al.23.

To calculate the SPEI index, the variable D, which is the difference between precipitation and potential evapotranspiration (i.e., D = P−PET), is used as a measure for the climatic water balance. For D data, the three-parameter log-logistic distribution is typically used, which is proposed by the developers of SPEI36. The three-parameter log-logistic distribution has been widely used to standardize the D series for calculating the SPEI across various time scales globally36,38. It also showed a good fit for the D data of the region under study in this paper. Therefore, the method for calculating the SPEI is similar to that of the SDI, with the key difference being that D is used instead of streamflow. Following Vicente-Serrano1, who introduced the SPEI, the Thornthwaite method39 was applied to estimate PET. Despite the existence of more physically based approaches such as Penman–Monteith, which requires numerous meteorological variables that are not available in many basins, the Thornthwaite method has been widely applied in climate change impact assessments due to its reliance only on temperature data6,20,40–42. This feature also makes it particularly suitable for future climate scenarios. Moreover, adopting the Thornthwaite method ensures consistency with previous analyses of drought in Tehran6, thereby facilitating comparability of results. A custom MATLAB code was specifically written by the author to calculate the SPI, SPEI, and SDI.

Hydrologic modeling

Random forest

Random forest (RF), introduced by Breiman43, is a supervised machine learning algorithm and a powerful ensemble method for classification and regression. It constructs multiple decision trees using bootstrap resampling. The key advantage of ensemble learning is that combining multiple models improves accuracy compared to individual models30. Each decision tree is built independently. At each node, the optimal split is selected from a randomly chosen subset of input variables rather than considering all features44. This strategy reduces correlation among trees, enhancing predictive performance. The final prediction is obtained by majority voting in classification tasks or averaging in regression tasks45.

Unlike traditional decision trees, RF mitigates overfitting, where a model shows high accuracy on training data but performs poorly on unseen data44. While individual decision trees tend to capture complex patterns and over fit their training data, RF reduces variance by averaging predictions from multiple trees trained on different subsets of the dataset30. It effectively handles nonlinearity, noise, and outliers, making it a robust extension of regression trees and rule-based models26. Additionally, RF can assess the nonlinear effects of variables, accommodate complex interactions, and remains unaffected by multi-collinearity30. The RF structure consists of multiple randomly grown binary regression trees, where each node is split based on an optimal partitioning variable and split point to minimize squared error44.

Random Forest is highly user-friendly because it requires only two parameters: the number of predictors randomly chosen at each node and the overall number of trees in the forest. Moreover, its performance is generally not highly sensitive to the specific values of these parameters45. The Random Forest method has been utilized in various studies for rainfall-runoff modeling, demonstrating good performance29,30.

For the random forest (RF) model, the 16-year training dataset was used for model construction and hyperparameter tuning. The key hyperparameters, including the number of trees, the number of predictors sampled at each split, and tree depth, were automatically optimized using MATLAB’s built-in optimization routine. The independent 5-year dataset was kept separate and used solely for final performance evaluation.

Generalized linear model

The Generalized Linear Model (GLM) provides a framework for establishing a linear relationship between a function of the response variable’s mean and the predictor variables through a link function, even when the underlying relationship is inherently nonlinear. Unlike traditional linear regression models, GLM does not require the response variable or the error terms to follow a normal distribution. Instead, it accommodates a range of distributions from the exponential family, including those that are skewed46,47. The GLM has been employed in several studies for rainfall-runoff modeling48,49.

Support vector machine

Support Vector Machine (SVM)50 is a supervised machine learning method applied to both classification and regression problems. The algorithm nonlinearly maps input data into a high-dimensional feature space, enabling it to model complex and nonlinear relationships between input and output variables51. By identifying support vectors, SVM finds an optimal hyperplane that effectively separates the data, which contributes to its high generalization ability and helps prevent overfitting52. The use of kernel functions allows SVM to capture nonlinear patterns within data, providing strong performance in complex tasks50. These characteristics make SVM a suitable and effective tool for rainfall-runoff simulation, where it has been applied in several studies48,53.

Artificial neural network

An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) consists of multiple interconnected processing units, known as neurons, which are organized into layers. Typically, the network includes an input layer, one or more hidden layers for data processing, and an output layer where results are generated54. During training, the network learns by adjusting the strengths of connections between neurons based on known input-output examples, aiming to produce accurate outputs. ANNs mimic the way biological neurons function and can approximate highly nonlinear patterns in data55. Detailed information about network structures, connection schemes, and transfer functions is available in the cited references55. Several studies have utilized ANNs for modeling rainfall-runoff processes53–56.

Development and application of the rainfall-runoff models

A customized MATLAB code was developed for each of the AI-based models to simulate the basin’s rainfall-runoff process. To simulate monthly streamflow, various combinations of precipitation, temperature, PET, Liquid Precipitation and Snowmelt (LPS), and lagged streamflow (accounting for the temporal dependency of streamflow) were evaluated as inputs to the rainfall-runoff model. The 21-year (1984–2004) dataset of the basin was divided into two independent subsets: the first 16 years were used for model training (calibration), and the remaining 5 years were reserved exclusively for model validation. This independent validation set, not involved in model training, ensured an unbiased evaluation of model performance and reduced the risk of overfitting.

For all AI-based models, hyperparameter tuning and optimization were performed using the 16-year training dataset. The RF model hyperparameters were tuned using Bayesian optimization in MATLAB. The final configuration consisted of 72 decision trees, with three predictors randomly selected at each node split. Model complexity was controlled through optimized constraints, including a minimum leaf size of 1 and a maximum of 116 splits per tree. For the ANN model, both structural and training hyperparameters were optimized using MATLAB’s Bayesian optimization routine. A feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer was adopted, with an optimal architecture of five neurons in the hidden layer. Training was conducted for a maximum of 109 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0075, and early stopping was employed to prevent overfitting. For the SVM model, key hyperparameters were optimized using MATLAB’s built-in optimization routine within a support vector regression (SVR) framework. A Gaussian (RBF) kernel was used, and the optimized parameters included a box constraint of 157.2, an epsilon-insensitive loss value of 0.1405, and a kernel scale of 194.0. Model training was performed using the sequential minimal optimization (SMO) algorithm. For the GLM, the selection of the distribution family and link function was guided by exploratory analysis of the training dataset, and the final configuration was chosen based on goodness-of-fit criteria and minimization of training error. Detailed results of the optimized GLM, including coefficients, standard errors, and statistical significance levels, are provided in Table S2 (Supporting Information). The independent 5-year dataset was reserved exclusively for final performance evaluation.

The best simulation of streamflow in month i was achieved using four variables: (1) LPS in month i, (2) PET in month i, (3) the average LPS from month i-1 to i-4, and (4) the average PET from month i-1 to i-4. ​​It should be noted that the coefficient of determination (R2) between monthly streamflow and precipitation is 0.02, whereas between LPS and monthly streamflow, it is 0.56. Furthermore, the seasonal cycle of monthly streamflow exhibits a significant correlation with LPS but not with precipitation (Figure S1 in the Supplementary Information). This is attributed to the fact that a substantial portion of precipitation in the mountainous basin area of the Karaj Dam occurs as snowfall. Monthly PET was estimated using the Thornthwaite equation, and LPS was calculated based on daily precipitation and temperature, for each elevation band within the basin. These values were then aggregated to the basin scale using area-weighted averaging. The temperature of each elevation band was derived from station temperature data and the basin-specific temperature-elevation relationship. A simple temperature-based model3 was used to estimate snowmelt (mm/day), calculated as 1.52 × mean daily temperature (°C) when above 0 °C. Based on a previous study2 conducted in the same region and analysis of observed meteorological and snowfall data from stations within the basin, a mean daily temperature of 2.9 °C was adopted as the threshold for snowfall. Precipitation below this threshold accumulates as snow, which melts when temperatures exceed 0 °C. Liquid precipitation and snowmelt were summed to calculate the LPS.

According to the evaluation guidelines proposed by Moriasi et al.1, the performance of a rainfall–runoff model is classified as very good when NSE > 0.75 and Bias < 10%. They reviewed rainfall–runoff model applications and evaluation methods, providing guidance on assessing model accuracy. Across various studies, the median NSE was 0.79 for calibration and 0.63 for validation, with maximum values of 0.91 and 0.93, respectively57. Shortridge et al.29 reported NSEs of 0.71–0.87 for monthly rainfall–runoff simulations in five Ethiopian basins, while Ojha et al.30 obtained an NSE of 0.79 for a basin in India. Nasirabadi et al.58 employed the SWAT conceptual, semi-distributed model to simulate the rainfall–runoff process in the Karaj Dam basin at the Sira station and obtained NSE values of 0.72 and 0.60 for the calibration and validation periods, respectively.

Compared to these findings, the results of the present work confirm the strong predictive capability of machine-learning models—particularly RF, SVM, and ANN—for simulating rainfall–runoff processes in the Karaj River basin. For these models, NSE values were close to the ideal value of 1.0, indicating very good performance. All three models can therefore be considered effective choices, and in this study RF was adopted as the final model.

This extended synthetic dataset is intended to capture a wide spectrum of climate variability, thereby enabling a more robust assessment of extreme events10,14,59. These results indicate that the performance of M-LARS-WG is significantly better than that of LARS-WG. The LARS-WG model failed to adequately simulate the monthly standard deviations of temperature in any month of the year. Consequently, the reproduction of the monthly standard deviations of PET and streamflow was also rejected in 12 and 8 months of the year, respectively. Additionally, the reproduction of the probability distributions of temperature and PET was rejected in 12 and 10 months of the year, respectively. In contrast, the M-LARS-WG model was rejected in only a few tests and successfully reproduced most statistical characteristics. Moreover, the performance of M-LARS-WG in reproducing the statistical properties of the observed streamflow was good and superior to that of LARS-WG. The weakness of LARS-WG in reproducing the monthly statistical characteristics of the variables stems from the fact that, like many conventional WGs, it lacks a component for capturing inter-annual variability. However, M-LARS-WG, with its additional monthly modeling component, is capable of reproducing monthly statistical characteristics and low frequency variability of the variables.

In contrast, the performance of M-LARS-WG in simulating these indices has been successful in all tests. However, when the outputs of the WGs are used as inputs to the rainfall-runoff model for simulating SDI, the performance of M-LARS-WG is quite good and significantly better than that of LARS-WG. For example, using LARS-WG, the reproduction of the standard deviation is rejected for SDI-12 in all 12 months of the year and for SDI-3 in 7 months. In comparison, the number of corresponding rejected F-tests using M-LARS-WG is only 1 out of 12. The results indicate that the monthly component of M-LARS-WG significantly improved the performance of the WG in simulating drought characteristics, particularly for hydrological droughts.

Both the LARS-WG and M-LARS-WG models, in combination with the hydrological model, successfully simulated the magnitude and duration of historical droughts based on monthly SDI at 12-month timescales, as assessed using the F-test, t-test, and K-S test at the 5% significant level. A drought event is defined when the monthly SDI-12 remains consistently negative, reaching − 1.0 or lower. The duration of a drought is defined as the time interval between the point at which the index first falls below zero and the point it rises back above zero. The drought magnitude is calculated as the absolute cumulative value of the index throughout this period.

Failure to adequately reproduce low-frequency variability (e.g., monthly variances) of weather variables propagates into secondary variables derived from weather data, such as streamflow and drought indices (SPI, SPEI, and SDI). In particular, because drought indices are typically calculated over periods of one month or longer, insufficient representation of low-frequency variability—which also manifests as biased distributions of the variables—may lead to biased or unreliable simulations of their extreme characteristics, including drought probability, duration, and magnitude. Given the superior performance of the M-LARS-WG model, it was used to assess the impact of climate change on streamflow and droughts in the Karaj Dam basin.

The future scenarios are derived from the outputs of four GCMs under four SSPs and are downscaled using the M-LARS-WG model. Accordingly, the mean monthly temperature increases in nearly all cases (Fig. 4.a). As temperatures rise, the median of future projections for mean PET also increases across all months (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, during the warmer months of the year, mean monthly PET is expected to rise under almost all scenarios. However, in colder months, some scenarios suggest a decrease in mean PET. According to the temperature-based PET equation, an increase in temperature above freezing leads to a rise in PET. However, it is important to note that an increase in the mean monthly temperature under a given scenario does not necessarily imply that all temperature values in the monthly time series will increase. This is because temperature variations depend not only on the mean but also on changes in variability (e.g., standard deviation).

The differences in drought frequency changes between Tehran and the Karaj Dam Basin are associated with variations in precipitation and PET at these locations, as well as their relative importance in the SPEI index. The average annual precipitation at the Tehran station is 241 mm, whereas at the Nesa station in the Karaj Dam Basin, it is 687 mm. In contrast, the mean annual PET at the Tehran station is 1088 mm, while in the Karaj Dam Basin, it is 543 mm. Consequently, precipitation changes play a more significant role in SPEI variations in the Karaj Dam Basin. Furthermore, precipitation changes in both regions are relatively similar, with a projected increase of 4% under the median of future scenarios. However, under the same conditions, PET is projected to increase by 20% in Tehran, while it is expected to rise by 11% in the Karaj Dam Basin. As a result, under the median of future scenarios, drought frequency based on SPEI is expected to increase significantly in Tehran, whereas in the Karaj Dam Basin, it is projected to decline slightly.

Khazaei6, in assessing the impacts of climate change on droughts in Tehran based on the SPEI index, demonstrated that the frequency, duration, and magnitude of future droughts are expected to increase significantly under nearly all the 16 projections due to rising temperatures and PET. However, the findings of this study, conducted in the mountainous basin of the Karaj Dam, indicate that surface water resources in this basin—which supply a significant portion of Tehran’s water—are projected to increase under the median of future scenarios. Additionally, the frequency of annual droughts, based on SPI, SPEI, and SDI indices, is expected to decline in the median of future projections, while the duration and magnitude of annual droughts, based on the SDI index, are also projected to decrease. This outcome can be attributed to the fact that the precipitation-to-PET ratio in the mountainous basin of the Karaj Dam is significantly higher than in Tehran. As a result, changes in streamflow and droughts based on the SPEI and SDI indices are primarily influenced by increased precipitation rather than rising PET. These findings mitigate concerns raised by Khazaei and Moshir Panahi et al.2,6. However, it should be noted that under pessimistic scenarios among the 16 future projections, a potential decline in water resources and an increase in droughts within the estimated range remain possible. The extent of these variations can provide valuable insights for decision-makers in implementing preventive measures to mitigate the impacts of droughts and water scarcity under climate change.

Conclusion

This study aims to employ effective and robust methods to project the future status of water resources and droughts in the Karaj Dam basin, one of the most important water supply systems for Tehran. Water scarcity and concerns over future water shortages in Tehran have become so critical that the government has even seriously considered relocating the capital. Changes in the dam’s water availability can directly affect the livelihoods and well-being of dependent communities, emphasizing the importance of sustainable water management under future climate scenarios. ​In this study, projections from four GCMs under four SSPs were utilized to encompass a range of possible outcomes and enhance result reliability. For downscaling the GCM projections, M-LARS-WG was employed, offering significant advantages as a WG for this type of study. The monthly streamflow of the Karaj River was simulated using four machine learning models (RF, SVM, ANN, and GLM) and RF was selected as the final model in this study. Through a trial-and-error process, PET and LPS for the basin were selected as inputs for the current month and the moving average of the past four months. By coupling the WG model with the RF model, long-term streamflow was simulated for both the historical period and future scenarios. Despite the advantages of M-LARS-WG, this study represents the first application of coupling the M-LARS-WG model with a rainfall-runoff model to assess the impacts of climate change on hydrological droughts and water resources.

The performance evaluation results of the models indicated that the M-LARS-WG model generally performed well in the evaluation tests for reproducing various weather and drought characteristics. Additionally, the RF model effectively simulated the basin’s streamflow, with NSE values of 0.96 and 0.95 for the calibration and validation periods, respectively. Furthermore, the coupling of the M-LARS-WG and RF models effectively captured streamflow and drought characteristics. M-LARS-WG significantly outperformed LARS-WG, a commonly used WG, both independently and in combination with the rainfall-runoff model, in reproducing climatic and hydrological characteristics due to its inter-annual variability adjustment component.

Based on the median of future climate scenarios, temperature and PET are projected to increase across all months. Precipitation is expected to rise in certain months and decline in others; however, the annual precipitation is anticipated to increase by 4%, with variations ranging from − 7% to + 13% across different scenarios. The seasonal cycle of streamflow is expected to change somewhat, with an annual increase of 4% according to the median of future scenarios, and possible variations between − 9% and + 12% across different scenarios.

​In the median of future scenarios, the probability of moderate or more intense annual droughts, as indicated by the SPI, SPEI, and SDI indices, is projected to decrease by approximately 3–4%. The seasonal pattern of SPI and SPEI will change slightly, with similar trends for both indices. However, based on the SDI, the probability of droughts significantly decreases in autumn and winter, but increases in spring due to rising temperatures and increased LPS during autumn and winter. Although SPI changes between this basin and the Tehran station are similar, SPEI changes in the mountainous Karaj basin are entirely different from those in Tehran. In the Karaj basin, changes in SPEI and SDI indices are strongly influenced by precipitation changes rather than by PET, possibly due to relatively low temperatures, low PET, and a high precipitation-to-PET ratio in the Karaj dam basin compared to Tehran. Based on the monthly SDI series with a 12-month time scale, the average drought duration and magnitude, which were 23.4 and 22.8 in the historical period, are projected to decrease to 20.6 and 20.2, respectively, according to the median of future projections.

It can be concluded that the impacts of climate change on water resources in the Karaj Dam basin differ significantly from those projected for Tehran, despite similar GCM projections, due to distinct historical climate conditions. While drought conditions are expected to worsen considerably in Tehran in the future, based on the median of future projections, the water resources of the Karaj Dam basin are anticipated to improve. However, under the most pessimistic projection (out of 16), a decrease in average streamflow could reach up to 9%. Beyond the Karaj Dam basin, the coupled M-LARS-WG and RF modeling framework developed in this study has the potential to be validated and applied in other basins. By integrating a weather generator that preserves inter-annual variability and provides significant advantages for climate change impact assessments with a machine learning rainfall–runoff model capable of accurately simulating streamflow, the methodology can be readily adapted to diverse hydro-climatic conditions, offering a transferable tool for regional water resource and drought assessments.

Beyond the uncertainties related to GCMs, emission scenarios, and the short length of observed records, which were considered in this study, other potential sources of uncertainty, including the structure of weather generators and rainfall–runoff models, may also influence the results. A key limitation is the common assumption of basin stationarity, which may be violated in the future due to climate-induced changes in land cover and associated hydrological processes. Acknowledging and addressing these uncertainties in future studies will enhance the robustness of climate change impact assessments and provide more reliable guidance for water resources management and adaptation planning.
