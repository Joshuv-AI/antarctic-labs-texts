Comparative evaluation of irrigant activation devices for removal of calcium hydroxide from artificial isthmuses connecting curved canals in 3D-printed resin molar models
Authors: Gülçin Cagay Sevencan, Sevcan Akça
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), confirmed effective in studies, is frequently used as an interappointment medicament in root canal treatment. The challenges related to removing this medication from the root canals before the filling negatively influence post-treatment success. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the efficacy of different irrigant activation devices—sonic activation (EDDY), passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), photon-induced photoacoustic streaming (PIPS), and shockwave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming (SWEEPS)—in removing Ca(OH)₂ from artificial isthmuses connecting curved root canals in 3D-printed resin molar models.MethodsSeventy resin-based upper molar models with two curved canals connected by three standardized isthmuses were 3D-printed (Hex 600, Edudent, Türkiye). After canal preparation and Ca(OH)₂ (Well-Paste, Korea) placement, samples were randomly assigned to five groups (control group n = 10, experimental group n = 15) for Ca(OH)₂ removal: conventional needle irrigation (control) (NaviTip, Ultradent, USA), EDDY sonic irrigation (VDW, Germany), PUI (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., LTD, China), PIPS, and SWEEPS (Fotona, Slovenia) laser-assisted irrigation. Irrigation protocols were standardized for volume and duration. The percentage of cleaned isthmus areas was quantified using pre- and post-irrigation imaging under microscopy and analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Statistical comparisons were performed using robust ANOVA.ResultsSignificant differences in Ca(OH)₂ removal efficacy were found between groups (p < 0.001). The SWEEPS group showed the highest median cleaning percentage (47.71%), followed by PIPS (37.96%), EDDY (29.30%), PUI (28.22%), and control (11.26%). SWEEPS showed the highest median cleaned isthmus percentage, followed by PIPS, while PUI and EDDY demonstrated comparable values. Conventional needle irrigation showed the lowest cleaning performance. Cleaning efficacy varied significantly among isthmus locations (coronal > middle > apical, p = 0.007). SWEEPS, PIPS, and EDDY showed similarly high cleaning efficacy, with PUI demonstrating comparable performance, while conventional needle irrigation showed significantly lower values. No significant differences were observed between groups in the apical isthmus.ConclusionNone of the irrigation activation techniques achieved complete removal of calcium hydroxide. Cleaning efficacy was significantly influenced by both irrigation method and isthmus location. Laser-activated irrigation systems showed higher efficacy in specific regions, while conventional needle irrigation consistently demonstrated the lowest performance. Overall, irrigation effectiveness in isthmus-containing root canals exhibited a clear anatomy-dependent pattern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09761-x.Keywords: Calcium hydroxide removal, Passive ultrasonic irrigation, Shockwave-enhanced Emission Photoacoustic Streaming (SWEEPS), Sonic activation, Three - dimensional (3D) printing model

Background

Apical periodontitis is a biofilm-induced disease, and the goal of root canal therapy is the eradication of the bacterial biofilm from the infected canal [1]. The root canal system is complex beyond the confines of the main root canal, with macroscopic parts beyond the reach of mechanical preparation. This anatomical complexity encompasses not only the main canals but also includes lateral and accessory canals, apical deltas, isthmuses, canal loops, and the intricate network of dentinal tubules [2]. The isthmuses are the narrow, ribbon-like connections between two root canal segments [3]. According to Hsu and Kim’s classification, isthmuses are divided into five main groups based on their structure. In Types I and II, there is no complete connection between the two canals, but in Type II, the connection is distinct. In Type III, there are three root canals connected to each other by a C-shaped isthmus that is not completely connected. In Type IV, there are two canal structures that widen towards the isthmus. In Type V, there is a corridor-like and complete connection between the two canals [4]. Isthmus formations are detected at varied frequencies across different layers of the root canal system. They are mainly present at the apical 5–7 mm level. The complete (Type V) isthmus configuration is the most often observed isthmus type [5]. Type V is a variation that is considered more difficult to clean the isthmus [6].

The ideal chemomechanical preparation is challenged by the anatomical complexity of the root canal system [7]. In these hard-to-reach areas, biofilm and necrotic pulp tissue may be left behind. This can ultimately lead to failure of the endodontic treatment [8]. Therefore, the use of medication between appointments is recommended for further reduction of the microbial load [9].

A substantial body of research has demonstrated the numerous advantages of calcium hydroxide as a preferred pharmaceutical agent. Its primary attributes include high alkalinity, tissue solubility, the capacity to neutralize endotoxins, and antibacterial properties [10]. As stated by Siqueira and Lopes, the pH of Ca(OH)₂ is approximately 12.5 [11]. The hydroxyl ions generate free radicals that result in the destruction of components of bacterial cell membranes. The generated free radicals exert their antimicrobial action by interacting with bacterial DNA, thereby inhibiting replication processes, suppressing cellular activity, and inducing mutagenic changes. Furthermore, the highly alkaline environment created by calcium hydroxide interferes with enzymatic function, leading to disruption of both cellular metabolism and structural protein integrity. Hydroxyl ions are capable of diffusing through dentinal tubules, elevating the pH of the surrounding dentin to approximately 9.0—a phenomenon referred to as transdentinal diffusion [12]. This effect may be of significance in the management of bacterial reservoirs within dentinal tubules.

Despite its potent antimicrobial properties, calcium hydroxide is difficult to eliminate from root canals, and residual deposits may adversely impact the outcomes of endodontic therapy [13]. Research indicates that leftover calcium hydroxide particles adhering to dentin walls may block dentinal tubules, hindering the infiltration of sealers and consequently increasing the risk of apical leakage following root canal therapy [14]. Therefore, before filling the root canal, the calcium hydroxide should be completely removed [15].

Traditional needle-based irrigating methods often fail to provide sufficient delivery and penetration of irrigants within the complex 3D microstructure of the canal system due to limited fluid penetration in the apical third and beyond the main canal [16, 17]. Therefore, irrigation activation techniques have been proposed to increase irrigation effectiveness and improve their distribution in the canal system [18].

Numerous contemporary techniques exist for the removal of intracanal medicaments. One such system is EDDY (VDW, Munich, Germany), which utilizes sonic activation technology. This device features polyamide tips that oscillate at a frequency of 6000 Hz. The tips measure 25/0.4, with a tip diameter of 0.2 mm and a total length of 28 mm [19]. EDDY is effective in removing organic tissue and debris from the canal walls [20].

Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) is a technique in which the irrigant is energized by a non-cutting ultrasonic tip oscillating within the central portion of the prepared root canal, enhancing the irrigant’s ability to clean complex canal anatomy [21]. Despite the observation of cavitation effects with PUI, its primary cleaning action is attributed to acoustic microstreaming [22].

The PIPS (Photon Induced Photoacoustic Streaming) methodology distinguishes itself from previous agitation techniques by requiring the activation tip to be positioned just in the coronal region. This technique employs an erbium:yttrium aluminum garnet (Er:YAG) laser equipped with both radial and stripped tips featuring an innovative design, operated at subablative power settings (0.3 W). Peak power peaks are generated using low energy levels and short microsecond pulse rates (50 ms). It facilitates three-dimensional movement of irrigation solutions by inducing a deep photoacoustic shock wave [23]. Research indicated that the smooth fiber tip within the pulp chamber can effectively deliver the irrigation solution to the end of the canal without compromising the apical tissue [24].

A new method, called Shockwave Enhanced Emission Photoacoustic Streaming (SWEEPS), has been recently proposed. This method uses a specialized laser (Er:YAG) to create a focused beam of energy (shockwave) for better cleaning and disinfection during endodontic treatments. The SWEEPS method creates a series of bubbles that gradually turn into secondary bubbles, which together generate a shockwave and enhance the photoacoustic current. These shockwaves and increased acoustic current move through the narrow canals, improving cleaning and disinfection during the irrigation process [25].

Recently, 3D printing technology has been incorporated into contemporary endodontics. This technology has also been utilized to create various models for endodontic education and research. The following comparisons have been made: the shaping of the obturation techniques for C-shaped root canals [26], the ability of different rotary instruments [27], the regenerative endodontics [28], and the irrigation techniques for biofilm removal [29].

Numerous studies have investigated the efficacy of different irrigation activation systems for the removal of intracanal medicaments [15, 30]. It is important to make sure that the irrigants reach the main and lateral canals and complex anatomy like the isthmus of the teeth. However, there are not many studies on the PIPS and SWEEPS methods. To the best of our knowledge, no study has evaluated the efficacy of EDDY, SWEEPS, PIPS, or PUI in removing medication from isthmuses of resin molar models.

The aim of this study was to compare the efficacy of different irrigation activation techniques in removing calcium hydroxide from isthmus regions of curved root canals using standardized 3D-printed models. This study also evaluated the influence of isthmus level and the interaction between irrigation technique and isthmus anatomy. The irrigation systems investigated ranged from conventional methods to advanced activation technologies, including devices not yet widely used in routine clinical practice, in order to provide comprehensive data for future research. Standardized 3D-printed models were used to ensure reproducibility and minimize methodological variability in complex root canal anatomy.

The null hypotheses were that there would be no significant differences among irrigation activation techniques in calcium hydroxide removal efficacy and that isthmus level would have no effect on calcium hydroxide removal, including no interaction between irrigation technique and isthmus anatomy.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was not required for this study because this laboratory-based in vitro study was conducted using artificial resin teeth and did not involve human participants, animals, or any human- or animal-derived biological specimens. This study is reported in accordance with the PRILE 2021 guidelines for reporting laboratory studies in endodontology.

Sample size calculation

The G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Heinrich-Heine-University Düsseldorf) to perform a one-way analysis of variance on the data from the pilot study, it was found that a minimum total sample size of 48 would be required to statistically substantiate differences between experimental groups with an effect size of 0.5, 80% power and a 5% level of significance. The final analysis included a total of 60 samples, with each irrigation protocol repeated 15 times (n = 15) experimental group and 10 times for control group (n = 10). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

3D model production

Specially designed for this study, 2 curved root canal models connected by 3 isthmuses were 3D printed from urethane-based light-curing resin (Hex 600, Edudent, Istanbul, Türkiye). In order to mimic its original form, the isthmus structure connecting the first and second mesiobuccal canals of the upper molar was used. The root canals have a total length of 15 mm, 0.02 taper, width of 0.24 mm apical diameter at the apex, and 40° apical curvature. The root curvature angle was determined using the Schneider method based on cross-sectional images taken in the model production software [31]. The coronal reservoir was produced as an access cavity with a depth of 5 mm by opening in all standard teeth. Each root canal has three isthmus structures with an apex distance of 4.5 mm, 7.5 mm, and 10 mm, respectively. Isthmus widths are 0.55 mm, 0.65 mm, and 0.70 mm in diameter, respectively.

Root canal preparation

The root canals were prepared with ProTaper Next X1, X2, and X3 files (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) respectively, and the final root end diameter was 30/0.07. During canal shaping, irrigation was performed with 2 ml of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Microvem, Altun Medical, Türkiye) at each instrument change, and apical patency was achieved with a #10 K file.

Calcium hydroxide placement

The apical foramen of each shaped canal was sealed with adhesive wax to form a closed-end system. Then, the mesiobuccal canals were filled with a water-based calcium hydroxide paste containing barium sulfate (Well-Paste, Lot.WS0N6100, Vericom, Gangwon-Do, Korea) with a plastic tip that can be penetrated into the canal, checking from transparent models under LED light that all the isthmuses were filled. If any part of the isthmus was not filled, the procedure was repeated. The access cavity was sealed with a temporary restorative material (3 M Cavit, Saint Paul, MN, USA). All specimens were subsequently stored at 37 °C and 100% relative humidity for a period of 3 weeks.

Calcium hydroxide removal

Seventy upper molar tooth models were randomly divided into 5 groups for Ca(OH)₂ removal. The tooth models were irrigated with different methods to remove Ca(OH)₂. The irrigation volume and duration per canal in each group were standardized as 6 mL and 60 s, respectively.

The examination of samples

Photographs of the samples were dimensionally standardized before comparison. Afterwards, before and after images were colorized with ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). During colorization, standard hue, value, and chroma were adjusted for all specimens. The obtained images were merged on top of each other, and the irrigated areas were determined (Fig. 1). After the calculation of the determined areas, results were obtained as the percentage of cleaned area. With these procedures, the coronal, middle, and apical isthmus areas on the model were examined. The calculation was done as follows.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS V23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the R programming language (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data did not meet normality assumptions, comparisons across groups and isthmus levels were performed using robust ANOVA implemented via the Walrus package. Pairwise multiple comparisons were conducted using a median-based percentile t bootstrap method. Quantitative data were expressed as median (minimum–maximum). Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²p) with corresponding confidence intervals. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

When the data obtained were analyzed, a significant difference was observed among the irrigation techniques regardless of isthmus level (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The median percentage of the cleaned area was 11.26% in the CNI group, 29.3% in the EDDY group, 28.22% in the PUI group, 37.96% in the PIPS group, and 47.71% in the SWEEPS group (Table 2). The SWEEPS group showed the highest median values and was significantly higher than all other irrigation groups (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The PIPS group ranked second, with significantly higher values than the PUI and CNI groups (all p < 0.001). The PUI group showed significantly higher values than the CNI group (p < 0.001). The EDDY group also demonstrated significantly higher values than the CNI group. The CNI group consistently showed the lowest cleaning values across all comparisons (p < 0.001) (Table 2). However, there was a significant difference between the cleaning rates of the isthmus regardless of the irrigation techniques (p = 0.007). The median cleaned area of the coronal isthmus was 38.54%, the median cleaned area of the middle isthmus was 34.88%, and the median cleaned area of the apical isthmus was 20.04%. The median cleaned area was greater in the middle and coronal isthmus compared with the apical isthmus (p = 0.007) (Table 2). The relationship between irrigation techniques and the isthmus was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The highest clearance rate was observed in the middle isthmus with SWEEPS irrigation, with a value of 52.07%, and the lowest clearance rate was found in the coronal isthmus with conventional irrigation, with a value of 7.18% (Table 2).

Partial eta squared (η²) values indicated that the main effect of the group was statistically significant and associated with a large effect size (η² = 0.176; 95% CI: 0.077–0.256). This value suggests that approximately 17.6% of the variance in the dependent variable was explained by the group factor, indicating a strong practical effect.

Although the main effect of isthmus was statistically significant, the effect size was considered small (η² = 0.050; 95% CI: 0.004–0.113). This finding indicates that the explanatory contribution of the isthmus factor was limited and its contribution to the total variance remained relatively low.

The Group × Isthmus interaction was both statistically highly significant and associated with a very large effect size (η² = 0.534; 95% CI: 0.423–0.592). This result demonstrates that the combined effect of the two factors on the dependent variable was substantial, explaining approximately 53.4% of the variance.

Reference thresholds for partial eta squared were interpreted as follows: 0.010–0.060 indicates a small effect, 0.060–0.140 indicates a medium effect, and values greater than 0.14 indicate a large effect size. A large effect was observed for the Group × Isthmus interaction (η² = 0.534), while Group showed a moderate effect (η² = 0.176) and Isthmus a small effect (η² = 0.050) (Table 1).

When comparing the median values of the percentage of cleared areas in the apical, middle, and coronal isthmuses in the statistical evaluation of the isthmuses, the SWEEPS and PIPS groups had significantly higher cleaning efficacy in the middle isthmus. In the coronal isthmus, the SWEEPS, PIPS groups showed significantly higher values than the PUI and CNI groups, but no significant difference was observed with EDDY. In all three isthmuses, the CNI group had the lowest cleaning efficacy with a significant difference. Apart from this, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the apical isthmus region (Table 2, Fig. 2). Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of various irrigation activation techniques in removing Ca(OH)₂ from isthmus-connected curved root canals using a standardized 3D-printed resin model. It has been reported that Ca(OH)₂ is removed using NaOCl and ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) together, but even when these solutions are used together, complete removal from the canal cannot be achieved [33]. For this study, we focused on comparing the activation methods rather than the solutions, so we used only NaOCl as the irrigation solution, consistent with similar studies in the literature [34, 35]. None of the tested activation techniques resulted in complete removal of Ca(OH)₂ from the isthmus areas. However, laser-activated irrigation (SWEEPS and PIPS) demonstrated higher cleaning efficacy compared to other activation systems, particularly in the middle isthmus. In the coronal isthmus, similar performance was observed among SWEEPS, PIPS, and EDDY, all of which showed higher values compared with conventional needle irrigation.

The use of a 3D-printed resin model replicating the complex isthmus morphology in curved molars represents a major strength of this study. Unlike traditional extracted tooth models that present high variability in canal dimensions and anatomical complexity [36], this approach provides a reproducible and controlled experimental environment [7]. Previous investigations have demonstrated the value of such models in studying irrigant dynamics and medicament removal in confined root canal spaces [7, 36, 37]. In contrast, other studies have relied on split-root techniques, groove preparations, or epoxy block models, which often fail to accurately simulate continuous three-dimensional canal anatomies and inter-canal communications [15, 38].

3D-printed models have certain disadvantages alongside all their advantages. The most significant disadvantage is the difference between the resin material simulating the root canal wall and the dentine tissue. Research has demonstrated that these resin materials are more hydrophobic than dentine tissue, causing irrigants to contact the surface at a higher contact angle. This difference negatively affects the penetration depth of the irrigants or dyes used [39–41]. The studies note that while the models significantly contribute to establishing a controlled experimental setup, the applicability of the findings to clinical practice is constrained by shifts in fluid dynamics resulting from this material difference [42, 43]. While the literature indicates that the elastic modulus of dentin tissue is higher than that of resin materials [44], to our knowledge, there is no study in the literature demonstrating the effect of this difference on shock waves and fluid dynamics in irrigation activation.

In terms of examination methods, the use of stereomicroscopy and image analysis to measure cleaned areas at different isthmus levels has enabled a spatially high-resolution assessment of cleaning effectiveness. While confocal microscopy and µCT imaging provide superior sensitivity, their applicability to larger sample numbers is limited due to substantial demands for time, money, skilled staff, and equipment [36, 37]. Despite these differences, the findings of our study are consistent with studies that examined micro-CT, which pointed to the inability to clean calcium hydroxide in the apical area [45–47].

In the present study, the irrigation group had a large and statistically significant effect on the percentage of cleaned area; this suggests that the irrigation technique is the primary determinant of cleaning effectiveness and explains a significant portion of the observed variance. In contrast, the isthmus type showed a statistically significant but small effect size; this suggests that anatomical variation makes a limited independent contribution to cleaning performance. Importantly, a very large and significant interaction effect was detected between the irrigation group and the isthmus level; this indicates that the effectiveness of irrigation systems is largely dependent on the anatomical location of the isthmus. This interaction accounts for a significant portion of the variability and underscores the clinical importance of considering anatomical complexity when evaluating irrigation performance during calcium hydroxide removal. This finding emphasizes that the effectiveness of irrigation strategies cannot be generalized without considering isthmus morphology.

Conventional needle irrigation (CNI) demonstrated the lowest efficacy in Ca(OH)₂ removal, particularly in the apical region, confirming the observations of Liu et al. [7] and Al-Jadaa et al. [48], who noted complications related to fluid dynamics and stagnation. Sonic devices like EndoActivator and EDDY exhibited moderate efficacy; nevertheless, despite EDDY’s elevated frequency (~ 6000 Hz) enhancing agitation, its performance was still inferior to PUI and laser methods, particularly in the apical isthmus [15]. Donnermeyer et al. reported that EDDY was more effective than manual irrigation; however, it was less effective than ultrasonic or laser techniques [37]. EDDY has moderate efficacy in the current study. It was determined to be considerably more successful than manual irrigation and significantly less effective than SWEEPS, with no significant difference observed between PUI and PIPS. Our findings suggest that sonic systems such as EDDY can be used as an advantageous intermediate step in clinical practice but may not be sufficient on their own in cases with complex morphologies. In particular, the cost and accessibility advantages of EDDY make it a viable option for routine clinical cases [49], while its combination with additional activation methods may be recommended in complex cases.

Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) is commonly considered the gold standard among non-laser techniques because of its capacity to generate acoustic streaming and cavitation within the canal. Based on the results, the PUI group demonstrated greater efficacy than the manual approach alone in removing Ca(OH)₂ from the isthmus. However, PUI had restricted effectiveness in the deeper regions of the isthmus, as evidenced by our results and those of Park et al. [50] and Karaoğlu & Aydın [51]. These results obtained in the apical region may arise from energy attenuation and limited tip mobility in narrow anatomical regions. This suggests that strategies such as enlarging the canal to accommodate the tip used and increasing the irrigation duration may enhance the effectiveness of PUI [52]. Additionally, combining PUI systems with more flexible tip designs is another approach that could be applied to address anatomical access limitations.

Laser-activated irrigation techniques provided the most efficient removal of Ca(OH)₂. This enhanced performance can be attributed not only to the powerful photoacoustic flow and shock wave formation but also to the consistent flow of the irrigant during the activation process. However, to our knowledge, no study has specifically examined the effects of continuous irrigation during activation. Nevertheless, the fluid dynamics generated in laser-assisted systems allow the irrigant to thoroughly penetrate narrow and curved isthmus regions without the need for deep tip insertion [36, 38, 53]. This benefit is especially significant in conservative endodontics, when minimal enlargement is preferred [50]. However, the superiority of specific techniques was not uniform across all anatomical regions. In the coronal isthmus, SWEEPS and PIPS demonstrated comparable performance in the coronal isthmus, with no statistically significant differences observed between SWEEPS, PIPS, and EDDY. However, both SWEEPS and PIPS showed higher cleaning efficacy compared with conventional needle irrigation and PUI, indicating improved performance of advanced activation systems in the coronal region, while differences among conventional and sonic/ultrasonic approaches were less pronounced. This finding is consistent with previous findings showing that activation in the coronal region increases irrigant penetration into lateral structures by facilitating greater propagation of acoustic and shock waves [48–52].

Similarly, in the middle isthmus, both SWEEPS and PIPS demonstrated improved cleaning compared with the other irrigation techniques; however, the difference between the two advanced activation systems was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the advantage of laser-activated systems may be more pronounced when compared to conventional methods rather than between advanced technologies themselves.

In contrast, in the apical isthmus, conventional needle irrigation showed significantly lower cleaning efficacy compared with all other activation techniques. No significant differences were observed among the activated irrigation systems in this region, highlighting the limited effectiveness of irrigation activation methods in the apical third, likely due to restricted irrigant exchange and reduced energy transmission, regardless of the activation mechanism used. This advantage can be attributed to the reduced energy transmission and irrigant volume reaching the apical third, irrespective of the activation method employed.

Laser-activated irrigation systems, particularly SWEEPS and PIPS, demonstrated higher cleaning efficacy in specific anatomical regions compared with conventional and sonic/ultrasonic methods. However, no technique was consistently effective across all isthmus regions. In light of the present findings, both null hypotheses were rejected.

Even the most efficient methods failed to attain absolute cleanliness in any area, confirming the findings of Kustarci et al. [38] and Yang et al. [53], who highlighted the challenges associated with the removal of Ca(OH)₂ from complex anatomies. The fact that no one method can provide absolute cleanliness demonstrates that the limitations of irrigation still exist and that multi-stage or combined activation protocols need to be developed. Clinically, the sequential or simultaneous use of different activation techniques can increase the effectiveness of irrigation. Furthermore, new-generation irrigation solutions, particle carrier agents or nanoparticle-supported systems, and new tip configurations are expected to be integrated into clinical practice in the future.

This study provided data on the resolution of a common clinical condition that affects treatment efficacy. Calcium hydroxide has an important place in endodontic clinical practice. We intended to get data applicable to the clinic through a planned investigation on contemporary procedures and complex canal regions regarding the cleanability of this material, which is the primary concern in its utilization. The root canal model produced was prepared in accordance with the clinical scenario related to this material, as it is both inclined and has an isthmus structure at 3 different levels. Despite earlier studies concentrating on the isthmuses, no investigations have been conducted in this region featuring such a morphology connecting the inclined canals. The wide range of irrigation techniques employed, including contemporary methods, enables the study to furnish critical data for clinicians and researchers utilizing these systems.

However, there are some limitations to our work. The first limitation of this study is the use of resin models, which, despite anatomical accuracy, differ from dentin in thermal conductivity, surface energy, and hardness, which are factors that may affect irrigant dynamics [7, 36, 39–43]. Future studies using dentin-like materials and fluid dynamics analysis may yield more clinically relevant insights. The other limitation is although NaOCl was used as the sole irrigant to focus on the comparison of activation techniques, in clinical practice it is typically used in combination with EDTA for calcium hydroxide removal. Therefore, this methodological choice may limit the direct clinical extrapolation of the findings. Another limitation is that the examination method used does not provide 3D data. Examinations such as micro-CT imaging provide more detailed data compared to the imaging method used in our study. However, the radiopacity of calcium hydroxide, when using micro-CT, can cause scattering to a small extent, leading to very minor deviations in the measurement of the cleaned area [54].

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, irrigation activation technique and isthmus anatomy significantly influenced the removal of calcium hydroxide from complex root canal systems. Consequently, future research should focus on optimizing irrigation strategies through combined or sequential activation approaches and validating these findings under clinical conditions.

Conclusion

In this ex vivo study using a standardized 3D-printed isthmus-connected root canal model, none of the irrigation activation techniques achieved complete removal of calcium hydroxide. Cleaning efficacy was influenced by both irrigation technique and isthmus location, with region-dependent differences in performance. Laser-activated irrigation systems demonstrated higher cleaning efficacy in specific isthmus regions; however, their performance varied across anatomical levels. Conventional needle irrigation consistently showed the lowest cleaning efficacy in all isthmus regions. In the apical isthmus, activation techniques showed comparable performance, whereas conventional needle irrigation exhibited significantly lower values than the other groups. Overall, the findings indicate that irrigation performance in isthmus-containing root canals follows a complex, anatomy-dependent pattern.
