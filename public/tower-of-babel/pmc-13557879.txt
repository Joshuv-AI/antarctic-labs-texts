Prognostic impact of smoking in non-small cell lung cancer: histology-specific findings from a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hyo Kyen Park, Geun Dong Lee, Sehoon Choi, Hyeong Ryul Kim, Yong-Hee Kim, Dong Kwan Kim, Seung-Il Park, Jae Kwang Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking negatively influences postoperative outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but its prognostic effect may differ by histologic subtype. However, whether this impact varies between adenocarcinoma (ADC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SqCC) remains poorly understood. We aimed to quantify this difference using a pre-specified interaction analysis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 8,103 patients who underwent curative-intent resection for pathologic stage I–III NSCLC between 2011 and 2022. Smoking status was categorized as never versus ever (smoking history). Overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were evaluated using multivariable Cox models with a smoking × histology interaction term and additional stage-stratified analyses.ResultsIn the primary multivariable interaction model, smoking history was associated with worse OS in the reference ADC subgroup [hazard ratio (HR): 1.33; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.10–1.60; P=0.003]. Histology-specific analysis showed that smoking history was associated with worse OS in ADC subgroup (HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.02–1.50; P=0.03), whereas in SqCC subgroup, the estimate was imprecise and binary smoking history did not clearly discriminate prognosis (HR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.67–1.50; P=0.98). The smoking × histology interaction for OS was not significant (interaction HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.50–1.09; P=0.12).ConclusionsSmoking history was associated with worse postoperative OS, with the most consistent prognostic signal observed in ADC subgroup. In SqCC cohort, binary smoking history showed limited prognostic discrimination, likely reflecting near-universal smoking exposure rather than absence of biological relevance. Smoking status should be interpreted within histologic context during postoperative risk assessment.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Smoking is associated with worse overall survival after curative-intent resection for non-small cell lung cancer, but its prognostic signal was most consistent in adenocarcinoma.

What is known and what is new?

• Smoking is associated with worse postoperative outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer, but whether its prognostic impact differs according to histologic subtype has remained uncertain.

• This study demonstrates that the prognostic information conveyed by smoking history should be interpreted according to histologic context. Binary smoking history shows limited prognostic discrimination in squamous cell carcinoma (SqCC), where smoking exposure is nearly universal; however, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that smoking lacks biological or clinical relevance in SqCC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Smoking history should be interpreted in the context of histologic subtype when assessing postoperative prognosis in resected non-small cell lung cancer.

• Binary smoking history should be interpreted cautiously as a standalone prognostic marker in SqCC.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is the leading etiologic factor for lung cancer and continues to influence clinical outcomes even after curative-intent treatment (1-4). Numerous studies have shown that smoking is associated with increased risk of recurrence and poorer long-term survival after resection of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, most previous studies have focused on early-stage disease or have not adequately accounted for histologic subtype in their analyses, leaving unclear whether the prognostic impact of smoking differs across tumor types (2,5-10).

This knowledge gap is particularly relevant in NSCLC because adenocarcinoma (ADC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SqCC) represent biologically and clinically distinct histologic subtypes. They differ substantially in terms of genetic alterations, smoking-related mutational patterns, therapeutic responsiveness, and competing risk structures (11). Given these differences, the prognostic impact of smoking may vary across histologic subtypes. Nonetheless, few studies have formally evaluated this possibility using interaction analysis, and most findings remain descriptive rather than inferential.

Recent large-scale cohort studies have suggested that smoking status may serve as an independent prognostic factor across multiple pathological stages and have even proposed its potential incorporation into staging systems in the future (12). However, these studies did not account for marked variation in histologic composition—particularly the shifting relative proportion of ADC and SqCC cases across stages—which may mask or distort differences in the effect of smoking. Without an evaluation of whether the impact of smoking is affected by histology, conclusions regarding its prognostic value may be misleading. To clarify this issue, we used a large, stage-inclusive surgical cohort to formally test the interaction between smoking status and histology, as well as to determine whether the prognostic value of smoking differs between ADC and SqCC. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0598/rc).

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed a prospectively maintained institutional database of patients who underwent curative-intent resection with systematic lymph node dissection for pathologic stage I–III NSCLC between January 2011 and December 2022. Patients were excluded if they had small-cell lung cancer; concurrent malignancies; prior neoadjuvant therapy; synchronous or metachronous double primary lung cancer; pathologic stage IV disease; minor histologic subtypes (e.g., large-cell carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, or lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma); incomplete (R1/R2) resection; bilobectomy, sleeve lobectomy or pneumonectomy; or were lost to follow-up immediately after surgery. The selection process used to recruit the final cohort included in the analysis is described in Figure 1.

Patient selection flowchart. SCLC, small cell lung cancer.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (No. 2025-1532; approved December 13, 2025), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection and variables

Clinical, surgical, and pathological data were extracted from the institutional electronic medical records. These included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), baseline comorbidity burden, preoperative pulmonary function [forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO)], tumor histology, pathological stage, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), extranodal extension (ENE), surgical extent (sublobar resection vs. lobectomy), surgical approach (thoracotomy vs. video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery), and adjuvant therapy. Baseline comorbidity burden was assessed as the number of predefined comorbid conditions and categorized as 0, 1, or ≥2. The assessed conditions were diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic respiratory disease, previous pulmonary tuberculosis, hepatic disease, renal disease, and previous malignancy. Pathologic stage was reassigned according to the 8th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification by retrospective chart review, with discrepancies adjudicated by a thoracic pathologist. Histologic subtype was classified as ADC or SqCC according to the World Health Organization classification.

Smoking status was determined from preoperative patient interviews and medical records. Patients who never smoked were defined as those who had never smoked or had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Patients who formerly smoked were defined as those who had quit smoking more than one year before diagnosis. Patients currently smoking at diagnosis were defined as those who were actively smoking at diagnosis or had quit within one year before diagnosis (13,14). For the primary analysis, smoking history was dichotomized into patients who never smoked versus patients with a smoking history, defined as those who formerly smoked or were currently smoking at diagnosis (15,16). Additional analyses evaluated smoking status using three categories: never, former, and current smoking. Postoperative smoking continuation data were not uniformly available and therefore were not assessed.

Outcomes

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS). OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to death from any cause. RFS was defined as the interval from surgery to the first documented recurrence or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients who were alive without recurrence at the last follow-up date were censored at the date of last contact. Patients were routinely subjected to clinical evaluation and chest CT every 3–6 months. Recurrence was diagnosed based on radiologic or pathologic confirmation in multidisciplinary review.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Analyses were performed using complete cases for the variables included in each model; missing values were minimal and imputation was not performed. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

The association between smoking history and survival outcomes was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Sex was included as a covariate in the primary multivariable models because it is a clinically relevant prognostic factor and was strongly associated with smoking history in this cohort (Cramér’s V =0.75). Smoking history × histology interaction terms were included to evaluate whether the prognostic association of smoking history differed between ADC and SqCC. Endpoint-specific multivariable models were constructed using clinically relevant covariates selected a priori. Stage-stratified analyses were also performed.

Additional sensitivity analyses included sex-specific models, histology-specific multivariable models, three-category smoking-status analyses, cumulative smoking exposure analyses by pack-year category, smoking cessation-duration analyses, histology-specific propensity score-matched analyses, a subgroup analysis limited to patients who did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy, and a precision assessment for the SqCC subgroup. Adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were coded according to whether patients received treatment. Regimen-level details, number of cycles, dose intensity, treatment completion, and treatment-related toxicity were not uniformly available. Potential selection bias related to surgical exclusions was assessed descriptively by comparing the final analytic cohort with patients in the pre-exclusion screening dataset who otherwise met the core eligibility criteria but were excluded because of extended/complex resection beyond standard lobectomy or incomplete resection.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with no major violations detected. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 11,512 patients underwent curative-intent lung cancer resection, of whom 8,103 met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analytic cohort (Figure 1). The median follow-up duration was 62 months [interquartile range (IQR), 37–91 months]. The mean age was 63.2±9.3 years, and 52.9% of the patients were male. ADC accounted for 85.9% of the tumors and SqCC for 14.1%. Overall, 54.4% of the patients had never smoked and 45.6% had a smoking history.

Compared with patients who had never smoked, those with a smoking history were more likely to be male, had poorer pulmonary function, and demonstrated higher rates of LVI and ENE (all P<0.001). The proportion of SqCC cases was substantially higher among patients with a smoking history, and they were more often diagnosed with stage III disease. Additional baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A more granular categorization of smoking status (never, former, and current smoking) is provided in Table S1. The distribution of smoking status differed markedly according to sex and histology. Among patients with SqCC, 1,048 of 1,140 (91.9%) had a smoking history, whereas only 92 (8.1%) had never smoked. In contrast, among female patients with ADC, 3,559 of 3,749 (94.9%) had never smoked, reflecting substantial sex- and histology-related imbalance in smoking exposure (Table S2).

Overall prognostic effect of smoking

Univariable associations with OS and RFS are shown in Table S3.

In multivariable Cox analyses including sex and other clinicopathologic covariates (Table 2), smoking history was associated with worse OS in the reference ADC group (HR, 1.33; 95% CI: 1.10–1.60; P=0.003). The smoking history × histology interaction for OS was not statistically significant (interaction HR, 0.74; 95% CI: 0.50–1.09; P=0.12). For RFS, smoking history was not significantly associated with the outcome (HR, 1.13; 95% CI: 0.96–1.34; P=0.14), and no significant smoking history × histology interaction was observed (interaction HR, 0.89; 95% CI: 0.56–1.44; P=0.64).

Histology-specific prognostic effect of smoking

In histology-specific multivariable models, smoking history was associated with worse OS in ADC subgroup (HR, 1.24; 95% CI: 1.02–1.50; P=0.03), whereas binary smoking history was less clearly discriminative in SqCC subgroup (HR, 1.01; 95% CI: 0.67–1.50; P=0.98; Figure 2 and Table S4). For RFS, smoking history was not significantly associated with outcome in either ADC (HR, 1.11; 95% CI: 0.94–1.32; P=0.22) or SqCC subgroups (HR, 1.01; 95% CI: 0.61–1.67; P=0.98).

Additional smoking-exposure analyses are summarized in the Supplementary Materials. In sex-specific models, the association between smoking history and worse OS in ADC was observed in both male and female patients, whereas estimates in SqCC were imprecise because of the small number of patients who had never smoked (Table S5). Among patients with SqCC, only 92 (8.1%) had never smoked. With 435 observed OS events and a never-smoking proportion of 8.1%, the estimated number of events required for 80% power was 2,125 for an assumed HR of 1.25 and 644 for an assumed HR of 1.50, indicating limited precision for detecting moderate smoking-associated survival differences in this subgroup (Table S6). In three-category analyses, patients currently smoking at diagnosis had the highest OS risk in the overall cohort compared with those who never smoked (HR, 1.45; 95% CI: 1.17–1.79; P<0.001; Table S7). The corresponding OS and RFS curves according to never, former, and current smoking status within each histologic subtype are shown in Figures S1,S2. Cumulative exposure and cessation-duration analyses showed higher OS risk with greater pack-year exposure and with current or recent smoking, while long-term cessation was not significantly associated with worse OS compared with never smoking (Tables S8,S9).

Stage-stratified analyses and surgical-exclusion assessment

In stage-stratified analyses, smoking history was associated with worse OS only in stage III disease (HR, 1.37; 95% CI: 1.02–1.86), but not in stage I (HR, 1.23; 95% CI: 0.94–1.61) or stage II disease (HR, 0.98; 95% CI: 0.67–1.43; Figure 3A). For RFS, no significant association was observed in stage I (HR, 1.18; 95% CI: 0.92–1.52), stage II (HR, 0.79; 95% CI: 0.56–1.12), or stage III disease (HR, 1.28; 95% CI: 0.97–1.69). The distribution of histology according to pathologic stage is shown in Figure 3B. Among patients who did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy, smoking history remained associated with worse OS in ADC subgroup (HR, 1.47; 95% CI: 1.17–1.85; P<0.001), whereas in SqCC cohort, the estimate was imprecise and binary smoking history did not show clear prognostic separation (HR, 0.94; 95% CI: 0.63–1.40; P=0.75; Table S10). Although the OS interaction term reached statistical significance in this subgroup, this exploratory finding should be interpreted with caution. Histology-specific propensity score matching was performed as an exploratory sensitivity analysis. In the ADC subgroup, matching improved covariate balance for most variables, although residual imbalance remained for age and LVI. After additional adjustment for these residual imbalanced covariates, smoking history was not significantly associated with OS or RFS in the matched ADC cohort (Tables S11,S12).

To assess potential selection bias from surgical exclusions, we compared the final analytic cohort with surgically excluded patients who otherwise met the core eligibility criteria. Extended/complex resection beyond standard lobectomy was more frequent among surgically excluded patients with SqCC than among those with ADC (81.8% vs. 69.2%), and smoking history remained highly prevalent among surgically excluded patients with SqCC (92.7%; Tables S13,S14).

Discussion

In this large surgical cohort of patients with resected stage I–III NSCLC, smoking history was associated with worse postoperative OS, with the most consistent adverse prognostic signal observed in the ADC subgroup. In the primary multivariable model including sex and other clinicopathologic covariates, the formal smoking history × histology interaction was not statistically significant. However, histology-specific analyses showed that smoking history remained associated with worse OS in ADC subgroup, whereas binary smoking history showed limited prognostic discrimination in SqCC cohort. These findings support histology-aware interpretation of smoking history in postoperative risk assessment, and underscore that limited prognostic discrimination in SqCC reflects exposure saturation rather than absence of biological relevance.

Although previous studies consistently showed that smoking was associated with worse postoperative outcomes in NSCLC, they did not differentiate between histologic subtypes (1-4). Most of these studies relied on descriptive or subgroup analyses, limiting their ability to determine true effect modification (2,7-10). A recent large-scale analysis from the IASLC 9th TNM project also identified smoking as an independent adverse prognostic factor across multiple subgroups, but the magnitude of its effect varied by histologic subtype—being more evident in ADC and less clearly discriminative in SqCC cohort (12). The present study extends these observations by evaluating smoking history across histologic subtypes in a large surgical cohort and by additionally examining sex-specific patterns, detailed smoking categories, cumulative exposure, cessation duration, and stage-stratified associations. In the present cohort, sex was strongly correlated with smoking history (Cramér’s V =0.75), reflecting the common epidemiologic pattern in East Asia where female patients with ADC are predominantly never smoking. Accordingly, the attenuation of the formal interaction after inclusion of sex highlights the strong interrelationship among smoking history, sex, and histology in this population.

Stage-stratified analyses provided additional context for this interpretation (Figure 3A). Although the smoking-associated hazard varied across pathologic stages, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because histologic composition also differed by stage (Figure 3B). ADC cohort comprised the majority of stage I and stage III tumors, but represented a smaller proportion of stage II tumors. Therefore, apparent stage-specific differences in the prognostic association of smoking may partly reflect differences in histologic composition across stages rather than stage alone.

The limited discriminatory value of binary smoking history in SqCC is best understood in the context of near-universal smoking exposure rather than absence of smoking-related biology. In our cohort, more than 90% of patients with SqCC had a smoking history, leaving only a small reference group of those who had never smoked. This exposure saturation limits the ability of a binary never-versus-ever smoking variable to separate prognostic risk within SqCC. By contrast, ADC subgroup included both smoking-related and non-smoking-related disease subsets, making smoking history more informative as a prognostic marker.

Several biological and epidemiologic mechanisms may explain why smoking history remained more informative in ADC than in SqCC cohort, despite the absence of a statistically significant formal interaction after multivariable adjustment. SqCC is typically characterized by extensive tobacco-related genomic injury, including frequent TP53 alterations, SOX2 amplification, and high tumor mutational burden. In this setting, additional stratification by binary smoking history may provide limited incremental prognostic information (17-19). In contrast, ADC includes both smoking-related tumors, often enriched for alterations such as KRAS and TP53, and tumors less strongly related to smoking, including those with targetable driver alterations such as EGFR or ALK (20). The high prevalence of EGFR-mutated ADC among patients who never smoked in East Asian populations may partly contribute to the more favorable prognosis of this subgroup and widen the observed survival gap according to smoking history. In addition, smoking-related cardiovascular and pulmonary comorbidities may influence OS and complicate interpretation of cancer-specific prognostic effects, particularly in SqCC, where patients with smoking history predominate. Finally, prior studies have associated smoking with aggressive histologic features in ADC, including solid or micropapillary components and spread through air spaces, which are linked to invasiveness, LVI, and nodal metastasis (21-26). These mechanisms may partly explain why smoking history remains clinically informative in ADC while showing limited discriminatory value in SqCC.

Additional smoking-exposure analyses support the clinical relevance of smoking status beyond the binary classification. Patients currently smoking at diagnosis showed the highest OS risk, and greater cumulative smoking exposure was associated with worse outcomes. Conversely, long-term smoking cessation was not significantly associated with worse OS compared with never smoking. These findings are consistent with previous reports, including the study by Wang et al., suggesting that smoking cessation around diagnosis may improve lung cancer outcomes (1-3,27,28). However, because postoperative smoking continuation was not uniformly available in our database, our results should be interpreted as reflecting smoking status at diagnosis or preoperative assessment rather than the effect of post-resection smoking behavior.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study, which may have limited generalizability to populations with different ethnic backgrounds, smoking patterns, and histologic distributions. Second, smoking exposure was obtained from preoperative interviews and medical records, and therefore recall bias or social desirability bias could not be excluded. Third, smoking status was primarily categorized as a binary variable (never versus ever), which may have not fully captured dose-response relationships. However, sensitivity analyses using three-category smoking status, pack-year categories, and smoking cessation duration yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Tables S7-S9), supporting the robustness of the binary classification as a pragmatic prognostic variable. Fourth, postoperative smoking continuation was not uniformly captured. Fifth, sex was strongly associated with smoking history in this cohort; therefore, residual confounding may have remained despite adjustment, and the histology-specific findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of effect modification. Although exploratory propensity score matching was performed, adequate balance could not be fully achieved in the ADC subgroup, with residual imbalance remaining for age and LVI. Sixth, the number of patients with SqCC who had never smoked was small, limiting precision for estimating smoking-associated hazards in this subgroup. Seventh, exclusion of patients who underwent extended/complex resection beyond standard lobectomy or incomplete resection may have introduced selection bias, particularly in SqCC cohort. To address this concern, we performed an additional descriptive comparison of surgically excluded patients by histologic subtype, but residual selection bias could not be excluded. Eighth, regimen-level details of adjuvant chemotherapy, treatment completion, dose intensity, and treatment-related toxicity were not uniformly available. Finally, molecular and genomic data were not available, and residual confounding inherent to observational studies could not be fully excluded.

Conclusions

Smoking history was associated with worse postoperative OS after resection for NSCLC, with the most consistent prognostic signal observed in ADC. In SqCC, binary smoking history showed limited prognostic discrimination, likely reflecting near-universal smoking exposure rather than the absence of biological relevance. These findings support histology-aware interpretation of smoking history during postoperative risk assessment and may inform more individualized prognostic counseling in patients with resected NSCLC.
