Enhanced anticancer efficacy of Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide-coated silver nanostars on breast cancer cells
Authors: Ítala C. Silva, Miguel P. de Almeida, Eulália Pereira, Octávio L. Franco, Sónia Gonçalves, Nuno C. Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer remains a major global health challenge due to its rising incidence and the limitations of conventional chemotherapeutic options. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) with dual antimicrobial and anticancer activities have emerged as promising alternatives; however, their clinical application is limited by issues such as stability, selectivity, and delivery. Nanocarrier systems, particularly silver nanostars (AgNSs), can address these challenges by improving peptide delivery and cellular interaction. This study explores the nanocomplex AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 as a combinatory therapeutic platform against breast cancer. AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibited enhanced anticancer effects against both hormone-dependent (MCF-7) and hormone-independent (MDA-MB-231) breast cancer cells, with higher sensitivity observed in MDA-MB-231 cells. Selectivity analysis indicated moderate preferential cytotoxicity toward cancer cells compared to non-tumorigenic MCF-10A cells. Biophysical and cellular assessments revealed subtype-dependent morphological and mechanical alterations, including changes in cell area, surface roughness, and elasticity, with more pronounced effects in cancer cells. Compared to free Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed a concentration-dependent therapeutic window with preferential activity against breast cancer cells, particularly MDA-MB-231, supporting its potential as a nanoplatform for further development in targeted cancer therapy.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies for women and a major cause of cancer-associated mortality, with outcomes strongly influenced by the marked molecular and clinical heterogeneity that characterizes luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtypes [1]. The lack of alternative treatments and the aggressive nature of the current chemotherapeutic strategies available are among the challenges to overcome in breast cancer therapy. Another limitation is associated with the uniqueness and heterogeneity of breast tumors [2,3]. Invasive tumor subtypes are associated with a higher risk of both local and distant metastasis, leading to relapses and shorter overall survival [2,4]. Particularly challenging is the TNBC subtype, which tends to metastasize to multiple visceral sites and has a poor prognosis [2,4]. The limited treatment options, primarily restricted to chemotherapy, combined with the aggressive nature of TNBC, make it an extremely difficult disease to treat [5].

One of the major challenges in breast cancer therapy is drug resistance [6]. Tumors may fail to respond to initial treatment due to intrinsic or acquired factors and can develop resistance over time through multiple mechanisms. These include increased drug efflux, upregulation of aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs), enhanced DNA repair capacity, dysregulation of the cell cycle, and evasion of apoptosis [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Additionally, tumor microenvironment–related factors, such as hypoxia and redox imbalance, play a critical role in modulating these resistance pathways [11]. Most conventional cancer treatments, including those used for breast cancer, are not selectively cytotoxic to malignant cells and therefore also affect healthy tissues. This lack of specificity highlights the need for therapeutic strategies that preferentially target tumor cells while sparing healthy ones [5,12,13].

In this context, tumor-targeting and tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoplatforms have emerged as promising strategies to improve therapeutic selectivity, enhance intratumoral drug accumulation, and enable controlled drug release [11]. An attractive feature of these systems is their ability to respond to breast cancer-associated stimuli, such as acidic pH, elevated glutathione levels, hypoxia, and specific enzyme activities. This allows them to remain stable during systemic circulation while selectively releasing drugs within the tumor microenvironment. This approach with stimulus-responsive nanoplatforms represents an important advance in nanomedicine for breast cancer, offering potential to improve therapeutic efficacy, biosafety, and controlled drug delivery [11]. Prodrug-based nanostrategies also represent a promising approach for breast cancer therapy. For example, disulfide bond-driven nanoassembly of lipophilic epirubicin for breast cancer enabled redox-responsive self-assembly and selective intracellular activation under reducing conditions, highlighting the translational potential of stimulus-responsive delivery systems [14]. Effective targeting strategies also rely on prognostic biomarker identification. Given the heterogeneity of breast cancer, computational approaches such as FUNMarker have shown that cluster-specific biomarkers can improve patient stratification and survival prediction, supporting more rational design of nanoparticle-, drug-, and ligand-based therapies [3]. In parallel, a mechanistic understanding of breast cancer progression should also be considered when framing new therapeutic strategies. Breast cancer progression can be linked to the degradation of key metabolic enzymes, such as citrate synthase or fumarate hydratase, via chaperone-mediated autophagy, reinforcing the role of metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial dysfunction, and stress adaptation in tumor aggressiveness [15]. This mechanistic context indicated that therapies targeting membrane integrity, redox balance, and intracellular stress responses may be particularly effective in aggressive tumor subtypes such as TNBC [15].

Recently, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional molecules used in cancer therapeutics [[16], [17], [18]]. Some AMPs demonstrate dual activity as both antimicrobial and anticancer peptides (ACPs) [19]. Most natural AMPs are short, amphipathic, and predominantly cationic peptides, also known as host defense peptides (HDPs). They are produced by various cells and tissues across virtually all living organisms [20]. AMPs exhibit immunomodulatory and antibacterial activities, supporting their broad-spectrum effectiveness against a wide range of infectious agents [21,22]. Their desirable physicochemical properties, including a cationic charge, amphipathic structure, membrane-targeting ability, small molecular size, and potential in drug design and novel therapeutic strategies, have garnered significant attention [23,24]. A key advantage of using ACPs in cancer treatment is their ability to selectively interact with negatively charged cancer cell membranes and overcome the multidrug resistance associated with conventional chemotherapeutic drugs [25].

Pa-MAP 1.9 is a cationic synthetic analogue derived from HPLC-8, a natural peptide isolated from the polar fish Pleuronectes americanus [26]. Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibits a random coil structure in water, which transitions to an α-helix structure upon interaction with lipid bilayers, leading to extensive interactions with negatively charged membranes and promoting bacterial cell membrane destabilization [27,28]. Recent studies have demonstrated the antimicrobial activity of Pa-MAP 1.9 against various bacterial strains, including multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae) [27,28]. Additionally, Pa-MAP 1.9 can act as an anti-biofilm agent by inhibiting biofilm formation and disrupting established bacterial biofilms [27]. This membrane-active behavior also supports a plausible anticancer mechanism, in which selective interactions with anionic breast cancer cell membrane may induce loss of membrane integrity, while triggering downstream stress responses and reduced viability [15]. Despite these advantages, AMPs face challenges that limit their clinical translation, including low bioavailability, short half-life, and susceptibility to proteolytic degradation [29]. Interactions with cancer cell membranes may result in lower diffusion rates compared to other therapeutic agents, and undesired interactions with healthy cells due to a lack of specificity may lead to toxicity toward mammalian cells [30,31]. These obstacles have slowed down the use of peptides in cancer therapeutics; however, they can be overcome through the integration of AMPs into advanced nanoparticle-based formulations [30].

Nanotechnology offers a promising approach for developing more selective and less toxic cancer therapies, with nanocarriers playing a pivotal role in enhancing drug properties in various ways [11,14,[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Among these nanomaterials, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are well-documented in the current literature for their antimicrobial properties and, more recently, for their anticancer potential [36,37]. Some studies have also highlighted the inherent antimicrobial activity of silver nanostars (AgNSs) [38,39]. In a short time, they have gained significance due to their unique branched morphology, large surface area, and tunable surface chemistry and optical properties, which allow their use in therapeutics, especially in biomedical applications including drug delivery, bioimaging, photothermal therapy, and cancer treatment [30,40,41] [42]. Furthermore, AgNPs can be engineered to selectively target cancer cell membranes, further supporting the relevance of Ag-based nanosystems in this context [43,44]. AgNPs have demonstrated anticancer activity and have been explored in the cancer research field in combination with different drugs [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. AgNPs are specifically noted for their ability to reach tumor sites via passive or active targeting [45,46,[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Proper synthesis methods are crucial for optimizing nanoparticle properties, including particle size, shape, stability, charge, and surface chemistry, which can significantly influence cellular uptake and drug mobility [55]. Particle size, shape, and charge are important characteristics that can influence cellular uptake and drug mobility. AgNSs present a large surface area-to-volume ratio, which maximizes the surface area available for molecule binding, therefore improving the distribution of higher local doses. Metal-based nanoplatforms beyond silver, such as copper- and calcium-based nanoparticles, are also being investigated for breast cancer therapy, as they may provide distinct catalytic, ionic, imaging, or delivery-related advantages [56].

From the perspective of combinatory cancer therapy, binding AMPs to AgNSs represents a promising strategy that could improve therapeutic efficacy and modulate selectivity toward breast cancer cells [47,57,58]. Thus, combining the membrane-active peptide Pa-MAP 1.9 with AgNSs is consistent with current advances in tumor-targeting and mechanism-based nanomedicine. This study investigated the anticancer activity of AgNSs coated with the cationic peptide Pa-MAP 1.9 (AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9) and examined how their interactions with breast cancer cells and their membranes influence their biological activity. The nanocomplex showed enhanced anticancer activity compared with the peptide alone, while showing differential responses across breast cancer cell lines and modest selectivity over non-tumorigenic breast cells. High-resolution imaging and biomechanical analyses provided insight into the effects of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 on the biophysical, morphological, and biomechanical modifications induced in breast cancer cell membranes following treatment, contributing to the understanding of the nanocomplex mechanism of action.

Materials and methods

General reagents

Hydroxylamine (50% w/v), silver nitrate, sodium hydroxide, sodium citrate, nitric acid, and hydrochloric acid were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The cell proliferation kit II (XTT) was acquired from Roche Applied Science (Mannheim, Germany). LIVE/DEAD viability/cytotoxicity kit for mammalian cells and FITC annexin V/dead cell apoptosis kit were purchased from Invitrogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). CellMask orange plasma membrane stain, Hoechst 33342 fluorescent stain, Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), Dulbecco's modified eagle medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12), heat-inactivated horse serum, cholera toxin, insulin, epidermal growth factor (EGF), hydrocortisone, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, TrypLE Express Enzyme, and trypsin-EDTA were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ cm, 25 °C) was used throughout the experiments. Other reagents were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), unless otherwise stated.

Peptides

The peptide Pa-MAP 1.9 (LAAKLTKAATKLTAALTKLAAALTAAAT) and its scrambled version, s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (KALAAATAAAAALATLTKTTKTLALLKA), were synthesized and purchased from Genscript Biotech (Rijswijk, Netherlands), with >95% purity. The scrambled peptide was generated by randomly permuting the original peptide sequence, while maintaining the total amino acid residue composition. To evaluate peptide localization in the cell, FITC-labeled Pa-MAP 1.9 (FITC-Ahx-LAAKLTKAATKLTAALTKLAAALTAAAT-COOH) was also used. HPLC and MS data of unlabeled and labeled peptides are presented as Supporting Figs. S1, S2, and S3. Peptide stock solutions were prepared in ultrapure water and stored at −20 °C until use.

Cell lines and growth conditions

MCF-7 human breast cancer cell line (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC), Public Health England, Salisbury, UK), MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell line, and MCF-10A human epithelial breast cell line (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA) were used in this study. MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. MCF-10A epithelial breast cell line was cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 5% heat-inactivated horse serum, 100 ng/mL cholera toxin, 10 μg/mL insulin, 20 ng/mL EGF, and 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone. All cell cultures were grown as a monolayer in T75 flasks and incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. To detach cells in subculturing, TrypLE Express Enzyme was used in the MCF-7 cell line, and Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) was used in the MDA-MB-231 and MCF-10A cell lines.

Synthesis and characterization of silver nanostars

AgNSs were prepared according to Garcia-Leis et al. [59], with some modifications [38]. The colloidal suspension was stored at 4ᵒC in the dark until further use. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) were used to characterize the synthesized nanoparticles. Experimental details can be found in the Supporting Information and below.

Transmission electron microscopy of nanoparticles

Nanostar samples for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging were prepared by the slow evaporation method. For measurements, a droplet of the aqueous suspension of AgNSs was placed on 150-mesh carbon-coated Formvar grids. Samples were dried and then imaged on a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN transmission electron microscope from FEI, operating at a voltage of 120 keV and equipped with an Olympus-SIS Veleta CCD Camera (FEI Company Philips Electron Optics, Hillsboro, OR, USA). The average diameter ± standard deviation (SD) of AgNSs was obtained by counting randomly selected particles in TEM images analyzed with ImageJ software v. 1.53t (NIH, USA) (https://imagej.net/nih-image/).

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to analyze AgNSs dispersed in Milli-Q water (pH 7.0). Samples were gently vortexed before preparation. Nanostars were adsorbed onto 100-mesh carbon-coated Formvar grids by briefly dipping the grids into the suspension. Excess liquid was removed by blotting with filter paper, and grids were glued onto conductive adhesive tabs and slightly dried with filter paper (nr 1). Grids were dried under a fume hood for at least 30 min before imaging. Glow discharge was not performed. Forceps were cleaned with ethanol between samples to prevent cross-contamination. SEM imaging was carried out using a Zeiss Gemini 360 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Jena, Germany) equipped with a secondary electron detector. Images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV, a working distance of 6.1 mm, and an aperture size of 30 μm.

AgNSs-peptide assembly and characterization

AgNSs-coated AMPs (AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9) were prepared by mixing different AgNSs/AMPs ratios, followed by overnight stirring at 4 ᵒC. To remove unbound peptides, samples were centrifuged for 30 min at 300 × g, at room temperature. The AgNSs-coated AMPs were suspended in ultrapure water at pH 7.0 before use. UV-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential (ξ) measurements were used to characterize the samples. The AgNSs:Pa-MAP 1.9 1:4 × 106 ratio yielded the best results and was therefore used in all experiments. The same ratio was used for the scrambled peptide. Detailed information related to methodologies used for characterization is described in the Supporting Information.

Synergy analysis

The combinatorial anticancer effects of AgNSs and Pa-MAP 1.9 were evaluated using the SynergyFinder web application (v. 2.0). Synergy was assessed across the three breast-derived cell lines (MCF-10A, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231) using four established reference models: highest single agent (HSA), Bliss, Loewe, and zero interaction potency (ZIP) [60]. Mean synergy scores were calculated across the entire dose–response matrix. The resulting synergy distributions were visualized as heatmaps. Positive scores indicate synergistic interactions, values around zero indicate additive effects, and negative values indicate antagonism.

Cytotoxicity

MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells were plated at a density of 2500-5000 cells/mL into TPP flat-bottom 96-well plates and allowed to attach and grow overnight. Cells were treated with different concentrations of Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (0-120 μM) and AgNSs (0-30 pM), and incubated for 2, 8, and 24 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Untreated cells were used as a negative control (100% cell viability). Cells treated with Triton X-100 were considered as positive control (0% cell viability). At the end of each incubation time, the tetrazolium dye 2,3-bis(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxamide (XTT) was added to each well, following the manufacturer's instructions, and incubated for 4 h. Then, the absorbance of each well was recorded in a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) between 450 and 700 nm. Cell viability was calculated as:Cellviability(%)=(Absorbanceofsample)×100(Absorbanceofuntreatedcontrol)

IC50 values were determined using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Hemolysis

Human blood samples were obtained from healthy donors from Instituto Português do Sangue e da Transplantação (Lisbon, Portugal). Ethical approval was not required for this study because only anonymized human blood samples were used. Human erythrocytes (RBCs) were isolated following the protocol described elsewhere [61]. Briefly, whole human blood samples were pelleted down by centrifugation at 220 × g for 15 min at room temperature. The plasma and buffy coat were removed, and the RBCs were washed three times with HEPES buffer (10 mM, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and then resuspended at 1% hematocrit. Then, RBC suspensions were incubated with increasing peptide concentrations for 1 and 24 h at 37 °C. Erythrocyte suspension without treatment was used as a negative control (0% erythrocyte lysis). Triton X-100 1% (v/v) was used for the positive control (100% erythrocyte lysis). Finally, hemoglobin release was monitored by measuring the supernatant absorbance at 540 nm in a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Experiments were performed in triplicate. The percentage of hemolysis was calculated by the following equation:%ofhemolysis=(Absorbanceoftreatedsample)−(Absorbanceofnegativecontrol)(Absorbanceofpositivecontrol−Absorbanceofnegativecontrol)×100

Cell viability

The permeability of cell membranes was evaluated with the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit containing calcein-AM and ethidium homodimer-1 fluorescent dyes. After cell detachment, the concentration was adjusted to 2.5 × 104 cells/mL (MDA-MB-231, MCF-7, and MCF-10A). Cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4, and then cultured in 35-mm cell culture imaging dishes (Ibidi, Fitchburg, WI, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS, and the supplemented growth medium was replaced. Then, cells were treated with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (60 μM), AgNSs (15 pM), AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (60 μM) and left to incubate for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells without treatment and treated with Triton X-100 were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Cells were stained using calcein-AM and ethidium homodimer-1 (EthD-1) fluorescent dyes, complying with the manufacturer's instructions. Calcein-AM 0.05 μM and EthD-1 2 μM were added to the plates and left to incubate at room temperature for 30 min, protected from light. Then, cells were washed once with PBS and images were acquired on a confocal point-scanning microscope Zeiss LSM 710 (Jena, Germany), equipped with argon (green; wavelengths 458, 488, and 514 nm) and DPSS 561-10 (red; wavelength 561 nm) lasers. Zen Black edition software was used for image acquisition. Images were acquired with an objective Plan-Apochromat 20× (NA = 0.8, WD = 0.55 mm). Imaging was conducted for three independent biological replicates.

Apoptosis assay

MCF-10A, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates overnight and treated at different concentrations of Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (0, 5, 15, 30, 50, and 100 μM), and with AgNSs (1.25, 3.75, 7.5, 12.5, and 25 pM) for 24 h. Cells were washed once with PBS, and cell apoptosis rates were measured by flow cytometry on a BD Accuri C6 Plus cell analyzer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). As a positive control for cell death, cells were heated to 60 °C for 15 min. Briefly, MCF-10A, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231 cells were counted and resuspended in 100 μL annexin-V binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL annexin V-FITC and 5 μL propidium iodide (PI) were added and incubated at room temperature for 15 min, protected from light. The percentage of apoptotic cells was determined by using FlowJo software v. 10 (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA) for three independent cell cultures.

Zeta potential measurements on living cells

MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF-10A cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. A cell suspension was prepared with a final concentration of 1.0 × 105 cells.mL−1 in PBS, and treated with different concentrations of Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (0, 5, 15, 30, 50, and 100 μM), and AgNSs (1.25, 3.75, 7.5, 12.5, and 25 pM). Cell suspensions were loaded into disposable zeta cells and allowed to equilibrate for 30 min at 37 °C. A concentration of 1.0 × 105 cells. mL−1 of MDA-MB-231, MCF7, and MCF-10A cells was cultured in TPP flat-bottom 48-well plates, treated and incubated at 37 ᵒC in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 2 and 4 h. After the incubation, treated cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. The cell suspension was then loaded into disposable zeta cells and allowed to equilibrate for 2 min at 37 °C. All measurements were made at 37 °C and 40 V, with a total of 15 measurements (40 runs each). ξ-potential values were obtained from at least two independent experiments.

Sample preparation for atomic force microscopy

Cells used for atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging and nanoindentation experiments were treated as follows: confluent cells (5 × 104 cells. mL−1) in ibiTreat 35-mm μ-dishes were cultured in monolayers at 37 °C for 48 h. Then, cells were washed twice with growth medium and treated for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (60 μM), AgNSs (15 pM), AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and s-Pa-MAP 1.9 (60 μM). Untreated cells (without peptide or AgNSs) at the same experimental conditions were used as a control. After incubation, the cells were washed three times before use.

AFM imaging

AFM images of cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and s-Pa-MAP 1.9 were obtained as previously described [[62], [63], [64]]. Treated cells were washed twice with PBS to remove the medium and gently fixed with 2% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were subsequently washed three times with PBS and sterile Milli-Q water and allowed to air-dry at room temperature. Cell imaging was carried out on a NanoWizard IV atomic force microscope (JPK Instruments, Berlin, Germany) mounted on the top of an Axiovert 200 inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The AFM head is equipped with a 15-μm z-range linearized piezoelectric scanner and an infrared laser. AFM imaging of the cells was performed in contact mode, in air. ACL tips (oxidized, sharpened silicon probes, Applied NanoStructures, Mountain View, CA, USA) with a tip radius of 6 nm, resonant frequency of about 190 kHz, and spring constant of 45 N m−1 were used for imaging. Imaging parameters were adjusted to minimize the force applied to the scanning of the sample's topography. Scanning speed was optimized to 0.3 Hz, with 512 × 512 acquisition points. For each experimental condition, approximately 10 high-resolution AFM images were obtained in two independent culture dishes with a total area of 100 × 100 μm2. Image analysis was performed using JPK Image Processing software v. 6.1.184 (JPK Instruments, Berlin, Germany). The area, volume, and elongation index of each imaged cell were quantified using SPIP software v. 6.7.4 (Image Metrology, Hørsholm, Denmark). The roughness of the cells was analyzed by Gwyddion software v. 2.61 (Czech Metrology Institute, Jihlava, Czech Republic).

AFM-based force spectroscopy

Cell elasticity experiments were carried out by AFM-based force spectroscopy on live cells, at 25 °C, in cell culture medium. For these measurements, a non-functionalized MSNL-type silicon nitride probe (Bruker, Camarillo, CA, USA) was used. The softest triangular cantilevers, with a tip radius of 2 nm and a resonant frequency of 15 kHz, were calibrated using the thermal fluctuation method, resulting in a nominal spring constant of 0.03 N/m. For every contact between a cell and a cantilever, the distance between the cantilever and the cell was adjusted to keep a maximum applied force of 200 pN before retraction. Cell elasticity was measured simultaneously at the top of the cell and over its nucleus, at five points in each cell region (a total of 10 points per cell). Five force-distance curves were obtained for each point (corresponding to a total of 25 curves per region), and on approximately 70 cells, from 2 independent cell cultures. Data collection for each distance cycle was performed at 1.5 Hz, with a z-displacement range of 4 μm. Data acquired on the nanoindentation experiments (force indentation curves) were analyzed to obtain the cell's Young's modulus (E), using JPK Data Processing v. 6.1.184, by the application of the Hertzian model. The probe was modeled as a quadratic pyramid, with a tip angle of 35° (half-angle to face) and a Poisson ratio of 0.50. Young's modulus histograms were constructed for each experimental condition studied. The ideal histogram bin size was chosen to achieve the best-fitted Gaussian model peak length. The maximum values of the Gaussian peaks represent different statistical measures of the Young's modulus of the cells. Values of AFM tip penetration depth into the cells were also obtained.

Sample preparation for cell uptake studies

The cellular uptake of AgNSs, Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 was assessed by TEM, imaging flow cytometry, and confocal microscopy. MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells were seeded in T25 flasks in complete medium. After 80% cell confluence, cells were washed once with PBS, and low-serum media was added to the cells. MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 were treated with DMEM + 2% FBS + 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and MCF-10A with DMEM/F12 + 2% horse serum + complements. At this step, cells were ready for TEM, imaging flow cytometry, and point scanning confocal microscopy measurements.

Transmission electron microscopy of cells

For TEM, cells were incubated with 100 μM of Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and 25 pM of AgNSs. Cells were washed with PBS, detached with 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA, and fixed with 2.5% formaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.2 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, for 2 h at room temperature. Samples were then processed at the GIMM Electron Microscopy Facility, as described by Schrand et al. [65], with a few modifications. Briefly, after fixation, cells were subsequently washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.2 M PBS. Afterward, cells were dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series and embedded in EPON resin. From the embedded cells, ultrathin (70 μm) sections were cut. Imaging of the samples was performed in a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN transmission electron microscope. For TEM analysis, 15 cells per condition were randomly selected, and three images were acquired for each cell to ensure adequate sampling.

Imaging flow cytometry

For imaging flow cytometry measurements, cells were incubated with 10 μM and 15 μM of FITC-labeled Pa-MAP 1.9 and FITC-coated AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 for 15 and 30 min at 37 ᵒC. Untreated cells in a low serum medium were used as controls. The cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with 0.05% trypsin to digest any surface-bound AMPs. Cells were washed with PBS containing 2% FBS and then incubated for 5 min in PBS with 2% FBS and CellMask Orange (1:1000 dilution of a 5 mg/mL stock solution) for cell membrane staining. Cells were washed twice using PBS with 2% FBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (4% PFA in PBS) for 15 min. Cells were washed three additional times with PBS with 2% FBS and finally incubated for 10 min with Hoeschst 333342 Blue for cell nuclei staining (1:2000). Cell suspensions were imaged on an ImageStreamX Mk II imaging cytometer from Amnis Corporation (Seattle, WA, USA). All samples were imaged at 60× magnification, using a low flow rate and high sensitivity, with INSPIRE software (San Diego, CA, USA). FITC and CellMask Orange were excited with 488 nm and 561 nm lasers and detected in Channel 2 (480–560 nm) and Channel 3 (560–595 nm), respectively. Image analysis was performed by IDEAS software v. 6.2 (Seattle, WA, USA), according to Wortzel et al. [66]. The internalization of the FITC-labeled Pa-MAP 1.9 and FITC-coated AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 was quantified by the values of intensity vs. max pixel of the FITC staining channel.

Point scanning confocal microscopy

To confirm the co-localization of internalized AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, 10 μL of the fixed cell suspensions stained as mentioned above were spotted on poly-L-Lysine-coated glass slides (Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted using the VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Cells were imaged using Zeiss LSM 710 (Carl Zeiss Microimaging, Jena, Germany). Acquired data from 405 nm (Hoechst), 488 nm (internalized labeled-Pa-MAP 1.9), 633 nm (internalized nanostar reflection), and 561 nm (CellMask-stained plasma membrane) channels were recorded as a series of z-stacks. Images were acquired with a Plan-Apochromat DIC 63 ×3 oil-immersion objective lens (NA = 1.4, WD = 0.19 mm). Zen Black edition software was used for image acquisition, and 3D projections of z-stack images were processed with ImageJ v. 1.53t. Images represent one of three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), FlowJo v. 10 (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA), IDEAS v. 6.2 (Amnis/Luminex, Seattle, WA, USA), SynergyFinder 2.0 for drug-combination analysis, ImageJ v. 1.53t (NIH, USA), JPK Data Processing software v. 6.1.184 (Bruker, Germany), and OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) for histogram construction and Gaussian fitting. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise stated. All assays (cell viability, hemolysis, imaging-based analyses, and apoptosis) were performed for at least three independent biological replicates. The number of replicates for AFM and zeta potential measurements is specified in the corresponding sections and/or figure legends. Concentrations were selected based on the IC50 values of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 in cancer cell lines, with doses up to 100 μM included to assess supra-IC50 effects and dose-dependent cytotoxic responses. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's multiple-comparison post hoc tests, as appropriate. For internalization experiments involving multiple factors, statistical significance was assessed using three-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's multiple-comparison test. IC50, HC50, and dose–response curves were determined by nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism. The specific statistical tests used for each dataset are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of silver nanostars

Nanostars were synthesized as previously described [59]. Synthesized nanoparticles were characterized using several analytical techniques. Transmission electron microscopy analysis revealed that the synthesized AgNSs exhibited a range of sizes and morphologies, including irregular spheres and rods (Fig. 1A–D). In agreement with the TEM observations, scanning electron microscopy further revealed well-defined branched nanostructures with high surface contrast and extensive interparticle association at higher magnification (Fig. 1A and B). Together, these complementary imaging techniques confirm the formation of AgNSs with the anticipated multi-armed morphology and suggest that some aggregation occurred during sample preparation without compromising the AgNSs' structure. The AgNSs predominantly displayed multiple tips protruding from a central core, with irregular tips that had rounded borders or were subdivided. The particle size distribution was polydisperse and is reported as the tip-to-tip length of the nanostars. The average tip-to-tip length was 103.9 ± 43.5 nm (mean ± SD), based on measurements of 482 particles from TEM images (Fig. 1E).Fig. 1Characterization of silver nanostars by SEM and TEM. (A-B) Micrographs of synthesized nanostars by SEM. (C-D) Micrographs of synthesized nanostars by TEM. (E) TEM image-based particle size distribution histograms of AgNSs.

Characterization of silver nanostars by SEM and TEM. (A-B) Micrographs of synthesized nanostars by SEM. (C-D) Micrographs of synthesized nanostars by TEM. (E) TEM image-based particle size distribution histograms of AgNSs.

The nanostar hydrodynamic size, size distribution, and concentration were determined using DLS and NTA, while their optical properties were assessed by UV–Vis spectroscopy. Although DLS is highly sensitive to the presence of large particles, it can be challenging to distinguish peaks in polydisperse samples such as nanostars [67,68]. To complement the DLS results, we used NTA, which individually tracks particles by analysing the light they scatter under laser illumination and is collected by a microscope objective, viewed with a digital camera [69]. This technique measures the same physical properties as DLS and is less sensitive to highly dispersed nanoparticle suspensions, providing more accurate size distribution and concentration measurements [70,71]. From NTA, AgNSs showed a mean hydrodynamic diameter (DH) of 100.9 ± 42.5 nm (mean ± standard error of the mean, SEM), with a DH mode of 83 nm (Supporting Fig. S4A). In comparison, DLS measured a DH of 88.52 ± 1.72 nm (mean ± SD). The DLS mean values are slightly smaller than the mean size values obtained by TEM and NTA. Based on all data, the synthesized AgNSs were found to have a consistent size distribution [38]. The stability of the AgNSs was confirmed by zeta potential measurements, which showed that the nanoparticles carried a negative net charge of −41.95 ± 0.85 mV (mean ± SD), indicating high colloidal stability [68].

UV-Vis spectroscopy is an analytical technique routinely employed in the characterization of nanomaterials, providing insights into the plasmonic resonance of nanoparticles [72]. This technique was used to investigate the optical properties and the aggregated state of the nanostars [73]. A single absorption peak between 400 and 500 nm confirms the formation of spherical NPs [74,75]. However, due to their unique structure, AgNSs are expected to show a UV-Vis absorption peak near 400 nm, with a distinct plasmon resonance band displaying a tail at longer wavelengths (>700 nm) [76]. The anisotropic morphology, size, and branched arms of AgNSs cause the appearance of the characteristic tail observed at wavelengths greater than 700 nm [59]. UV-Vis spectra recorded from the synthesized AgNSs showed a maximum absorption peak at λmax = 382.5 nm (Supporting Fig. S4B), corresponding to the average peak for AgNSs. These results demonstrate that AgNSs synthesis was successfully achieved [38,[76], [77], [78]].

AgNSs were coated with the Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide (AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9) and the s-Pa-MAP 1.9 scrambled peptide (AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9) via electrostatic interactions. To confirm the peptide coating, UV-Vis, DLS, and zeta potential measurements were used (Supporting Fig. S5). Stability tests of the optimal AgNSs:AMP ratio were obtained by measuring the UV–Vis spectra of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 with the addition of NaCl (Supporting Fig. S5A). UV-Vis analysis showed that after the coating with Pa-MAP 1.9, the AgNSs adsorption peak was red-shifted (from 382.5 to 389.0 nm), and the intensity of the absorption band decreased, indicating particle surface modification by peptide adsorption [76,79]. The UV-Vis spectra of AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 resulted in a peak shift to 361.5 nm with a broadened band, suggesting nanoparticle-peptide aggregation (Supporting Fig. S5B) [59,76].

The coating of the peptides on the AgNSs surface affects the overall surface zeta potential and size distribution (Supporting Fig. S5C–D). The negative zeta potential of AgNSs (approximately −41.95 mV) changed to values above +30.0 mV after coating with Pa-MAP 1.9, which indicated the dominance of positively charged peptide on the AgNSs surface and further confirmed the successful peptide coating. The peptide charge distribution, amino acid sequence, and hydrophobicity are linked to their specific structural features during interaction with silver nanoparticle surfaces [80]. Pa-MAP 1.9 is a peptide rich in lysine amino acid residues that electrostatically adsorbs onto the negatively charged AgNS surface, providing stability as a coating agent and conferring biological activity to the nanoplatform, acting also as a surface-functionalizing agent [81,82]. The mean hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS increased from 88.52 ± 1.72 nm for bare AgNSs to values varying from 100.40 ± 9.63 nm to 160.92 ± 13.94 nm for AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, indicating an increase in the hydrodynamic size following peptide coating. The s-Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide successfully coats the AgNSs, as evidenced by the positive shift in zeta potential, to approximately +18.0 mV. Zeta potential values above +30 mV and below −30 mV are desirable to ensure nanoparticle complex stability, as steric repulsion prevents the aggregation of coated nanostars [80]. The s-Pa-MAP 1.9 coating is compromised when aggregation decreases the surface area available for peptide binding [83]. AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 formed large aggregates, with hydrodynamic diameter values above 500 nm, in agreement with zeta potential results indicating low colloidal stability.

In Fig. 2A, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 demonstrated significant time-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells, with an IC50 of 61.86 ± 7.0 μM. AgNSs alone exhibited an IC50 of 29.7 ± 1.8 pM. Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 all exhibited IC50 values greater than 100 μM. Qualitative analysis using confocal microscopy confirmed reduced cell viability in MCF-7 cells treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, indicated by increased EthD-1 fluorescence (Fig. 2B), consistent with the quantitative data in Fig. 2A. Similarly, MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3A) showed significantly reduced viability with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 treatment, achieving the lowest IC50 value (28.03 ± 6.7 μM). Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs alone exhibited IC50 values of 57.27 ± 1.8 μM and 8.98 ± 0.5 pM, respectively. Calcein/EthD-1 double staining corroborated the XTT assay results, showing increased EthD-1 fluorescence with Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 treatments. Notably, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 demonstrated the highest efficacy against MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3B).

As a control, non-malignant breast epithelial cells (MCF-10A) were exposed to the same treatments. Both Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed limited toxicity, with IC50 values above 100 μM (103.8 ± 2.0 μM and 125.2 ± 2.1 μM, respectively; Fig. 4A). Pa-MAP 1.9 dose-response curves indicated that the peptide acts quickly, as no differences were observed between the effects at 2 and 24 h of incubation. Cell viability results, together with the EthD-1 fluorescence images, indicate that Pa-MAP 1.9 seems to induce greater cytotoxicity than AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 against MCF-10A cells. Consistent with the XTT assay, the calcein/EthD-1 assay images revealed no cell damage with s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9, or AgNSs treatments (Fig. 4B).

AMPs designed to disrupt cell membranes can also cause hemolysis in human erythrocytes (RBCs) due to similar mechanisms [85,86]. Given RBCs' major abundance in circulation, evaluating the interaction between nanoparticles and peptides with RBCs is essential for ensuring safety and hemocompatibility [87]. The hemolytic activity (HC50) was determined by the concentration of Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs required to lyse 50% of human RBCs (Supporting Fig. S6A–E). Hemolysis was measured after 1 and 24 h of incubation. After 24 h, Pa-MAP 1.9 showed an HC50 of 19.0 ± 2.4 μM. A reduction in hemolysis was observed when RBCs were treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, which had an HC50 of 35.0 ± 4.2 μM. None of the concentrations of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 tested were able to reach 100% hemolysis.

AgNSs alone showed significant hemolytic activity at low concentrations, with an HC50 of 1.2 ± 0.1 pM, indicating high toxicity to RBCs. The scrambled peptide s-Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibited no toxicity towards RBCs in all experiments. These results suggest that the hemolytic abilities of Pa-MAP 1.9 become slightly reduced when they are attached to the surface of AgNSs.

Our group's recent studies with the peptide Pa-MAP 1.9 demonstrated its affinity for negatively charged lipid vesicles, indicating its potential activity against cancer cell membranes [28]. Pa-MAP 1.9 is a polyalanine-rich cationic peptide designed to strategically position amino acid residues, allowing for a more efficient distribution of hydrophobic and positively charged faces [27]. This enhances the molecule's amphipathicity and promotes α-helical structuring [27,88]. These structural characteristics of Pa-MAP 1.9 significantly influence its membrane-interacting properties and biological activity. In contrast, the random rearrangement of the peptide's original sequence can significantly disrupt its amphipathic organization and α-helix conformation, leading to a less defined separation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, which directly influences its biological activity [89,90]. Therefore, the results from scrambled peptides exhibiting reduced biological activity confirm the linear sequence-specific activity of Pa-MAP 1.9 towards breast cancer cell membranes. Our findings demonstrate that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 reduced cell viability in both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, supporting the preservation of the peptide's biological activity following nanostars functionalization.

We evaluated the combinatorial effect of AgNSs and Pa-MAP 1.9 (Supporting Figs. S7–S9). In MCF-7 cells, the AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 treatment showed clear synergy across all scoring methods (ZIP 13.92, Loewe 9.47, HSA 10.69, and Bliss 15.73), with an increased drug combination sensitivity score (CSS) of 30.93, indicating a strong synergistic interaction and enhanced anticancer activity. In MDA-MB-231 cells, the combination was strongly antagonistic across all metrics (ZIP −19.16, Loewe −23.58, HSA −15.40, and Bliss −19.11), showing only a synergistic window with low concentrations. They showed a high CSS (62.04); however, the co-administration reduces the combined efficacy. In MCF-10A cells, the combination showed consistent antagonism across all models (ZIP −14.11, Loewe −36.97, HSA −18.97, and Bliss −16.21), indicating reduced combined effects and no synergistic toxicity in normal cells. Overall sensitivity was low (9.71), supporting a limited cytotoxic impact in this non-tumorigenic context. Across models, the interaction profile was strongly cell-line dependent, ranging from pronounced antagonism in non-tumorigenic MCF-10A cells to clear synergy in MCF-7 cancer cells, and to a low-dose synergistic effect with predominantly antagonistic interactions in triple-negative breast cancer cells [60,91].

Breast cancer and normal cells responded differently to the treatments. To assess the preferential efficacy of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, we calculated the selectivity index (SI) as CC50/IC50, where CC50 refers to the concentration reducing viability by 50% in normal cells [92]. AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed a higher SI in MDA-MB-231 cells (4.46) than in MCF-7 cells (2.02), indicating greater preferential cytotoxicity toward the triple-negative breast cancer cell line. Overall, these results suggest modest selectivity for cancer cells over non-tumoral cells under the tested conditions.

For the normal breast cell line MCF-10A (Fig. 7), the population of early and late apoptosis increased significantly at concentrations higher than 50 μM. Untreated cells showed early- and late-stage apoptosis percentages of 25.3% and 2.9%, respectively. Cells treated with 100 μM of Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 resulted in early apoptosis percentages of 40.1% and 51.2%, respectively, alongside significant increases in late apoptosis percentages to 33.1% and 32.1%, respectively. Neither s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9, nor AgNSs treatments showed differences in early and late apoptosis in any of the three cell lines.

AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 induced apoptosis in MCF-7 cells at concentrations above 100 μM, whereas it showed cytotoxicity to these cells with an IC50 of around 60 μM, highlighting the regulated nature of apoptosis in contrast to general cell damage, as assessed by the viability assay. In MDA-MB-231, the AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 can induce apoptosis and necrosis with concentrations above 100 μM, while the Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide with the same concentration can only induce apoptosis in these cells. In MCF-10A, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 and Pa-MAP 1.9 induce apoptosis and a high rate of necrosis. Until now, these results suggest that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 may induce cell death in MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A breast cells by different mechanisms. The apparent discrepancy between metabolic viability and apoptosis readouts likely arises from differences in assay sensitivity and the fact that MCF-10A cells can maintain metabolic activity while undergoing early apoptotic or sublethal remodeling [94,95]. Data suggest that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibits higher cytotoxicity toward breast cancer cells compared to MCF-10A cells but still induces measurable apoptotic effects in normal cells at the highest dose tested (100 μM). AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 seems to exhibit a therapeutic window supporting a differential but not absolute selectivity profile. Therefore, their effects on apoptosis vary with cell type.

These differences reflect the AMP's ability to interact with lipid cell membranes, inducing distinct modes of membrane disruption [96,97]. Peptides can also penetrate the lipid bilayer either directly or through endocytosis, activating apoptosis-inducing targets [96]. The presence of the Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide may facilitate an endocytic pathway for the entry of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 into cells, eventually triggering distinct cell death pathways [98,99]. Some peptides, such as the cationic nrCap18, have shown a specific cytotoxic effect on MDA-MB-231 cells, inducing apoptosis in a time-dependent manner [100,101]. The LL-37 peptide may exert its effects through electrostatic interactions with the cell membrane of breast cancer cells [102]. The improved cytotoxic effect of an AgNP–P1 peptide conjugate on MDA-MB-231 cells results in cell death via necrosis or apoptosis [103]. Additionally, TAT peptide–functionalized, green-synthesized silver nanoparticles have demonstrated enhanced induction of apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells [104]. However, the nature of the interaction between the peptides or conjugates and the cell membrane of breast cancer cells, as well as the molecular mechanisms by which these peptides act, remain unclear. Therefore, further studies are needed to understand the mechanism underlying the differential interactions of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 with breast cancer cell membranes and the distinct apoptotic/necrotic effects.

AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 breast cancer cell membrane interaction and internalization

Zeta potential measurements represent a biophysical approach that can indicate alterations in the cell surface charge potential formed at the slipping plane and can be used to monitor peptide-membrane interactions [105]. Given the time-dependent anticancer activity of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, the zeta potential was measured at different time points to assess its interaction profile over time with the membranes of MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells.

The mean zeta potential values for untreated MCF-7 cancer cells (Fig. 8A) and MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 8B) cancer cells were −21.45 ± 1.01 mV and −21.88 ± 1.32 mV, respectively. After 30 min of incubation (Fig. 8), MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNPs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed a significant increase in zeta potential, leading to a more neutral charge due to the high cationic profile of the peptides. For normal cells, the mean zeta potential values of the untreated control were −21.30 ± 3.28 mV (Fig. 8C). Pa-MAP 1.9 uniquely interacted with the cell membrane, significantly increasing zeta potential to −11.27 ± 1.59 mV after 30 min of incubation (Fig. 8), and further enhancing it to −2.86 ± 3.13 mV by 120 min (Supporting Fig. S10). These data highlight Pa-MAP 1.9's ability to neutralize surface charge before cell death of normal cells. By 240 min of incubation, the zeta potential shifted to −20.22 ± 4.16 mV, which may indicate internalization of the peptide by MCF-10A cells as well (Supporting Fig. S11). In contrast, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed no significant difference from the control MCF-10A at any time point, suggesting that it may not directly disrupt the membranes of MCF-10A cells, unlike Pa-MAP 1.9. Additionally, AgNSs did not cause significant changes in zeta potential across the three tested cell lines, despite exhibiting cytotoxicity towards breast cancer cells. This lack of zeta potential alteration can be attributed to the negative surface charge of AgNSs, which limits their interaction with cell membranes through electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged AgNSs and the cell membrane [106]. Since AgNPs are typically internalized via endocytosis, significant membrane-level alterations were not expected [107]. Despite this, there were no significant membrane-level effects over time in breast cancer cells exposed to AgNPs-Pa-MAP 1.9. We hypothesized that the anticancer effects of these compounds may primarily result from their retention within or internalization into cells, followed by the activation of intracellular targets, rather than direct membrane disruption.

The mechanisms underlying the uptake of AMPs and AgNPs by cancer cells remain unclear due, among other reasons, to their complex physicochemical properties and the intricate nature of tumors [98,99]. Although the full extent of AgNP toxicity in breast cancer cells has yet to be determined, it has been associated with loss of cell membrane integrity, apoptosis, and oxidative stress through different mechanisms [[100], [101], [102]]. Understanding the mechanisms that regulate the internalization of peptides and nanoparticles in breast cancer cells is crucial. To test the hypothesis that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 is internalized by breast cancer cells, we conducted peptide internalization studies. Given the technical challenges of quantifying AgNSs internalization, we employed a combination of approaches. First, FITC-labeled Pa-MAP 1.9 was tracked using ImageStreamX imaging flow cytometry to quantify the percentage of internalized FITC-Pa-MAP 1.9 and FITC-AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 in MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells. Then, we assessed the effects of time and peptide concentration on cell uptake. Cells were exposed to Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 at concentrations of 10 and 15 μM for 15 and 30 min. To ensure accurate assessment of internalization, we included a trypsin-mediated degradation step during the cell culture and labeling protocols to remove externally bound peptides, thereby confirming that cells exhibiting green fluorescence had internalized the labeled peptide into the cytoplasm.

Imaging flow cytometry is a technique that combines single-cell acquisition with imaging, allowing a robust assessment of a large number of cells [108]. Our acquisition information and gate strategy can be found in Supporting Fig. S12 [109,110]. The quantification of internalization was based on the population of cells that exhibited intensity in the green channel, indicating that they had internalized Pa-MAP 1.9 or AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. An additional analysis was made by a spot count tool to determine the ability of the cells to internalize low or high amounts of Pa-MAP 1.9 or AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 (Fig. 9). The results showed a significant increase in the percentage of MCF-7 cells internalizing AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 in a time-dependent manner. With 10 μM of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 and 15 min of incubation, MCF-7 cells showed 38% internalization, increasing to 70% after 30 min of incubation (Fig. 9A). MCF-7 cells preferentially internalize low amounts of both Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. There is no significant difference in peptide uptake when the peptide is bound to the nanostars, as after 30 min of incubation, MCF-7 cells internalized nearly 70% of all samples tested (Fig. 9B and C). Almost 90% of MDA-MB-231 cells internalize Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 after 30 min of incubation, and no differences in the internalization of Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 were found. Altogether, these results support the data from zeta potential measurements. For MCF-7 cells treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, zeta potential data showed a shift to a more negative charge profile only after 240 min of incubation, which supports the time-dependent uptake and internalization process in these cells. No alteration in the zeta potential of MDA-MB-231 cells after 30 min is likely due to the rapid interaction and internalization of high amounts of Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. In MCF-10A, Pa-MAP 1.9 seems to interact with the cell membrane and be internalized, whereas AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 looks to be preferentially internalized.

To confirm the intracellular presence of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 without detachment from the nanostar surface, confocal point-scanning microscopy was employed to observe all cell lines. These experiments utilized the same fixed cell culture samples as those used in the imaging flow cytometry assay to complement the previous experiment. AgNPs are known for their ability to reflect light, allowing visualization of light intensity backscattered from nanoparticles inside cells using the reflection mode on confocal microscopy, without the need for fluorescence labels [111]. In reflection mode, colocalization of AgNSs and the FITC-labeled Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide in all cell lines was achieved by overlapping the green fluorescence channel (Pa-MAP 1.9) with the reflected light from AgNSs particles. Maximum projection of Z-stack images confirmed the presence of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 particles inside treated cells, demonstrating that they remain associated with the nanostar surface (Supporting Figure S113).

Based on the cumulative evidence, Pa-MAP 1.9 was revealed to be a promising anticancer peptide (ACP) as well [111]. Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 have distinct internalization mechanisms among different breast cancer cell lines and non-tumorous breast cell lines. Since intracellular trafficking determines nanoparticle localization and interaction with intracellular targets, the distinct internalization profiles observed among the different MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A prompted further investigation of the intracellular trafficking pathways that may be involved.

Cellular uptake studies

Literature describes how various sizes and shapes of bare and surface-functionalized AgNPs are taken up by cells through mechanisms like phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, receptor-mediated endocytosis (clathrin or caveolin), and transcytosis [[112], [113], [114], [115], [116]]. However, their uptake can vary widely, being challenging to generalize [117]. Understanding how AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 is taken up by breast cancer cells is crucial for gaining insights into its internalization mechanisms. TEM is widely utilized in biomedical research due to its high resolution, benefiting also from the strong electron density of metal nanoparticles, enabling their clear identification inside cells without contrast enhancement procedures [118]. Cellular ultrastructural examination of MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells exposed to AgNSs, Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 were focused on imaging cellular modifications associated with the endocytosis process. Other structural alterations in cell compartments were also observed and described below.

Fig. 10 shows the effects of MCF-7 cells treated with AgNSs, Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. After 30 min of incubation, AgNSs were not in the cytoplasm of MCF-7 cells. However, after 120 min, AgNSs were visible as aggregates within membrane-bound compartments, namely lysosomes. In addition, AgNSs aggregates were also detected in early endosomes. When AgNSs were coated with the peptide, they possibly triggered different endocytic mechanisms, with AgNSs and Pa-MAP 1.9 being visible inside endocytic vesicles after 30 min of incubation. MCF-7 cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 for 30 and 120 min exhibited coated vesicles resembling clathrin-coated pits and lamellipodia or ruffled membrane projections [[119], [120], [121], [122]]. Both small (<100 nm) and large intracellular vesicles (>200 nm) were imaged, with a predominance of large vesicles. For MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 11), all treatments exhibited a low presence of clathrin-coated vesicles and small intracellular vesicles. TEM found cell membrane protrusions that suggest membrane remodeling events, potentially related to membrane ruffles and invaginations [123]. Apparent discontinuities observed in some membrane regions may reflect the plan of sectioning or membrane remodeling events associated with vesicle budding or shedding [123]. AgNSs were observed inside cells within enclosed compartments (lysosomes and early endosomes). MDA-MB-231 cells showed a predominance of multivesicular bodies and multi-lamellar structures with double membrane resembling autophagosomes at both 30 and 120 min of incubation [124]. Clathrin-coated vesicles and both small and large vesicles are present in cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9; however, large autophagic vesicles and multivesicular bodies were predominant. Pa-MAP 1.9 was also associated with swollen endoplasmic reticulum in some cells. Distinct trafficking profiles were observed across cell lines, suggesting that intracellular processing contributes to the different biological responses. As AgNSs were not observed freely in the cytoplasm, uptake appears to occur mainly through endocytic pathways, with no evidence of nuclear or mitochondrial entry. These results suggest that multiple endocytic mechanisms are involved in the uptake of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 in both breast cancer cell lines and normal breast cells.

The toxicity of silver nanoparticles may be influenced by their cellular uptake, which is determined by their surface chemistry [125,126]. The size and surface characteristics of nanoparticles play a crucial role in determining their cellular endocytic pathway, with larger particles typically being internalized via macropinocytosis [127]. Clathrin-mediated endocytosis is a well-established pathway for the internalization of positively charged nanoparticles, facilitated by the coating provided by plasma proteins [128].

Peptide internalization into cells can occur through different pathways, including endocytosis, macropinocytosis, energy-independent translocation, and pore-forming mechanisms [111]. Endocytosis, a crucial cellular process, often becomes altered in cancer, impacting the uptake of chemotherapeutic agents, and is a well-studied mechanism in oncology [117]. One notable endocytic process is macropinocytosis, where cancer cells scavenge extracellular proteins to sustain growth and proliferation, particularly in nutrient-deprived or hypoxic tumor microenvironments [127].

MCF-7 cells exhibit constitutive macropinocytic activity, whereas MCF-10A cells do not typically engage in macropinocytosis under normal conditions [129]. While macropinocytosis facilitates nutrient uptake, excessive vacuolization within cancer cells may be detrimental. This vacuolization ultimately leads to a new form of non-apoptotic cell death known as methuosis [127,130]. This characteristic has been exploited to deliver anticancer drugs effectively through the macropinocytic pathway [129,131,132]. Wang et al. [133]. demonstrated that the lysine/leucine-rich peptide L-K6 interacts with MCF-7 cell membranes, enhancing permeability without causing significant disruption. The peptide is preferentially internalized by MCF-7 cells mainly through a clathrin-independent macropinocytosis mechanism. Furthermore, the L-K6 peptide induces nuclear damage, leading to cell death, without causing significant cytoskeleton disruption or mitochondrial impairment [133]. Liu et al. [134] described an enhancement in the internalization of AgNPs through conjugation with the TAT peptide (AgNP-TAT) in Caco-2 cells. The enhanced internalization of AgNP-TAT was associated with increased cytotoxicity against various cancer cell lines, including MCF-7 and drug-resistant MCF-7 cell lines. However, the specific endocytic pathways involved were not investigated [134].

The size of AgNPs is an important parameter for toxicity and efficiency of cellular uptake. Wu et al. [107] investigated the cellular uptake of AgNPs of varying sizes (5, 20, 50, and 100 nm) in a mouse melanoma cell line (B16) [107]. They found that the efficiency of cell uptake was highest for AgNPs with a particle size of 100 nm and lowest for those with 20 nm [107]. Moreover, the type of endocytic mechanism involved in uptake may vary depending on the nanoparticle size [107]. While clathrin-mediated endocytosis was the predominant pathway for larger nanoparticles (50 and 100 nm), smaller nanoparticles (5, 20, and 50 nm) were preferentially internalized via caveolin-mediated endocytosis, and micropinocytosis was favored for larger nanoparticles (50 and 100 nm) [107].

Swanner et al. [135] observed that AgNPs cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells was dependent on nanoparticle uptake, which was independent of the particle size, shape, surface charge, or stabilizing agent used [135]. They also found that MCF-10A cells internalized nearly twice as many AgNPs as MDA-MB-231 cells, with most nanoparticles becoming cell-associated within the first 6 h of exposure. Furthermore, TEM analysis revealed the presence of AgNPs and their degradation products in autophagic vesicles within MDA-MB-231 cells, indicating nanoparticle processing via autophagy [135].

The distinct uptake patterns observed for MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-10A cells suggest differences in cellular internalization behavior of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, although specific uptake mechanisms cannot be conclusively determined from the current data. Notably, the observed selectivity indices indicate a preferential cytotoxic effect toward cancer cells compared to MCF-10A cells. Further studies are required to clarify the endocytic pathways involved in the cellular uptake and cytotoxic effects of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, which may help in the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies.

Fig. 13 illustrates untreated MCF-7 cells with their typical morphology, with smooth surfaces, well-defined boundaries, and centrally located nuclei. Treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 induce noticeable morphological alterations, including irregular cell peripheries and increased cell surface area (736.8 ± 35.1 μm2), and volume (385.2 ± 16.7 μm3) compared to controls (629.0 ± 25.7 μm2 and 289.7 ± 16.4 μm3, respectively). No significant change in the elongation index was observed, indicating that overall cell deformability was not markedly affected under the tested conditions. Importantly, surface roughness decreased from 71.8 ± 5.42 nm to 50.22 ± 3.17 nm, indicating a reduction in membrane topographical features consistent with altered cell surface morphology. Cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9 or AgNSs alone showed no significant differences in these parameters, compared to controls.

MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 14) showed no significant changes in cell area, volume, or elongation index following treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. However, a reduction in surface roughness (88.02 ± 7.18 nm vs. 118.7 ± 7.74 nm in controls), indicated subtle modifications in membrane surface features. Treatment with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs alone did not produce significant changes in the evaluated morphological parameters, suggesting limited impact under the tested conditions.

For MCF-10A cells (Fig. 15), untreated cells displayed smooth surfaces and regular surfaces. Treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 resulted in increases in cell area (879.9 ± 21.6 μm2), volume (381.8 ± 11.5 μm3), and surface roughness (62.63 ± 4.82 nm), when compared to control (579.2 ± 23.3 μm2, 261.1 ± 9.38 μm3, and 41.54 ± 2.96 nm, respectively). Similarly, Pa-MAP 1.9 treatment increased cell area (757.5 ± 26.8 μm2) and surface roughness (76.28 ± 4.48 nm), compared to controls (579.2 ± 23.3 μm2 and 41.54 ± 2.96 nm, respectively), suggesting altered membrane topography and morphological rearrangements relative to control cells. Overall, these AFM results indicate that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 induces cell-type–dependent morphological and surface alterations, with more pronounced effects observed in MCF-7 and MCF-10A cells, compared to MDA-MB-231 cells. However, these observations should be interpreted as descriptive biophysical changes rather than definitive evidence of specific mechanistic pathways.

The AFM analysis reveals clear, cell-type–dependent biophysical responses following treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. AFM images of breast cancer cells show intact cell membranes without morphological changes, supporting our hypothesis that Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 target intracellular sites with different effects across MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines. In MCF-7 cells, the increase in cell area and volume, together with the reduction in surface roughness, suggest substantial remodeling of the plasma membrane and underlying cytoskeletal organization. In contrast, MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited minimal changes in these parameters, featuring only a reduction in surface roughness. MCF-10A cells showed pronounced changes in area, volume, and surface roughness. The increase in surface roughness in these cells, compared to the decrease observed in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, further supports different effects between normal and cancer cells. This is consistent with the apoptosis data, which show significant induction of both early and late apoptotic populations in MCF-10A cells at 100 μM, indicating a moderate preferential cytotoxic effect toward cancer cells, compared to MCF-10A cells, although measurable effects are also observed in non-tumorigenic cells at higher concentrations.

Cell surface roughness, a sensitive indicator of cellular physiological states, can be affected by cytoskeleton alterations, interactions with anticancer drugs, and nanoparticle adsorption [137]. Together, our studies demonstrate that MCF-7 cells treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibit time-dependent internalization and anticancer activity. This interaction may increase surface roughness upon binding and uptake. Membrane disruption mechanisms were ruled out since no cell membrane damage was observed in TEM or AFM images. In contrast, the aggressive cytotoxic response towards MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited upon treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 displays no time-dependent internalization, as shown by imaging flow cytometry experiments, suggesting that membrane alterations may stabilize within 24 h. MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited more limited morphological responses, which is evidenced by the decrease in surface roughness, maintenance of cellular morphology, and the presence of small pores in the surface without compromising integrity. Morphological changes in MCF-10A cells treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed similarities with those observed for MCF-7 cells. Nevertheless, these changes do not necessarily correspond to higher cytotoxicity. In line with the XTT and viability results, they may reflect membrane remodeling, cytoskeletal rearrangement, or increased vacuolization, rather than irreversible cell damage. The limited membrane disruption observed for these cells indicates that the effects detected are more consistent with a non-lytic and concentration-dependent response of the AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, rather than cell membrane damage [138].

Morphological alterations indicative of apoptosis (cell shrinkage and apoptotic bodies) were absent in all cell lines studied, reinforcing that apoptosis may not be the primary cell death mechanism in those cells, as supported by our flow cytometry results (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7) [139]. This could explain the low apoptosis rates observed in MCF-7 cells treated with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9. These results are consistent with our apoptosis assay for MDA-MB-231 cells, which showed significant levels of necrosis: 33.1% and 32.1% for Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, respectively (Fig. 6C). Necroptotic cells exhibited reduced surface roughness, cell detachment, and the presence of small membrane pores [139]. In MCF-10A cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, cell detachment during fixation, along with membrane perturbations and pore formation, as well as increased roughness, cell swelling, and membrane rupture, were observed [130,139]. Further studies are required to clarify which mechanisms are involved in AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9-induced cell death in breast cancer cells.

Taken together, the cellular cytotoxicity, uptake patterns, biophysical alterations, and apoptotic responses suggest that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 interacts with breast cells in a multifactorial manner, involving membrane-associated effects and subsequent intracellular stress responses. However, the current dataset does not allow definitive assignment of specific uptake routes or mechanistic cell death pathways. Instead, the results support a model in which peptide-functionalized silver nanostars exert selective, but not exclusive, cytotoxicity through combined membrane interaction and concentration-dependent cell stress induction.

The use of AFM has revolutionized nanoscale assessment across various scientific disciplines [[140], [141], [142], [143]]. In cancer research, AFM plays a crucial role in elucidating alterations in cell morphology, elasticity, and adhesion properties induced by both endogenous factors related to carcinogenesis and exogenous stimuli such as anticancer drugs [144,145]. Cell stiffness serves as a key indicator of changes in the cytoskeleton, particularly actin filaments and microtubules, which are crucial for maintaining cell structure and function [136]. Those dynamic structures are involved in cancer progression and may affect cell shape, motility, and the ability to metastasize [136]. The elastic modulus of cells, determined by the cytoskeletal network, can be measured using AFM [136].

To assess cellular biomechanical properties, live cells were treated with Pa-MAP 1.9, s-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9, AgNSs-s-Pa-MAP 1.9, and AgNSs for 24 h, and their Young's modulus was determined. MCF-10A cells treated with Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 exhibited decreased stiffness in the nuclear region, with no alteration in the cytoplasmic region when compared to untreated cells (Fig. 16B). Given the non-uniform distribution of stiffness across the cells, particularly in the nuclear area, measuring both regions is essential for understanding potential variations during data acquisition [139].

Our findings highlight distinct biomechanical responses in MCF-7 cells induced by AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 and Pa-MAP 1.9, compared to MDA-MB-231 cells. Pa-MAP 1.9 lead to an increase in cell stiffness, whereas AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 decreased it, indicating a global cell softening. However, MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited similar decreased stiffness for both the peptide and the nanocomplex. This illustrates the differing effects observed between the peptide alone (showing no cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells) and the AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 nanocomplex, which exhibits effective enhanced cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells.

Cytoskeletal changes enable cancer cells to adapt their mechanics, facilitating processes like metastasis, and reflect responses to biological processes such as differentiation, adhesion, migration, internalization, and cell death [146]. Cancer cells are generally softer than non-tumorigenic cells, with previous AFM studies showing that MCF-7 cells exhibit a lower Young's modulus than MCF-10A cells due to alterations in actin organization [[146], [147], [148]]. Our results demonstrate reduced stiffness in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines, potentially reflecting AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9-induced remodeling of the cytoskeleton and plasma membrane [[149], [150], [151]]. Similar reductions in cell stiffness have been reported following MCF-7 cells exposure to AgNP, with AFM measurements revealing a dose-dependent decrease in Young's modulus associated with actin disorganization, oxidative stress, and apoptosis [152]. These findings suggest that changes in stiffness observed after AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 treatment do not necessarily correlate directly with loss of viability but rather reflect cellular stress response to peptide–nanostars interactions [152]. In contrast, MCF-10A cells showed a significant reduction in stiffness for both Pa-MAP 1.9 and AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 treatment, indicating that the nanocomplex also influences the mechanical properties of non-tumorigenic cells, although with limited cytotoxicity under the conditions tested. Further investigations are needed to clarify whether the anticancer activity of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 stems directly or indirectly from their impact on cytoskeletal structure and to understand the implications for mechanisms of cell death. Overall, the use of AFM, together with other characterization methods employed in this study, has been instrumental for assessing the anticancer activity of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 in breast cancer cells.

Conclusions

In summary, this study collectively demonstrates that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 exerts a multifaceted, cell-type-dependent, and concentration-dependent biological response characterized by moderate selectivity between cancer cells and non-tumorigenic breast cells. Pa-MAP 1.9 peptide demonstrated anticancer activity in TNBC cells, showing a proven capability for cell internalization. Enhanced through nanotechnology, AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 showed limited cytotoxicity toward non-tumorigenic breast cells, MCF-10A and RBCs, and enhanced anticancer activity in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cancer cells. Overall, these integrated observations indicate moderate selectivity toward cancer cells, as supported by the selectivity index, suggesting a modest safety window and potential for further investigation in therapeutic contexts. This study demonstrated that AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 were internalized by both breast cancer cells and non-tumorous breast cells. At the biophysical level, AFM studies revealed valuable insights into cell morphology and biomechanics. The distinct alterations in cell size, surface roughness, and stiffness across cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments induced by the nanocomplex were more evident in MCF-7 cancer cells but were also detectable in MCF-10A cells at higher concentrations, highlighting a non-exclusive but preferential cytotoxic profile. In MDA-MB-231 cells, treatment with AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 induced pronounced cytotoxic effects, accompanied by biophysical alterations, and exhibited higher sensitivity to the nanocomplex. AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9 represents a distinctive approach to targeting breast cancer cells, offering a promising foundation for advancing anticancer therapies, particularly against aggressive breast cancer subtypes. Overall, the present findings support the anticancer potential of AgNSs-Pa-MAP 1.9; however, further mechanistic and biological studies are required to clarify the mode of action and strengthen the evidences presented here.
