Vocal cord dysfunction: Does laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy predict disease severity and response to laryngeal retraining therapy?
Authors: David Ahmadian, Nader Wehbi, Claire M. Gleadhill, Natalie Monahan, Charles J. Gallego, Jonathan R. Skirko, Helena T. Yip
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVocal cord dysfunction (VCD) is a complex disorder characterized by episodic adduction of the vocal folds during inspiration and expiration, which can lead to dyspnea, wheezing, cough, and acute‐onset respiratory distress. Currently, there is a lack of standardized criteria among treating physicians across multiple disciplines, including otolaryngologists, pulmonologists, allergists, and speech and language pathologists, for diagnosis and treatment of VCD, although laryngeal‐respiratory retraining therapy (LRT) has emerged as the preferred treatment modality.ObjectiveIn the present study, we examined the efficacy of LRT in patients presenting with a clinical diagnosis of VCD in the presence and absence of laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy.ResultsOverall, 74.1% of the cohort showed a response to LRT, of which 62.1% were partial and 12.1% were significant responses. When comparing between patients with and without laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy, there were no significant differences in the number of sessions of LRT undertaken, mean time to response, and overall response rate between the groups.ConclusionOur findings suggest that LRT should be utilized for all patients presenting with symptoms of VCD, even in the absence of laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy.

INTRODUCTION

Vocal cord dysfunction (VCD), also referred to as paradoxical vocal fold motion (PVFM) or inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO), is a complex and often clinically elusive disorder characterized by abnormal, episodic adduction of the vocal folds during either the inspiratory or expiratory phase of respiration. 1 , 2 , 3 Although the pathophysiology of this disordered adduction is poorly understood, the current prevailing hypothesis is that it is due to an aberrant laryngeal response because of hypersensitive laryngeal sensory receptors, however, other underlying neurological etiologies such as brainstem compression, upper/lower motor neuron injury, movement disorder, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), factitious disorder, and malingering, have also been proposed. 1 Previously thought to be a somewhat rare disease primarily occurring in young female patients, more recent investigations have demonstrated that VCD can affect both male and female patients at any age, although it appears more common in female patients at a 2:1 or 3:1 ratio. 3 , 4 The true prevalence of VCD is unknown. However, it is likely underdiagnosed, with some reports estimating it at roughly 2%–3% of all patients with frequent ER visits due to dyspnea of sudden onset or admission to rehabilitation units due to respiratory problems, up to 27%. 5 , 6 Following the COVID‐19 pandemic, certain institutions noted a nearly two‐fold increase in the incidence of VCD, and case reports have emerged of patients previously affected with SARS‐CoV‐2 presenting with “post‐viral VCD,” underscoring the more recent resurgence in VCD as a clinical entity. 7 , 8

Patients experiencing VCD often describe a range of symptoms from mild dyspnea, wheezing, and cough, to acute‐onset respiratory distress. 1 , 4 Patients can report a wide variety of triggers for their symptoms, including exercise, exertion, allergens, chemical irritants, speaking, laughing, certain temperatures, swallowing, and drinking. 1 , 2 , 3 Due to the overlap of symptoms with asthma and sometimes alarming presentation, many VCD patients are often incorrectly diagnosed with asthma and started on asthma medications, sometimes presenting to the Emergency Room and requiring intubation, with no subsequent improvement in symptoms. 6 This delayed diagnosis not only severely impacts the quality of life of patients but also has a significant economic toll on the healthcare system. 5 Li et al. analyzed factors predicting a true diagnosis of VCD prior to videostroboscopy and found that a presentation of younger patients with dyspnea, lack of objective criteria for asthma diagnosis, and poor response to asthma pharmacotherapy should prompt investigation for VCD. 4 The diagnosis of VCD is further complicated by the fact that a substantial proportion of VCD patients can also have a true, comorbid diagnosis of asthma. 9 , 10

The workup of VCD begins with a thorough investigation of patient symptoms. As there currently exists no unified definition of VCD, there are no validated patient questionnaires that can be used to assess the likelihood of true disease given a set of symptoms or assess treatment response, although several have been proposed. 9 , 11 , 12 The current workup includes pulmonary function tests (PFTs), and flexible laryngoscopy to determine the presence of laryngeal adduction, with the latter considered the gold standard of diagnosis. 3 , 13 , 14 However, given the episodic nature of VCD, laryngeal adduction is not always appreciable on laryngoscopy, particularly if the patient is asymptomatic at the time of evaluation. This absence of laryngeal adduction at rest has led some to investigate the diagnostic utility of exercise laryngoscopy and continuous laryngoscopy during exercise (CLE). 15 Additional provocations used include odor, phonation and breathing challenges. 3

Long‐term management of VCD typically consists of a combination of breathing retraining and laryngeal‐respiratory retraining therapy (LRT), its efficacy demonstrated in a recent systematic review 16 and multiple other studies. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Pharmacological agents such as benzodiazepines and botulinum toxin injections may be effective in carefully selected patients. 21 , 22 , 23

Thus, the goal of the present study is to describe a cohort of patients with presumed VCD treated primarily with respiratory/laryngeal retraining, as well as their response to treatment. More importantly, given the episodic nature of VCD and the lack of laryngeal adduction on flexible laryngoscopy in many patients with the presumed disease, we aimed to compare the response to LRT for patients with and without laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy.

METHODS

A retrospective chart review was performed for patients referred to the senior author from 2018 to 2022 for possible VCD. The acronym VCD was chosen as this was the most commonly used nomenclature for referral to the senior author and the most searchable term in our electronic medical records system. Local Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained prior to initiation of the study. Patients referred for possible VCD included those with dyspnea out of proportion to their workup, which usually included a PFT or who previously failed empiric treatment for asthma, allergic rhinitis or GERD. Patients with posterior glottic and subglottic/tracheal stenosis were excluded from the study. Patients with a lack of follow‐up data or who had not undergone a single session of respiratory/laryngeal retraining were excluded from the final analysis. Patients were evaluated both at rest and with exercise to provoke dyspnea. Flexible laryngoscopy at rest was performed after instillation of topical anesthesia and decongestion with an aerosolized mixture of lidocaine/neosynephrine mixture into both nasal cavities. The Olympus VH flexible laryngoscope was placed in the hypopharynx, taking care to not encroach on the supraglottis. When patients poorly tolerate the VH laryngoscope in the nasal cavity, the pediatric V3 flexible laryngoscope was also employed. Quiet breathing was first observed for two to three cycles. Then patient was asked to forcefully inhale and exhale and again the vocal fold motion was observed for two to three cycles. If no laryngeal adduction was observed during inhalation at rest, then the patients were instructed to proceed to exercise. The exercises included running up and down the stairs in the stairwell in the outpatient clinic building, or simply walking down a long corridor, depending on the patient's reported exercise tolerance. Once the patient felt dyspneic, then he/she immediately returned to the exam chair and was examined with the flexible laryngoscopy again in the same manner. Odor and chemical irritant challenges were not routinely performed usually because of the patient's report of laryngospasm and the extreme accompanying discomfort. Laryngoscopy was evaluated by the senior author and considered abnormal if laryngeal adduction was observed for more than 50% of the inhalation phase. Only laryngeal adduction on inspiration was documented, even when it could be observed in both phases. The duration of closure was a subjective assessment and was not based on an objective measurement of the time duration. Other laryngoscopy findings such as paresis, or supraglottic squeeze on phonation were not included in the evaluation.

The laryngoscopy was displayed on a video screen and the patient was able to view it although the exam was being performed, as well as during playback. Patients deemed appropriate for breathing retraining exercises were recommended to undergo an evaluation by our Speech and Language pathologists. However, insurance plans in the state of Arizona for patients of lower socioeconomic status did not cover outpatient speech pathology services. It has been our routine practice to circumvent this barrier by providing one free session of evaluation and treatment by having the patient scheduled under the laryngologist for one visit, but actually seen concurrently by the speech and language pathologist whose visit would not be billable. Hence, many patients in our cohort only were able to get one session with our Speech and Language pathologist. However, even that one session is hard to achieve because of the high demand and long wait time to see a speech and language pathologist in our practice. The sessions were delivered with a combination of in‐person and Telehealth visits, as the study period encompassed the COVID‐19 Pandemic. Whether the use of a computer and access to the internet would interfere with participating in Telehealth was not the focus of this study and could certainly play a role also in not undergoing it. All patients with suspected VCD were recommended to undergo LRT as the initial therapy.

Respiratory retraining was provided by two voice and upper airway specialized speech‐language pathologists with 10 and 5 years of experience, or via co‐treatment with second‐year SLP graduate students. A comprehensive voice and upper airway breathing evaluation was conducted by the SLP after ENT evaluation or contemporaneously during a multidisciplinary clinic. Patients were then scheduled for 50‐minute treatment sessions scheduled once per week. The typical duration of treatment was two to four sessions with more complex presentations requiring up to six or eight sessions. 24 Treatment included patient education, identification of triggers, establishment of optimal relaxed abdominal or rib‐focused thoracic breathing patterns 25 , 26 cultivation of awareness of neck and throat tension with intentional relaxation. 26 Rescue or recovery strategies included sniff inhalation, relaxed throat breathing, pursed lip breathing, and biphasic breathing. 24 Deconstructing postural tension, circumlaryngeal massage, neck, jaw, and tongue stretches, and mind–body or progressive relaxation‐type exercises were introduced and encouraged when appropriate. 24 , 25 , 26 Initially, patients were guided to focus on internalization of skills when dyspnea was not present or was minimally present and asked to demonstrate skills in session. Once relaxed breathing and recovery techniques were established at rest, patients were challenged to utilize strategies when safely triggered in the treatment environment. For example, after running the stairs, walking the halls, or exposure to strong scents. Biofeedback with flexible laryngoscopy was incorporated as needed to support the application of techniques and promote buy‐in. 27 All interventions were personalized to the patient's unique needs and grounded in the foundational techniques above.

A chart review was performed to identify patient demographics, comorbidities, and response to LRT treatment. Asthma in our cohort was verified based on the presence of a documented diagnosis for the patient in the electronic medical record (no pulmonary function testing was performed in our clinic). Patient symptoms were stratified qualitatively based on self‐reported severity of symptoms into four categories: constant (symptoms present daily), moderate (symptoms present at least weekly), intermittent (symptoms present at least monthly), and exertional (symptoms present only on exertion). Response to treatment was also stratified categorically based on self‐reported improvements into three categories: no response, partial response (any improvement in symptoms), and significant response (self‐reported major improvements in symptoms or complete resolution of symptoms). Mean time to response was calculated for all patients in various subgroups who did have a clinical response, whether it was a full or partial response. Descriptive and statistical analysis was performed for the cohort using independent t‐tests for the comparison of quantitative variables and the chi‐squared test for the comparison of categorical variables. All calculations were performed in the SPSS Version 29.0.2.0 software. A level of significance of .05 was chosen.

RESULTS

Cohort demographics/comorbidities

95 patients with possible VCD were identified in the initial chart review, of which 58 were included in the final analysis. Of the 37 patients who did not undergo respiratory/laryngeal retraining, 10 (27%) of them had a health insurance plan that did not cover outpatient LRT. The average of the group of patients who did not undergo LRT was 47.5 years of age. Of the 58 patients who did receive LRT, 3 (5%) of them had the same health insurance plan. The cohort who did undergo LRT consisted of 45 females and 13 males, with a mean age of 54.8 years (SD = 17.4) and an age range of 12–81. Mean BMI for the cohort was 32.2 (SD = 9.5). In terms of the most common comorbidities, 29 (49.2%) patients had a history of asthma, 23 (39.7%) had a history of GERD, 10 (17.2%) had a history of OSA, and 7 (12.1%) had a history of allergic rhinitis.

Cohort clinical presentations

In attempting to characterize the etiologies and clinical presentation of VCD within the cohort, several factors were analyzed (Table 1). Overall, 20 (34.4%) patients reported a history of a memorable, inciting event prior to the onset of their VCD symptoms. The most prevalent of these inciting events was an episode of intubation, which 6 (10.3%) of patients mentioned.

56 (96.6%) patients reported distinct triggers that preceded or exacerbated their VCD symptoms. Of these, the most common triggers were exercise/exertion (65.5%), chemical irritants (25.9%), speaking/laughing (20.7%), and environmental allergens (17.2%). In terms of symptom severity, 31 (53.4%) reported constant dyspnea, 10 (17.2%) reported moderate dyspnea, 14 (24.1%) reported intermittent dyspnea, and 3 (5.2%) reported only exertional dyspnea. The most commonly associated symptoms included hoarseness in 31 (53.4%), chest tightness in 22 (37.9%), globus/dysphagia in 21 (36.2%), throat tightness in 18 (31%), coughing in 11 (18.9%), stridor/wheezing in 8 (13.7%), sore throat in 5 (8.6%), and dizziness in 2 (3.4%) of the cohort. Abnormal laryngoscopy findings of laryngeal adduction were found in 33 (56.9%) of the cohort, either at rest or upon exertion, whereas 25 (43.1%) of the cohort had normal laryngoscopy findings both at rest and exertion.

Cohort response to LRT

Among the cohort, the mean number of LRT sessions completed was 4.0 (SD = 3.0). 43 (74.1%) of patients showed a response to therapy, of which 36 (62.1%) were partial and 7 (12.1%) were significant. The mean time to respond was 3.1 months (SD = 2.7).

Laryngoscopy stratification

Following overall cohort analysis, patients were stratified into two groups based on abnormal or normal laryngoscopy findings, defined by the presence or absence of laryngeal adduction, respectively, to elucidate meaningful clinical differences between the subgroups (Tables 2 and 3). The distribution of normal vs. abnormal laryngoscopy was 43.1% and 56.9%, respectively. In terms of demographics and comorbidities, there were no significant differences in age, BMI, comorbidities, intubation history, previous workup, disease triggers, or severity of symptoms between the groups.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort review, the authors examined the clinical presentations and response to LRT of a cohort presenting with a clinical diagnosis of VCD. Overall, the majority of patients (74.1%) reported a response to therapy, of which 36 (62.1%) were partial responses and 7 (12.1%) were significant responses. Patients with a partial response on average required 3.97 speech pathology sessions and patients with a significant/complete response on average required 4.5 sessions (p = .62). Thus, there were no significant differences in the number of speech pathology sessions between partial vs. significant response groups. When comparing patients with or without laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy, there were no significant differences in the number of LRT sessions undergone, mean time to response, and overall response rate between the groups. Taken as a whole, the authors believe that this data supports the idea that patients with a presumed clinical diagnosis of VCD based on symptoms should be managed with respiratory/laryngeal retraining, regardless of the presence or absence of laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy. This retrospective review suggests that the diagnosis and treatment of VCD may be guided mostly by presentation and symptoms, and not defined by laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy. Other authors have previously found normal laryngoscopy findings in patients with unexplained dyspnea. Olin et al had found a subset of these patients without any glottic or supraglottic changes during continuous exercise laryngoscopy, which made this patient group difficult to name. 28 Kitt et al studied a cohort of 30 patients with upper airway dyspnea (UAD), and 93.4% of them did not have inappropriate laryngeal adduction. Symptomatically, they were similar to patients with VCD, and ILO. Kitt also stratified the chronic and episodic dyspnea groups based on the level of anxiety and found no difference. 29 In our cohort, 31 patients (53.4%) reported constant dyspnea and constituted actually the majority of the patients in our study. 10 (17.2%) reported moderate dyspnea, 14 (24.1%) reported intermittent dyspnea, and 3 (5.2%) reported only exertional dyspnea. These studies where dyspnea is found in the presence of a patent airway do beg the question of whether or not laryngeal dyspnea is more of a sensory disturbance rather than a physical obstruction. To be clear, in the present study, the authors are not suggesting that the diagnosis and treatment of VCD may be guided solely by clinical presentation and symptoms. Rather, the presence of laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy affirms the diagnosis of VCD, but the absence does not preclude patients from being considered for respiratory retraining therapy, as the condition may in fact have heterogeneous laryngoscopic examinations.

The entity of unexplained dyspnea in otherwise healthy individuals dates back four decades ago, in a seminal report of five patients in the New England of Medicine in 1983. 30 The nomenclature has proliferated, and imaging has improved since then. However, several landmarks systemic reviews/expert panels in the last decade still showed continued uncertainty in terms of diagnosis, treatment or etiology. A multidisciplinary expert panel convened in Europe in 2017 led by Halvorsen et al., examined the literature on ILO and screened studies by positive findings of laryngeal adduction on laryngoscopy. They listed some known facts which included dyspnea related to the inspiratory phase. The authors concluded that there is a paucity of data in terms of diagnostic means and treatment. They also made recommendations for a surrogate diagnostic test to be developed as laryngoscopy is uncomfortable and the very test being performed may alter the aerodynamics of the larynx. In this study, the authors recognized the disease as being heterogeneous. They pushed for less of an emphasis on laryngoscopy, but the replacement diagnostic criteria were not clear. They saw several areas needing development including acquiring normative data for laryngeal cross‐sectional area for respiration at rest or under exertion, as the evaluation of laryngeal movement remained largely subjective. Recommendations were made to conduct randomized control trials regarding the efficacy of the various interventions used for VCD. 13

Since this task force report in 2017, the suggested areas for research and development have remained active but still not answered. Leong et al. proposed four phenotypes of VCD/ILO based on comorbidities and triggers to facilitate early recognition and treatment. 31 Objective measures for laryngeal angle or cross‐sectioning area can be done on dynamic CT scan but there is concern for radiation exposure. 32 Point‐of‐care ultrasound has been used but again normative data is needed for the imaging to be meaningful. 33 The attempt to conduct a randomized control trial to ascertain the efficacy of LRT Koh et al. failed and the failure was attributed to patients learning about the placebo arm from “Dr. Google” and withdrawing from the study, and speech pathologists being reluctant to administer the placebo exercises in the face of severe VCD. 34

In a systemic review by Mahoney et al from 2022, quasi‐experimental studies were included, and the review focused on various interventions for VCD, including respiratory retraining, botulinum toxin injections, inspiratory muscle strength training and amitriptyline, all were associated with symptom reduction. They were all deemed to be low in quality of evidence and high in bias. The inclusion criteria also mandated an abnormal adduction of the larynx for more than 50% of inhalation and/or exhalation. The diagnosis was made either on laryngoscopy or dynamic CT. It excluded studies that made the diagnosis on symptom questionnaires. Thirteen of the studies used glottic airway and respiratory retraining in their interventions. VCD symptom reduction was reported in 12 out of 13 studies. Statistical significance of symptom reduction was reported in 7 studies and the remaining studies had descriptive improvement only. 35

This systemic review lends support to the use of respiratory retraining, as the primary treatment modality offered by speech and language pathologists for VCD patients. It consists of a combination of patient education, investigation of symptom triggers, and targeted inspiratory and expiratory exercises to help during acute episodes. 18 , 19 , 36 , 37 , 38 The Olin EILOBI study utilized a novel breathing technique for the management of exercise‐induced laryngeal obstruction, EILO, and demonstrated that the majority of patients were able to employ their breathing techniques during exercise, leading to symptomatic improvement. 20 In Schonman et al.'s multi‐institutional 66% of patients who had utilized breathing techniques reported them as effective for managing their symptoms, compared to 80% of patients who utilized lifestyle modifications. 39

Most recently, to address the lack of clarity in the diagnosis and management of VCD, the Delphi study led by Paul Leong et al from 2023 was a consensus statement of 49 experts, mostly congruent with the 2017 European systemic review by Mahoney. Most experts were pulmonologists (60%); however, a wide range of professions were represented including.

Otolaryngology (19%), speech‐language therapy/speech pathology (16%), allergy/immunology (13%), and pediatrics (9%). The modifications to the diagnostic criteria included “if the patient does NOT manifest their typical symptoms during laryngoscopy, VCD/ILO is not excluded. The examination may still be useful to identify and/or rule out other processes.” This modification is in line with our proposal that laryngeal adduction was not the prerequisite or gold standard in the diagnosis of VCD. An additional update in the current Delphi statement is the removal of the requirement for an inducer or trigger because an inducer sometimes not be identified. 40

The large‐scale systemic reviews and expert panels have repeatedly highlighted the need to better define normal and abnormal laryngeal movement, and a need to develop and validate standardized laryngoscopy protocols. Until normative data is gathered, it is uncertain what would actually constitute abnormal adduction. What complicates the picture further is that laryngeal adduction could be found as an incidental finding on laryngoscopy in individuals without any dyspnea.

In light of the heterogeneity of the disease frequency and severity, as well as the often absence of laryngeal adduction on an exam, the approach to the diagnostic criteria perhaps should be loosened and not require visualization of laryngeal adduction. These patients often have had an exhaustive medical workup that was unremarkable and empiric treatment without improvement in dyspnea. Given its low risk, respiratory/laryngeal retraining may benefit this cohort of patients with unexplained dyspnea.

Associations between VCD, PVFM, and chronic refractory cough have been described in the literature. 32 , 33 The multiple interventions used for the treatment of VCD are quite similar for chronic refractory cough as well. 17 , 36 , 41 It is important in future studies to include other manifestations of laryngeal hypersensitivity such as ease of coughing and gagging on laryngoscopy. Also, hoarseness is the most associated symptom for patients with unexplained dyspnea. Therefore, future studies in VCD should also include any laryngoscopy abnormalities noted during phonation. Muscle tension dysphonia perhaps may play a role as well. Vance et al studied a cohort of 40 patients with PVFM in 2021. They found that a combination of voice therapy (VT), LPR treatment, and botulinum toxin injection achieved a reduction in symptoms in 90% of patients, whereas just VT and LPR treatment achieved symptom reduction in 40% of patients. Botox was given to 23% of the patients and the authors did not specify the selection criteria for those undergoing Botox treatment. The response to Botox suggested to the authors that perhaps laryngeal dystonia may be an etiology in PVFM as well. 37

The association of VCD with asthma and GERD can further complicate the picture and delay proper diagnosis of VCD. The 49.2% association with asthma and 39.7% association with GERD is not surprising and points to an airway hypersensitivity component which has previously been noted by other authors. 28 , 35 , 38 , 41 , 42

In our cohort, 49.2% of patients had a previous diagnosis of asthma, and 69% of patients had had a previous workup by either pulmonologists, gastroenterologists, or allergists. The overlap in clinical presentation between VCD and asthma makes for a challenging diagnosis, as well as the reality that 25%–50% of patients with asthma may have a concomitant diagnosis of VCD. 6 , 10 , 43 Traister et al. developed a novel scoring system to distinguish VCD from asthma, which demonstrated good sensitivity and specificity and the ability to accurately diagnose VCD in 77.8% of computer‐generated, simulated patients. 6 In their system, they found the symptoms of throat tightness, dysphonia, absence of wheezing, and presence of odors as a symptomatic trigger, as more predictive of VCD over asthma. Being able to distinguish between a patient with true VCD and one with both conditions and treat accordingly has been shown to successfully allow for the de‐escalation of asthma medications. 43

Although several validated questionnaires for the assessment of symptom severity and monitoring of cough, dyspnea, and voice disorders, there exists only one for the evaluation of VCD specifically. 11 , 12 The VCD Questionnaire (VCDQ) formulated by Fowler et al., consists of a series of 12 questions answered on a 5‐point Likert scale, was able to demonstrate test–retest reliability, as well as differentiate between VCD (laryngeal adduction noted on laryngoscopy), asthma, and healthy (control) patients. 11 As our data suggests, the absence of laryngeal adduction noted on laryngoscopy does not preclude patients with presumed VCD from benefitting from LRT, and thus, the future creation and widespread adoption of more validated patient‐reported outcome measure (PROM) instruments such as the VCDQ may lead to more accurate monitoring of patient response to treatment. However, the authors of the present study do not claim that questionnaires such as the VCDQ are sufficient for the diagnosis of VCD. This agrees with Fowler et al. who, in their original article when describing the formulation of the VCDQ, states that the questionnaire in itself is not intended to be a diagnostic tool. 11

This study has several important limitations. First, the study is limited in the retrospective nature of the study, allowing for the possibility of a lack of certain key data and biases to occur, as well as a lack of a control group. Our sample size was small. It would be important to establish the incidence of laryngeal adduction in normal healthy controls on flexible laryngoscopy. Finally, our study relied on chart review to assess patient‐reported, subjective improvement in symptoms, which may be less reliable compared to utilizing a validated PROM questionnaire to assess response to treatment.

CONCLUSION

In this retrospective cohort study, the authors found that laryngeal adduction of patients with presumed VCD can be present or absent. Both groups of patients can present with varying degrees of dyspnea. Both groups can equally benefit from laryngeal restraining. Therefore, we conclude that VCD is a clinical diagnosis and should not be based on laryngoscopy findings. Patients with presumed VCD and yet normal laryngoscopy findings should still undergo laryngeal retraining as a first‐line treatment.
