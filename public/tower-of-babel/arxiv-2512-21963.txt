Topological properties of generalized Markoff mod p p graphs
Shohei Satake, Yoshinori Yamasaki

Abstract
. The generalized Markoff mod p p graph is defined via the equation x 2 + y 2 + z 2 = x ​ y ​ z + κ x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=xyz+\kappa over the finite field 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} of prime order p p . In this paper, we investigate the topological properties of the graph such as non-planarity, surface embeddability, and the existence of short cycles. Our approach is based on a systematic construction of K 3 , 3 K_{3,3} -subdivisions, integrating techniques from graph theory, computer algebra, and number theory.

1.
Introduction

The Diophantine equation

(1.1)

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=xyz

is called the
Markoff equation
,
and a solution
(x,y,z)
is called a
Markoff triple
.
For a prime
p>3
,
let
{\mathcal{M}(p)}
denote the set of all Markoff triples over
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
, the finite field with
p
elements.
The
Markoff mod
p
graph

{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is a finite
3
-regular graph (possibly with loops) whose vertex set is
{\mathcal{M}(p)}\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}
, and two triples are adjacent if and only if they are transformed into each other by one of the following
Vieta involutions
:

R_{1}(x,y,z)=(yz-x,y,z),\quad R_{2}(x,y,z)=(x,zx-y,z),\quad R_{3}(x,y,z)=(x,y,xy-z).

The graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
has been extensively studied in the literature on “strong approximation” for the Markoff equation (
1.1
).
Indeed, Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak
[
2
]
conjectured that
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is connected for all primes
p>3
.
Recently Chen
[
9
]
made a breakthrough toward this conjecture by proving that
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is connected for any sufficiently large prime
p
.
A key result here is that the size of any component of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
must be divisible by
p
. Furthermore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are only
O(p^{\varepsilon})
vertices that are not contained in the giant component (
[
2
]
) while
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
has approximately
p^{2}
vertices (see (
1.3
) below).
Martin
[
24
]
provided an elementary proof of Chen’s result concerning the divisibility of the component sizes of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
.
In addition, Eddy, Fuchs, Litman, Martin, and Tripeny
[
14
]
established an explicit lower bound for primes
p
for which the connectivity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is guaranteed, namely,
p>3.448\times 10^{392}
.
There are several algorithmic results towards the connectivity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
, and it was verified in
[
4
]
that
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is connected for
p<10^{6}
.

On the other hand, de Courcy-Ireland
[
9
]
established the non-planarity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
:
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is planar if and only if
p=7
. This provides (indirect but positive) evidence for another well-known conjecture by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak
[
2
,
3
]
asserting that
\{{\mathcal{G}(p)}\}_{\text{$p$:prime}}
is in fact an expander family, which would imply higher connectivity for
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
(for further details on expander family, see e.g.
[
18
,
23
]
).
The proof of non-planarity consists of two parts: an asymptotic evaluation of the Euler characteristic
of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
and explicit constructions of subdivisions of the complete bipartite graph
K_{3,3}
.
The Kuratowski-Wagner theorem shows a complete characterization for planar graphs in terms of graph minors, that is, a graph is planar if and only if it contains neither
K_{3,3}
nor the complete graph
K_{5}
as a graph minor.
Since
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is a
3
-regular graph,
K_{3,3}
is the unique obstruction to planarity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
in the sense that
a
3
-regular graph cannot contain a subdivision of
K_{5}
, and more generally, any
3
-regular graph containing
K_{5}
as a minor must also contain a subdivision of
K_{3,3}
.

There are several generalizations of (
1.1
), such as the following equation: for a (rational) integer
\kappa
,

(1.2)

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=xyz+\kappa.

Throughout this paper, we naturally treat
\kappa
as an element of
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
when considering the solutions of (
1.2
) modulo
p
.
For the set of all such solutions over
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(p)}
,
Carlitz
[
5
]
proved that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\#{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(p)}=p^{2}+\left(3+\left(\frac{\kappa}{p}\right)\right)\left(\frac{\kappa-4}{p}\right)p+1,

where
(\frac{\cdot}{p})
is the Legendre symbol.
The
generalized Markoff mod
p
graph

{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
(with respect to (
1.2
)) is defined as a
3
-regular graph
whose vertex set is
{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(p)}

with the adjacency relation defined in the same manner as for
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
(hence
\mathcal{G}_{0}(p)
coincides with
{\mathcal{G}(p)}\sqcup\{(0,0,0)\}
, where
(0,0,0)
is an isolated vertex).
The graph structure of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
depends sensitively on
\kappa
and
p
in general.
For example, in the case of
\kappa=4
(corresponding to a degenerate surface called the
Cayley cubic
, cf.
[
8
]
), the graph
\mathcal{G}_{4}(p)
possesses many small components.
While
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is no longer connected in general,
it is still expected that for
\kappa\neq 4
,
there exists a unique
giant component

\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p)
in

{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
such that
\#({\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p))=o_{\kappa}(p^{2})
. In a recent preprint
[
25
]
, Martin announced that this in fact holds for a majority of primes.
Moreover, the main result of
[
25
]
would imply that
\#({\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p))=O(1)
, representing a significant advance in the understanding of the global structure of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
.
However, the precise structure of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
(and
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p)
) is still largely unexplored.

In this paper, we investigate the topological properties of the generalized Markoff mod
p
graph
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
by combining perspectives of graph theory, computer algebra, and number theory.
In particular, by leveraging computational algebraic methods including resultants and Gröbner bases, in conjunction with the Chebotarev density theorem, we derive the following results based on graph minor theory.
Throughout this paper, the term
K_{3,3}
-subdivision
refers to a subdivision of the complete bipartite graph
K_{3,3}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
.
Then the followings hold:

(1)

(\mathrm{Theorems}~\ref{thm-n=1},\ \ref{thm:n=2 non-planarity}\ \text{and}\ \ref{thm-n=(p-1)/2})

For each such
\kappa
and for infinitely many primes
p
(depending on
\kappa
) of natural density at least
1/2
,
there exists an explicit
K_{3,3}
-subdivision in
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
.

(2)

(\mathrm{Theorem}~\ref{thm:np density})

Moreover, for almost all
\kappa
,
the natural density of primes
p
for which
(1)
holds is at least
13/16
.

Theorem
1.1
yields several topological properties of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
.
In particular, the following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the same assumptions on
\kappa
and
p
of Theorem
1.1

(1)
,
the followings hold:

(1)

(\mathrm{Corollary}~\ref{cor:sufficient condition non-planarity})

{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is non-planar.

(2)

(\mathrm{Corollary}~\ref{cor:cycles})

{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
has cycles (i.e. closed paths with no repeated vertices or edges) of lengths
6,9,10,15
, and
18
,
all of which can be constructed explicitly.

Note that it would be interesting to obtain results analogous to Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
for other generalizations of the Markoff mod
p
graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
(e.g.
[
10
]
); these will be discussed in our subsequent works.

Recall that
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
admits the following non-trivial graph automorphisms, known as
double sign changes
:

(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,-z),\quad(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,-z),\quad\text{and}\quad(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z).

These automorphisms, together with the identity, form a group isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.
Combining this symmetry of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}

with Theorem
1.1
,
we obtain an explicit construction of mutually vertex-disjoint
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

For each
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
,
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
contains at least four mutually vertex-disjoint
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions for infinitely many primes
p
(depending on
\kappa
) of natural density at least
1/4
if
\kappa=2
and
1/2
otherwise.
Moreover, for almost all
\kappa
, the natural density of primes
p
for which this holds is at least
5/8
.

Theorem
1.3
strengthens Corollary
1.2
(1). Consider an embedding of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
into a closed surface (i.e. a compact topological surface without boundary); see e.g.
[
17
,
27
]
for the details on surface embeddings of graphs.
Recall that the Euler characteristic
\chi
of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is defined by
V-E+F
, where
V,E
, and
F

denote the numbers of vertices, edges and faces, respectively.
Combining
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
with Theorem
1.3
,
it follows that
\chi\leq-6

for
\kappa
and
p
as in Theorem
1.3
,
whereas Euler’s formula requires
\chi=2
if
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is planar.
Moreover, the existence of mutually vertex-disjoint
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions highlights the robustness of the non-planarity of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
in the sense that, by Theorem
1.3
, one must remove at least four vertices to make the graph planar.
It is worth noting that, in light of
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
, our construction of
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions implies that the Euler characteristic of the giant component
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p)
is at most
-6
for any
\kappa\neq 4

and infinitely many primes
p
(see Section
5
for details).
From these structural perspectives, our results serve as heuristic evidence for the expander property of the giant component
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p)
.
At least,
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}(p)
in fact possesses certain complex structure similar to that of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
.

It is also worth mentioning that in cryptography and number theory, studying the structure of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is crucial for understanding the security of the associated cryptographic hash functions proposed in
[
16
,
30
]
, which are expected to be expander hash functions
[
6
]
.
In particular, it is a challenging open problem to investigate the existence and distribution of short cycles in
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
, as these properties directly affect the collision resistance of the hash functions (
[
11
,
16
]
).
Corollary
1.2
(2) provides a modest step toward addressing this problem.

In addition, for
\kappa=0
,
we give a complete characterization for primes
p
for which the graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
admits an embedding into closed surfaces of minimal topological complexity beyond the plane.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem
6.1
)
.

Let
p>3
be a prime. Then the graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
admits an embedding into the torus
(i.e. the orientable closed surface of Euler characteristic zero)
or the (real) projective plane
(i.e. the non-orientable closed surface of Euler characteristic one)
if and only if
p=7
.

This result is achieved through Theorem
1.1
by finding suitable forbidden minors for these embeddings, as well as the numerical verification of the connectivity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
for
p<10^{6}
(
[
4
]
).
Note that the graph
\mathcal{G}(7)
is planar, and hence one can draw it on any closed surface without edge crossings.
Consequently, Theorem
1.4
immediately implies the non-planarity of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
for any
p\neq 7
, and hence strengthens the main result of
[
9
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the basic and relevant properties of Chebyshev polynomials, which play a key role in constructing
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions in
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
.
In Section
3
, we present a systematic construction of
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions based on careful computations of Chebyshev polynomials and their associated resultants.
Briefly, we first reduce the construction to the problem of solving systems of algebraic equations via Gröbner bases to obtain candidates for the sextuples of triples that form the desired subdivisions.
Then we establish sufficient conditions for the “properness”, which guarantees the claimed topological properties, of the subdivisions corresponding to the obtained sextuples.
Based on these arguments, Section
4
provides explicit
K_{3,3}
-subdivisions.
Finally, Sections
5
and
6
are devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, respectively.

2.
Chebyshev polynomials

In this section,
we recall some basic properties of the Chebyshev polynomials and introduce a key polynomial
A_{m}(x)
that plays a central role in our study.

The Chebyshev polynomials
T_{m}(x),U_{m}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
of the first and second kind, respectively, are defined by the following recurrence relations:

(2.1)

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\quad T_{m+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2xT_{m}(x)-T_{m-1}(x)\quad(m\geq 1),

(2.2)

\displaystyle U_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad U_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=2x,

\displaystyle\quad U_{m+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2xU_{m}(x)-U_{m-1}(x)\quad(m\geq 1).

For convenience, we extend these definitions to negative indices by setting
T_{-1}(x)=x
,
T_{-2}(x)=2x^{2}-1
and

U_{-1}(x)=0
,
U_{-2}(x)=-1
.
We summarize the properties used in this paper below.

Lemma 2.1
.

(1)

We have

(2.3)

\displaystyle T_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}_{j=0}t_{m,j}(2x)^{m-2j},\quad

\displaystyle t_{m,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{j}\left\{\binom{m-j}{j}+\binom{m-j-1}{j}\right\},

(2.4)

\displaystyle U_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}_{j=0}u_{m,j}(2x)^{m-2j},\quad

\displaystyle u_{m,j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\binom{m-j}{j}.

(2)

U_{m}(0)=(-1)^{m/2}
if
m
is even and
0
otherwise.

(3)

U_{m}(\pm 1)=(\pm 1)^{m}(m+1)
.

(4)

U_{2m}(x)+1=2T_{m}(x)U_{m}(x)
.

(5)

U_{2m}(x)-1=2T_{m+1}(x)U_{m-1}(x)
.

(6)

U_{2m-1}(x)=2T_{m}(x)U_{m-1}(x)
.

Define the polynomial
A_{m}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
of degree
m
by

(2.5)

\displaystyle A_{m}(x)\coloneq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,|\,2m+1\\
d\geq 3\end{subarray}}\Psi_{d}(2x),

where
\Psi_{m}(x)
is the minimal polynomial of
2\cos(2\pi/m)
over
\mathbb{Q}
, which was studied, for example in
[
22
]
.
As shown in
[
21
, Lemma 2.2]
,

A_{m}(x)
satisfies the recurrence relation

(2.6)

\displaystyle A_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad A_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=2x+1,

\displaystyle\quad A_{m+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2xA_{m}(x)-A_{m-1}(x)\quad(m\geq 1).

Together with (
2.2
), this shows that

(2.7)

\displaystyle A_{m}(x)=U_{m}(x)+U_{m-1}(x).

In this paper,
A_{m}(x/2)
plays an important role.
The first few are given as follows:

\displaystyle A_{0}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1,\quad A_{1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)=x+1,\quad A_{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)=x^{2}+x-1,\quad A_{3}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)=x^{3}+x^{2}-2x-1,

\displaystyle A_{4}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{4}+x^{3}-3x^{2}-2x+1,\quad A_{5}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)=x^{5}+x^{4}-4x^{3}-3x^{2}+3x+1.

We also summarize several properties of

Throughout this paper, we denote the discriminant of a polynomial

Lemma 2.2

(1)

We have

(2.8)

(3)

(4)

For

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}

p>3

the graph
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
contains no subgraph isomorphic to

Proof.

Suppose that the six triples

K_{3,3}

Then the only possibility is that

Y_{2}=R_{1}(X_{3})=R_{3}(X_{1})

Y_{3}=R_{2}(X_{1})=R_{1}(X_{2})

This implies

a_{2}=b_{2}=c_{2}

a_{3}=b_{3}=c_{3}

This contradicts the assumption that the triples form

Suppose that

(1) By symmetry of

-

(i)

3.2.
Rewriting the conditions for
{T^{\mathrm{all}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}

To study the system of algebraic equations in
{T^{\mathrm{all}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}
,
let

{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}\coloneq\left\langle A_{n}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right),f_{\kappa}(x,y)\right\rangle

denote the ideal of
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}[x,y]
generated by

A_{n}(x/2)
and
f_{\kappa}(x,y)
.
To study
{T^{\mathrm{all}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}
,
we need a good basis of
{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}
.
To do that, we recall the definition of the resultant of two polynomials:
Let
R
be a commutative ring.
For polynomials
f(x)=\sum^{n}_{i=0}a_{i}x^{i}

and
g(x)=\sum^{m}_{i=0}b_{i}x^{i}
in
R[x]
,
the resultant
\Res(f,g)\in R
of
f
and
g

is defined by

\Res(f,g)\coloneq\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}a_{n}&a_{n-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&&&\\
&a_{n}&a_{n-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&a_{n}&a_{n-1}&\cdots&a_{0}\\
\hline\cr b_{m}&b_{m-1}&\cdots&b_{0}&&&\\
&b_{m}&b_{m-1}&\cdots&b_{0}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&b_{m}&b_{m-1}&\cdots&b_{0}\end{array}\right|.

Now, define

\displaystyle B_{n,\kappa}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Res_{x}\left(A_{n}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right),f_{\kappa}(x,y)\right)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}a_{n,0}&a_{n,1}&\cdots&a_{n,n-1}&a_{n,n}\\
&a_{n,0}&a_{n,1}&\cdots&a_{n,n-1}&a_{n,n}\\[5.0pt]
\hline\cr 2-y&0&y^{2}-\kappa&\\
&2-y&0&y^{2}-\kappa&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&2-y&0&y^{2}-\kappa&\\
\end{array}\right|,

where
\Res_{x}
denotes the resultant with respect to the variable
x
,
and the coefficients
a_{n,j}
are defined as in (
2.8
).
Note that
B_{n,\kappa}(y)
is a monic polynomial in
y
of degree
2n
.
The following proposition enables us to obtain
(\alpha,\beta)\in{T^{\mathrm{all}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}
by solving the equation
B_{n,\kappa}(y)=0
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}

and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
satisfying

2n+1\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
Define

(3.28)

\eta_{n,\kappa}\coloneq\Res\left(A_{n}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right),x^{4}-8x^{2}+4\kappa\right)\in{\mathbb{F}_{p}}.

If
\eta_{n,\kappa}\neq 0
,
then there exists a unique polynomial
C_{n,\kappa}(y)\in{\mathbb{F}_{p}}[y]
of degree
<2n
such that

(3.29)

{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}=\left\langle B_{n,\kappa}(y),x-\frac{1}{(\kappa-4)^{n-1}}C_{n,\kappa}(y)\right\rangle.

Proof.

It is clear that
{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}

is a zero-dimensional ideal.
Since
D(A_{n}(x/2))=(2n+1)^{n-1}

by Lemma
2.2
(4),
our assumption on
n
ensures that

A_{n}(x/2)
has no multiple roots.
Moreover, since
D_{y}(f_{\kappa}(x,y))=x^{4}-8x^{2}+4\kappa
, where
D_{y}
denotes the discriminant with respect to the variable
y
,
the assumption
\eta_{n,\kappa}\neq 0

implies that
f_{\kappa}(\gamma,y)
has no multiple roots
for any root
\gamma
of
A_{n}(x/2)=0
.
These observations show that
{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}
is a radical ideal of
{\mathbb{F}_{p}}[x,y]
.
Therefore, we can apply the so-called shape Lemma (see, e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 3.7.25)]
),
which guarantees the existence of
unique polynomials

such that

is monic,

\deg h(y)<\deg g(y)
,
and

(such a basis is called a shape basis, which is a special type of reduced Gröbner basis).
Since
g(y)
generates the elimination ideal
{I_{n,\kappa}(p)}\cap{\mathbb{F}_{p}}[y]
, we may take it to be the resultant
B_{n,\kappa}(y)
.
Finally, we put
C_{n,\kappa}(y)\coloneq(\kappa-4)^{n-1}h(y)
,
where the factor is introduced as a normalization.
∎

Now, by (
3.29
),
we have
A_{n}(\beta/2)=f_{\kappa}(\beta,\alpha)=0
if and only if
B_{n,\kappa}(\alpha)=\beta-C_{n,\kappa}(\alpha)/(\kappa-4)^{n-1}=0
.
Moreover, if
\xi_{n,\kappa}\neq 0
and
\eta_{n,\kappa}\neq 0
, where

\xi_{n,\kappa}\coloneq\Res\left(A_{n}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right),x^{3}-3x^{2}+\kappa\right)\in{\mathbb{F}_{p}},

then neither of the systems

A_{n}(x/2)=x^{2}(3-x)-\kappa=0
and

A_{n}(x/2)=x^{2}(8-x^{2})-4\kappa=0

has a common root.
Therefore, one obtains the following:

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
and

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
satisfying
2n+1\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
Assume that
\xi_{n,\kappa}\eta_{n,\kappa}\neq 0

(1)

We have

(3.30)

{T^{\mathrm{all}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}={T^{\mathrm{dist}}_{n,\kappa}(p)}=\left\{\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\in{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\times{\mathbb{F}^{\times}_{p}}\,\left|\,B_{n,\kappa}(\alpha)=0,\ \beta=\frac{C_{n,\kappa}(\alpha)}{(\kappa-4)^{n-1}}\right.\right\}.

(2)

If

B_{n,\kappa}(\alpha)=B_{n,\kappa}(\overline{\alpha})=0

Hence, the roots of

Proof.

The above observations show all assertions except for

The remaining one follows from

∎

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{0}&=3,\quad S_{1}=-2a,\quad S_{2}=2a^{2}+8c,\\

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
.
Assume that

\xi_{2,\kappa}\eta_{2,\kappa}\neq 0

Proof.

Let

where

{\mathbb{F}_{p}}

Hence, we can apply the above results.
In this case, the sequence

defined in (
4.2
) is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{0}&=3,\quad S_{1}=\frac{1}{4}(16\kappa-29),\quad S_{2}=\frac{1}{16}(16\kappa-49)(16\kappa-19),\\[5.0pt]
S_{n+3}&=\frac{1}{4}(16\kappa-29)S_{n+2}-\frac{5}{16}(16\kappa-9)S_{n+1}+\frac{625}{64}S_{n}\quad(n\geq 0).\end{split}

Choose any
\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}

Then, we have

S_{n}=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{n}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{n}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{n}.

If
\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}\in{\mathbb{F}_{p}}

\displaystyle S_{p+1}=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{2}=S_{2}.

\displaystyle S_{(p-1)/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=5^{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\left(8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\left(8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{5}{p}\right)+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{p}\right)+\left(\frac{8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{p}\right).

Therefore,

if and only if

(ii)

,

\displaystyle S_{p+1}=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{2}+2\left(\frac{8\kappa-17+2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)\left(\frac{8\kappa-17-2\sqrt{\eta_{2,\kappa}}}{4}\right)=\frac{275}{16}.

Consequently,

This completes the proof from (
4.3

4.5.
Natural densities

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
.
From Theorem
4.1
, Theorem
4.4
(B),
or Theorem
4.7
,
it follows from Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions that there are infinitely many primes
p
for which

{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
contains a
K_{3,3}
-subdivision, which yields topological properties of
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
such as the non-planarity and existence of short cycles by Corollaries
3.3
and
3.4
, respectively.
This motivates us to consider the natural density of such primes
p
:

\delta_{\kappa}\coloneq\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\#\{p\leq x\,|\,\text{${\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}$ contains a $K_{3,3}$-subdivision}\}}{\#\{p\leq x\}}.

It holds that
\delta_{0}=1

since
\mathcal{G}(p)
is non-planar for any
p\neq 7

by
[
9
, p.115]
.
For any
\kappa\neq 4
, Theorem
4.1
and the Chebotarev density theorem (see, e.g.
[
29
]
)
imply that
\delta_{\kappa}\geq 1/2
.
Moreover, combining the three theorems above,
we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

(1)

For each
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
such that none of

\eta_{1,\kappa}
,

\eta_{2,\kappa}
,

5
, and
\kappa-4

is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
,
and that the ratio of any two of them is not a square in
\mathbb{Q}
,
we have

(4.4)

\delta_{\kappa}\geq\frac{13}{16}.

That is,
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
contains a
K_{3,3}
-subdivision
for at least
81.25\%

of primes
p
asymptotically.

(2)

The natural density
of
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
for which
(1)
holds
is equal to
1
.

Remark 4.9
.

If either
\eta_{1,\kappa}
or
\kappa-4
is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
,
then
{\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}(p)}
is non-planar for all but finitely many primes
p
. Consequently,
\delta_{\kappa}=1
for such
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
.
Note that, one can check that
\eta_{1,\kappa}
is a square if
\kappa=t^{2}+t+2
for some ineteger
t
.
On the other hand, if
\eta_{2,\kappa}
is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
,
which occurs if and only if
\kappa=10
,
then, since the conditions in
Theorem
4.4
(A)
reduces to
(\frac{5}{p})=1
,

\delta_{\kappa}\geq 1/2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.8
.

We first prove
(1)
.
For
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}

and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r}\in\{\pm 1\}
, define

Q(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r};\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r})\coloneq\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\#\{p\leq x\,|\,(\frac{a_{1}}{p})=\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,(\frac{a_{r}}{p})=\varepsilon_{r}\}}{\#\{p\leq x\}}.

By the Chebotarev density theorem and the prime number theorem,
if none of
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
is a square in
\mathbb{Z}
and
the ratio of any two of them is also not a square in
\mathbb{Q}
,
then

(4.5)

Q(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r};\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r})=\frac{1}{2^{r}}.

Notice that
2^{r}
is the order of the Galois group of the multiquadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{a_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{a_{r}})
over
\mathbb{Q}

under our assumptions.
Then, by Theorem
4.1
, Theorem
4.4
(B), and Theorem
4.7
,
and applying the inclusion-exclusion principle,
we have

\displaystyle\delta_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\geq Q(\eta_{1,\kappa};1)+Q(\eta_{2,\kappa},5;-1,1)+Q(\kappa-4;1)

\displaystyle\quad-Q(\eta_{1,\kappa},\eta_{2,\kappa},5;1,-1,1)-Q(\eta_{2,\kappa},5,\kappa-4;-1,1,1)-Q(\kappa-4,\eta_{1,\kappa};1,1)

\displaystyle\quad+Q(\eta_{1,\kappa},\eta_{2,\kappa},5,\kappa-4;1,-1,1,1)

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{16}.

We next show
(2)
.
For
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{4\}
, put

Moreover, define

\displaystyle u_{i}(x)

,
and

for

as

It is easy to see that

and
u_{2}(x)=u_{3}(x)=O(\sqrt{x})

For

.

For

.

.
Similarly,
v_{13}(x)=O(\sqrt{x})
.
For
v_{14}(x)

.

.

.

(a,b)

(a,b)\in\{(\pm 1,\pm 1),(\pm 5,\pm 2)\}

.

.
Therefore,
a

.
Hence, by applying again the standard theory of this type of equation, together with the above observation, we obtain
v_{24}(x)=O(\log{x})

Finally, by the same argument, we have

The embeddability of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}

In this section, we discuss the embeddability of the Markoff mod
p

{\mathcal{G}(p)}
.
We say a graph
G
is
toroidal
if it can be embedded into the torus.
Similarly,
G
is said to be
projective-planar
if it admits an embedding into the the projective plane.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
p>3
be a prime. Then we have the followings.

(1)

The graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is toroidal if and only if
p=7
.

(2)

The graph
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is projective-planar if and only if
p=7
.

Proof.

We provide the proof for (1),
as (2) follows from an analogous argument.
To this end we show that if
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
is toroidal then
5\leq p\leq 17
.
Let
\chi
denote the Euler characteristic of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
, and
V
be the number of vertices in the giant component of
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
.
Then by the same discussion to obtain (3.4) in
[
9
]
, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle V\leq 15\biggl(p-4-\Bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\Bigr)\biggr)+6s+2h-14\chi

where
s
and
h
denote the numbers of squares and hexagons in
{\mathcal{G}(p)}
, respectively.
Hence, the counting result for
s
and

(
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
)
combined with the inequality (
6.1
) yield

\displaystyle V\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}26p-90-14\chi&\text{if $p\equiv 1\pmod{12}$},\\
26p-82-14\chi&\text{if $p\equiv 5\pmod{12}$},\\
17p-51-14\chi&\text{if $p\equiv 7\pmod{12}$},\\
17p-43-14\chi&\text{if $p\equiv 11\pmod{12}$}.\end{array}\right.

Indeed, it was proved in
[
9

(6.6)

\displaystyle V\geq\frac{1}{2}p\phi(p+1)\geq\frac{p(p+1)}{1000\log\log(p+1)}
