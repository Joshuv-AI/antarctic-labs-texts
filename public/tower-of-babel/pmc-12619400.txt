Intensive Care management of Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis: a case series

Background

Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a syndrome characterized by severe hyperinflammation and immune dysfunction with concomitant immune system activation [1] whose exact incidence in the general and in intensive care population is currently unknown. It often requires supportive management in intensive care units (ICU) (> 60% in pediatric HLH cases) with high mortality rates (36–40% of pediatric and 41–68% of adult cases) [1].

Traditionally, HLH is divided into a primary/familial form (F-HLH) and secondary reactive form (sHLH). F-HLH is an inherited disease conferred by highly penetrant genetic mutations/variations affecting cytolytic functions, lymphocyte survival, or inflammasome activation. In contrast, secondary HLH is mainly caused by acquired factors, such as chronic inflammation, infections, or malignancy [2].

sHLH is commonly triggered by infections, malignancy, rheumatologic disease, allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), Chimeric Antigen Receptor – T cell (CAR-T) therapy [3] drug hypersensitivity or other underlying causes. Immune dysfunction in sHLH is characterized by reversible dysfunction of Natural Killer (NK) or CD8 + T cells, which occurs in some viral infections or rheumatologic disorders, or by NK-cell deficiency, which can occur after chemotherapy or during sepsis.

Diagnosis of HLH in ICU can be difficult due to the lack of specific laboratory, radiological and histopathologic findings. For example, bone marrow hemophagocytosis may be observed in the absence of proven hemophagocytic syndrome, particularly after blood transfusion or in the setting of sepsis [4]. Furthermore, the clinical features of HLH may overlap with the Multi-Organ Dysfunction syndrome (MODs) that characterizes most ICU admission.

Scores tacking into account clinical and laboratory finding, such as HScore and HLH-2004, have been developed for HLH diagnosis and their use is suggested in the ICU setting [4, 5].

Suggested treatment strategies are based on the HLH-2004 protocols which include high dose corticosteroids, etoposide and cyclosporine, followed by adjunctive/rescue therapy such as intravenous administration of immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis and tocilizumab [1, 5]. However, these therapeutic protocols are borrowed from pediatric and hematology guidelines and only scattered studies with data regarding ICU treatment have been reported. To date, the literature on cases in critically ill patients is scarce and fragmentary, while a growing interest and novel awareness of this syndrome is spreading among ICU physicians.

Here we present the clinical characteristics, possible triggers and outcomes of a cohort of six patients admitted to our ICU for HLH or HLH-induced MOD.

Methods

All patients admitted to the general ICU of Città della Salute e della Scienza Hospital in Turin (Italy) between January 2016 and March 2024 with a clinical or autoptic diagnosis of HLH were included.

Demographic data, comorbidities, characteristics at ICU admission, laboratory and microbiological data, autopsy histopathological results, treatment and outcome were collected retrospectively.

The study has been approved by the local ethics committee (Comitato Etico AOU Interaziendale Città della Salute e della Scienza, AO Ordine Mauriziano, ASL Città di Torino) as part of the PROSAFE network [6] a prospective multicentric observational project involving 192 Italian ICU partecipating to GiViTI (Gruppo Italiano per la Valutazione degli Interventi in Terapia Intensiva), The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics Committee waived the need for patients’ consent due to the retrospective observational nature of this study.

SOFA and SAPSII score at admission were calculated. All but one patient had a HScore with high probability of HLH, even when not all HScore items were tested. Three patients developed pancytopenia, two had bilineage cytopenia and one had monolineage cytopenia.

Two patients did not receive a specific HLH diagnosis during the ICU stay and the diagnosis was only made on autopsy. The first was treated for septic shock with broad spectrum antimicrobial therapy and intravenous acyclovir for disseminated HSV1 infection, but a genomic mutation for acyclovir resistance [7] was later found. The second developed multi-organ failure (MOF) during status epilepticus caused by autoimmune encephalitis with hypotension refractory to vasopressors.

We found infection as a potential trigger in all patients. Four patients had a malignancy, three of which were of hematological origin. Four patients were treated with invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and one with continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT).

All patients diagnosed with HLH were treated with high dose corticosteroids, one received tocilizumab, one etoposide, one intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and one cyclosporine. All therapeutic strategies were decided in agreement with the consultant hematologist.

Bone Marrow Biopsy (BMB) was performed in all patients, and resulted diagnostic for HLH, except for the two patients for whom only an autoptic diagnosis was obtained.

All patients died before hospital discharge; one patient was discharged form ICU but subsequently died in hematology ward.

Discussion

Considering the rarity of this syndrome and the lack of diagnostic and therapeutic standard in ICU patients, we believe that this case series, although limited, can provide important insights to draw the attention of intensivists to HLH and its characteristics.

First, it is important to emphasize that out of six cases, only three had early clinical suspicion. It is, however, true that the only score currently considered the gold standard in ICU, namely the HScore, proposed in 2014 and applied to ICU patients in the American Consensus Guidelines in 2022 [1], was clearly positive in 5 out of 6 patients. This result reiterates the importance of early clinical suspicion, as well as the potential value of a composite score, combining clinical and laboratory factors with possible histological finding.

However, biopsy is not strictly necessary to define the diagnosis since BMB is only one of the elements to be considered for HLH diagnosis, together with cheaper and easier-to-perform laboratory exams, such as ferritin, fibrinogen and triglycerides. Nonetheless, BMB still plays a central role in HLH diagnostic workup, and this can lead to late or even incorrect diagnosis.

In our series, HLH was induced by different trigger, such as viral infection, HSCT and COVID-19, in line with current literature [1, 8]. In two cases, due to the severity of presentation and the overlap of a disseminated viral infection with septic shock, the diagnosis was only autoptic.

Pathophysiology of HLH in critical care population is still not clearly understood anyway a strong association with some specific triggers has been established by two recent studies from a french restrospective cohort [9] and a german pooled analysis [8] (Table 2). Sepsis, determining an infection-mediated immune dysregulation, can be a HLH trigger in a vast majority of cases. Intracellular infections, especially viral infection such as CMV and Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) are also important triggers. Malignancy related HLH accounted for about one third of cases but was associated with higher mortality. In about 1 in 10 HLH cases no specific trigger was recognized.

Clinical presentation was always characterized by multi-organ disfunction (respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation, renal failure requiring CRRT, liver failure, neurologic failure with super-refractory status epilepticus) confirming that HLH represents a relevant, although rare, condition that must be considered for differential diagnosis in sepsis and septic shock of unknown origin.

The differential diagnosis of HLH in critically ill patients is particularly challenging due to the overlap of clinical and laboratory features with severe sepsis, septic shock, multiorgan failure, and other hyperinflammatory syndromes such as cytokine release syndrome or catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome. Evidence-based tools, including the HScore, adapted HLH-2004 criteria, and age- or context-specific modifications, have been developed to assist in identifying patients at high risk, although their performance in ICU cohorts remains variable. Serial assessment of ferritin kinetics, triglycerides, fibrinogen, soluble IL-2 receptor (sCD25), and natural killer (NK) cell activity, when available, may further improve diagnostic accuracy. Integrating these tools with structured clinical pathways and early multidisciplinary review—particularly involving hematology—can reduce the likelihood of misdiagnosis, expedite targeted therapy, and avoid unnecessary or potentially harmful interventions in patients with mimicking conditions.

Corticosteroids were used in all case in which HLH was diagnosed during ICU admission. In three cases dexamethasone was used, as suggested as first-line therapy by current literature [1, 5], and in one case methylprednisolone, already used for fibrotic-evolving pneumonia, was maintainted. Corticosteroids and other HLH-specific therapeutic strategies were decided in agreement with hematology. To note, the therapeutic approach of these cases (first line: dexamethasone, etoposide; additional/salvage therapies: intravenous Immunoglobulin, tocilizumab, ciclosporine) followed the general protocols for HLH, adapting them to the critical clinical conditions of the patients, despite the lack of specific literature indications.

There is no widely accepted, ICU-specific HLH protocol, and clinical practice remains heterogeneous between centres. Traditional cytotoxic/immunosuppressive regimens derived from paediatric protocols (HLH-94/HLH-2004; etoposide + dexamethasone ± ciclosporin) retain a central role for many clinicians [10], especially when a rapidly progressive, severe hyperinflammatory phenotype is present. However, more recent ICU-relevant strategies favour earlier use of targeted immunomodulation (for example, IL-1 blockade [11], JAK inhibition [12], or IFN-γ neutralisation [13]) either alone or combined with de-escalated cytotoxic therapy, particularly in secondary/mas-type presentations where limiting infection risk and drug toxicity is critical [2]. Randomised evidence in critically ill adults is scarse and until more definitive data will be available, therapeutic decisions in the ICU must balance the urgency of controlling hyperinflammation against risks of cytopenia, infection and organ-specific toxicities. Prospective ICU-focused trials, pragmatic biomarker-guided algorithms and adaptive platform designs are therefore priorities to resolve these uncertainties [14].

Mounting evidence indicates that early involvement of a multidisciplinary team with expertise in the recognition and management of HLH/MAS can significantly impact patient outcomes [14]. Such teams not only facilitate prompt identification of the syndrome but also enable more judicious use of immunosuppressive therapies and the timely initiation of protocol-based treatment regimens (e.g., HLH-94, HLH-2004). Although the optimal structure of these teams has yet to be firmly established, they frequently include specialists from hematology/oncology, infectious diseases, rheumatology, immunology, and clinical pharmacy, among others, according to local resources and case complexity. Hematology consultation is of particular importance to guide bone marrow biopsy interpretation, initiate appropriate genetic testing, and tailor therapy in alignment with established protocols. In addition to streamlining communication and standardizing clinical decision-making, these collaborative groups are well-positioned to incorporate emerging evidence into practice, lead quality improvement initiatives, and participate in multicenter research aimed at refining diagnostic criteria and therapeutic strategies for HLH/MAS.

In our experience, the direct participation of hematology specialists within the ICU care team has proven pivotal in confirming the clinical suspicion of HLH, ensuring that bone marrow biopsy was promptly performed in all feasible cases based on the patient’s clinical condition, and facilitating early assessment of key laboratory parameters and emerging biomarkers. Moreover, their expertise enabled timely consideration of targeted therapeutic strategies in accordance with established HLH treatment protocols.

Key literature gaps in HLH management in ICU include the lack of harmonised diagnostic criteria across specialties, limited epidemiological data from resource-constrained settings, insufficient prospective ICU-specific registries, and the absence of high-quality trials addressing biomarker-guided therapy, rapid genetic testing, and the role of multidisciplinary response teams; addressing these priorities is essential to improve timely recognition and outcomes in critically ill patients. Two recent studies from a french restrospective cohort [9] and a german pooled analysis [8] highlighted the lack of pathophysiology definition and targeted therapeutic strategies tailored for critically ill patients.

Conclusion

In the ICU setting, HLH is a rare but life-threatening condition characterised by hyperinflammation and immune dysregulation, often presenting with clinical features indistinguishable from septic shock. The severity and rapid progression of HLH warrant prompt clinical suspicion, particularly in cases of refractory distributive shock without an identified etiology. Diagnostic scores, such as the HScore, can provide a high probability of HLH diagnosis even in the absence of bone marrow biopsy, thereby supporting earlier initiation of appropriate treatment. Although no ICU-specific therapeutic protocols currently exist, our findings emphasise the value of early multidisciplinary involvement—including hematology consultation—to facilitate timely diagnosis, optimise biomarker evaluation, and consider targeted therapy. Integrating these insights into future ICU protocols may enhance the standardisation of diagnostic pathways, refine risk stratification, and support earlier intervention strategies, ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes in this complex and rapidly evolving clinical scenario.
