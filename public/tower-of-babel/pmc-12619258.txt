From complexity to resilience: clean innovation reshapes the load capacity curve dynamics
Authors: Elma Satrovic, Ummara Razi, Magdalena Radulescu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the environmental implications of economic complexity and clean technology innovations have been individually addressed across various empirical contexts, their joint dynamics in fostering ecological resilience, specifically with the advanced economies, remain analytically unsettled. In this context, by farming the analysis within the Load Capacity Curve (LCC) hypothesis, the study assessed the direct impacts of economic prosperousness, economic complexity, clean technology innovation, financial advancement, and dirty, and clean energy on the ecological resilience. Notably, the objective of the study is to analyze the moderating effect of clean technology innovations in the economic complexity-ecological resilience relationship, considering the Group of Seven (G7) economies over the 1995–2020 period. The findings of the Method of Moments Quantile Regression and Fully Modified Ordinary Least Squares validated the LCC hypothesis. While economic complexity and reliance on dirty energy are associated with ecological degradation, clean energy, financial advancement, and clean innovation show resilience-enhancing effects. Importantly, the positive coefficient of the clean innovation-economic complexity interaction term elucidates that the innovation pattern facilitates a shift toward eco-sustainable economic sophistication. Hence, G7 economies are advised to encourage investments in sophisticated clean technologies like resource-efficient manufacturing processes to counteract the ecological aftermath of complex production.Keywords: Ecological resilience, Energy composition, Economic complexity, Clean innovation, Load capacity curve, Group of Seven

Introduction

Environmental sustainability across high-emission sectors, most notably manufacturing, transportation, and energy, has emerged as a critical concern, given their direct contribution to ecological degradation [56]. The substantial share of these sectors in emissions is directly tied to the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal 13 (Climate Action). Recent climate negotiations, particularly United Nations Climate Change Conferences COP28 and COP29, emphasized the urgency of decarbonizing economic systems through renewable energy adoption, green financing mechanisms, and technological innovation, positioning them centrality to broader sustainability pathways [13]. The change to resilient economic systems is becoming one of the most compelling motions in global environmental and economic policy debates. Based on the urgency to address climate change acceleration, especially after COP29, countries worldwide are focusing on harmonizing economic prosperity with ecological sustainability [31]. The Group of Seven (G7), the world’s most advanced economies, plays a significant role in this transition, as they are responsible for the economic and environmental footprint. According to the World Bank (2023), nearly 30% of global GDP originates from G7 countries, while these countries account for approximately 25% of energy-based pollutants (IEA, 2023). However, these nations have the financial muscle to opt for modern technological solutions, even though their resilience, measured through the load capacity factor (BCEF), has shown significant divergence, which reflects the complex connection between economic complexity, economic prosperity, and innovation in clean technologies [64].

Although clean technology innovation and economic complexity have garnered prominent scholarly attention as potential drivers of ecological resilience, their interdependent influence on ecological resilience remains conceptually underdeveloped and empirically underinvestigated within the broader sustainability discourse. Previous studies have either focused on the independent effects of economic complexity on ecological or on how clean technology innovation has helped alleviate environmental degradation [6]. Nonetheless, the relationship between these two variables has yet to be thoroughly examined. This gap is particularly critical based on the theoretical premise that economic complexity is expected to coincide with economic advancement, but may exacerbate environmental degradation if not paired with advancements in clean technology.

The growing issue of ecological resilience within advanced economies has led to an outpouring of studies examining the relationship between economic pursuits and ecological sustainability. Economic prosperity has a dualistic effect on ecological resilience, as suggested by the load capacity curve (LCC) hypothesis, through a U-shaped relationship. This hypothesis suggests that during early economic growth, industrialization increases the rate of resource exploitation, thereby magnifying ecological degradation and suppressing the BCEF below unity, a critical threshold indicating an ecological deficit [21]. However, as economies develop, the evolution towards knowledge- and capital-based industries promotes cleaner production methods, which reduce the environmental footprint and improve biocapacity [67]. However, unlike EKC, the LLC explicitly incorporates ecological limitations, warning that if economic activities exceed the ecosystem’s carrying capacity, degradation may become irreversible.

Economic complexity, or a country’s ability to manufacture and export advanced, knowledge-based goods, is another key driver of ecological resilience. High economic complexity can either facilitate or mitigate ecological pressure. On the one hand, it promotes structural change towards high-tech industries and advanced manufacturing, which can minimize resource intensity and maximize ecological efficiency [47]. On the other hand, it facilitates high energy usage levels and increased natural resource dependency as part of complex good production, leading to higher ecological deterioration [24]. Clean technology innovation is a key mediator that can mitigate the negative ecological impacts of economic complexity. By fostering advancement in energy efficiency, waste minimization, and pollution abatement, clean technology innovation can potentially disengage economic growth from environmental deterioration [53, 64]. G7 countries, collectively accounting for over 40% of environmental technology patents, have emerged as world leaders in clean technology development.

The study’s novelty lies in its examination of how clean technological innovation interacts with economic complexity to influence ecological resilience within the framework of the load capacity curve hypothesis (LCC). It addresses the three central research questions: (i) whether economic prosperity follows a U-shaped relationship with ecological resilience, (ii) whether economic complexity alone undermines or enhances resilience, and (iii) whether clean technology innovation moderates this nexus to transform complexity into a driver of sustainability. Thus, the contribution of this study is multifold. First, it extends the LCC framework by systematically incorporating clean technology innovation as a moderating factor and extends the theoretical boundaries. Second, unlike prior studies that mainly applied ARDL, FMOLS, DOLS, or VAR frameworks in single or limited multi-country contexts, this study employs both the Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MMQR) approach and FMOLS for robustness, thereby offering stronger inference on long-run dynamics and heterogeneity. Third, it fills an important gap in the literature by analyzing the joint impact of economic complexity and clean technology innovation on ecological resilience, which remains underexplored despite its great policy relevance in the framework of COP29’s ambitions. This contribution is particularly distinctive from the previous studies because the moderating role of clean technology innovation in the complexity-resilience nexus has not been systematically evaluated in the context of advanced economies. Moreover, by using the balanced panel of G7 economies and incorporating two alternative indicators of ecological resilience, this study provides distribution-sensitive evidence that is more comprehensive than analyses relying on aggregate or developing economy datasets.

Following the introduction, the second section summarizes relevant literature and identifies research gaps. Section three describes data sources, the construction of variables, and the econometric framework, highlighting the use of the method of moment-quantile regression (MMQR) to address distribution dynamics. The fourth section presents empirical results and their implications regarding the direct and moderating effects of clean technology innovation on the economic complexity-ecological resilience relationship. The last section discusses policy implications, concludes the study, and suggests avenues for future research.

Literature review

Economic complexity and ecological resilience

Economic complexity, measured as the knowledge-intensive and diverse character of a nation’s productive structure, holds a contradictory relationship with environmental resilience. Economic complexity is a driving force towards sustainability because it enables the substitution of old, resource-extraction and industry-dependent models for newer, knowledge-intensive industries with lower resource consumption and a green orientation [11]. This view aligns with the theory of ecological modernization, which posits that technological innovation endemic in advanced economies can enhance production efficiency, minimize waste, and mitigate environmental degradation. For instance, Ahmed et al. [3] evaluated India from 1970 to 2017 within the STIRPAT (Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence and Technology) framework using the ARDL (Autoregressive Distributed Lag) approach and found that economic complexity directly reduces the ecological footprint and mitigates the adverse environmental consequences of economic growth. In different settings, Lyubimov et al. [33] applied the network-based approach and demonstrated that higher productive sophistication enhances export diversification capacity, which reinforces the structural potential for green development in Russia. Similarly, Wang and Yang [61], employed a finite mixture model (FMM) and showed that green complexity reduces carbon emission within countries having high institutional quality. Drawing on data from 2003–2020, Bashir et al. [12] found that rising economic complexity and greenfield investments play a crucial role in advancing energy innovation in newly industrialised economies. Through panel quantile and CS-ARDL (Cross-Sectional Autoregressive Distributed Lag) analysis, the study highlights pathways toward cleaner and sustainable ecological development.

Ahmad and Satrovic [1] further validate this positive link between G7 economies by employing MMQR within a STIRPAT framework. Hassan et al. [24] used the Dynamic Autoregressive Distributive Lag (DARDL) and Kernel-based Regularized Least Squares (KRLS) and found that the United States exemplifies how economic complexity can catalyze technological innovation that aligns with circular economy principles. Particularly, its capacity to produce and export sophisticated, knowledge-intensive goods has led to advancement in clean technologies, such as waste-to-energy systems and energy-efficient manufacturing. These developments highlight strategies for decoupling economic activity from environmental degradation, contributing to greater ecological resilience through reduced resource extraction, enhanced recycling, and minimized emissions. This aligns with the study by Li et al. [32], who investigated BRICST economies by using CS-ARDL, AMG (Augmented Mean Group), and CCEMG (Common Correlated Effects Mean Group) and found that complexity contributes to reducing environmental degradation by promoting knowledge-intensive products and diversification. Sun et al. [57] investigate how economic complexity influences green innovation in China, using annual data from 1995 to 2020. Employing wavelet coherence and the Bootstrap ARDL method, it finds that economic complexity positively drives green innovation in the long term, reinforcing China’s environmental sustainability agenda.

However, this positive narrative does not apply in all cases. The structural change that accompanies economic complexity also increases ecological pressure, particularly when the production of complex goods relies on energy-intensive processes using dirty energy sources. The empirical evidence, for instance, Neagu [35] examined 48 complex economies from 1995 to 2014. The author applied the fully modified ordinary square (FMOLS) and dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS) model and explored the idea that economic complexity enhances the ecological footprint of production, suggesting that more sophisticated economies may exert greater environmental stress due to energy-intensive outputs and carbon-intensive supply chains. Rafique et al. [44] also used FMOLS and DOLS models and reached similar conclusions in a study of the ten most complex economies from 1980 to 2017, demonstrating that economic complexity significantly enhances ecological pressure driven by embedded resources from carbon externalities. By adopting the method of moments quantile regression (MMQR) approach, Ahmad and Satrovic [2] examined OECD countries and explored that economic complexity reduces energy efficiency in environmentally weak states. However, fiscal decentralization can moderate this effect by enhancing policy responsiveness. Rafei et al. [43] reveal through the panel data vector autoregressive (PVAR) model that the shift towards sophisticated manufacturing and high-tech production often demands extensive natural resources and generates substantial emissions, exacerbating ecological degradation.

Clean technology innovation: a pathway to green complexity

Clean technology innovation (CTI) becomes the determining factor that brings about an alternative transformation of the harmful ecological consequences of economic complexity into resilience-based consequences. Empirical evidence confirms that CTI leads to efficient resource use, reduced pollution intensity, and a shift in economic complexity toward green complexity by increasing sustainable manufacturing and preventing waste generation [6, 64]. Implementing sophisticated technologies, such as carbon capture equipment, waste recycling plants, and renewable energy facilities, reduces emissions and enables the manufacture of complex, eco-friendly products. For instance, Aliani et al. [6], using the CS-ARDL and AMG estimation, suggested that CTI, operationalized through environmental technologies and renewables, significantly mitigates emissions, aligning with the objective of green complexity by enhancing efficiency and low-carbon capabilities. Building on similar empirical settings, Xing et al. [64] demonstrated that green innovations are tightly interwoven with the prospects of the G7’s green growth and climate mitigation, as well as energy security challenges. They applied the FMOLS and DOLS approaches and found that CTI bolsters the capacity for clean energy deployment and facilitates the shift toward net zero by reducing CO2 emissions.

Apergis et al. [8] evaluated the environmental impact of clean and brown energy consumption, energy technology investment, and ecological resilience in the US between 1980 and 2015. By applying the novel Fourier estimation, they emphasized the importance of policy support in scaling up CTI. From the review of studies it has observed that the environmental advantages of CTI are not evenly spread among nations since some countries, despite having high technological ability, still depend on fossil fuels and, therefore, cancel out any possible environmental benefits [10] Using spatial Durbin and threshold regression models, Qingran et al. [42] reveal that higher complexity and digital development significantly enhance manufacturing upgrading, aligning with goals of economic complexity and sustainable industrial growth. Additionally, the synergic impact of CTI and economic complexity is apparent in the empirical results of the current research, such as Cai et al. (2025) employing the Pooled Mean Group Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL) model shows that the moderating function of CTI transforms the ecological costs of complexity into resilience-bolstering results. Specifically, the interaction term between CTI and economic complexity is statistically significant and positive, implying that environmental innovations allow G7 countries to manufacture complex products more ecologically sustainably. Research gap and contribution

Despite a vast literature on the individual effects of economic complexity and clean technology innovation on environmental resilience, few studies have explored the combined effects within an integrated framework, particularly for advanced economies such as the G7. Although earlier studies have identified the capability of clean technology innovations to reduce the environmental costs associated with economic complexity [6, 32], the extent to which these innovations can transform complexity-led ecological destruction into green complexity is under investigation. In addition, previous research primarily focuses on developing economies or aggregated global datasets, ignoring the distinctive economic systems and technological capacities of G7 nations. Furthermore, as shown in Table 7, the empirical technique used in prior studies varies widely, ranging from ARDL and VAR to spatial and panel threshold models. However, few have incorporated quantile-based estimators capable of unveiling heterogeneity in ecological outcomes across the distribution. The current study bridges this gap by exploring the moderating effect of clean technology innovations in the complexity-resilience relationship using a Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MMQR) approach to capture heterogeneity across quantiles and uncover distributional dynamics in ecological resilience results.

Methodology

Data construction and sample selection

The present study examines the relationship between economic complexity and ecological resilience for G7 countries, including Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States, spanning the period from 1995 to 2020. Moreover, the direct impact of economic prosperity, clean technology innovation, financial advancement, and both dirty and clean energy on ecological resilience is also analyzed under the load capacity curve theoretical framework. The time scope of the study was determined based on the availability of data. For instance, SEC data were first reported in 1995, while data on clean technology innovation were available until 2020. Theoretical foundation and modelling

The aim of this study is to assess the ecological implications of economic complexity within the G7 economies by employing the LCC framework to capture the complex relationship between economic expansion and environmental resilience. LCC postulates a U-shaped association between economic growth and BCEF (load capacity factor). LLC is the extension of EKC, which assesses the environmental impact from the demand side, as it mostly opts for the anthropogenic emissions and ecological footprint to measure ED, subsequently disregarding the supply side. To overcome this obstacle, the present study employs the load capacity factor, which considers not only the demand for natural capital (ecological footprint) but also the supply (biocapacity) to accurately measure ecological resilience (ERL). In recent decades, the global increase in capital mobility has accelerated structural economic shifts, making the ecological effects of economic complexity a pressing topic. The economic complexity-ecological resilience nexus is twofold. On the one hand, greater complexity can enhance environmental quality by fostering cleaner technologies [57], promoting green production, and utilizing efficient resources through innovations and specializations [11]. On the other hand, increased complexity intensifies environmental stress if it relies on resources and energy-intensive processes, specifically when driven by fossil fuel energy and material-intensive outputs [2, 24]. Thus, economic complexity may either enhance or undermine ecological resilience depending on the structure of production and energy consumption, signifying the context-specific effect ( or ).

Clean technology innovation plays a crucial role in the transition towards cleaner production processes, as it supports the utilization of power generated from clean sources. CTI enables countries to adopt advanced equipment and technological solutions that enhance productivity while reducing energy intensity, contributing to ecological resilience [6, 64]. By promoting energy diversification and fostering low-carbon, knowledge-intensive sectors, CTI helps decouple economic growth from environmental degradation [53]. The advantageous ecological impact of CTI is fairly explained by the proposition of ecological modernization theory, which suggests that technology represents an acceptable route for nations to achieve economic prosperity while mitigating ecological pressure. From the preceding narrative, and taking into account that our sample incorporates economically powerful countries, it is hypothesized that clean technology innovations will encourage the ecological resilience agenda of the G7 ().

Energy is an intermediate input in production and a critical driver of economic growth. Yet its consumption, specifically from fossil fuel sources, has significant environmental costs. Dirty energy sources, such as coal, oil, and natural gas, release high levels of greenhouse gases and pollutants, contributing to deforestation, climate change, and ecological degradation. Despite the ongoing transition to clean energy, G7 countries remain heavily dependent on fossil fuels [9]. It is worth mentioning that the G7 produces more than a quarter of the fossil fuels, with the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada alone responsible for around 50% of the pollution associated with new oil and gas drilling projects to be realized before 2050 (IISD, 2024). While economically appealing, the continuous extraction of dirty energy releases harmful substances that cause air, water, and soil contamination, significantly threatening ecological resilience [50, 66]. Accordingly, this study anticipates an adverse ecological impact from dirty energy ().

Tackling energy intensity and accelerating the adoption of clean energy form the critical tools to transition away from dirty energy sources. The energy system’s decarbonization is vital to achieve pollutant emission targets. Adapting clean energy sources can help in phasing out substantial amounts of fossil fuels, as 65% of power could be generated from clean sources within the next 25 years [29]. As such, clean energy sources have great potential to encourage net-zero economies and sustainable growth trajectories [5, 59].This is justifiable because clean energy comes from renewable sources that generate substantially lower pollution levels than dirty sources. Additionally, to generate electricity through clean sources, countries do not rely on non-renewable resources that cannot be replenished once depleted [52]. Clean energy sources also support energy security and are thus expected to maintain the ecological resilience of G7 nations (i.e. ).

The ecological influence of financial advancement has been met with conflicting thoughts as it may instead foster or impair ecological resilience (i.e. or ). As the first option, financial advancement may encourage the acceleration of high-polluting industrial processes, as cheap loans are readily available and there are ample investment options. This can augment the use of dirty energy sources and intensify the inefficient use of production equipment, contributing to ecological vulnerability [14]. Financial advancement can have dual ecological effects; for instance, weak environmental oversight may lead financial institutions to prioritize profit over funding for polluting industries, thereby worsening ecological degradation [51]. While aligned with green objectives, financial development can support clean technologies, promote research and development, and attract environmentally friendly investments. Affordable financing enables consumers and firms to adopt low-emission products, enhancing productivity while reducing environmental deterioration [7, 17, 18].

This study also scrutinizes the moderating role of clean technology (cleantech) innovation in the ecological resilience-economic complexity nexus. The assessment of the joint CTI-SEC ecological impact is of great importance, as the improvement of CTI encourages G7 countries to adopt cleaner production techniques, allowing these countries to introduce more complex items without adverse ecological impacts. By promoting recycling, clean energy use, and efficient waste management, CTI mitigates the ecological costs of complex production [45]. It also facilitates a structural shift toward green complexity by supporting sustainable manufacturing, skill development, and efficient resource use [60, 61]. In this vein, CTI facilitates the stepwise replacement of exhaustible natural assets with renewable ones in producing highly complex goods, thereby preserving nature [32]. Given these arguments, innovative activities are facilitating the production of high green complexity products and are expected to mitigate the adverse ecological consequences of SEC (). The main component of the econometric strategy is the utilization of the Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MMQR) by Machado and Silva [34] to estimate models without moderation (Eq. 2) and models with moderation (Eq. 3). This econometric strategy is preferred in the present study as it considers dynamics in distributional characteristics of ecological resilience against its drivers [54]. Accordingly, MMQR permits the analysis of the relationship between ecological resilience and its stipulated determinants over heterogeneous quantiles of ERL. In other words, MMQR enables the assessment of tenuous linkages amid conditional means under the umbrella of LCC hypothesis. This is of vital importance to design robust policy insights that will address the unique ecological patterns of the selected G7 economies as the aftermaths of economic prosperousness, economic complexity, clean technology innovation, financial advancement, dirty, and clean energy may appreciably differ across inspected economies with low, middle, and upper levels of ERL. It also involves conditional means and permits individual effects to affect the total conditional distribution [20]. In addition, MMQR provides accurate outcomes in the case of outliers, CSSS, and HGSR issues [30]. Unlike conventional techniques, the MMQR provides reliable findings even when there is no linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables and when endogeneity issues are present [55]. Unlike the negative ecological impact of LPP, its squared term is positively associated with ecological resilience, with elasticities that are statistically significant across the LQ, middle quantiles (MQ), and UQ. These findings imply that accelerated economic expansion impairs the load capacity factor in the early stages of industrialization. It can be justified that intensified production demands more natural resources, such that their extraction increases the ecological footprint but reduces biocapacity, subsequently hindering the load capacity factor. However, later stages of growth are characterized by a shift from primary and secondary towards tertiary economic activities, which encourage resource conservation, energy efficiency, and pollutant mitigation in favor of ecological resilience in G7. The adverse ecological impact of PP in the early stages and its beneficial impact in the later stages may arise from the absence of strict environmental regulations, as countries in the early industrial stages prioritize economic over ecological targets [48]. However, substantial PP encourages the advancement of clean innovations dedicated to boosting production site productivity, reducing energy intensity, foster growth in BCEF, and supporting ecological resilience. The existence of a U-shaped nexus between PP and BCEF is legitimized by Fang et al. [21] and Dai et al. [19]. Specifically, Fang et al. [21] authenticated the load capacity curve in the case of Thailand. To coincide with these findings, Dai et al. [19] hinged on the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, exemplifying that advanced nations are more environmentally aware and tend to instigate ecological quality by introducing advanced production technologies and strict environmental legislation.

Next, the impact of economic complexity on load capacity factors varies across heterogeneous quantiles of the dependent variable, such that the elasticities of SEC are negative but statistically significant only between the 30th and 70th quantiles in Model 1. However, the estimation of Model 2 gauges that economic complexity impedes the ecological resilience of G7 countries, with a significant impact across MQ and UQ. The model specifications with moderators (models 3 and 4) yield a negative coefficient for SEC; however, in all model specifications, this influence becomes weaker as the level of ecological resilience (LQ to UQ) increases. Indeed, a higher SEC means that a nation is prosperous and thus exports sophisticated items. However, product sophistication and the variety of export mixes may demand more energy from dirty rather than clean resources [65]. In the former case, the SEC is likely to generate more pollution and abate the ecological resilience of the G7 countries. Specifically, to satisfy such energy demand, more natural resources (oil, coal, natural gas) will be exploited, potentially hindering the ecological resilience of the considered countries. In justification of our outcomes, Hassan et al. [24], Li et al. [32], and Rafei et al. [43] confirmed that economic complexity can elevate ecological pressure, specifically in fossil fuel-reliant or early-growth economies. For instance, Hassan et al. [24] posited that the promotion of economic complexity jeopardizes the environmental quality of the United States. Notwithstanding these findings, Li et al. [32] authenticated the adverse ecological impact of economic complexity in emerging countries. Moreover, Rafei et al. [43] unveiled a positive association between economic complexity and ecological footprint in the 1995–2017 time span.

Clean technology innovation encourages nations to introduce advanced technological equipment into production and acquire sophisticated technical prospects to support ecological resilience. Subsequently, the coefficient of CTI is positive and statistically significant across quantiles 0.1–0.9 in Model 1. However, the outcomes of Model 2 revealed that clean technology innovation significantly boosts the load capacity factor of G7 countries only across quantiles 0.3–0.8. The advantageous ecological impact of CTI becomes stronger as G7 countries advance in load capacity factors. For countries with an ecological deficit or reserve, progress in the CTI is underway to achieve the ecological resilience objective through energy efficiency improvements [45]. The favorable ecological impact of CTI is quite expected, as it can support structural changes from low- to high-complexity economic sectors, enabling countries to adopt cleaner technologies and phase out dirty energy sources. As such, clean technology innovations encourage the development of cleaner production sites that utilize natural capital more efficiently and help nations enhance their ecological resilience trajectory. The findings are consistent with those of Aliani et al. [6], Ramzan et al. [46], and Xing et al. [64], who highlight that CTI aids in pollution control, natural resource efficiency, energy security, and green growth. Notably, Aliani et al. [6] outlined that investment in clean technology innovations advocates the G7’s green agenda. Correspondingly, Xing et al. [64] found that boosting green innovation is vital for the ecological resilience of G7 countries. More recently, Ramzan et al. [46] unveiled that the diffusion of green investments aligns with the sustainability targets of Spain.

Equivalently, Models 2 and 4 yield a decreasing trend such that dirty energy jeopardizes ecological resilience more vigorously in G7 economies with lower BCEF. It is reasonable since the economic prosperousness and structural change in G7 countries are disheveled with elevated consumption of natural resources and intensified use of energy generated from dirty sources. Our outcomes align with the findings of Fan et al. [20] and Roy [50] for emerging countries and India, respectively. For instance, Fan et al. [20] deciphered that extracting dirty energy sources involves capital-intensive activities, accompanied by substantial emissions of greenhouse gases. In the same fashion, Roy [50] ruled out the adverse ecological impact of dirty energy sources attributable to soil erosion associated with fossil fuel exploitation.

On the other hand, clean energy is positively associated with ecological resilience in all model specifications. The present study utilizes two twin indicators of clean energy to evaluate the ecological impact of total energy derived from clean sources (CES)—Models 1 and 3—and the electricity generated from clean sources (CEG)—Models 2 and 4. Our empirical outcomes, detailed in Table 6 and Fig. 3, elucidate that both variables are significant across LQ, MQ, and UQ and have a positive relationship with the load capacity factor. The consistency of the signs of the elasticities across all models and quantiles of ecological resilience. Mainly, the elasticities of clean energy exhibit a decreasing trend from LQ to UQ. The positive coefficient of clean energy reflects its low emissions, renewability, and cost efficiency, supporting the transition away from dirty sources. This aligns with the findings of Akpanke et al. [5], Liu et al. (2024), and Udemba et al. [59], who confirm their role in enhancing ecological resilience and mitigating environmental degradation. Similarly, Rasheed et al. [49] elucidate that renewable energy conveys beneficial ecological implications by reducing the generation of greenhouse gas emissions in Asian economies. In the same fashion, Ahmed et al. [4] unveiled that renewable energy needs to be prioritized over fossil fuels to accelerate the clean energy shift.

Likewise, financial advancement enhances the ecological resilience of G7 countries, as there is a positive association between financial advancement and biodiversity conservation efforts in all models. The magnitude of coefficients decreases from lower to higher quantiles, divulging that financial advancement accelerates BCEF, with a more magnified impact in G7 nations with low ecological resilience. Our findings suggest that financial advancement serves as a measure of the ecological resilience of G7 countries, as it motivates enterprises and households to adopt cleaner production and consumption practices through the lens of more affordable loans. Another possible justification is that financial advancement may be a driving force of clean technology innovation as it mobilizes funds to be invested in research and development projects [52]. The outcomes are consistent with those of Dada et al. [18], Ansari et al. [7], and Charfeddine et al. (2024), who confirm that FA supports cleaner production and knowledge-based activities, while enabling clean energy funding in developing economies, thus improving BCEF, reducing emissions, and fostering environmental sustainability.

Clean technology innovation may set the stage for a green economic complexity and reverse the SEC’s harmful ecological resilience impacts into advantageous ones. Models 3 and 4 examine the moderating impact of clean technology innovation on the BCEF-SEC nexus. Table 6 and Fig. 3 demonstrate the positive elasticities of moderators in the inverse direction of the baseline impacts of SEC. By considering the joint impact of CTI-SEC, the response of ecological resilience to economic complexity is conditional on clean technology innovation. The empirical outcomes revealed a positive moderator coefficient across quantiles of BCEF, which was statistically significant across LQ, MQ, and UQ in Model 3 and across MQ in Model 4. Despite the adverse ecological impact of economic complexity, the upgrade in clean technology innovation is efficient in switching this impact into a beneficial one. These outcomes can be attributed to the fact that CTI motivates the inspected countries to leverage innovative technologies and enhance their economic complexity, while ensuring compliance with their production processes and net-zero objectives. Another possible justification of our findings is that incentivizing green innovation can phase out the negative externalities of continued complexity upgrade through the lens of energy savings, recycling, and clean energy to be used in the production process of more complex items. CTI may also motivate G7 countries to develop infrastructure and support the adoption of green technology so that the overconsumption of natural resources will be reduced, as well as the eco-detrimental impacts. Ul-Durar et al. [60] endorsed these findings, emphasizing that clean technology innovations can facilitate green structural transformation and ongoing complexity progress as CTI enhances the capacity to produce more complex items in an eco-friendly way.

Conclusion and policy implications

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the intricate relationships between economic complexity, economic prosperity, and ecological resilience in G7 nations, with clean technology innovation being a key moderating variable. The empirical findings confirm the presence of a U-shaped relationship between economic prosperousness and ecological resilience, consistent with the Load Capacity Curve (LCC) hypothesis. Baseline results indicate that economic complexity negatively affects ecological resilience because producing high-level goods tends to result in greater resource extraction and consumption, which is responsible for high GHG emissions. However, integrating clean technology innovation has a significant moderating impact. The economic complexity-clean technology innovation interaction term indicates that clean technology can convert economic complexity from an environmental burden into a resilience-building asset. Such transformation becomes more vivid when observed within higher quantiles of BCEF, as more evolved economies utilize technical progress to mitigate the environmental repercussions of complexity through cleaner production and green structural transformations. In addition, the study’s results confirm that renewable energy adoption and financial advancement are crucial complementary drivers of resilience, while reliance on dirty energy undermines ecological sustainability. The consistency of these findings across MMQR quantiles and robustness checks using FMOLS highlights the reliability of the evidence.

Policy implications

The empirical data of this research highlight the need for G7 nations to adopt targeted, actionable policy interventions that align economic complexity with ecological resilience, using clean technology innovation as a strategic moderator. The following policy interventions are recommended: For instance, the governments must establish performance-based grants and subsidies for targeted sectors with high economic complexity but low ecological efficiency. This strategy encourages companies to invest in sophisticated clean technologies, such as carbon capture systems, waste-to-energy plants, and resource-efficient manufacturing processes, thereby neutralizing the ecological impact of complex production. They can even give high priority to funding for industries with significant export potential, such as automobiles and electronics, where adopting green technology reduces the environmental impact of high-value-added products.

Additionally, tax credits can be imposed on companies that demonstrate authenticated reductions in resource use and pollutant discharge in complex manufacturing activities. Apart from this, they must design a tiered system of tax incentives that provides more generous tax deductions to companies that bring in new patents on clean technology, thus linking economic complexity with ecological sustainability. Moreover, creating a “Green Complexity Certification” initiative that measures and marks complex products based on their environmental impact incentivizes G7 exporters to use cleaner production processes and makes sustainable complex products more competitive globally. Partnerships with global trade organizations to export certified green complex products to international markets, incorporating ecological sustainability as a primary factor in trade agreements.

For the public–private partnership, they should establish innovation clusters that bring together government agencies, research institutions, and private firms to co-develop and deploy clean technology solutions, targeting industries where economic complexity has led to ecological degradation. Additionally, they should encourage large companies in complex industries to collaborate with start-ups in digital and AI-based clean technologies, providing co-funding and technical assistance to drive the diffusion of green technology.

Limitations and future research directions

While this study provides novel insights into the role of economic complexity and clean technology innovation in shaping ecological resilience across G7 economies, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is confined to the 1995 to 2020 period due to data availability, which may not fully capture more recent policy shifts and technological breakthroughs in green innovation. Second, although real GDP per capita is a widely used proxy for economic prosperousness, it does not account for ecological considerations. Third, the study focuses on G7 economies, which, while relevant as advanced economies and global clean technology leaders, limit the generalizability of the findings to emerging or resource-dependent economies with different structural and institutional conditions.

Future research may extend this analysis by incorporating post-2020 data to account for the accelerating policy and technological responses following COP 29. In addition, exploring firm-level or sector-specific datasets could uncover micro-level mechanisms through which economic complexity and innovation interact to enhance ecological resilience. Comparative studies across advanced and developing economies would also provide a deeper understanding of context-specific dynamics. Finally, employing a novel indicator of economic prosperousness that aligns with ecological resilience could further strengthen policy relevance.
