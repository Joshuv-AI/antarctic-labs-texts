Effects of topically applied liquid N-acetylcysteine for the management of burning mouth syndrome
Authors: Ji Won Kim, Ho-Ryun Won, Choung-Soo Kim, Jeong-Seok Choi, Seung Hoon Woo, Dong Won Lee, Minsu Kwon, Young-Hoon Joo, Bon Seok Koo, Kwang-Jae Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is a chronic neuropathic pain condition with limited treatment options. N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a thiol-based antioxidant with neuroprotective properties, has not been clinically evaluated as a topical agent for BMS. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of topically applied liquid NAC in reducing symptoms and improving quality of life of patients with BMS. In a multicenter, prospective, nonrandomized, open-label study, 114 patients with BMS were allocated to receive liquid NAC oral rinse, oral clonazepam, or combination therapy for eight weeks. Symptoms were assessed at baseline, week 4, and week 8 using the visual analog scale (VAS) and the Korean version of the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14 K). Across all three groups, VAS scores declined significantly within groups from baseline to week 4 and to week 8; however, the magnitude of VAS change did not differ between groups at either time point. For OHIP‑14 K, significant within‑group decreases were observed in the liquid NAC and combination groups from baseline to weeks 4 and 8, whereas the clonazepam group showed a significant decrease only from week 4 to week 8 and not from baseline. At week 4, the combination group achieved a larger OHIP‑14 K reduction than either monotherapy; by week 8, between‑group differences were no longer significant. Topically applied liquid NAC, alone or in combination with clonazepam, effectively reduced pain and enhanced patient-reported outcomes. These findings support the potential of a localized and safe strategy targeting neuropathic mechanisms in BMS. Topically administered liquid NAC demonstrates potential as an effective and well-tolerated therapeutic strategy for managing BMS, offering symptom relief with minimal systemic burden. Introduction

Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is a chronic, idiopathic pain disorder characterized by persistent intraoral burning or dysesthetic sensations lasting for more than two hours per day for at least three months, in the absence of clinically detectable mucosal abnormalities or identifiable pathological lesions1,2. BMS predominantly affects middle-aged and postmenopausal women, with a reported female-to-male ratio of 5:1 to 7:1. The estimated prevalence ranges from 0.7% to 15%, with an annual incidence of approximately five cases per 100,000 individuals1–4.

Pharmacological interventions commonly used for BMS include benzodiazepines, antioxidants such as alpha-lipoic acid (ALA), and certain antidepressants. Clonazepam, a benzodiazepine derivative, has been widely used because of its anxiolytic and analgesic properties. However, its efficacy is often limited by incomplete symptom relief and side effects, especially in older populations5–9.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a derivative of L-cysteine, is primarily known for its antioxidant properties, which act through direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species and enhancement of intracellular glutathione levels. It has shown potential in treating various neurodegenerative and neuropathic disorders10–15. In the context of BMS management, a recent study reported that NAC may contribute to symptom alleviation16.

Topically administered agents have also shown promise in the treatment of BMS, offering localized symptom control while minimizing systemic exposure. Among these, topical clonazepam and capsaicin have demonstrated clinical efficacy17,18. In addition to these established topical options, emerging evidence suggests that topical amitriptyline may provide clinically meaningful pain relief with acceptable tolerability in BMS, whereas topical doxepin has demonstrated analgesic efficacy in head-and-neck mucositis pain in randomized trials19,20. Nevertheless, evidence for alternative topical formulations beyond these established agents remains limited and heterogeneous, with few randomized trials to date and largely observational data. Among other potential topical strategies, the use of liquid NAC as an oral rinse represents a particularly promising approach. To date, however, its application to the oral mucosa has thus far been explored only in the context of improving xerostomia and salivary viscosity in patients undergoing treatment for head and neck cancer21. To date, no studies have evaluated the neuroprotective effects of a topically applied liquid NAC oral rinse in patients with BMS.

This study aimed to systematically assess the clinical utility of topically applied liquid NAC administered as an oral rinse in patients with BMS. We hypothesized that the topical delivery of liquid NAC via oral rinses may provide localized neuroprotective and mucosal-stabilizing effects, resulting in symptom relief and improved oral health-related quality of life.

Materials and methods

Study design

This multicenter prospective study was conducted at seven hospitals across the Republic of Korea between August 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions before patient enrollment.

All patients were initially evaluated and diagnosed with BMS according to the diagnostic criteria defined by the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-III)22. Although a more recent ICOP 2020 taxonomy exists—and a recent systematic review found no material clinical or epidemiologic differences versus ICHD-III—we based enrollment on ICHD-III to preserve continuity and comparability23,24.

Exclusion criteria were applied before enrollment and included the following: individuals under 18 years of age; history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or head and neck malignancy involving the oral cavity; secondary BMS, defined by abnormal findings on laboratory tests including complete blood count, serum biochemical panels, thyroid function tests, autoimmune markers, or nutritional deficiencies (e.g., serum vitamin B12, folate, or iron); clinical or microbiological evidence of oral candidiasis, including a positive fungal culture; mucosal abnormalities of the tongue suggestive of immune-mediated or inflammatory disorders (e.g., erythema multiforme, recurrent aphthous stomatitis, pemphigus); and regular use of anxiolytic medications before enrollment.

A total of 216 patients were initially enrolled after applying the exclusion criteria. All eligible participants were managed in the Department of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery at their respective institutions and attended routine outpatient follow-ups throughout the study period.

Patients were allocated to three groups according to the treatment modality. Group 1 received a topically applied liquid NAC oral rinse using a 10% acetylcysteine solution (Mucomyst Solution®, Boryung Pharmaceutical Co., Republic of Korea; excipients/carrier: edetate disodium hydrate, water for injection, and sodium hydroxide; 8 mL per dose, rinsed three times daily, with each rinse applied for 30–60 s followed by expectoration) for 8 weeks. Group 2 received oral clonazepam tablets (0.5 mg once or twice daily, PO) for 8 weeks. Group 3 received combination therapy consisting of liquid NAC oral rinse (8 mL three times daily, oral rinse) and oral clonazepam tablets (0.5 mg once or twice daily, PO), administered concurrently for 8 weeks.

All participants were instructed to complete a set of validated symptom assessment questionnaires at three time points: baseline (week 0), mid-treatment (week 4), and end of treatment (week 8). Of the initial cohort, 102 patients were excluded from the final analysis owing to incomplete questionnaire data or loss to follow-up, yielding a final analytical sample of 114 patients. Assessment tools for symptoms of patients with BMS

The severity of BMS was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) and the 14-item Korean version of the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14 K). Symptom intensity, including oral pain and burning sensation, was measured using the VAS, in which patients rated their symptoms on a 10-point scale (0 = no symptoms, 10 = worst imaginable symptom intensity). To evaluate oral health-related quality of life, a validated Korean translation of the OHIP-14 was administered25. The OHIP-14 K comprises 14 items distributed across seven conceptual domains, with two items per domain: functional limitation, physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap. For each item, the participants were asked to rate the frequency of experiencing specific problems on a five-point scale: 0 = never, 1 = hardly ever, 2 = occasionally, 3 = fairly often, and 4 = very often. The total OHIP-14 K score was calculated as the sum of all item scores, yielding a range of 0–56, with higher scores indicating poorer oral health-related quality of life.

Additionally, the patients were asked to complete supplementary assessments mid-treatment (week 4) and at the end of treatment (week 8) to evaluate their subjective experiences with the assigned treatments. These assessments included: (1) ease of use of the treatment, rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = very easy, 1 = easy, 2 = neutral, 3 = difficult, 4 = very difficult); and (2) satisfaction with symptom relief, also rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = very satisfied, 1 = satisfied, 2 = neutral, 3 = dissatisfied, 4 = very dissatisfied). Tolerability and safety were assessed concurrently: adverse events (AEs) were solicited at each visit and recorded; serious AEs were defined per ICH-GCP.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS ver. 30.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables, including sex, were compared using the Pearson χ² test. Continuous variables, such as VAS, OHIP-14 K, and supplementary assessments (ease of use of the treatment and satisfaction with symptom relief), were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s method. For within-subject comparisons over time, the multivariate approach to repeated measures or the Mann-Whitney U test was applied because Mauchly’s test indicated a violation of the sphericity assumption. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 114 patients were included in the final analysis and stratified into three groups according to the treatment modality. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced across the groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in age (p =.065), sex distribution (p =.492), baseline symptom severity as measured by VAS scores (p =.169), or oral health-related quality of life as assessed by the OHIP-14 K (p =.637), indicating that the groups were comparable at baseline (Table 1). These characteristics support the validity of the subsequent within-group and between-group comparisons.

Within-group changes in symptom Severity, quality of Life, and subjective treatment experience

All three groups demonstrated significant reductions in VAS scores from baseline to weeks 4 and 8 (p <.001 for all). Further improvements in VAS scores were also observed between weeks 4 and 8 in Groups 1 (p =.001), 2 (p =.009), and 3 (p =.006).

Regarding the OHIP-14 K scores, both Groups 1 and 3 exhibited significant reductions from baseline to week 4 (p =.002 and p =.001, respectively) and from baseline to week 8 (p =.002 and p =.012, respectively). In both groups, the OHIP-14 K scores continued to decline slightly between weeks 4 and 8, but these changes were not statistically significant (p =.113 in Group 1; p =.980 in Group 3). In contrast, Group 2 showed no significant improvement in the OHIP-14 K scores from baseline to week 4 (p =.988) or week 8 (p =.113). However, a significant reduction was observed between weeks 4 and 8 (p =.003), indicating a delayed improvement (Fig. 2).

In addition, ease of use and satisfaction with symptom relief declined slightly over time across all groups, but no statistically significant changes were observed between weeks 4 and 8 (all p >.05). Between-group comparison of symptom Severity, quality of Life, and subjective treatment experience

Between-group comparisons of VAS score reductions from baseline to either weeks 4 or 8 revealed no statistically significant differences among the three groups (p >.05 for all pairwise comparisons).

In contrast, the OHIP-14 K score changes from baseline differed significantly between the groups at week 4, with Group 3 showing a greater improvement compared to Groups 1 (p =.048) and 2 (p <.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between Groups 1 and 2 (p >.05). However, at week 8, no significant differences were observed between any of the groups (p >.05 for all pairwise comparisons) (Fig. 3).

In addition, no significant between-group differences were observed in ease of use or satisfaction with symptom relief at either weeks 4 or 8 (all p >.05). Across all groups, ease of use scores consistently fell within the “easy” to “neutral” range, while satisfaction scores were in the “satisfied” to “neutral” range (Online Resource 2).

Safety and tolerability

Throughout the 8-week treatment period, no serious AEs occurred in any group. Mild AEs were infrequent: in Group 1 (liquid NAC rinse), 1/43 (2.3%) reported transient, nonspecific dizziness; in Group 2 (oral clonazepam), 1/24 (4.2%) reported dry mouth; and in Group 3 (combination), 3/47 (4.3%) reported somnolence (n = 1) and an unpleasant gargle taste associated with reduced adherence (n = 2). No treatment discontinuations or additional clinical interventions were required.

Discussion

BMS is a chronic idiopathic orofacial pain disorder for which current therapeutic strategies offer inconsistent efficacy and are frequently limited by tolerability concerns26. Among the pharmacological modalities investigated to date, clonazepam has been the most widely utilized owing to its anxiolytic and central nervous system-modulating properties18. However, its clinical utility is limited by safety concerns linked to prolonged benzodiazepine exposure—spanning cognitive effects, falls and fractures, physiologic dependence, pneumonia, and respiratory complications—with particularly heightened vulnerability in older adults and those with sleep-disordered breathing, and with long-term use associated with increased all-cause mortality27–31. Similarly, in BMS, antioxidants such as alpha-lipoic acid; non-benzodiazepine pharmacotherapies (tricyclics/SNRIs and gabapentinoids); topical counter-irritants (capsaicin rinses); behavioral interventions; and device-based approaches such as low-level laser therapy have been proposed and investigated. However, these modalities have demonstrated variable benefit and, due to inconsistent outcomes and tolerability constraints, have not achieved a consensus recommendation26,32–38.

Recent evidence has highlighted the role of oxidative stress and neuronal dysfunction in the pathogenesis of peripheral neuropathy, providing a rationale for exploring agents with neuroprotective and antioxidant properties10. NAC, a thiol-containing compound and glutathione precursor, exerts cytoprotective effects by directly scavenging reactive oxygen species and upregulating endogenous antioxidant defenses. NAC has demonstrated potential in mitigating neuronal injury and improving symptom burden in various preclinical and clinical models of neurodegenerative and neuropathic conditions, including Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia, and peripheral neuropathy10–15. In BMS specifically, Han et al. demonstrated that systemic administration of NAC exerted therapeutic benefits, and that combination therapy with clonazepam produced superior outcomes in both pain reduction and quality of life compared to monotherapy16.

Systemic formulations have traditionally been preferred for BMS treatment; however, topical drug delivery offers distinct advantages by enabling localized symptom modulation, minimizing systemic side effects, and enhancing patient compliance. Evidence from a randomized, placebo-controlled trial indicates that topical clonazepam yields clinically meaningful short-term pain reduction on VAS compared with placebo, supporting local mucosal receptor–level modulation; nevertheless, safety reports of topical solutions note sedation and related central nervous system adverse reactions in a minority of patients, occasionally requiring dose adjustment or discontinuation5. Similarly, a double-blind, cross-over study of a 0.02% capsaicin mouth rinse demonstrated short-horizon analgesic benefit versus placebo, but its utility is constrained by local tolerability (transient burning/stinging, unpleasant taste) and a limited magnitude/duration of effect, with some withdrawals attributable to discomfort during use34. Beyond these established agents, topical amitriptyline has recently shown promising real-world effectiveness with acceptable tolerability in observational cohorts, yet rigorous randomized evaluations remain warranted to define effect size, optimal formulation, and durability19. By contrast, topical doxepin mouthwash has proven analgesic efficacy for radiation-induced oral mucositis pain in head-and-neck cancer, but—based on current evidence—has not been evaluated as a BMS-specific therapy and was therefore not used here as a direct comparator20.

Positioned against these topical agents, a ready-to-use liquid NAC oral rinse represents a mechanistically distinct and readily implementable option for BMS. By replenishing glutathione and attenuating redox- and MMP-driven peripheral sensitization—while potentially modulating glutamatergic signaling—NAC targets neuropathic and neuroinflammatory pathways that complement the GABAergic effects of topical clonazepam and the TRPV1 desensitization induced by capsaicin14. From an implementation standpoint, the standardized liquid formulation enables rapid local absorption with minimal systemic exposure and ensures uniform mucosal coverage, consistent dosing, and prolonged mucosal contact, features likely to reduce the formulation heterogeneity that has hampered prior topical studies and to enhance adherence21.

This multicenter prospective study is the first clinical investigation to assess the therapeutic efficacy of a topically applied liquid NAC oral rinse in patients with BMS using a standardized 8-week protocol at multiple tertiary care institutions. By incorporating three parallel treatment arms–liquid NAC oral rinse monotherapy, oral clonazepam monotherapy, and combination therapy–the study design enabled a systematic comparison of monotherapeutic and adjunctive treatment approaches. The use of validated patient-reported outcome measures, including VAS and OHIP-14 K, at predefined intervals facilitated a robust longitudinal evaluation of symptom progression and health-related quality of life.

The results of this study demonstrated that liquid NAC oral rinse monotherapy and in combination with clonazepam led to significant and sustained reductions in symptom severity over an 8-week period. Notably, patients receiving combination therapy (Group 3) exhibited the greatest improvement in oral health-related quality of life at week 4, as measured by the OHIP-14 K, compared to those in the liquid NAC monotherapy group (Group 1) and the clonazepam monotherapy group (Group 2). The magnitude of improvement was comparable across all groups by week 8. However, this early superiority remains clinically meaningful despite convergence by week 8. Relative to either monotherapy, the greater OHIP‑14 K reduction at week 4 indicates a faster onset of benefit and clarifies that the combination confers speed rather than sustained superiority. In practice, and in view of clonazepam’s adverse‑effect profile, a short, time‑limited course of the combination during the initial 4 weeks may be considered when rapid quality of life improvement is prioritized, whereas liquid NAC monotherapy is likely sufficient over an 8‑week horizon for patients for whom minimizing benzodiazepine exposure is preferred.

Despite these promising findings, some methodological limitations must be considered. First, the lack of a placebo-controlled and double-blind design may have introduced expectation bias, particularly given the subjective nature of symptom evaluation in BMS. Statistical comparisons between the groups were rigorously performed; however, the absence of blinding limited the interpretability of patient-reported outcomes. Second, this study did not investigate varying concentrations or establish a dose–response relationship for the liquid NAC oral rinse, precluding conclusions regarding the optimal therapeutic formulation and exposure parameters. Third, the follow-up period was restricted to 8 weeks, which limited our ability to assess the durability of treatment effects or identify potential delayed benefits or adverse events. Fourth, because of the multicenter design, clonazepam dosing was not uniformly standardized and was adjusted based on institutional clinician preference (0.5 mg once or twice daily, PO). This inter-site variability may have influenced treatment outcomes, particularly in between-group comparisons. Future studies should adopt a standardized dosing protocol to minimize confounding and ensure interpretability. Fifth, the study was not prospectively powered; post hoc a priori sample-size determinations across prespecified scenarios suggested that larger per-arm cohorts would be required to detect modest between-group effects under conventional assumptions. Allocation was also unequal (Group 1: n = 43; Group 2: n = 24; Group 3: n = 47), and standardized mean differences (SMDs) at baseline—0.557 for age, 0.471 for baseline VAS, 0.235 for OHIP-14 K, and 0.165 for sex—indicated small-to-moderate heterogeneity. Collectively, these features may have reduced the precision of cross-arm estimates and increased the probability of type II error; accordingly, between-group comparisons should be interpreted with appropriate caution. We sought to mitigate these concerns through transparent reporting of SMDs and note that the internal consistency of within-group changes—presented with 95% confidence intervals—supports the interpretability of the arm-specific findings. Sixth, attrition and missing data warrant cautious interpretation. Of the 216 enrolled patients, 102 (47.2%) were not included in the primary analysis because of incomplete questionnaires or loss to follow-up. To quantify the potential impact, we reanalyzed the full enrolled cohort (n = 216) using two complementary sensitivity approaches—a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) under a Missing At Random (MAR) assumption and multiple imputation (MI) followed by pooled MMRM. Both analyses yielded consistent estimates and inferences, confirming that the primary conclusions remain robust despite high attrition and missingness; however, completer–dropout SMDs at baseline (VAS, 0.249; OHIP-14 K, 0.356) indicate residual selection/attrition bias and limited generalizability.

Therefore, future studies should incorporate randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled designs to rigorously validate the efficacy of topically administered liquid NAC and rule out placebo effects. Moreover, investigations into the dose–response relationship and mucosal pharmacokinetics of liquid NAC could inform the optimization of its concentration and administration. Additionally, mechanistic studies using mucosal biopsies or salivary biomarkers may elucidate the local biological effects of NAC on nociceptive pathways and oxidative stress regulation.

In conclusion, this multicenter prospective study provides the first clinical evidence supporting the utility of a topically applied liquid NAC oral rinse as a topical therapeutic agent for BMS. Both liquid NAC monotherapy and its combination with clonazepam were associated with sustained symptom reduction over the 8-week treatment course, with combination therapy yielding the most pronounced early improvement in quality of life. These findings emphasize the clinical relevance of targeting oxidative stress and peripheral neuropathic mechanisms in the management of BMS. Despite its methodological limitations, this study provides a foundation for future controlled studies aimed at refining topical antioxidant-based therapies and advancing personalized approaches for this multifactorial pain disorder.
