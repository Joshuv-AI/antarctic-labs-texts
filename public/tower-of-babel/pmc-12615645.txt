AI-driven prediction of progression to oral squamous cell carcinoma using a multiresolution pathology model
Authors: Yingci Liu-Swetz, Sead Niksic, Raja R. Seethala, Alivia Shasteen, David J. Foran, Elizabeth A. Bilodeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Potentially malignant oral lesions are visible mucosal changes with risk of progressing to squamous cell carcinoma. These lesions are currently graded as no, mild, moderate, or severe dysplasia per WHO guidelines. While widely used, this system is subjective, imprecise, and limited in distinguishing low-risk from high-risk lesions. To address this, we developed a multiresolution deep learning model using digital pathology to predict malignant transformation. Trained on 221 digitized whole-slide images (111 progressors, 110 non-progressors), our vision transformer (ViT) model outperformed traditional CNN-based models, achieving 80.0% accuracy, an F1-score of 77.3%, and an AUROC of 0.798. Importantly, AI-predicted progression aligned with histopathologic features such as abnormal keratinization, increased apoptosis, and nuclear changes characteristic of malignancy. While further validation in larger, prospective cohorts is needed, our model demonstrates promise as an adjunctive tool for identifying high-risk lesions, potentially improving risk stratification, and guiding treatment decisions.

Introduction

Oral carcinogenesis is a multi-step process driven by the accumulation of aberrant genetic alterations, leading to the clonal expansion of cells with unchecked proliferative potential1. These genetic changes manifest as phenotypic alterations at the tissue level that are clinically visible as leukoplakia, erythroplakia, mixed lesions or ulceration during oral cancer screenings. Upon biopsy and microscopic evaluation, these lesions are diagnosed as hyperplasia, keratosis, and/or dysplasia2,3. These potentially malignant oral lesions have a reported progression rate to oral squamous cell carcinoma of up to 50% within five years, depending primarily on the degree of dysplastic change3,4. Consequently, the accurate assessment and timely management of high-risk dysplastic lesions are critical to preventing oral cancer, which is a disease that is associated with significant morbidity and mortality (68% overall survival)5.

The World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for assessing the potential of progressing to malignancy rely chiefly on a three-tiered histopathological grading of dysplasia (mild, moderate, severe)6,7. Chaturvedi et al. reported that in a cohort of 4886 patients, the risk of malignant transformation correlates with the severity of dysplasia, with hazard ratios reported at 4.9 [CI: 2.3–10.7] for mild and 15.8 [CI: 5.5–45.6] for severe dysplasia as compared to lesions without dysplasia2. This risk stratification informs clinical management strategies, where severe dysplasia necessitates complete excision with margin evaluation, while mild and moderate dysplasia are often monitored via a “watch-and-wait” approach8–11. While the three-tiered grading system (mild, moderate, severe) provides some prognostic utility, it lacks precision in distinguishing high-risk from low-risk progressors. Additionally, this system is prone to high inter- and intra-rater reliability (κ: 0.41–0.5)12–15, as pathologists’ interpretations of cytologic and architectural changes often differ. In fact, approximately 20–35% of severe dysplasia and 5–15% of mild and moderate dysplasias progress to carcinoma without treatment2,9,16–18. These limitations underscore the need for more objective, reliable and reproducible methods for predicting malignant progression.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches are steadily gaining acceptance and adoption in diagnostic and investigative pathology because of the potential improvements they offer in performing cancer risk assessment19–22. Non-imaging-based prediction models that utilize clinical data from electronic health records (EHR) have also been developed to predict carcinoma progression. While these models show reasonably strong performance (AUC: 0.80–0.87), they rely heavily on accurate histopathologic interpretation documented in pathology reports and on the availability of well-curated datasets that include a multitude of clinical and behavioral variables, which are not consistently recorded in real-world outpatient settings23–26. Our approach predicts directly from histopathologic imaging independent of EHR data completeness. With the increase in digitization of pathology specimens into whole-slide images (WSIs), new algorithms and methods can be developed, optimized and implemented directly to clinical cases as adjunctive diagnostic tools without significant changes to routine clinical workflows17,27–29. To this end, researchers have begun to develop new methods and strategies for performing automated lesion identification and, more challengingly, predicting disease progression based upon subtle visual cues indicative of aggressive phenotypes for oral squamous cell carcinoma and related precursor lesions. Although deep learning models have demonstrated high accuracy (73–92%) for detecting dysplasia and carcinomatous changes30,31, their performance in predicting malignant transformation is significantly lower. Current metrics indicate that only 19–58% of model-predicted cases progress to oral cancer, with an F1-score of 0.69 and AUROCs of 0.68–0.77, where reported18–20,22,32,33. These studies also rely on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), whereas recently vision transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a powerful alternative to CNNs for medical image analysis. The primary advantage of ViTs relates to their ability to capture contextual relationships for global relational analysis by segmenting images into sequential tokens34–36. As such, ViTs have demonstrated significant potential in computational pathology, often outperforming traditional CNNs in diagnostic and prognostic tasks37,38.

Furthermore, these studies use single-resolution microscopic images and/or predefined nuclear features, all of which are limiting, as these parameters introduce biases related to spatial kernel structures or nuclear morphology. Single-resolution studies utilize patches generated at a single zoom level (e.g., ×20). This approach can limit the model’s ability to capture slide-level context, as it only “sees” one patch at a time. As a result, larger architectural features, such as verrucous or papillary architecture, bulbous (drop-shaped) rete processes, or basal cell clustering, may be overlooked. In this study, we seek to train models that mimic the human (pathologist) approach of adjusting resolution to capture both global and local features of regions of interest. To accomplish our objective, we develop a multiresolution framework utilizing a DINOv2-based Vision Transformer (ViT) model39 to predict cancer transformation in potentially malignant oral lesions. The model’s performance is compared against state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks (CNNs)40–42 and the WHO grading system. To provide context for the model’s predictions, we correlated ViT outputs with those rendered using classic histopathologic features, including cytologic and architectural characteristics of dysplasia linked to malignant transformation. This study presents two key innovations: First, it introduces a deep learning framework that dynamically integrates global tissue context and fine-grained cellular detail across multiple microscopic resolutions for pathology image analysis. Second, it provides the first direct comparison of multiresolution deep learning architectures, traditional convolutional neural networks versus vision transformers, for the clinical task of predicting malignant progression from oral potentially malignant lesions.

Vision transformer models outperform CNNs

To compare the classification performance of popular deep learning architectures, we trained convolutional neural networks (VGG16, InceptionV3, and ResNet50) and a vision transformer (Dinov2) on a subset of the training and validation data patches. We evaluated the classification ability of each multiresolution model (3-backbone) using a validation set of held-out patient cases, assessing performance on a patch-wise basis (Table 2). The Dinov2-based models consistently outperformed the CNNs on most metrics, except for sensitivity compared to VGG16. Notably, the specificity of the CNNs was considerably lower than that of the Dinov2 model, despite efforts to fine-tune the CNN hyperparameters to improve the discriminative power of the convolutional feature maps. Interestingly, the performance of naïve and Hibou initialized ViT models was similar. When we employed 5-fold cross-validation on the Dinov2 models, the results demonstrate that the multiresolution ViT models consistently outperformed the CNNs in the majority of the metrics, with average sensitivity (0.721), precision (0.653), and accuracy (0.670). Prediction of malignant disease progression in test data

We then applied our best-performing model to a test set of 50 whole-slide images (WSIs) of oral dysplasias from patients across three institutions (Fig. 4); the AUROC for our best-performing model was 0.798. To optimize performance on WSIs and identify the ideal threshold for classifying a case as positive, we tested various thresholds on the validation set. The threshold that yielded the best classification performance on WSIs was determined to be 0.38, which can be adjusted higher or lower to balance sensitivity and precision according to specific needs. Test set performance is presented in Fig. 4. The multiresolution ViT model achieved an accuracy of 80.0% and an F1-score of 0.773. The precision was 0.739, which is significantly higher than the WHO’s three-tiered dysplasia grading system, where the maximum PPV for severe dysplasia is 0.543,44. To compare with binary dysplasia classification9, we averaged the dysplasia scores assigned by three pathologists and categorized the cases as high-grade/high-risk for moderate to severe dysplasia, and low-grade/low-risk for mild dysplasia. The ViT models outperformed the pathologists binary classification for prediction of progression to invasive carcinoma across all performance metrics, including precision (0.739 vs. 0.696) and sensitivity (0.810 vs. 0.762) (Fig. 4A). The potential clinical utility of the model was evaluated using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), as shown in Fig. 4B. Figure 4C visualizes our AI model’s application to whole-slide images (WSIs), where red regions indicate areas predicted to progress to carcinoma, while blue regions represent areas predicted to remain benign over five years. The reconstructed WSIs from the test set are overlaid with model predictions. Following our validation set analysis and threshold optimization, a lesion was classified as high-risk for malignant progression if over 30% of the slide area was marked as red. When WSIs were analyzed to interpret the model’s predictions, we found that regions with pronounced cytologic and architectural atypia were more frequently classified as high-risk, while areas with mild atypia were associated with non-progression. For example (Fig. 4C), Case 1 exhibits prominent nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, and a proliferative rete basilar layer, and Case 2 shows severe dyskeratosis and premature keratinization. In contrast, Case 3, a non-progressor, displays a milder overall degree of atypical features. Among the false positive cases, common histopathologic features included prominently downward-projecting rete ridges, marked dyskeratosis, and dense subepithelial inflammatory infiltrates. In one notable case, cytologic and nuclear atypia appeared to be reactive in nature, likely associated with an adjacent ulcer rather than true neoplastic progression. False negative cases generally exhibited minimal architectural distortion and low levels of inflammation. These observations suggest that increasing the representation of such nuanced histologic patterns in the training dataset may help address current feature recognition deficiencies and improve the model’s diagnostic performance.

Discussion

Computational pathology in the areas of disease detection and tumor subtyping has improved significantly, demonstrating the potent ability of AI models to recognize visual differences between healthy and cancerous, often achieving accuracy rates above 85 percent33,45–49. However, AI-assisted prognosis relating to disease progression remains more difficult with significantly lower predictive ability20–22,50,51. To address this prognostic challenge for potentially malignant oral lesions, we developed a model that surpasses the sensitivity and precision of traditional three-tier dysplasia grading in predicting malignant transformation over five years. Additionally, we evaluated multiple deep learning architectures and introduced a multiresolution approach, an innovation that, to our knowledge, has not been previously applied to assessing premalignant oral lesions. Although AI studies on predicting malignant progression remain limited, our findings align with previous research using convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which report F1-scores ranging from 0.40 to 0.6020–22,33,45,46. Interestingly, our Dinov2-ViT model outperformed the CNN architectures on most metrics except for sensitivity (Table 2), aligning with findings from several previous studies37,38,52. This suggests that while CNNs excel at capturing local features due to their convolutional kernels and application of feature filters to images, these characteristics also limit CNNs in pickup long-range dependencies and correlations present in histopathologic images. ViTs, though requiring larger training datasets, are better suited for identifying global patterns and structural relationships across the entire image, which is an advantage we observed in our classification performance. Across all CNN-based models, specificity remained low, reinforcing the notion that CNNs are less powerful at capturing non-adjacent feature dependencies. Prior studies have highlighted fundamental differences between convolutional and transformer-based architecture in that CNNs rely on local receptive fields and convolutional filters, limiting their ability to capture the global context necessary for accurately identifying negative cases (specificity)38,53. In contrast, ViTs process images as sequences of patches, enabling them to model long-range dependencies and assign importance to spatially distant regions within an image38,54. In the context of our clinical task, these long-range dependencies may reflect architectural patterns characteristic of high-risk premalignant disease. Given their differing strengths, CNNs and ViTs may be better suited to different types of tasks, especially when working with small or moderately sized datasets, though access to an extremely large dataset could potentially mitigate the limitations of either architecture. Our results also indicate that ViT initialized with pretrained histology weights (Hibou) outperformed a naïve ViT for predicting malignant transformation (Fig. 4A), as expected.

Several similar studies on potentially malignant oral lesions rely heavily on feature engineering and selection as key steps in developing their machine learning models, where new histopathologic features are either created or existing ones are selected. Notably, one study used a convolutional neural network (CNN) to build nuclear masks from the epithelium and then generate 168 morphologic/nuclear characteristics, which are fed into a random forest classifier, achieving an AUROC of 0.7521. While feature engineering can improve model performance and interpretability, we chose an alternative approach by allowing the vision transformer model to ‘self-select’ features from patches. This decision was based on the inherent limitations of feature engineering, as it can introduce bias in feature selection and may fail to capture important, non-linear interactions between features, leading to missed relationships that could enhance model performance. Though our undirected approach resulted in a slightly higher AUROC and F1-score, further work is needed to assess whether one approach is superior for the specific clinical task of predicting malignant progression on histopathology. A key distinction of our study is the inclusion of ablation experiments (Table 1) to assess the impact of multiresolution image analysis on predictive performance. We incorporated multiple spatial resolutions, mimicking the diagnostic approach used by pathologists who assess tissue architecture at lower magnifications and cellular/nuclear details at higher magnifications. The number of backbones (three) used in our models was chosen to balance spatial resolution coverage with computational feasibility. Our results indicate that increasing the number of backbones improves predictive power, likely because different resolutions capture different histopathologic features that impact prognosis.

Additionally, independent dysplasia grading by three blinded pathologists on the test set showed that the AI model’s classification reflected human expert assessments, as lesions predicted to progress were typically graded as moderate to severe dysplasia (grading score: 2.17), while non-progressor lesions were graded as mild to moderate (grading score: 1.24). The AI model demonstrates strong statistical correlations between histopathologic severity and progression likelihood, suggesting a greater reliance on visual features within the WSI without a priori knowledge about the disease or influence of other clinically-relevant variables. The AI model’s predictions were also found to be associated with specific features strongly linked to malignant progression (Fig. 5), reinforcing the notion that the AI model’s predictions were not random but grounded in histopathologic patterns. Notably, all eight features that were more prevalent and severe in the actual progressor cohort were also identified by the model as key predictors (p ≤ 0.01). This congruency provides strong evidence that the model is leveraging established histopathologic markers6,17,55 of high-grade disease to inform its predictions.

In summary, this study introduces a novel multiresolution, ViT-based computational pathology tool that can be directly applied to WSIs to predict the progression of oral epithelial lesions to carcinoma. Prior large-scale studies indicate that up to 18–50% of high-grade dysplasia (moderate and severe) cases progress to oral cancer using the WHO grading system and its derivative classifications56–61. In comparison, our model outperformed human pathologists in binary grading (high-risk vs. low-risk), achieving a higher positive predictive value (PPV: 0.739 vs. 0.696) and sensitivity (0.810 vs. 0.762), as shown in Fig. 4A. As digital pathology continues to expand into clinical practice, this AI model has the potential for clinical integration, providing tools for tumor detection and subtyping, including providing clinical decision support for potentially malignant oral lesions. If implemented effectively, it could aid in the early detection of high-risk lesions and reduce oral cancer incidence. Additionally, the same multiresolution ViT framework can be adapted for other diseases in computational pathology, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.

Despite these promising results, our AI model has key limitations. The dataset size for training, validation, and testing is relatively small, limiting definitive interpretation about its robustness and ability to generalize. There remains a lack of well-supported, available imaging data for oral potentially malignant disorders, despite a reported ~65 percent 5-year survival rate following malignant transformation to squamous cell carcinoma. Additionally, the model does not incorporate non-histopathologic clinical factors such as alcohol and tobacco use, which are important contributors to oral cancer development and progression. This reliance solely on imaging data restricts the model’s ability to capture the full spectrum of disease determinants. To enhance model performance and move toward clinical implementation, data sharing and cross-institutional collaboration will be critical for developing robust and generalizable risk assessment tools. Future efforts should prioritize infrastructure to support these collaborative initiatives as well as integrate clinical variables into a multimodal AI framework for a more comprehensive and accurate risk prediction system.

Methods

Case selection

Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from the Rutgers Institutional Review Board (Pro2022000834) for this retrospective study, which was deemed minimal risk and exempt from informed consent requirements due to its retrospective nature. To develop a classification model, we identified progressors as patients with diagnoses of a previously benign or potentially malignant oral lesion who subsequently developed a malignant diagnosis using the following search strings: “squamous cell carcinoma” and/or “carcinoma in situ.” The search strategy began with the identification of patients with malignant diagnoses and retroactively querying the record for corresponding prior oral biopsy diagnoses, for which we then retrieved hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained glass slides (2010 to 2022). Prior diagnoses representing the spectrum of dysplasia, including atypical epithelial hyperplasia, were included in the study. All potential progressor cases were verified for their progression to squamous cell carcinoma in the health record. Glass slides with significant staining or tissue defects were excluded. In addition, a minimum interval of 6 months was required for transformation to carcinoma to minimize the inclusion of cases with potential tissue sampling errors. We also retrieved H&E slides for control cases or non-progressors (2000 to 2018), defined as patients with oral dysplasia diagnoses without evidence of malignant transformation, with a minimum follow-up of 60 months.

Model development

Glass slides were digitized into whole-slide images (WSIs) using an Aperio AT2 scanner. ImageScope software was used to annotate regions of interest (ROIs) within the oral epithelium for training. Due to the large size of WSIs, each digital slide was partitioned into square patches of 224 × 224 or 512 × 512 pixels before being fed into the training pipeline. Although maximum spatial resolution varied across WSIs due to scanning differences, we applied magnification standardization across patches using the OpenSlide library62.

Cases and their corresponding patches were each labeled as “progressor” or “non-progressor” based on the case outcomes described in the previous section. To prevent data leakage and ensure the integrity of the train-validation-test split, patches from a single case were exclusively assigned to one of the three sets. In total, 72,911 patches from 142 cases were used for training and validation purposes (Fig. 2). Deep learning experiments and training were conducted on 64 GB NVIDIA GPU-based computing nodes using Python and the PyTorch machine learning framework (v2.4.1). To improve learning efficacy, various data augmentations were applied to the image patches, including random horizontal and vertical flips (each with a 0.5 probability), random color adjustments for brightness, hue, and saturation, and scaling. Classification performance of several state-of-the-art convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures (ResNet50, Inceptionv3, VGG16) was compared alongside a Dinov2-based Vision Transformer (ViT) model. To train a multiresolution model that mimics how a human pathologist examines biopsies at varying zoom levels, we experimented with feature extraction backbones applied to patches with a common center but at different spatial resolutions (×10, ×20, ×40). We utilize multiple feature extraction backbones applied to patches centered at the same location but captured at different spatial resolutions. The extracted features were then fused and passed through a deep learning architecture for classification. Figure 3 provides an overview of the multiresolution architecture and the model prediction pipeline. This multiresolution framework was implemented for both CNN and ViT architectures. Specifically, for the ViT model, outputs from the Dinov2 feature extraction branches were concatenated and passed through a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). In addition, we were also able to leverage pre-existing histology weights from Hibou63. For all experiments, we fine-tuned both the feature extractor (DINOv2 backbone) and the MLP on our training data. We evaluated model performance using several types of feature extraction backbones, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs).

Model validation and evaluation

To evaluate each model during training, the “held-out” validation cases were used. Model weights were updated through backpropagation after each training epoch if validation loss improved. Early stopping was implemented to save the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation loss. To assess the stability and generalizability of our best-performing architecture, we performed 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the validation dataset. Stratification was based on progression status to ensure that each fold maintained the roughly even proportion of progressors and non-progressors. In each iteration, the model was trained on four folds and evaluated on the fifth, with the process repeated five times so that each subset served as the validation fold once. Standard classification metrics, including sensitivity, precision (PPV), specificity, accuracy, and F1-score, were computed to assess performance on a patch-wise basis (Table 2). For our best-performing model, additional threshold refinement was conducted to optimize slide-level classification on the test cases. The validation set was used to identify the best threshold for classifying at the slide-level through precision-recall curve analysis.

For test set evaluation, the pathologists were blinded to case outcomes while scoring histopathologic features and grading dysplasia. The mode score for each feature and the average dysplasia grade were recorded for analysis (Figs. 4, 5). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was generated using the WSI average of the predicted probabilities of progression by applying a softmax function to the final layer logits during model inference.
