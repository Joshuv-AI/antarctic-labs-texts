The prevalence of E198A and F200Y single nucleotide polymorphism in Haemonchus contortus populations from Polish goat herds
Authors: Zofia Nowek, Marcin Mickiewicz, Michał Czopowicz, Agata Moroz-Fik, Adrian-Valentin Potărniche, Kinga Biernacka, Tomasz Nalbert, Olga Szaluś-Jordanow, Paweł Górski, Alistair Antonopoulos, Iwona Markowska-Daniel, Emilia Bagnicka, Marián Várady, Jarosław Kaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A cross-sectional descriptive study was carried out on a study population of 81 goat herds to investigate the prevalence and frequency of two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; E198A and F200Y) associated with resistance to benzimidazoles (BZ) in Haemonchus contortus populations of goats in Poland. The distribution of gastrointestinal nematode (GIN) genera/species in a pooled fecal sample from each herd was determined in larval cultures and the presence of H. contortus DNA in larval cultures was confirmed using real-time PCR. SNPs were detected and their frequency was quantified using pyrosequencing. The Hardy–Weinberg principle was employed to estimate frequencies of H. contortus genotypes based on frequencies of alleles containing each SNP. At least one BZ resistance-associated SNP was detected in each within-herd (WH) H. contortus population—F200Y SNP in 79/81 WH H. contortus populations (98%) and E198A SNP in 80/81 WH H. contortus populations (99%). The median WH frequencies of F200Y and E198A were 86% and 8%, respectively. In the total Polish H. contortus population, the overall frequency of BZ-resistant genotypes was estimated at 89% (CI 95%: 82%–93%) and was positively and independently associated with the purchase of goats abroad (adjusted odds ratio [ORadj] 22.8 [CI 95%: 8.9–58.4]; p < 0.001), large size of herd (> 100 goats; ORadj 7.5 [CI 95%: 1.8–30.5]; p = 0.006), use of levamisole for goat deworming (ORadj 3.1 [CI 95%: 1.2–7.8]; p = 0.019), and multiple anthelmintic treatments per year (ORadj 3.1 [CI 95%: 1.0–9.5]; p = 0.046). The study shows that genetic resistance to BZ is widespread in H. contortus infecting Polish goats. Therefore, reconsideration of routine BZ use and implementation of integrated and sustainable parasite control strategies appears to be highly recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54564-3.

Introduction

Parasitic infections are one of the most significant threats to small ruminant health and welfare worldwide1–4. In goats, a principal role is played by gastrointestinal nematodes (GIN) from the family Trichostrongylidae, mostly from the genera Trichostrongylus spp. and Teladorsagia spp. as well as the species Haemonchus contortus5. For decades GIN control in goats has relied on regular use of anthelmintics of 3 basic chemical classes—benzimidazoles (BZ; such as albendazole, fenbendazole, oxfendazole), macrocyclic lactones (ML; such as ivermectin, doramectin, eprinomectin), and imidazothiazoles (mainly levamisole, LEV)4,6. As deworming is usually neither preceded by evaluation of the parasitic burden nor followed by evaluation of its effectiveness, both unnecessary and ineffective anthelmintic treatments are common. This, along with other factors, has led to the emergence and spread of anthelmintic resistance (AR) in goat populations in many European countries including the United Kingdom7,8, the Netherlands9, Spain10, Portugal11 Germany12,13, Switzerland14, Italy15,16, France17–21, Norway22,23, Denmark24, Bosnia and Herzegovina25, Lithuania23, Romania26,27, the Czech Republic28, Slovakia29,30, and Poland31. Given the considerably bigger population and higher economic importance of sheep compared with goats in most European countries, reports and studies regarding AR in sheep are even more numerous and come from almost all European countries23,32,33, which suggests that prevalence of AR in goat populations may still be underestimated34.

AR occurs when a proportion of parasitic population survives a properly conducted treatment with a normally effective dose of an anthelmintic35,36. The occurrence of AR may be detected by revealing resistant parasites i.e. adult worms capable of yielding eggs despite proper deworming (fecal egg count reduction test, FECRT), eggs able to hatch (egg hatch test, EHT) or larvae able to complete development up to the invasive third stage (larval development test, LDT) in the anthelmintic drug solution37. AR often arises from mutations in the genome35, commonly single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP), which lead to a coding change at the corresponding position in the translated protein. These mutations are heritable and can be transmitted to progeny38. The ability of parasites to survive stems from molecular modifications of their structural or functional proteins and these, in turn, result from alterations in these parts of genome which code proteins essential for proper activity of anthelmintics35. Emerging AR manifests itself with replacing wild-type (susceptible) GIN genotypes by the mutated ones. Detection of mutated alleles of genes (resistance-associated alleles) is an appealing alternative to classic diagnostic approach, if a well-defined alteration in genome responsible for AR to a given anthelmintic class is known. To date, mechanisms of resistance based on SNPs have been at least partly clarified for BZ39, and LEV resistance in Haemonchus contortus40. However, to date no SNP markers have been validated for ML resistance.

BZ inhibit the polymerization of α- and β-tubulin dimers to microtubules41 which results in the inhibition of glucose uptake and intracellular transport and eventually leads to death of the parasite42. To exert their effect, BZ molecules bind to the β-tubulin monomer43. Point substitution mutations in the isotype-1 β-tubulin coding sequence (referred to as SNP) have been shown to markedly reduce tubulin-BZ affinity and most likely account for the molecular mechanism of BZ resistance in nematodes44, although BZ resistance may in fact be determined by more than one gene45. Heredity of BZ resistance is an autosomal (not sex-linked) incomplete dominant (semi-dominant) trait45–49. At least 7 SNPs associated with BZ resistance have so far been identified in 3 codons—167 (F167Y), 198 (E198A, E198K, E198V, E198L), and 200 (F200Y, F200L). Three of them are considered canonical: substitution of thymine with adenine (transversion) in codon 167 which results in replacing phenylalanine (F) with tyrosine (Y)—F167Y50,51, substitution of adenine with cytosine (transversion) in codon 198 which results in replacing glutamate (E) with alanine (A)—E198A52, and substitution of thymine with adenine (transversion) in codon 200 which results in replacing phenylalanine (F) with tyrosine (Y)—F200Y53,54. Since 3 codons are so far known to be affected, at least 8 combinations of mutations exist (3 single mutations, 3 double mutations, 1 triple mutation and 1 non-mutated) which renders BZ resistance a trait mediated by multiple alleles. Additional affected codons have already been identified in other nematodes (e.g. codon 134 in a hookworm Ancylostoma caninum55,56) which makes the list of possible resistance alleles still open.

The BZ resistance-associated SNPs may be detected using various molecular biology methods including classic PCR, real-time PCR, droplet or microchamber-based digital PCR, PCR-Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism, pyrosequencing, and deep amplicon sequencing57–63. An increasing number of epidemiological studies on AR based on detection of resistance-associated alleles of β-tubulin genes using molecular methods have been recently carried out in GIN infecting small ruminants all over the world64–68.

In Poland, BZ remain the most commonly used anthelmintics in goat farming31. BZ resistance was reported in goats for the first time in 2017 using FECRT and EHT69. A large-scale cross-sectional study based on LDT carried out in the Polish goat population in 2018–2019 showed that BZ resistance was widespread, with a herd-level prevalence of 88% (CI 95%: 75%–95%)31. BZ resistance was earlier confirmed using phenotypic methods in Polish sheep, cattle, pigs, and horses70–72. However, the only molecular study conducted so far in Poland failed to reveal the presence of F200Y SNP in H. contortus infecting sheep due to technical problems72. Therefore, we decided to carry out a cross-sectional study based on pyrosequencing to investigate the prevalence and frequency of two canonic SNPs, E198A and F200Y, in H. contortus populations of goats in Poland.

Materials and methods

Goat population and questionnaire survey

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study based on convenience sampling73. All goat herd owners who volunteered to cooperate with the parasitological laboratory of the Division of Veterinary Epidemiology and Economics at the Warsaw University of Life Sciences-SGGW were potential candidates for inclusion. The herd was enrolled in the study when the following criteria were satisfied: i) the owner granted informed consent for participation in the study and use of the results in scientific publications; ii) fecal samples were collected from a representative group of goats in the herd or, if and where possible, from all goats in the herd and delivered to the laboratory.

Pyrosequencing is regarded as one of reference tests for SNP detection44,59. Therefore, for the needs of sample size determination diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of pyrosequencing were assumed to be perfect (100%). A minimum representative number of goats from each herd (n) was calculated assuming the minimum within-herd prevalence of SNP (minimum proportion of goats harboring H. contortus with SNP) to be detected was 25% (Pexp; one fourth of the herd) and a level of confidence (LoC) of 95%, according to the following formula74:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{n}=\mathrm{N}\times [1-{(1-\mathrm{L}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{C})}^{1/({\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}}\times \mathrm{N})}]$$\end{document}where N was herd size.

In each herd, the questionnaire survey was carried out and the following data were collected: location of the farm, the number of goats kept on the farm including both adult goats and goat kids (< 6 month-old), grazing on the pasture (including length of grazing period), presence of other livestock (mainly sheep and cattle) on the farm, purchase of goats from other herds in Poland or abroad (including the country of origin), and the history of deworming including the number of times the herd had been dewormed in previous 3 years and anthelmintics which had been used. Herds were arbitrarily categorized into very small (≤ 20 goats), small (21–50 goats), medium (51–100 goats), and large (> 100 goats). This categorization corresponds to the one used in our previous epidemiological studies as well as to categorization employed by The Agency for Restructuring and Modernization of Agriculture (ARMA), responsible for registering farm animals (including goats) in Poland. The blank questionnaire is presented in the Supplementary files (Table S1).

Poland was divided into 6 major socio-economic regions according to the EUROSTAT Nomenclature of territorial units for statistics level 1 (NUTS1) classification (https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/345175/7451602/2021-NUTS-1-map.pdf) modified by merging central (PL7) and Mazovian (Mazowieckie, PL9) into one region (Fig. S1).

This study did not involve experimental procedures on animals requiring approval by an institutional ethics committee or licensing body under Polish law. All procedures were conducted in accordance with Polish legal

regulations, the standards recommended by EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments, Good Laboratory Practice, and the Act of the Polish Parliament of 15 January 2015 on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific or Educational Purposes (Journal of Laws 2015, item 266). Participation of goat owners was voluntary and written informed consent for herd participation, sample collection, and use of the data in scientific publications was obtained from each farmer prior to data collection. No approval from a human bioethics committee was required since the questionnaires concerned animal health, husbandry, and management and did not constitute a medical experiment on humans under the Polish Pharmaceutical Law (Journal of Laws 2001 No. 126, item 1381). All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Faecal sample collection and examination

Fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum or immediately after defecation, by farmers or by the scientific team during veterinary consultations on goat farms. If collected by the scientific team, fecal samples were vacuum-packed, immediately transported to the laboratory at room temperature and examined the same day. If collected by farmers, fecal samples were packed in sealed bags, delivered to the laboratory at temperature as close to room temperature as possible, and examined within 24 h after collection. Cooling packs (packed so that they did not have direct contact with fecal samples) were used only in the summer when air temperature exceeded 30 °C. Fecal egg count (FEC) was determined using a modified McMaster technique with an analytical sensitivity (limit of detection) of 50 eggs per gram (epg) according to the guidelines of the World Association for the Advancement of Veterinary Parasitology (WAAVP)75,76.

Larval cultures (coprocultures)

Larval cultures were prepared for each herd by mixing 5 g of feces collected from each animal into one pool that was incubated in the dark for 7 days at 26 °C. After Baermannization, 100 third-stage larvae (L3) from each pool were identified at the genus/species level following the procedure described by van Wyk and Mayhew77, and the percentage of each nematode genus/species was calculated. After morphological identification, the entire larval sample from each herd was divided into at least two 2 ml Eppendorf tubes and frozen at -20 °C until DNA extraction.

A particular GIN species was considered as predominant if it accounted for > 80% of counted larvae. Diversity of GIN species composition was determined within a herd using the Simpson’s diversity index (SDI) calculated according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{SDI}} = 1 - \frac{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{{{\mathrm{i}} = 1}}^{{\mathrm{k}}} {\mathrm{n}}_{{\mathrm{i}}} \left( {{\mathrm{n}}_{{\mathrm{i}}} - 1} \right)}}{{{\mathrm{N}}\left( {{\mathrm{N}} - 1} \right)}}$$\end{document}where ni was the number of larvae of each genus or species (k) and N was the total number of counted larvae.

Genomic DNA extraction

The NucleoSpin DNA Stool (Macherey Nagel, Germany) was used to extract genomic DNA from pooled larval cultures containing at least 10 000 larvae, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The DNA was eluted in 100 μl of the elusion buffer, and the DNA samples were stored at -20 °C until further testing.

Haemonchus contortus-specific PCRs

The presence of H. contortus DNA in larval cultures was confirmed using real-time PCR. The assay was performed using the HaeCon dtec-qPCR Monodose Kit with internal control (Genetic PCR Solutions, Spain) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each TargetSpecies MonoDose qPCR tube contained a dehydrated reaction mix including specific primers and probe, DNA polymerase, dNTPs, and buffer. For each reaction, 6 μL of template DNA and 14 μL of RNase-free water were added to reconstitute the mix to a final volume of 20 μL. Reactions were performed on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland). Thermal cycling consisted of an initial activation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, and hybridization/extension with fluorescence data collection at 60 °C for 20 s. Each run included a negative control (no-template control), a positive control, and a sample inhibition control to ensure assay analytical sensitivity, specificity, and the absence of PCR inhibitors in the tested samples.

Determination of resistance allele frequency using pyrosequencing.

Pyrosequencing was used to detect transversion from thymine (TTC, TTT) to adenine (TAC, TAT) in the codon 200 (F200Y) and transversion from adenine (GAG, GAA) to cytosine (GCG, GCA) in the codon 198 (E198A) of the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene. Alleles of the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene containing at least one of these mutations were referred to as resistance alleles. F200Y mutation results in expression of tyrosine (Y) instead of phenylalanine (F) while E198A mutation results in expression of alanine (A) instead of glutamic acid (glutamate, E).

PCR reactions were carried out with PyroMark PCR Kit (QIAGEN) to analyze SNPs in codons 200 and 198 of H. contortus. Each 25 μl reaction consisted of 12.5 μl PyroMark PCR Master Mix (QIAGEN), 0.2 mM biotinylated primer, 0.4 mM non-biotinylated primer, 2.5 μl CoralLoad Concentrate, and 5 μl of genomic DNA extracted from pooled larval cultures. Cycling conditions were: initial denaturation and polymerase-activation step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 1 min, 53 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, and a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min. β-tubulin isotype-1 was amplified using the forward primer HcPy2PCR For (5′-GAC GCA TTC ACT TGG AGG AG-3′) and a biotinylated reverse primer HcPy2PCR Rev (5′Biotin-CAT AGG TTG GAT TTG TGA GTT-3′). A 400 bp region of the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene was amplified. Gel electrophoresis was used to verify template DNA amplification, with 5 μl of the PCR product running on a 2% agarose gel. Pyrosequencing assays were conducted for β-tubulin isotype-1 codons 198 and 200. The assays were carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol using PyroMark Q48 Autoprep (QIAGEN), PyroMark Q48 Advanced reagents (QIAGEN), and sequencing primer HcPyIso1Seq198_200 (5′-GGT AGA GAA CAC CGA TG-3′).

Calculation of genotype frequency (proportion of H. contortus bearing resistant alleles).

All GIN larvae obtained from coproculture from a given herd were referred to as a within-herd (WH) GIN population and from an epidemiological standpoint were separated open populations73. All GIN larvae from 81 goat herds were referred to as a representative sample of the total Polish GIN population.

Owing to pyrosequencing, frequencies of alleles containing F200Y and E198A SNPs became known. Normal alleles were denoted by the symbol of an amino acid present in non-mutated codon (i.e. F200 and E198) while mutated alleles (resistance-associated alleles) were denoted by the symbol of a replacing amino acid (i.e. 200Y and 198A). The Hardy–Weinberg principle was employed to estimate frequencies of H. contortus genotypes using two approaches: (1) with the focus on each SNP separately (mutation-oriented approach); (2) with the focus on the combination of SNPs responsible for BZ resistance (resistance-oriented approach).

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were examined for normality of distribution using the normal probability Q-Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk W test. As normality assumption was violated in all cases, numerical variables were summarized using median, interquartile range (IQR), and range. They were compared between 2 groups with the Mann–Whitney U test (unpaired groups) or Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test (paired groups) while comparisons between > 2 groups were performed with the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test (unpaired groups) or Friedman’s test (paired groups). Correlations between numerical variables were measured with Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient (Rs) and categorized as Rs = 0.00 to 0.19—very weak, 0.20 to 0.49—weak, 0.50 to 0.69—moderate, 0.70 to 0.89—strong, and 0.90 to 1.00—very strong. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and proportions (%), and compared between groups using McNemar’s test (paired groups) or maximum likelihood G test (unpaired groups). The 95% confidence intervals (CI 95%) for proportions were calculated according to Wilson’s score method78. The CI 95% for the within-herd F200Y / E198A ratio was calculated according to the delta (Taylor) method. Association between various factors and frequency of resistant H. contortus genotypes was investigated using the logistic regression model with robust residual regression coefficients estimation based on relative Hessian divergence. First, univariable logistic regression was performed with each factor separately and factors with p < 0.15 were introduced into the multivariable logistic regression with backward stepwise elimination procedure. The strength of the relationship was expressed as the odds ratio (OR; crude OR in the univariable analysis and adjusted OR [ORadj] in the multivariable analysis) and was classified as mild (OR 0.5 to < 1 or > 1 to 2), moderate (OR 0.2 to < 0.5 or > 2 to 5), strong (OR 0.1 to < 0.2 or > 5 to 10), and very strong (OR < 0.1 or > 10)79. All statistical tests were two-tailed and significance level (α) was set at 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed in TIBCO Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA) and IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). The maps were created in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and then manually adjusted in Microsoft PowerPoint.

Results

Study population characteristics

The study included 81 goat herds from Poland examined between 2017 and 2024. The number of goats kept in the herd ranged from 2 to 208 with a median (IQR) of 28 (10–65) goats. Most herds were very small (≤ 20 goats; 32/81 herds [39%]) or small (21–50 goats; 24/81 herds [30%]). Only one fifth of the herds were medium (51–100 goats; 17/81 herds [21%]) and 10% were large (> 100 goats; 8/81 herds). The herds were scattered over the area of entire country with the highest number in Mazowieckie (n = 12) and Małopolskie province (n = 11) and the lowest in Zachodniopomorskie (n = 2) and Kujawsko-Pomorskie province (n = 1). Regarding NUTS1 geographic regions, the highest number of herds were located in the central region (n = 22), followed by southern and eastern (n = 15 in each), northern (n = 12), northwestern (n = 9), and southwestern region (n = 8) (Fig. S1).

In 61/81 herds (75%), a single goat breed was kept. Virtually half the herds (39/81 [48%]) kept only Polish local breeds, i.e. Polish White Improved or Polish Fawn Improved. Anglo-Nubian goats were kept as the only breed in 19 herds, Saanen goats in 2 herds, and Carpathian goats in 1 herd. In the remaining 20 herds, various mixtures of 2 or rarely 3 of the aforementioned breeds were kept, with a few herds keeping also miniature, angora, or alpine goats.

Goats on 76/81 farms (94%) were grazed on pastures, typically from April to October. In most herds, goats were grazed constantly on the same pastures while only on 4/76 farms (5%) pastures were rotated. Goats were the only ruminants kept on 55/81 farms (68%). Sheep were kept on 14 farms, cattle on 8 farms, and both ruminant species on 4 farms. These species were usually kept on separate pastures but sometimes shared the same pasture, without any regularity.

Only 18/81 farms (22%) relied solely on their own replacements. The owners of the remaining 63 herds tended to purchase new goats from Poland (47 herds), both Poland and abroad (10 herds) or only from other countries (6 herds). Goats were purchased from direct neighbors of Poland namely Germany (6 herds), the Czech Republic (6 herds), and Slovakia (2 herds) as well as from countries located in western Europe such as the Netherlands (5 herds), Austria (1 herd), and the United Kingdom (1 herd). One herd imported goats from Hungary (Fig. S2).

Anthelmintic treatment practices

According to farmers’ declarations goats were regularly dewormed in all herds—usually twice a year (in 52/81 herds [64%]); once a year in 15/81 herds (19%), and 3 or 4 times in 14/81 herds (17%). The most common groups of anthelmintics were oral BZ (85%; usually albendazole, rarely fenbendazole) and ML (68%; usually pour-on eprinomectin, less often injectable ivermectin) (Table 1). Most farmers used both BZ and ML (38/81 [47%]), sole BZ (24/81 [30%]) or sole ML (10/81 [12%]). Gastrointestinal nematode populations

Flotation and microscopic examination were negative in only 1 herd. In the remaining 80 herds, the WH GIN prevalence ranged from 17% to 100% with the median (IQR) of 100% (80%–100%). In 77 herds in which FEC was determined, mean FEC ranged from 8 to 5005 epg with the median (IQR) of 1062 (521–1616) epg. FEC was significantly positively correlated with herd size (p = 0.001), although this correlation was weak (Rs = 0.36).

Despite negative flotation and microscopic fecal examination in 1 herd, coprocultures from all herds were positive for GIN larvae. The herd-level prevalence was highest for H. contortus (78/81 herds [96%, CI 95%: 90%–99%]) and Trichostrongylus spp. (76/81 herds [94%, CI 95%: 86%–97%]). The herd-level prevalence of Teladorsagia spp. (44/81 herds [54%, CI 95%: 44%–65%]) and Oesophagostomum spp. (36/81 herds [44%, CI 95%: 34%–55%]) were significantly lower (p = 0.001). Despite the fact that coprocultures from 2 herds were negative for H. contortus, real-time PCR confirmed the presence of H. contortus DNA in all 81 herds with cycle threshold (Ct) between 8 and 34 (median Ct = 15; IQR: 12–19). Therefore, all 81 herds were considered infected by H. contortus and included in the SNP and genotypic analyses.

In the coprocultures positive for larvae of a particular GIN, H. contortus larvae were most abundant, followed by Trichostrongylus spp. larvae (p = 0.021). Teladorsagia spp. and Oesophagostomum spp. larvae were equally uncommon (p = 0.629) (Fig. 1; Table S2). SDI ranged from 0 (larvae of only one GIN genus/species present) to 0.72 with the median (IQR) of 0.31 (0.17–0.53). Mean FEC in the herd was weakly positively correlated with the frequency of H. contortus larvae in the coproculture (Rs = 0.27, p = 0.014) and weakly negatively correlated with the frequency of Trichostrongylus spp. larvae in the coproculture (Rs = -0.22, p = 0.053). Frequency of benzimidazole-resistance-associated SNPs

Both BZ resistance-associated SNPs were detected in the vast majority of WH H. contortus populations—F200Y SNP in 79/81 WH H. contortus populations (98% [CI 95%: 91%–99%]) and E198A SNP in 80/81 WH H. contortus populations (99% [CI 95%: 93%– > 99%]). There was no WH H. contortus population free from both BZ resistance-associated SNPs.

The frequencies of the two SNPs significantly differed (p < 0.001)—F200Y SNP frequency ranged from 0% to 100% with a median (IQR) of 86% (74%–92%), while E198A SNP frequency ranged from 0% to 28% with a median (IQR) of 8% (5%–12%) (Fig. 2). The WH frequency of F200Y SNP was 8.5-times higher (CI 95%: 7.3–9.7) than of E198A SNPs. E198A SNP frequency was higher than F200Y SNP frequency in only 5 WH H. contortus populations, and these were H. contortus populations in which SNP frequency was generally low (< 10%). Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate the prevalence and frequency of BZ resistance-associated SNPs in the β-tubulin gene of H. contortus populations in Polish goat herds and estimate the frequency of BZ-resistant H. contortus genotypes. It clearly shows that the F200Y SNP is both widespread at the herd-level (98% of WH H. contortus populations carry this SNP) and very frequent in H. contortus populations within goat herds (median of 86% of H. contortus in the WH GIN populations carry this SNP). On the other hand, the E198A mutation is equally widespread at the herd-level (99%) but rather sparse in H. contortus populations within goat herds (median of 8%). There seems to be no important geographic diversity of either SNP distribution. Given that our previous large-scale epidemiological study based on LDT showed widespread BZ resistance in Polish goat herds31, this observation suggests that the F200Y mutation plays the major role in the development of apparent (phenotypic) BZ resistance in H. contortus population of Poland. There seems to be considerably weaker pressure on the emergence of E198A SNP, and its role appears to be secondary or auxiliary. However, this should not be considered as a general rule. Similar observations have been made e.g. in Brazil81 or Uruguay82, while studies from e.g. China83 and Pakistan84 indicate the frequency of the E198A mutation to be equal or higher than of the F200Y mutation. Therefore, the true importance of each BZ resistance-associated SNP is still awaiting its clarification.

We used the known frequencies of two resistance mutations (F200Y and E198A) and the Hardy–Weinberg principle to estimate frequency of various combinations of H. contortus genotypes. It must be stressed that genotype frequencies presented in our study are only estimates contrary to direct detection of combinations of alleles performed e.g. by Silvestre and Humbert85. The estimates are true if the underlying assumptions are sufficiently satisfied. Of five main assumptions of the Hardy–Weinberg principle86,87, only two were almost certainly fulfilled in our study—a very large H. contortus population under analysis and random mating of all individuals in the population with production of similar numbers of offspring. They were likely fulfilled owing to H. contortus being a relatively primitive type of organism, compared to e.g. vertebrates. Regarding the remaining assumptions, mutations did occur in our target population; in fact, a particular type of mutations (BZ resistance-associated SNPs) were our main interest. Natural selection for resistant (i.e. bearing particular mutations) H. contortus took place due to BZ treatments administered by farmers, and finally, migration of H. contortus between goat herds as well as between Polish and foreign GIN populations occurred via movement of goats between herds. Therefore, estimates of frequencies of homozygotes and heterozygotes should be treated with caution. This made us carry out only one general analysis of factors associated with the presence of genotypes bearing at least one mutated allele of the β-tubulin gene, without delving into partial and complete resistance because, in fact, the proportions should be perceived rather as a rough general view than a highly precise and accurate description of the real situation.

We identified four factors significantly associated with higher frequency of BZ-resistant H. contortus genotypes. The strongest association was found for purchasing goats abroad. This result is not very surprising. Spread of GIN resistant to anthelmintics has been shown to occur through animal movement23,88–91. In our study, farmers reported to have imported goats mainly from a semicircular belt of countries neighboring Poland from the south and west in which BZ resistance in small ruminant populations is not only widespread but also had been described many years earlier than in Poland (Fig. S3). Other studies have shown that BZ resistance in GIN populations is positively associated with the diversity of origin of the animals purchased when the goat herd was established57. Moreover, spontaneous mutations producing BZ-resistant alleles appear to be a much less efficient means of emergence of BZ-resistant GIN populations than migration of BZ-resistant GIN between herds along with animal movement, followed by positive selection on farms through intensive use of BZ85,90.

The second factor was a large herd size, arbitrarily set at > 100 goats. It is especially interesting as no large herds in our study reported to have purchased goats abroad, so this factor appears to be completely independent from the previous one. Large herds also less frequently imported goats for replacement from other Polish herds (4/8 large herds [50%] vs. 59/73 [81%] other herds, p = 0.066). Owners of large herds did not practice more frequent deworming (p = 0.630), nor did they use BZ significantly more often than owners of other herds (8/8 large herds [100%] vs. 61/73 other herds [84%]; p = 0.100). A possible explanation of a high frequency of resistant H. contortus in large herds may be the deworming procedure and dosing of anthelmintics, especially in relation to the body weight of goats. For logistical reasons, in large herds deworming of each animal has to be done faster which may result in automatic use of the same dose and hence increase the risk of under-dosing. Under-dosing is considered one of the most important risk factors for BZ resistance92. It appears especially effective in promoting partly BZ-resistant heterozygotes. In the study of Silvestre et al.92, ¼ of the recommended fenbendazole dose was sufficient to eliminate susceptible homozygotes but was insufficient to kill BZ-resistant heterozygotes. Moreover, under-dosing is difficult to confirm in questionnaire surveys as farmers under-dose anthelmintics unconsciously, either due to underestimation of goats’ body weight or using too low “sheep” doses of anthelmintics85. Therefore, it is not unlikely that the “large herd” category in our study serves as a surrogate of some improper deworming procedures.

The use of LEV was identified as significantly linked with higher frequency of BZ resistance. Even though it would be tempting to consider this result as an indicator of a common mechanism via which BZ and LEV induce AR development, such causal interpretation is probably unjustified. It is more likely that the actual direction of this relationship is opposite: when the most popular anthelmintics (BZ and ML) prove ineffective, farmers turn to older drugs like LEV. AR to BZ and ML has been shown to be present in approximately 90% of Polish goat herds while AR to LEV is rare31. Therefore, this significant association is likely a consequence of high BZ resistance, rather than its cause. The same mechanism may apply to multiple deworming: farmers having problems with effective parasite control in their animals may try to enforce an expected effect by repeating the procedure. On the other hand, multiple deworming by itself is a well-known risk factor of AR development31,34,93, thus, the net of relationships may in fact be multidirectional in this case. However, a longitudinal rather than cross-sectional study design should be employed to clarify the nature of this observation73, and is a worthwhile objective of future research.

Interestingly, the use of BZ did not turn out to be significantly linked to the frequency of BZ-resistant genotypes. BZ-resistant genotypes were also frequent in 12 herds whose owners declared not to have used BZ. Such a phenomenon has been observed also in the case of ML resistance23 and attributed to importation of carriers of resistant GIN. This may be true as the only one of these 12 herds that relied only on their own replacements had low frequency of BZ-resistant genotypes (approx. 20%). On the other hand, we collected all management information regarding examined herds only by means of questionnaires and therefore they should be interpreted with caution—the fact that a farmer denied using BZ in recent years does not exclude intensive deworming with BZ earlier. A significant improvement in the efficacy of MLs and LEV in GIN populations has been reported in some instances, following the application of AR management programs in New Zealand89. The improvement in the efficacy of BZ was observable, but not significant94. This result, however, is likely due to the maintenance of a refugium of susceptible alleles within the wider population, which may slowly dilute resistance-associated alleles over time, rather than a reversion to susceptibility of resistant populations over timennn94,95. This highlights the importance of maintaining an understanding of the prevalence of resistance within GIN populations so as to guide treatment advice, which in turn can help maintain the sustainability of existing anthelmintics.

Conclusions

Both BZ resistance-associated SNPs, F200Y and E198A, are widespread across the H. contortus populations of Polish goat herds, however, only the F200Y SNP is very frequent in the WH H. contortus populations. Between 80% and 90% of a total H. contortus population in Polish goats have BZ resistance-associated genotype (at least one allele with at least one SNP), with approximately 15% of H. contortus population being double mutants. BZ resistance-associated genotypes appear to be more frequent in H. contortus populations from herds that have imported goats from abroad, count over 100 goats, or practice multiple dewormings per year. Bearing this in mind, Polish farmers should be encouraged to switch from routine use of BZ to more integrated and sustainable methods of GIN control.
