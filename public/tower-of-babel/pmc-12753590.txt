Carbapenem resistance in enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis isolated from colonoscopy candidates
Authors: Narges Nasoohian, Ebrahim Fallahzadeh, Sahar Sabour, Saeed Soleiman-Meigooni, Taher Azimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study aimed to determine the carbapenem resistance among Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) isolated from patients candidates to colonoscopy in Shiraz, Iran. A total of 201 stool samples were gathered from 201 patients. The detection of B. fragilis group, B. fragilis, and ETBF was performed using a PCR assay targeting bfg, 16S rRNA, and bft genes, respectively. The enterotoxin isotype-encoding genes (bft-1, bft-2, and bft-3) were detected in ETBF using a PCR assay. Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of ETBF against imipenem and meropenem was determined using the Liofilchem® MIC Test Strips. The Ana-CIM assay was used for the phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production in ETBF strains. The presence of the cfiA and insertion sequence (IS) encoding genes (IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, IS942) was determined using a PCR assay. Overall, 71.64%, 33.83%, and 20.4% of patients were positive for B. fragilis group, B. fragilis, and ETBF, respectively. 28 (68.3%) tested positive for the bft-1 gene, whereas only one isolate (2.4%) contained the bft-3 gene. 80% (n = 4/5) of patients with polyp in sigmoid colon + colorectal polyp and 72.7% (n = 8/11) of patients with hemorrhoids were positive to ETBF. 2.4% and 48.8% of ETBF isolates were resistant to imipenem and meropenem, respectively. Out of the 41 ETBF isolates, 28 (68.3%) were positive for carbapenemase production. 46.3% (n = 18/41) of ETBF were positive for the cfiA gene. 27 isolates (96.4%) harbored the IS1187 gene. Resistance rates of ETBF to meropenem were high. Most patients with a polyp in the sigmoid colon, colorectal polyp, and hemorrhoids were positive for ETBF.Keywords: Bacteroides fragilis, Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, ETBF, Carbapenem resistance, Colonoscopy, Iran

Introduction

Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis), an anaerobic microorganism with a Gram-negative cell structure, forms a vital part of the microbial community in the human intestinal tract (Kierzkowska et al. 2023). As an opportunistic pathogen, B. fragilis can cause health issues such as abscesses and bloodstream infections (Vu et al. 2021). Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) is known for producing B. fragilis toxin (BFT), which is linked to various health problems, including inflammatory diarrhoea, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal cancer (Zamani et al. 2020). The presence of ETBF can damage the epithelial cells of the intestinal lining, compromising the protective barrier and increasing inflammation within the gastrointestinal tract (Yang et al. 2024). The ability of ETBF strains to produce toxins is associated with the presence of the bft gene (Hwang et al. 2020). BFT is a zinc metalloprotease encoded by the bft gene on a specific pathogenicity island known as the B. fragilis pathogenicity island (BfPAI) (Casterline et al. 2017). The BFT toxin is classified into three subtypes: BFT1, BFT2, and BFT3, with BFT1 being the most common in human populations (Guo et al. 2023). While these subtypes are highly similar, they differ in their DNA sequences, especially in the region coding for the mature toxin (Haghi et al. 2019).

Carbapenems play a crucial role as antimicrobials in managing infections attributed to ETBF, owing to their extensive efficacy against anaerobic bacterial strains. Due to their potency against resistant strains, they represent a crucial treatment option when other antibiotics are ineffective (Jha et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the prevalence of carbapenem resistance in B. fragilis is on the rise (Jha et al. 2023; Nagy et al. 2011). The primary mechanism through which ETBF exhibits resistance to carbapenems is the synthesis of metallo-β-lactamases. These enzymes are generally encoded by the cfiA gene (Jeverica et al. 2019). The genotypic detection of carbapenem resistance in bacterial species is achieved through the identification of the cfiA gene. The activation of the cfiA gene is often initiated by an upstream insertion sequence (IS), which enhances the expression of enzymes and ultimately leads to antibiotic resistance (Jha et al. 2023). Phenotypic detection of carbapenem resistance in B. fragilis is accomplished using methodologies such as the Carba NP assay and the Anaerobic Carbapenem Inactivation Method (Ana-CIM) (Jha et al. 2023; Eberly et al.). These diagnostic techniques are employed to elucidate the presence of carbapenemase enzymes, predominantly encoded by the cfiA gene, which confer resistance to carbapenems in this anaerobic species (Jha et al. 2023). In the present study, we aimed to determine the phenotypic and genotypic carbapenem resistance among ETBF strains isolated from colonoscopy candidates in Shiraz, Iran.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The present research was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1403.395).

Study design and participants

The present cross-sectional study was performed from July 2024 to March 2025 on 201 colonoscopy candidates at Shahid Motahari Clinic in Shiraz, Iran. All included patients were selected based on the physician’s prescription. At the first step, the aims of the research were clarified to all patients, and consent was acquired from all individuals. Demographic and clinical information was gathered through a questionnaire, which included details such as sex, age, blood types, COVID-19 history, records of pre-existing conditions, smoking habits, instances of anal bleeding, and a history of fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, as well as antibiotic use in the two weeks before sampling. Following the colonoscopy procedure, the findings from the colonoscopy were analysed by both hepatologists and gastroenterologists.

Sample collection and processing

Overall, a total of 201 unique stool samples were gathered from 201 patients in specific stool containers and were immediately transported to the microbiology laboratory at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, located in Shiraz, Iran, within a two-hour timeframe. At the first step, all stool samples were cultured on Bacteroides Bile Esculin (BBE) agar (Merck Co., Germany) containing gentamicin (Sigma Co.) and incubated at 37˚C for 24 to 48 h in an anaerobic atmosphere using the Anoxomat jar system (MART Microbiology B.V., the Netherlands) with a gas mixture of 80% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen, and 10% carbon dioxide (CO2). Following the incubation period, the presence of black colonies on the BBE medium was interpreted as a positive indication for the B. fragilis group. Ultimately, Gram staining was conducted on all the black colonies, leading to the identification of Gram-negative coccobacilli.

DNA extraction and molecular detection of B.fragilis

All colonies classified as B. fragilis via phenotypic methods were grown on Brucella blood agar (Merck Co., Germany) and incubated anaerobically at a temperature of 37 °C for 48 h. DNA was extracted from the colonies using a DNA extraction Mini kit (Favorgen, Taiwan) in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed utilising a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and the DNA samples obtained were preserved at − 70 °C for future use.

The final detection of B. fragilis group and B. fragilis was performed using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) assay targeted bfg and 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) genes, respectively. The sequences of primers used in the PCR reactions are shown in Table 1. PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 25 µl containing 11 µl of super PCR master mix 2X (Yekta Tajhiz Co.), 1 µl of each forward and reverse primers, 4 µl of template DNA, and 8 µl of nuclease-free distilled water. Amplification was performed using an Eppendorf thermocycler (Manheim, Germany) under the specified conditions: an initial denaturation phase at 95 °C for 5 min, succeeded by 35 cycles comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, annealing at 52 °C for 45 s (for both genes), and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, culminating in a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. Finally, all PCR products were analysed by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel.

B. fragilis toxin (BFT) and bft gene subtypes

Detection of the bft gene encoding B. fragilis toxin (BFT) was performed using a PCR assay. The PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 25 μl, which comprised 11 μl of Super PCR master mix 2X (Yekta Tajhiz Co., Iran), 8.5 μl of double-distilled water, 4 μl of DNA template, and 0.75 μl of both forward and reverse primers. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 5 min (initial denaturation); 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min (denaturation), 58 °C for 45 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 1 min (extension); and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

The enterotoxin isotype-encoding genes (bft-1, bft-2, and bft-3) were detected in ETBF (bft gene-positive isolates) using a PCR assay. The sequences of primers used in the PCR assay are shown in Table 1. The PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 25 µl containing 11 µl Super PCR master mix 2X (Yekta Tajhiz Co., Iran), 1 µl of each forward and reverse primer, 4 µl of extracted DNA, and 8 µl double-distilled water. Reaction was carried out the following conditions: initial denaturation (94 °C, 5 min); 36 cycles of denaturation (94 °C, 45 s), annealing (52 °C for bft-1 gene, 50.5 °C for bft-2 gene, and 53.5 °C for bft-3 gene for 30 s), and extension (72 °C for 45 s); followed by a final extension step (72 °C for 5 min). Finally, 1.5% agarose gel was prepared and stained with DNA safe stain (Yekta Tajhiz Co, Iran). All PCR products were analysed by electrophoresis under UV light.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of ETBF against imipenem and meropenem was determined using the Liofilchem® MIC Test Strips. Briefly, bacterial colonies were suspended in Brucella broth medium (Merck Co., Germany) to achieve a turbidity equivalent to a 1.0 McFarland standard (3 × 108 CFU/ml). The prepared suspension was then inoculated onto Brucella blood agar (Merck Co., Germany) supplemented with 10% sheep blood, hemin (5 mg/l), and vitamin K (1 mg/l). Liofilchem® MIC Test Strips were applied to the inoculated plates, and all plates were incubated at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions for 48 h. The susceptibility of isolates and the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antibiotics were determined according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, 2023).

Ana-CIM test

Anaerobic Carbapenem Inactivation Method (Ana-CIM) was used for phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production in ETBF isolates (Eberly et al. 2022). Briefly, ETBF isolates were subcultured on Brucella blood agar (Merck Co., Germany) supplemented with 10% sheep blood, hemin (5 mg/l), and vitamin K (1 mg/l). At the next step, a 10 mg meropenem disc (Mast Co., England) was placed in 5 ml of Brucella broth (Merk Co., Germany) for 15 min at room temperature to allow antibiotic diffusion. Bacterial colonies were then suspended in Brucella broth medium (Merck Co., Germany) containing a meropenem disk (MAST Co., England) to achieve a turbidity equivalent to a 1.0 McFarland standard (3 × 108 CFU/ml). Suspension was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 6 h. Following incubation, the meropenem discs were carefully transferred using a sterile 10 µl loop on Mueller–Hinton agar (Merck Co, Germany) plates that had been pre-inoculated with Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (0.5 McFarland standard). All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Finally, the inhibition zone diameters were measured, and results were interpreted based on the previously published study (Eberly et al. 2022).

Detection of the cfiA-mediated carbapenem resistance gene

The presence of the cfiA-mediated carbapenem resistance gene was determined using a PCR assay (Table 1). PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 25 µl containing: 11 µl Super PCR master mix 2X (Yekta Tajhiz Co, Iran), 1 µl of each forward and reverse primers, 4 µl DNA template, and 8 µl double-distilled water. The amplification was done in an Eppendorf thermocycler (Manheim, Germany) with the following condition: 94 °C for 5 min (initial denaturation); followed by 36 cycles including 94 °C for 45 s (denaturation), annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. All PCR products were screened on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Detection of insertion sequence-encoding genes

The B. fragilis isolates that tested positive for the cfiA gene were also assessed for the presence of insertion sequence (IS) encoding genes (IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, IS942) through a PCR assay. The PCR primers are listed in Table 1. The PCR mixture was prepared in a total volume of 25 μl containing 12 μl of super PCR master mix 2X (Yekta Tajhiz Co, Iran), 4 μl of template DNA, 1 μl of each forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water to a final volume. The reaction was performed in a thermocycler under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s (for IS1186, IS1187, IS1188) and 52 °C for 30 s for IS942, extension at 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Finally, 1.5% agarose gel was prepared and stained with DNA safe stain (Yekta Taghiz Azma, Iran). The PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel, and DNA fragments were visualised under UV light.

Statistical analysis

Data were included in a statistical package SPSS v.23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) file and were analysed using the Chi-Square test. A P-value of < 0.05 was set as significant.

Results

Clinical and demographic variables of patients

In total, 201 stool samples were collected throughout a six-month duration. Table 2 presents the clinical and demographic data regarding the patients. Among the patients, 56.2% (n = 113) were female. Our analysis revealed that 57.7% (n = 116/201) of the participants were aged over 50 years. The predominant blood type identified was O positive, accounting for 24.4% of the population. The main reasons for performing a colonoscopy were rectal bleeding (28.9%), followed by chronic constipation (17.4%), abdominal pain (7.0%), and positive fecal occult blood test (6.5%). Rectal bleeding, occurring in 17.4% of cases, was the clinical symptom most commonly reported by patients. The most prevalent colonoscopy findings among patients were colorectal polyps, which occurred in 17.9% of cases, and hemorrhoids, which were present in 13.9%.

Baseline characteristics and frequency of B. fragilis, ETBF, bft-1 and cfiA genes

Phenotypic and genotypic detection of B. fragilis

Based on the black colony formation on BBE agar medium and microscopic morphology in Gram staining, the B. fragilis group was detected in 80.1% (n = 161/201) of samples. The PCR results indicated that 71.6% (n = 144/161) of the isolates were positive for the bfg gene, thus validating their classification within the B. fragilis group. Among the 144 bfg gene-positive isolates, 68 isolates (47.2%) were also found to be positive for the 16S rRNA gene, thereby confirming their classification as B. fragilis species (Fig. 1).

Frequency of Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis

Out of the 68 confirmed isolates of B. fragilis, 41 isolates, which account for 60.3%, tested positive for the bft gene and are classified as ETBF. Among the 41 ETBF strains, 28 (68.3%) tested positive for the bft-1 gene, whereas only one isolate (2.4%) contained the bft-3 gene. No samples showed the presence of the bft-2 gene. Table 2 presents the association between the frequency of B. fragilis and ETBF strains in relation to demographic and clinical variables. Although no statistically significant relationship was found (p > 0.05), several descriptive trends were observed. The prevalence of ETBF strains was greater in patients who were 50 years old or younger (67.6%), while non-toxigenic B. fragilis showed a slightly higher prevalence in those over 50 years (54.0%). ETBF was frequently isolated from patients with A⁻ (66.7%) and B⁺ (55.6%) blood types. Moreover, ETBF had the highest frequency among patients with hypertension (75.0%), diabetes (77.8%), and a history of antibiotic use (71.4%). The colonoscopy findings demonstrated that conditions like extensive colitis, melanosis coli, and diverticulosis with hemorrhoids were correlated with a 100% detection rate for both B. fragilis and ETBF.

Carbapenem resistance in ETBF

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of 41 ETBF isolates was evaluated through the Liofilchem® MIC Test Strips for two carbapenems: imipenem and meropenem (Table 3). The results demonstrated that 97.6% (n = 40/41) of ETBF isolates were susceptible, while 2.4% (n = 1/41) were resistant (9 isolates showed intermediate susceptibility) to imipenem (MIC range: 0.38 µg/ ml – 16 µg/ ml). In contrast, only 51.2% of isolates were susceptible to meropenem, while 48.8% exhibited resistance (MIC range: 0.125 µg/ ml – 0.38 µg/ ml).

Ana-CIM test

The Ana-CIM was used to assess carbapenemase production in 41 ETBF isolates (Table 3). This method detects enzyme-mediated inactivation of meropenem based on the diameter of the inhibition zone: < 8 mm, 9–14 mm, and ≥ 15 mm indicate positive, intermediate, and negative results, respectively. Out of the 41 ETBF isolates, 28 (68.3%) were positive for carbapenemase production. In positive cases, although inhibition zones up to 25 mm were noted (mean: 20.9 mm), bacterial growth was visible within the zones (Fig. 2). In contrast, the negative isolates exhibited distinct zones measuring between 15 and 26 mm (average: 22.0 mm), with no growth observed. 39.3% and 76.9% of Ana-CIM-positive and negative ETBF strains were susceptible to meropenem, respectively. In contrast, the obtained data revealed that 67.9% and 92.3% of Ana-CIM-positive and negative isolates were susceptible to meropenem, respectively. Only one Ana-CIM positive isolate (3.6%) was found to be resistant to imipenem.

Prevalence of cfiA and insertion sequence-encoding genes

Molecular analysis showed that 41.2% (n = 28/68) and 46.3% (n = 18/41) of B. fragilis and ETBF strains carried the cfiA gene, respectively (Table 3). Our analyses revealed that 50.0% and 38.5% of Ana-CIM-positive and negative ETBF strains were positive for the cfiA gene, respectively. The present study investigated the presence and frequency of four insertion sequences (IS942, IS1186, IS1187, and IS1188) among 28 cfiA-positive B. fragilis isolates. Results showed that out of 28 cfiA-positive B. fragilis isolates, 27 isolates (96.4%) harbored the IS1187 gene, while none of the isolates carried IS942, IS1186, or IS1188 genes. Our analyses revealed that 93.3% (n = 14/15) of imipenem susceptible isolates were positive for IS1187. In contrast, 9 of the 18 IS1187-positive isolates (50%) were susceptible to meropenem.

Relationship between colonoscopy results and ETBF isolates

The relationship between colonoscopy results and the presence of B. fragilis, ETBF isolates, toxin subtypes and cfiA gene is shown in Table 4. Results showed that 46.2% of patients with colorectal polyps and 38% of patients with normal colonoscopy results were positive for B. fragilis isolates. In contrast, 80% (n = 4/5) of patients with a polyp in the sigmoid colon + colorectal polyp and 72.7% (n = 8/11) of patients with hemorrhoids were positive to ETBF. Moreover, 80% (n = 4/5) of B. fragilis isolated from patients with polyps in the sigmoid colon + colorectal polyps were positive for the cfiA gene.

Dicussion

As resistance to commonly prescribed antibiotics continues to increase, infections caused by B. fragilis must be treated in accordance with the results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Sood et al. 2022). Carbapenems are classified as highly effective antibiotics and are regarded as the drug of choice for the treatment of severe and persistent infections caused by the B. fragilis (Kierzkowska et al. 2023). B. fragilis clinical isolates might show resistance to carbapenems, considered the last chance β-lactam antibiotic; therefore, it is important to monitor resistance closely (Nagy et al. 2011). In the present study, we aimed to survey the phenotypic and genotypic carbapenem resistance among ETBF strains isolated from patients candidates to colonoscopy in Shiraz, Iran.

Our results showed that 33.83% of patients were positive for B. fragilis, respectively. Moreover, we revealed that 60.3% of B. fragilis isolates were positive for the bft gene and are classified as ETBF. ETBF strains are closely linked to the incidence of inflammatory bowel disease, as well as colitis-associated colorectal cancer. ETBF encodes a particular virulence factor, the 21 kDa B. fragilis toxin, which cleaves E-cadherin on the host epithelium, influences epithelial permeability, and induces intestinal inflammation. Moreover, enterotoxin leads to the activation of interleukin 17 (IL-17)-dependent inflammatory pathways, contributing to the onset of distal CRC (Sabour et al. 2025). The data obtained from the present study revealed that 80% of patients with a polyp in the sigmoid colon + colorectal polyp and 72.7% of patients with hemorrhoids were positive to ETBF. Numerous studies have examined the prevalence of ETBF across various groups of individuals. Khodaverdi et al. surveyed the prevalence of ETBF strains in paraffin-embedded tissues of Iranian colorectal cancer patients. They showed that 45% of samples were positive for ETBF. Moreover, they revealed that the frequency of ETBF strains was high in tumor samples compared to the control group (Khodaverdi et al. 2021). In another study, Haghi et al. investigated the association between ETBF with CRC. They showed that the frequency of ETBF in CRC cases was significantly higher than in controls (Haghi et al. 2019). Zamani et al. reported that 51.4% of B. fragilis isolates from patients with ulcerative colitis were positive for bft genes. They indicated that ETBF is linked to the onset of ulcerative colitis and acts as a causative factor for the emergence of diarrhea in these individuals (Zamani et al. 2017). Ignacio et al. revealed that 100% and 4.7% of stool samples collected from children were positive for B. fragilis and ETBF, respectively (Ignacio et al. 2015). Moreover, Toprak et al. showed that the bft gene was detected in stool samples of 38% of CRC patients (Ulger Toprak et al. 2006). The differences in the frequency of B. fragilis and ETBF strains could be due to the geographic region, diversity of sample type, sample size and applied detection methods.

Our findings demonstrated that 68.3% bft positive B. fragilis were positive for the bft-1 gene, whereas only one isolate (2.4%) contained the bft-3 gene. No samples showed the presence of the bft-2 gene. Kouhsari et al. from Iran revealed that among ETBF, 52.4% harbored bft-1, 38% harbored bft-2 isotypes, and 9.5% harbored bft-3 isotype (Kouhsari et al. 2019). In contrast, Haghi et al. reported that the bft-2 isotype was detected with higher frequency among CRC patients than healthy controls (Haghi et al. 2019).

According to the EUCAST criteria, the intermediate definition means isolates that are sensitive with increased antibiotic exposure. Phenotypic results revealed that 2.4% and 48.8% of ETBF strains were resistant to imipenem and meropenem, respectively. Our findings revealed that imipenem has good activity against anaerobes. In previously published studies, Jamal et al. (Jamal et al. 2020), Wang et al. (Wang et al. 2020), and Wybo et al. (Wybo et al. 2011) reported that 17%, 19% and 10% of B. fragilis isolates were resistant to meropenem, respectively. In contrast, Vishwanath et al. stated that 100% of B. fragilis isolates were susceptible to meropenem (Vishwanath et al. 2019). In another studies, Kierzkowska et al. from Poland (Kierzkowska et al. 2023) and Jha et al. from India (Jha et al. 2023) revealed that 5.2% and 15% of B. fragilis isolates were phenotypically resistant to meropenem.

Research conducted previously has suggested that phenotypic techniques, like the Ana CIM and Carbapenemase Nordmann-Poirel (Carba NP) tests, are a reliable alternative to genetic methods for identifying the cfiA gene in Bacteroides spp. It is also suggested that the findings from the Ana CIM and Carba NP tests, in combination with the antibiogram, allow for the prediction of therapeutic effectiveness (Kierzkowska et al. 2023; Eberly et al. 2022). In the present study, the results of the Ana CIM test revealed that 68.3% of ETBF strains were positive for carbapenemase production. Similarly, Eberly et al. from the USA indicated that the Ana-CIM accurately detected carbapenem resistance in 87% of B. fragilis isolates (Eberly et al. 2022). Both results suggested that Ana-CIM can be efficiently deployed in clinical laboratory environments at a reduced cost for identifying carbapenemase-mediated resistance in B. fragilis.

The resistance of B. fragilis to carbapenems is typically attributed to the expression of the class B metallo-beta-lactamase, which is encoded by the cfiA gene located on the chromosome. The cfiA gene encodes a zinc metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) classified under Ambler class B, which is capable of hydrolysing most β-lactams, including cephamycins and carbapenems (Rong, Rodloff, and Stingu 2021; Jeverica et al. 2019). A literature review has indicated that the proportion of B. fragilis that possess the cfiA gene varies globally, with reported rates between 7.6% and 38.9% (Gao Qiong et al. 2019; Jeverica et al. 2019; Kawamoto et al. 2021; Gupta et al. 2021). However, we showed that 41.2% and 46.3% of B. fragilis and ETBF strains carried the cfiA gene, respectively.

The expression of the cfiA gene may be at different levels, contingent upon the presence of IS situated upstream of cfiA (Kierzkowska et al. 2023). In B. fragilis, the insertion sequences IS612, IS613, IS614, IS615, IS616, IS942, IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, and IS4351 have been associated with cfiA, exhibiting different levels of promotion efficiency (Sóki 2013; Litterio et al. 2017). In the present study, 96.4% of cfiA-positive B. fragilis isolates harbored the IS1187 gene, while none of the isolates carried IS942, IS1186, or IS1188 genes. Similarly, Kierzkowska et al. evaluated the phenotypic and genotypic carbapenem resistance in B. fragilis clinical strains. They showed that among four IS encoding genes (IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, IS942), the presence of IS1186 was detected in one B. fragilis strain. No other IS (IS1187, IS1188, IS942) were detected in their study (Kierzkowska et al. 2023). In another study, Kierzkowska revealed that all cfiA-positive B.fragilis isolates were negative to IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, and IS942 (Kierzkowska et al. 2019). Toprak et al. from Turkey reported thet among Sixty-six nonduplicate Bacteroides clinical isolates, 21 (32%) of strains were positive to IS1187, five of which were also cfiA gene positive and resistant to carbapenems (Toprak et al. 2012).

The current research has several significant limitations: (1) although we screened isolates for insertion sequences (IS1186, IS1187, IS1188, IS942), due to budget constraints, we were unable to show their localization upstream of the cfiA gene; (2) due to the small sample size, we could not establish a definitive correlation between the patients’ clinical conditions and the presence of ETBF; (3) unfortunately, biochemical methods or MALDI-TOF MS were not available for the detection of bacteria. Therefore, the initial detection of the B. fragilis group was performed using phenotypic methods. According to the unreliability of phenotypic-based methods, we utilised genome-based methods for the detection and final identification of B. fragilis isolates.

In conclusion, the present study examined the prevalence and antimicrobial resistance of B. fragilis isolated from patients candidates to colonoscopy. According to the EUCAST breakpoint, the resistance rates of ETBF to meropenem and imipenem (resistance and intermediate isolates) were high. Moreover, we revealed that most patients with a polyp in the sigmoid colon, colorectal polyp and hemorrhoids were positive for ETBF. Therefore, detection of ETBF or bft-1 isotype may be a helpful marker of colorectal cancer.
