Traumatic Compression of a Percutaneous Valve-in-Valve Right Ventricle–to–Pulmonary Artery Conduit in a Young Athlete
Authors: Suniva Kansakar, Ben Holloway, Lucy Cowie, Sarah Bowater, Lucy Hudsmith, Sayqa Arif, Andrew Constantine, William Moody, Paul Clift, Nada Al-Sakini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise is beneficial in adult congenital heart disease, but patients with prosthetic materials, including valve-in-valve right ventricle–to–pulmonary artery (RV-PA) conduits, require tailored advice regarding high-impact sports.Case SummaryA 19-year-old man with repaired pulmonary atresia, ventricular septal defect, and valve-in-valve RV-PA conduit placement developed exertional dyspnea after blunt chest trauma during competitive ice hockey. Imaging revealed significant prosthetic conduit stenosis due to stent compression. He underwent urgent surgical conduit replacement, with full recovery.DiscussionThis case illustrates a rare complication of blunt trauma leading to prosthetic conduit failure. It highlights the need for dynamic imaging and reinforces guideline-based counseling to avoid collision sports in those with valve-in-valve RV-PA conduits. Holistic approaches including shared decision-making regarding exercise prescription and regular reassessment is essential in managing exercise safely in patients with adult congenital heart disease.Take-Home MessageTrauma threatens anterior prosthetic valves, with timely dedicated imaging and personalized exercise prescription being essential.Key words: adult congenital heart disease, cardiac surgery, sports injury, valve-in-valve implantation

A 19-year-old man with a history of repaired pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect presented after a heavy-impact blow to his chest while playing national-league ice hockey, after collision with another player led to a near-syncopal response. In the following days, he experienced left-sided chest pain and shortness of breath (SOB) on exertion, causing him to call the ACHD (adult congenital heart disease) Helpline. He was advised to attend the accident and emergency department at his nearest district general hospital.

Our patient had an extensive cardiothoracic history, detailed in Figure 1. This included a thoracotomy for Blalock-Taussig shunt during the neonatal period, 2 sternotomies (infancy and 2013), and 1 percutaneous valve-in-valve right ventricle–to–pulmonary artery (RV-PA) conduit (2022).

Presenting Symptoms

At initial presentation, the patient's primary complaint was SOB on exertion after a high-impact compound collision injury leading immediately to significant presyncopal symptoms. The injury was sustained during competitive ice hockey, where he had been subjected to a direct lateral shoulder impact (body check) from an opposing player while in a high-velocity forward motion. On examination, he was hemodynamically stable and euvolemic. The previously soft systolic grade 2/6 murmur had become more pronounced, progressing to a harsh grade 3-4/6 murmur.

Investigations

Initial chest x-ray (Figure 4A) conducted at his local district general hospital showed clear lung fields, with no changes being identified compared with previous x-rays. The initial electrocardiogram was unchanged compared to previous (Figure 3B). Because SOB persisted, focused transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was carried out, which showed a higher velocity gradient through the valve-in-valve RV-PA conduit compared with previous TTE. The patient was immediately referred to his ACHD tertiary care center and was seen urgently in the ACHD clinic.

As the patient's background, clinical history, and initial imaging raised a high level of suspicion that the ice hockey collision had elevated the valve-in-valve RV-PA conduit velocity, he was at risk of significant deterioration. Urgent TTE by the ACHD team showed significant pulmonary valve stenosis. There was significant increment in the tricuspid valve regurgitant velocity to 4.6 m/s (Figure 4B) as well as color-flow turbulence (Figure 4C, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). There was an abnormal Melody valve contour and substantial turbulence on color Doppler, with a Vmax of 6 m/s (mean gradient: 92 mm Hg) across the percutaneous valve, with critical stenosis (Figure 4D, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). This prompted further imaging. Subsequent computed tomography pulmonary angiography showed marked new compression of the proximal valve-in-valve RV-PA conduit stent, causing significant stenosis (Figure 4E).

Management

Given the significant trauma-induced distortion caused to the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), the patient underwent urgent third-time sternotomy for RV-PA conduit replacement. The mediastinum was accessed via previous median sternotomy sites. Moderate adhesions were found within the mediastinum, the conduit was heavily calcified, and the stent had a narrowed waist at the valvar level. The valved conduit was replaced with a 27-mm Magna-Ease valve (Edwards Lifesciences) in a 30-mm Gelweave graft (Terumo).

Postprocedural transesophageal echocardiography showed a stable conduit with no leaks. There was considerable improvement to right ventricular function. Tricuspid regurgitation was reduced to mild, with a velocity of 3 m/s. Laminar flow was restored through the RVOT, and there was no pulmonary valve regurgitation.

Outcome

The procedure was uncomplicated, with an uneventful postoperative recovery.

A well-functioning conduit was restored, and the patient was able to gradually return to preincident fitness. He was advised to cease playing ice hockey to prevent the reoccurrence of such serious injury, thus prematurely ending his sports career. He returned for routine annual ACHD follow-up, with stable results.

Discussion

Regular exercise in patients with surgically repaired CHD is generally encouraged given its substantial benefits in improving cardiovascular fitness, general health, and psychological well-being.1, 2, 3 However, engagement in high-risk or collision sports requires specific consideration, particularly for individuals with prosthetic cardiac material.1,2,4 A careful, personalized consideration must be taken in approaching ACHD patients who participate in sport.1 A “tailored to functional capacity” approach must be taken to account for complexities involved in the physiological impacts of exercise.3

The European Society of Cardiology provides detailed guidance on exercise recommendations in patients with cardiovascular disease, categorizing sports based on their endurance and strength components (Figure 5). Recommendations are stratified based on physiological criteria (Figure 6).4 Patients with moderate to severe CHD, including those with prosthetic RV-PA conduits, are generally advised to limit participation to moderate-intensity activities (target heart rate: 60% to 75% of maximum, Borg rating of perceived exercise: 13-14).4 Collision sports, including ice hockey, exceed these parameters and are typically discouraged because of their high dynamic and traumatic potential.4,5

Our case, where a young competitive athlete with an anteriorly located valve-in-valve percutaneous pulmonary conduit developed severe RVOT obstruction after blunt chest trauma, exemplifies the structural vulnerability of anteriorly located prosthetic valves to external mechanical forces. Hormann et al6 reported a similar case of a sporting injury leading to trauma-induced damage of a percutaneous Melody valve, highlighting the importance of tailored multimodality imaging strategies when evaluating patients with anteriorly located conduit repairs and a history of participation in high-impact sports. Critical valve stenosis involving high-impact mechanisms occurred in both cases, with imaging revealing valve-in-valve conduit compression and high transvalvular gradients. Imaging facilitated urgent surgical conduit replacement before the development of hemodynamic deterioration.

However, it must be noted that Hormann et al6 referred to an early-generation, single-frame Melody valve. First-generation Melody valves implanted in non-prestented conduits have been consistently associated with high rates of stent fracture,7, 8, 9 which has been significantly mitigated by the introduction of prestenting.10 Risk-factor analysis conducted by McElhinney et al7 demonstrated that valve-in-valve implantation and prestenting conduits were protective against spontaneous frame fractures by providing protected landing zones and reduction in mechanical fatigue.

Our case involved prestenting using a 23 × 45 mm Cheatham Platinum stent (NuMed) (Videos 4 to 6), in which a valve-in-valve 22-mm Melody percutaneous pulmonary valve (Medtronic) was deployed (Videos 7 to 9). This led to a fully patent RVOT with no regurgitation (Video 8). Given the low fracture rate reported in valve-in-valve Melody implants, which are protected within rigid surgical structures,11,12 spontaneous stent fracture was an unlikely mechanism of critical conduit stenosis. Additionally, the patient's hemodynamic stability, continued participation in competitive sport until the time of blunt chest trauma, and the sudden-onset SOB immediately after the impact strongly support a trauma-induced crush injury, transmitting force through the prestent and causing Melody valve deformation.

Notably, our patient had resumed high-level competitive sport after multiple surgical and percutaneous interventions, without apparent restriction beyond 6 weeks postimplantation. However, based on physiological parameters in accordance with European Society of Cardiology guidelines (detailed in Figure 6), he should have been advised low/moderate-intensity sports. This reflects data in which clinician-advised activity levels are inconsistent with guideline-recommended thresholds.5 Clinical recommendations must carefully consider the effects of high-impact/high-intensity sports in a population at risk of stent fracture/valve dysfunction. However, the literature relating to the impact of high-intensity or contact sports on patients with prosthetic valves is very limited.

The psychosocial impacts of sport restriction are equally important. ACHD patients reported significant emotional and psychological challenges when limited in their activity, particularly participation in team sports.13 Dean et al5 demonstrated that quality of life correlates with increased physical activity, with highest quality scores being in patients participating in competitive sports. Thus, while safety remains paramount, a balanced, patient-centered approach that includes psychological well-being is essential in exercise counseling. Instigating voluntary sports restrictions can be seen as a catastrophic event for professional athletes,14 and therefore a sensitive approach must be taken toward such implementations. Patients must be fully informed of the risks of sports participation before making decisions to participate in competitive sports.

Shared decision-making, early patient education (ideally during adolescence), and the setting of realistic expectations are essential for effective long-term activity planning in ACHD.1 For high-risk patients with strong athletic identities, clinicians must provide enhanced safety-netting strategies: education on red-flag symptoms, clear escalation pathways after injuries, and regular follow-up with access to advanced imaging.

This case highlights not only an unusual mechanism of prosthetic valve failure, but also the broader need for multidisciplinary management of exercise in ACHD patients, combining cardiac physiology, psychology, and patient preference to optimize outcomes.

Conclusions

This report presents a rare but significant case of trauma-induced valve-in-valve conduit failure in a patient with an RV-PA Melody valve-in-valve, highlighting the risks of collision sports in individuals with prosthetic material. Timely imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosing mechanical complications that static studies may overlook. Clinicians should provide sport-specific guidance early with regular re-evaluation, prioritizing both safety and psychological health. Patients must be fully educated of risks to be able to make informed decisions.

Take-Home Messages

•In patients with anteriorly located prosthetic valves, high-impact trauma should prompt strong suspicion of valvular damage, and dynamic imaging—particularly Doppler—may be crucial for accurate postinjury assessment.
