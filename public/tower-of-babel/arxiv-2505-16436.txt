On QFT in Klein space
Bin Chen, Zezhou Hu, Xin-cheng Mao

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the quantum field theory in Klein space that has two time directions. To study the canonical quantization, we select the “length of time” q q as the evolution direction of the system. In our novel construction, some additional modes beyond the plane wave modes are crucial in the canonical quantization and the later derivation of the LSZ reduction formula. We also derive the free two-point function by using Wick contraction in the canonical quantization formalism. Moreover, we introduce the path-integral formalism in which we can redefine the vacuum states and rederive the correlation functions. We show that all the results in the Klein space derived in our novel approach match those obtained via analytical continuation from the Minkowski spacetime.

2
Basics of Klein space

In this section, we briefly introduce the fundamental aspects of Klein space
\mathbb{K}^{2,2}
, including its geometric structure, conformal boundaries, and dual space (known as the momentum space). Let us start from its Penrose diagram.

In Cartesian coordinates, the flat metric of
\mathbb{K}^{2,2}
takes the form:

\text{d}s^{2}=-(\text{d}x^{0})^{2}-(\text{d}x^{1})^{2}+(\text{d}x^{2})^{2}+(\text{d}x^{3})^{2}.

(1)

The Klein space can be decomposed into two orthogonal 2D planes, each of which can be parameterized using polar coordinates

(x^{0},x^{1})=q(\cos\psi,\sin\psi),\qquad(x^{2},x^{3})=r(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi),

(2)

such that the metric can be rewritten as

\text{d}s^{2}=-\text{d}q^{2}-q^{2}\text{d}\psi^{2}+\text{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\text{d}\varphi^{2}.

(3)

Then, with the following coordinate transformations

Q=\arctan(q+r)+\arctan(q-r),\quad R=\arctan(q+r)-\arctan(q-r),

(4)

the metric can be rewritten as

\text{d}s^{2}=\Omega^{-2}(-\text{d}Q^{2}+\text{d}R^{2}-\sin^{2}Q\text{d}\psi^{2}+\sin^{2}R\text{d}\varphi^{2}),

(5)

where the conformal factor is defined as

\Omega=2\left|\cos\left(\frac{R+Q}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{R-Q}{2}\right)\right|.

(6)

The infinite coordinate ranges
q,r\in[0,\infty)
are conformally mapped to finite ranges
Q,R\in[0,\pi)
. The conformal boundary emerges at the singularity of
\Omega^{-1}
, with null infinity
\mathscr{I}
located at
Q+R=\pi
. The endpoints
(Q,R)=(0,\pi)
and
(Q,R)=(\pi,0)
correspond to the spacelike infinity
i_{0}
and timelike infinity
i^{\prime}
, respectively.

Figure 1:
The Penrose diagram of Klein space.

The Penrose diagram can thus be depicted in the
(Q,R)
coordinates, as shown in Fig.
1
. In this diagram, the black dashed line and the yellow wavy line (45°) denote the worldlines of massive and massless particles, respectively. Unlike the Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
, which has two null boundaries, the Klein space possesses only a single null infinity. This structural difference necessitates replacing the conventional S-matrix with an S-vector to describe scattering processes, following the discussion in
Witten:2001kn
.

The Klein space admits a natural foliation by
q=
constant hypersurfaces, represented by red curves in Fig
1
, where the
q=0
limit approaches the hypersurface
Q=0
and
q\rightarrow\infty
limit converges to null infinity
\mathscr{I}
. For our subsequent analysis, we employ polar coordinates for the temporal dimensions only while maintaining Cartesian coordinates for the spatial dimensions. This hybrid coordinate system proves particularly advantageous when examining the
q=
constant hypersurface. Under this choice, the metric takes the form

\text{d}s^{2}=-\text{d}q^{2}-q^{2}\text{d}\psi^{2}+(\text{d}x^{2})^{2}+(\text{d}x^{3})^{2}.

(7)

The momentum space, as a dual space of Klein space, inherits the same geometric structure as
\mathbb{K}^{2,2}
. Consequently, we can parameterize the 4-momentum using analogous coordinates:

p^{\mu}=(p^{0},p^{1},p^{2},p^{3})=(\omega\cos\theta,\omega\sin\theta,\vec{p})\,,\quad\vec{p}=(p^{2},p^{3})\,,\quad\omega>0.

(8)

Then the
SO(2,2)
-invariant momentum measure can be defined in the similar manner as that in Minkowski space

\int\text{d}^{4}p\delta(p^{2}+m^{2})=\int\omega\text{d}\omega\text{d}\theta\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}\delta(-\omega^{2}+\omega_{\vec{p}}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\text{d}\theta\int\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}\bigg|_{\omega=\omega_{\vec{p}}}

(9)

where

\omega=\sqrt{(p^{0})^{2}+(p^{1})^{2}},\qquad\omega_{\vec{p}}=\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m^{2}},\qquad|\vec{p}|=\sqrt{(p^{2})^{2}+(p^{3})^{2}}.

(10)

The
\vec{p}
denotes the spacial momentum, and the
\omega_{\vec{p}}
is the on-shell energy.

3
Canonical quantization for real scalar in Klein space

The canonical quantization of field theories in Minkowski spacetime is fundamentally based on a 3+1 decomposition within the Hamiltonian framework. However, this approach presents a significant challenge when applied to Klein space due to its two equivalent temporal dimensions. The absence of a preferred time direction means that selecting either temporal coordinate would explicitly break the symmetry between them, leading to an ambiguous quantization procedure. To resolve this issue, we introduce a novel quantization scheme that preserves the inherent symmetry of Klein space while maintaining the essential features of canonical quantization. Instead of using one of two temporal coordinates, we adopt the temporal ”radial coordinate”
q
—the invariant length in the time plane-to foliate the space. In this framework, we employ the coordinates specified in (
7
) to perform the canonical quantization.
The induced metric of
q=
constant hypersurface
\Sigma_{q}
is then

\text{d}s^{2}_{\Sigma_{q}}=-q^{2}\text{d}\psi^{2}+\text{d}\vec{x}^{2},\quad\vec{x}=(x^{2},x^{3}).

(11)

Therefore, the determinant of the induced metric is
h=\text{det}[h_{ab}]=-q^{2}
.

In this section, we investigate the canonical quantization for real scalar fields, with the Lagrangian expressed in terms of the coordinates in (
7
) as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+m^{2}\phi^{2})-V(\phi)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_{q}\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{q^{2}}(\partial_{\psi}\phi)^{2}-(\vec{\partial}\phi)^{2}-m^{2}\phi^{2}\right]-V(\phi),\end{split}

(12)

where
V(\phi)
denotes the interacting term relying only on the field
\phi
. The conjugate momentum is then

\pi_{q}=\sqrt{h}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{q}\phi)}=iq\partial_{q}\phi.

(13)

Therefore, the canonical quantization yields

\begin{split}[\phi(q,\psi_{2},\vec{x}_{2}),q\partial_{q}\phi(q,\psi_{1},\vec{x}_{1})]&=\delta(\psi_{12})\delta^{2}(\vec{x}_{12}),\\
[\phi(q,\psi_{1},\vec{x}_{1}),\phi(q,\psi_{2},\vec{x}_{2})]&=0,\end{split}

(14)

where

\delta(\psi_{12})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in\psi_{12}},\qquad\int^{2\pi}_{0}\text{d}\psi\delta(\psi-\psi^{\prime})f(\psi)=f(\psi^{\prime}).

(15)

Before moving on, it is also useful to introduce the definition of Klein-Gordon (KG) inner product between two fields
\phi^{1}
and
\phi^{2}

\langle\phi^{1},\phi^{2}\rangle\equiv i\int_{\Sigma_{q}}\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}x(\phi^{2}\pi^{1}_{q}-\phi^{1}\pi_{q}^{2})=\int_{\Sigma_{q}}\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}x\phi^{1}\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{q\partial_{q}}\phi^{2}.

(16)

Here we need only partial derivative
\partial_{q}
rather than covariant derivative, as we are considering scalar fields.

3.1.1
Mode expansion

Let us first consider the free real scalar (

(-\partial^{2}+m^{2})\phi=\left[\frac{1}{q}\partial_{q}(q\partial_{q})+\frac{1}{q^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\psi}+(m^{2}-\vec{\partial}^{2})\right]\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})=0\,.

(17)

The solution can be generally expressed as

\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-in\psi}\int\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}f_{n}(q)a_{n,\vec{p}},

(18)

where the function

obeys the equation

u^{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{n}(u)+uf^{\prime}_{n}(u)+(u^{2}-n^{2})f_{n}(u)=0,\qquad\omega_{\vec{p}}q=u.

(19)

This is the Bessel equation, which has two linearly independent solutions

\begin{split}J_{n}(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-)^{k}(x/2)^{n+2k}}{k!\Gamma(n+k+1)}=(-)^{n}J_{-n}(x),\\
N_{n}(x)&=\lim_{\nu\rightarrow n}\frac{J_{\nu}(x)\cos\pi\nu-J_{-\nu}(x)}{\sin\pi\nu},\end{split}

(20)

where
J_{n},N_{n}
are referred to as
n
-th order Bessel function and Neumann function (also called Bessel function of the second kind), and they form the Bessel basis as the solutions of the Klein Gordon equation when multiplied with
e^{-in\psi+i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}
. However, based on the following asymptotic behavior of
N_{n},J_{n}

\begin{split}&x\rightarrow 0:\ \begin{cases}&J_{0}(x)\rightarrow 1,\quad J_{n\neq 0}\rightarrow\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{n},\\
&N_{0}(x)\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\ln x,\quad N_{n\neq 0}(x)\rightarrow-\frac{(n-1)!}{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^{n}\end{cases}\\
&x\rightarrow\infty:\ \begin{cases}&J_{n}(x)\rightarrow\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\left(x-\frac{n}{2}\pi-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\\
&N_{n}(x)\rightarrow\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\sin\left(x-\frac{n}{2}\pi-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\end{cases},\end{split}

(21)

the Neumann function
N_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)
is divergent when
q\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, the
N_{n}
modes are forbidden in the classical solution, as the field
\phi(x)
must remain finite for any value of the coordinates at the classical level. As a result, the classical solution is given by

\phi^{\text{cl}}(q,\psi,\vec{x})=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}}J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)e^{i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)},

(22)

where the superscript “cl” is the abbreviation of “classical”, and
a^{(J)}
is independent of the coordinates
(q,\psi,\vec{x})
. The reality condition
\phi^{*}=\phi
is then equivalent to

a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(J)*}=(-)^{n}a_{-n,-\vec{p}}^{(J)}\,,

(23)

because of
J_{n}=(-)^{n}J_{-n}
.

The mode expansion (
22
) indicates that there are no two linearly independent modes for the classical solution in Klein space. This becomes much clearer when compared to the Minkowski case. To facilitate this comparison, we re-express the classical solution (
22
) in terms of plane-wave modes using Cartesian coordinates as

\displaystyle\phi^{\text{cl}}(q,\psi,\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\text{d}^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta(p^{2}+m^{2})a_{p}^{(J)}e^{ipx}

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int\frac{\text{d}\theta\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}a_{\theta,\vec{p}}^{(J)}e^{i(-\omega_{\vec{p}}q\cos(\theta-\psi)+\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}\,,

where
\theta
is the polar angle of the momentum component

, and we used the integral representation of Bessel function

J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)=\frac{i^{n}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\text{d}\theta e^{-in\theta}e^{-i\omega_{\vec{p}}q\cos\theta}\,,

(25)

and the relation between the coefficients

a_{\theta,\vec{p}}^{(J)}=a_{p}^{(J)}\Big|_{p^{2}+m^{2}=0}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{n}a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(J)}e^{in\theta}\,,

(26)

In comparison, the mode expansions via plane waves in Minkowski

\phi^{\text{cl}}_{\text{Mink}}(t,\vec{x})=\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\omega_{\vec{p}}}\left[a_{\vec{p}}e^{-i\omega_{\vec{p}}t+i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}+a^{*}_{-\vec{p}}e^{i\omega_{\vec{p}}t+i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}\right]\,,

Thus, there must be two independent modes associated with two different conformal boundaries. But in Klein space, whose Penrose diagram is topologically equivalent to gluing together the two conformal boundaries of the Penrose diagram of Minkowski spacetime, there is only one conformal boundary. The gluing of the boundaries transforms the worldlines of massive plane waves to closed curves (see the black dashed line in Fig.

For convenience, one can perform Fourier transformation to Eq.(

) and obtains

\begin{split}a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(N)}&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left\langle e^{-i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q),\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})\right\rangle\\
&=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}\text{d}\psi}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{q\partial_{q}}J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q).\end{split}

(39)

Similarly, the
a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(J)}
can also be derived in terms of the Klein-Gordon inner product as

\begin{split}a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(J)}&=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left\langle e^{-i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}N_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q),\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})\right\rangle\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}\text{d}\psi}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{q\partial_{q}}N_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q).\end{split}

(40)

It is important to reiterate that the Eqs (

) and (

q
. The canonical quantization (

\left[a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}_{1}},a^{(N)}_{m,\vec{p}_{2}}\right]=(-)^{n}(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{m+n,0}\delta^{2}(\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2})

(41)

or equivalently,

\left[a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}_{1}},a^{(N)*}_{m,\vec{p}_{2}}\right]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{m,n}\delta^{2}(\vec{p}_{12}).

(42)

3.1.2
Hamiltonian and evolution operator

The Hamiltonian of the free real scalar can be derived from the Legendre transformation of the Lagrangian

\begin{split}H_{q}^{(0)}&=\int q\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}(\phi\partial_{q}\phi-\mathcal{L})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}\left[-\frac{1}{q}\pi_{q}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}(\partial_{\psi}\phi)^{2}+(\vec{\partial}\phi)^{2}+m^{2}\phi^{2}\right].\end{split}

(43)

The Hamiltonian is then
q
-dependent. Furthermore, the Hamiltonians at different values of
q
do not commute with each other, while Hamiltonians at the same
q
commute

\left[H^{(0)}_{q_{1}},H^{(0)}_{q_{2}}\right]\neq 0,\quad\left[H^{(0)}_{q},H^{(0)}_{q}\right]=0.

(44)

The commutators between
H^{(0)}_{q}
and the field
\phi
or the conjugation momentum
\pi_{q}
at the same
q
are derived from the canonical commutator (
14
) as

\begin{split}\left[H^{(0)}_{q},\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})\right]&=-\partial_{q}\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x}),\\
\left[H^{(0)}_{q},\pi_{q}(q,\psi,\vec{x})\right]&=-\partial_{q}\pi_{q}(q,\psi,\vec{x}),\end{split}

(45)

which are the Heisenberg equations. The solution of the Heisenberg equations are

\begin{split}\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})&=U^{-1}_{(0)}(q,q_{0})\phi(q_{0},\psi,\vec{x})U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})\,,\\
\pi_{q}(q,\psi,\vec{x})&=U^{-1}_{(0)}(q,q_{0})\pi_{q_{0}}(q_{0},\psi,\vec{x})U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})\,.\end{split}

(46)

Here,
q_{0}
is the reference time (or initial time), and the evolution operator
U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})
with the initial condition
U_{(0)}(q_{0},q_{0})=1
satisfies the schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger equation

\partial_{q}U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})=U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})H_{q}^{(0)},\quad\partial_{q}U^{-1}_{(0)}(q,q_{0})=-H_{q}^{(0)}U^{-1}_{(0)}(q,q_{0}).

(47)

These two equations are actually equivalent, and they have the solution

U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\text{exp}\left[\int^{q}_{q_{0}}H^{(0)}_{q^{\prime}}\text{d}q^{\prime}\right]\quad\text{when}\ q>q_{0}\,,

(48)

and

U_{(0)}(q,q^{\prime})=U^{-1}_{(0)}(q^{\prime},q)\quad\text{when}\ q<q^{\prime}\,,

(49)

where
\mathcal{Q}^{-1}
is the anti-
q
order operator placing the operators with larger
q
on the right. Note that
H_{q}^{(0)}
is actually the Hamiltonian in the Heisenberg picture. The evolution operator
U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})
takes a complex form because the fact that
H_{q}^{(0)}
’s at different
q
do not commute with each other.

The Hamiltonian in the Schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger picture is

\begin{split}H_{q}^{(0)(S)}&=U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})H_{q}^{(0)}U^{-1}_{(0)}(q,q_{0})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}\left[-\frac{1}{q}\pi_{q_{0}}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}(\partial_{\psi}\phi(q_{0}))^{2}+(\vec{\partial}\phi(q_{0}))^{2}+m^{2}(\phi(q_{0}))^{2}\right],\end{split}

(50)

where
\phi(q_{0})
is a shorthand version of
\phi(q_{0},\psi,\vec{x})
. The Hamitonian in these two pictures are linked at the initial time
q_{0}

H^{(0)(S)}_{q_{0}}=H^{(0)}_{q_{0}}.

(51)

Note that even in the Schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger picture, the Hamiltonian for free scalar still depends on
q
, and

\left[H^{(0)(S)}_{q_{1}},H^{(0)(S)}_{q_{2}}\right]\neq 0\quad(q_{1}\neq q_{2}),\quad\quad\left[H^{(0)(S)}_{q},H^{(0)(S)}_{q}\right]=0.

(52)

The Heisenberg equation (
47
) can be rewritten in term of
H^{(0)(S)}_{q}
as

\partial_{q}U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})=H^{(0)(S)}_{q}U_{(0)}(q,q_{0}).

(53)

Then, the evolution operator can be expressed in terms of Hamiltonian in the Schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger picture as

U_{(0)}(q,q_{0})=\mathcal{Q}\text{exp}\left[\int^{q}_{q_{0}}H^{(0)(S)}_{q^{\prime}}\text{d}q^{\prime}\right],\quad\text{when}\ q>q_{0}

(54)

where
\mathcal{Q}
represents the
q
-ordering operator placing the fields with larger
q
to the left of those with smaller
q
.

3.1.3
Correlation functions

On the other hand, the quantum field theory in Klein space can also be regarded as the analytical continuation from Euclidean space by implementing Wick rotation
q_{E}\rightarrow iq(1-i\epsilon)
. Here,
q_{E}
denotes the radial direction on 2D plane
(x^{0}_{E},x^{1}_{E})
in Euclidean space parametrized by the coordinates
(x^{0}_{E},x^{1}_{E},x^{2},x^{3})
. This is similar to the analytical continuation from Euclidean space to Minkowski space

x^{0}
-ordered (time-ordered) correlation function. For more information about Wick rotation, see Appendix

n

-ordered expectation of

\langle\phi_{1}(x_{1})\cdots\phi_{n}(x_{n})\rangle=\bra{\infty}\mathcal{Q}\phi_{1}(x_{1})\cdots\phi_{n}(x_{n})\ket{0}\,.

Instead of using

as the bra vacuum state in the correlation function, because here we need to work in an “in-out” formalism rather than an “in-in” formalism. The “in-out” formalism can be translated to the usual path integral and related to the results from analytical continuation, while the “in-in” formalism can be translated to a closed-time-path functional integral

. The field
\phi

\displaystyle\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[a^{(H^{(1)})}_{n,\vec{p}}H_{n}^{(1)}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)+a^{(H^{(2)})}_{n,\vec{p}}H_{n}^{(2)}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)\right]e^{i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}

(56)

\displaystyle\underset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\omega_{\vec{p}}q}}\left[a^{(H^{(1)})}_{n,\vec{p}}e^{i\left(\omega_{\vec{p}}q-\frac{n\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}+a^{(H^{(2)})}_{n,\vec{p}}e^{-i\left(\omega_{\vec{p}}q-\frac{n\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\right]e^{i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}

with

a^{(H^{(1)})}_{n,\vec{p}}=\frac{a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}}-ia^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}}{2}\,,\quad a^{(H^{(2)})}_{n,\vec{p}}=\frac{a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}}+ia^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}}{2}\,,

(57)

where we reformulated the mode expansion (
33
) by the first-kind and the second kind Hankel functions

and

defined as

H_{n}^{(1)}(x)=J_{n}(x)+iN_{n}(x)\,,\quad H_{n}^{(2)}(x)=J_{n}(x)-iN_{n}(x)\,.

(58)

The modes
e^{i\left(\omega_{\vec{p}}q-\frac{n\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}

are similar to

and

in the Minkowski case. Thus, one of them should be interpreted as the annihilation operator and the other as the creation operator concerning the state
\langle\infty|
. We will see that the right choice is

\langle\infty|a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(H^{(1)})}=0\,.

(59)

Since the state
\langle\infty|
is annihilated by the Hankel function coefficient acting from the right side, we will call it the Hankel vacuum.
Now we can calculate any correlation functions via the Wick contraction, based on the annihilation conditions of Neumann vacuum (
35

\displaystyle\langle\infty|\phi(x)\phi(0)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{n}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{6}}e^{i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}H_{n}^{(2)}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)\langle\infty|a_{n,\vec{p}}^{(H^{(2)})}a_{0,\vec{p}^{\prime}}^{(J)}|0\rangle

(60)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi}{2}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}H_{0}^{(2)}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}\,\langle\infty|0\rangle.

where we used the commutator (
41
) in the last step. Note that the inner product
\langle\infty|0\rangle
is non-vanishing

3

3

Since we define the state
|0\rangle
and
\langle\infty|
by the annihilation conditions, there are ambiguities in normalizations, see Eq.(

In general, the two-point propagator can be expressed in a covariant form. This can be achieved by acting Klein-Gordon operator

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{\partial_{q}}{q}(q\partial_{q})+\frac{\partial_{\phi}^{2}}{q^{2}}-\vec{\partial}^{2}+m^{2}\right)\langle\infty|\mathcal{Q}\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})|0\rangle\\

(61)

where

denotes the step function. This differential equation can be re-expressed by using (

3.2
The interacting real scalar field and LSZ reduction formula

For the interacting real scalar, the potential
V(\phi)
in the Lagrangian is non-vanishing, such that the E.o.M (
17
) should be modified to include potential terms. In a QFT textbook, people usually use the interaction picture, which is convenient for perturbative calculations, see appendix
C
. However, in this subsection, we adopt the Heisenberg picture in which the mode expansion can be formally performed in a similar form to that of free field theory (
33
),

\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)+a^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)N_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)\right]e^{i(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}-n\psi)}\,.

(64)

But now the coefficients
a^{(J)}(q),a^{(N)}(q)
are no longer independent of the coordinate
q
. As a result, they do not satisfy (
41
) except for
q\rightarrow\infty
where the field tends to be free. As usual, the reality condition also yields

a^{(J)*}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)=(-)^{n}a^{(J)}_{-n,-\vec{p}}(q)\,,\quad a^{(N)*}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)=(-)^{n}a^{(N)}_{-n,-\vec{p}}(q)\,.

(65)

The interacting field
\phi
also satisfies the Heisenberg equation

\partial_{q}\phi=-[H_{q},\phi]\,,

(66)

where the Hamiltonian for interacting real scalar theory is given by

\begin{split}H_{q}&=H_{q}^{(0)}+H_{q}^{(\text{int})}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}\left[-\frac{1}{q}\pi_{q}^{2}-\frac{1}{q}(\partial_{\psi}\phi)^{2}+(\vec{\partial}\phi)^{2}+m^{2}\phi^{2}\right]+\int q\text{d}\psi\text{d}^{2}\vec{x}V(\phi)\,.\end{split}

(67)

Therefore, the interacting field
\phi
evolves as

\phi(q,\psi,\vec{x})=U^{-1}(q,q_{0})\phi(q_{0},\psi,\vec{x})U(q,q_{0})

(68)

where the evolution operator
U(q,q_{0})
satisfies the Schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger equation

\partial_{q}U(q,q_{0})=U(q,q_{0})H_{q},

(69)

the solution of which is

U(q,q_{0})=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\text{exp}\left[\int^{q}_{q_{0}}H_{q^{\prime}}\text{d}q^{\prime}\right].

(70)

The evolution operator can also be expressed by using the Hamiltonian in the Schr
\ddot{\text{o}}
dinger picture. This is similar to the discussion in section
3.1.2
, and we will not repeat it here.

Moreover, at the quantum level,
\phi(q\rightarrow 0)|0\rangle
also needs to be finite, leading to

a^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q=0)\ket{0}=0\qquad\forall n,\vec{p}\,,

(71)

or equivalently,

a^{(N)}_{\theta,\vec{p}}(q=0)\ket{0}=0\qquad\forall\theta,\vec{p}\,.

(72)

In general,
a^{(N)}_{\theta,\vec{p}}(q=\infty)\ket{0}\neq 0
as
a^{(N)}_{\theta,\vec{p}}(\infty)
differs from
a_{\theta,\vec{p}}^{(N)}(0)
in a non-trivial way. This can be shown by computing the following Klein-Gordon inner product
4
4

4

Note that the Klein Gordon product
\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\rangle\Big|_{q}
is not independent of
q

\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
satisfying the equation of motion with non-trivial potential
V(\phi)
because the dual of the Klein-Gordon current

is no longer closed here,
\text{d}*J^{12}\neq 0
.

\langle e^{-ip\cdot x},\phi(x)\rangle\Big|_{q}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{n}e^{in\theta}J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{q\partial_{q}}\left[a^{(J)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)J_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)+a^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)N_{n}(\omega_{\vec{p}}q)\right].

(73)

Fortunately, through the asymptotic behavior of the Bessel functions (
21
), one can find that

a^{(N)}_{\theta,\vec{p}}(q)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{n}e^{in\theta}a^{(N)}_{n,\vec{p}}(q)=\langle e^{-ipx},\phi(x)\rangle\Big|_{q}\,,\quad\text{when}\ q\rightarrow 0,\infty.

(74)

Now with the Klein-Gordon product at the asymptotic region

and

The scattering amplitude is defined by the inner product between the scattering state living on the boundary of Klein space and the S-vector
|S\rangle\equiv|0\rangle
(originally mentioned in section
2
), or more explicitly

\displaystyle=\langle\infty|\mathcal{Q}\left(a^{(N)}_{\theta_{1},\vec{p}_{1}}(\infty)-a^{(N)}_{\theta_{1},\vec{p}_{1}}(0)\right)\cdots\left(a^{(N)}_{\theta_{n},\vec{p}_{n}}(\infty)-a^{(N)}_{\theta_{n},\vec{p}_{n}}(0)\right)|0\rangle

(79)

5
Conclusions and discussions

In this work, we investigated the canonical quantization and the path-integral quantization of a scalar field theory in Klein space. In canonical quantization, we selected the “length of time”
q
as the evolution parameter such that the field can be expanded in terms of the Bessel functions and the Neumann functions. Though the Neumann functions are divergent at the original point, they are crucial in canonical quantization. We imposed the regularity condition to constrain the Neumann vacuum state
|0\rangle
. On the other hand, we also define the Hankel vacuum state
\langle\infty|
which is associated with the asymptotic region
q\rightarrow\infty
, where the mode expansions behave as
e^{\pm i\omega_{\vec{p}}q}
. By using these constructions, we explicitly calculated the free two-point function, which is consistent with the covariant one. For general
q
-ordered correlation functions with small interactions, they can be calculated perturbatively. The details can be found in appendix
C
. And then we deduced the LSZ reduction formula in the Heisenberg picture. Furthermore, we reconstructed the states and the correlation functions in the framework of path-integral quantization.

One subtle point is that the order in
q
in our formalism depends on the choice of the original point. A different choice of the original point could lead to a different ordering of operators in the correlation functions. In other words, the
q
-ordering operator
\mathcal{Q}
does not commute with spacetime translations. This is similar to the radial quantization of CFT, where the radial-ordering operator
R
in the definition of correlation functions does not commute with the spacetime translations. In contrast, the time-ordering operator
T
in Minkowski spacetime does commute with the spacetime translations (but does not commute with the Lorentz boosts generally).

The novel modes associated with the Neumann functions need further study. They may play an essential role in flat holography. One unanswered question in flat holography concerns the extrapolation dictionary between the operators in boundary field theory and the bulk fields. It would be interesting to investigate the role of these novel modes in the bulk reconstruction in flat holography.

As a final remark, most of our discussions apply to a more general spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{p,q}(p,q\geq 2)
, which also has only one asymptotic boundary. Although the solution basis of the Klein-Gordon equation would be different, the basic physical picture would remain the same. We can still define “the length of time” and introduce additional modes beyond plane waves in the associated coordinate system. The canonical quantization, the path integral quantization, and the LSZ reduction would also be realizable.

In this appendix, we will show how to perturbatively calculate an interactive real scalar field theory in Klein space within the canonical quantization formalism.

In this work, we constructed the Klein correlators
\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{K}
and
\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{K}^{(1)}
as they are given by the Feynman path integral and interpreted as the
q
-ordered correlation functions in the canonical quantization formalism.

The causal structure of the spacetime is reflected in the correlation function, as the branch points of the correlation functions are located at the light cone of the original point. First, we see that the correlation function (
127
) is definitely positive and only has a single pole at
x=0
, which means a “light cone” in Euclidean space is the original point itself. In the Minkowski spacetime, consider the correlation function (
128
) as a holomorphic function of complex
x^{0}
with fixed
\vec{x}=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
, we have two branch points located at
x^{0}=\pm|\vec{x}|
corresponding to the future and past light cones, respectively. In the Klein space, consider the correlation function (
136
) as a holomorphic function of complex
q
with fixed
r
, we have only one meaningful branch point located at
q=r>0
corresponding to the only light cone in Klein space. In short, the form of the correlation functions is determined by whether
x
and the original point are space-like or time-like separated from each other: we have
\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{E},\,\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{M},\,\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{K}
if they are space-like separated; we have
\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{M}^{(1)/(2)},\,\langle O(x)O(0)\rangle_{K}^{(1)/(2)}
if they are time-like separated. The two different branches are traced back to the opposite
\epsilon
-prescription.
