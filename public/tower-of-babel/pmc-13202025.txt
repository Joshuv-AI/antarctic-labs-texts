World in crisis should accelerate universal health reforms
Authors: Rob Yates, Katri Bertram, Arush Lal, Neena Joshi, Maarinke van der Meulen, Justin Koonin
Published: 2026

The world is once again at a dangerous inflection point—and in some ways, more precarious than ever before. Governments now face an unprecedented convergence of crises, with the aftershocks of the pandemic still being felt, geopolitical tensions on the rise and a conflict in West Asia eroding international norms and potentially triggering the largest energy security threat in history. For governments everywhere, this is translating into higher inflation, tighter public finances and mounting pressure ahead of elections.

The instinctive political response is to play it safe: invest in defence, reduce public spending, delay expansive reforms and wait for calmer times. We, the universal health coverage (UHC) Accelerator, argue that history suggests otherwise. Investing in universal health reforms—especially in times of crisis—is a winning strategy for governments and all the people they serve.

Political leaders are under intense pressure to act quickly to shield their populations from the impacts of current crises. Slovenia, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Philippines have all imposed fuel rationing,1 while inflation is eroding real wages and increasing the cost of living. As a result, many countries have prioritised subsidising energy prices or financing security sectors.2 However, most people are struggling with broader cost increases particularly healthcare.

In wealthy countries, including the USA and throughout Europe, out-of-pocket payments for healthcare are increasingly affecting the general population, not only those in the lower socioeconomic segments.3 Some of the poorest countries—those most reliant on development assistance—have seen their global health aid slashed by more than half,4 5 which is projected to result in up to 22 million additional preventable deaths by 2030.6

At a time when families are struggling to afford basic necessities, removing the out-of-pocket cost of healthcare can provide immediate and highly targeted relief. Free access to primary care, essential medicines, vaccines and diagnostics directly benefit those most in need, while strengthening the foundations of national health systems and building trust in public institutions and government services—critical elements to support rapid recovery. Therefore, investing in universal, free health services as a form of social protection is not only the ethical thing to do in times of crisis—it is also politically smart.

History shows that crises can be powerful drivers of such reforms. The Chatham House Commission on Universal Health documented how many countries have introduced or expanded UHC in response to various shocks.7 Successful UHC reforms were often launched during or immediately after crises—including wars (UK, France, Japan, Ukraine), financial crises (Thailand, New Zealand, Cyprus), natural disasters (the Philippines, after Typhoon Haiya),8 health emergencies (China after SARS, South Africa after COVID-19) and democratic transitions (Mexico, Kenya, Sri Lanka). In every case, the reform delivered both population health gains and significant political returns for the leaders who championed them. These moments created both urgency and public demand, fostering a broad appetite among the general population for systemic reform, which enabled leaders to push through changes that might otherwise have faced resistance from powerful interest groups.

This is not an unaffordable ambition

Analysis by the Commission showed that even in periods of crisis, countries made significant progress towards UHC by increasing public spending on primary healthcare by only 1% of gross domestic product (GDP).7 For example, the UK’s postwar government launched the NHS in a period of severe austerity, and Thailand introduced its popular Universal Coverage Scheme in 2001 in the aftermath of the Asian financial crisis—both with only 1% of GDP.7 This level of investment is modest compared with the scale of emergency spending mobilised during recent crises. Governments spent US$1.3 trillion on explicit fossil fuel subsidies in 2022—more than ten times the additional investment needed to bridge the financing gap for UHC in many countries.2 9 When it comes to investing in health, the real barrier is not fiscal capacity, but political prioritisation.

Importantly, UHC reforms can be implemented quickly—particularly when focused on primary care. As seen in the UK, Thailand, and most recently in Ukraine, removing user fees for basic services, ensuring free access to essential medicines, and expanding community-based care can all be achieved within relatively short timeframes.7 These measures deliver immediate, visible benefits to households, making them politically attractive as well as technically sound.

In today’s context, such reforms would serve a triple purpose. From a health perspective, UHC-oriented health systems are also inherently more resilient during crises—as Thailand, Vietnam and Costa Rica demonstrated during COVID-19, maintaining essential services alongside effective outbreak control.10 Economically, they would act as a targeted form of social protection cushioning families from the rising cost of living. Politically, they would signal that governments are taking concrete steps to support their populations during a time of hardship.11 In contrast to blunt instruments like energy subsidies for large corporations, UHC is inherently progressive, directing resources towards those who need them most.12

This is particularly relevant for countries that have recently elected new, reform-minded governments. In nations such as Nepal, Bangladesh and Namibia, there is a window of political opportunity to pursue ambitious social policies. Launching or accelerating UHC could become a defining achievement for these administrations—one that delivers both immediate relief and long-term resilience. Furthermore, UHC could also be a winning strategy for future electoral campaigns, including in the USA—where nearly 17% of adults avoid or delay care due to cost.13

The case for such action is further strengthened by the broader benefits of UHC. Beyond improving health outcomes, they enhance economic productivity, reduce poverty and build social cohesion. They also strengthen preparedness for future shocks, whether from pandemics, armed conflict, climate events or economic downturns.7

Ultimately, the question facing today’s leaders is not whether they can afford to pursue UHC reforms, but whether they can afford not to. The current polycrisis is exposing the vulnerabilities of existing systems and the inadequacies of traditional policy responses. It is also creating a rare alignment of public demand and political necessity.14 Therefore, The George Institute for Global Health launched the UHC Accelerator in December 2025 to build on this alignment and catalytic momentum. By convening and partnering with national political champions and providing mentorship and rigorous evidence, the UHC Accelerator will support countries to become more sustainable, resilient and equitable by advancing progress on UHC.15

The best time to launch UHC reform is before a crisis. The second best time is during one. The pressures unleashed by destabilised energy prices, collapsing aid budgets and increasing inequality are forcing governments to rethink their priorities. By choosing to invest in universal, free health services during crises—rather than waiting for a quieter window that may never arrive—leaders have an opportunity to catalyse progress by delivering better health outcomes, stronger economies and lasting political legacies, while laying the groundwork for a more equitable and resilient future. In times of upheaval, one of the best decisions a government can make is to invest in the health of its people.
