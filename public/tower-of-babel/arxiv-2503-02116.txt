Multi-Agent Fact Checking
Ashwin Verma, Soheil Mohajer, Behrouz Touri

Abstract
We formulate the problem of fake news detection using distributed fact-checkers (agents) with unknown reliability. The stream of news/statements is modeled as an independent and identically distributed binary source (to represent true and false statements). Upon observing a news, agent i i labels the news as true or false which reflects the true validity of the statement with some probability 1 − π i 1-\pi_{i} . In other words, agent i i misclassified each statement with error probability π i ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \pi_{i}\in(0,1) , where the parameter π i \pi_{i} models the (un)trustworthiness of agent i i . We present an algorithm to learn the unreliability parameters, resulting in a distributed fact-checking algorithm. Furthermore, we extensively analyze the discrete-time limit of our algorithm.

Introduction

As online social networks become increasingly effective in disseminating information, the task of distinguishing between true and false information becomes increasingly challenging. This growing efficiency of information dissemination has led to several studies on how misinformation spreads through networks
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
25
,
23
]
. Conversely, there is growing interest in the development of automated fact-checkers that can perform tasks such as document retrieval, evidence extraction, and claim validation in an automated manner
[
18
,
19
,
30
]
.

When there are multiple imperfect
fact checkers
, determining the validity of a source based on their responses becomes a challenge. In such cases, it is important to know the reliability statistics of the fact checkers in question. As a result, a natural question arises: in the presence of multiple imperfect fact checkers, how can we formulate and learn their reliability over time? We provide a model for distributed fact-checking using unreliable or imperfect agents. A key step in our model is to quantify the unreliability of each agent as the cross-over probability of certain BSC channels. Given an estimate of the unreliability parameters, a weighted thresholding estimator can be used to identify the validity of the statement
[
28
,
24
,
33
]
, where the weights are the log-odds based on the agents’ unreliability estimates. We propose and study a learning rule to estimate the reliability parameters of the agents. Our algorithm provides the advantage of requiring minimal memory and having a simplified update rule.

In our problem, we are working with a mixture of product distributions. Determining the parameters of an identifiable mixture has been widely researched
[
13
,
15
,
16
,
9
,
12
]
. The parameter estimation problem typically involves finding a hypothetical model that produces samples with a distribution that closely resembles the true model.
The main contributions of the paper involve:

i.

Formulation of Distributed Fact-Checking
: We study a model for distributed
fact checking
, introduced in
[
33
]
, which constitutes agents modeled as Binary Symmetric Channels with unknown reliability in Problem
2
.

ii.

Online Estimator
: In Section
3
we propose an online estimator for the unreliability parameters of the agents which makes use of the likelihood ratio between the source being fake or true given the agents’ conclusion about the validity of the statement computed using the error estimate at a given time.

iii.

Convergence Analysis
: We study the convergence properties of the proposed online estimator involving truncated iterations. To establish convergence, we utilize results akin to the Stochastic Approximation theorem presented in
[
4
]
. However, since the hypotheses of the theorem, specifically the assumptions on the Lyapunov function, in
[
4
]
are not met in our case, we extend the result and provide a proof tailored to our specific problem.

Organization/Contribution of the Paper
:
After a brief review of the literature related to this topic,
in Section

, we formally define the distributed fact-checking framework and describe the problem of interest to this work, namely the estimation of unreliability parameters, which is framed in Problem

. Additionally, we revisit the results from

Section

Related Work
: Given the unreliability parameters of the agents, an optimal approach to reconstructing unknown labels involves employing weighted majority voting. In this approach, the weights assigned to the output provided by each agent are equal to the log-odds based on the knowledge of the workers’ unreliability
[
28
,
24
]
. In
[
33
]
, we provide the characterization of weights that would result in the optimal estimator for labeling the validity of statements.

On the other hand, the estimation of unreliability parameters is closely intertwined with the literature on crowdsourcing labeling where data labeling is crowdsourced to multiple unreliable workers. This process is susceptible to errors arising from various factors, such as task complexity, low incentive to accurately label the tasks, and the repetitive nature of the tasks. Estimating the unreliability of workers is challenging, as the true labels of the data are unknown.

The challenge of distributed fact-checking shares similarities with crowdsourcing labeling tasks, which researchers have extensively studied within the Dawid-Skene model, introduced through empirical studies in 1979
[
10
]
. The model emerged from medical applications where multiple clinicians label a patient’s state. In this context, Dawid and Skene proposed an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. Over the years, various extensions and variants of this algorithm have emerged
[
27
,
3
,
29
,
20
]
, with a notable line of work employing spectral analysis of matrices representing correlations between agents and labeling tasks
[
34
]
.

Recent years have witnessed a growing body of research focused on performance guarantees for EM and its variants. Notably, Chao and Dengyong
[
14
]
, as well as Zhang et al.
[
34
]
, have provided performance guarantees for different versions of EM employing diverse initialization techniques.

The convergence analysis of these variants of the Dawid-Skene estimator, rooted in the EM algorithm, has been explored for the offline scenario. In this context, where the sequence of statements to be verified is available as a batch, studies by Gao et al.
[
14
]
and Zhang et al.
[
34
]
have delved into the convergence aspects.

The analyses of the EM-based algorithms hinge on a sufficiently accurate initialization derived from the output of a substantial batch of statements being validated. Importantly, all these works assume access to the storage of all labels of all agents, given their focus on an offline setting. The only notable work presenting an algorithm in a streaming setting, without the necessity to store the entire dataset, is found in the work of Bonald and Combes
[
5
]
. Their proposed Triangular Estimation (TE) algorithm focuses on estimating the unreliability parameters of agents based on correlations between triplets of agents. This algorithm directly utilizes three agents, rather than the entire set, for estimating the unreliability parameter of a specific agent. The knowledge of all agents’ output becomes indirectly relevant in determining which three agents to select for computing the unreliability parameter of a given agent. Our work is the first attempt at providing an online estimator which has similarities to the EM variants. In establishing convergence results for our online estimator, we draw connections to stochastic approximation concepts within the literature of control theory
[
4
]
.

2
Problem Formulation

Consider a source that streams a sequence of statements where each statement can be true or false. We use a
hidden
variable
S(t)\in\{+1,-1\}
to denote the label (true/false) of the statement at discrete-time instance
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
We assume that the stream symbols are independently and identically distributed according to the Rademacher distribution, i.e.,

{\mathbb{P}(S(t)=+1)=\mathbb{P}(S(t)=-1)=\frac{1}{2}}
,
for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
. A
fact-checker
is interested in evaluating the validity of the statements using imperfect (inexpert) agents.

Model for the fact checker:

We model a
fact-checker
as an overseer of multiple agents, where each agent is responsible for testing the validity of the statement provided to it. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]
be the set of agents verifying the validity of the statements. At each time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the agents provide noisy/imperfect labels or judgment for
S(t)
to the fact checker by returning their assessment of the statement.
In other words, if the agent considers the statement correct, it marks the statement as True. Otherwise, it marks it as False. However, due to their limited expertise, the agents’ assessments may be different from the actual label of the statements. Mathematically, we model
the assessment at
agent
i\in[n]
as a memoryless Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC) with the
error probability
or
crossover probability

\pi_{i}\in(0,1)
, which takes the input
{S(t)}
and outputs

R_{i}(t)
, where for every
s\in\{\pm 1\}
, the distribution of the output is

{\mathbb{P}(R_{i}(t)\hskip-1.0pt=-s|S(t)\hskip-1.0pt\!=\hskip-1.0pts)\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt1\!\!-\!\!\mathbb{P}(R_{i}(t)=s|S(t)\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pts)=\pi_{i}}.

Therefore, agent
i\in[n]
produces
an output
R_{i}(t)
, which is independent of the past. Here,
\pi_{i}
represents the unreliability of agent
i
since the agent misclassifies the statement with probability
\pi_{i}
.
Note that
\pi_{i}\in(0,1/2)
embodies the fact that the agents are reliable on ‘average’. We represent the collection of crossover probabilities by
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and the sequence of all agents’ outputs at time
t
by
\boldsymbol{R}(t)
.

Properties of Output distribution:

Let us discuss some properties of output distribution.

i.

Since the statement stream
\{S(t)\}
is independent, and each agent is viewed as a memoryless channel, the random vector process
\{\boldsymbol{R}(t)\}
is an independent process.

ii.

At any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, given
S(t)
, the outputs
\{R_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent of each other. Moreover,
for any
{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and for every
{i\in[n]}
,
R_{i}(t)
has the Rademacher distribution.

iii.

The joint distribution of the output
\boldsymbol{R}(t)
given the true crossover parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is given as

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{R}(t)\hskip-1.6pt=\hskip-1.0pt\boldsymbol{r};\boldsymbol{\pi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{\frac{1+r_{i}}{2}}\bar{\pi}_{i}^{\frac{1-r_{i}}{2}}\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{\frac{1-r_{i}}{2}}\bar{\pi}_{i}^{\frac{1+r_{i}}{2}}\hskip-1.0pt\right)\hskip-1.0pt,

where
\boldsymbol{r}\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}
, and
recall that

\bar{x}=1-x
.

For brevity, given unreliability (crossover) parameters of the agents are
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we define
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}:\{+1,-1\}^{n}\to(0,1)
to be the distribution of the output vector
\boldsymbol{R}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})\hskip-1.0pt

\displaystyle=\hskip-1.0pt\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{R}\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt\boldsymbol{r};\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\!\hskip-1.0pt\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\frac{1+r_{i}}{2}}\bar{x}_{i}^{\frac{1-r_{i}}{2}}\hskip-2.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\frac{1-r_{i}}{2}}\bar{x}_{i}^{\frac{1+r_{i}}{2}}\hskip-2.0pt\right)\hskip-1.0pt.

In this notation,
g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{R}(t))
refers to the true distribution of the output vector
\boldsymbol{R}(t)
at any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

The distributed fact-checker faces two key challenges. The first challenge is to estimate the true label of each statement. In other words, it should develop an estimator that minimizes the error probability in determining the validity of statements. To address this,
[
33
]
examined a class of estimators that make decisions based on whether linear combinations of individual agents’ outputs exceed a certain threshold. Such an estimator can be expressed as

\displaystyle S_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau}(\boldsymbol{R}):=\mathsf{sgn}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}R_{i}-\tau\right),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
are given parameters.
In
[
33
]
, the set of optimal parameters for minimizing error probability
\mathbb{P}(S_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau}(\boldsymbol{R})\neq S)
is characterized. It turns out that when dealing with a uniform ly distributed source (e.g., this work),
S_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},0}(\boldsymbol{R})
would be an optimal estimator where
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=\left(\ell_{\pi_{1}},\dots,\ell_{\pi_{n}}\right)=\left(\log\frac{1-\pi_{1}}{\pi_{1}},\dots,\log\frac{1-\pi_{n}}{\pi_{n}}\right)}
. This leads to the second challenge of the fact-checker, which is also, the focus of this paper: estimating the agents’ unreliability parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

In this paper, our goal is to obtain reliable
estimate for the unreliability parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
If the true statement validity sequence
\{S(t)\}
were known,
\boldsymbol{\pi}
could be easily estimated as the fraction of time at which
R_{i}(t)\neq S(t)
. The challenge here is to estimate the channel parameters without the knowledge of channel input.

The ideal problem would be to identify an estimator that converges
almost surely
(a.s.) to the true estimates of the unreliability parameter. However, some collections of parameters may result in indistinguishable distribution for the agents’ output. It can be argued that those parameters cannot be distinguished from each other.
Thus, we focus on the following problem for multi-agent fact-checker.

Problem 1
.

Consider a fact-checker with access to the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{R}(t)\}
of the assessments of
n
agents, with unknown unreliability parameters
\pi_{i}
, for
i\in[n]
.
Determine an online estimator
\{\boldsymbol{P}(t)\}
of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
based on the output of the
n
agents such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}d(\boldsymbol{P}(t),\mathcal{S})=0
a.s.,
where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of parameters that result in indistinguishable distribution for the agents’ output, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})=g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{r})\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{r}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}\}.

(2)

In fact, we can characterize the set
\mathcal{S}
as follows.

Lemma 1
.

For
n\geq 3
and

\pi_{i}\in(0,1)\setminus\{{1}/{2}\}
for
i\in[n]
, the set
\mathcal{S}
defined in (
2
) is given by
{\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}\}}
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is provided in Appendix
A
.

3
Estimator

First, let us introduce an online estimator for the unreliability parameters of the agents comprising the fact checker for any number of agents
n\geq 2
.
We have provided convergence guarantees for this algorithm for
n=2
agents in
[
31
]
.
Consider the stream of output observed by the fact-checker
\{\boldsymbol{R}(t)\}
.
At any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the fact-checker has an estimate
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
of the unreliability parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, which depends on
(\boldsymbol{R}(1),\dots,\boldsymbol{R}(t))
. At time instance
t+1
and upon observing the output
\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)
, the estimate can be updated to
\boldsymbol{P}(t+1)
.

Recall that if
\boldsymbol{\pi}
was known, the fact checker could evaluate the likelihood ratio
\frac{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)|S(t+1)=-1)}{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)|S(t+1)=+1)}
to decode
S(t)
. Now, without
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, we can use its estimate
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
to compute an approximate likelihood ratio

L(t)
of
S(t+1)=-1
to
S(t+1)=+1
based on
\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)
.
For this, let us define

L:\{+1,-1\}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_{i}}{1-x_{i}}\right)^{R_{i}}.

(3)

This represents the likelihood function of receiving
\boldsymbol{R}
given the unreliability parameters
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Now, for the received vector
\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)
and an estimate
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
of their unreliability parameters, for brevity, let

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle:=L(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1),\boldsymbol{P}(t)).

(4)

Using
L(t)
,
we can estimate
S(t+1)
by setting

\displaystyle\hat{S}(t+1):=2\mathbbm{1}_{\{L(t)<1\}}-1=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-1&\textrm{if $L(t)\geq 1$}\\
+1&\textrm{if $L(t)<1$}\end{array}\right..\hskip-5.0pt

We are ready to discuss the update rule for the unreliability parameters’ estimates, given the source symbol estimate
{\hat{S}(t+1)}
and the output vector
\boldsymbol{R}(t+1)
.
Note that
{R_{i}(t+1)}
agreeing with
{\hat{S}(t+1)}
suggests that it is unlikely that the agent was introducing error at time
t
and hence, we average
P_{i}(t)
with a value less than half to obtain
P_{i}(t+1)
.
Similarly, if
R_{i}(t+1)
disagrees with
\hat{S}(t+1)
, we average it with a value greater than half.
More precisely, the proposed algorithm/dynamics updates the unreliability parameters as

\displaystyle P_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=(1\hskip-1.0pt-\hskip-1.0pt\eta_{t})P_{i}(t)\hskip-1.0pt+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{t}\left(1\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\frac{L(t)-1}{L(t)+1}R_{i}(t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1)\hskip-1.0pt\right)\!,

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in[n]
, with some initial condition
{\boldsymbol{P}(0)\in(0,1)^{n}}
,
where
\{\eta_{t}\}
is a pre-decided step-size sequence, and
L(t)
is given in (
4
). Lext us rewrite the above iteration in compact form,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{P}(t)+\eta_{t}{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1),\boldsymbol{P}(t)),

(7)

where
{\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}:\{+1,-1\}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is the vector field with its
i
th coordinate
is
given by

\displaystyle{\tilde{f}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{L(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{x})-1}{L(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{x})+1}R_{i}\right)-x_{i}.

(8)

Note that (
7
) is a stochastic approximation-type iteration whose asymptotic behavior resembles the asymptotic behavior of the mean-field Ordinary Differential Equations (ODE)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{f}\hskip-1.0pt:\hskip-1.0pt\mathcal{X}\hskip-1.0pt\to\hskip-1.0pt\mathcal{X}
is defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{R}\sim g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{x})].

(10)

We will expand on this viewpoint in Section
5
.

3.2
The Step Size

For the step-sizes
\eta_{t}
, we assume they satisfy the following stochastic approximation step-size assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The step-sizes
\{\eta_{t}\}
are positive, non-increasing, and satisfying
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
and
{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty}
.

One popular choice for the step-size sequence is the harmonic sequence
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{t+1}
for all
{t\in\mathbb{N}}
.
To grasp the motivation behind the estimator using such a step-size sequence, examine the scenario when the fact checker
knows
the source sequence symbols
\{S(t)\}
.
Since, at any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, conditioned on
S(t)
, the output distributions of the agents
are
independent of each other, the problem of estimating the unreliability parameters of the agents is equivalent to
n
uncoupled problems of estimating the parameter of a Bernoulli distribution from its independent samples. The estimation of the parameters for this problem is well-studied.
In particular, an effective class of estimators to solve this problem
is the
add-constant
estimator
[
21
]
. For the current setting, for each
i\in[n]
the add-
\beta
estimator, where
\beta\geq 0
, for parameter
\pi_{i}
at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
is given by

\displaystyle Q_{i}(t)=\frac{\beta+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{R_{i}(k)\neq S(k)\}}}{t+2\beta}.

The estimator makes use of the empirical frequency of agent
i
misclassifying the source symbol and can be expressed recursively as

\displaystyle Q_{i}(t+1)=(1-\nu_{t})Q_{i}(t)+\nu_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{R_{i}(t+1)\neq S(t+1)\}}.

Here,
\nu_{t}:=\frac{1}{t+1+2\beta}
and
Q_{i}(0)=1/2
.
The convergence properties of estimator
Q(t)
for different values of
\beta
and various loss functions are studied in
[
21
]
. Different values of
\beta
lead to well-known estimators, including the empirical estimator
(
\beta=0
)
, the Krichevsky–Trofimov (KT) estimator (
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
), and Laplace estimator (
\beta=1
).

To see the connection to our setting, where the source symbol is unknown, consider an extreme case where
L(t)\gg 1
(which implies
\hat{S}(t+1)=-1
). For
R_{i}(t+1)=+1
, we get

\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{L(t)-1}{L(t)+1}R_{i}(t+1)+1\bigg)=\frac{L(t)}{L(t)+1}\approx 1,

whereas for
R_{i}(t+1)=-1
we have
{\frac{1}{L(t)+1}\approx 0}
. Thus,

\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{L(t)-1}{L(t)+1}R_{i}(t+1)+1\bigg)\approx\mathbbm{1}_{\{R_{i}(t+1)\neq\hat{S}(t+1)\}}.

A similar situation holds when
L(t)\approx 0
.
Therefore, the update rule (
7
) with
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{t+1}
can be viewed as an imperfect and adaptive version of the add-
\beta
estimator with
\beta=0
.

3.3
The Truncation Sets

With the presence of multiple agents making decisions, in order to obtain convergence guarantees, it is important to stay away from the boundary where two or more agents have estimated unreliability close to
0
or
1
.
In particular, in line with the concept of expanding truncations for stochastic approximation in
[
8
, Chapter 2]
, we
introduce
a collection of growing truncation sets. These compact sets gradually converge to a superset. The estimate (but not the process) is reset to an arbitrary initial value each time it crosses the
current
truncation set.

We define
\{\mathcal{K}_{t}\}
to be a sequence of increasing compact truncation sets for
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e., (i)
{\bigcup_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{t}=\bar{\mathcal{X}}}
, (ii)
\mathcal{K}_{t}
is compact for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and (iii)
\mathcal{K}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{t+1}
for every
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
There are many choices for the sequence of increasing truncation sets
\mathcal{K}_{t}
that satisfy
these three conditions
.
One such class of sets has the form

{\mathcal{K}_{t}\!=\!\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{\boldsymbol{x}\hskip-1.0pt\in\hskip-1.0pt[0,1]^{n}\!\!\mid\!\!x_{i}\in[0,1],|x_{j}\hskip-1.0pt-\hskip-1.0pt\frac{1}{2}|\leq r_{t},\forall j\hskip-1.0pt\in\hskip-1.0pt[n]_{-i}\},}

where
\{r_{t}\}
is a strictly increasing sequence satisfying
{\lim_{t\to\infty}r_{t}=\frac{1}{2}}
.

3.4
Estimator with Resettings

Consider the set of increasing truncation covers
\{\mathcal{K}_{t}\}
and an initial estimate
\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(0)\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
.
Let
\{\gamma(t)\}
be a sequence of non-negative integers that keeps track of the current

\gamma(0)=0

t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, consider the update of the estimate

{\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t)+\eta_{t}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1),\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t))}
. We reset the dynamics if
\boldsymbol{y}
is outside the current active set
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma(t)}
, otherwise, let
\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t+1)=y

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t+1)=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{y},&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{K}_{\gamma(t)}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{0},&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{y}\not\in\mathcal{K}_{\gamma(t)}\end{cases},

(12)

\boldsymbol{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{0}

\displaystyle\gamma(t+1)=\gamma(t)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{y}\not\in\mathcal{K}_{\gamma(t)}\}}.\vskip-5.0pt

Conjecture 3

(Characterization of
\mathcal{E}
)
.

For

and
\pi_{i}\in(0,1)\setminus\{1/2\}
for

\mathcal{E}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{\pi},\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}\}

For

On the other hand, to understand the points on the boundary, note that

(2)

5.1
Stochastic Approximation

A stochastic approximation of an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) is a recursive method to find/approximate the solutions of an ODE. For a mean-field ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z})
with
\boldsymbol{F}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, generated by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}(t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1)

\displaystyle\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt\boldsymbol{Z}(t)+\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{Z}(t))+\boldsymbol{\xi}(t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1)),

(15)

for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{Z}(0)\in\mathcal{Z}
is an initial point, and
\{\eta_{t}\}
is a step-size sequence that is often assumed to satisfy Assumption
1
. Furthermore, {
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\}
is a martingale-difference sequence, i.e.,

{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}(t+1)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0}

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
{\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{Z}(k),\xi(k):k\leq t)}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the past. Then, the update vectors
\boldsymbol{Z}(t)
can be viewed as an approximation of the path taken by the mean-field ODE. More precisely, if
\boldsymbol{F}
is a Lipschitz function,
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)
is bounded in expectation, and
\{\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\}
remains bounded, then, the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{Z}(t)\}
converges to the solution of
\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{z})

[
6
]
.

To prove the main result, we first show that the difference in the updates of the estimates in (
7
) can be decomposed into a deterministic part and a zero-difference martingale.

Lemma 2
.

For
t\ \in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the online estimator (
7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{P}(t)+\eta_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{P}(t))+\boldsymbol{M}(t+1)\right),

where
\boldsymbol{f}
is the mean-field ODE given in (
10
) and

\boldsymbol{M}(t+1):=\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{R}(t+1),\boldsymbol{P}(t))-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{P}(t)).

Moreover, for the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{M}(t)\}
,
we have

(i)

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{M}(t+1)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
and

(ii)

\|\boldsymbol{M}(t+1)\|_{\infty}\leq 2
(and hence, bounded in-expectation),

with probability one for all
t\geq 0
. Here,
the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
is defined as
{\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{P}(k),\boldsymbol{M}(k):k\leq t)}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

In other words
\{\boldsymbol{M}(t)\}
is a bounded martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}.

The proof of Lemma
2
is provided in Appendix
B
.

Although the discrete-time process
\{\boldsymbol{P}(t)\}
satisfies the conditions to be viewed as the Stochastic Approximation scheme for
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
, we cannot use the standard results in stochastic approximation
[
6
,
22
]

to guarantee
the convergence of our learning rule, since the function
\boldsymbol{f}
is not a Lipschitz function.
Instead, we use certain Lyapunov functions and their properties to show the convergence of the proposed online estimator.

In the following lemma, we provide an alternative
representation
for the probability of the output vector
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})
and the update value
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{x})
. We refer to Appendix
C
for the proof.

Lemma 3
.

For any
\boldsymbol{r}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the expression of output probability for
\boldsymbol{R}
based on unreliability vector
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{r};\boldsymbol{x})
, is given as

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})\!\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\exp{\frac{-\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle}{2}}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\exp{\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle}{2}}\right)\!\!\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{x_{i}(1\hskip-1.0pt-\hskip-1.0ptx_{i})}

\displaystyle=\cosh\left(\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle}{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{x_{i}(1-x_{i})},

(16)

where the
i
th coordinate of vector
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is given by
{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x_{i}}=\log\frac{1-x_{i}}{x_{i}}}
.

Moreover, the function
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{x})

introduced in (
8
)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{1}-\tanh\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle\right)\boldsymbol{r}\right)-\boldsymbol{x}.

(17)

Next, we introduce a correction term
\boldsymbol{\rho}({t})
to express the resetting based update rule (
12
) in a recursive stochastic approximation form. The correction term becomes active only at the resetting iterations. Later in Lemma

, we show that the correction term
\boldsymbol{\rho}({t})

The update rule
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t)\}
defined in (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1)\hskip-1.0pt=\hskip-1.0pt\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t)\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\eta_{t}[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t))\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt\boldsymbol{M}({t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1})+\boldsymbol{\rho}({t\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0pt1})],

(18)

are as discussed in Lemma

5.2
Lyapunov Function

In this section, we propose and study a Lyapunov function for the mean-field ODE (
9
),
and we prove the desirable properties related to the function to establish the almost sure convergence of the update rule (
12
). To define the Lyapunov function, we utilize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence which is defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Kullback-Leibler Divergence
[
26
]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be
two
distributions on discrete set/alphabet
\mathcal{U}
. The KL-divergence between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

{D_{\text{KL}}(\mu\|\nu)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mu(u)\log\frac{\mu(u)}{\nu(u)}}
, where we use the conventions (i)
0\cdot\log\frac{0}{0}=0
, (ii) if there exists
u\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\nu(u)=0
and
\mu(u)>0
then
D_{\text{KL}}(\mu\|\nu)=\infty.

For a Lyapunov candidate, we focus on the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the
distribution of the output
\boldsymbol{R}
generated by the agents when the unreliability parameters vector is
\boldsymbol{\pi}
or
\boldsymbol{x}
. More precisely, for
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{X}
we define the Lyapunov candidate as
V:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
with

\displaystyle{V(\boldsymbol{x}):=D_{\text{KL}}(g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|g_{\boldsymbol{x}})}.

(19)

Note that here the alphabet is
\mathcal{U}=\{+1,-1\}^{n}
.

In addition, the Lyapunov function is finite for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}=(0,1)^{n}
as
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})>0
for every
\boldsymbol{r}\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}
.
Moreover,
V(\boldsymbol{x})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{x})=C_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\sum\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}}g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{r})\log{g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})},

where
C_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}}g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{r})\log{g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{r})}
is a finite constant for any
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{X}
. Since the second term is a convex combination of
2^{n}
functions that are logarithms of polynomials in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we can conclude that
V(x)
is
a continuously differentiable function.

Extension to
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
:
Note that the Lyapunov candidate function (
19
) can be extended to the set of singly-extreme vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}}
.
For
r\in\{+1,-1\}
and
x\in(0,1)
we can rewrite

x^{\frac{1+r}{2}}(1-x)^{\frac{1-r}{2}}=\frac{1+r}{2}x+\frac{1-r}{2}(1-x)
.
From the above representation for
x=0
, we adopt the convention
0^{\frac{1+r}{2}}:=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1+r}{2}x+\frac{1-r}{2}(1-x)=\frac{1-r}{2}
for
r\in\{-1,+1\}
.
Based on the convention, we can extend the definition of the output distribution,
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}
. For
i\in[n]
, for a singly-extreme vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}^{(i)}
and vectors
\boldsymbol{r}\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}
, the output distribution is defined as

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{r}):=\begin{cases}\prod_{k\in[n]_{-i}}x_{k}^{\frac{1-r_{k}}{2}}(1-x_{k})^{\frac{1+r_{k}}{2}}&\text{ if }r_{i}=(-1)^{x_{i}}\\
\prod_{k\in[n]_{-i}}x_{k}^{\frac{1+r_{k}}{2}}(1-x_{k})^{\frac{1-r_{k}}{2}}&\text{ if }r_{i}\neq(-1)^{x_{i}}\end{cases}.

(20)

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}
, we extend the definition of
V(\boldsymbol{x})
using the definition in (
20
) for
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Moreover, for
i\in[n]
, we define the gradient of
V(\boldsymbol{x})
,
denoted by

\nabla V(\boldsymbol{x})
, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}^{(i)}
, through its
j
th element

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{j}}=\begin{cases}\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{V(\boldsymbol{x}+(-1)^{x_{i}}h\boldsymbol{e}_{i})-V(\boldsymbol{x})}{(-1)^{x_{i}}h}&\text{ if }j=i\\
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{V(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{e}_{j})-V(\boldsymbol{x})}{h}&\text{ if }j\neq i\end{cases},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the
i
th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the following theorem, we show that
V(\boldsymbol{x})
satisfies the conditions to be a Lyapunov function for the ODE
{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}
.

Theorem 4
.

For the mean-field dynamics (
9
) and the function
V(\boldsymbol{x})
given in (
19
), we have
\left\langle\nabla V(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle\leq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Furthermore the equality holds iff
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{E}
, where
{\mathcal{E}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{0}\}}
is the set of equilibrium points of the ODE.

The proof of Theorem
4
is provided in Appendix
C
.

To prove the convergence, we use the Stochastic Approximation result in
[
4
]
.
In
[
4
, Theorem 2.3]
, it is established that under certain assumptions
on
the functions
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
V(\boldsymbol{x})
, if the updates stay in a compact subset
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathcal{X}
, and the step-sizes and error term satisfy certain boundedness conditions, then the updates converge to the set
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{E}
.
In doing so a key property that is being used is the compactness of the sublevel set of Lyapunov function
V(\boldsymbol{x})
associated with the ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

However, note that the Lyapunov function
V(\boldsymbol{x})=D_{\text{KL}}(g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|g_{\boldsymbol{x}})
defines a non-compact sublevel set for levels greater than a certain value.
In other words, for large enough
M
, the set

{\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid V(\boldsymbol{x})\leq M\}}

is not a compact set, as will be clarified as a consequence of Lemma
4
in Section
5.3
.

Consequently, if we focus only on the updates over
\mathcal{X},
the compactness assumption essential for
Theorem

5
,
that proves the convergence behavior of particular trajectories (linked to any
\omega\in\Omega_{\text{conv}}
) of the stochastic process
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{pr}}(t)\}
, would not hold.
However, we can address this issue by extending the function to the set
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
ensuring the assumptions are satisfied, as detailed in the following subsection.

5.3
Boundary Behavior with Extreme Unreliability

In this subsection, we discuss the behavior of the Lyapunov function

and the mean-field function
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
for the singly-extreme vectors (see Definition
1

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{bound}}

6
Conclusion and future work

We presented a model for fact-checking of binary facts involving agents modeled as memoryless binary symmetric channels and proposed an online algorithm to estimate the unreliability parameters of the agents. We proved that the estimates form a dynamic process which is a stochastic approximation scheme and using results from stochastic approximation theory, we showed that it converges almost surely to the set of equilibrium points of the mean-field ODE over an extended domain
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
. In proving the convergence we studied the properties of the KL divergence used as the Lyapunov function
V(\boldsymbol{x})
for the mean field ODE
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
The online estimator proposed in this paper and its analysis open up a variety of avenues for future work. We conjecture that the set to which the online estimator converges can be further reduced to a smaller set containing the stable equilibrium points such as
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}
when we have
\pi_{i}\neq\frac{1}{2}
for any
i\in[n]
. Further work involves studying the convergence of variants of the proposed online estimator when not all the agents participate in the fact-checking task at every time
t
.
