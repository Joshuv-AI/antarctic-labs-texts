Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities not only in language generation but also in advancing scientific discovery. A growing body of work has explored ways to improve their reasoning, from self-consistency and chain-of-thought to multi-agent debate. Inspired by the dynamics of scientific committees and The Society of Mind , we introduce Roundtable Policy , a complementary inference-time reasoning framework that performs inference through the weighted consensus of multiple LLMs. Our findings indicate that this approach significantly enhances reasoning in complex heterogeneous scientific tasks and improves scientific narratives in terms of creativity, rigor, and logical coherence, while reducing hallucinations that single models are prone to. Our approach emphasizes structured and interpretable consensus rather than opaque convergence, while requires only black-box access and uniform procedures, making it broadly applicable to multi-LLM reasoning. Project website at https://ai4scidisc.github.io/roundtable/ .



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities in text generation, reasoning, and few-shot adaptation. These advances have opened exciting opportunities for scientific discovery, where complex, interdisciplinary problems demand not only accurate reasoning but also coherent narrative construction. However, current LLMs may hallucinate facts, introduce implausible logical jumps, or produce fragmented explanations. Such limitations are particularly problematic in scientific contexts, where reliability and clarity are equally critical. For instance, a scientific proposal must not only provide factually correct content, but also organize it into a structured and persuasive narrative. Addressing these dual requirements calls for mechanisms that go beyond the capacity of a single LLM.

A natural motivation is therefore to leverage multiple LLMs, aggregating their complementary strengths. Prior work has explored self-consistency with multiple sampled reasoning paths
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, multi-agent debate frameworks
(
Du & et al. 2023
;
Madaan & et al. 2023
)
, and tool-augmented multi-agent systems
(
Schick et al. 2023
;
Ghafarollahi & Buehler 2024
)
. However, these approaches are often limited to single-task reasoning, rely on emergent dynamics that are difficult to interpret, or lack systematic mechanisms for weighting contributions across heterogeneous domains. Ensemble-style methods such as majority voting treat all models equally, overlooking that different models excel in different domains or evaluation dimensions. Debate-style methods, while sometimes converging to better outputs, remain inherently opaque: it is often unclear why a particular answer was selected, or hard to quantify which models were more trustworthy in the process. In scientific applications, this lack of transparency poses a major barrier, since researchers require not only correct outputs but also interpretable evidence supporting the decision.

At a broader level, scientific problem solving is inherently multi-faceted, requiring both precise reasoning and coherent long-form narratives. While individual LLMs have made impressive progress, they still face challenges in consistently meeting these diverse demands across domains and tasks. This motivates the need for a structured approach to harness collective intelligence—one that integrates complementary expertise, surfaces and resolves disagreements transparently, and preserves accountability in the decision process.

Building on these considerations, we introduce
Roundtable Policy
, an inference-time framework that formalizes deliberation among multiple LLMs into a principled process for trustworthy scientific reasoning and narrative generation (Figure
1
). In our approach, diverse models first propose candidate responses, which are then jointly evaluated to record their performances. These evaluations are distilled into a confidence-weight table that encodes both historical reliability and uncertainty for each candidate across tasks and dimensions. At inference, this confidence-weight table guides a fusion process that integrates the diverse responses into a single consensus answer—balancing factual accuracy with narrative coherence while preserving transparency in how the decision is reached. As illustrated in Figure
1
, even on a scientific question such as explaining why nucleation accelerates at greater undercooling while growth dominates at smaller undercooling, individual models provide partial or inconsistent accounts. The roundtable process fuses these perspectives into a coherent explanation, showing how consensus-driven reasoning can elevate fragmented insights into a more complete scientific narrative.

We evaluate
Roundtable Policy
in two complementary scientific regimes. In the
MultiTask
setting, heterogeneous problems from geoscience, biology, mathematics, and materials science are solved simultaneously, testing cross-domain reasoning. In the
SingleTask
setting, models generate structured scientific proposals, testing long-form narrative construction. Across both, our findings show that
Roundtable Policy
consistently improves factual reliability, reasoning quality, and narrative coherence, yielding an average
13.01% improvement in MultiTask
and
11.04% improvement in SingleTask
compared to individual models.

Unlike ensemble-style methods such as majority voting, which treat all models equally, our approach adaptively weights models based on demonstrated reliability. And in contrast to debate-style frameworks, whose convergence dynamics are opaque,
Roundtable Policy
produces explicit, auditable signals that make the aggregation process transparent. This combination of principled weighting and interpretability distinguishes our method as a scalable and accountable alternative for multi-model scientific reasoning.

In summary, our contributions are threefold:
(i) we propose
Roundtable Policy
, an inference-time framework for performance-weighted consensus aggregation among multiple LLMs;
(ii) we demonstrate that this approach enhances both factual reasoning and scientific narrative across heterogeneous tasks; and
(iii) we release a graduate-level materials science benchmark derived from UCLA PhD qualifying exams, which serves as a rigorous, domain-authentic testbed and complements existing evaluations of scientific LLMs. More broadly, the principles underlying
Roundtable Policy
apply across multi-model scientific workflows, offering an interpretable and generalizable path toward AI-assisted reasoning and communication in science.

2
Methodology

This section formalizes the
Roundtable Policy
framework. We begin with the intuition behind the
Roundtable Policy
and then present the task settings and notation (a complete list of symbols is provided in Appendix §
A
). We next describe how the confidence-weight table is learned from repeated interactions between players and graders, and how it subsequently guides inference through consensus-weighted fusion.

2.1
Design Intuitions behind
Roundtable Policy

Scientific decision-making rarely relies on a single voice. In research committees, for example, each member independently evaluates a proposal, but the final judgment is not determined by a simple vote. Instead, reviewers’ assessments are weighed by domain expertise, reliability, and the clarity of justification before being synthesized into a collective decision. This process balances agreement with accountability, ensuring that the outcome is both credible and transparent.

Roundtable Policy
draws on the same principle. Rather than treating LLMs as isolated predictors, we view them as members of a committee. A set of
players
propose candidate responses informed by diverse inductive biases. These are evaluated by an ensemble of
graders
, whose rubric-based assessments provide explicit signals of quality and uncertainty. Finally, a structured
fusion step
aggregates these evaluations into a single consensus output—analogous to how a committee chair synthesizes diverse reviews into a reasoned decision.

This design is further motivated by the perspective of
The Society of Mind

(
Minsky 1988
)
, which frames intelligence as the coordination of many specialized, relatively simple agents. By embracing this philosophy,
Roundtable Policy
transforms the inherent diversity of LLMs into a strength, enabling collective reasoning that is both interpretable and robust.

2.2
Task Settings

We evaluate
Roundtable Policy
in two complementary scientific regimes that mirror the dual demands of scientific reasoning: solving pointwise problems and constructing coherent narratives.

MultiTask.
Models are simultaneously queried with nine heterogeneous scientific questions spanning geoscience, biology, mathematics, and materials science (with six distinct sub-categories). Task formats include multiple-choice, fill-in-the-blank, and short-answer questions. Intuitively, this setting resembles a written exam with diverse problem types—success requires breadth of expertise rather than mastery of a single domain.

SingleTask.
Models are prompted to generate structured scientific proposals following a standardized three-paragraph template with sections for
Background
,
Methodology
, and
Impact
. This setting captures the narrative aspect of scientific work: beyond producing isolated facts, one must weave them into a logically consistent and persuasive storyline.

Across both regimes, we deploy
L_{p}
pretrained LLMs (
players
) to generate candidate responses and
L_{g}
LLMs (
graders
) to form the AI Committee. Just as in a real committee, players provide diverse answers while graders act as evaluators who judge each contribution against shared standards. In MultiTask, graders jointly evaluate the
L_{p}\times N
responses per round, analogous to examiners scoring a large pool of student answers. In SingleTask, graders assess proposals paragraph-by-paragraph along three rubric dimensions—creativity, scientific rigor, and logical coherence—reflecting the multifaceted criteria by which scientific proposals are judged.

Each evaluation yields a trust weight with two components: (i) a quality score in
[-100,100]
, where negative values indicate incorrect or misleading responses, and (ii) a calibrated 95% confidence interval representing grader certainty. Together, these form the equivalent of both a “grade” and a measure of “grading confidence,” ensuring that downstream decisions reflect not only evaluation outcomes but also their reliability. The confidence-weight table
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{p}\times N}
serves as a structured memory of each player’s historical reliability across subtasks (MultiTask) or rubric dimensions (SingleTask). Unlike fine-tuning approaches,
Roundtable Policy
does not alter the parameters of the underlying LLMs. Instead,
\vartheta
is iteratively updated from repeated rounds of evaluation, much like how a committee gradually learns which members are most reliable in which topics.

At round
j
, a query
q^{j}
is sampled from the task dataset. Players generate responses
r_{i}^{j}=A^{P}_{i}(q^{j})
, which are pooled into
r^{j}
. Graders then evaluate these responses, producing
(s_{l}^{j},u_{l}^{j})
, which are averaged across
L_{g}
graders to yield
(s^{j},u^{j})
. After
R
rounds, the cumulative evaluations are distilled into:

\vartheta_{i,n}=\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{R}s^{j}_{i,n},\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{R}u^{j}_{i,n}\Biggr),

(1)

capturing both the estimated quality and the associated uncertainty of each player. Intuitively, this table plays the role of a “track record” or “reputation system” for each player, ensuring that strong but inconsistent voices are weighted differently from consistently reliable ones. This design calibrates the committee’s trust in different players without retraining them, enabling adaptability and efficiency.

2.4
Inference with the Confidence-Weight Table

Once
\vartheta
has been learned, it guides consensus fusion at inference. Given a new query
q
, each player generates a response
r_{i}=A^{P}_{i}(q)
. The collection
r=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{L_{p}}\}
is passed to the fusion process
A^{F}
, which aggregates them using the pretrained
\vartheta^{\text{pretrained}}
:

\tilde{r}=A^{F}(r,\vartheta^{\text{pretrained}}).

(2)

Here,
\vartheta
ensures that expertise is applied where it matters most. In MultiTask, it emphasizes domain-specific reliability (e.g., assigning higher weights to players consistently strong in mathematics). In SingleTask, it captures stylistic and analytical strengths across rubric dimensions, reflecting how different voices contribute to different aspects of a proposal.

The fusion process itself is not fine-tuned; it acts as an orchestrator that conditions on
\vartheta
to produce a consensus answer. This mimics how a committee chair integrates both the content and credibility of reviewers before issuing a final recommendation. Optionally, the AI Committee can re-evaluate
\tilde{r}
to assign a quality score and confidence interval, preserving interpretability and accountability throughout the process.

Qualitative Analysis.

These results illustrate how RT acts like a committee process rather than a flat average. In MultiTask, the confidence-weight table down-weights unreliable contributions and amplifies complementary strengths, thereby “rescuing” weaker players without diluting strong ones. This mirrors how, in a committee, flawed proposals can still contribute useful fragments when evaluated critically. The large gains on reasoning-heavy tasks (e.g., Tasks 6–7) highlight how diverse error patterns across models can be combined into substantially stronger reasoning chains.

In SingleTask writing, weaker players show the most pronounced gains in Impact and Methodology. For instance, GPT-4o exhibits long right tails in Impact–Scientific Rigor and Impact–Logical Coherence, while GPT-o3 shows consistently positive medians with bounded variance. Even strong baselines such as DS-chat rarely degrade under RT, suggesting that consensus seldom harms high-quality drafts but frequently elevates weaker ones. Mechanistically, the confidence-weight table enforces rubric-aware, non-redundant fusion: systematically weak contributors are down-weighted, while demonstrated strengths are amplified. The pattern resembles how peer reviewers specialize—some focusing on methodology, others on impact—and the editor synthesizes these perspectives into a well-rounded final decision.

Conclusion & Future Directions

We presented the
Roundtable Policy
, a inference-time reasoning framework that fuses outputs from multiple LLMs through confidence-weighted aggregation without finetuning base models. Unlike single-model prompting or naive ensemble methods such as majority voting, our approach does not treat all models equally nor rely on opaque emergent convergence, as in debate-style methods. Instead, it introduces a principled mechanism that explicitly encodes historical reliability and uncertainty, producing consensus answers that are not only more accurate but also transparent and auditable. Across structured
MultiTask
benchmarks and open-ended
SingleTask
proposal writing, we showed that
Roundtable Policy
consistently improves factual reliability, reasoning consistency, and narrative coherence, highlighting its value as a trustworthy tool for scientific reasoning.

Looking forward, several avenues can extend this framework. First,
adaptive arbitrator strategies
could allow the confidence-weight table to evolve dynamically with task complexity, reallocating trust in real time. Second, coupling the fusion process with
reinforcement learning
and human or experimental feedback may enable autonomous optimization of weight assignments through trial and error. Third, incorporating richer feedback signals—such as calibrated uncertainty, tool-augmented checks, or domain-specific rubrics—could further strengthen robustness in complex scientific workflows.

More broadly, the principles underlying
Roundtable Policy
generalize beyond the settings studied here. By tailoring consensus mechanisms to domain-specific evaluation criteria, the framework can support applications ranging from drug discovery and computational chemistry to astrophysics and other data-intensive sciences. In this sense,
Roundtable Policy
is not merely another ensemble method, but a scalable and interpretable paradigm for collective intelligence in LLMs—one that advances the rigor, reliability, and accountability of AI-assisted scientific discovery.

Ethics Statement

Our method significantly enhances the performance of sequential model editing, making it invaluable for updating and managing knowledge in real-world applications. While the ability to directly modify stored knowledge introduces potential risks, such as the introduction of false or harmful information, we strongly urge researchers to implement strict validation and oversight to ensure the ethical use of these techniques. Nevertheless, the original goal of model editing is positive, aiming to facilitate efficient updates of large models in the future. Therefore, we encourage researchers to leverage this technology responsibly and with care.

A.2
Formal Hypothesis Testing Framework for Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Significance Validity

To rigorously assess whether the Roundtable Policy (RT) provides statistically significant improvements over individual LLM baselines, we apply the non-parametric
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
to paired, round-level performance data. This test evaluates whether the median of the differences between two matched samples significantly exceeds zero, under the null hypothesis of no improvement.

Notation.

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the set of evaluation rounds and
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{L}\}
be the set of
L
baseline players.

We define:

•

s_{r}^{(\texttt{RT})}
: score obtained by RT in round
r\in\mathcal{R}
,

•

s_{r}^{(p)}
: score obtained by player
p
in the same round,

•

g_{r}:=\dfrac{s_{r}^{(\texttt{RT})}-s_{r}^{(p)}}{|s_{r}^{(p)}|}\times 100
: relative gain of RT over player
p
in round
r
(as percentage).

Hypothesis Test.

We conduct the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:\text{Median}(g_{r})\leq 0\quad\text{(no improvement)}

(3)

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:\text{Median}(g_{r})>0\quad\text{(RT improves over }p\text{)}

Test Procedure.

Given a sample of paired differences
\{g_{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
, we perform:

1.

Discard entries with
g_{r}=0
.

2.

Rank the absolute values:
\text{rank}(|g_{r}|)
.

3.

Compute the signed-rank statistic:

W^{+}=\sum_{r:g_{r}>0}\text{rank}(|g_{r}|).

(4)

4.

Using the null distribution of
W^{+}
(or a normal approximation), we calculate a
p
-value.

Statistical significance is established if:

p<\alpha=0.05.

(5)

Continuity Correction for Normal Approximation.

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, the test statistic
W^{+}
approximately follows a normal distribution when the sample size
n
is large (typically
n\geq 20
). In this case, we compute a
z
-score using the continuity-corrected normal approximation:

z=\frac{W^{+}-\frac{n(n+1)}{4}-0.5}{\sqrt{\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24}}},

(6)

where
n
is the number of non-zero
g_{r}
values (i.e., rounds with non-zero paired differences), and
0.5
is the continuity correction term to account for the discreteness of the rank sum distribution.

The resulting
z
-score is then used to compute a one-sided
p
-value based on the standard normal distribution. This approximation is used in our evaluation whenever
n
exceeds the small-sample regime (
n\geq 20
), ensuring computational efficiency without sacrificing statistical validity.

A.2.1
Application to
MultiTask
Evaluation

In the MultiTask evaluation, each task represents a distinct skill domain (e.g., math, geoscience, mechanics). Let
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{K}\}
be the set of
K=9
benchmark tasks.

For each player
p
and task
t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}
, and for each round
i
, we compute the percentage gain:

g_{i}^{(k)}=\frac{s_{i,t_{k}}^{(\texttt{RT})}-s_{i,t_{k}}^{(p)}}{|s_{i,t_{k}}^{(p)}|}\times 100.

(7)

We test each pair
(p,t_{k})
independently. To assess holistic advantage, we also aggregate over tasks:

\mathbf{g}_{\text{agg}}^{(p)}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\left\{g_{i}^{(k)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}.

(8)

We consider RT statistically superior to player
p
if:

•

the Wilcoxon test yields
p<0.05
on at least 8 of 9 tasks, or

•

the aggregate test on
\mathbf{g}_{\text{agg}}^{(p)}
satisfies
p<0.05
.

A.2.2
Application to
SingleTask
Evaluation

In the SingleTask evaluation, each round produces rubric-based scores over 9 dimensions
\mathcal{D}=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{9}\}
(covering three traits for each of Background, Methodology, and Impact).

For each player
p
, round
r
, and dimension
d\in\mathcal{D}
, we compute:

g_{r}^{(d)}=\frac{s_{r}^{(\texttt{RT}),d}-s_{r}^{(p),d}}{|s_{r}^{(p),d}|}\times 100.

(9)

We conduct Wilcoxon tests:

•

Per-dimension
: for each
d
independently, to assess fine-grained quality improvements.

•

Aggregate-level
: pool all scores:

\mathbf{g}_{\text{agg}}^{(p)}=\{g_{r}^{(d)}:r\in\mathcal{R},d\in\mathcal{D}\},

and test for overall statistical significance.

Interpretation.

The MultiTask analysis evaluates whether RT consistently improves performance across structurally diverse problems and domains. The SingleTask analysis inspects RT’s advantage across nuanced dimensions of academic writing quality. In both cases, aggregate-level tests offer a robust indicator of RT’s overall statistical superiority over individual LLM baselines.

A.3
Formal Framework for Kendall’s Tau Rank Correlation Analysis

To quantify the agreement between different graders’ rankings, we adopt the Kendall’s Tau rank correlation coefficient
(
Kendall 1938
)
, a non-parametric statistic that measures the ordinal association between two rankings.

Mathematical Definition.

Let
\pi^{(a)}=(\pi^{(a)}_{1},\dots,\pi^{(a)}_{n})
and
\pi^{(b)}=(\pi^{(b)}_{1},\dots,\pi^{(b)}_{n})
denote the rankings of
n
items (players) produced by graders
a
and
b
, respectively. For each unordered pair
(i,j)
with
i<j
, define:

\text{sign}_{a}(i,j)=\operatorname{sign}(\pi^{(a)}_{i}-\pi^{(a)}_{j}),\quad\text{sign}_{b}(i,j)=\operatorname{sign}(\pi^{(b)}_{i}-\pi^{(b)}_{j}).

A pair
(i,j)
is called
concordant
if
\text{sign}_{a}(i,j)=\text{sign}_{b}(i,j)
and
discordant
otherwise. Let
C
and
D
be the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs, respectively. Then Kendall’s Tau is defined as

\tau(a,b)\;=\;\frac{C-D}{\tbinom{n}{2}}\;\in[-1,1].

Here
\tau=1
indicates perfect agreement,
\tau=-1
perfect disagreement, and
\tau=0
statistical independence of the rankings.

Application to MultiTask.

In the MultiTask setting, each task
t\in\{1,\dots,9\}
yields a separate ranking of player models by each grader. For every round
r
and task
t
, we compute
\tau_{r,t}(a,b)
for all pairs of graders
(a,b)
using their per-round rankings of the nine players. Averaging over all rounds gives

\overline{\tau}_{t}(a,b)=\frac{1}{R_{t}}\sum_{r=1}^{R_{t}}\tau_{r,t}(a,b),

where
R_{t}
is the number of valid rounds for task
t
. This produces a
4\times 4
correlation matrix per task, summarizing grader consistency across domains such as mathematics, geoscience, and biology.

Application to SingleTask.

In the SingleTask setting, each round produces rubric-based scores across nine dimensions
\mathcal{D}=\{\text{Background/Creativity},\dots,\text{Impact/Logical~Coherence}\}
. For each dimension
d\in\mathcal{D}
, graders generate rankings of the players, and we compute
\tau_{r,d}(a,b)
for all pairs
(a,b)
. Averaging across rounds yields

\overline{\tau}_{d}(a,b)=\frac{1}{R_{d}}\sum_{r=1}^{R_{d}}\tau_{r,d}(a,b),

where
R_{d}
is the number of valid rounds for dimension
d
. This results in a
4\times 4
correlation matrix for each rubric dimension, capturing grader consistency in evaluating creativity, scientific rigor, and logical coherence across sections of the proposal.

Summary.

C.3
QK-Pair Overview

Unlike ensemble approaches that rely on static averaging or manually crafted heuristics, our framework enables fine-grained, task-dependent variability in response aggregation. It supports dynamic arbitration by adapting both model selection and integration strategies to the empirical strengths of each LLM on a per-task basis, thereby facilitating compositional reasoning across heterogeneous scientific inputs.

Tasks are categorized into two principal types according to the nature of supervision:

Ground-truth-based tasks.
This category includes multiple-choice questions, numerical derivations, and factual reasoning problems, where each model’s prediction can be directly compared against a known reference. Scoring in this regime is deterministic and fully automated.

Non-ground-truth tasks.
This category includes open-ended generation problems, such as scientific proposal writing, where no unique ground truth exists. Evaluation is therefore conducted by the AI Committee using rubric-based qualitative assessment, accounting for creativity, scientific rigor, and logical coherence.

To ensure reproducibility and modularity, all tasks are stored in a structured
(Q,K)
format, where
Q
denotes the input query and
K
the expected response (if applicable). In ground-truth settings,
K
provides the exact solution or numeric tolerance reference. In non-ground-truth settings,
K
instead encodes evaluation rubrics or stylistic constraints, guiding qualitative assessment rather than exact matching.

We evaluate the framework under two complementary protocols:

MultiTask.
Each round presents a batch of heterogeneous tasks spanning multiple scientific domains as shown in Figure
9
. The AI Committee jointly evaluates player responses, testing the arbitration system’s ability to generalize across diverse input types, modalities, and difficulty levels.

SingleTask.
Each round focuses on a scientific proposal generation as shown in Figure
10
, allowing the Committee to learn nuanced arbitration strategies within a fixed domain and structured output format.

D.3
AI Graders

A central component of our evaluation pipeline is the use of AI graders (arbitrators) to provide rankings of model outputs.
Unlike human experts, who bring domain expertise but are costly and inconsistent across annotators, AI graders offer scalable, replicable, and transparent evaluation.
Nevertheless, just as in human peer review, the presence of
bias
(systematic preference of a grader) and the degree of
consistency
(agreement across graders) are critical for ensuring the reliability of aggregated judgments.
The purpose of this section is to examine these properties in detail, thereby motivating our design choice of adopting multiple independent graders rather than relying on a single arbitrator.

Motivation.

Bias in grading arises when one arbitrator disproportionately favors certain ranks across repeated evaluations.
For example, a grader may consistently assign lower (more critical) ranks, while another may tend to be more lenient.
If such biases are not controlled, downstream evaluations may unfairly penalize or reward certain systems, obscuring the true effect of the Roundtable Policy.
Consistency, on the other hand, reflects whether different graders produce comparable relative orderings.
High consistency increases our confidence that the rankings reflect robust quality differences, while low consistency signals noise and arbitrariness.
By quantifying both bias and consistency, we establish the validity of our evaluation methodology.

Methodology.

We assess grader bias by visualizing the empirical rank distributions assigned by each arbitrator.
Figure
3
presents kernel density estimates of the rank distributions for four graders across both the SingleTask and MultiTask evaluations.
Systematic deviations in the distributions (e.g., skewed toward higher or lower ranks) indicate bias.
To measure consistency, we compute pairwise Kendall’s Tau correlations between graders’ rankings.
This non-parametric statistic captures the degree of ordinal agreement, with values close to
1
indicating strong concordance, values near
0
suggesting randomness, and negative values reflecting disagreement.
The results are shown as inset heatmaps in Figure
3
.

Findings.

In the SingleTask setting, we observe substantial variation in grader behavior.
The rank distributions are uneven, with some graders systematically harsher or more generous than others.
The Kendall’s Tau correlations confirm this observation, with moderate agreement values that indicate significant variability across graders.
Such findings echo common phenomena in human peer review, where individual reviewers bring idiosyncratic standards and biases.
By contrast, in the MultiTask setting, the distributions are more balanced, and the Kendall’s Tau values are consistently higher.
This suggests that when grading across diverse tasks, individual biases are averaged out, leading to more stable and reliable inter-grader consensus.

Implications.

These analyses reinforce two important principles.
First, reliance on a single grader is inherently risky due to potential bias; employing multiple graders and aggregating their judgments is essential for fairness.
Second, broader task coverage increases reliability, as MultiTask grading mitigates the influence of any single arbitrator’s bias.
Together, these results highlight the necessity of designing evaluation protocols that balance scalability with robustness, ensuring that AI grader-based assessments approximate the fairness and credibility traditionally associated with human expert panels.
