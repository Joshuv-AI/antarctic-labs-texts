1.
Introduction

In a general algebraic setting, if
R
is a commutative ring,
S
a multiplicatively closed subset of
R
and
G
an abelian group, there is a bijection

\left\{\begin{aligned} &RS^{-1}-\text{module}\\
&\text{ structures on }G\end{aligned}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{aligned} &R-\text{module structures on }G\\
&\text{that are }S-\text{divisible and }S-\text{torsion-free}\end{aligned}\right\}

However, in the case of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules, the structure is always unique. This means that an abelian group admits a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module structure if and only if it is
S
-divisible and
S
-torsion-free, which is a completely intrinsic characterization.

Furthemore, if an abelian group
A
is both a
R
-module and a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module at the same time, it is automatically a bimodule (this is for example helpful in Lemma
3.2.2
and Proposition
3.1.2
). Another useful feature is that any abelian group homomorphism between
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules is automatically
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-linear.

Within the context of
LCA
groups, things complicate slightly: Being a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module does not seem to guarantee that the scalar multiplication is continuous. Whether this happens or not can be summarized in the following way:

•

Given a
LCA
group
G
that is
S
-divisible and
S
-torsion-free, it is sufficient to check if the map

\displaystyle S\times G

\displaystyle\to G

\displaystyle(s,g)

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{s}g

is continuous.

•

Because
S
is discrete, the above is equivalent to saying that for each
s\in S
,
g\mapsto\frac{1}{s}g
is continuous, which is the same as
g\mapsto sg
being an automorphism of
G
(because of local compactness and Hausdorffness of
G
, this is also equivalent to saying that it is a proper map).

If
\mu
is a Haar measure on
G
and
s\in S
, multiplication by
s
being an automorphism implies that there exists some
\lambda(s)>0
such that
\mu(sE)=\lambda(s)\mu(E)
for any Borel set
E\subset G
. This means that multiplication by
s
”uniformly scales” subsets of
G
.

Even though it is not entirely clear what restrictions such property imposes on the topology of
G
, one sufficient condition for continuity of scalar multiplication to hold is
\sigma
-compactness, since it allows us to apply the
Open Mapping Theorem
.

Despite a lack of a satisfactory intrinsic characterization of locally compact topological
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules, they nonetheless admit Structure Theorems (Theorems
3.2.2
,
3.2.1
and
3.2.3
) which hold several similarities to those of
LCA
groups (and in a certain sense, they impose restrictions on the structure of those groups). The objective of this article is to prove them, while setting up relevant context before doing so.

From here on, any time one talks about a locally compact
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module, it is implicitly assumed that the module action is continuous. All groups and modules are taken to be topological and Hausdorff throughout.

The body of this paper is divided as follows:

•

Section
2
introduces some definitions and well known but essential results throughout subsections
2.1
-
2.4
. On subsection
2.5
, the notions of module of
Lie type
and
NSS
property are introduced for locally compact modules over a topological ring, and some results that are typically applied to topological groups (like the Open Mapping Theorem) are adapted to suit the context of this article.

•

On Section
3
, we first start by defining the ”ring of Adeles” associated to a localization
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
of the integers, as well as the ”finite Adeles” (Definition
3.1.1
). It is proved in Subsection
3.1
that any locally compact module over the latter is a
restricted product
of finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, as
p
ranges over a family of prime numbers
\Sigma
uniquely associated to
S
, before moving to the next subsection.

•

Subsection
3.2
is entirely dedicated to proving the Structure Theorems for
LC

\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules, and has a small corollary attached to it, which gives a classification of locally compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces.

•

Subsection
3.3
is rather small, and asks some questions about classifying
LC

\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules of Lie type.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.0.1
.

Let
G
and
G^{\prime}
be topological groups.
G
and
G^{\prime}
are said to be locally isomorphic if there exist open neighbourhoods
U\subset G
and
V\subset G^{\prime}
of
e_{G}
and
e_{G^{\prime}}
respectively, and a homeomorphism
f\colon U\to V
with the following properties:

•

For any
x,y\in U
such that
xy\in U
,
f(xy)=f(x)f(y)
.

•

For any
x\in U
such that
x^{-1}\in U
,
f(x^{-1})=f(x)^{-1}
.

•

Furthermore, if
G
and
G^{\prime}
are abelian and also left topological modules over a topological ring
R
, it is required that whenever
x\in U
and
r\in R
are such that
rx\in U
,
f(rx)=rf(x)
.

In particular, a
LCA
-group
G
is said to be of Lie type if it is locally isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Theorem 2.0.1

(Structure theorems)
.

Let
G\in LCA
.

(1)

G
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times H
, for some
n\geq 0
and
H
containing a compact open subgroup.

(2)

G
is compactly generated if and only if it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}\times K
, for some
n,k\geq 0
and
K
a compact abelian group.

(3)

G
is of Lie type if and only if it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{k}\times D
, where
n,k\geq 0
and
D
is discrete.

Because
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\cong(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{k}
and compact abelian groups are dual to discrete abelian groups, the second and third structure theorems allow us to conclude that compactly generated
LCA
-groups are in correspondence with
LCA
-groups of Lie type through Pontryagin Duality.

Chapter 4 of
[
1
]
is dedicated to proving these theorems.

2.1.
Annihilators and closed subgroups

Let
G\in LCA
. Its Pontryagin Dual is denoted
\widehat{G}
.

Definition 2.1.1
.

If
S\subset G
, we define
A(S)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{{\chi}\in\widehat{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ {\chi}|_{S}=1\}
and for
X\subset\widehat{G}
,
X^{\bot}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{g\in G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\chi(g)=1\ \text{for all}\ \chi\in X\}
. These sets are called the annihilator of
S
and orthogonal complement of
X
, respectively. It’s easy to check that
A(S)=A(<S>)=A(\overline{<S>})
for any
S\subset G
.

One can note that for
X\subset\widehat{G}
,
X^{\bot}=\eta_{G}^{-1}\left(A(X)\right)
, by
Pontryagin Duality
. So anything that holds for annihilators will also hold for orthogonal complements.

Proposition 2.1.1
.

If
G\in LCA
and H is a closed subgroup of G:

(1)

\widehat{G/H}\cong A(H)

(2)

A(H)^{\bot}=H

(3)

\widehat{G}/A(H)\cong\widehat{H}

The full proof won’t be given since it is standard, but the way one arrives at
1.
and
3.
is by
dualizing
the projection
\pi\colon G\to G/H
and inclusion
\iota\colon H\to G
respectively (and then checking the respective isomorphisms by doing some analysis).
2.
is a consequence of
1.
along with the fact that characters of
LCA
groups separate their elements.

Corollary 2.1.1
.

If
H\leq G
is closed, then

•

H is compact if and only if A(H) is open (co-discrete).

•

H is co-compact if and only if A(H) is discrete

This is concluded by the fact that compact abelian groups are dual to discrete abelian groups, along with the above Proposition.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

If
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a family of subsets of G, then

A(\bigcup_{i}S_{i})=\bigcap_{i}A(S_{i})

This is easy to check by the definition of annihilator.

Corollary 2.1.2
.

If
\{H_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a family of subgroups of G, then

A(\overline{\sum_{i}H_{i}})=A(\sum_{i}H_{i})=A(<\bigcup_{i}H_{i}>)=A(\bigcup_{i}H_{i})=\bigcap_{i}A(H_{i})

Furthermore, if they are closed, then

A(\bigcap_{i}H_{i})=\overline{\sum_{i}A(H_{i})}

Proof.

\bigcap_{i}H_{i}=\bigcap_{i}A(H_{i})^{\bot}=(\overline{\sum_{i}A(H_{i})})^{\bot}

by what was just seen, and because the
H_{i}
’s are closed. Applying annihilators to both sides, we get

A(\bigcap_{i}H_{i})=A((\overline{\sum_{i}A(H_{i})})^{\bot})=\overline{\sum_{i}A(H_{i})}

∎

2.2.
The subgroup of compact elements

The notion of elliptic
LCA
groups and their duality with totally disconnected
LCA
groups is helpful throughout this article.

Definition 2.2.1
.

If
x\in G
, x is said to be a compact element if
\overline{<x>}
is compact. C(G) will denote the set of all compact elements of G. It is also equal to the union of all compact subgroups of G, because of Hausdorffness.

Theorem 2.2.1
.

Given
G\in LCA
,

A(G_{0})=C(\widehat{G}),

where
G_{0}
is the connected component at the identity of
G
.

To prove this, the following lemma is useful:

Lemma 2.2.1
.

Let
G\in LCA
be totally disconnected. Then for any
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, there exists a compact open subgroup H of G such that
\psi(H)={1}
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
an open neighbourhood of 1 that does not contain any nontrivial subgroups. Then
\psi^{-1}\left(U\right)
is an open neighbourhood of 0 in G. By Theorem
A.0.1
, there exists a compact open subgroup H of G such that
H\subset\psi^{-1}\left(U\right)
.

\psi\left(H\right)
will then be a subgroup of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
contained in U, therefore it is trivial.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2.1
.

If
\chi\in A(G_{0})
, then by the construction done in the first part of
2.1.1
, we get a character
\psi\colon G/G_{0}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
given by
\psi(g+G_{0})=\chi(g)
.
G/G_{0}
is totally disconnected by Lemma
A.0.1
, so there exists some
X\leq G/G_{0}
compact and open such that whenever
g+G_{0}\in X
,
\chi(g)=\psi(g+G_{0})=1
. Therefore,
H=\pi^{-1}\left(X\right)
is an open subgroup of G on which
\chi
acts trivially.

Conversely, if
\chi
acts trivially in some open subgroup H, then since by Lemma
A.0.1
,
G_{0}
is necessarily contained in H, it follows that
\chi\in A(G_{0})
. This together with Corollary
2.1.1
implies

A(G_{0})=\bigcup_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}H\leq G\\
Hopen\end{subarray}}}A(H)=\bigcup_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}X\leq\widehat{G}\\
X\ compact\end{subarray}}}X=C(\widehat{G})

∎

Note that there would be no reason to assume that
C(\widehat{G})
is closed since it is an arbitrary union of closed sets. This result also implies, by Pontryagin duality, that
C(G)
is always a closed subgroup of G.

Corollary 2.2.1
.

For
G\in LCA
,
C(G)=G
if and only if
\widehat{G}
is totally disconnected, and
C(G)=0
if and only if
\widehat{G}
is connected.

Furthermore,
\widehat{G/C(G)}\cong A(C(G))=(\widehat{G})_{0}
, which is connected, therefore
G/C(G)
has no nontrivial compact subgroups. This can be seen as an analogue of the fact that for any discrete abelian group
D
,
D/T(D)
is torsion-free.

In particular, if
K
is a compact abelian group,
A(K_{0})=T(\widehat{K})
, the torsion subgroup of
\widehat{K}
, which means that,
K
is connected if and only if
\widehat{K}
is torsion-free and
K
is totally disconnected if and only if
\widehat{K}
is a torsion abelian group.

Corollary 2.2.2

(Definition of elliptic)
.

C(G)=G
if and only if
G
is a union of compact and open subgroups. In this situation,
G
is called elliptic (local compactness will be assumed by default in the definition of elliptic).

Proof.

If
C(G)=G
, then
\widehat{G}
is totally disconnected, therefore

\bigcap_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}X\leq\widehat{G}\\
X\ open\ and\ compact\end{subarray}}}X=\{1\},

which by Corollary
2.1.2
implies

\overline{\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}H\leq G\\
H\ open\ and\ compact\end{subarray}}}H}=G.

But the sum of all compact and open subgroups of
G
is an open subgroup, (since it is the sum of a non-empty open set with something else) and therefore closed. At the same time, this sum is also equal to the union of all compact and open subgroups of G:

If
x
belongs to that sum, then there exist compact open subgroups
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}
such that
x\in H_{1}+\dots+H_{n}
and
H_{1}+\dots+H_{n}
is also a compact and open subgroup, so x is in the mentioned union. In conclusion,

G\quad=\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}H\leq G\\
H\ open\ and\\
compact\end{subarray}}}H\quad=\quad\bigcup_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}H\leq G\\
H\ open\ and\\
compact\end{subarray}}}H

∎

Corollary 2.2.3
.

If
G\in LCA
is elliptic, any compact subset of
G
is contained in a compact open subgroup. In particular, any compactly generated subgroup of
G
has compact closure.

Proof.

Let
K\subset G
be compact. For each
x\in K
, let
H_{x}
be a compact open subgroup of
G
containing
x
. Then by compactness of
K
,

K\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}H_{x_{i}}\subset H=\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{x_{i}},

for
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in K
. So
H
is a compact open subgroup of
G
containing
K
.
∎

2.3.
Pontryagin Dual of a module

Let R be a topological ring and
M\in LC_{R}M
(that is, M is a locally compact left topological
R
-module). Then for
\chi\in\widehat{M}
and
r\in R
, we can define

\displaystyle\chi^{r}\colon

\displaystyle M\ \to\mathbb{S}^{1}

\displaystyle m\mapsto\chi(rm)

\chi^{r}
is still an element of
\widehat{M}
because M is a topological R-module.
It is easy to check that for all
\chi,\psi\in\widehat{M}
and
r,r^{\prime}\in R
:

(1)

\chi^{1_{R}}=\chi

(2)

(\chi\psi)^{r}=\chi^{r}\psi^{r}

(3)

\chi^{rr^{\prime}}={(\chi^{r})}^{r^{\prime}}

(4)

\chi^{r+r^{\prime}}=\chi^{r}\chi^{r^{\prime}}

So
\widehat{M}
becomes a right (not necessarily topological) R-module, under multiplicative notation.

Theorem 2.3.1
.

If R is locally compact, then
\widehat{M}
is a topological R-module with respect to the above construction.

Proof.

We need to show that the map

\displaystyle\widehat{M}\times R\

\displaystyle\to\widehat{M}

\displaystyle(\chi,r)

\displaystyle\mapsto\chi^{r}

is continuous. We show this through net convergence: Let
(\chi_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net converging to
\chi\in\widehat{M}
and
(r_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converging to
r\in R
.

\chi_{\alpha}^{r_{\alpha}}\chi^{-r}=\chi_{\alpha}^{r_{\alpha}}\chi^{-r-r_{\alpha}+r_{\alpha}}=(\chi_{\alpha}\chi^{-1})^{r_{\alpha}}\chi^{r_{\alpha}-r}.

We want to show that the above expression converges to the trivial character uniformly on compact sets. Note that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(\chi_{\alpha}\chi^{-1})^{r_{\alpha}}\chi^{r_{\alpha}-r}-1\right\rvert}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(\chi_{\alpha}\chi^{-1})^{r_{\alpha}}-1\right\rvert}\ +\ \mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi^{r_{\alpha}-r}-1\right\rvert}
.

Let
V
be a compact neighbourhood of
0_{R}
,
K
be a compact subset of M and
\epsilon>0
. Then
r+V
is a compact neighbourhood of r and there exists
\beta_{1}
such that
\alpha\geq\beta_{1}\ \implies r_{\alpha}\in r+V
. For
\alpha\geq\beta_{1}
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha}(r_{\alpha}m)\chi(-r_{\alpha}m)-1\right\rvert}=\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha}(r_{\alpha}m)-\chi\left(r_{\alpha}m\right)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{m\in(r+V)K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha}(m)-\chi(m)\right\rvert}

Since
\chi_{\alpha}\longrightarrow\chi
and
(r+V)K
is compact, there exists
\beta_{2}
such that for
\alpha\geq\beta_{2}
, the last supremum becomes smaller than
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. So we take
\beta
to be some index that is bigger than both
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
.

Let
f\colon R\times M\to M
be the R-module multiplication in M. Then
f\arrowvert_{(r+V)\times K}
is uniformly continuous by Theorem
A.0.3
, so for any neighbourhood U of
0
in M, there exists an open set
A\subset R\times M
containing
(0_{R},0)
such that whenever
(r_{1},m_{1}),(r_{2},m_{2})\in(r+V)\times K
and
(r_{1}-r_{2},m_{1}-m_{2})\in A
,
r_{1}m_{1}-r_{2}m_{2}\in U
.

In particular, A will contain
W\times\{0\}
, for some neighbourhood of
0_{R}
, W.

Let
\tau_{1}
be such that
\alpha\geq\tau_{1}\implies r_{\alpha}-r\in W
and
\tau_{2}
such that
\alpha\geq\tau_{2}\implies r_{\alpha}\in r+V
. Then if
\tau
is some upper bound of
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
, we get that for
\alpha\geq\tau
and any
m\in K
,

(r_{\alpha},m),(r,m)\in(r+V)\times K,\ (r_{\alpha}-r,m-m)=(r_{\alpha}-r,0)\in W\times\{0\}\subset A.

So for
\alpha\geq\tau
, we get that
r_{\alpha}m-rm\in U
, for any
m\in K
.
In particular, let
U=\chi^{-1}\left(\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\}\right)
.
Then

\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi(r_{\alpha}m)-\chi(rm)\right\rvert}=\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi(r_{\alpha}m-rm)-1\right\rvert}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

for
\alpha\geq\tau
.

Let
\sigma\geq\beta,\tau
. Then for
\alpha\geq\sigma
, we get that

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha}^{r_{\alpha}}(m)\chi^{-r}(m)-1\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{\alpha}(r_{\alpha}m)-\chi\left(r_{\alpha}m\right)\right\rvert}+\sup_{m\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi(r_{\alpha}m)-\chi(rm)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon

In other words,
\chi_{\alpha}^{r_{\alpha}}
converges uniformly on compact sets to
\chi^{r}
, so
\widehat{M}
is a topological R-module.
∎

Proposition 2.3.1
.

Let
M\in LC_{R}M
. The Pontryagin map
\eta_{M}
is R-linear, therefore
M
and
\widehat{\widehat{M}}
are isomorphic as topological R-modules. Besides that, if
f\colon M\to N
is a continuous R-linear homomorphism, then so is
\widehat{f}
(see Definition
B.1.1
)

Proof.

Let
r\in R
,
m\in M
and
\chi\in\widehat{M}
.

{}^{r}\eta_{M}(m)(\chi)=\eta_{M}(m)(\chi^{r})=\chi^{r}(m)=\chi(rm)=\eta_{M}(rm)(\chi)

In other words,
{}^{r}\eta_{M}(m)=\eta_{M}(rm)
.

If
f\colon M\to N
is R-linear, then given
\psi\in\widehat{N}
,
m\in M
and
r\in R
,

\widehat{f}(\psi^{r})(m)=\psi^{r}\circ f(m)=\psi(rf(m))=\psi(f(rm))=\psi\circ f(rm)=(\widehat{f}(\psi))^{r}(m)

So
\widehat{f}(\psi^{r})=(\widehat{f}(\psi))^{r}

∎

Therefore, we get a contravariant functor
F_{R}
from
LC_{R}M
to
LCM_{R}
.
Since continuous R-linear maps are in particular continuous group homomorphisms,
\eta
will still make the respective diagrams commute, so
\eta
will still be a natural isomorphism between the identity functor in
LC_{R}M
and
F_{R^{op}}\circ F_{R}
. As such, we get an equivalence of categories

LC_{R}M\cong(LCM_{R})^{op}\cong(LC_{R^{op}}M)^{op}

This implies that we are also allowed to swap direct and inverse limits when talking about modules, and that
F_{R}
commutes with finite products in general, up to isomorphism. It also does the same thing with direct sums of discrete left
R
-modules and products of compact right
R
-modules.

Proposition 2.3.2
.

If
M\in LC_{R}M
and
S\subset M
is closed under scalar multiplication, then
A(S)
is a closed submodule of
\widehat{M}
.

Proof.

We already know it is a closed subgroup. Let
\chi\in A(S)
and
r\in R
. Given
s\in S
,
\chi^{r}(s)=\chi(rs)=1
, since
rs\in S
. Therefore,
\chi^{r}\in A(S)
.
∎

So annihilators will establish a correspondence between closed R-submodules of M and closed R-submodules of its dual, and the same isomorphisms from Proposition
2.1.1
hold.

Corollary 2.3.1
.

M_{0}
and
C(M)
are always closed R-submodules of M.

Proof.

Combining Theorem
2.2.1
and Proposition
2.3.2
, it suffices to show that
M_{0}
is a closed under scalar multiplication.

Let
r\in R
. Then
rM_{0}
is a connected subgroup of
M
, so it must be contained in
M_{0}
, which proves the statement.
∎

It is easy to see that if
R
is an elliptic ring, then because it is also assumed to be unital, any topological module over it will be elliptic. In the locally compact case, Pontryagin Duality along with Lemma
2.2.1
allows us to conclude that any
LC
topological module over
R
is both elliptic and totally disconnected.

2.4.
LC modules over the profinite integers

Locally compact modules over
the profinite integers

\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
can be intrinsically characterized, and this will be useful for Lemma
3.2.2
. A very useful fact about
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
is that the integers are a dense subring. This implies that any topological module structure over
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
is unique, and any closed subgroup of a topological
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
-module is a submodule.

Proposition 2.4.1
.

Let
K
be a compact abelian group. Then
K
is totally disconnected (equivalent to being profinite as an abelian group in this case) if and only if
K\in LC_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}}M
.

Proof.

If
K\in LC_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}}M
, it has to be totally disconnected by the final comment in the previous subsection.

Conversely, if
K
is totally disconnected, then
A=\widehat{K}
is a discrete torsion abelian group (Corollary
2.2.1
). So given any
x=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}c_{i}i!\in\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
and
a\in A
, the expression
(\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}c_{i}i!)a
is well defined, because
a
has finite order. This allows us to define a natural
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
-module structure on
A
. The scalar multiplication

\displaystyle f\colon\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times A

\displaystyle\to A

\displaystyle(x,a)

\displaystyle\mapsto xa

being continuous is equivalent to it being locally constant, since
A
is discrete. But given
(x,a)\in\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times A
with
na=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can choose
U=(x+n\hat{\mathbb{Z}})\times\{a\}
, and we clearly have that
f(u)=xa
, for any
u\in U
. So if
A
is torsion, it admits a topological
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
-module structure, which implies
K\in LC_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}}M
as well by Theorem
2.3.1
.

∎

Theorem 2.4.1
.

Let
G\in LCA
. Then
G\in LC_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}}M
if and only if
G
is elliptic and totally disconnected.

Proof.

The first implication is a result of
G
and
\widehat{G}
being both elliptic.

So suppose
G
is elliptic and totally disconnected. By what was seen in Corollary
2.2.2
,
G
is a union of compact open subgroups, and all of those will also be totally disconnected. So in this case,
G
is a union of profinite open subgroups
(P_{i})_{i\in I}
. For each
i\in I
, let
f_{i}\colon\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times P_{i}\ \to P_{i}\subset G
the topological
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
-module structure in
P_{i}
.

f_{i}|_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times(P_{j}\cap P_{i})}=f_{j}|_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times(P_{j}\cap P_{i})}
for all
i,j\in I
because the topological
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
-module strucures are all unique. Since
(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times P_{i})_{i\in I}
is an open cover of
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times G
, we can extend to a unique continuous function
f\colon\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times G\to G
such that
f|_{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\times P_{i}}=f_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

We note now that
f
gives
G
a module strucure over
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
given by
x\cdot g=f(x,g)
(and the action is continuous, since
f
is):

Let
x,y\in\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
,
g,h\in G
with
g\in P_{i}
and
h\in P_{j}
.

(1)

1\cdot g=f_{i}(1,g)=g
.

(2)

(x+y)\cdot g=f_{i}(x+y,g)=f_{i}(x,g)+f_{i}(y,g)=x\cdot g+y\cdot g

(3)

(xy)\cdot g=f_{i}(xy,g)=f_{i}(x,f_{i}(y,g))=x\cdot(y\cdot g)

(4)

x\cdot(g+h)=f_{ij}(x,g+h)
, where
P_{ij}=P_{i}+P_{j}
.

f_{ij}(x,g+h)=f_{ij}(x,g)+f_{ij}(y,g)=x\cdot g+y\cdot g
, because
x,y\in P_{i}+P_{j}
.

So if
G
is elliptic and totally disconnected, it admits a topological module structure over
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
.

∎

There is another definition which intends to generalize the notion of

If

Let

3.
Structure Theorems for LC modules over localizations of the integers

3.1.
Rings of ”Adeles” associated to localizations

The rings defined below, among other things, have no compact open
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules and are self-dual. They are quite helpful for the Structure Theorems over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
because they allow to ”separate” any
LC

\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module into a component that can be described numerically and a component that has a compact open submodule.

Definition 3.1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a set of prime numbers,
S
the multiplicative submonoid of
\mathbb{N}
generated by
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}\subset\mathbb{Q}
the localization of
\mathbb{Z}
with respect to
S
.

•

The ring of Adeles associated to
S
, denoted by
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
, is the restricted product
\mathbb{R}\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(\mathbb{Q}_{q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})
.

•

The ring of finite Adeles associated to
S
, denoted by
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
, is the above restricted product without the
\mathbb{R}
component.

•

The ring of
\Sigma
-adic integers is the product
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. It is in particular a quotient of
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
and is also a compact open subring of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
.

All of these are locally compact topological rings, and they will be quite important for locally compact
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules. In particular,
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
will have an analogous role in
LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
that
\mathbb{R}
has in
LCA
.

Example 3.1.1
.

If
\Sigma=\mathbb{P}
, the set of all primes, then
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}=\mathbb{Q}
and indeed,
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\prod_{q\in\Sigma}^{{}^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is typically known as the ring of Adeles of the rational numbers, and the ring of
\mathbb{P}
-integers is
\prod_{q\ prime}\mathbb{Z}_{q}\cong\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
.

If we instead take
\Sigma=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
as a finite set of primes, then
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}=Z[1/n]
, where
n=p_{1}\dots p_{n}
, and the ring of
\Sigma
-adic integers is the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(the ring of n-adic integers).

Lemma 3.1.1
.

For each
p\in\Sigma
,
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is a quotient of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
.

The obvious kernel of this quotient will be constantly denoted as
\prod_{q\in\Sigma\setminus\{p\}}\mathbb{Q}_{q}
.

Proof.

Consider the projection
\pi_{p}\colon\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. It is a surjective group homomorphism, so we only need to check continuity and openness at the identity. Because
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
is
\sigma
-compact, continuity suffices to prove both, by the Open Mapping Theorem.

Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\pi_{p}^{-1}\left(p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\right)
can be written as
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}X_{q}
, where

X_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}(\mathbb{Q}_{q},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),\ \text{if}\ q\neq p\\
(p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p},p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\ otherwise,\end{cases}

since this restricted product is clearly a subset of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
. It is also open since it can be written as

\bigcup_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}g\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{Z}\\
|supp(g)|<+\infty\\
g(p)=n\end{subarray}}}\quad\quad\left(\prod_{q\in\Sigma}q^{g(q)}\mathbb{Z}_{q}\right),

which is an union of open sets.

Therefore
\pi_{p}
is continuous, which proves the result.

∎

The relation between
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
is somewhat similar to that between
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, for a fixed prime p, as will be seen in the two following propositions.

Proposition 3.1.1
.

\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
can be algebraically seen as the localization of
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
over

, as an abelian group.

Proof.

Let
(x_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}

v_{q}(x_{q})<0\end{subarray}}}q^{-v_{q}(x_{q})}\in S,

and we get that

(x_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}=\frac{1}{s}(y_{q})_{q\in\Sigma},

where each

.
∎

Proposition 3.1.2
.

Let

and let

is generated over

Proof.

such that

. Note as well that by Proposition

for some

Therefore,

∎

Proposition 4-13 of

Before doing that however, it is useful to note that restricted products of locally compact vector spaces over fields of p-adic numbers do not depend on the choice of compact open subgroups, by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
(Q_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}
be a family of
LCA

is a topological
\mathbb{Q}_{q}
-vector space, and let

be a compact open subgroup of

for each

(n_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}
, where each

and

\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(Q_{q},Z_{q})\cong\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}},\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}}),

Because of this, restricted products of this form will be denoted as
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}^{{}^{\prime}}Q_{q}\cong\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}}
.

As a corollary, we also have that
Q=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}Q_{q}
has no small
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules (because the identity neighbourhood
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}}\subset\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}}
does not contain any nontrivial
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules).

Proof.

By Proposition 4-13 of
[
2
]
, for each
p\in\Sigma
, there exists an isomorphism
\phi_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q_{p}\to\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n_{p}}

As

is a compact open subgroup of

\phi_{p}
is of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{n_{p}}p^{k_{i,p}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, with

, by Corollary

. But
\phi_{p}
can be composed with the automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n_{p}}
that is multiplication by
(p^{-k_{i,p}})_{i=1}^{n_{p}}
and therefore we can suppose without loss of generality that
\phi_{p}(Z_{p})=\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n_{p}}
.

So we can consider

\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(Q_{q},Z_{q})

\displaystyle\to\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(Q_{q}^{n_{q}},\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}})

\displaystyle(v_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\phi_{q}(v_{q}))_{q\in\Sigma}.

f
is a composition of homomorphisms, so it is an abelian group homomorphism (making it
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-linear), and it is bijective as a consequence of each
\phi_{p}
being an isomorphism that sends
Z_{p}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n_{p}}
.

By Proposition
3.1.2
,
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}},\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}})
is
\sigma
-compact, so as long as
f
is open, which corresponds to saying that its inverse is continuous,
f
will also be continuous and therefore an isomorphism by the Open Mapping Theorem.

It suffices to check openness at the identity, so let

U=\prod_{q\in\Sigma}U_{q},

where each
U_{q}
is a compact open subgroup of
Q_{q}
, of the form
\phi_{q}^{-1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{q}}q^{l_{i,q}}\mathbb{Z}_{q}\right)
, for
l_{i,q}\in\mathbb{Z}
, with all but a finite number of the
U_{q}
’s different from
Z_{q}
.

We have that

f(U)=\prod_{q\in\Sigma}(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{q}}q^{l_{i,q}}\mathbb{Z}_{q}).

This image is a subgroup of
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}},\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}})
that contains
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}q^{l_{q}}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}}
, where
l_{q}=\max_{i=1,\dots,n_{q}}\{l_{i,q}\}
. Because all but finitely many of the
U_{q}
’s are different from
Z_{q}
, it follows that all but finitely many of the
l_{q}
’s are non-zero, therefore
(q^{-l_{q}})_{q\in\Sigma}
is an element of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
.

As such,
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}q^{l_{q}}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}}
is homeomorphic to
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n_{q}}
, through multiplication by
(q^{-l_{q}})_{q\in\Sigma}
(since it is an invertible element in
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
), therefore open.

In other words,
f(U)
is a subgroup with non-empty interior, which makes it open. As
f
sends basic open sets at the identity to open sets,
f
is open.
∎

Corollary 3.1.1
.

For
(Q_{q},Z_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}
as above and
Q=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}Q_{q}
,
Q
is self-dual as a topological
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module.

Proof.

Since additive functions between
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules are linear, it suffices to prove that
Q\cong\widehat{Q}
as topological abelian groups. By Theorem
C.0.1
,

\widehat{Q}\cong\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(\widehat{Q_{q}},A(Z_{q})).

Since each
A(Z_{q})
is a compact open subgroup of
\widehat{Q_{q}}
and each
\widehat{Q_{q}}
is a topological
\mathbb{Q}_{q}
-vector space isomorphic to
Q_{q}\cong\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}}
(this isomorphism exists because the
p
-adic numbers are self-dual), it follows from Theorem
3.1.1
that
Q\cong\widehat{Q}
.
∎

Theorem 3.1.2
.

Let
Q\in LC_{\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}},f}M
. Then
Q\cong\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}Q_{q}
, where
Q_{q}
is a topological
\mathbb{Q}_{q}
-vector space for each
q\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a compact open subgroup of
Q
(in particular it will be a
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
-submodule). By Proposition
3.1.2
, we have
Q=\bigcup_{s\in S}\frac{1}{s}C
. For each
p\in\Sigma
, let
Q_{p}=Ann_{Q}(\prod_{q\in\Sigma\setminus p}^{{}^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{q})
. Then
Q_{p}
is a locally compact topological
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-vector space by Corollary
3.1.1
and Lemma
A.0.2
, therefore isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n_{p}}
for some
n_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, by Proposition 4-13 of
[
2
]
.

At the same time,
Z_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{p}\cap C
is a compact open subgroup of
Q_{p}
and in particular a topological
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-submodule of
Q_{p}
.

For
p\in\Sigma
, let
e_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\delta_{q,p})_{q\in\Sigma}
denote the unit vector of
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
associated to
p
. Then given
m\in Q
,
e_{p}m\in Q_{p}
and if
m\in C
,
e_{p}m\in Z_{p}
.

We can first consider the map

\displaystyle f\colon C

\displaystyle\to\prod_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\mapsto(e_{q}c)_{q\in\Sigma}.

f
is a continuous group homomorphism and also injective: If
e_{q}c=0
for all
q\in\Sigma
, then
c=1_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}}c=(\sum_{p\in\Sigma}e_{q})c=\sum_{q\in\Sigma}e_{q}c=0
.

As for surjectivity, note that the image of
f
is closed in
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}
by compactness of
C
, therefore if it contains
\bigoplus_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}
, it has to be equal to the whole space. Given
(v_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}\in\bigoplus_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}
, with
v_{q_{1}},\dots,v_{q_{k}}\neq 0
, just consider
c=\sum_{i=0}^{k}v_{q_{i}}
, and we have that for
p\in\Sigma
,
e_{p}v_{q_{i}}
is
0
if
p\neq q_{i}
(by definition) and
v_{q_{i}}
otherwise (since in this case,
e_{p}
acts like the identity on
Z_{p}
, due to its unique
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module structure). Therefore,
f(c)=(v_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}
and
f
is surjective. Because
C
is compact,
f
is an isomorphism of topological groups.

Since
Q=\bigcup_{s\in S}\frac{1}{s}C
, we can lift
f
to the
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-homomorphism

\displaystyle\overline{f}\colon Q

\displaystyle\to\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(Q_{q},Z_{q})

\displaystyle m=\frac{1}{s}c

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{s}f(c).

This is well defined because because for
s\in S
and
p\in\Sigma
not dividing
s
,
\frac{1}{s}e_{p}c\in Z_{p}
for all
c\in C
(this matters since only a finite number of primes divide
s
, guaranteeing that
\frac{1}{s}f(c)
is in the restricted product), and because the family
(\frac{1}{s}C)_{s\in S}
is directed with respect to inclusion: If
s,t\in S
, then
\frac{1}{s}C,\frac{1}{t}C\subset\frac{1}{st}C
.

\overline{f}
is an extension of a continuous group homomorphism, therefore continuous, and is injective because of how it’s defined. Surjectivity of
\overline{f}
follows from surjectivity of
f
and from the fact that

\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}Q_{q}=\bigcup_{s\in S}\frac{1}{s}(\prod_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}).

(
\prod_{q\in\Sigma}Z_{q}
is a compact open subgroup of
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}(Q_{q},Z_{q})
by Proposition
C.0.1
and we can apply Proposition
3.1.2
).

So
\overline{f}
is a continuous bijective
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-homomorphism and since we know that
Q
is
\sigma
-compact, it follows by the open mapping Theorem that
\overline{f}
is an isomorphism.
∎

Corollary 3.1.2
.

Let
L\in LC_{\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}}M
. Then
L\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}}
as a topological
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module, for
n\geq 0
and
n_{q}\geq 0
for all
q\in\Sigma
. In particular,
L
is self-dual by Corollary
3.1.1
, compactly generated (over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
) and has no small submodules.

Proof.

We can consider
L^{\prime}=Ann_{L}(0\times\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})
and
L^{\prime\prime}=Ann_{L}(\mathbb{R}\times 0)
. Both of these are closed
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules of
M
, and admit topological
\mathbb{R}
-vector space and
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
-module structures respectively, which makes them
\sigma
-compact in particular.

Clearly,
L=L^{\prime}+L^{\prime\prime}
and
L^{\prime}\cap L^{\prime\prime}=0
, so by Corollary
2.5.2
,
L\cong L^{\prime}\times L^{\prime\prime}
and we can apply the two above theorems to get the isomorphism.

L is compactly generated and has no small submodules because it is a product of two
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
topological modules with both of those properties.
∎

Because of the above Corollary and Theorem,
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
and
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
-modules will be referred to without using any restricted product notation from now on, and they can be treated in the same way that finite dimensional real vector spaces are treated (since each such module is uniquely characterized by a sequence of dimensions).

3.2.
Structure Theorems

It is first important to note that for any localization of
\mathbb{Z}
, we can find a set of primes uniquely associated to it that allows us to do a similar analysis to that of the previous subsection.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
be multiplicatively closed. Then there exists
Z\subset\mathbb{Z}
also multiplicatively closed such that
Z
is multiplicatively generated by a set of prime numbers and
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}=\mathbb{Z}Z^{-1}
:

Just take
Z
to be the submonoid of
\mathbb{N}
generated by all prime factors of elements of
S
. Since
S\subset Z
, clearly
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}\subset\mathbb{Z}Z^{-1}
.

Conversely, if
\frac{1}{z}\in\mathbb{Z}Z^{-1}
, with
z=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
and each
\alpha_{i}\geq 1
, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
take
s_{i}\in S
such that
s_{i}=p_{i}a_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\frac{1}{z}=\frac{1}{p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p^{\alpha_{n}}}=\frac{a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots a_{n}^{\alpha^{n}}}{s_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots s_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}\in\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}.

As
1Z^{-1}\subset\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
, it follows that
\mathbb{Z}Z^{-1}\subset\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
.

This means that we can always suppose without loss of generality that
S
is generated by a unique set of prime numbers
\Sigma
, and we will do so.

We can now start the process of proving the Structure Theorems in
LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
. Unlike in the classical case for
LCA
groups, where the Theorems are proven in order, we will start by proving the 2nd Structure Theorem, which means characterizing the compactly generated modules.

The lemma below is very simple, but it is one of the main factors that facilitates their study:

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Let
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
with
(M,+)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times H
as given by the 1st Structure Theorem for
LCA
groups. Then
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0
is a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodule of
M
. In particular,
H
is also a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module (by being a quotient of
M
).

Proof.

Given
(x,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0
and
s\in S
, we can note that
s(\frac{1}{s}x,0)=(x,0)=(s\frac{1}{s})(x,0)
. The fact that
M
is a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module implies therefore that
\frac{1}{s}(x,0)=(\frac{1}{s}x,0)
which means that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0
is a closed submodule of
M
.

∎

Because of this, we can suppose without loss of generality that
M
contains a compact open subgroup.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

Let
M\in LC_{ZS^{-1}}M
as above be compactly generated. Then
M\cong(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times C(M)
for some
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
M
contains a compact open subgroup,
M/C(M)
is discrete and compactly generated, which makes it finitely generated. At the same time, it contains no nontrivial finite subgroups by Corollary
2.2.1
. In particular, it is torsion free over
\mathbb{Z}
, which is equivalent to being torsion free over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
. Therefore, by the Structure Theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID,
M/C(M)
is free of finite dimension, which means we get a continuous surjective map
M\to(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}
for some
k\geq 0
, with kernel
C(M)
. By Corollary
2.5.1
,
M\cong(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times C(M)
.
∎

The above lemma and proposition, combined, tell us that it suffices to classify elliptic compactly generated modules over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
in order to arrive at the 2nd Structure Theorem.
However, it is useful to first look at the totally disconnected ones.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

Let
N\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
be compactly generated, elliptic and totally disconnected. Then
N\cong Q\times C
, where
Q
is a topological module over
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
, and
C
is a compact
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-topological module.

Proof.

N
is a topological module over
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\prod_{q\in\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{Z}_{q}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\times\prod_{q\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
, by Theorem
2.4.1
. With this identification of the profinite integers, we write its elements in the form
(x,y)
where
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
and
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
.

Let
N^{\prime}=Ann_{N}(0\times\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma})
and
N^{\prime\prime}=Ann_{N}(\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\times 0)
.

N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime\prime}
are closed subgroups of
N
, and can also be easily seen to be
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules.

Given
n\in N
, we have
n=(1,1)n=(1,0)n+(0,1)n\in N^{\prime\prime}+N^{\prime}
, therefore
N=N^{\prime}+N^{\prime\prime}
. It is also clear that
N^{\prime}\cap N^{\prime\prime}=0
. Since
N
is compactly generated over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
, it is also
\sigma
-compact, which implies that
N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime\prime}
have the same property. As such,
N\cong N^{\prime}\times N^{\prime\prime}
by Corollary
2.5.2
.

N^{\prime}
is a topological module over
\frac{\hat{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
and over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
at the same time. By Proposition
3.1.1
,
N^{\prime}
admits an obvious module structure over
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
. The action given by this structure is also continuous, because
\bigcup_{s\in S}\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\times N^{\prime}
is an open cover of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}\times N^{\prime}
, and the restriction of the action to each
\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}\times N^{\prime}
is the composition of the continuous
\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
-module structure of
N^{\prime}
with multiplication by
\frac{1}{s}\in\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
. Therefore
N^{\prime}
is a topological module over
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
and we can take
Q=N^{\prime}
.

As for
N^{\prime\prime}
, note that since it is a quotient of
N
, it is also compactly generated over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
, while being a topological module over
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
at the same time. In particular, we can write
N^{\prime\prime}=\bigcup_{s\in S}\frac{1}{s}K
, where
K
is some compact open subgroup of
N^{\prime\prime}
, by Corollary
2.2.3
. But given
s\in S
,
s
is not divisible by any prime
q\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma
, which makes it invertible in each associated
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

As such, given
k\in K
,

\frac{1}{s}k=\frac{1}{s}((1_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}})_{q\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}\ k)=(\frac{1_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}}{s})_{q\in\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}K.

As
K
is a closed subgroup of
N^{\prime\prime}
, it is also a
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-submodule, therefore
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}K\subset K
and
\frac{1}{s}K\subset K
for every
s\in S
. In other words,
N^{\prime\prime}=K
is compact and we can take
C=N^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2.1
.

Let
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
be compactly generated and elliptic. Then
M\cong Q\times K
, where
Q
is a topological
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
-module and
K
is a compact
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module such that
K/K_{0}
is a topological
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-module and
K_{0}\cong M_{0}
. For convenience purposes, we will simply say that
K
is a compact
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
extension by
M_{0}
(since there exists a short exact sequence
0\longrightarrow M_{0}\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow K/K_{0}\longrightarrow 0
).

Proof.

Since
M/M_{0}
is in the conditions of the above Lemma,
M/M_{0}\cong T\times C
, where
T
is a topological
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f}
-module and
C
is a compact topological
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-module. Therefore,

C(\widehat{M})=A(M_{0})\cong\widehat{M/M_{0}}\cong\widehat{T}\times B,

where
B=\widehat{C}
is a discrete topological
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-module and therefore in particular, torsion. We can suppose that
\widehat{T}
and
B
are contained in
C(\widehat{M})
, with
\widehat{T}+B=C(\widehat{M})
and
\widehat{T}\cap B=0
, for convenience.
Since
\widehat{T}
is in particular a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
, it is injective as a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module.

At the same time, because of
B
being discrete,
\widehat{T}
is an open injective submodule of
C(\widehat{M})
which is also open in
\widehat{M}
(since
\widehat{M}
contains a compact open subgroup). As such, by Corollary
2.5.3
, there exists a discrete submodule
D
of
\widehat{M}
such that
\widehat{M}\cong\widehat{T}\times D
(in particular,
D\cap\widehat{T}=0
as well). Because
B
is a torsion abelian group and
\widehat{T}
is torsion-free (reminder that
T\cong\widehat{T}
),
B
has to be contained in
D
and indeed, we can note that

C(D)=D\cap C(\widehat{M})=D\cap(\widehat{T}+B)=D\cap B=B,

where the third equality holds because
B\subset D
and
D\cap\widehat{T}=0
.

Dualizing, we get that

M\cong T\times K,

where
K=\widehat{D}
is compact and
K_{0}=A_{D}(C(D))=A(B)
.

K/K_{0}=\widehat{D}/A_{D}(C(D))\cong\widehat{C(D)}=\widehat{B}\cong C
is a topological
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
module.

K_{0}\cong M_{0}
because under the above isomorphism,
M_{0}\cong 0\times K_{0}
.
∎

With this, we can arrive at the 2nd Structure Theorem for
LC
modules over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
.

Theorem 3.2.1

(2nd Structure Theorem)
.

Let
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
be compactly generated. Then putting Lemma
3.2.1
together with Proposition
3.2.1
and Corollary
3.2.1
,

M\cong A\times(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times K,

for
k\geq 0
,
A
a topological module over
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
and
K
a compact
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
extension by
C(M)_{0}=C(M)\cap M_{0}\cong K_{0}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2.1
and Proposition
3.2.1
,
M\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times C(M)
. Applying Corollary
3.2.1
, we get the intended result.
∎

The above Structure Theorem is sufficient to prove the 1st one, because locally compact modules contain atleast one open compactly-generated submodule.

Theorem 3.2.2

(1st Structure Theorem)
.

Let
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
. Then

M\cong A\times N,

where
A
is a topological
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
-module, and
N
contains a compact open
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
extension by
N_{0}\cong M_{0}\cap C(M)
(such extension is always taken to be a submodule).

Proof.

Let
V
be a compact neighbourhood of the identity in
M
and consider
M^{\prime}
to be the submodule generated by
V
.
M^{\prime}
is an open compactly generated submodule of
M
and by the 2st Structure Theorem, there exists an isomorphism
\phi\colon A\times(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times K\to M^{\prime}
as above, where
K
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
extension by
(M^{\prime})_{0}\cap C(M^{\prime})
, the latter identified with
0\times 0\times K_{0}
through
\phi
.

Let
Q=\phi(A\times 0\times 0)
and
L=\phi(0\times(\mathbb{Z}S^{-1})^{k}\times K)
. Then
L+Q=M^{\prime}
, which is open in
M
,
L
and
Q
are both
\sigma
-compact with
L\cap Q=0
and
Q
is an injective
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-module. As such, by Lemma
2.5.2
, there exists a closed submodule
N
of
M
such that
L
is open in
N
, and
M\cong Q\times N\cong A\times N
.

K^{\prime}=\phi(0\times 0\times K)
is open in
L
therefore also open in
N
. At the same time, it is a compact
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{P}\setminus\Sigma}
-extension by
\phi(0\times 0\times K_{0})=(K^{\prime})_{0}=N_{0}
(Corollary
A.0.1
). Under the above isomorphism on
M
,
M_{0}\cap C(M)
is identified with
N_{0}
, proving the result.

∎

Before getting to the third Theorem for modules, it is important to look at the 3rd Structure Theorem of
LCA
groups in a different perspective: It says that
G\in LCA
is of Lie type if and only if it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{k}\times D
, where
n,k\geq 0
and
D
is discrete.

But we can note that if
H
is an abelian Lie group (of finite dimension), it will also be of this form:
H_{0}
is a connected abelian Lie group of finite dimension, therefore of the form
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{k}
, and it is also open in
H
since
H
is locally connected. So
H
contains an open injective subgroup, which implies

In other words, the 3rd Structure Theorem implies that

is of Lie type if and only if it is isomorphic (as a topological group) to an abelian Lie group, which is also equivalent to admitting a Lie group structure. At the same time, Hidehiko Yamabe proved in
[

that a locally compact group with the no small subgroups property is a Lie group (in the sense of admitting a Lie group structure), and it is also known that any Lie group of finite dimension has such property.

This means that the 3rd Structure Theorem for

has the NSS property if and only if

, for

and

as above.

With this in mind, in the case of modules we deal with the no small submodules property as well.

This means that even though

Let

•

(Classification of locally compact vector spaces over

3.3.
Modules of Lie type

We will take for granted here that
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
is isomorphic to
\frac{\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}}{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
, where
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
embeds anti-diagonally through
x\mapsto(x,-x,-x,-x,\dots)
. A proof of this fact is given for the case
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}=\mathbb{Q}
in
[
4
]
, and for the generalized case the proof is practically the same, but instead of working over all prime numbers, we work with prime numbers in
\Sigma
(note that in the case where
\Sigma
is empty, we get the quotient
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{S}^{1}
, as expected).

The anti-diagonal embedding of
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
has a discrete image, since its intersection with
]-1,1[\times\mathbb{Z}_{\Sigma}
is trivial. This means that
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
is locally isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
and locally compact
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
modules of Lie type are those locally isomorphic to finite powers of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
.

Let
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
be of Lie type and
\phi\colon U\to V\subset M
a local isomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}\cong(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}})^{l}
to
M
, for some
l\geq 0
. We can suppose
U=U_{1}\times U_{2}
, where
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are open identity neighbourhoods in
\mathbb{R}^{l}
and
(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
, respectively.

Analogously to the trick done in Lemma 4.2.19 of
[
1
]
, we can extend
\phi
to a continuous homomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
as follows: Given
(v,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
, choose
s\in S
such that
\frac{1}{s}v\in U_{1}
and
sa\in U_{2}
, and define

\psi(v,a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=s\phi(\frac{1}{s}v)+\frac{1}{s}\phi(sa).

This is well defined: If
s,t\in S
such that
\frac{1}{s}v,\frac{1}{t}v\in U_{1}
and
sa,ta\in U_{2}
, then

s\phi(\frac{1}{s}v)+\frac{1}{s}\phi(sa)=st\phi(\frac{1}{st}v)+\frac{1}{st}\phi(sta)=t\phi(\frac{1}{t}v)+\frac{1}{t}\phi(ta),

where the above manipulations make sense precisely because
\phi
is a local isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules.

\psi
is additive: If
(v,a),(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
, choose
s\in S
such that
\frac{1}{s}(v+w),\frac{1}{s}v,\frac{1}{s}w\in U_{1}
and
s(a+b),sa,sb\in U_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\psi(v+w,a+b)=s\phi(\frac{1}{s}(v+w))+\frac{1}{s}\phi(s(a+b))=

\displaystyle s(\phi(\frac{1}{s}v)+\phi(\frac{1}{s}w))+\frac{1}{s}(\phi(sa)+\phi(sb))=\psi(v,a)+\psi(w,b),

where the above manipulations make sense because
\phi
is locally additive.

\psi
being additive also makes it a
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
homomorphism, and continuity at the identity makes
\psi
continuous. Because
\psi|_{U}=\phi
,
\psi
is locally injective and therefore has discrete kernel.

At the same time,
Im\ \psi
contains
\phi(U)
, which is open and as such,
\psi
has open image in
M
. As
\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
is divisible over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
,
Im\ \psi
also is, therefore being injective over
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
.

So
Im\ \psi
is an open injective submodule of
M
and by Corollary
2.5.3
,
M\cong Im\ \psi\times D
, where
D
is discrete. At the same time, because
\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}
is
\sigma
-compact,
Im\ \psi\cong\frac{\mathbb{R}^{l}\times(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1},f})^{l}}{\ker\psi}
, by the Open Mapping Theorem.

This means that classifying locally compact
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-modules of Lie type in a similar way to what is given in the 3rd Structure Theorem for
LCA
groups amounts to classifying quotients of finite powers of
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}
by discrete submodules.

We also know that given
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
of Lie type,
M
has no small submodules by Proposition
2.5.3
, which means we can see
M
under the guise of the the 3rd Structure Theorem.

This leads to the following questions to end this article:

(1)

What are the discrete
\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}
-submodules of
(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}})^{l}
for
l\geq 1
, and what are their associated quotients up to isomorphism?

(2)

Given
M\in LC_{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}}M
having no small submodules and

M\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\prod}_{q\in\Sigma}\mathbb{Q}_{q}^{n_{q}}\times(\widehat{\mathbb{Z}S^{-1}})^{k}\times D
as given by the the 3rd Structure Theorem, are there conditions on the numbers
n,(n_{q})_{q\in\Sigma}
and
k
that characterize whether
M
is of Lie type?

Appendix A
Some elementary facts about topological groups and modules

Lemma A.0.1
.

If
G
is a topological group and
G_{0}
is its connected component at the identity,
G_{0}
is contained in any open subgroup of
G
.

Furthermore,
G_{0}
is a closed normal subgroup of
G
such that the quotient
G/G_{0}
is totally disconnected, and given
x\in G
,
xG_{0}
is the connected component of
x
.

Theorem A.0.1
.

If a locally compact topological group
G
is totally disconnected, then it admits a neighbourhood basis at the identity consisting of compact open subgroups. The converse of this statement is a direct consequence of the previous lemma (since in such case,
G_{0}=0
).

Furthermore, if the group is compact, the compact open subgroups can be taken to be normal.

Proof of both above results is found, for example, at the beginning of chapter 4 in
[
1
]
.

Corollary A.0.1
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group. Then

G_{0}=\bigcap_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}O\leq G\\
O\ open\end{subarray}}}O.

Furthermore, if
H
is an open subgroup of

.

Proof.

Let

be the quotient map and let

such that

. Then
\pi(x)
is in all open subgroups of

(since those are all of the form

, where

.

Let

be open. Then
H
is also locally compact so by the first statement of this corollary,

H_{0}=\bigcap_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}O\leq H\\
O\ open\end{subarray}}}O=\bigcap_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}O\leq G\\
O\ open\end{subarray}}}O\cap H=G_{0}\cap H=G_{0},

since

.
∎

Theorem A.0.2
.

Given

a compact subgroup of

Theorem A.0.3
.

Let

\displaystyle f^{\prime}\colon R/I\times M

\displaystyle\to M

\displaystyle(r+I,m)

A.1.
profinite integers and finite dimensional p-adic vector spaces

Definition A.1.1
.

The ring of profinite integers

is the set of formal series of the form

\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\ni x=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}c_{i}i!,

where
0\leq c_{i}\leq i
for all

. The topology on
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}

.

\hat{\mathbb{Z}}

\mathbb{Z}
can be seen as a dense subring of
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem A.1.1
.

The profinite integers are a profinite topological ring. Furthermore, there exists an isomorphism

\hat{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\prod_{q\ prime}\mathbb{Z}_{q}

of topological rings, where
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is the ring of q-adic integers.

Lemma A.1.1
.

Any proper nontrivial closed subgroup of the p-adic numbers

is of the form

for some

.

.

For

, any compact open subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
is of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{n_{i}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}

Let

be a compact open subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n}
. Since

is open, it contains a set of the form

, for

. Furthermore, we can suppose that each

. At the same time, letting

be the

Appendix B

Given a

Lemma B.0.1
.

For
\chi_{0}\in\widehat{G}
, defining

V_{K,\epsilon}(\chi_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\chi\in\widehat{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \sup_{g\in K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi(g)-\chi_{0}(g)\right\rvert}<\epsilon\},

for
K\subset G
compact and
\epsilon>0
, the sets
V_{K,\epsilon}(1)
form a neighbourhood basis at the identity of
\widehat{G}
as
K
and
\epsilon
vary (reminder that the topology on
\widehat{G}
is generated by the sets of the form
L(K,O)=\{\chi\in\widehat{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \chi(K)\subset O\}
, as
K\subset G
compact and
O\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
open vary).

Proof.

Let
U=L(K,O)
be a sub-basic neighbourhood of the identity in
\widehat{G}
, where
O\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
is open and
\emptyset\neq K\subset G
is compact. Because
1_{\widehat{G}}\in L(K,O)
,

and therefore there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \mathinner{\!\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert}<\epsilon\}\subset O
, by
O
being open. As such,
V_{K,\epsilon}(1)\subset L(K,O)
.

Because
V_{K,\epsilon}(1)
is also an open neighbourhood of the identity in
\widehat{G}
and is contained in
L(K,O)
, we can conclude that the identity in
\widehat{G}
admits a neighbourhood basis consisting of finite intersections of the sets of the form
V_{K,\epsilon}(1)
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,\cup K_{n}\subset G
be compact and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}>0
.

Then if
K=K_{1}\cup\dots\cup K_{n}
and
\epsilon=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}\}
,

V_{K,\epsilon}(1)\subset V_{K_{1},\epsilon_{1}}(1)\cap\dots\cap V_{K_{n},\epsilon_{n}}(1).

Therefore, sets of this form form a neighbourhood basis at
1_{\widehat{G}}
instead of just a subbasis.

\widehat{f}

(2)

It is easy to see that
\widehat{f}

Appendix C
Restricted products

Arbitrary products of locally compact groups need not remain locally compact, so restricted products are introduced to deal with this issue.

Definition C.0.1
.

Let
(G_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of locally compact groups such that the set
J\subset I
of indices for which
G_{i}
contains a compact open subgroup has finite complement. For
i\in J
, we choose a compact open subgroup
H_{i}
, and for
i\in I\setminus J
, we take
H_{i}=G_{i}
.

The restricted product
\prod_{i\in I}^{{}^{\prime}}(G_{i},H_{i})
is defined as

\{(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ g_{i}\in H_{i}\ \text{for all but a finite number of indices}\}.

It is easy to see that this is a subgroup of the usual cartesian product.
The topology in the restricted product is generated by a neighbourhood basis at the identity given by sets of the form

\prod_{i\in I}U_{i},

where each
U_{i}
is an identity neighbourhood in
G_{i}
and
U_{i}=H_{i}
for all but a finite number of indices.

Proposition C.0.1
.

Given
(G_{i},H_{i})_{i\in I}
as above, let
G
be the aforementioned restricted product.

•

G
is a locally compact topological group.

•

A subset
Y\subset G
has compact closure if and only if
Y\subset\prod_{i\in I}K_{i}
, where each
K_{i}
is compact in
G_{i}
and
K_{i}=H_{i}
for all but finitely many indices.

•

The canonical maps
\iota_{i}\colon G_{i}\to\prod_{i\in I}^{{}^{\prime}}(G_{i},H_{i})
are isomorphisms under their images.

•

If the
G_{i}
’s are locally compact abelian, then

\widehat{G}\cong\prod_{i\in I}^{{}^{\prime}}(\widehat{G_{i}},K(H_{i})),

where
K(H_{i})=\begin{cases}A(H_{i}),\ \text{if}\ H_{i}\ \text{is compact and open}\\
\widehat{G_{i}}\ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

]

It is worth noting that if each
G_{i}
also admits a topological ring structure, then the restricted product is also a topological ring, by an analogous argument to the one that proves the restricted product is a topological group.
