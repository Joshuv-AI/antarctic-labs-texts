Abstract
The Growth-at-Risk (GaR) framework has garnered attention in recent econometric literature, yet current approaches implicitly assume a constant Pareto exponent. We introduce novel and robust econometrics to estimate the tails of GaR based on a rigorous theoretical framework and establish validity and effectiveness. Simulations demonstrate consistent outperformance relative to existing alternatives in terms of predictive accuracy. We perform a long-term GaR analysis that provides accurate and insightful predictions, effectively capturing financial anomalies better than current methods. Keywords: Growth-at-Risk, Robust Estimation, Forecast Distributions

Introduction

Recent literature has seen a burgeoning interest in the study of extreme outcomes, exemplified by frameworks such as
Financial Stability at Risk as measured by CoVaR
(
Adrian and
Brunnermeier 2016
)
,
Employment at Risk
(
Ramey and
Zubairy 2018
)
,
Growth at Risk
(
Adrian
et al. 2019
)
,
Inflation at Risk
(
Loria
et al. 2022
)
,
and Term Structure at Risk
(
Adrian et al. 2022
)
,
to name but a few.
Given the scarcity of data in the tails of outcome distributions, conventional econometric methods are often inadequate for these analyses.
To address this challenge, researchers have developed novel econometric techniques tailored specifically to evaluate and quantify risks associated with such extreme outcomes.

To fix ideas, we focus on the Growth-at-Risk (GaR) framework of
Adrian
et al. 2019
.
Let
Y_{t+h}
denote the average annual GDP growth rate between periods
t
and
t+h
.
Let
X_{t}
denote a vector of observed controls.
Researchers are interested in measuring the tail features of the conditional distribution of
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}
.
For instance, a primary object of interest is the conditional quantile

\displaystyle Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)

of
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}=x
at extremely small
\tau
or extremely large
\tau\in(0,1)
.
Furthermore, the expected shortfall and expected longrise are defined by

\displaystyle SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+h}|Y_{t+h}\leq Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x),X_{t}=x]\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+h}|Y_{t+h}\geq Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(1-\pi|x),X_{t}=x],

respectively.

While the existing literature offers several valuable methods for analyzing these measures, it also has notable limitations.
For example, if one begins with linear quantile regressions
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)=x^{\prime}\beta(\tau)
and extrapolates them to the tails using conditional skewed-t or Pareto-Lévy distributions
(
Mandelbrot 1960
)
of
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}=x
, then the resulting estimation strategy implicitly assumes a conditional Pareto exponent (equivalent to the degree of freedom of Student-t) that remains constant with respect to
x
.
Consequently, the expected shortfall
SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
and expected long-rise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
become proportional to the baseline linear quantile regression
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)=x^{\prime}\beta(\tau)
.
In this paper, we will theoretically demonstrate these results.

Given the limitations of existing methods, we propose a more robust econometric approach for analyzing these parameters.
Unlike existing methods, our approach does not imply a constant Pareto exponent with respect to
x
.
We provide a formal asymptotic theoretical foundation to validate our proposed method.
Additionally, simulation studies based on data-generating processes that mimic the real-world data used in
Adrian
et al. 2019
demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms existing alternatives in terms of prediction accuracy.

We analyze GaR over the long history of the U.S. economy by combining the MacroHistory dataset with supplementary historical data. We demonstrate that our newly proposed method provides more informative GaR predictions, better capturing financial anomalies compared to existing methods. Specifically, our approach successfully predicts the sharp downturn in 2009 following the 2007–2008 financial crisis, whereas predictions from the existing method remain overly modest. Moreover, our method’s out-of-sample predicted quantiles contain the realized real GDP growth rates at frequencies close to the nominal coverage probabilities, outperforming the existing approach.

Section

Section

Section
5
presents simulation evidence supporting our method.
Detailed mathematical derivations are provided in the appendix.

2
Existing Methods

2.1
Review of the Existing Method

We begin with reviewing a popular existing econometric methods.
While we highlight the work of
Adrian
et al. 2019
due to its prominence, the other existing approaches exhibit similar underlying characteristics.

Adrian
et al. 2019
propose the following estimation strategy.

Step 1:

First, estimate the linear quantile regression model
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(x)=x^{\prime}\beta(\tau)
by

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}(\tau)=\arg\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{\tau}(Y_{t}-X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)

for each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}=\{0.05,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.95\}
,
where
\rho_{\tau}(u)=u\cdot(\tau-\mathbbm{1}\{u<0\})
.
Write

\tilde{Q}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)=x^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\tau).

Step 2:

Next, let
F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\ \cdot\ ;\theta(x))
denote the conditional CDF of the skewed-t distribution of
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}=x
with the parameters
\theta(x)=(\mu(x),\sigma(x),\alpha(x),v(x))^{\prime}
representing the location
\mu(x)
, scale
\sigma(x)
, skewness
\alpha(x)
, and degree of freedom
v(x)
, all of which are indexed by the conditioning variable
X_{t}=x
.
Estimate
\theta(x)
by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}(x)=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\tilde{Q}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)-F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}^{-1}(\tau;\theta)\right)^{2},

i.e., fitting the skewed-t conditional distribution to the linear quantile regression estimates at
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.

Step 3:

If one wishes to proceed with estimating the expected shortfall
SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x)
or the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x)
with tail probability
\pi
, then they can be estimated by extrapolating the estimated skewed-t distribution:

\displaystyle\widehat{SF}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau;\hat{\theta}(x))d\tau\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\widehat{LR}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{1-\pi}^{1}Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau;\hat{\theta}(x))d\tau.

2.2
Limitations of the Existing Methods

2.2.1
Constancy of
v(x)
in
x

The first limitation of the existing method outlined in Section
2.1
is the implied constancy of
v(x)
in
x
, as formally stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

Suppose that (i)
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\tau|x\right)=x^{\prime}\beta\left(\tau\right)
holds for
\tau\in[1-\varepsilon,1)
or
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon>0
and (ii)
F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\ \cdot\ |x\right)
is a skewed-t distributed with degree of freedom
v\left(x\right)
.
Then
v(x)
must be constant in
x
.

This result originates from
Wang and
Li 2013
, who study the constancy of the tail index.
We tailor their argument for the skew-t distribution in our setup.
A proof is found in Appendix
A.1
.
This proposition establishes that the linear quantile regression model
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(x)=x^{\prime}\beta(\tau)
assumed in Step 1 of Section
2.1
and the skewed-t distribution of
F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\ \cdot\ |x\right)
assumed in Step 2 of Section
2.1
necessarily imply that degree of freedom
v(x)=\overline{v}
is constant in observed predictor
x
.
Hence, the procedure of
Adrian
et al. 2019
forces the tail exponent to be constant in
x
.

2.2.2
Constant Proportionality of
SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\cdot|x)
and
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\cdot|x)
to
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\cdot|x)

The second limitation of the existing method outlined in Section
2.1
is that it forces the expected shortfall
SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
and the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
to be constant proportions of their threshold value
\pi
regardless of the value of
x
.
Hence, there appears little point of making predictions based on
x
.

Since the mathematical analysis is symmetric, our presentation focuses on the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
in this section.

Proposition 2

Suppose that the conditional distribution of
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}=x
is skewed-t with
v(x)
degrees of freedom.
Then as
\pi\rightarrow 0
,

\frac{LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\pi|x\right)}{Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(1-\pi|x)}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{v(x)}{v(x)-1}&\text{if }v(x)>1\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

A proof is found in Appendix
A.2
.
This proposition shows that the proportion of the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
to its threshold value
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(1-\pi|x)
depends on only the degree of freedom
v(x)
, and does
not
depend on the location
\mu(x)
, scale
\sigma(x)
, or skewness
\alpha(x)
.
In other words, the linear regression model
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(1-\pi|x)=x^{\prime}\beta(1-\pi)
must have already accounted for all the effects of the location
\mu(x)
, scale
\sigma(x)
, or skewness
\alpha(x)
on the expected longrise.

When moment exists, it follows from combining Propositions
1
–
2
that

\displaystyle LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)\approx\frac{\overline{v}}{\overline{v}-1}\cdot x^{\prime}\beta(1-\pi)

holds for sufficiently small
\pi
, where the degrees of freedom
\overline{v}=v(x)
is constant in
x
.
In other words, the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)
is roughly a constant multiple of the linear quantile regression
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(1-\pi|x)=x^{\prime}\beta(1-\pi)
.

While the above discussion focuses on the expected longrise for brevity of exposition, an analogous conclusion holds also for the expected shortfall.
Namely,

\displaystyle SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x)\approx\frac{\overline{v}}{\overline{v}-1}\cdot x^{\prime}\beta(\pi)

holds for sufficiently small
\pi
.

3
Our New Econometric Method of Growth at Risk

To overcome the limitations of the existing method, we propose to consider a nonparametric family of conditional distributions
Y_{t+h}
given
X_{t}=x
that admit the Pareto tail approximation:

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+h}>y|Y_{t+h}>y_{\min},X_{t}=x\right)\sim\left(\frac{y}{y_{\min}}\right)^{-v\left(x\right)}

(1)

for a sufficiently large
y_{\min}
,
where
v(x)
denotes the tail exponent.
We emphasize that the skewed-t distribution employed by
Adrian
et al. 2019
satisfies this approximation condition (
1
) with
v(x)
corresponding to the degrees of freedom.
Besides the skewed-t distribution, it accommodates many parametric and nonparametric families
(e.g.,
de Haan and
Ferreira 2006
, Chapter 1)
.
Hence, this assumption (
1
) is fairly weak and much weaker than the implicit assumptions made in the prior literature.

Furthermore, since the problem with the existing method was the constancy of
v(x)
in
x
, we explicitly model its dependence on
x
through

v\left(x\right)=\exp\left(x^{\prime}\beta\right)

(2)

for example.
Again, we want to emphasize that this model (
2
) is much more general than the implicit assumption imposed in the prior literature that
v(x)=\overline{v}
is constant in
x
.

3.1
The Estimation Procedure

3.1.1
Algorithm for the Upper Tail

Given the asymmetry, we estimate the upper and lower tail features separately.
Our proposed procedure for the upper tail is as follows.
We denote the query point by
x_{0}
.

Step 1:

Run the tail index regression by solving

\hat{\beta}=\arg\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{t+h}\geq y_{\min}\}\cdot\left\{\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)\log\left((Y_{t+h}-\overline{Y})/(y_{\min}-\overline{Y})\right)-X_{t}^{\prime}\beta\right\},

where
\overline{Y}
is the sample median of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t}
and
y_{\min}
is the 90% empirical quantile of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t}
.

Step 2:

Use
\hat{\beta}
to estimate

\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)=\exp(x_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\beta}).

Step 3:

Estimate the
\tau
-th extreme quantile
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})
in the upper tail quantiles by

\hat{Q}^{\text{Upper}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})=(y_{\min}-\overline{Y})\cdot\left(\frac{1-\tau}{1-\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\min}|x_{0})}\right)^{-1/\hat{v}(x_{0})}+\overline{Y},

where
\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\min}|x_{0})
is a kernel estimator of
F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\min}|x_{0})
with bandwidth
b_{T}
.

Step 4:

Estimate the expected longrise
LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x_{0})
by

\widehat{LR}_{Y_{t+h}|X}(\pi|x_{0})=\left(\hat{Q}^{\text{Upper}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x_{0})-\overline{Y}\right)\cdot\frac{\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)}{\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)-1}+\overline{Y}.

We provide some discussions.
Step 1 is the tail index regression proposed by
Wang and
Tsai 2009
.
We modify their approach by subtracting the sample median
\overline{Y}
, which will not change the asymptotic distribution.
The tail cutoff
y_{\min}
serves as a tuning parameter, close in spirit to the bandwidth in kernel regression.
We use 90% quantile as a simple rule-of-thumb choice here and present a more sophisticated data-driven choice in Section
3.3
.
Once we obtain the tail index estimator
\hat{v}(x_{0})
, we now take advantage of the Pareto tail approximation to estimate in the conditional extreme quantile as in Step 3.
The expected longrise can be estimated analogously.

3.1.2
Algorithm for the Lower Tail

Our proposed procedure for the lower tail is same as before.
We present it here for completeness and easy reference.

Step 1:

Run the tail index regression by solving

\hat{\beta}=\arg\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{t+h}\leq y_{\max}\}\cdot\left\{\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)\log\left((Y_{t+h}-\overline{Y})/(y_{\max}-\overline{Y})\right)-X_{t}^{\prime}\beta\right\},

(3)

where
\overline{Y}
is the sample median of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t}
and
y_{\max}
is the 10% empirical quantile of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t}
.

Step 2:

Use
\hat{\beta}
to estimate

\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)=\exp(x_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\beta}).

Step 3:

Estimate the
\tau
-th extreme quantile
Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})
in the lower tail quantiles by

\hat{Q}^{\text{Lower}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})=(y_{\max}-\overline{Y})\cdot\left(\frac{\tau}{\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\max}|x_{0})}\right)^{-1/\hat{v}(x_{0})}+\overline{Y},

where
\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\max}|x_{0})
is a kernel estimator of
F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(y_{\max}|x_{0})
with bandwidth
b_{T}
.

Step 4:

Estimate the expected shortfall
SF_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi;x_{0})
by

\widehat{SF}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x_{0})=\left(\hat{Q}^{\text{Lower}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\pi|x_{0})-\overline{Y}\right)\cdot\frac{\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)}{\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)-1}+\overline{Y}.

3.2
Standard Errors

3.2.1
Standard Errors for the Upper Tail

We now provide estimators for the standard errors.
The standard error for
\hat{Q}^{\text{Upper}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})
is given by

\frac{\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{F}\left(y_{\min},y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)}\cdot\hat{Q}^{\text{Upper}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}{\sqrt{Tb_{T}^{\text{dim}(X_{t})}}\cdot\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)},

where
\hat{\Sigma}_{F}\left(y_{\min},y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)
denotes some consistent estimator for the asymptotic variance of the kernel estimator
\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)
, that is,

\frac{\int K(u)^{2}du}{\hat{g}(x_{0})}\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)\left(1-\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)\right),

where
K(\cdot)
denotes the kernel function and
\hat{g}(x_{0})
denotes some estimator of the density of
X_{t}
evaluated at
x_{0}
.
Similarly, the standard error for
\widehat{LR}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)
is given by

\frac{\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{F}\left(y_{\min},y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)}\cdot\widehat{LR}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}{\sqrt{Tb_{T}^{\text{dim}(X_{t})}}\cdot(1-\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right))}.

3.2.2
Standard Errors for the Lower Tail

The standard error for
\hat{Q}^{\text{Lower}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})
is analogous to the previous case:

\frac{\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{F}\left(y_{\max},y_{\max}|x_{0}\right)}\cdot\hat{Q}^{\text{Lower}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}{\sqrt{Tb_{T}^{\text{dim}(X_{t})}}\cdot\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right)}.

Similarly, the standard error for
\widehat{SF}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)
is given by

\frac{\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{F}\left(y_{\max},y_{\max}|x_{0}\right)}\cdot\widehat{SF}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x)}{\sqrt{Tb_{T}^{\text{dim}(X_{t})}}\cdot(1-\hat{v}\left(x_{0}\right))}.

3.3
Rule-of-Thumb Choice of the Threshold

The choice of
y_{\min}
(and
y_{\max}
) is similar to that of the bandwidth in kernel regression.
There is a large literature in studying the optimal choice of
y_{\min}
in the case without the covariate.
See,
Danielsson et al. 2001
;
Danielsson et al. 2016
, among many other.
In the case with covariate, we provide a data-driven method to choose the threshold, following
Wang and
Tsai 2009
.
For simple illustration, we focus on the right tail.

If
Y_{t+h}
is Pareto distributed conditional on
X_{t}
with the Pareto exponent being
\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)
, then
\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)\log(Y_{t+h})
is standard exponential, and according
\exp(-\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\beta)\log(Y_{t+h}))
becomes uniformly distributed over
(0,1)
.
Therefore, we aim to choose the threshold
y_{\min}
such that the data above
y_{\min}
satisfy the uniform distribution to the best extend.
To this end, given any choice of
y_{\min}
, we first construct the estimator
\hat{\beta}
and construct

\hat{U}_{t}=\exp(-\exp(X_{t}^{\prime}\hat{\beta})\log(Y_{t+h}/y_{\min})),

which should be approximately uniform.
To select the optimal
y_{\min}
, we can compare the empirical distribution of
\hat{U}_{t}
with the standard uniform by considering the discrepancy measure

D_{T}(y_{\min})=T_{0}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-h}\left(\hat{U}_{t}-\hat{F}_{U}(\hat{U}_{t})\right)^{2}\times 1[Y_{t+h}>y_{\min}],

where
T_{0}=\sum_{t=1}^{T-h}1[Y_{t+h}>y_{\min}]
is the
tail
sample size, and
\hat{F}_{U}(\cdot)
denotes the empirical distribution of
\hat{U}_{t}
.
Accordingly, we can select the optimal choice of
y_{\min}
to minimize
D_{T}(\cdot)
, i.e.,

y^{*}_{\min}=\arg\min_{y_{\min}}D_{T}(y_{\min}).

4
Asymptotic Theory

We now derive the asymptotic distribution of our estimators to formally justify the procedure outlined in Section
3
.
For brevity, we focus on the case of upper tails presented in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.2.1
.
Symmetric arguments will provide counterpart results for the lower tails.

We make the following high-level assumptions.

Assumption P (Pareto-Type Tail)

\{Y_{t},X_{t}\}
is strictly stationary. The conditional CDF satisfies

1-F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y|x\right)=c_{1}(x)y^{-v(x)}\left(1+c_{2}(x)y^{-\rho(x)}+r(y,x)\right),

where
v(x)=\exp(x^{\prime}\beta)
,
0<\inf_{x}c_{1}(x)\leq\sup_{x}c_{1}(x)<\infty
,
0<\sup_{x}\left|c_{2}(x)\right|<\infty
,
\inf_{x}\rho(x)>0
, and
\sup_{x}\left|r(y,x)\right|y^{-\rho(x)}\rightarrow 0
as
y\rightarrow\infty
. The support of
X_{t}
is compact.

Assumption N (Nonparametric Estimation)

There exists a sequence of constants
r_{T}\rightarrow 0
such that

T^{1/2}r_{T}\left(\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\ \cdot\ |x_{0}\right)-F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\ \cdot\ |x_{0}\right)\right)\Rightarrow Z(\cdot),

where
Z
is a Gaussian process with zero mean and covariance function
\Sigma_{F}
satisfying
\Sigma_{F}\left(y,y|x_{0}\right)\sim c(x_{0})F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y|x_{0}\right)\left(1-F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(y|x_{0}\right)\right)
as
y\rightarrow\infty
for some constant
c(x_{0})>0
.

Assumption T (Tail Index Regression)

The tail index regression estimator (
3
) satisfies that

\hat{\beta}-\beta=O_{p}(\mathbb{E}[y_{\min}^{-v(X_{i})/2}])
as
y_{\min}\rightarrow\infty
(and
T\rightarrow\infty
).

Assumption R (Convergence Rates)

y_{\min}
and
r_{T}
satisfy
(i)
y_{\min}^{-v(x_{0})/2}\sqrt{T}r_{T}\rightarrow\infty
,
(ii)
\left(\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\min}^{-v(X_{t})}\right]\right)^{-1/2}r_{T}\log\left(1-\tau\right)\rightarrow 0
, and
(iii)
y_{\min}^{-v(x_{0})\rho(x_{0})}\sqrt{T}r_{T}\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption P imposes a second-order conditional Pareto tail, with the term
y^{-\rho(x_{0})}
characterizing the deviation from the exact Pareto – see also
Wang and
Tsai 2009
.
Assumption N requires that the nonparametric estimator
\hat{F}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}\left(\cdot|x_{0}\right)
uniformly converges to a Gaussian process with the variance-covariance proportional to
1-F_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\cdot|x_{0})
in the right tail.
Specifically, this can be established for kernel estimation with
r_{T}=b^{\text{dim}(X)/2}_{T}
, where
b_{T}
is the bandwidth.
The constant
c(x_{0})
then becomes
\int K(u)^{2}du/g(x_{0})
where
g(\cdot)
denotes the density of
X
and
K(\cdot)
the kernel function
(
Cai 2002
;
Li and
Racine 2008
, e.g.,)
.

Then, this condition essentially requires a sufficiently well approximation by imposing
\rho(x_{0})>1/2
.

.

The following theorem establishes the limiting distributions of our proposed estimators.

Theorem 3

Suppose Assumptions P, N, T, R hold. Then,

\left(T^{1/2}r_{T}\right)\Sigma_{Q}^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\hat{Q}^{\text{Upper}}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}{Q_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}-1\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)

holds as
T\rightarrow\infty
, where

\Sigma_{Q}=\frac{\Sigma_{F}\left(y_{\min},y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)}{v\left(x_{0}\right)^{2}}.

In addition,

\left(T^{1/2}r_{T}\right)\Sigma_{T}^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\widehat{LR}_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}{LR_{Y_{t+h}|X_{t}}(\tau|x_{0})}-1\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right),

holds as
T\rightarrow\infty
, where

\Sigma_{T}=\frac{\Sigma_{F}\left(y_{\min},y_{\min}|x_{0}\right)}{\left(v\left(x_{0}\right)-1\right)^{2}}.

Summary and Discussions

This paper discusses certain limitations of existing econometric methods concerning Growth-at-Risk (GaR) and, in light of these limitations, proposes a novel method that is robust to them. We demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed method, both theoretically and through numerical simulations, in comparison to existing alternatives.

With this new tool, we study the GaR in the long history of the U.S.
Applying our novel method to the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, combined with the excess cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) yield constructed by Robert J. Shiller, we find that our approach successfully predicts low GaR values that capture the impact of the financial crisis, whereas the existing alternative fails to do so. Moreover, our method achieves the intended frequencies of GaR, while the existing method yields overly conservative predictions. In light of these findings, we recommend adopting our proposed method as a more robust alternative.

While our primary focus is on the GaR framework, the proposed method is broadly applicable to other contexts involving the study of extreme outcomes, including
