The effect of sumac and cinnamon on lipid profile and quality of life in patients with acute myocardial infarction, type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity: study protocol for a randomized double-blind controlled trial
Authors: Ashkan Golmohammadi, Amir Peikar, Farzad Karimpour, Fatemeh Zadeh Bagheri, Seyed Bahman Panahande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading cause of global mortality, with ischemic heart disease, stroke, and other related conditions contributing to over one-third of deaths worldwide. The rising prevalence of CVDs is driven by aging populations and various risk factors, including poor diet, physical inactivity, and dyslipidemia. Recent studies suggest that natural supplements like sumac (Rhus coriaria L.) and cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum J. Presl) may have beneficial effects on lipid profiles and overall cardiovascular health. This study is designed to investigate the combined effects of sumac and cinnamon supplementation on lipid profiles and quality of life in a high-risk population of patients with acute myocardial infarction (MI), type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity.MethodsThis randomized, double-blind controlled trial will involve 76 participants meeting the specified criteria. Participants will be randomly assigned to one of four groups: sumac (S), cinnamon (C), a combination of sumac and cinnamon (S + C), or a placebo, for eight weeks. The primary outcome is the change in serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) from baseline to week 8. Secondary outcomes include other lipid parameters (HDL-C, TG, TC, VLDL), pain levels, quality of life (QoL), and inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). A Complete Blood Count (CBC) will be performed as well. Blinding success will be assessed using the Bang Blinding Index (BBI). Blood samples will be collected at baseline and at the end of the intervention period to measure the relevant biomarkers. Lipid profiles will be measured using standard photometric/enzymatic methods, while IL-6 and hs-CRP will be assayed using ELISA. Data analysis will involve comparisons between the four groups using ANCOVA, adjusting for baseline values and statin intensity.DiscussionThis study will provide valuable insights into the potential of sumac and cinnamon, both individually and in combination, as complementary therapies for managing lipid profiles and improving the quality of life in patients with CVDs. The findings could contribute to the development of novel dietary interventions aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk and promoting heart health. Additionally, the study will explore the combined effect of sumac and cinnamon, offering insights into whether their joint use provides synergistic benefits beyond those observed with each supplement individually.Trial statusVersion 4.1 — Protocol, dated 10 June 2026. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) encompass a range of disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels, standing as a formidable global health challenge and a leading cause of death worldwide [1]. In 2019 alone, CVDs claimed the lives of approximately 17.9 million individuals, representing a staggering one-third of total global mortality [1, 2]. Predominantly, ischemic heart disease, stroke, heart failure (HF), cardiomyopathy, and atrial fibrillation (AF) collectively contribute to over 95% of cardiovascular disease-related deaths [3].

The increasing prevalence of CVDs is exacerbated by demographic shifts, particularly the aging of the global population, playing a pivotal role in optimal cardiovascular system function [4]. Additionally, a myriad of risk factors, including unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, gender, genetic determinants, and disorders of lipid metabolism, contribute to the rising incidence of CVDs [1]. These risk factors manifest as elevated blood pressure (hypertension), increased blood glucose (diabetes), raised blood lipids (dyslipidemia), or present as obesity [1, 5]. These intermediate risk factors collectively serve as indicators for evaluating the risk of heart attacks, strokes, heart failure, and other complications associated with CVDs [1]. In the traditional approach to CVD risk assessment, measurements of serum lipid profile levels have played a crucial role [1, 6, 7]. The National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) continues to prioritize low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) as a primary target for lipid-lowering therapy in the context of cardiovascular diseases [6].

A cohort study of 12.8 million adults found that the lowest all-cause mortality occurred at total cholesterol levels of 210–249 mg/dL, except for specific age-gender groups [8]. Similar findings from the British Heart Foundation and the World Health Organization, dating back to 2005, showed that optimal total cholesterol concentrations ranged between 200 mg/dL and 240 mg/dL for lowest all-cause mortality, with the lowest mortality from cardiovascular diseases occurring at approximately 190–225 mg/dL [7].

Atherosclerosis is a key underlying factor in many cardiovascular diseases [9]. This chronic condition involves inflammation and plaque buildup within artery walls, leading to vessel narrowing and stiffening [10]. The complex pathophysiology of atherosclerosis is influenced by multiple factors, including endothelial dysfunction, abnormal lipid metabolism, inflammatory processes, and immune responses [11]. When the inner lining of blood vessels (endothelium) becomes damaged due to risk factors such as high blood pressure, unhealthy cholesterol levels, or smoking, it triggers an inflammatory response [10]. This inflammation attracts white blood cells and causes the accumulation of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDL). As these lipid-rich plaques build up within artery walls, they can cause vessels to become narrowed and stiffened, ultimately contributing to cardiovascular disease progression [10]. Current treatment strategies for atherosclerosis aim to mitigate cardiovascular risk factors and stabilize vulnerable plaques to reduce the risk of acute cardiovascular events [12]. These include lifestyle modifications (e.g., smoking cessation, regular exercise, and dietary interventions), pharmacotherapy (e.g., statins, antiplatelet agents, and antihypertensive medications), and, in select cases, invasive interventions such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [13].

The consumption of certain diets can significantly impact an individual’s risk of experiencing cardiovascular events (CVD) [14, 15]. Studies have shown that adherence to a Mediterranean-style diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats is associated with a reduced incidence of CVD [16, 17]. This may be attributed to the high levels of antioxidants and polyphenols present in these foods, which can help mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation within the body [14, 18].

Rhus coriaria L. also known as sumac, is a member of the Anacardiaceae family that is mainly used in western Asia and Mediterranean area in the form of dried powder of its fruit [19]. This powder is mainly used as a spice, flavoring, and coloring agent in foods [20]. Sumac is a rich source of polyphenols such as tannins, flavonoids, flavonols, anthocyanins, quercetins, organic acids, and antioxidants and has demonstrated various effects on the body that can contribute to overall health improvement [19, 21–23]. In studies, sumac consumption has been associated with decreased cardiovascular risk factors such as reduced blood pressure, improved lipid profile, and lower levels of inflammation [23–25]. Previous clinical trials have shown the beneficial effects of sumac supplementation on metabolic markers in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM), overweight and obese individuals, and patients with hyperlipidemia [22, 26, 27]. Additionally, sumac’s antioxidant properties can help protect cells from oxidative stress and damage caused by free radicals [20]. A review indicated that sumac could reduce blood glucose levels, glycated hemoglobin, serum insulin, and insulin resistance; However, studies on its effects on blood lipids and obesity have yielded inconsistent results, and there is no conclusive evidence regarding the impact of sumac on lipid profiles [19]. Nonetheless, its blood pressure-lowering effect has been confirmed in existing studies [19]. These possible effects make sumac a promising candidate for the prevention and management of cardiovascular diseases [19].

Another spice that could have a possible effect on CVD risk factors and metabolic syndrome, is Cinnamomum also known as Cinnamon [28]. It is one of the oldest herbals that was used to treat some disease [29]. Several clinical studies have explored cinnamon’s potential effects on lipid profiles, insulin sensitivity, systolic blood pressure, and blood antioxidant levels [30–32]. Additionally, a meta-analysis combining data from ten randomized controlled trials found a consistent decrease in TC and TG levels in subjects who were supplemented with cinnamon. These clinical results suggest that cinnamon may have potential lipid-modulating effects, making it a promising option for managing cardiovascular risk factors [33].

As mentioned earlier, cardiovascular diseases are among the leading causes of death globally. Sumac (Rhus coriaria L.) and cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum J. Presl) are two promising candidates with evidence suggesting their beneficial effects on lipid profiles and quality of life in patients with CVDs. Patients in the acute phase of MI with concomitant type 2 diabetes and overweight represent a high-risk subgroup where inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation are pronounced, offering a critical window for adjunct dietary interventions. This study aims to investigate the combined effect of sumac and cinnamon supplementation on the lipid profile and quality of life in this specific patient population through a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of this study is to determine the effect of sumac and cinnamon supplementation on serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels in patients with acute MI, type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity. Secondary objectives include evaluating the impact of supplementation on other lipid parameters (HDL, VLDL, TC, and TG), hematological status (CBC), pain levels [39], inflammatory markers (IL-6, hs-CRP, and ESR), and overall quality of life [38].

Trial design {8}

This study adopts a randomized, double-blind controlled trial design to assess the impact of the effect of sumac and cinnamon on lipid profile and quality of life in patients with acute MI, type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity. The trial adheres to a parallel-group design with four intervention arms (sumac, cinnamon, combined and placebo) and a total of 76 patients, allocated equally among the groups. The allocation ratio is 1:1:1:1. This study is designed as a hypothesis-testing trial, although its findings in this specific population will be considered preliminary, requiring confirmation in larger definitive studies.

Prior to enrolment, all participants will sign a written informed consent form, approved by the Ethics Committee, to ensure their voluntary participation. Stringent measures will be in place to uphold the confidentiality of the information gathered through questionnaires.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The selection of sumac and cinnamon, both individually and in combination, stems from their traditional use in improving lipid profiles and overall well-being. If effective, testing these interventions on patients with acute MI, type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity could offer valuable insights. The rationale for choosing these spices is based on previous studies that suggest their potential health benefits.

Sumac has been shown to possess antioxidant properties that may contribute to improved lipid metabolism [24, 25], making it a suitable candidate for investigation in the context of CVD. Cinnamon, known for its anti-inflammatory effects and ability to enhance insulin sensitivity [32], presents another promising avenue for exploration. The decision to include both sumac and cinnamon in the study protocol is also driven by their complementary mechanisms of action, which may lead to additive or synergistic effects when combined. The third group receiving the combination of sumac and cinnamon will enable us to assess whether this co-administration can result in improved outcomes compared to either spice alone.

This design choice ensures that our trial addresses a range of potential therapeutic options for patients with acute MI, type 2 diabetes, and overweight/obesity while also allowing for an evaluation of the efficacy of each intervention individually. The use of a standard control group (not mentioned here) will provide a basis for comparison and help us determine whether these spices have any beneficial effects on lipid profiles and quality of life in this patient population.

Intervention description {11a}

The Intervention begins by identifying potential participants in Imam Sajad Hospital’s Cardiac Care Unit (CCU) through their cardiologist, who assesses patients based on the entry criteria. Seventy-six eligible patients meeting the entry criteria will then be divided into three intervention groups and one control group, each comprising 19 participants, by the randomization manager. After that, participants will be introduced to the group supervisors. Each supervisor obtains consent, and assists in completing questionnaires if needed. This study employs a double-blind method, so neither the participants nor any involved personnel will know the assigned group intervention. Group supervisors will then take blood samples prior to any intervention, and participants will be instructed to continue taking their regular medications.

The sumac and cinnamon used in this study are from species Rhus Coriaria and Cinnamomum verum, respectively. The sumac utilized in this study was sourced from the region of Sabzevar, Iran, while the cinnamon was procured from India. Upon acquisition, the samples were subjected to examination in the Quality Control Laboratory to assess microbial contamination. The analysis specifically targeted E. coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, and yeast, and the samples were approved based on the microbial analysis results. After accurately weighing the plant material using a Sartorius digital balance (Practum 224–1 S model, precision ± 0.001 g), extraction was carried out using the aqueous–alcoholic maceration method (70% ethanol) with a Soxhlet-type apparatus (Aseman Lab Co., Iran) for 48 h at 40 °C, maintaining a plant-to-solvent ratio of 1:10 (w/v). To prevent degradation of phenolic compounds, the obtained extract was concentrated in a rotary evaporator (Rotavapor R-3, Büchi, Switzerland) under the following conditions: water bath temperature 35 °C, vacuum pressure 400 mbar, rotation speed 120 rpm. The process continued until the volume was reduced to one-fourth of the original. The concentrated extract was transferred into sterile 50 mL Falcon tubes and freeze-dried using a lyophilizer (Alpha 2–4 LDplus, CHRIST, Germany) under the following conditions: initial freezing temperature − 80 °C, vacuum pressure 0.01 mbar, processing time 48 h. The resulting powder was weighed with a precision of ± 0.0001 g. The standardized extract powder was mixed with permitted oral excipients and binders, such as starch and gelatin, and compressed into 1 g tablets using a tablet press. Quality control tests, including weight uniformity, disintegration time, and dissolution, were performed according to USP standards. Participants will receive tablets formulated so the active extract in each tablet is equivalent to the raw spice. For the single-extract groups, each tablet will contain an amount of sumac or cinnamon extract that provides the equivalent of 3.0 g of raw sumac or 1.0 g of raw cinnamon. This choice was based on previous studies [23, 27, 29, 33–37]. For the mixed group, each tablet will contain amounts of sumac and cinnamon extract that together provide the equivalent of 2.0 g of raw sumac plus 1.0 g of raw cinnamon. All tablets will have a final weight of 1.0 g after addition of inert excipients to ensure uniform tablet mass and manufacturability. Participants will take one tablet daily. The packaging of the supplements will be identical and opaque, prepared by a team external to the study. To mask the distinctive taste and odor, tablets will be produced using film coating by a certified supplement manufacturer external to the research team.

The tablets will be in closed packages and delivered to the study implementers. The contents of the tablets will be confidential and will be sealed in an envelope to remain confidential until the study’s completion and analysis of results. The intervention will last for 8 weeks, during which participants will be continuously monitored by the team. Adherence will be assessed by weekly phone check-ins and by counting returned tablets at the final visit. The team manager will conduct regular weekend check-ins with the entire team through meetings or calls to ensure smooth progress and address any emerging issues or concerns. After this period, participants will be asked to give blood samples again and complete any necessary questionnaires. The collected data will then be processed, and the results will be individually reported to the participants. Statistical analysis will be conducted using SPSS software, and the results will be published in a future paper.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Throughout this trial, participants are expected to maintain their regular healthcare habits and concomitant care. Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this randomized controlled trial (RCT) is the change in serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) from baseline to the end of the 8-week intervention period. LDL-C will be measured using standard photometric/enzymatic methods on a biochemical auto-analyzer.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes will assess additional Lipid Parameters, hematological status, systemic inflammation, and patient-reported well-being from baseline to week 8. Additional Lipid Parameters include changes in high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) that will be measured using standard photometric/enzymatic methods. Also Very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) will be calculated based on TG measurements. A Complete Blood Count (CBC) will be performed using an automated hematology analyzer to monitor hematological parameters during the intervention period. Inflammatory Markers including Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) will be measured using the ELISA method to evaluate changes in the inflammatory response. Another inflammatory marker is Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) which will be assessed using the Westergren-Katz method. For evaluating patients’ overall well-being, World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire will be used [38]. Also changes in pain intensity will be measured using the McGill Pain Questionnaire [39].

Statistical analysis for QoL and pain outcomes will be adjusted for baseline symptom severity, and interpretation of these results will consider established minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) where available. All biochemical and patient-reported assessments will be conducted at two time points: baseline (Week 0) and post-intervention (Week 8).

Participant timeline {13}

Participants in this randomized controlled trial (RCT) will follow a structured timeline from enrollment to the completion of the study. The timeline will consist of the following key phases which is also shown in Fig. 1.

Screening and enrollment (Week 0)

Eligible participants will be identified and invited to participate in the study. Informed consent will be obtained, and baseline assessments, including demographic data, medical history, and baseline measurements (lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, quality of life, and pain levels), will be conducted using standardized methods.

Randomization (Week 0)

Following the baseline assessments, participants will be randomly assigned to the intervention or control group.

Intervention period (Weeks 1–8)

Participants will undergo the assigned intervention for 8 weeks. During this period, they will be monitored for adherence to the intervention protocol and any adverse events. No additional study visits are planned during this period unless necessary.

Mid-intervention checkpoint (Week 4)

An optional mid-intervention checkpoint may be conducted to assess interim adherence and address any participant concerns. No formal measurements will be taken at this time.

Post-intervention assessment (Week 8)

At the end of the 8-week intervention period, participants will return for follow-up assessments. The same measurements taken at baseline (lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, quality of life, and pain levels) will be repeated using standardized methods.

Study completion and debriefing (Week 8)

After the post-intervention assessments, participants will be debriefed about the study’s purpose and outcomes. They will also receive information on the study’s results and any further follow-up if necessary.

Sample size {14}

The sample size for this study was calculated using STATA formula with a Type I error of 5%, power of 80%, and standard deviation of LDL-c levels as 102 ± 24.3 in the intervention group and 131 ± 31.9 in the placebo group based on Sabzeghabaei’s findings [20], along with an assumed correlation of 0.3 and using ANCOVA analysis. This resulted in a required sample size per group of 14 participants; however, accounting for a potential 30% dropout rate increased this to 19 participants per group, ultimately necessitating a total study population of 76 individuals.

Recruitment {15}

To ensure the success of this study, we are implementing two key strategies to recruit eligible participants.

Collaboration with cardiologists and healthcare providers

We have established partnerships with cardiologists and healthcare providers within the hospital to identify potential participants who meet our inclusion criteria. This collaboration enables us to pinpoint individuals who would greatly benefit from participating in this study, thereby maximizing recruitment efforts.

Clinic-based advertising

To reach a broader audience, we will be showcasing study details prominently throughout the clinic premises. This targeted advertising approach aims to engage patients attending CVD appointments and inform them about our research opportunity.

By combining these two strategies – collaborating with cardiologists and employing effective clinic-based advertisements – we are confident that we can enhance participant recruitment efforts and meet our target sample size, ultimately ensuring a successful study outcome.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

To ensure an unbiased allocation process, we have implemented a robust randomization strategy.

Computer-generated allocation sequence

The allocation sequence for participants will be generated using the Randomization.com website, eliminating any potential bias or human influence.

Confidential blocking record

In addition to the allocation sequence, we will maintain a separate and confidential record detailing the planned restrictions (blocking). This document will not be accessible to individuals involved in participant enrollment or intervention assignment, further safeguarding the integrity of our randomization process.

By maintaining strict confidentiality and separating this information from the allocation sequence, we can ensure that participants are randomly assigned to treatment groups without any influence or bias.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

To maintain confidentiality throughout the allocation process, we have implemented a secure method for executing the randomization sequence.

Sealed envelopes

The allocation sequence will be contained within sequentially numbered, opaque, and sealed envelopes. These envelopes are prepared in advance to ensure that they remain unopened until the moment of intervention assignment.

Confidential allocation

Each envelope is opened solely at the time when an individual’s treatment group needs to be determined. This process ensures that the allocation sequence remains concealed from all parties involved, including researchers and participants.

By using sealed envelopes as a secure method for executing our randomization sequence, we can guarantee that the integrity of our study design is maintained throughout the entire trial period.

Implementation {16c}

The allocation sequence will be devised by SBP, an individual not directly engaged in participant enrollment or intervention assignment, ensuring impartiality. An external team will also prepare the tablets for each group. AG will oversee participant enrollment and the assignment to interventions. AG will utilize sealed envelopes containing the allocation sequence to perform the assignments.

Assignment of interventions: Blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

In this double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT), the trial participants, care providers, outcome assessors, and data analysts will be blinded to ensure the integrity of the study. Participants will be unaware of their assigned intervention group to prevent any bias in their behavior or reporting. The individuals administering the interventions will not know which treatment group participants belong to, ensuring unbiased care. Those evaluating the outcomes will be blinded to participants’ group assignments to avoid any influence on their assessments, and data analysts responsible for interpreting the data will also be blinded to group assignments to prevent bias in the analysis. The process is explained in the section above. The success of blinding will be assessed at the end of the study using the Bang Blinding Index (BBI) by asking participants and outcome assessors to guess the treatment allocation [40].

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

To maintain the integrity of our blinded study design, we have established strict guidelines for unblinding.

Reserved for critical situations

Unblinding will only occur in exceptional circumstances where knowledge of an individual’s treatment allocation is essential to ensure their safety or well-being.

Authorized personnel only

The decision to unblind and access the secure document containing treatment allocations rests with the principal investigator or another authorized member of the research team, who are not directly involved in trial procedures.

Secure unblinding process

To safeguard participant confidentiality, we will implement a robust process for accessing treatment allocation information. This will involve storing treatment allocations in a separate and secure document.

Minimal Disclosure: Participant identities and allocated interventions will only be revealed when absolutely necessary for their medical care, thereby minimizing the risk of bias or compromise.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Outcome assessment

In this randomized controlled trial (RCT), trained professionals will utilize standardized measures to assess primary and secondary outcomes. Key outcome metrics include lipid profiles, such as LDL, HDL, triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) levels, which will be evaluated using standard photometric/enzymatic methods on a biochemical autoanalyzer from blood samples collected at both the initiation and conclusion of the intervention period. Very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) will be calculated based on TG measurement. Inflammatory markers like interleukin-6 (IL-6), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) will be assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to ensure accuracy and reliability. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) will also be assessed using the Westergren-Katz method, and for CBC using an automated hematology analyzer. Furthermore, quality of life and pain levels will be evaluated utilizing validated tools, including the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire for assessing overall well-being and the McGill Pain Questionnaire for evaluating pain intensity. A standardized medication record form will be used to document all concomitant drugs, including type, dose, and any changes, at baseline and follow-up.

Data quality processes

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Data analysis will be performed using SPSS software version 26. To determine the normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk test will be used, supported by histograms and measures of skewness and kurtosis. Quantitative variables will be summarized using means and standard deviations, while qualitative variables will be reported as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons for continuous outcomes will be conducted using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for baseline values and potential confounders. Specifically, statin therapy will be categorized by intensity (low, moderate, high) according to current clinical guidelines and included as a covariate in the ANCOVA models. Other major lipid-lowering agents (e.g., ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors) will also be included as covariates. For post-hoc pairwise comparisons following a significant omnibus test, the Tukey HSD or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment will be applied. The significance level for all tests is set at P < 0.05.

Interim analyses {21b}

No interim analyses are planned for this trial. Stopping guidelines are not applicable due to the low-risk nature of the intervention. Decisions to terminate the trial, if necessary, will be made by the principal investigators based on overall trial progress and ethical considerations.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

Exploratory post-hoc analyses will be conducted for key subgroups, including gender, baseline LDL-C levels, and statin use. The success of the blinding procedure will be formally assessed at study completion using the Bang Blinding Index (BBI) [40]. This will involve asking participants and outcome assessors to guess the treatment allocation and calculating the BBI to quantify the degree of blinding achieved. Additionally, statistical analysis for quality of life and pain outcomes will be adjusted for baseline symptom severity, and results will be interpreted in the context of established minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs).

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. For handling missing outcome data, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) will be employed under the missing-at-random assumption. To ensure robustness, at least 20 imputed datasets will be generated, each using 10 or more iterations. Auxiliary variables, including age, sex, baseline outcome values, and medication classes, will be included in the imputation models. A specific sensitivity analysis will be performed by excluding participants who undergo major changes in lipid-lowering therapy during the 8-week intervention period. A second sensitivity analysis using complete-case analysis will be conducted to compare with the primary ITT results.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

The full protocol is available via the trial registry (IRCT). Upon reasonable request and after signing a data access agreement, de-identified participant data and the statistical code used for the primary analysis will be made available to researchers for the purpose of replicating the results.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

The coordinating center for our study is led by the research team, responsible for training, monitoring, data management, and overall operational support. The trial steering committee (TSC) comprises key investigators, a statistician, and consumer representatives, with the Chief Principal Investigator chairing the TSC to provide leadership and ensure integrity.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

Given the low-risk nature of this nutritional intervention, a formal Data Monitoring Committee was not established; however, the Principal Investigator (PI) will conduct continuous internal safety monitoring. This oversight has been expanded to include mandatory weekly safety reviews by the PI to assess trial progress and participant well-being. Most importantly, all Serious Adverse Events (SAEs) will be reported to the Ethics Committee within 24 h of the research team becoming aware of the occurrence.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Adverse events (AEs) will be actively monitored through weekly phone check-ins and at the final clinic visit. Any adverse event, whether related or unrelated to the sumac and cinnamon supplements, will be documented and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5.0). The study intervention will be discontinued immediately if a participant experiences a severe adverse reaction, as determined by the treating physician or the PI, based on predefined stopping criteria.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The trial conduct will be monitored internally by the research team to ensure adherence to the protocol and ethical standards, with auditing conducted independently of investigators and sponsors if circumstances warrant. However, given the low-risk nature of this intervention, a Data Monitoring Committee was not deemed necessary initially.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g. trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

Our study has a well-defined plan for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties, including notifying investigators, Research Ethics Committees (REC), trial participants, trial registries (IRCT), and regulatory authorities. This will be done systematically and on time, following established guidelines and ethical considerations. In the event of significant modifications, such as changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes, or analyses, all stakeholders will be promptly informed to maintain the integrity of the trial and uphold ethical standards. Communication channels will include official letters, email notifications, and updates on trial registries.

Trial results will be communicated via publication in scientific journals, through presentations at International Clinical Nutrition Congress. The target journals will be chosen to reach specific academic audiences interested in Cardiology, nutrition, and related fields.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few trials investigating the combined effects of sumac and cinnamon, and the first study to specifically focus on patients in the acute phase post-myocardial infarction with comorbid diabetes and obesity. Prior evidence suggests that the high polyphenol and antioxidant content in these spices may significantly benefit cardiovascular health and overall well-being (14, 18, 20, 23–25, 32, 33, 60). By initiating the intervention within the Cardiac Care Unit (CCU), we aim to target the critical early inflammatory phase following an acute myocardial infarction, a period where metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation is most pronounced and potentially most responsive to adjunctive nutritional support. The selected dosing strategy for the combination group (2 g sumac and 1 g cinnamon) was designed to explore potential additive effects while maintaining patient compliance; however, we acknowledge that the reduced sumac dose in this arm compared to the 3 g used in the single-extract group is a limitation for definitively interpreting true “synergy”. To ensure high methodological rigor and avoid issues of multiplicity, we have designated LDL-C as our sole primary outcome, while reclassifying inflammatory markers, quality of life [38], and pain levels [39] as secondary endpoints. Our statistical plan further strengthens the study’s validity by categorizing statin therapy by intensity and including it as a covariate in the ANCOVA models, alongside sensitivity analyses that exclude participants undergoing major medication changes to control for confounding variables. Additionally, the implementation of the Bang Blinding Index (BBI) at study completion allows for a formal, quantified assessment of blinding success among participants and assessors. We recognize several study limitations, including the 8-week duration which may not fully capture long-term changes in quality of life [38], the potential influence of the natural recovery process on subjective pain outcomes [39], the single-center design, and the absence of biochemical adherence markers to objectively verify supplement intake. Ultimately, the publication of this protocol ensures transparency and avoids duplication of efforts [41], providing preliminary evidence on whether these common spices can serve as effective, low-risk complementary therapies for high-risk cardiovascular populations.
