A control variate method based on polynomial approximation of Brownian path
Josselin Garnier, Laurent Mertz

Abstract
We present a novel control variate technique for enhancing the efficiency of Monte Carlo (MC) estimation of expectations involving solutions to stochastic differential equations (SDEs). Our method integrates a primary fine-time-step discretization of the SDE with a control variate derived from a secondary coarse-time-step discretization driven by a piecewise parabolic approximation of Brownian motion. This approximation is conditioned on the same fine-scale Brownian increments, enabling strong coupling between the estimators. The expectation of the control variate is computed via an independent MC simulation using the coarse approximation. We characterize the minimized quadratic error decay as a function of the computational budget and the weak and strong orders of the primary and secondary discretization schemes. We demonstrate the method’s effectiveness through numerical experiments on representative SDEs.



1
Introduction

The Monte Carlo (MC) method is one of the most effective numerical techniques for estimating the expectation associated with the solution of a stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by Brownian motion, particularly in disciplines such as quantitative finance, physics, and engineering
[
13
,
17
]
.
To obtain an accurate estimation, the method requires simulating many trajectories of a discretized version of the SDE and averaging the quantity of interest across these paths. Naturally, accuracy improves with a finer time discretization and a larger number of MC samples.
However, to achieve high precision, the computational cost of such simulations can be substantial.
To mitigate the cost, employing high-order schemes and variance reduction techniques can be highly effective.

High-order discretization methods are often necessary to balance accuracy and efficiency. Standard schemes such as the Euler–Maruyama method converge with strong order
1/2
and weak order
1
, which can be prohibitively inefficient.
Higher-order schemes, such as the Milstein method or stochastic Runge–Kutta (RK) methods, achieve better convergence rates by incorporating additional information from stochastic integrals
[
13
,
16
]
. This improved accuracy reduces the discretization bias, meaning fewer time steps are required to reach a desired tolerance, which is crucial when simulations are embedded within MC frameworks where computational costs scale with both the numbers of samples and time steps.

Variance reduction techniques have been extensively studied to overcome the slow convergence of classical MC methods. Early approaches focused on control variates (CVs) and antithetic variates
[
10
]
, as well as importance sampling strategies tailored to rare-event problems
[
1
]
.
CVs improve efficiency by exploiting correlations between the target functional and auxiliary quantities with known or cheaply estimable expectations, thereby reducing variance without biasing the estimator
[
10
]
. In SDE applications, natural CVs include analytically tractable approximations
[
5
]
, coarse discretization approximations
[
6
]
, and conditional probabilistic representations
[
18
]
. Recent developments extend this principle to adaptive and data-driven CVs, including regression-based methods
[
14
]
and machine-learning–enhanced strategies
[
12
]
.
A major breakthrough came with the introduction of the multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) method
[
6
]
, which exploits a hierarchy of discretizations to dramatically reduce variance at near-optimal computational complexity, spurring further extensions such as the multilevel quasi-MC
[
9
]
and the multi-index MC framework
[
11
]
.

In this paper, we introduce a novel CV approach designed under a computational budget constraint, which significantly enhances the efficiency of MC methods for SDEs driven by Brownian motion. Our method introduces a CV constructed from a discretized version of the same SDE, but driven by a piecewise parabolic approximation of Brownian motion
[
4
]
, using a coarse time step. The key innovation lies in conditioning this approximation on the same fine Brownian increments used in the primary SDE discretization with a fine time step. This coupling between the CV and the target quantity enables a significant reduction in variance. The expectation of the CV is estimated through MC sampling using the unconditioned parabolic approximation of BM, which is computationally much cheaper than simulating the full SDE with fine discretization. In this context, for a given computational budget, the four parameters – fine and coarse time steps, and the two sizes of MC samples – are optimized to achieve the highest accuracy.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents an overview of the results. It highlights the advantages of our method by comparing its minimized quadratic error decay rate with respect to the computational budget with that of the optimized standard MC method. Section
3
presents our novel CV method, including the construction of both primary and secondary discretization schemes, the coupling mechanism, and the optimization of parameters under a fixed computational budget to achieve the maximal variance reduction. Section
4
presents numerical illustrations that confirm the variance reduction, using several examples involving Euler and RK schemes for the primary discretization and the parabola method for the secondary scheme. Special attention is given to the inhomogeneous geometric Brownian motion (IGBM)
[
22
,
23
]
, which challenges the analysis due to the very small constant in the strong convergence error of the parabola method. In Section
5
, we discuss the integration of our approach into a multilevel MC framework.

2
Overview of the results

In our CV method, the central innovation lies in the construction of a CV using a piecewise parabolic approximation of Brownian motion
[
4
]
, conditioned on the fine-scale Brownian increments employed in the costly, primary SDE simulation. This CV is computed using a coarse time-step discretization, which is significantly less expensive than the fine-scale simulation. The method is formulated under a computational budget constraint, and we derive optimal choices for four key parameters: the fine and coarse time steps, and the respective numbers of MC samples.

Our theoretical analysis shows that the proposed CV estimator achieves a quadratic error decay rate of the form

\mathcal{E}\lesssim\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}},

where
\mathcal{C}
is the computational budget,
\alpha
denotes the weak convergence order of the primary discretization scheme and
\gamma
denotes the strong convergence error of the secondary scheme.
For instance, using the Euler–Maruyama method as the primary scheme (
\alpha=1
) and the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion method as the secondary scheme (
\gamma=1
) yields an error rate of
\mathcal{C}^{-6/7}
. This improves the optimized standard MC method based on the primary scheme where

\mathcal{E}\lesssim\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}.

In the latter case, optimization is performed over only two parameters: the time step and the corresponding number of MC samples

[
3
]
. For example, using the Euler–Maruyama method yields an error rate of
\mathcal{C}^{-2/3}
.
Schemes with higher order than the Euler–Maruyama method can be used, and similar improvements due to the use of a CV based on the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion
can be obtained.

Numerical experiments support the theoretical predictions and demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed CV method across a variety of SDE examples, including systems with multiplicative noise and Brownian particles in multi-well potentials.
In these academic and realistic test cases, we will see that the theoretical predictions are confirmed for moderate and large computational budgets.
Particular attention is given to the inhomogeneous geometric Brownian motion, which is considered as a reference test case in the literature because it is a simple SDE but yet has no known method of exact simulation. We will see that this example involves constants in the convergence rates that differ significantly from standard cases. Consequently, the predicted asymptotic behavior becomes observable only for very large computational budgets, which may be impractical. In such scenarios, we propose a mitigation strategy to reconcile theoretical expectations with practical performance.

3
Control variate method for stochastic differential equations

We consider a general SDE on the interval
[0,1]
and study the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued stochastic process
X_{t}
solution of

\textup{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\textup{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\textup{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=\xi,

(1)

where
W_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
b,\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are smooth vector fields (with bounded derivatives).
The objective is to estimate a quantity of interest (QoI) of the form
I=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]
, where
F
is a smooth and bounded function.

We will first describe a standard MC approach to the estimation of the QoI based on the use of a primary discretization scheme.
We will next describe an original CV approach based on the appropriate use of two discretization schemes (primary and secondary) that have specific properties.

3.1
Primary discretization scheme

As we will see below,
the primary discretization scheme should have the following properties so as to allow for an efficient MC estimation method of the QoI
I
:

- it is based on the small time step
h^{\prime}=1/N^{\prime}
.

- for each time step, it requires a limited number of calls to the drift
b
(ideally, only one call).

- its implementation depends on a family of i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) standard normal variables
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
that is obtained by a linear transform
{\cal G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}:{\cal C}([0,1],\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}}
applied to the Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.
Here the upper index notation
{\rm p}
stands for primary.

- it should have a high weak order
\alpha
.

In this paper, the total number of calls to the drift
b
is interpreted as the cost of the method because it is often this term that is computationally expensive (as in the case of Brownian particles in complex potentials).

The primary scheme gives an approximation
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
of the solution of the SDE (
1
) at time
1
that is a deterministic function of
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
.
This random vector is made of
d^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
i.i.d. standard normal variables.
We present below two schemes that can be used as primary schemes.

Euler–Maruyama scheme.

The solution of (
1
) can be approximated with the Euler–Maruyama scheme as follows:
X_{0}^{h^{\prime}}=\xi
, for each
1\leq i^{\prime}\leq N^{\prime}
,
t_{i^{\prime}}=i^{\prime}h^{\prime}
and

X_{t_{i^{\prime}}}^{h^{\prime}}=X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{h^{\prime}}+b\left(X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{h^{\prime}}\right)h^{\prime}+\sigma\left(X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{h^{\prime}}\right)\sqrt{h^{\prime}}g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}.

(2)

We remark that
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}

(and more generally,
\{X_{t_{i^{\prime}}}^{h^{\prime}},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
) is a deterministic function of

{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\left\{g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\right\},

(3)

where

g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}\big(W_{i^{\prime}h^{\prime}}-W_{(i^{\prime}-1)h^{\prime}}\big)

are indeed i.i.d. standard normal variables.
At each iteration, the drift coefficient
b(\cdot)
is called only once.
The method has weak order
\alpha=1

[
13
]
.

SRA1 scheme.

When
\sigma
is constant, we can consider a higher-order method such as a stochastic RK method SRA1, that is:
X_{0}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}=\xi
, for each
1\leq i^{\prime}\leq N^{\prime}
,
t_{i^{\prime}}=i^{\prime}h^{\prime}
and

X_{t_{i^{\prime}}}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}=X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{1}{3}b\left(X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}\right)+\frac{2}{3}b\left(X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}+\frac{3}{4}\theta_{i^{\prime}-1}\right)\right)h^{\prime}+\sigma\sqrt{h^{\prime}}g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},

(4)

where

\theta_{i^{\prime}-1}=b\left(X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}}^{{\rm rk},h^{\prime}}\right)h^{\prime}+\sigma\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\left(g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}f^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}\right).

(5)

We remark that
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}

is a deterministic function of

{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\{g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},f^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\},

(6)

where

\displaystyle g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}\big(W_{i^{\prime}h^{\prime}}-W_{(i^{\prime}-1)h^{\prime}}\big),

(7)

\displaystyle f^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}\Big[\frac{2}{h^{\prime}}\int_{(i^{\prime}-1)h^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}h^{\prime}}(W_{u}-W_{(i^{\prime}-1)h^{\prime}}){\rm d}u-\big(W_{i^{\prime}h^{\prime}}-W_{(i^{\prime}-1)h^{\prime}}\big)\Big]

(8)

are indeed i.i.d. standard normal variables.
Here, at each iteration, the drift coefficient
b(.)
is called twice.
See Equation (6.14) and Table 6.3 in
[
19
]
for details on SRA1. This is a two-stage stochastic RK scheme.
The method has weak order
\alpha=2
.

3.2
Optimized standard Monte-Carlo method

In a standard MC method, one uses the primary discretization method
with fine time size
h^{\prime}
for the SDE that provides an approximation
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
of
X_{1}
, and one estimates the QoI
I=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]
by

\hat{I}_{M^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}}),

(9)

where
X_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
,
k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,M^{\prime}
, are i.i.d. copies of
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
.

The quadratic error
{\cal E}=\mathbb{E}[(\hat{I}_{M^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}}-I)^{2}]
is the sum of a squared bias term and a variance term:

{\cal E}=\big(\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}{\rm Var}\big(F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})\big).

If the discretization method has weak order
\alpha
, that is to say:

\big|\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]\big|\lesssim{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha},

then the quadratic error satisfies:

{\cal E}\lesssim{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}},

because
{\rm Var}\big(F({X}_{1}^{h^{\prime}})\big)\simeq{\rm Var}\big(F({X}_{1})\big)(1+o(1))
.
Here
\lesssim
/
\simeq
means that the inequality / equality is valid up to a multiplicative constant.

The total cost of the simulation is

{\cal C}\simeq\frac{M^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}},

because the grid step is
h^{\prime}
(hence there are
1/h^{\prime}
time steps) and the MC sample size is
M^{\prime}
.

For a fixed total cost, one can minimize (the upper bound of) the error by choosing

h^{\prime}\simeq{\cal C}^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\>\mbox{and}\>M^{\prime}\simeq{\cal C}^{\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}

(10)

and then

{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}.

(11)

This result is obtained by looking at the critical points of the function
h^{\prime}\mapsto{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+{\cal C}^{-1}{h^{\prime}}^{-1}
obtained by substituting
M^{\prime}={\cal C}h^{\prime}
into the expression of the upper bound of the error
{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}
.
This result has been known for a long time
[
3
]
and numerical examples there and in
[
13
]
show that this asymptotic behavior can be clearly observed on finite samples.

Remark 1
.

For the Euler–Maruyama method, one has
\alpha=1

[
13
]
and then
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{2}{3}}
.
For second-order schemes such as the ones proposed in
[
15
,
20
,
21
]
or the stochastic RK scheme SRA1 (which is valid when the diffusion coefficient is constant and which requires two calls to the drift function at each step)
[
19
]
, one has
\alpha=2
and then
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{4}{5}}
.

3.3
Secondary discretization scheme

As we will see below, the secondary discretization scheme should have the following properties so as to allow for an efficient CV estimation method of the QoI
I
:

- it is based on the moderate time step
h=1/N
, which is such that
h=qh^{\prime}
, with
q
an integer that is designed to be large.

- for each time step, it requires a limited number of calls to the drift
b
(ideally, one call).

- its implementation depends on a family
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
of i.i.d. standard normal variables that is obtained by a linear transform
{\cal G}^{{\rm s},h}:{\cal C}([0,1],\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{{\rm s},h}}
applied to the Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Here the upper index notation
{\rm s}
stands for secondary.

- it should have a high strong order
\gamma
.

As the primary and secondary schemes depend on Gaussian vectors extracted linearly from the driving Brownian motion, it is possible to develop two versions of the secondary scheme.

- The unconditioned version is the usual scheme obtained by sampling
d^{{\rm s},h}
i.i.d. standard normal variables that form the Gaussian vector
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
. This gives the approximation
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
, which is a deterministic function of
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
.

- The conditioned version (conditioned to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
) is the usual scheme using a sample that has the distribution of

{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
given
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
.
This is possible and easy to implement by the Gaussian conditioning theorem as the joint vector
({\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}},{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h})
is Gaussian.
The distribution of
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
given
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}

has the form
{\bf A}{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}+\hat{\bf B}\hat{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
, where
{\bf A}
is a deterministic
d^{{\rm s},h}\times d^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
matrix,
{\bf B}
is a deterministic
d^{{\rm s},h}\times\hat{d}^{{\rm s},h}
matrix, and
\hat{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
is an independent family of
\hat{d}^{{\rm s},h}
i.i.d. standard normal variables. This gives the approximation
\hat{X}_{1}^{h}
, which is a deterministic function of
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h}
and
\hat{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}
.
Therefore,
{X}_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
and
\hat{X}_{1}^{h}
are correlated via
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
. This correlation is critical in the CV method proposed in the next section.
We present below two schemes that can be used as secondary scheme.

Milstein method.

Milstein method is a discretization scheme that has strong order
\gamma=1

[
15
]
.
In the unconditioned version,

\check{X}_{0}^{{\rm mi},h}=\xi
and

\check{X}_{t_{i}}^{{\rm mi},h}=\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}+b\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)h+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\cdot\nabla)\sigma\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)h\big((g^{{\rm s}}_{i})^{2}-1\big)+\sigma\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)\sqrt{h}g^{{\rm s}}_{i},

for each
1\leq i\leq N
, with
t_{i}=ih
.
We remark that
\check{X}_{1}^{h}

is a deterministic function of
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}=\left\{g^{{\rm s}}_{i},i=1,\ldots,N\right\}
,
where

g^{{\rm s}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\big(W_{ih}-W_{(i-1)h}\big)

are indeed i.i.d. standard normal variables.

In the conditioned version (conditioned to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\{g_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
defined by (
3
) or to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\{g^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},f^{{\rm p}}_{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
defined by (
6
)), we have
\hat{X}_{0}^{{\rm mi},h}=\xi
and

\hat{X}_{t_{i}}^{{\rm mi},h}=\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}+b\left(\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)h+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma\cdot\nabla)\sigma\left(\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)h\big((g_{i}^{{\rm s}})^{2}-1\big)+\sigma\left(\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)\sqrt{h}{g}_{i}^{{\rm s}},

for each
1\leq i\leq N
,
where

g_{i}^{{\rm s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}g_{(i-1)q+j}^{{\rm p}}.

Note that this method requires to call the gradient of
\sigma
.
In the case where
\nabla\sigma
is not known analytically, it is possible to use a slightly different scheme in which the term
(\sigma\cdot\nabla)\sigma\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)h\big((g^{{\rm s}}_{i})^{2}-1\big)
is replaced by
\sigma\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}+h\big((g^{{\rm s}}_{i})^{2}-1\big)\sigma\big(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\big)\right)-\sigma\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{{\rm mi},h}\right)
.

Parabolic approximation.

As introduced in
[
4
]
, the (piecewise) parabolic approximation of a Brownian motion is the (piecewise) second order polynomial that satisfies some particular interpolation relations detailed in Appendix
A.1
.
Let us consider a time step
h=1/N
. The piecewise parabolic approximation
\wideparen{W}^{{\rm s},h}
of the Brownian motion
W
on
[0,1]
is, for
u\in[(i-1)h,ih)
:

\wideparen{W}_{u}^{{\rm s},h}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}g_{j}^{{\rm s}}+\frac{u-(i-1)h}{\sqrt{h}}g_{i}^{{\rm s}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}(u-(i-1)h)(ih-u)}{h^{3/2}}g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime},

(12)

where

g_{i}^{{\rm s}}=\frac{W_{(i-1)h,ih}}{\sqrt{h}},\qquad g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{12}H_{(i-1)h,ih}}{\sqrt{h}},

(13)

and
W_{s,t}
and
H_{s,t}
are defined by

W_{s,t}=W_{t}-W_{s}\>\mbox{ and }\>H_{s,t}=-\frac{1}{2}W_{s,t}+\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{s}^{t}W_{s,u}\textup{d}u.

(14)

Note that the piecewise parabolic approximation of the Brownian motion depends only on
{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}=\{g_{i}^{{\rm s}},g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime},i=1,\ldots,N\}
,
which is a family of i.i.d. standard normal variables.

The unconditioned secondary discretization scheme consists in solving the SDE by replacing the Brownian motion
W
by its piecewise parabolic approximation
\wideparen{W}^{{\rm s},h}
and by integrating the corresponding ordinary differential equation.
If it is possible to integrate the ordinary differential equation driven by the piecewise parabolic approximation exactly, then one gets an approximation denoted
\check{X}^{h}_{1}
that has strong convergence order
\gamma=1
as shown in
[
4
]
.
If it is not possible to integrate the ordinary differential equation driven by the piecewise parabolic approximation exactly, then one can use a RK type solver that requires one call to the drift function (and four calls to the diffusivity function) per time step and that gives an approximation denoted
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
that has the same strong order
\gamma=1
. This solver is detailed in Appendix
C
.

The conditioned secondary discretization scheme consists in solving the SDE by replacing the Brownian motion
W
by its conditional piecewise parabolic approximation
\wideparen{W}^{{\rm s},h}
(conditioned to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
) and by integrating the corresponding ordinary differential equation. The form of the conditional piecewise parabolic approximation depends on the form of the conditioning
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
:

i)
The conditional piecewise parabolic approximation
\wideparen{W}^{{\rm s},h}
conditioned to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\{g_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
defined by (
3
)
has the form (
12
), but

g_{i}^{{\rm s}},g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}
are now given by (see Appendices
A.2.1
and
A.2.3
)

g_{i}^{{\rm s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}g_{(i-1)q+j}^{{\rm p}},\qquad g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{q}}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+\frac{1-2j}{q}\right)g_{(i-1)q+j}^{{\rm p}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3q}}\hat{g}_{i}^{{\rm s}}\right],

(15)

and
\hat{\bf G}^{{\rm s},h}=\{\hat{g}_{i}^{{\rm s}},i=1,\ldots,N\}
is a family of i.i.d. standard normal variables independent of
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
(they are defined by

\hat{g}_{i}^{{\rm s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}f^{{\rm p}}_{(i-1)q+j}
with
f^{{\rm p}}_{(i-1)q+j}
given in terms of
W
by (
8
)).

ii)
The conditional piecewise parabolic approximation
\wideparen{W}^{{\rm s},h}
conditioned to
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}=\{g_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p},f_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p},i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}
defined by (
6
)
has the form (
12
), but

g_{i}^{{\rm s}},g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}
are now completely determined by
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
and given by (see Appendices
A.2.2
and
A.2.4
)

g_{i}^{{\rm s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}g_{(i-1)q+j}^{{\rm p}},\qquad g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{q}}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+\frac{1-2j}{q}\right)g_{(i-1)q+j}^{{\rm p}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}f^{{\rm p}}_{(i-1)q+j}\right].

(16)

In both cases, this gives the approximation denoted
\hat{X}^{h}_{1}
.

3.4
Optimized control-variate method

CV estimators are a powerful class of reduced variance estimators
[
10
]
.
In the proposed CV method for the solution of the SDE (
1
) one uses

1)
an approximation
(\hat{X}_{1}^{h},X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})
of
X_{1}
, where
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
is obtained with the primary discretization scheme with fine time step
h^{\prime}
and
\hat{X}_{1}^{h}
is obtained with the secondary discretization scheme
conditioned by the Gaussian vector
{\bf G}^{{\rm p},h^{\prime}}
used to generate
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
(we will take a coarse time step
h=qh^{\prime}
with
q\gg 1
),

2) an approximation
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
of
X_{1}
, based on the unconditioned secondary discretization scheme
(this is a cheap method because
h
is much larger than
h^{\prime}
).

One estimates the quantity of interest
I=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]
by

\hat{I}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}F(\check{X}_{1}^{h,k})+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}\big[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}})-F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})\big],

(17)

where
\check{X}_{1}^{h,k}
,
k=1,\ldots,M
, are i.i.d. copies of
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
, and
(X_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}},\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})
,
k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,M^{\prime}
, are i.i.d. copies of
(X_{1}^{h},\hat{X}_{1}^{h^{\prime}})
.

Remark 2
.

We can take the optimal form of the CV estimator:

\hat{J}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}F(\check{X}_{1}^{h,k})+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}\big[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}})-\hat{\lambda}_{M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})\big],

with

\hat{\lambda}_{M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}=\frac{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F({X}_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}})F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})-\Big[\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F({X}_{1}^{h^{\prime},k^{\prime}})\Big]\Big[\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})\Big]}{\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})^{2}-\Big[\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{M^{\prime}}F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h,k^{\prime}})\Big]^{2}}.

We can gain some advantage (variance reduction) in using this version, but the optimal control coefficient
\lambda
is very close to one in our framework because the variables
(X_{1}^{h},\hat{X}_{1}^{h^{\prime}})
are strongly correlated as soon as
h^{\prime}\ll h\ll 1
. As a consequence, there is no significant loss in using
\lambda=1
instead of
\hat{\lambda}_{M^{\prime}}
.

Bias.

Since
\hat{X}_{1}^{h}
and
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
have the same distribution, the bias of the estimator (
17
) is

\mathbb{E}[\hat{I}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}]-I=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})].

Quadratic error.

The quadratic error
{\cal E}=\mathbb{E}[(\hat{I}_{M,M^{\prime}}^{h,h^{\prime}}-I)^{2}]
is the sum of a squared bias term and two variance terms:

{\cal E}=\Big(\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{M}{\rm Var}\Big(F(\check{X}_{1}^{h})\Big)+\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}{\rm Var}\Big(F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})-F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h})\Big).

If the discretization method for
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
has weak order
\alpha
and strong order
\beta
, that is to say:

\big|\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h^{\prime}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]\big|\lesssim{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha},\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}-X_{1}|^{2}\big]^{1/2}\lesssim{h^{\prime}}^{\beta},

and if the discretization method for
\check{X}_{1}^{h}
has strong order
\gamma
, that is to say:

\mathbb{E}\big[|\check{X}_{1}^{h}-{X}_{1}|^{2}\big]^{1/2}\lesssim{h}^{\gamma},

then the quadratic error satisfies

{\cal E}\lesssim{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M}+\frac{h^{2\gamma}+{h^{\prime}}^{2\beta}}{M^{\prime}},

because
{\rm Var}\big(F(\check{X}_{1}^{h})\big)\simeq{\rm Var}\big(F({X}_{1})\big)(1+o(1))
.
The total cost of the simulation is

{\cal C}\simeq\frac{M}{h}+\frac{M^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}},

because the time steps are
h
and
h^{\prime}
and the sizes of the MC samples are
M
and
M^{\prime}
, respectively, in the two terms of Eq. (
17
).

Proposition 3
.

For a fixed total cost, provided
\alpha,\beta>\gamma/(2\gamma+1)
, the error is minimized by choosing

h\simeq{\cal C}^{-\frac{1}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}},\>\>h^{\prime}\simeq{\cal C}^{-\frac{2\gamma+1}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}},\>\>M\simeq{\cal C}^{\frac{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}},\>\>\mbox{ and }M^{\prime}\simeq{\cal C}^{\frac{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha-2\gamma}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}}

(18)

(note that
h^{\prime}\ll h\ll 1
and
1\ll M^{\prime}\ll M
), and then:

{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}}.

(19)

The proof of the proposition is given in Appendix
B.1
.
For example, for the Euler–Maruyama method for
X_{1}^{h^{\prime}}
, one has
\alpha=1
,
\beta=1/2

[
13
]
, and for the parabola method for
\hat{X}_{1}^{h}
one has
\gamma=1

[
4
]
, and then
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{6}{7}}
.
If we replace the Euler–Maruyama method by the SRA1 scheme (valid when the diffusion coefficient is constant)
[
19
]
, one has
\alpha=2
,
\beta=3/2
and then
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{12}{13}}
.

Remark 4
.

The parabola method (or the Milstein method) has weak order
\gamma=1

[
4
]
. Hence, if ones uses this method alone in the standard MC method described in Subsection
3.2
, then one gets a quadratic error that satisfies
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}
, that is to say
{\cal E}\lesssim{\cal C}^{-\frac{2}{3}}
.
Comparing with Remark
1
(that deals with optimized standard MC methods) and Eq. (
19
), it is clear that the proposed CV method has a clear advantage over the optimal uses of the two distinct methods.

Remark 5
.

The CV approach proposed in this section is compatible with the many-query context.
In this context, the objective is to compute a large number of MC estimations of
expectations of functions of the solutions of parameterized SDEs
[
2
]
.
This situation is encountered in finance, for instance, where option pricing often involves calculating the price of an option for different values of the parameters of a parameterized local volatility model to be calibrated.

and
\check{X}^{h}

\hat{X}^{h}

where
\tilde{b}=b-(1/2)\sigma^{\prime}\sigma

\tilde{b}_{i}=b_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\sigma_{j}.

4.1
Euler scheme based method

Let

h^{\prime}

h^{\prime 1/2}g_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p}

(\hat{X}_{t_{i}}^{h})_{0\leq i\leq N}

\hat{X}_{0}^{h}=\xi

1\leq i\leq N

\hat{X}_{t_{i}}^{h}=z\left(1;\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{h},\hat{A}_{i-1,i},\hat{B}_{i-1,i}\right)

where

\hat{A}_{i-1,i}=g_{i}^{\rm s}+\sqrt{3}g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime},\>\>\hat{B}_{i-1,i}=-\sqrt{12}g_{i}^{{\rm s},\prime}

with

g_{i}^{\rm s}
and
g_{i}^{\rm s,\prime}

defined in the equation (
15
)
and
for any
z_{0},A,B
and

is obtained from

\begin{cases}&z(0)=z_{0},\\
&{\rm d}z(u)/{\rm d}u=h\tilde{b}(z(u))+h^{1/2}\sigma(z(u))(A+Bu),\quad u\in(0,1].\end{cases}

If

does not have a closed form expression, then we propose the approximation with local error of order

\hat{X}^{h}

and

).

4.3
Special case: Inhomogeneous geometric Brownian motion

We consider the inhomogeneous geometric Brownian motion (IGBM) that is the solution of

\textup{d}X_{t}=a(b-X_{t})\textup{d}t+\sigma X_{t}\textup{d}W_{t},\,t>0,\,\mbox{with an initial condition}\,X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

This process has no known method of exact simulation, it is frequently encountered in mathematical finance and other fields, and it is used as a model to test and compare various numerical methods
[
22
]
.
We recall there is an explicit formula, using the notation
\tilde{a}=a+\sigma^{2}/2
, that is

\forall t\geq 0,\>X_{t}=e^{-\tilde{a}t+\sigma W_{t}}\left(y_{0}+ab\int_{0}^{t}e^{\left(\tilde{a}u-\sigma W_{u}\right)}\textup{d}u\right).

(24)

Therefore, with
N\geq 1,\,h:=1/N
fixed as above, for any
1\leq i\leq N
,

X_{ih}=e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma(W_{ih}-W_{(i-1)h})}\left(X_{(i-1)h}+ab\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}e^{\tilde{a}(u-(i-1)h)-\sigma(W_{u}-W_{(i-1)h})}\textup{d}u\right).

We will build our CV estimator using the following discretization schemes.

1.

Euler–Maruyama method with fine time step
h^{\prime}=1/N^{\prime}
.

Define the sequence
(X_{t_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}})_{0\leq i^{\prime}\leq N^{\prime}}
where
t_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}:=i^{\prime}h^{\prime}
by
X_{0}^{h^{\prime}}:=X_{0}
and

X_{t_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}}=X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}}+a(b-X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}})h^{\prime}+\sigma X_{t_{i^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}^{h^{\prime}}\sqrt{h^{\prime}}g_{i^{\prime}}^{\rm p},\>\mbox{for}\>1\leq i^{\prime}\leq N^{\prime}.

2.

Conditioned-parabola ODE method with coarse time step
h
.

Define the sequence
(\hat{X}_{t_{i}}^{h})_{0\leq i\leq N}
where
t_{i}:=ih
by
\hat{X}_{0}^{h}:=X_{0}
and

\hat{X}_{t_{i}}^{h}=e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma\sqrt{h}g_{i}^{\rm s}}\left(\hat{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{h}+ab\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}e^{\tilde{a}(u-(i-1)h)-\sigma\left(\wideparen{W}_{u}^{{\rm s},h}-\wideparen{W}_{(i-1)h}^{{\rm s},h}\right)}\right)\textup{d}u,

where
\wideparen{W}_{u}^{{\rm s},h}

is defined in (
12
), depending on
g_{i}^{\rm s}
and
g_{i}^{\rm s,\prime}
in
(
15
).
We remark

\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}e^{\tilde{a}(u-(i-1)h)-\sigma\left(\wideparen{W}_{u}^{{\rm s},h}-\wideparen{W}_{(i-1)h}^{{\rm s},h}\right)}\textup{d}u=\mathcal{I}(h,\hat{W}_{i-1,i},\hat{H}_{i-1,i}),

where

\mathcal{I}(h,W,H):=h\int_{0}^{1}e^{\tilde{a}hv-\sigma(vW+6v(1-v)H)}\textup{d}v.

and

\hat{W}_{i-1,i}=\sqrt{h}g_{i}^{\rm s}\mbox{ and }\hat{H}_{i-1,i}=\frac{\sqrt{h}}{2\sqrt{3}}g_{i}^{\rm s,\prime}.

3.

Free-parabola ODE method with coarse time step
h
.

Define the sequence
(\check{X}_{t_{i}}^{h})_{0\leq i\leq N}
where
t_{i}:=ih
by
\check{X}_{0}^{h}:=X_{0}
and

\check{X}_{t_{i}}^{h}=e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma\check{W}_{i-1,i}}\left(\check{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{h}+ab\>\mathcal{I}(h,\check{W}_{i-1,i},\check{H}_{i-1,i})\right),

where

\check{W}_{i-1,i}=\sqrt{h}g_{i}^{\rm s}\mbox{ and }\check{H}_{i-1,i}=\frac{\sqrt{h}}{2\sqrt{3}}g_{i}^{\rm s,\prime}.

Here, the variables
g_{i}^{\rm s}
and
g_{i}^{\rm s,\prime}
are an unconditioned version of (
13
).

The integral
\mathcal{I}(h,W,H)
can be approximated by a 3-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature numerical method as follows

5
Towards a Multilevel Monte Carlo approach

The multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) method is a variance reduction technique for simulating SDEs. Instead of relying solely on fine discretizations (which are accurate but expensive), MLMC combines simulations across a hierarchy of time steps: many cheap simulations with coarse discretizations and fewer simulations with fine discretizations. The main idea is to estimate expectations using a telescoping sum of corrections between successive levels. This greatly reduces the overall computational cost while maintaining the accuracy of fine-level simulations
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
be the number of numerical schemes or numerical levels used in MLMC generalizing our estimator (
17
) for which
L=2
.
We use the notation
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{L})
where
0<h_{1}<\cdots<h_{L}
and
\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{L})\in(\mathbb{N}^{\star})^{L}
.
Define

I_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{M}}=\frac{1}{M_{L}}\sum\limits_{m_{L}=1}^{M_{L}}F(\check{X}_{1}^{h_{L},m_{L}})+\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{M_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{m_{\ell}=1}^{M_{\ell}}F(X_{1}^{h_{\ell},m_{\ell}})-F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell+1},m_{\ell}}),

(26)

where we consider
L
independent families of i.i.d. random variables:

\left\{\check{X}^{h_{L},m_{L}}\right\}_{m_{L}=1}^{M_{L}},\quad\text{and for each }\ell=1,\dots,L-1,\text{ the pairs }\left\{\left(\hat{X}^{h_{\ell+1},m_{\ell}},X^{h_{\ell},m_{\ell}}\right)\right\}_{m_{\ell}=1}^{M_{\ell}}.

In the multilevel estimator (
26
) one uses

1)

L-1
independent approximations
(\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell+1}},X_{1}^{h_{\ell}})
of
X_{1}
for each
1\leq\ell\leq L-1
, where
X_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
is obtained with a local primary discretization scheme with the time step
h_{\ell}

finer than
h_{\ell+1}

and
\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell+1}}
is obtained with a local secondary discretization scheme
conditioned by the Gaussian increments used to generate
X_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
.
Note that the local secondary scheme employed
\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell+1}}
at level
\ell
which is employed as the local primary scheme for
X_{1}^{h_{\ell+1}}
at level
\ell+1
. Consequently, both variables
\hat{X}^{h_{\ell+1}}
and
X^{h_{\ell+1}}
are generated using the same numerical scheme and share the same distribution. However,
\hat{X}^{h_{\ell+1}}
is conditioned on Gaussian vector used to generate
X^{h_{\ell}}
, whereas
X^{h_{\ell+1}}
is generated independently (unconditioned).

2)
an approximation
\check{X}_{1}^{h_{L}}
of
X_{1}
, based on the unconditioned local secondary discretization scheme of the last level
L
.

Bias.

Since for each
1\leq\ell\leq L-1
, the random variables
\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
and
X_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
, as well as
\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{L}}
and
\check{X}_{1}^{h_{L}}
, share the same distribution, the bias of the estimator (
26
) is (telescoping terms)

\mathbb{E}[I_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{M}}]-I=\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h_{1}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})].

Quadratic error.

The quadratic error
{\cal E}=\mathbb{E}[(I_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{M}}-I)^{2}]
is the sum of a squared bias term and the variance term:

{\cal E}=\Big(\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1}^{h_{1}})]-\mathbb{E}[F(X_{1})]\Big)^{2}+{\rm Var}\big(I_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{M}}\big).

The variance term can be decomposed as:

{\rm Var}\big(I_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{M}}\big)=\frac{1}{M_{L}}{\rm Var}\left(F(\check{X}_{1}^{h_{L}})\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{M_{\ell}}{\rm Var}\left(F(X_{1}^{h_{\ell}})-F(\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell+1}})\right).

If the discretization method for
X_{1}^{h_{1}}
has weak order
\alpha

and if, for each
1\leq\ell\leq L
, the discretization method for
\hat{X}_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
(or
X_{1}^{h_{\ell}}
) has strong order
\gamma_{\ell}

then the quadratic error satisfies

{\cal E}\lesssim{h_{1}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{L}}+\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\frac{h_{\ell}^{2\gamma_{\ell}}+{h_{\ell+1}}^{2\gamma_{\ell+1}}}{M_{\ell}},

(27)

where we use
{\rm Var}\big(F(\check{X}_{1}^{h_{L}})\big)\simeq{\rm Var}\big(F({X}_{1})\big)(1+o(1))
.
The total budget cost is

\mathcal{C}=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{M_{\ell}}{h_{\ell}}.

In essence, the sum on the right-hand side of the inequality (
27
) is asymptotically dominated by the term that decays to zero most slowly when
h_{L}
and
M_{\ell}
go to
0
and
+\infty
, respectively.
This implies that, in the asymptotic regime, the leading contributions to the error arise from only two levels, effectively reducing the analysis to the case

L=2
.

Appendix A
Parabolic approximation of Brownian motion

This appendix provides the mathematical foundation for the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion, including its interpolation properties and Gaussian conditioning. It presents explicit formulas essential for implementing the numerical schemes discussed in the main text.

A.1
Definition of the parabolic approximation

We consider a real-valued standard Brownian motion
\{W_{u},u\geq 0\}
. Fix
t>s
.
As introduced in
[
4
]
, the parabolic approximation of
W
on
[s,t]
, denoted by
\wideparen{W}
, is the second order polynomial that satisfies

\wideparen{W}_{s}=W_{s},\>\wideparen{W}_{t}=W_{t},\>\mbox{and}\>\int_{s}^{t}\wideparen{W}_{u}\textup{d}u=\int_{s}^{t}W_{u}\textup{d}u.

It is uniquely defined by the three conditions above, which lead to the following formula

\wideparen{W}_{u}=W_{s}+\frac{u-s}{t-s}W_{s,t}+\frac{6(u-s)(t-u)}{(t-s)^{2}}H_{s,t},

where
W_{s,t}
and
H_{s,t}
are defined by (
14
).
As a consequence,

\int_{s}^{t}\wideparen{W}_{u}\textup{d}u=(t-s)\left(W_{s}+\frac{1}{2}W_{s,t}+H_{s,t}\right)\>\>\mbox{ and }\>\>\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}u}\wideparen{W}_{u}=\frac{1}{t-s}W_{s,t}+\frac{6(t+s-2u)}{(t-s)^{2}}H_{s,t}.

It is clear that
(W_{s,t},H_{s,t})
is a centered Gaussian vector. A direct calculation shows that
\mathbb{E}(W_{s,t}H_{s,t})=0
. Therefore,
W_{s,t}
and
H_{s,t}
are independent, centered,

\mathbb{E}W_{s,t}^{2}=t-s\>\mbox{ and }\>\mathbb{E}H_{s,t}^{2}=(t-s)/12.

We can therefore write

\wideparen{W}_{u}=W_{s}+\frac{u-s}{\sqrt{t-s}}g+\frac{\sqrt{3}(u-s)(t-u)}{(t-s)^{3/2}}g^{\prime}.

where

g=\frac{W_{s,t}}{\sqrt{t-s}},\qquad g^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{12}H_{s,t}}{\sqrt{t-s}}

are independent and identically distributed standard normal variables.

A.2
Two-level time discretization and piecewise parabolic Brownian motion

In this section we explain the dependence structure of the increments of the Brownian motions and the coefficients of the parabolic approximation. The results follow from Gaussian conditioning theorem.
Consider
N^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
.
Let
h^{\prime}=1/N^{\prime}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
. The coarse time step is
h=ph^{\prime}
, and the corresponding number of time steps is
N=N^{\prime}/q
(provided
N^{\prime}
is a multiple of
q
).

A.2.1
Decomposition of Brownian motion (part 1)

We denote

\delta W_{i}^{j}=W_{(i-1)h+jh^{\prime}}-W_{(i-1)h+(j-1)h^{\prime}}
,
\delta{\bf W}_{i}=(\delta W_{i}^{j})_{j=1}^{q}
,

\Delta W_{i}=W_{ih}-W_{(i-1)h}
and

\Delta I_{i}=\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}W_{u}-W_{(i-1)h}{\rm d}u
.

Result.

The distribution of

(\Delta W_{i},\Delta I_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
given
(\delta{\bf W}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is Gaussian with mean

\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\delta W_{i}^{j},h^{\prime}\sum_{j=1}^{q}(q+\frac{1}{2}-j)\delta W_{i}^{j}\Big)_{i=1}^{N}

and covariance

\oplus_{i=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{qh^{\prime 3}}{12}\end{pmatrix}.

In other words, given
(\delta{\bf W}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
the vector
(\Delta W_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is deterministic and equal to
(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\delta W_{i}^{j})_{i=1}^{N}
and the vector
(\Delta I_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is made of independent Gaussian variables with means
h^{\prime}\sum_{j=1}^{q}(q+\frac{1}{2}-j)\delta W_{i}^{j}
and variances
\frac{qh^{\prime 3}}{12}
.

Proof.

We first note that
(\delta{\bf W}_{i},\Delta W_{i},\Delta I_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are independent and identically
(q+2)
-dimensional Gaussian vectors.
Their distribution is

\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\bf W}_{i}\\
\Delta W_{i}\\
\Delta I_{i}\end{pmatrix}\sim{\cal N}\bigg(\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 0}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}h^{\prime}{\bf I}_{q}&h^{\prime}{\bf 1}&h^{\prime 2}(j+\frac{1}{2}-q)_{j=1}^{q}\\
h^{\prime}{\bf 1}^{T}&h&\frac{h^{2}}{2}\\
h^{\prime 2}{(j+\frac{1}{2}-q)_{j=1}^{q}}^{T}&\frac{h^{2}}{2}&\frac{h^{3}}{3}\end{pmatrix}\bigg)

We then apply the Gaussian conditioning theorem to get the distribution of
(\Delta W_{i},\Delta I_{i})
given
\delta{\bf W}_{i}
for any
i
. ∎

We denote by
g_{i}^{j}
the normalized elementary increments

g_{i}^{j}=(W_{(i-1)h+jh^{\prime}}-W_{(i-1)h+(j-1)h^{\prime}})/\sqrt{h^{\prime}}.

From the previous result, we find that,
given the elementary increments
(g_{i}^{j})_{1\leq j\leq q,1\leq i\leq N}
,

W_{ih}-W_{(i-1)h}
is deterministic:

\displaystyle W_{ih}-W_{(i-1)h}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}G_{i},

with
G_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}g_{i}^{j}
,
W_{ih}
is deterministic

W_{ih}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}G_{i^{\prime}}

and
\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}W_{u}du
is
of the form

\displaystyle\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}W_{u}{\rm d}u={h^{\prime}}^{3/2}\Big(q\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}G_{i^{\prime}}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}(q+\frac{1}{2}-j)g_{i}^{j}+\frac{\sqrt{q}}{2\sqrt{3}}\hat{G}_{i}\Big),

where the
\hat{G}_{i}
’s are independent standard Gaussian variables (and independent from the
g_{i}^{j}
’s).

A.2.2
Decomposition of Brownian motion (part 2)

Define

{{\bf H}_{i}}=(H_{i}^{j})_{j=1}^{q}
where

H_{i}^{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta W_{i}^{j}+\frac{1}{h^{\prime}}\int_{(i-1)h+(j-1)h^{\prime}}^{(i-1)h+jh^{\prime}}(W_{u}-W_{(i-1)h+(j-1)h^{\prime}})\textup{d}u.

The pair

(\Delta W_{i},\Delta I_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
given
(\delta{\bf W}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
({{\bf H}_{i}})_{i=1}^{N}
has the following deterministic representation

\Delta W_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\delta W_{i}^{j}\>\mbox{ and }\>\Delta I_{i}=h^{\prime}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\left(H_{i}^{j}+\left(q-j+\frac{1}{2}\right)\delta W_{i}^{j}\right).

We denote by
f_{i}^{j}
the normalized elementary “parabolic adjustment” defined by

f_{i}^{j}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{{h^{\prime}}^{1/2}}H_{i}^{j}.

We can notice that the normalized elementary increment
g_{i}^{j}=\delta W_{i}^{j}/{h^{\prime}}^{1/2}
and “parabolic adjustment”
f_{i}^{j}
are independent, both distributed with a standard normal distribution.

A.2.3
Conditioned piecewise parabolic Brownian motion (part 1)

We define

\mathcal{G}_{0}=\{g_{i}^{j},\>1\leq i\leq N,\>1\leq j\leq q\}.

The lower index runs through the grid with the coarse time step while the upper index is for the grid with the finer one. We use the notation

G_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}g_{i}^{j}.

Additionally, we also define

\hat{\mathcal{G}}=\{\hat{G}_{i},\>1\leq i\leq N\}
and
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{0}\cup\hat{\mathcal{G}}
. The two families
\mathcal{G}_{0}
and
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
are independent and they consist of i.i.d. standard normal variables.
For each
1\leq i\leq N
,
the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion (conditioned on
\mathcal{G}
) on
[(i-1)h,ih]
, denoted by
\hat{W}
, is the second-order polynomial that satisfies

\hat{W}_{(i-1)h}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}G_{r},\>\>\hat{W}_{ih}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{r=1}^{i}G_{r}

and

\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}\hat{W}_{u}\textup{d}u=(h^{\prime})^{3/2}\left(q\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}G_{r}+\frac{1}{2}G_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left(q-j\right)g_{i}^{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{q}{3}}\hat{G}_{i}\right).

It is uniquely defined by the three conditions above, which lead to the following formula

\forall u\in[(i-1)h,ih],\>\hat{W}_{u}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}G_{r}+(u/h-i+1)\hat{W}_{i-1,i}+6(u/h-i+1)(i-u/h)\hat{H}_{i-1,i}

(28)

where

\hat{W}_{i-1,i}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}G_{i}\>\mbox{ and }\>\hat{H}_{i-1,i}=\frac{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}{2}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+\frac{1-2j}{q}\right)g_{i}^{j}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3q}}\hat{G}_{i}\right].

(29)

Direct calculations show that the pair

(\hat{W}_{i-1,i},\hat{H}_{i-1,i})

and the pair

(W_{s,t},H_{s,t})
, with
s=(i-1)h
and
t=ih
, have the same law.

A.2.4
Conditioned piecewise parabolic Brownian motion (part 2)

We define

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{f_{i}^{j},\>1\leq i\leq N,\>1\leq j\leq p\}

and

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}_{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq N
,
the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion (conditioned on
\mathcal{H}
) on
[(i-1)h,ih]
, denoted by
\hat{W}_{u}^{C}
, is the second-order polynomial that satisfies

\hat{W}_{(i-1)h}^{C}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}G_{r},\>\>\hat{W}_{ih}^{C}=\sqrt{h^{\prime}}\sum_{r=1}^{i}G_{r}

and

\int_{(i-1)h}^{ih}\hat{W}_{u}^{C}\textup{d}u=(h^{\prime})^{3/2}\left(q\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}G_{r}+\frac{1}{2}G_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left(q-j\right)g_{i}^{j}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}f_{i}^{j}\right).

It is uniquely defined by the three conditions above, which lead to a similar formula as (
28

\hat{H}_{i-1,i}
is replaced by

\hat{H}_{i-1,i}^{C}
with

\hat{H}_{i-1,i}^{C}=\frac{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}{2}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+\frac{1-2j}{q}\right)g_{i}^{j}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}q}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}f_{i}^{j}\right].

Appendix B
Error minimization

This appendix contains the technical derivation of the error minimization strategy for the CV MC estimator. It includes the proof of Proposition
3
and an analysis that accounts for small multiplicative constants in pre-asymptotic regimes.

B.1
Proof of Proposition
3

The problem is to minimize

{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M}+\frac{h^{2\gamma}+{h^{\prime}}^{2\beta}}{M^{\prime}}
under the constraint
\frac{M}{h}+\frac{M^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}}={\cal C}
.
By substituting
M={\cal C}h-\frac{M^{\prime}h}{h^{\prime}}
into the expression of the function to be minimized, one obtains the unconstrained minimization problem: find the minimum of
{\cal H}:(h,h^{\prime},M^{\prime})\mapsto{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{h^{\prime}}{{\cal C}hh^{\prime}-M^{\prime}h}+\frac{h^{2\gamma}+{h^{\prime}}^{2\beta}}{M^{\prime}}
.
A critical point satisfies the three equations
\partial_{h}{\cal H}=\partial_{h^{\prime}}{\cal H}=\partial_{M^{\prime}}{\cal H}=0
. Solving this system of three equations gives the three values for
h,h^{\prime},M^{\prime}
.

Two remarks:

1) The critical point is a minimum that corresponds to an admissible value for
M^{\prime}\simeq{\cal C}^{\frac{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha-2\gamma}{4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha+1}}
(a large integer) because
4\alpha\gamma+2\alpha-2\gamma>0
(indeed, this is equivalent to
\alpha>\gamma/(2\gamma+1)
).

2) We observe that the term
{h^{\prime}}^{2\beta}
is negligible in front of
h^{2\gamma}
. Indeed the ratio of these two numbers is
h^{4\gamma\beta+2\beta-2\gamma}
which is small because
4\gamma\beta+2\beta-2\gamma>0
(indeed, this is equivalent to
\beta>\gamma/(2\gamma+1)
).

B.2
Error minimization when
\mathcal{C}
and
\epsilon
are fixed

We are interested in minimizing

{h^{\prime}}^{2\alpha}+\frac{1}{M}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}h^{2\gamma}+{h^{\prime}}^{2\beta}}{M^{\prime}}

with

h^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}^{-y},\>h=\mathcal{C}^{-x},\>M^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}^{1-y},\>M=\mathcal{C}^{1-x}.

Thus we consider the function

e(x,y)=\mathcal{C}^{-2\alpha y}+\mathcal{C}^{x-1}+\mathcal{C}^{y-1}(\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}^{-2\gamma x}+\mathcal{C}^{-2\beta y}).

(30)

At a critical point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
, we have

\frac{\partial e_{\epsilon}(x^{\star},y^{\star})}{\partial x}=0\iff\mathcal{C}^{x^{\star}-1}=2\gamma\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}^{y^{\star}-1-2\gamma x^{\star}}

and (with
\beta=1/2
)

\frac{\partial e_{\epsilon}(x^{\star},y^{\star})}{\partial y}=0\iff 2\alpha\mathcal{C}^{-2\alpha y^{\star}}=\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}^{y^{\star}-1-2\gamma x^{\star}}.

Taking the
\log
and dividing by
\log(\mathcal{C})
gives

\begin{pmatrix}1+2\gamma&-1\\
-2\gamma&1+2\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\star}\\
y^{\star}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\log(2\gamma\epsilon^{2})}{\log(\mathcal{C})}\\
\displaystyle 1+\frac{\log\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)}{\log(\mathcal{C})}\end{pmatrix}.

That is

x^{\star}=\left(1+\frac{\log((2\gamma)^{2\alpha+1}2\alpha)}{\log(\mathcal{C})}+\frac{\log(\epsilon^{4\alpha})}{\log(\mathcal{C})}\right)/(1+2\alpha+4\alpha\gamma)

and

y^{\star}=\left(1+2\gamma+\frac{\log((4\gamma\alpha)^{2\gamma}2\alpha)}{\log(\mathcal{C})}-\frac{\log(\epsilon^{2})}{\log(\mathcal{C})}\right)/(1+2\alpha+4\alpha\gamma).

Appendix C
Runge-Kutta-type solver for the parabolic scheme

This appendix introduces a RK-type solver tailored for ordinary differential equations driven by the parabolic approximation of Brownian motion. It demonstrates that the solver achieves strong convergence of order one, requiring only one evaluation of the drift function and four evaluations of the diffusion function per time step.

Let
P:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function (it is a second-order polynomial in the application). Let
b,\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be smooth functions.
In this appendix
z(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the solution to

\dot{z}(u)=hb(u)+h^{1/2}\sigma(z(u))P(u),\quad u>0,

(31)

starting from
z(0)=z_{0}
.

Lemma 7
.

Denote

\displaystyle I_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}P(u)\textup{d}u,\qquad I_{3}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u.

We have

z(1)=z_{0}+hB_{1}+S_{2}-S_{0}(1-h^{1/2}I_{1})+\frac{h^{1/2}}{6}(S_{3}-2S_{1}+S_{0})I_{1}+O(h^{2}),

(32)

where

S_{0}=\sigma(z_{0}),

B_{1}=b\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}S_{0}I_{3}\big),\quad S_{1}=\sigma\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}S_{0}I_{1}\big),

S_{2}=\sigma\big(z_{0}+hS_{0}I_{2}+h^{3/2}B_{1}I_{4}\big),\quad S_{3}=\sigma\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}S_{0}I_{1}+h^{1/2}S_{1}I_{1}\big).

This lemma shows that it is possible to compute an approximation of
z(1)
accurate up to a term of order
O(h^{2})
with four calls to
\sigma
and only one call to
b
.
When
\sigma
is constant, Eq. (
32
) reads:
z(1)=z_{0}+hb(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma I_{3})+h^{1/2}\sigma I_{1}+O(h^{2})
.

The strong order of convergence of the parabolic scheme using the solver presented in Lemma
7
at each step then follows from
[
13
, Theorem 11.5.1]
. This result is similar to the one stated in
[
4
, Theorem 3.17]
that addresses the parabolic scheme in the case where Eq. (
31
) can be integrated exactly.

Proof.

If
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is sufficiently smooth,
then for any
u\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle f(z(u))=

\displaystyle f(z_{0})+h^{1/2}(f^{\prime}\sigma)(z_{0})\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle+h\left((f^{\prime}b)(z_{0})u+[(f^{\prime}\sigma)^{\prime}\sigma](z_{0})\int_{0}^{u}\int_{0}^{u_{1}}P(u_{2})P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{2}\textup{d}u_{1}\right)+O(h^{3/2}),

where
f^{\prime}=(\partial f_{i}/\partial z_{j})_{ij}
stands for the Jacobian matrix of
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
Applying this result with
f=b
and
f=\sigma
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}b(z(u))\textup{d}u=b(z_{0})+h^{1/2}(b^{\prime}\sigma)(z_{0})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u+O(h)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(z(u))P(u)\textup{d}u=\sigma(z_{0})\int_{0}^{1}P(u)\textup{d}u+h^{1/2}(\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)(z_{0})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{1}P(u)\textup{d}u

\displaystyle+h\left((\sigma^{\prime}b)(z_{0})\int_{0}^{1}uP(u)\textup{d}u+[(\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)^{\prime}\sigma](z_{0})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}\int_{0}^{u_{1}}P(u_{2})P(u_{1})P(u)\textup{d}u_{2}\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u\right)+O(h^{3/2}).

Therefore, we have

z(1)=z_{0}+h^{1/2}\zeta_{1/2}+h\zeta_{1}+h^{3/2}\zeta_{3/2}+O(h^{2}),

(33)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sigma(z_{0})I_{1},

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=b(z_{0})+\sigma^{\prime}\sigma(z_{0})I_{2},

\displaystyle\zeta_{3/2}

\displaystyle=(b^{\prime}\sigma)(z_{0})I_{3}+(\sigma^{\prime}b)(z_{0})I_{4}+[(\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)^{\prime}\sigma](z_{0})I_{5},

and

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}P(u)\textup{d}u
,
I_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})P(u)\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u
,

I_{3}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}P(u_{1})\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u
,

I_{4}=\int_{0}^{1}uP(u)\textup{d}u
,

I_{5}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}\int_{0}^{u_{1}}P(u_{2})P(u_{1})P(u)\textup{d}u_{2}\textup{d}u_{1}\textup{d}u
.
Note that all these quantities depend only on
I_{1}
and
I_{4}
since
I_{2}=I_{1}^{2}/2
,
I_{3}=I_{1}-I_{4}
,
I_{5}=I_{1}^{3}/6
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle hb(z_{0})+h^{3/2}b^{\prime}\sigma(z_{0})I_{3}=hb\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{3}\big)+O(h^{2}),

\displaystyle h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{1}+h\sigma^{\prime}\sigma(z_{0})I_{2}+h^{3/2}\sigma^{\prime}b(z_{0})I_{4}

\displaystyle\quad=\sigma\big(z_{0}+h\sigma(z_{0})I_{2}+h^{3/2}b\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{3}\big)I_{4}\big)-\sigma(z_{0})\big(1-h^{1/2}I_{1}\big)+O(h^{2}),

(here we use
b\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{3}\big)
instead of
b(z_{0})
in the first term of the right-hand side in order to save one call to
b
),
and

\displaystyle h^{3/2}\left((\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)^{\prime}\sigma\right)(z_{0})I_{1}^{3}

\displaystyle=h^{1/2}\Big[\sigma\Big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{1}+h^{1/2}\sigma\big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{1}\big)I_{1}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-2\sigma\Big(z_{0}+h^{1/2}\sigma(z_{0})I_{1}\Big)+\sigma(z_{0})\Big]I_{1}+O(h^{2}).

Substituting into (
33
) gives the desired result.
∎
