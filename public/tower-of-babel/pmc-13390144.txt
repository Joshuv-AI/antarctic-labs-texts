Pharmacological and transcutaneous auricular vagal targeting of endoplasmic reticulum stress in the trigeminal ganglion alleviates migraine-like behaviors
Authors: Huan-Jun Lu, Meng-Xuan Huang, Yi-Fan Wang, Ning-Yi Ren, Chun-Yu Dong, Meng-Lu Liu, Xin-Yi Huang, Yong-Jing Gao, Hao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress has emerged as a key player in the development of diverse chronic pain disorders; however, its specific contribution to migraine pathophysiology has yet to be fully elucidated. Although accumulating evidence indicates that transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) mitigates chronic pain, the precise molecular mechanisms governing this effect remain poorly understood. This study investigates the functional contribution of ER stress and its downstream signaling mechanisms in a nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced mouse model of chronic migraine, and whether taVNS exerts analgesic effects by modulating ER stress within trigeminal ganglion (TG) neurons.MethodsChronic migraine was induced in mice via repeated intraperitoneal injection of NTG. Facial mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed using Von Frey filaments. Light-aversive and anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated through open field testing and light/dark box testing. mRNA and protein expression were analyzed using quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Western blot, respectively. The cellular localization of alpha7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) receptors in the TG was determined by immunofluorescence staining. Background

Migraine is a common neurological disorder with a global prevalence of approximately 14%, severely impacting patients’ quality of life and social functioning [1, 2]. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of moderate to severe throbbing headache, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia [3]. Although the pathogenesis of migraine remains incompletely understood, activation of the trigeminovascular system, neuroinflammation, and peripheral sensitization are considered key pathological mechanisms [4, 5]. Central to these pathological cascades, the trigeminal ganglion (TG) serves as the principal relay station where peripheral meningeal nociceptive inputs are integrated, amplified, and transmitted to central pain circuits. Aberrant plasticity and hyperexcitability of TG neurons contribute critically to the establishment and maintenance of peripheral sensitization, thereby establishing the TG as a pivotal node linking peripheral triggers to chronic migraine pathophysiology [6].

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress represents a series of adaptive responses activated when cells encounter internal or external stimuli that cause accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins within the ER lumen, collectively termed the unfolded protein response (UPR) [7, 8]. Accumulating evidence positions ER stress as a critical driver of chronic pain pathophysiology. In dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, sustained UPR activation not only enhances neuronal excitability via ion channel modulation but also amplifies neuroimmune interactions through induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby establishing a feedforward loop between ER dysfunction and nociceptive sensitization [9]. However, the involvement of ER stress in migraine pathology, particularly within the TG remains poorly characterized. Unlike DRG neurons, TG neurons exhibit intense secretory activity characterized by high-capacity CGRP synthesis, imposing substantial biosynthetic burden on the ER [10]. Given that the TG serves as the principal relay for craniovascular nociception and undergoes repeated activation by inflammatory mediators during migraine attacks, ER homeostasis in TG neurons may represent a critical, yet unexplored, determinant of trigeminovascular sensitization and migraine chronification.

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is an emerging neuromodulation therapy that has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in epilepsy, depression, and inflammatory disorders [11, 12]. Clinically, both invasive cervical VNS and noninvasive transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS) have demonstrated efficacy in aborting acute migraine attacks and reducing chronic attack frequency [13, 14]. Recent studies further indicate that taVNS attenuates trigeminovascular activation and central sensitization [15, 16]. However, whether taVNS exerts its antinociceptive effects in migraine through modulation of ER stress within TG neurons, and specifically whether it interrupts the feedforward cycle between ER dysfunction and inflammatory mediator release, remains entirely unclear.

In the present study, we found that nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced chronic migraine is accompanied by robust ER stress activation in TG neurons, which subsequently drives neuroinflammation and nociceptive sensitization through the PI3K-Akt signaling-mediated neuroinflammation. Furthermore, we found that taVNS alleviates these migraine-like behaviors by suppressing ER stress and downstream neuronal hyperexcitability via alpha7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR).

Materials and methods

Animal

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–7 weeks were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Nantong University. Animals were maintained under standardized housing conditions featuring a 12-hour light/dark cycle and thermostatically controlled ambient temperature (22 ± 1 °C), with free access to food and water. Experimental protocols received prior approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nantong University (approval no. S20250318-003) and were conducted in strict accordance with guidelines set forth by the International Association for the Study of Pain.

Migraine model

The migraine mouse model was established through intermittent intraperitoneal administration of NTG according to previously established protocols [17]. The NTG concentrate (1 mg/ml) was freshly reconstituted in 0.9% sterile saline immediately before each dosing session. Control animals received equivalent volumes of physiological saline. Experimental subjects underwent five consecutive NTG injections (10 mg/kg, i.p.) administered on alternate days over a 9-day observation period (days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9).

Facial mechanical hypersensitivity

Mice were individually placed in transparent observation chambers with wire mesh floors and acclimatized for 30–60 min until spontaneous grooming and exploratory behaviors subsided. A graded series of von Frey filaments with calibrated forces ranging from 0.02 g to 2.0 g (0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0, 2.0 g) was applied to the periorbital skin (V1 ophthalmic division territory of the trigeminal nerve). Tests were initiated with the 0.16 g von Frey filament as the starting stimulus. Each filament was applied perpendicularly to the skin for 2–3 s, with an inter-stimulus interval of ≥ 5 s. Positive responses were defined as vigorous head withdrawal or immediate scratching of the stimulated area with the ipsilateral forepaw. Mechanical thresholds were quantified using the Dixon up-and-down method. Baseline measurements were recorded prior to model induction, with subsequent tests performed on days 1, 5, and 9 following NTG injection.

Open field test

Open field test was performed according to methods described in previous studies [18, 19]. Mice were acclimatized to the behavioral testing suite for ≥ 30 min prior to evaluation to mitigate transport-induced stress. Each subject was gently restrained at the base of the tail and placed in a predetermined corner of the open field apparatus, facing the wall, to standardize the initial exploratory vector and minimize experimenter bias. Automated video tracking (EthoVision XT) was initiated immediately as the experimenter exited the testing area, marking the onset of the 5-min observation period. Inter-trial intervals included thorough sanitation of the arena walls and floor with 75% ethanol followed by complete air-drying and ventilation (≥ 2 min) to prevent odorant carryover between subjects.

Light-aversive test

The light/dark box apparatus comprised two compartments of equal floor area connected by a central doorway. To standardize diurnal conditions, testing was conducted during the light phase (09:00–15:00) across all subjects. Mice were acclimatized to the testing suite for ≥ 30 min prior to evaluation. Each subject was gently restrained at the base of the tail and placed in the center of the illuminated compartment, facing the doorway leading to the darkened compartment. Video acquisition commenced immediately upon the experimenter’s exit, initiating the 5-min observation period. Transitions between compartments and time spent in each zone were automatically quantified via video tracking software.

TG injection

Under isoflurane anesthesia (2% in oxygen), mice were secured in a lateral recumbent position with the maxilla immobilized. A microinjection needle (0.33 mm external diameter, 13 mm length) was introduced percutaneously into the infraorbital foramen, advanced retrogradely through the infraorbital canal and foramen rotundum, until the bevel was localized within the TG. Infusions of tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) (0.5–2 µg) were performed using a microsyringe pump. Methyllycaconitine (MLA) at a dose of 2 µg was administered via infraorbital foramen injection in mice. Facial nociceptive behaviors were evaluated after unilateral microinjection into the TG.

RNA-seq

TG tissues were collected 6 h after infraorbital foramen injection of TUDCA or Vehicle (n = 3/group). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), treated with DNase I (Takara) to remove genomic DNA contamination, and assessed using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN > 8.0) and NanoDrop 2000. Libraries were constructed from 1.5 µg RNA using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina): mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) beads, fragmented, reverse-transcribed into double-stranded cDNA, end-repaired, adapter-ligated, purified with AMPure XP beads (200–250 bp), and PCR-amplified (12 cycles). After quality validation, libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp paired-end, Allwegene). Raw data were converted using bcl2fastq and processed with FastQC to remove adapters and low-quality bases (Q < 20). Clean reads were aligned to the GRCm39 genome using Hisat2, quantified by featureCounts and StringTie, and normalized to FPKM. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma (|log2FC|>1, FDR < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment was analyzed using KOBAS v3.0 (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.05).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with precipitation by isopropanol and washing with 75% ethanol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Genomic DNA was eliminated from 1 µg of total RNA using DNase I, followed by reverse transcription with GoScript™ Reverse Transcriptase (Promega). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a Rotor-Gene 6000 system using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) in a 10 µL reaction volume (initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 45 s). Specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Relative expression levels of target genes were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated using the 2^(–ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

TGs were swiftly excised and mechanically dissociated in ice-cold lysis buffer (Sigma‒Aldrich) fortified with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails. Protein yields were quantified via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to electrophoretic separation. Post-transfer, membranes underwent blocking with 5% non-fat milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting: activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) (1:600), PERK (1:1000), phosphorylated RNA-dependent protein kinase R-like ER kinase (p-PERK) (1:1000), immunoglobulin heavy chain-binding protein (GRP78) (1:800), X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) (1:400), p-AKT (1:1000), AKT (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:10000) — all procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology except where noted. Subsequent to stringent washing, blots were probed with IRDye 800CW-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit, 1:1000; LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE) for 2 h at ambient temperature. Immunoreactive bands were captured using the Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences) and semiquantitatively analyzed by densitometry (ImageJ, NIH) relative to predicted molecular weight markers.

Immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemical procedures adhered to our established protocols [20, 21]. Briefly, TGs were harvested from deeply anesthetized mice and immediately preserved via immersion in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (0.1 M PBS buffer, pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C. Following fixation, tissues underwent cryoprotection through a graded sucrose series (20%, and 30%), then were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound and snap-frozen. Cryosections (20 μm) were permeabilized and blocked (1% BSA, 0.01% Triton X-100 in PBS, 30 min), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting α7nAChR, IBA1, FABP7, TUJ1, and p-AKT. After triple 10-min rinses with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, sections were probed with Cy3- or FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies. Fluorescence micrographs were acquired on a Leica SP8 confocal system and processed using ImageJ.

Primary TG neuronal culture

Following deep anesthesia with 2% isoflurane inhalation, TGs were micro-dissected under sterile, ice-cold conditions and subjected to three sequential washes in ice-cold Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free PBS to eliminate residual meninges, vasculature, and connective tissue. The purified ganglia were minced and incubated in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution at 37 °C for 20–25 min (water bath) to facilitate enzymatic dissociation. Proteolysis was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS and DNase I. The partially digested tissue was mechanically dissociated by gentle pipetting through fire-polished Pasteur pipettes of decreasing bore diameter until a unicellular suspension was achieved. Cellular debris was removed by passing the suspension through a sterile 70 μm nylon mesh filter. Cells were harvested by low-speed centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in complete neurobasal medium. Viable neurons were plated at 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL onto PLL-precoated 35 mm dishes or coverslips and cultivated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere. Cultures exhibiting strong substrate adhesion were utilized for electrophysiological recording.

Action potential recording in TG neurons

The method for the action potential recording followed our previous descriptions [22, 23]. Whole-cell current-clamp recordings were performed 24–48 h post-plating, targeting small-diameter TG neurons at ambient temperature (20–22 °C) The extracellular solution contained (in mM): 125 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1.2 MgCl₂, 2.4 CaCl₂, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, 10 Glucose, 26 NaHCO₃ and 5 HEPES (pH 7.4). Patch pipettes (resistance 3–5 MΩ) were filled with (in mM): 121 Potassium Gluconate, 20 KCl, 0.2 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 0.4 GTP-Tris salt, 2 MgCl₂ and 4 Na₂ATP (pH 7.4). Membrane potentials were recorded using an EPC-10 amplifier (HEKA Elektronik, Germany) controlled by PatchMaster/Pulse v8.80 software. Action potential discharge was evoked by incremental depolarizing current injections (1 s duration, 20 pA steps). Signals were low-pass filtered at 5 kHz, digitized at 20 kHz, and analyzed offline.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation

After the mice were deeply anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, ear-clip electrodes were connected to the output of the stimulator and gently clipped onto the cymba conchae of the mouse ears, ensuring good contact between the electrodes and the skin. Once connected, the stimulator was turned on to deliver transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve electrical stimulation according to the preset parameters as follows: an output current of 0.2 mA, a pulse width of 0.2 ms, a frequency of 15, 20–25 Hz, and continuous stimulation for 10 min. For sham stimulation, mice were anesthetized and handled identically to the active taVNS group, with the same duration of anesthesia and identical electrode placement and fixation over the cymba concha target region. The stimulator was operated using the same programmed parameters and stimulation duration; however, the output current intensity was set to 0 mA, resulting in no effective electrical output. Apart from the absence of current delivery, all other intervention conditions (including electrode type, fixation method, and environmental handling) were kept strictly identical between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with n denoting biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed Student’s t-tests for pairwise comparisons, or by one-way/two-way repeated-measures ANOVA for multiple group analyses, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or Bonferroni’s post hoc correction where appropriate. Calculations were performed in GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Repeated NTG injection induces migraine-like behaviors and activates ER stress in the TG

Consistent with previous studies [17, 24], a chronic migraine mouse model was established via repeated intraperitoneal injection of NTG. Behavioral assessments revealed that by 5 times of consecutive NTG administration, mice developed significant facial mechanical allodynia (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, in the open field test, NTG-treated mice exhibited markedly fewer entries into and less time spent in the central zone (anxiety-like behavior) (Fig. 1B-C), with locomotor activity remaining intact (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In the light-dark box test, these mice showed reduced time spent and distance traveled in the light chamber (photophobia phenotype) (Fig. 1D-E), collectively indicating successful establishment of a chronic migraine model.

Given the emerging role of ER stress in chronic pain, we examined the expression changes of key ER stress initiators in the TG. Western blot analysis showed that the NTG group exhibited significantly upregulated expression levels of ATF6, GRP78, XBP-1 and p-PERK in the TGs (Fig. 1F-I). These results indicate that the NTG-induced migraine model is accompanied by significant activation of ER stress in TG tissue.

ER stress in the TG functionally contributes to migraine pain modulation

To establish the causal role of TG ER stress in migraine, we employed the ER stress inhibitor TUDCA for intervention. TUDCA is a clinically validated ER stress modulator that restores ER homeostasis by UPR [25]. We administered low (0.5 µg), medium (1 µg), and high (2 µg) doses of TUDCA via intra-TG microinjection (Fig. 2A). The results showed that medium and high doses significantly alleviated facial mechanical allodynia, whereas the low dose showed no significant effect, indicating a dose-dependent analgesic response (Fig. 2B-D).

We then selected the optimal medium dose for subsequent mechanistic studies. Behavioral analysis revealed that TUDCA treatment significantly ameliorated anxiety-like behaviors and photophobia phenotypes in NTG-treated mice (Fig. 2E-H), without affecting locomotor activity (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Western blot confirmed that TUDCA significantly downregulated the expression of ATF6, GRP78, XBP-1 and p-PERK in the TG (Fig. 2I-L). These findings confirm that ER stress in the TG functionally contributes to the development and maintenance of NTG-induced migraine.

ER stress promotes migraine by enhancing excitability of TG neurons

Neuronal hyperexcitability represents the core mechanism of peripheral sensitization in migraine [26]. To investigate whether ER stress directly regulates the electrophysiological properties of TG neurons, we employed whole-cell patch-clamp recording to examine small diameter neurons (< 30 μm, corresponding to the nociceptor population). Compared to the control group, neurons from the NTG model group exhibited significantly enhanced excitability, as indicated by a significantly decreased rheobase (Fig. 3A-B) and increased action potential firing frequency (Fig. 3C-D), suggesting that NTG induces alterations in neuronal membrane properties leading to reduced firing thresholds. However, injection of TUDCA significantly reversed these abnormalities. Neurons in the TUDCA-treated group showed normalized rheobase and restored action potential firing frequency to baseline levels (Fig. 3A-D). The other parameters such as resting membrane potential (Fig. 3E), amplitude (Fig. 3F), maximal rising slope (Fig. 3G) and maximal decaying slope (Fig. 3H) remained intact between two groups. These electrophysiological findings directly link ER stress to neuronal plasticity changes, providing a novel perspective on the molecular basis of peripheral sensitization in migraine.

ER stress regulates migraine through activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling axis and pro-inflammatory chemokine networks

To systematically dissect the downstream molecular network by which ER stress regulates migraine, we performed transcriptomic sequencing (RNA-seq) on TG tissue following TUDCA treatment. Three-dimensional PCA confirmed distinct transcriptional separation between the two groups (Fig. 4A), thus supporting the robustness of the dataset. Compared to the Vehicle group, the TUDCA group yielded 497 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 19 upregulated and 478 downregulated genes (|log2 fold change| ≥ 1, adjusted P ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 4B-C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated genes were significantly enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, chemokine signaling pathways, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 4D), suggesting that ER stress drives migraine pathology through immune-metabolic coupling mechanisms. Heatmap analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles among groups, with TUDCA-treated TGs showing significant downregulation of inflammation-associated genes, such as chemokine C-C motif ligand 6 (Ccl6), chemokine C-C motif ligand 8 (Ccl8), chemokine C-C motif ligand 11 (Ccl11) and interleukin-16 (Il-16) (Fig. 4E). These candidates were prioritized for qPCR validation based on their established roles in pain pathophysiology. CCL6, CCL8, and CCL11 are CC chemokines known to be upregulated in sensory ganglia and spinal cord under neuroinflammatory pain states, where they drive microglial activation and nociceptor sensitization [27]; specifically, CCL8 and CCL11 have been implicated in neuropathic pain with elevated expression in dorsal root ganglia and cerebrospinal fluid [28]. IL-16, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, has been associated with trigeminal inflammatory pathology and identified as a risk factor for trigeminal neuralgia in genetic epidemiological studies [29]. qPCR validation demonstrated that chronic injection of NTG caused upregulation of mRNA levels of these pro-inflammatory mediators in the TG (Fig. 4F-I). However, TUDCA significantly downregulated above genes (Fig. 4J-M).

Next, we investigated whether the PI3K-Akt pathway serves as a downstream signaling mechanism for NTG-induced ER stress in TG neurons. Western blot analysis demonstrated a 1.28-fold elevation in phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) levels in TG tissues from NTG-injected mice compared to controls (Fig. 5A); however, p-AKT activation was significantly suppressed following TUDCA treatment (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that NTG elevated the proportion of p-AKT+ neurons in the TG; however, TUDCA treatment significantly attenuated this increase (Fig. 5C-D). Furthermore, pretreatment with the PI3K-Akt inhibitor MK-2206 attenuated NTG-induced upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators in the TG (Fig. 5E-H). These results indicate that ER stress constructs a TG neuron-immune microenvironment interaction network by concurrently activating the PI3K-Akt survival signaling axis and pro-inflammatory chemokine cascades, collectively promoting migraine chronification.

taVNS alleviates migraine-like behaviors and suppresses ER stress in the TG

Based on the therapeutic potential of VNS in clinical migraine prophylaxis, we hypothesized that taVNS might exert analgesic effects by modulating TG ER stress. Beginning on day 5 of NTG model establishment, mice received continuous taVNS intervention for 5 days (Fig. 6A). We first optimized stimulation parameters: comparing analgesic effects at pulse frequencies of 15 Hz, 20 Hz, and 25 Hz, we found that 20 Hz produced the most significant relief of facial mechanical allodynia) (Fig. 6B-D).

We next applied the optimized 20 Hz parameter for mechanistic studies. taVNS significantly alleviated anxiety-like behaviors in NTG mice (Fig. 6E-F) and reduced photophobic responses (Fig. 6G-H). In addition, locomotor activity remained intact, as the total distance traveled in the open-field test was unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Molecular-level detection revealed that taVNS significantly downregulated the expression of ER stress marker proteins ATF6, GRP78, XBP-1, and p-PERK in the TG (Fig. 6I-L), while concurrently reducing mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory factors including Ccl6, Ccl8, Ccl11 and Il-16 (Fig. 6M–P). These results suggest that taVNS may alleviate migraine by restoring TG ER homeostasis.

taVNS suppresses ER stress through an α7nAChR-mediated TG neuronal mechanism

The α7nAChR serves as a core receptor mediating central cholinergic signaling, is highly expressed in sensory ganglion neurons, and regulates neuronal excitability and synaptic plasticity [30, 31]. Given that acetylcholine (ACh) release from vagal efferent fibers constitutes the primary neurotransmitter basis of taVNS effects, we hypothesized that α7nAChR represents the key molecular mediator through which taVNS targets TG ER stress. To test this hypothesis, we administered intraganglionic microinjection of the α7nAChR-selective antagonist MLA (2 µg) prior to taVNS (Fig. 7A). Results showed that MLA completely blocked the analgesic effect of taVNS (Fig. 7B), indicating that α7nAChR activation is a necessary condition for taVNS-mediated migraine relief. Cellular localization analysis revealed that α7nAChR showed high colocalization with the neuronal marker TUJ1 (82.2%), but significantly lower colocalization with IBA1 (12.4%) and FABP7 (28.9%), indicating that α7nAChR is predominantly expressed in TG primary sensory neurons rather than glial cells (Fig. 7C). We then investigated the effect of MLA pretreatment on ER stress in the TG. MLA pretreatment not only blocked the analgesic effect of taVNS but also significantly attenuated the suppressive effect of taVNS on TG ER stress marker expression level (Fig. 7D-F).

Next, we examined the effects of taVNS and α7nAChR blockade on TG neuronal excitability. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings revealed that taVNS markedly attenuated NTG-induced hyperexcitability in TG neurons, evidenced by an elevated rheobase current (Fig. 7G and H) and a significant reduction in action potential firing frequency (Fig. 7I and J). However, administration of MLA substantially reversed the therapeutic effects of taVNS. Neurons in the MLA-treated group exhibited a decreased rheobase and restored firing rates and resting membrane potential to higher levels compared to those receiving taVNS alone (Fig. 7G-K). The other parameters such as amplitude (Fig. 7L), maximal decaying slope (Fig. 7M) and maximal rising slope (Fig. 7N) remained intact among groups. These results confirm that α7nAChR activation is a necessary component for taVNS to inhibit the ER stress signaling axis and neuronal hyperexcitability in TG neurons.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the pivotal role of ER stress in a NTG-induced migraine model and its downstream molecular mechanisms, and revealed a mechanism whereby taVNS exerts analgesic effects through α7nAChR-mediated regulation of ER stress (Fig. 8). The key findings of this study are: (1) NTG-induced chronic migraine is accompanied by significant activation of ER stress in TG neurons, and inhibition of ER stress effectively alleviates migraine-like behaviors; (2) ER stress promotes neuroinflammation and neuronal hyperexcitability through the PI3K-Akt signaling axis; (3) taVNS alleviates migraine by suppressing ER stress and neuronal hyperexcitability in TG neurons through activation of α7nAChR. These findings provide novel insights into the pathological mechanisms of migraine and offer a mechanistic basis for ER-targeted intervention strategies.

ER stress as a critical driver in migraine pathogenesis

The ER serves as the primary site for protein synthesis, folding, and quality control in eukaryotic cells. Under pathological conditions, the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins triggers the UPR, a conserved adaptive mechanism mediated by three primary sensors: PERK, IRE1, and ATF6 [32, 33]. While acute UPR activation represents a protective response, chronic or excessive ER stress leads to cellular dysfunction and apoptosis [34, 35]. Our study demonstrates that repeated NTG administration induces robust activation of multiple ER stress markers, including ATF6, GRP78, XBP1, and p-PERK, in TG neurons. This finding aligns with emerging evidence positioning ER stress as a common denominator in chronic pain disorders.

Notably, TG neurons exhibit unique secretory characteristics distinct from DRG neurons. The trigeminal system is characterized by high-capacity synthesis and release of CGRP, a neuropeptide critically involved in migraine pathophysiology [36]. The intensive secretory activity of TG neurons imposes substantial biosynthetic burden on the ER, rendering these cells particularly vulnerable to ER stress [25]. Our observation that NTG-induced migraine is accompanied by significant ER stress activation suggests that the trigeminovascular system may represent a tissue-specific context where ER homeostasis is critical for normal physiological function. Disruption of this homeostasis may constitute a key pathological event in migraine chronification. The functional significance of ER stress in migraine was confirmed through pharmacological intervention using TUDCA, a clinically validated ER stress inhibitor. TUDCA administration not only suppressed ER stress markers but also produced comprehensive symptomatic relief, including alleviation of facial mechanical allodynia, anxiety-like behaviors, and photophobia. These findings establish a causal relationship between ER stress and migraine-like phenotypes, positioning ER stress as a potential therapeutic target for migraine intervention.

Importantly, ER stress directly contributes to neuronal hyperexcitability, the electrophysiological hallmark of peripheral sensitization in migraine. Our patch-clamp recordings revealed that NTG-treated TG neurons exhibited significantly enhanced excitability, characterized by decreased rheobase and increased action potential firing frequency. TUDCA treatment normalized these electrophysiological abnormalities, establishing a direct functional link between ER stress and neuronal plasticity. The mechanisms by which ER stress modulates neuronal excitability are likely multifaceted. ER stress can alter the expression and function of ion channels, including voltage-gated sodium channels, potassium channels, and TRP channels, through UPR-mediated transcriptional and translational reprogramming [9]. Additionally, ER stress-induced inflammatory mediators may further enhance neuronal excitability through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms. These findings demonstrate that ER stress serves as an upstream driver of both neuroinflammation and neuronal hyperexcitability in the TG, establishing a mechanistic foundation for migraine chronification.

ER stress-driven neuroinflammation via the PI3K-Akt axis

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that TUDCA treatment significantly downregulated multiple inflammation-associated genes, with CCL6, CCL8, CCL11, and IL-16 emerging as key targets. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway as one of the most significantly affected pathways, suggesting that ER stress drives migraine pathology through immune-metabolic coupling mechanisms. Our findings demonstrate that ER stress concurrently activates the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and pro-inflammatory chemokine networks in TG neurons.

The PI3K-Akt pathway is traditionally associated with cell survival and metabolic regulation [37], however, growing evidences suggest its involvement in nociceptive sensitization through modulation of ion channel activity and inflammatory responses [31, 32]. Critically, our experimental results establish a clear hierarchical signaling cascade: ER stress activates the PI3K-Akt axis, which in turn drives the upregulation of downstream pro-inflammatory mediators. Western blot analysis demonstrated a 1.28-fold elevation in p-AKT levels in TG tissues from NTG-injected mice, and this activation was significantly suppressed following TUDCA treatment. More importantly, pharmacological inhibition of PI3K-Akt signaling by MK-2206 significantly attenuated NTG-induced upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators. These results confirm that the PI3K-Akt pathway serves as a critical signaling node linking ER stress to neuroinflammation, with inflammatory cytokines and chemokines functioning as downstream effectors of this axis. The coordinated activation of PI3K-Akt and pro-inflammatory mediator networks suggests that ER stress constructs a complex neuron-immune microenvironment interaction network that promotes peripheral sensitization and migraine chronification. Collectively, our findings support a working model in which ER stress in TG neurons activates the PI3K-Akt signaling axis, which subsequently drives the production of pro-inflammatory mediators. These inflammatory factors, in turn, enhance neuronal excitability and promote peripheral sensitization, ultimately leading to migraine-like behaviors.

taVNS targets ER stress through α7nAChR activation

VNS has emerged as a promising neuromodulation therapy for migraine, with both invasive and non-invasive approaches demonstrating clinical efficacy. However, the peripheral mechanisms underlying VNS-mediated analgesia have remained incompletely understood. Our study reveals that taVNS alleviates migraine-like behaviors by suppressing ER stress in TG neurons, providing a novel mechanistic explanation for its therapeutic effects.

The cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, mediated primarily through α7nAChR, has been extensively characterized as a key mechanism by which vagal signaling modulates immune responses [38, 39]. Previous studies have demonstrated that VNS attenuates systemic inflammation through α7nAChR-dependent mechanisms in various disease models, including endotoxemia [40], sepsis [41], and inflammatory bowel disease [42]. In the nervous system, α7nAChR activation has been shown to suppress neuroinflammation and protect neurons against ischemic injury [43]. Our findings demonstrate that α7nAChR is predominantly expressed in TG neurons and that pharmacological blockade of α7nAChR with MLA completely abolishes the analgesic and ER stress-suppressive effects of taVNS. These results establish α7nAChR activation as a necessary component for VNS-mediated regulation of TG ER stress. The observation that taVNS suppresses both ER stress markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines suggests that VNS may interrupt the feedforward cycle between ER dysfunction and inflammatory mediator release in TG neurons. This mechanism aligns with the known immunomodulatory properties of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and extends its relevance to organellar stress responses in sensory neurons. The ability of VNS to restore ER homeostasis through α7nAChR activation represents a novel therapeutic strategy that targets upstream cellular stress mechanisms rather than merely symptomatic relief.

Our study has some limitations. First, while the NTG-induced migraine model recapitulates key features of chronic migraine, including cutaneous allodynia and central sensitization, it does not fully capture the complex pathophysiology of human migraine. In addition, our study focused primarily on the TG as the site of ER stress-mediated pathology. While the TG represents a critical node in migraine pathophysiology, ER stress may also occur in other relevant tissues, including the brainstem, thalamus, and meninges. The relative contribution of ER stress in different anatomical locations to migraine symptoms warrants further investigation. migraine symptoms warrants further investigation. Third, this study was conducted exclusively in male mice. Given the strong female predominance in migraine prevalence and emerging evidence for sex-specific differences in migraine pathophysiology, the generalizability of our findings to female subjects remains limited and requires validation in sex-balanced cohorts. Future studies should validate these observations in cortical spreading depression and genetic migraine models, and further explore whether pharmacological or molecular chaperone-mediated ER stress modulation translates to clinical efficacy in patient cohorts with distinct migraine endophenotypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals the critical role of ER stress in an NTG-induced migraine model and establishes the ER stress-PI3K-Akt-neuroinflammation signaling axis as a novel framework for migraine pathogenesis. Our findings demonstrate that ER stress activates the PI3K-Akt pathway, which subsequently drives the upregulation of downstream pro-inflammatory mediators, ultimately leading to neuronal hyperexcitability and pain sensitization. Furthermore, we elucidate the mechanism by which taVNS exerts analgesic effects through α7nAChR-mediated regulation of ER stress. These findings provide theoretical rationale and experimental support for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting ER stress and neuro-immune interactions in migraine.
