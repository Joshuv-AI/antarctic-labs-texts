High-performance supercapacitors from composites derived from recycled alkaline batteries
Authors: Thuy Trang T. Vuong, Vu Van Thang, Thi Nhan Tran, Nguyen Minh Hieu, Phi Long Nguyen, Thi Viet Bac Phung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, carbon–manganese dioxide (MnO2) composites were synthesized using carbon recovered from spent alkaline batteries through a simple wet-chemical process conducted at room temperature. The resulting materials were thoroughly characterized using XRD, Raman spectroscopy, FTIR, SEM, and electrochemical techniques. The composites exhibited a well-defined structure and outstanding electrochemical performance. Specifically, the 0.01 M MnO2 composite delivered a high specific capacitance of 215.46 F g− 1 at a current density of 1 A g− 1 in 0.25 M NaCl electrolyte and retained 91.3% of its capacitance after 2000 charge-discharge cycles - significantly outperforming pristine carbon (66.23% retention). Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations confirmed that charge transfer from carbon to MnO2 enhanced the composite’s electrical conductivity and contributed to lattice stability. This research not only demonstrates a promising approach to recycling spent batteries but also offers a cost-effective and eco-friendly strategy for developing high-performance supercapacitor electrode materials.

Introduction

The rapid increase in battery usage for electronics, electric vehicles, and renewable energy systems has raised environmental concerns, as improper disposal leads to soil and water contamination by heavy metals1–3. Pollution can occur in various forms and persist for long durations, causing ecological and health impacts1,4,5, including climate change, ozone depletion, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion6,7. Limited natural resources have prompted the development of energy storage technologies as alternatives to non-renewable sources8, with sustainable, high-efficiency systems gaining attention9–11. Among these, batteries and supercapacitors are the most favorable energy storage devices12,13.

Batteries are indispensable in modern life, from electronics to renewable energy applications14. However, their end-of-life disposal can release heavy metals into the environment15,16. Recycling is therefore critical, both to reduce environmental impact and to recover valuable metals such as lithium, cobalt, zinc, and manganese12. Battery recycling has gained significant attention, particularly for lithium-ion and zinc–carbon batteries, with approximately 3,800 and 650 publications respectively from 2015 to 2025 (Scopus and Web of Science). These studies primarily focus on recovering valuable metals like lithium, cobalt, zinc, and manganese for reuse in energy storage or catalysis. In contrast, the reuse of carbon from alkaline batteries, especially for high-performance supercapacitor electrodes, remains significantly underexplored, with fewer than 25 publications addressing this application. This study addresses this research gap by investigating recycled carbon from alkaline batteries as a sustainable electrode material for advanced supercapacitors, thereby supporting the circular economy17 and environmental sustainability.

Supercapacitors (SCs) represent an emerging energy storage technology that is essential in advancing efficient and green energy solutions18,19. They are highly favored due to their outstanding advantages, including versatility as energy storage devices, high specific power, substantial energy density, extended lifespan, superior operational efficiency, environmental sustainability, robust safety, and rapid charge/discharge capabilities8,20,21. Unlike batteries, which store energy through chemical reactions, supercapacitors utilize electrostatic charge accumulation on electrode surfaces as their primary energy storage mechanism. This allows them to achieve high power density, rendering them perfect for applications demanding immediate power, such as engine starting, stabilizing renewable energy sources, or supporting mobile electronic devices22,23. Amid the increasing demand for clean and efficient energy technologies, supercapacitors have emerged as a promising subject of research and practical application24–26. With the development of new materials like graphene, nanomaterials, and advanced manufacturing techniques, supercapacitors are gradually being improved in both energy density and overall performance, opening potential opportunities for their use.

Carbon-based materials are widely used in supercapacitors thanks to their large surface area, excellent conductivity, chemical stability, porosity, abundance, and structural versatility8,21,27. They also offer corrosion resistance, light weight, durability, and cost-effectiveness, making them highly suitable for energy storage28. Different forms of carbon can enhance energy density, power output, and cycle life21,29. Their porosity and morphology can be tuned through activation, improving ion adsorption and diffusion during charge/discharge21,28. Recycled carbon from batteries - such as graphitic or black carbon—has attracted interest as a sustainable electrode material, since treatments can increase surface area and electrochemical stability30,31. This approach reduces pollution while maintaining strong energy-storage performance32,33. In this study, carbon extracted from used alkaline batteries-one of the most common waste sources-was employed for supercapacitor electrodes.

Transition-metal oxides (Ni, Mn, Co, Fe) and their composites with carbon have also shown great promise for pseudocapacitor electrodes due to their high electrochemical activity and cost-effectiveness34,35. When combined with carbon materials such as graphene, activated carbon, or carbon nanotubes, these composites leverage the advantages of both: metal oxides provide high electrochemical activity, while carbon enhances surface coverage, charge transport ability, and mechanical strength34–37. Among them, MnO2 is particularly attractive because of its high charge-storage capacity, low cost, abundance, and environmental sustainability38,39. Combining electric double-layer capacitance with Faradaic pseudocapacitance, MnO2 delivers enhanced capacitance, cycling stability, and energy density, making it a leading candidate for SC electrodes38,40. Furthermore, when integrated with carbon materials, significantly contributes to enhancing the overall efficiency of supercapacitor electrodes38,39.

Here, we synthesized MnO2@Carbon nanoparticle composites using a wet chemical method with a KMnO4 solution and carbon derived from thoroughly washed used alkaline batteries. Various characterization techniques were employed to analyze the structure, morphology, and electrochemical properties of these recycled materials, providing crucial insights into enhancing battery recycling efficiency.

Experiments

Materials

Post-consumer alkaline batteries are purchased from the supermarket. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 99%) and carbon black were acquired from Mackalin. N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP) and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) were secured through procurement from China. KMnO4, HCl (37%), and NaCl were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. The carbon paper substrate was supplied by a Taiwanese company.

Characterization of materials

The morphology and structure of samples were analyzed using various characterization techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy (Horiba), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8000 and JEOL JSM-IT800).

Preparation of carbon and C@MnO2

Fig. 1Process for recovering carbon from alkaline batteries and synthesizing C@MnO2.

Process for recovering carbon from alkaline batteries and synthesizing C@MnO2.

To ensure safe handling of spent AA alkaline batteries, disassembly was performed in a fume hood to minimize exposure to residual electrolyte salts. The components were rinsed with deionized water to remove soluble salts, followed by ultrasonic treatment in acetone and ethanol, and acid washing with HCl to eliminate impurities, including potential traces of heavy metals. This process ensured both safety and the purity of the recovered carbon (Fig. 1).

The carbon powder was synthesized by rapid ultrasonic agitation. The solution consists of 5 g of carbon particles extracted from used alkaline batteries, which are mixed with 500 mL of a solution containing acetone and ethanol and sonicated for 1 h. The acetone/ethanol mixture was selected to efficiently dissolve and remove residual electrolytes and organic binders, while the subsequent acid treatment helped eliminate metallic impurities and introduce surface functional groups. Repeated washing and filtration were performed to minimize possible residual contaminants from the battery waste. Afterward, 50 mL of HCl solution is added and the mixture is sonicated for another hour. Finally, the solution is filtered, washed multiple times, and dried for 24 h to obtain carbon nanoparticles. Approximately 2 g of carbon was recovered per AA battery (10 wt%). Reproducibility was verified across three batches (10 batteries each), with consistent XRD (Fig. 2a) and SEM (Fig. 3) results, and specific capacitance within ± 5%.

The C@MnO2 composite was synthesized through simple steps at room temperature. First, a mixture of 100 mg of carbon nanoparticles extracted from used alkaline batteries is washed and placed in 100 mL of ethanol, then sonicated for 1 h. Next, 100 mL of KMnO4 solution is added at concentrations of 0.0005 M, 0.001 M, 0.005 M, 0.01 M, 0.05 M and 0.1 M, and the mixture is stirred on a magnetic stirrer at 500 rpm. Finally, the mixture was filtered, washed multiple times, and dried in a fume hood at 120 °C to obtain C@MnO2 materials with different concentrations, named sequentially from CM1 to CM6.

Electrochemical studies

The electrode fabrication followed a straightforward protocol, incorporating the sample (80 wt%), carbon black (10 wt%), and PVDF (10 wt%) in 2 mL NMP solvent, with continuous stirring to promote uniform dispersion. Subsequently, a 20 µL of slurry was deposited onto a carbon paper substrate (1 cm²) and vacuum-dried at 80 °C. Each of the fabricated graphene electrodes had a final mass of 1 mg.

Electrochemical characterization was performed using a standard three-electrode configuration in 0.25 M NaCl aqueous solution, which was chosen for its adequate ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability, and compatibility with the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The prepared electrode served as the working electrode, with a platinum foil as the counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference.

For symmetric cell assembly, two identical electrodes were assembled face-to-face with a solid electrolyte and a porous insulating separator sandwiched between them. This configuration enabled evaluation of the electrode material’s performance under practical two-electrode conditions.

The charge-storage capability of the samples was investigated via cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) measurements, conducted on a CS 310 electrochemical workstation (Corrtest, China).

Results and discussion

Structural and morphological attributes

The structural and compositional properties of the samples were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy, with results presented in Fig. 2. In Fig. 2a, the CM0 sample displays a strong diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 26°, corresponding to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon, indicating partial structural ordering in the material33,41. Additional peaks at 2θ ≈ 41° and 55°, assigned to the (100) and (004) planes of carbon (ICDD PDF#41–1487), further suggest medium-range ordering of carbon layers, though the presence of structural defects cannot be excluded42–44. The observed peak characteristics indicate that the material retains a degree of graphitic ordering but also contains defects, which can influence its electrochemical properties. When MnO2 is introduced into carbon to form the composites (CM1 - CM6), the XRD patterns exhibit significant changes. Specifically, the intensity of the graphitic peak at 2θ ≈ 26° gradually decreases from CM1 to CM6, indicating that the MnO2 coating progressively covers the carbon surface, thereby reducing its crystallinity. Simultaneously, new diffraction peaks emerge at 2θ ≈ 21°, 37°, and 65°, which are characteristic of γ-MnO2 phases (JCPDS14–0644). The intensity of these MnO2-related peaks increases with higher MnO2 content, confirming the successful deposition of MnO2 onto the carbon matrix45. Notably, in the CM6 sample, the MnO2 peaks become completely dominant, while the graphitic peak at 2θ ≈ 26° nearly disappears. This suggests the layer is thick enough to completely obscure the underlying carbon signal, indicating a full coating of the carbon surface.

The Raman spectra provide further insights into the carbon and MnO2 characteristics (Fig. 2b). For CM0, two typical peaks appear at ~ 1350 cm− 1 (D-band, defects/disorder) and ~ 1600 cm− 1 (G-band, C = C stretching in graphitic domains)32,33. With MnO2 introduction (CM1–CM6), an additional peak emerges at ~ 600 cm− 1 (Mn–O vibration), which becomes more intense as MnO2 content increases, confirming successful deposition onto the carbon framework46,47. The I(D) / I(G) ratio, reflecting structural disorder, varies significantly across samples. It increases from 0.22 (CM0) to 0.84 (CM1), decreases sharply in CM2 (0.13), then rises moderately in CM3 - CM5 (0.2–0.4), and finally reaches 1.85 in CM6. These variations suggest that MnO2 incorporation dynamically alters defect density and order in the carbon lattice, with excessive loading leading to highly disordered structures. Such interactions strongly affect the electronic and electrochemical behavior of the composites.

Figure 4 presents SEM images of the CM0, CM2, and CM4 samples. Figure 4a, b show the SEM images of pristine carbon (CM0) extracted from alkaline batteries, revealing a structure composed of large particle clusters with uneven distribution and irregular porosity. Additionally, large carbon blocks can be observed aggregating into clusters. However, when MnO2 is deposited using a KMnO4 concentration of 0.005 M (CM2) (Fig. 4c, d), MnO2 coatings start to appear on the carbon surface, but the distribution remains non-uniform. The material also exhibits increased porosity compared to CM0. At higher magnification (Fig. 4d), MnO2 is observed in the form of nanosized particles or thin layers covering the carbon surface, creating a non-homogeneous structure. This modification could strengthen the material’s charge storage capabilities by promoting efficient electron and ion transfer through increased active sites. When the KMnO4 concentration is increased to 0.01 M (CM4) (Fig. 4e, f), the changes become more pronounced, with a thicker, more widespread, and uniformly distributed MnO2 coating. This structural transformation could have a direct impact on the capacitive behavior of supercapacitors, offering unique electrode material for energy storage applications. Additionally, post-cycling SEM analysis of CM4 after 2000 charge-discharge cycles (Fig. 4g, h) shows that the overall uniformity of the MnO2 coating and porosity remains intact, with no significant delamination or loss of adhesion to the carbon matrix, supporting the observed 91.3% capacitance retention. This demonstrates the structural stability of the material after cycling process.

Electrochemical traits

Electrochemical response of materials in a three-electrode configuration

Figure 5 presents the charge-storage behavior in cyclic voltammograms (CV) of CM0 to CM6 electrodes within a stable electrochemical working range from − 1.2 to 1.2 V versus the Ag/AgCl reference electrode, using a 0.25 M NaCl electrolyte solution. In Fig. 5a–g, CV curves were recorded under different voltage scan conditions ranging from 5 to 100 mV s− 1 for CM0 to CM6 samples. These curves illustrate the redox reactions, electrochemical energy storage mechanisms of the materials, and unambiguously illustrate the pseudocapacitive behavior of MnO2 - based supercapacitors51. Increasing the scan rate results in noticeable shifts of the redox peaks, indicating that both surface phenomena and ion transport dynamics play a significant role in determining the reaction kinetics. The variation in peak position at higher scan rates indicates that redox reactions tend to be more surface-controlled than diffusion - controlled, which is a typical behavior of pseudocapacitive materials such as MnO251,52. Compared to pristine carbon (CM0), C@MnO2 samples exhibit a larger enclosed CV area stemming from the interactive contribution of MnO2 and the porous carbon structure, enhancing energy storage performance53. However, an excessively high concentration of MnO2 can lead to the formation of a thick MnO2 layer that obscures the carbon particles, which in turn diminishes the electric double-layer capacitor behavior. In the comparative plot (Fig. 5h), the electrochemical response at a sweep rate of 5 mV s− 1 reveal distinct electrochemical characteristics depending on the MnO2 content. To further quantify the contribution of MnO2, the gravimetric capacitance (CCV) of each sample was extracted from Eq. 1 and is presented in Table 1. According to Table 1, the CM4 sample exhibits the superior specific capacitance, with a quantification of 152.44 F g− 1 at a scan rate of 5 mV s− 1. The intrinsic capacitance of all samples tends to exhibit a downward trend with higher scan rates. The specific capacitance of the CM0 sample gradually decreases from 131.95 F g− 1 at 5 mV s− 1 to 40.29 F g− 1 at 100 mV s− 1. This phenomenon is attributed to the fact that during fast scanning conditions, electrolyte ion transport is hindered due to insufficient time to diffuse deeply into the material structure, thereby limiting charge storage on the electrode surface54.

The galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) behavior of the samples across different current densities is illustrated in Fig. 6, operating across a potential window of to using as the electrolyte. Figure 6a presents the GCD curve of pristine carbon (CM0), which exhibits a nearly triangular shape, indicating efficient ion transport, high specific capacitance, excellent reversibility, and outstanding discharge characteristics32,33. For the C@MnO2 composites (Fig. 6b–g), the GCD curves deviate from the ideal triangular shape stemming from the hybrid capacitance effect arising from Faradaic reactions in metal oxides and electrostatic charge accumulation in activated carbon53. The Faradaic reaction contributes to a longer charge/discharge duration compared to pristine carbon, leading to higher capacitance53. Consequently, at low current density (1 A g− 1), a notably prolonged charge/discharge duration is observed, indicating a higher charge storage capacity at low current conditions. However, upon increasing the current density (up to 10 A g− 1), the charge/discharge time substantially shortens, which is typically attributed to the decline in energy storage efficiency as the electrochemical reaction rate increases55. The intrinsic symmetry of the charge/discharge curves reveals a highly reversible electrochemical process, which is essential for long-term stability in practical applications. Figure 6h compares the GCD curves of CM0 and C@MnO2 composites (CM1–CM6) at a predetermined current density of 1 A g− 1, providing insights into their electrochemical performance. Among all samples, CM4 exhibits the longest charge/discharge time, indicating a strong Faradaic impact on the electrode interface, paving the way for excellent charge storage capability and potentially high capacitance. Conversely, CM2 demonstrates the shortest charge/discharge time. This suggests that the low loading in this composite may not effectively utilize the carbon surface, leading to reduced electrical conductivity and hindered ion adsorption within the material.

The Nyquist plots of the samples, measured over a broad frequency range of 10 kHz to 0.1 Hz with an amplitude of 5 mV, are demonstrated in Fig. 7a. In these plots, the real impedance (Z’, x-axis) characterizes the real impedance component, reflecting the ohmic resistance of the system, which includes the solution resistance, electrode resistance, and interfacial contact resistance. Meanwhile, the imaginary part of impedance (-Z’’, y-axis) denotes the imaginary part of impedance, which is associated with ion diffusion and Faradaic processes51,56. A closer examination reveals that the impedance curves exhibit similar shapes with an inclination of approximately 45°, characteristic of Warburg diffusion, which corresponds to the ion penetration process into the electrode56. Among the samples, CM4 exhibits the lowest equivalent series resistance (ESR) of 6.42 Ω, encompassing the bulk resistance of electrode material, the electrode-electrolyte interfacial resistance, and the ionic resistance of the electrolyte. This finding indicates that CM4 has superior ion adsorption capability compared to other samples. Furthermore, to further investigate the charge storage capability of the electrodes, electrochemical capacitance values at different levels of applied current were determined from GCD curves using Eqs. 2, 4, and 5, as depicted in Fig. 7b, c. Notably, CM4 exhibits the best-performing capacitance value of 215.46 F g− 1 at 1 A g− 1, which aligns well with capacitance values derived from CV curves. Conversely, CM2 depicts the lowest specific capacitance of 157.09 F g− 1 at 1 A g− 1, even lower than pristine carbon (CM0), which has a capacitance of 168.2 F g− 1 at 1 A g− 1. These results demonstrate exceptional electrochemical performance compared to previous studies, as summarized in Table 2. Figure 7c illustrates the relationship between energy density and power density for the samples. At a power density of 1200 W kg− 1, the energy densities (W h kg− 1) for CM0 to CM6 are 134.57, 142.4, 125.67, 152.44, 172.36, 145.98, and 130.03, respectively. CM2 shows the lowest energy density among all samples, which is consistent with its previously reported low specific capacitance. As power density increases, a gradual decline in energy density is observed for all samples. This behavior is primarily attributed to the shortened charge/discharge time at higher power levels, which restricts ion diffusion and limits their participation in electrochemical reactions51. This emphasizes the importance of selecting materials with an optimized structure to ensure stable performance in practical applications.

To assess the performance in a practical device configuration, we selected two representative samples, we selected two representative samples for further supercapacitor investigations: the pristine carbon sample (CM0) and the sample with the highest specific capacitance (CM4). These samples were assembled into a symmetric supercapacitor (SSC) configuration, consisting of electrode/gel polymer electrolyte/electrode, to assess their potential for practical supercapacitor applications.

Figure 8a, b illustrate the CV curves of the CM0 and CM4 devices within a permitted voltage span of − 1.2 to 1.2 V under various scan rates. In Fig. 8a, the CV curve of CM0 exhibits a near-rectangular shape, characteristic of the electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC) mechanism, indicating that charge storage occurs primarily through ion adsorption on the electrode surface. As the scan rate is progressively increased, the current response also increases, but the curve slightly distorts. This is typically attributed to the influence of resistance and sluggish reaction kinetics. In contrast, Fig. 8b shows the CV curve of CM4 with clearly defined redox peaks, demonstrating higher charge storage capacity. This behavior suggests better ion exchange capability or a pseudocapacitive contribution (Faradaic process)66. As the scan rate increases, the distortion of the curve becomes more pronounced, indicating the effects of ion diffusion and internal resistance.

Figure 11 illustrates the effect of temperature on the electrochemical performance of two devices, CM0 and CM4, through three measurements: cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), conducted at 25 °C, 50 °C, and 80 °C. From the CV curves at a scan rate of 5 mV s− 1 (Fig. 11a, d), changes in the curve area with increasing temperature can be observed. For CM0, the CV curve shows only minor changes, indicating that the charge storage performance is not significantly affected by temperature. In contrast, for CM4 – the device containing a carbon–MnO2 composite material – the CV curve at 80 °C exhibits a significantly reduced area compared to 25 °C, revealing a decline in charge storage performance at elevated temperatures. This suggests that the thermal stability of MnO2 is a limiting factor under harsh operating conditions. The EIS analysis (Fig. 11b, e) further clarifies this mechanism. For CM0, the impedance increases with temperature, particularly in the low-frequency region, indicating a decline in ion diffusion capability. In contrast CM4 also exhibits increased impedance at higher temperatures, it maintains better ionic conductivity than CM0, owing to the conductive and ion-transport-facilitating nature of the composite structure. However, this improvement is insufficient to compensate for the degradation in electrochemical activity at high temperatures. The electrochemical behavior of CM0 and CM4 under thermal stress is more distinctly reflected in the GCD curves at a current density of 0.25 A g− 1 (Fig. 11c, f). As the temperature increases from 25 °C to 80 °C, both devices exhibit significantly shortened discharge times, with CM4 showing a more pronounced effect. At elevated temperatures, ion diffusion in the PVA–NaCl electrolyte accelerates, coupled with improved electrical conductivity of the electrode materials, resulting in enhanced charge–discharge kinetics. Notably, for CM4, which incorporates a carbon–MnO2 composite, the synergistic effect between carbon and MnO2 plays a critical role: carbon forms an efficient conductive network for fast electron transport, while MnO2 contributes to charge storage via pseudocapacitance mechanisms. This phenomenon is attributed to the acceleration of the pseudocapacitive reactions at the MnO2 surface and enhanced ion diffusion kinetics at higher temperatures, leading to rapid charge release and significantly shortened discharge durations. Crucially, the reduced discharge time reflects accelerated kinetics, not a loss of intrinsic capacitance. In contrast, CM0 which relies solely on from carbon with lower intrinsic electrochemical activity, exhibits a slower kinetic response and less pronounced change in discharge time. Hence, the shortened GCD curve of CM4 at elevated temperatures reflects its fast charge transport and superior reactivity, highlighting the potential of this material for energy applications that demand rapid electrochemical responses.

Cycling stability is a critical criterion for evaluating the long-term reliability of electrode materials in energy storage systems, particularly supercapacitors. Figure 12 presents the capacity retention performance of two devices, CM0 and CM4, under continuous operation over 2000 charge–discharge cycles at a constant current density of 0.25 A g− 1. The results show that device CM0 retained only 66.23% of its initial capacity after 2000 cycles. This significant decline reflects limitations in structural stability and electrochemical reversibility of the electrode material. Possible causes include the dissolution of active material, low active surface area, sluggish ion transport, or weak interactions between the material and the electrolyte, leading to performance degradation under repeated cycling. In contrast, device CM4, employing a carbon–MnO2 composite electrode, demonstrated superior performance, retaining up to 91.3% of its initial capacity after the same number of cycles. The retention curve shows a stable and gradual decrease without abrupt fluctuations, indicating that the composite structure offers excellent electrochemical durability and maintains activity effectively throughout the test. This high stability can be attributed to the synergistic effect between carbon and MnO2: carbon forms a highly conductive network that enhances mechanical robustness and electron transport, while MnO2 contributes to charge storage through pseudocapacitive mechanisms. The outstanding capacity retention of 91.3% confirms that supercapacitors based on C@MnO2 electrodes possess remarkable electrochemical durability, making them promising candidates for high-performance and long-lifespan energy storage applications.

In addition to electrochemical evaluation, we provide a preliminary mass balance and energy consumption analysis to demonstrate the eco-friendly nature of the recycling and synthesis route. Approximately 10 wt% of carbon can be recovered from spent alkaline batteries (~ 2 g per AA cell), which is subsequently converted into ~ 11–12 g of C@MnO2 composite depending on MnO2 loading. Other fractions (MnO2, steel casing, electrolytes) can be directed to conventional recycling streams, minimizing waste. The total energy requirement for the recovery and synthesis steps, which involve only sonication, magnetic stirring, and drying at 120 °C, is estimated to be below 50 kWh per kg of carbon recovered. This value is at least one order of magnitude lower than conventional high-temperature syntheses of activated carbon or MnO2, thus substantiating the green claim of our approach. In addition, the processing cost is estimated at ~ 2–3 USD kg− 1 of electrode material, compared with 10–20 USD kg− 1 for commercial carbons, demonstrating a clear economic advantage of the recycling-based approach.

Density functional theory calculations

To further understand the geometric structure and electronic properties of MnO2@Carbon nanoparticle composites, first-principles calculations were conducted by utilizing the plane-wave periodic Density Functional Theory (DFT) approach implemented within Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)67,68. Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) parameterization of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)69 was used to treat the exchange and correlation energy of electrons along with the projected augmented wave (PAW) method for handing the interaction between ion cores and electrons. We performed a vertical stacking of 3 × 3 × 1 supercell of graphene with a single unicell of MnO2 cubic to have a lower lattice mismatch between each other. Due to the presence of localized d states in MnO2, the DFT + U method was applied with a Hubbard U parameter of 1.970,71 for the Mn d orbitals to more accurately describe electron correlation effects. A vacuum space with the thickness of 28 Å was introduced along the z direction to avoid periodic interactions. A cutoff energy of 540 eV was applied during the optimization process and the energy convergence threshold between consecutive steps was established to be below 10 − 5 eV for all calculations. The relaxation of lattice parameters and atomic positions was achieved when the Hellmann-Feynman force occurring on each atom was reduced to less than 0.001 eV/Å for the simulated models. A Monkhorst-Pack scheme72 3 × 3 × 3 and 6 × 6 × 1 k-mesh is required to converge the plane-wave basis set for the optimization and electronic calculations of the C@MnO2 sample.

Figure 13 presents the optimized structures of graphene, MnO₂, and C@MnO2 composite based on the vertical stacking of the MnO2 unicell son the 3 × 3 × 1 graphene whereas Table 3 summarizes their lattice parameters. The calculated lattice constants as well as the C-C bond length of pristine graphene are in good agreement with the values provided in literatures73,74. For pristine MnO2, the lateral lattice constants are a = b = 7.85 Å and the average Mn-O bond length is 1.91 Å, similar to the reported data75,76 and quite close the lattice parameters of λ- MnO277. Although both graphene and MnO2 exhibit a high symmetry in the geometric structure with the lattice constant parameters a = b, α = β = 90°, γ = 120° for graphene, and γ = 90° for MnO2, the formation of the Graphene/MnO2 composite breaks the symmetry structures with a = 7.71 Å, b = 6.73 Å, indicating that both components undergo adjustments to achieve a stable structure. In the composite structure, the C-C bond length is 1.49 Å, enlarging of approximately 4.9%, whereas the Mn-O bond length is 1.88 Å, compressing 1.6%. The distortion of the MnO2 crystal caused from the interaction between graphene layer and the MnO2 is obviously observed. However, the stacked MnO2 almost retains the probe trigonal omega-like structure as pristine one without graphene, in which each Mn4+ makes to six equivalent O2− atoms to form edge-sharing MnO6 octahedra. The shortest distance between the graphene layer and the MnO2 cubic lattice in the composite is 3.52 Å which is within the interfacial spacing range of the composites based on the C materials78. Such the interfacial spacing within the composite is enough to ensure the lattice structure stability of the fabricated C@MnO2 sample observed by SEM (Table 4).

It revealed that the MnO2 cubic receives electron charge from the graphene layer with amount of 0.253 e, enriching the outer orbitals of Mn. Conclusions

In summary, pristine carbon materials and C@MnO2 composites were synthesized using a simple method, producing materials with relatively good quality. The most notable aspect of this study is the nearly comprehensive and easily implementable recovery of carbon from alkaline battery recycling. The DFT calculations proves the lattice structure stability and good electrical conductivity with metal characteristics of that C@MnO2 exhibits, which can be a potential material for application as a good electrode of the capacitors with high performance. The composites were also synthesized using a simple method at room temperature in the laboratory. Electrochemical surveys with a three-electrode system demonstrated that the samples performed well in a 0.25 M NaCl electrolyte, with CM4 exhibiting the highest specific capacitance. For supercapacitor applications, CM0 and CM4 were tested at different temperatures, showing minimal changes at 25, 50, and 80 °C. Their lifespan was also evaluated over 2000 cycles, with CM4 retaining 91.3% of its initial capacitance and CM0 retaining 66.23%. These results confirm that the C@MnO2 composite is stable and suitable for supercapacitor applications.
