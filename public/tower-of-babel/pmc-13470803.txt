Effect of Aluminum Loading on Mesoporous Silica-Supported Catalysts for Enhanced Solketal Synthesis from Glycerol via Acetalization: An Integrated Response Surface Methodology and Artificial Neural Network Framework for Parameter Optimization
Authors: Phonsan Saetiao, Panadda Solod, Wannadear Nawae, Napaphat Kongrit, Jakkrapong Jitjamnong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The conversion of surplus glycerol from biodiesel production into solketal represents an effective strategy to enhance biofuel sustainability; however, the development of efficient and reusable solid acid catalysts with optimized acidity remains challenging. In this study, sulfated mesoporous SiO2/Al2O3 catalysts with varying Si/Al ratios were synthesized and evaluated for glycerol acetalization with acetone. Process parameters were optimized using response surface methodology (RSM), considering catalyst loading, temperature, reaction time, and acetone: glycerol molar ratio. An artificial neural network (ANN) model was further developed to capture nonlinear relationships and validate predictive performance within the experimental domain. The Si/Al (50:1) catalyst exhibited excellent performance, achieving 70.0% glycerol conversion, 64.9% solketal yield, and 92.8% selectivity under the optimized conditions (7 wt % catalyst, 60 °C, 120 min, and 6:1 acetone: glycerol molar ratio). NH3-TPD analysis revealed that this catalyst possesses a high density of moderate-strength acid sites, which are primarily responsible for its superior catalytic performance. The RSM model demonstrated high predictive accuracy, with a deviation of less than 2.2% between predicted and experimental values. The ANN model also showed strong predictive capability (overall R = 0.9962), confirming its suitability as a complementary nonlinear modeling tool within the studied range. Reusability tests indicated a gradual decline in activity while maintaining high selectivity (>87%) after four cycles. This deactivation behavior is associated with a reduction in accessible acid sites, as supported by NH3-TPD analysis. These results demonstrate that controlled Si/Al composition effectively tunes catalyst acidity and performance, while the combined use of RSM and ANN provides a complementary modeling approach for reliable prediction within the defined experimental space, offering a practical strategy for sustainable glycerol valorization under mild conditions.

Introduction

The growing demand for sustainable chemicals and fuel additives has intensified research on biomass-derived platform molecules. Among these, glycerol, an abundant byproduct of biodiesel production, has attracted significant attention as a renewable feedstock for value-added chemical synthesis. − The oversupply of crude glycerol has driven extensive efforts toward its valorization into high-value products, thereby improving the overall economics and sustainability of biomass-based processes. − Among the various conversion pathways explored, catalytic glycerol transformation has proven particularly versatile, enabling the production of value-added chemicals such as glycerol carbonate, − 1,2-propanediol, − 1,3-propanediol, , acrolein, , and solketal. −

Solketal (4-hydroxymethyl-2,2-dimethyl-1,3-dioxolane), synthesized via the acid-catalyzed acetalization of glycerol with acetone, has attracted particular interest as a sustainable fuel additive. Its favorable physicochemical properties, including improved cold-flow behavior, increased octane number, reduced kinematic viscosity, and enhanced combustion characteristics, make it an attractive blending component for biodiesel, gasoline, and aviation fuels. − In addition, solketal exhibits low toxicity and high biodegradability, reinforcing its suitability for environmentally friendly applications.

However, the industrial implementation of solketal synthesis remains limited by challenges related to catalytic efficiency, process sustainability, and operational robustness. Conventional homogeneous acid catalysts, such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and p-toluenesulfonic acid, exhibit high catalytic activity but suffer from drawbacks, including corrosion, separation difficulty, and the generation of hazardous waste streams. −

To address these limitations, heterogeneous solid acid catalysts have been extensively investigated as greener alternatives, offering advantages such as facile separation, reusability, reduced corrosion, and improved process integration. , However, their performance is often limited by insufficient acidity, poor active site accessibility, and mass-transfer constraints associated with bulky glycerol molecules. , Overcoming these constraints requires rational catalyst design that simultaneously enhances the acidity and preserves efficient reactant diffusion.

Mesoporous silica (mSiO2) materials, including MCM-41, SBA-15, and related frameworks, have emerged as promising catalyst supports for glycerol acetalization due to their exceptionally high surface areas, tunable mesoporous structures (2–50 nm), and excellent thermal and hydrothermal stability. ,− Their ordered pore networks facilitate mass transport, while their surface chemistry enables versatile functionalization. In this study, the mSiO2 framework was intentionally selected as a fixed support platform to isolate the effects of Al loading and process variables, rather than comparing different mesoporous structures (e.g., MCM-41 vs SBA-15). Accordingly, the experimental framework is designed to optimize reaction conditions for a given support system, not to evaluate or optimize across different silica frameworks. However, pristine mSiO2 exhibits negligible intrinsic acidity, necessitating modification to introduce catalytically active acid sites.

Among various modification strategies, the incorporation of aluminum (Al; typically as alumina, Al2O3) into the mSiO2 framework has proven particularly effective for generating both Brønsted and Lewis acid sites. These sites originate from framework substitution (Si–O–Al) and extra-framework aluminum species, respectively, enabling synergistic catalytic behavior during glycerol acetalization.

Brønsted acid sites facilitate the protonation of acetone, enhancing its electrophilicity, while Lewis acid sites stabilize intermediates and improve glycerol adsorption, leading to enhanced catalytic activity and selectivity. The Si/Al ratio plays a critical role in determining acidity, structural integrity, and catalytic performance. Excessive Al loading may lead to pore blockage or structural distortion, whereas insufficient loading results in low acidity. Therefore, optimizing this balance is essential. In parallel, solketal synthesis is influenced by multiple interacting process variables, including catalyst loading, temperature, reaction time, and reactant molar ratio. These interactions are often nonlinear, making conventional optimization approaches inefficient.

Response surface methodology (RSM), particularly when coupled with central composite design (CCD), enables the development of predictive models that capture the main effects, interactions, and curvature with minimal experimental effort. , Artificial neural networks (ANNs), in contrast, are highly effective at modeling nonlinear relationships through data-driven learning, often providing superior predictive accuracy. , The integration of RSM and ANN offers a powerful hybrid framework that combines statistical interpretability with predictive robustness, yet their application to solketal synthesis over mSiO2-based catalysts remains limited.

Despite extensive studies on solid acid catalysts, systematic correlations between Al loading, acidity, mesostructure, and catalytic performance remain insufficiently explored. For instance, Vivian et al. demonstrated enhanced glycerol conversion using gallium-incorporated mSiO2, which was attributed to the cooperative roles of Lewis and Brønsted acid sites. Similarly, while Matkala et al. reported high activity over sulfated Al-MCM-41 catalysts due to the presence of strong acid sites and preserved mesoporosity, it is noteworthy that, although water is generated during the acetalization reaction and may partially hydrate Lewis acid sites to form additional Brønsted acidity, both types of acid sites can coexist under the reaction conditions and contribute synergistically to catalytic performance. Specifically, Brønsted acid sites are primarily responsible for the initial activation of acetone via protonation, while Lewis acid sites facilitate the adsorption and orientation of glycerol molecules and stabilize intermediate species such as hemiacetals. This dual functionality enhances both the reaction rate and selectivity toward solketal formation. Nevertheless, the combined use of RSM and ANN as an integrated optimization and prediction framework in this context has rarely been reported, representing a notable knowledge gap.

The present study systematically investigated the effect of the Al loading level on the physicochemical properties and catalytic performance of mSiO2-supported catalysts for glycerol acetalization to solketal. The RSM–ANN framework focuses on four key operating variables (catalyst loading, reaction temperature, reaction time, and acetone:glycerol molar ratio), selected based on preliminary experiments and literature reports identifying them as the most influential parameters for solketal production. Other factors were kept constant to isolate their effects and to ensure statistical reliability within the limited experimental design (n = 30).

Materials and Methods

Materials

Purified glycerol, obtained as previously reported Saetiao et al., was used as the reactant for solketal synthesis. Acetone (analytical grade, Macron Fine Chemicals) served as the ketalization agent. Commercial mesoporous SiO2 (Sigma-Aldrich Pte. Ltd.) was employed as the catalyst support. Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate [Al­(NO3)3·9H2O; 98% purity] was used as the Al precursor, while H2SO4 (1.0 N, Kemaus) was utilized as the sulfating and acidifying agent during catalyst activation. n-Butylamine (99%, Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd.) was used as a probe molecule for acidity determination. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Catalyst Preparation

The mSiO2-supported Al catalysts (hereafter Si/Al) with varying Si/Al molar ratios (20:1, 50:1, and 100:1) were synthesized via the incipient wetness impregnation method, using mSiO2 as the support. For example, the preparation of the 50:1 Si/Al catalyst is described herein. The dried mSiO2 (0.50 g) was impregnated with an aqueous solution containing 0.062 g of Al­(NO3)3, corresponding to the desired Si/Al ratio. The slurry was magnetically stirred to ensure uniform dispersion, aged at ambient temperature for 24 h, and subsequently dried at 105 °C overnight. The dried material was calcined under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere at 550 °C for 6 h with a heating rate of 10 °C min–1 to promote Al incorporation and stabilize the mesostructure. Subsequently, 1.0 g of the calcined Al-modified mSiO2 was subjected to wet impregnation with 15 mL of 1.0 N H2SO4 under continuous stirring for 2 h to introduce strong acidic functionalities. The solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator at 105 °C, followed by a second calcination at 550 °C for 6 h under a nitrogen atmosphere to generate sulfated Si/Al catalysts. At this calcination temperature (550 °C), the resulting catalyst is likely to contain a mixture of sulfate species, including surface-bound SO4 2– groups, amorphous Al2(SO4)3, and possibly Al2O­(SO4)2 species, in addition to dispersed Al2O3 domains. These sulfate species are responsible for the generation of strong Brønsted acidity via protonated surface groups. A schematic illustration of the catalyst preparation process is shown in Figure . The resulting materials were denoted as Si/Al (20:1), Si/Al (50:1), and Si/Al (100:1) and stored in a desiccator prior to catalytic testing.

Catalyst Characterization

Catalyst characterization was performed using TGA, N2 adsorption–desorption, FTIR, XRD, FESEM–EDX, FETEM, n-butylamine titration, and NH3-TPD analyses to investigate the thermal, textural, structural, morphological, compositional, and acidic properties of the prepared catalysts. Detailed descriptions of the instrumentation, experimental conditions, and characterization procedures are provided in the Supporting Information (Section S1).

Experimental Design and Process Optimization Using RSM

The RSM approach was employed to quantify the effects of key operating parameters on solketal selectivity and to identify optimal reaction conditions for solketal synthesis. The CCD was generated using Design-Expert software (version 13, trial), enabling efficient estimation of linear, interaction, and quadratic effects with a limited number of experiments. Four independent variablescatalyst loading, reaction temperature, reaction time, and acetone: glycerol molar ratiowere investigated at five coded levels, as summarized in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. The experimental matrix comprised 30 runs, including six center-point replicates to estimate pure error and assess model adequacy (Table S2 in the Supporting Information).

The statistical significance and adequacy of the model were evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Acetalization of Glycerol to Solketal

Acetalization of glycerol with acetone was carried out in a 250 mL three-necked round-bottom flask fitted with a reflux condenser and placed on a magnetic stirrer hot plate. A predetermined amount of glycerol and acetone (acetone:glycerol molar ratio = 6:1) was charged into the reactor and heated under continuous stirring at 400 rpm until the desired reaction temperature (60 °C) was reached. The catalyst was then added to the pre-equilibrated mixture at a loading of 7 wt % relative to glycerol, and the suspension was maintained at the set temperature for the specified reaction time (120 min) to produce solketal formation. At selected time intervals, the solid catalyst was separated from the reaction mixture by filtration. The excess acetone in the filtrate was subsequently removed using a rotary evaporator. The resulting samples were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). Separation of reactants and products was achieved on an HP-Innowax capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness). Results and Discussion

Catalyst Characterization

The TGA (Figure a) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles (Figure b) of SiO2 and the Si/Al (x: 1) catalysts reveal multistep but overall limited mass losses over 30–1000 °C, evidencing good thermal robustness in the range relevant to solketal synthesis. For SiO2, the first mass decrease of 0.96 wt % between 35 and 150 °C is attributed to the desorption of weakly bound, physisorbed water from the hydrophilic SiO2 surface. A second set of small losses around 370 °C and near 520 °C is associated with the removal of more tightly bound water and residual organics, ,, while the high-temperature step beginning above 700 °C corresponds to progressive dehydroxylation and condensation of surface silanol groups, yielding a total mass loss of only 5.29 wt % at 1000 °C.

(a) Representative TGA and (b) DTG curves illustrating the thermal decomposition behavior and stability of the synthesized catalysts.

The Si/Al (20:1) catalyst exhibits a more complex decomposition pattern and a larger overall mass loss (18.2 wt %), consistent with its higher concentration of surface functionalities. The DTG profiles show weight loss peaks at approximately 190 and 530 °C, which can be attributed to the removal of physisorbed water and the thermal decomposition of surface hydroxyl groups (−OH) and sulfate species (−SO3H), respectively. ,, These processes account for cumulative losses of several weight percent and indicate that a significant fraction of strongly bound acidic and oxygenated species decomposes in this temperature window. A pronounced DTG feature observed in the high-temperature region (745–800 °C), accompanied by an additional mass drop of about 9.4 wt %, is assigned to the decomposition of aluminum sulfate species (Al2(SO4)3) into Al2O3. The intensity of this peak correlates with the amount of sulfate species associated with alumina, indicating higher sulfate loading in the Si/Al (20:1) catalyst.

In contrast, the Si/Al (50:1) and Si/Al (100:1) catalysts display intermediate behavior, with total weight losses of only 6.8–7.5 wt % up to 1000 °C. In both cases, a modest initial loss below 150 °C corresponds to the desorption of physically adsorbed water, while smaller, broader DTG features between 200 and 600 °C are associated with the gradual decomposition of surface hydroxyl and sulfate species (−OH and −SO3H) rather than abrupt degradation events. The relatively shallow DTG minima and limited mass losses at higher temperatures indicate that these catalysts undergo slower, more controlled defunctionalization of their surfaces and retain structurally intact Si/Al frameworks even after significant heating.

The TGA/DTG results show that all the Si/Al catalysts maintain more than 80–90 wt % of their mass at 800 °C and above, confirming the high thermal stability of the sulfated Si/Al matrices. This balance of structural stability and sufficiently robust acidic sites underpins their suitability as durable solid acids for solketal production.

The N2 adsorption––desorption isotherms (Figure a) and corresponding BJH pore-size distributions (Figure b) of the catalysts are summarized in Table , offering detailed information on their textural properties. The adsorption–desorption curves exhibit Type IV isotherms with stepwise uptake at a low relative pressure and a marked increase at higher P/P 0, which is characteristic of mesoporous solids. , The BJH analysis reveals a pore-size maximum in the 35.80–52.32 nm range, thereby confirming that all synthesized samples possess a well-developed mesoporous nature.

Representative (a) N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and (b) BJH pore-size distributions of the prepared (1) SiO2, (2) Si/Al (20:1), (3) Si/Al (50:1), and (4) Si/Al (100:1) catalysts.

The introduction of Al markedly alters the textural properties of the materials. For the Si/Al (20:1) catalyst, the S BET decreased, while the V p and average pore diameter increased, indicating partial pore widening and the development of additional interparticle voids upon Al incorporation. The Si/Al (50:1) and Si/Al (100:1) catalysts exhibit a higher S BET (137 and 140 m2 g–1, respectively) with substantial mesopore volumes (V p = 0.89–1.05 cm3 g–1) and pore diameters around 43 nm, suggesting an optimal balance between accessible surface and pore openness that favors mass transport of glycerol and acetone during the reaction. Overall, the BET results demonstrate that tuning the Si/Al ratio allows systematic modulation of mesoporosity and surface exposure without collapsing the mesostructure, which is crucial for achieving high catalytic efficiency in solketal synthesis.

The FTIR spectra of the Si/Al catalysts with varying Al contents reveal distinct structural transformations in the SiO2 matrix upon Al incorporation (Figure ). Compared to pure SiO2, the catalyst spectra exhibit marked changes in transmittance around the 1075–1079 cm–1 region, which are attributed to the fundamental stretching modes of Si–O groups within the mesoporous framework. Notably, the band in this region is more appropriately interpreted as an overlapping contribution of Si–O–Si vibrations and sulfate (SO4 2–) species associated with alumina (Al–SO4). In particular, bands observed at approximately 1170 and 1035 cm–1 are assigned to SO stretching vibrations of sulfate groups, indicating the successful incorporation of sulfate species into the catalyst. The increase in band intensity with increasing Al content suggests a higher concentration of sulfate species, consistent with the formation of Al–SO4 structures.

Representative FTIR spectra of the synthesized catalysts: (a) full FTIR spectra in the range of 4000–600 cm–1 and (b) enlarged view of the Si–O–Si vibration region (1300–700 cm–1).

As the Al content increased, both a downshift and a reduction in peak intensity were observed, implying a progressive disruption of Si environments due to the isomorphic substitution of silicon by Al atoms. This structural alteration leads to a reorganization of the siloxane network and significantly influences the acid character and active-site distribution within the catalyst.

Although the appearance of a subtle band near 960 cm–1 (Si–O–Al vibration) may indicate Al bridging within the silica lattice, this feature is barely perceptible in the present analysis. The absence or lower intensity of such absorption may suggest a preference for nonbridged Al environments at the studied loadings, or convolution with other framework-related signals typical of highly mesoporous supports. The spectra also show the consistent presence and evolution of the symmetric Si–O–Si stretching band at approximately 805 cm–1 across all catalyst samples. Even as the Al content increased, this band remained well-resolved, attesting to the preservation of the SiO2 mesostructure despite framework modification. Additionally, weak bands observed at approximately 870 and 685 cm–1 are attributed to Al–O stretching and bending vibrations, respectively, further confirming the incorporation of aluminum species. These spectral features are consistent with the XRD results (Figure ), which confirm the presence of aluminum sulfate (Al–SO4) phases in the catalysts.

Representative XRD diffraction patterns of the prepared Si/Al catalysts.

The catalytic performance of Al2O3/SiO2 materials is strongly influenced by the nature and distribution of acid sites. In the present system, both Brønsted and Lewis acid sites are generated due to the incorporation of aluminum species into the silica framework. The formation of Si–O–Al linkages gives rise to bridging hydroxyl groups (Si–OH–Al), which act as Brønsted acid sites capable of protonating the carbonyl group of acetone, thereby enhancing its electrophilicity. In parallel, coordinatively unsaturated Al3+ species function as Lewis acid sites, facilitating the adsorption and activation of reactant molecules. Furthermore, the introduction of sulfate groups increases the overall acidity by generating additional strong Brønsted acid sites through electron-withdrawing effects. The synergistic interaction between Brønsted and Lewis acid sites is therefore crucial for promoting the acetalization of glycerol to solketal, where Brønsted acidity primarily drives protonation steps, while Lewis acidity assists in intermediate stabilization. This explains the superior catalytic performance observed for the optimized Si/Al ratio, which provides a balanced distribution of accessible acid sites.

Wide-angle XRD patterns of the synthesized materials are presented in Figure . All catalysts exhibit a broad halo centered at a 2θ of around 21°, characteristic of predominantly amorphous or poorly ordered SiO2. This confirms that the SiO2 framework remained largely noncrystalline after the incorporation of Al and subsequent sulfation. ,, The parent SiO2 shows only this diffuse feature, whereas the Si/Al (20:1) and Si/Al (50:1) catalysts display additional weak reflections at 2θ of 15.24°, 21.0°, 25.04°, 25.49°, 30.68°, 33.61°, 34.38°, 38.84°, 40.93°, 44.34°, 46.77°, 52.32°, and 55.47°, which can be indexed to aluminum sulfate-type phases, as referenced by ICDD No. 01-081-1835, indicating the presence of finely dispersed alumina-containing domains at these compositions.

The gradual increase in overall diffraction intensity suggests a slight enhancement in structural ordering and electron density upon Al introduction, consistent with the partial formation of Al-rich clusters on the SiO2 surface. In contrast, the Si/Al (100:1) catalyst retains an essentially amorphous pattern, with no resolvable peaks attributable to crystalline Al-containing phases. This implies that Al species at this higher Si/Al ratio are highly diluted and remain either in an XRD-amorphous state or as nanometric entities below the detection limit. The predominance of amorphous SiO2–Al2O3 matrices is beneficial for catalysis because disordered networks can expose a larger fraction of unsaturated surface atoms and defect sites, which may act as Brønsted and Lewis acid centers and thereby promote glycerol acetalization and related reactions.

The surface texture and elemental distribution of the mSiO2 support and the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst (Figure ) were evaluated by using FESEM. The pristine SiO2 displays a relatively open, sponge-like network composed of interconnected, string-shaped domains with fairly uniform primary particle sizes, consistent with an mSiO2 framework that can facilitate reactant diffusion. In contrast, the incorporation of Al and subsequent sulfation lead to the formation of more compact aggregates of finer particles in the Si/Al (50:1) sample, indicative of partial restructuring of the SiO2 surface and deposition of Al2O3/sulfate species that generate a rougher, more heterogeneous morphology.

Representative FESEM images of (a) SiO2 and (b) Si/Al (50:1).

EDX analysis corroborates these morphological changes. The SiO2 support contained only Si, O, and adventitious C, whereas the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst exhibited additional signals corresponding to Al and S, confirming the successful introduction of Al and sulfate groups onto the SiO2 framework. The elemental mapping revealed that Si, Al, and S are uniformly distributed over the observed area, with no evidence of large Al- or S-rich domains, suggesting that the active species are finely dispersed on the support surfacean attribute that is generally favorable for achieving high catalytic activity and stability in acetalization reactions.

Further insight into the nanostructure of the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst was obtained using FETEM. The TEM micrographs (Figure ) reveal aggregates of nearly spherical to slightly elongated particles with clearly discernible, uniform mesopores, indicating that the ordered pore network of the SiO2 framework is retained after Al incorporation and subsequent sulfation. The pore walls appear well-defined and free from noticeable blockages or secondary deposits, in good agreement with the N2-sorption results, which show preserved mesoporosity and a high accessible surface area for this material.

Representative FETEM images of the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst.

The high-resolution images show no contrast features corresponding to large crystalline domains, and the selected-area electron diffraction pattern displays diffuse halos rather than distinct diffraction rings, confirming the predominantly amorphous/mesoporous character of the Si–Al–S matrix. This combination of an open mesoporous architecture with an amorphous, defect-rich framework is advantageous for catalysis, as it simultaneously offers short diffusion paths for reactants and a high density of coordinatively unsaturated surface sites that can act as active centers for glycerol acetalization and related transformations.

The acidity of the synthesized Si/Al catalysts was quantified by n-butylamine titration, and the results are summarized in Table . The total acid site density varies with the Si/Al ratio, indicating a strong dependence of the surface acidity on catalyst composition. The Si/Al (20:1) catalyst exhibited an acidity of 0.275 mmol g–1, which increased to 0.375 mmol g–1 for Si/Al (50:1), representing the highest acid site density among the fresh catalysts and correlating with its superior catalytic performance in glycerol acetalization. A slight decrease to 0.325 mmol g–1 was observed for Si/Al (100:1), suggesting dilution of acid sites at an excessive SiO2 content.

To further elucidate the nature and strength distribution of acid sites, NH3-TPD analysis was performed, and the representative desorption profiles are presented in Figure . All catalysts exhibit broad NH3 desorption peaks spanning low- to high-temperature regions, indicating the coexistence of weak, moderate, and strong acid sites.

Representative NH3-TPD profiles of the synthesized Si/Al catalysts, illustrating the distribution and strength of surface acid sites.

The Si/Al (20:1) catalyst displays relatively weak and low-intensity desorption features, primarily in the low-temperature region (<200 °C), suggesting a predominance of weak acid sites. This is consistent with its comparatively low total acidity and limited catalytic performance. In contrast, the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst exhibits a pronounced and sharp desorption peak centered at approximately 280–300 °C, corresponding to a high density of moderate acid sites. These sites are widely recognized as the most effective for glycerol acetalization, as they provide sufficient acidity to activate the carbonyl group of acetone while minimizing undesired side reactions.

For the Si/Al (100:1) catalyst, the desorption profile becomes broader and shifts toward higher temperatures, indicating the presence of stronger acid sites but with reduced overall intensity. This behavior suggests a partial dilution of accessible acid sites due to excessive silica content, in agreement with the decrease in total acidity observed from the n-butylamine titration.

Notably, the reused Si/Al (50:1) catalyst shows a significant reduction in both peak intensity and total desorbed ammonia, indicating a substantial loss of acid sites after the reaction. The disappearance of the sharp moderate-acidity peak further suggests that the most catalytically active sites are preferentially deactivated, which is consistent with the marked decrease in the measured acidity (Table ). This deactivation may be attributed to factors such as coke deposition, pore blockage, or partial leaching of active Al species during the reaction.

Influence of Si/Al Loading on the Catalytic Capability

The influence of the Si/Al ratio on the catalytic performance during glycerol acetalization is summarized in Figure . The pristine mSiO2 exhibited negligible catalytic activity, yielding zero glycerol conversion and solketal formation. This result confirms that pure SiO2, which lacks intrinsic Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, is catalytically inactive toward glycerol acetalization.

Representative effect of the Si/Al catalyst loading on glycerol conversion, solketal yield, and solketal selectivity via the acetalization reaction. The reactions were conducted under the following conditions: reaction temperature of 60 °C, catalyst loading of 7 wt %, reaction time of 120 min, and an acetone-to-glycerol molar ratio of 6:1.

Incorporation of Al into the SiO2 framework markedly enhances the catalytic performance. The Si/Al (20:1) catalyst achieved a glycerol conversion of 56.8%, with a solketal yield and selectivity of 28.4% and 50.0%, respectively. This substantial improvement relative to SiO2 highlights the critical role of Al-derived acid sites in facilitating the acetalization reaction. As confirmed by NH3-TPD analysis (Figure ), this catalyst predominantly exhibits weak acid sites, which are less effective in promoting the acetalization reaction. Structurally, the Brønsted acid sites are primarily associated with surface sulfate groups (−SO3H) anchored to aluminum species (Al–SO4–H) as well as bridging hydroxyl groups (Si–OH–Al) within the aluminosilicate framework. Specifically, Brønsted acid sites (e.g., −SO3H and surface hydroxyl groups) protonate the carbonyl group of acetone, increasing its electrophilicity, while Lewis acid sites originate from coordinatively unsaturated Al3+ centers present in Al–O–Si linkages, which can accept electron pairs and promote the coordination and activation of glycerol molecules.

A further increase in the catalytic performance was observed when the Si/Al ratio was increased to 50:1. The Si/Al (50:1) catalyst delivered the highest glycerol conversion level (70%) and solketal yield (64.9%), together with an exceptionally high selectivity of 92.8%. This pronounced enhancement indicates that an intermediate Al loading provides an optimal balance between acid site density and accessibility within the mesoporous framework. This observation is further supported by NH3-TPD analysis (Figure ), where the Si/Al (50:1) catalyst exhibits a pronounced desorption peak centered at ∼280–300 °C, corresponding to a high density of moderate-strength acid sites. These sites are particularly effective for glycerol acetalization, as they provide sufficient acidity for reactant activation while minimizing side reactions. In this study, the Si/Al ratio was treated as a catalyst design parameter and initially screened using a one-factor-at-a-time approach to identify the optimal composition prior to process optimization. The Si/Al (50:1) catalyst, which exhibited the best overall performance, was therefore selected as a fixed catalyst formulation for subsequent RSM–ANN analysis. Incorporating the Si/Al ratio as an additional variable in the RSM design would significantly increase the experimental complexity and the required number of runs, which is not practical given the limited data set (n = 30). Thus, the adopted strategy separates catalyst optimization from process optimization, allowing more reliable modeling of operating variables while maintaining statistical robustness.

At this composition, the acid sites are sufficiently abundant to drive the reaction efficiently while remaining well-dispersed and readily accessible to bulky glycerol molecules. This optimal performance reflects a well-balanced distribution of structurally defined Brønsted (Al–SO4–H, Si–OH–Al) and Lewis (Al3+ in Al–O–Si) acid sites. The superior performance is also attributed to the synergistic interaction between Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, which facilitates both the formation and stabilization of the cyclic ketal intermediate. Similar trends have been reported for aluminosilicate and mesoporous catalysts, where moderate Si/Al ratios maximize catalytic efficiency by minimizing diffusion limitations and preventing excessive acid site clustering.

In contrast, further increasing the Si/Al ratio to 100:1 resulted in a noticeable decline in catalytic performance, with the glycerol conversion and solketal yield decreasing to 68.0% and 40.8%, respectively, and selectivity dropping to 60.0%. Notably, this reduction occurred despite only marginal changes in the S BET and pore size distribution, suggesting that textural properties alone do not govern the catalytic behavior. Instead, the decrease in activity is attributed to a reduced concentration of effective acid sites, leading to insufficient activation of glycerol and acetone. This interpretation is consistent with the NH3-TPD profile, which shows a broader and less intense desorption pattern for the Si/Al (100:1) catalyst, indicating a lower density of accessible active sites despite the presence of relatively stronger acid sites. Moreover, the weaker Brønsted acidity and diminished Brønsted–Lewis cooperative interactions at high Si/Al ratios reduce the stabilization of key reaction intermediates, thereby favoring side reactions and lowering selectivity. This behavior is consistent with a lower density of Al–SO4–H species and fewer accessible coordinatively unsaturated Al3+ centers. The Si/Al (50:1) catalyst provided the most favorable compromise between acid site density, accessibility, and mesostructural integrity.

Influence of Operating Variables on Solketal Synthesis

Selectivity was prioritized as the primary response variable because it reflects the intrinsic efficiency of the catalyst in directing the reaction toward the desired solketal while suppressing the formation of thermodynamically stable oligomeric byproducts. This criterion is particularly relevant for evaluating the influence of Al loading on the balance of acid site functionality and the mesoporous structure. Therefore, selectivity serves as a reliable proxy for the overall catalytic performance in this system.

The response surface plots in Figure a show that increasing the catalyst loading from lower to higher levels enhances solketal selectivity at all investigated temperatures, reflecting the greater availability of active sites for the acetalization of glycerol with acetone. At the same time, elevating the reaction temperature promotes higher selectivity by accelerating reaction kinetics and improving mass transfer, although the curvature of the surface implies diminishing returns at the upper temperature range, consistent with an approach to equilibrium. , The nearly flat top region of the surface suggests an operating range in which small variations in catalyst loading or temperature have only a minor effect on the selectivity, which is beneficial to process robustness. This behavior confirms that the interaction between catalyst loading and temperature is synergistic up to an optimal region, beyond which further increases provide a limited additional benefit.

Among the investigated process variables, reaction time exhibits the most substantial impact on solketal selectivity (Figure a), as evidenced by a steep decline in selectivity accompanying deviations from the central experimental value. In contrast, catalyst loading, reaction temperature, and acetone/glycerol molar ratio exhibit relatively minor variations in selectivity near their midpoints, highlighting their reduced sensitivity within this region. This differentiation emphasizes the pivotal role of precise reaction time control to achieve optimal conversion, while also indicating that adjustments to the other parameters may be made with less risk of compromising selectivity.

ANN Model Analysis

In this work, the ANN model is employed as a complementary approach to the RSM model, providing data-driven validation of the predictive trends without relying on predefined quadratic assumptions. This dual-model strategy enhances the reliability of the modeling results within an investigated design space. The nonlinearity between process variables and solketal selectivity was modeled using an ANN, where catalyst loading, reaction temperature, reaction time, and acetone molar ratio were used as input variables, while solketal selectivity served as the output variable. The experimental data set was derived from a CCD comprising 30 operating conditions, including factorial points, axial points, and replicated center points. To enhance model robustness and account for experimental variability, each operating condition was repeated three times, yielding a total of 90 experimental observations for the ANN development. Rather than averaging the replicate responses, all individual measurements were incorporated into the ANN training data set, enabling the network to capture the variability inherent in the experimental results. This strategy expanded the effective data set size and minimized the impact of random experimental errors during model training and validation.

The ANN modeling was implemented using MATLAB Online (Basic), which allowed for full access to the Neural Network Toolbox. The parameter configuration of the developed ANN model, which includes the network topology, training algorithm, activation functions, and data division strategy, is summarized in Table . The available experimental data were randomly partitioned into 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing, following a standard approach. Specifically, 62 data points were used for training, 14 for validation, and 14 for testing. This data partitioning is commonly adopted for small data sets and, together with validation monitoring, helps control overfitting while maintaining predictive capability. Similar ANN architectures, training algorithms, and data partition strategies have been successfully applied in energy- and engine-related studies involving limited experimental data sets.

Catalyst Reusability

The reusability of the optimized Si/Al (50:1) catalyst was assessed over four consecutive reaction cycles under the determined optimized conditions (7 wt % catalyst loading, 60 °C, 120 min, and acetone: glycerol molar ratio of 6:1). After each reaction cycle, the catalyst was recovered by filtration, thoroughly washed with ethanol to remove residual reactants and adsorbed organic species, and subsequently dried at 100 °C for 6 h prior to reuse in the next cycle. The corresponding glycerol conversion level and the solketal yield and selectivity are shown in Figure S2.

The catalyst exhibited a gradual loss of activity upon being reused. Glycerol conversion decreased from 70.0% in the first cycle to 45.8% in the fourth cycle (34.6% relative loss), accompanied by a decrease in solketal yield from 64.9% to 40.2%. In contrast, solketal selectivity remained high, decreasing only slightly from 92.8% to 87.8%, indicating preservation of the preferential pathway toward solketal formation. The disproportionate decline in conversion and yield relative to selectivity suggests that catalyst deactivation was primarily due to the reduced number of accessible active sites rather than a change in the intrinsic reaction selectivity. ,

Textural analysis confirms that deactivation is not related to structural collapse. The S BET decreased from 137 to 94 m2 g–1 after reuse, while the total V p remained nearly unchanged, and the average pore diameter increased (Figure a), indicating partial loss of accessible surface area rather than degradation of the mesoporous framework.

Physicochemical characterization of the reused Si/Al (50:1) catalyst after four consecutive reuses: (a) N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution, (b) FTIR spectra, (c) FESEM images with EDX elemental mapping, and (d) FETEM micrographs.

Structural integrity of the catalyst was further confirmed by FTIR, XRD, and microscopy analyses. The FTIR spectra (Figure b) retained the characteristic Si–O–Si bands at ∼1080 and ∼810 cm–1, with no additional bands indicative of carbonaceous deposits, while XRD patterns (Figure ) remained amorphous, with no crystalline Al-containing phases being detected. Moreover, EDX mapping (Figure c) showed distributions of Si, Al, O, and S, suggesting that no significant migration or aggregation of active components occurred after reuse. The FETEM images (Figure d) revealed preservation of the disordered mesoporous architecture, with no evidence of pore collapse or severe pore blockage.

Consistent with the observed decrease in acidity to 0.100 mmol g–1 (corresponding to an approximately 73% reduction in accessible acid sites), catalyst deactivation is primarily attributed to a loss of accessible active sites rather than a fundamental change in their intrinsic catalytic function. This interpretation is further corroborated by NH3-TPD analysis (Figure ), which reveals a marked reduction in desorption peak intensity for the reused catalyst, indicating a substantial loss of accessible acid sites. Notably, the overall desorption profile shape remains largely unchanged, suggesting that the intrinsic acid strength distribution is preserved despite the decrease in total acidity. These results suggest that although fewer active sites are available for reaction, the remaining sites retain their ability to selectively produce solketal, thereby explaining the relatively minor decline in selectivity. The reduction in accessible acidity may be associated with surface coverage by strongly adsorbed species and/or partial leaching of active sulfate groups or loosely bound Al species during the reaction. In particular, the attenuation of moderate-strength acid sites, identified as the most catalytically active sites for glycerol acetalization, likely plays a dominant role in the observed decline in catalytic activity. These findings confirm that catalyst deactivation is governed primarily by the loss of accessible active sites rather than structural degradation or changes in intrinsic acidity.

Conclusions

This study aimed to elucidate the role of Al loading in mSiO2–Al2O3 catalysts and to optimize the acid-catalyzed acetalization of glycerol to solketal through an integrated experimental and statistical approach. The optimization was primarily achieved using RSM, while ANN was employed as a complementary tool to validate the predictive trends within the same experimental domain.

Sulfated Si/Al catalysts with controlled compositions were successfully synthesized, and Si/Al (50:1) emerged as the optimal formulation, exhibiting the highest acidity (0.375 mmol g–1) and superior catalytic performance. This optimal behavior is further supported by NH3-TPD analysis, which revealed a high density of moderate-strength acid sites, identified as the most catalytically active sites for glycerol acetalization. Under the optimized conditions, as identified by RSM (7 wt % catalyst, 60 °C, 120 min, and a 6:1 acetone: glycerol molar ratio), this catalyst achieved 70% glycerol conversion, 64.9% solketal yield, and 92.8% selectivity, with a high overall desirability (0.956). The close agreement between predicted and experimental results (deviation <2.2%) confirms the robustness of the regression model. The ANN model demonstrated consistent predictive performance (R = 0.9962), supporting the reliability of the RSM-derived trends without introducing additional optimization beyond the defined design space.

The results reveal a clear structure–activity relationship, in which moderate Al incorporation maximizes accessible Brønsted acid sites while preserving mesoporosity. Excessive or insufficient Al loading alters both the density and strength distribution of acid sites, as evidenced by NH3-TPD analysis, leading to reduced catalytic efficiency, underscoring the importance of balancing acid density and textural integrity for glycerol acetalization.

From a sustainability perspective, this work advances biomass valorization by converting low-value glycerol into a high-value fuel additive under mild conditions using a reusable solid catalyst. Reusability tests demonstrate reasonable stability, with selectivity remaining above 87% after four cycles, although activity declines due to the partial loss of accessible acid sites. This deactivation behavior is consistent with NH3-TPD results, which indicate a significant reduction in the total acid site density after reuse while preserving the overall acid strength distribution. This study demonstrates that rational acidity control in mesoporous Si/Al catalysts enables efficient, selective, and sustainable solketal production, providing a viable pathway for upgrading biodiesel-derived glycerol.
