A Space-Efficient Algebraic Approach to Robotic Motion Planning
Matthias Bentert, Daniel Coimbra Salomao, Alex Crane, Yosuke Mizutani, Felix Reidl, Blair D. Sullivan

Abstract
We consider efficient route planning for robots in applications such as infrastructure inspection and automated surgical imaging. These tasks can be modeled via the combinatorial problem Graph Inspection . The best known algorithms for this problem are limited in practice by exponential space complexity. In this paper, we develop a memory-efficient approach using algebraic tools related to monomial testing on the polynomials associated with certain arithmetic circuits. Our contributions are two-fold. We first repair a minor flaw in existing work on monomial detection using a new approach we call tree certificates. We further show that, in addition to detection, these tools allow us to efficiently recover monomials of interest from circuits, opening the door for significantly broadened application of related algebraic tools. For Graph Inspection , we design and evaluate a complete algebraic pipeline. Our engineered implementation demonstrates that circuit-based algorithms are indeed memory-efficient in practice, thus encouraging further engineering efforts.

Algebraic Simulation of Dynamic Programming
.
One theoretical remedy to dynamic programming algorithms with exponential space complexity are algebraic approaches which “simulate” dynamic programming by constructing compact arithmetic circuits and testing the polynomials represented by these circuits for the presence of multilinear monomials

12

13

Our basic algorithmic strategy, outlined in

Tree Certificates

Our main theoretical contribution is the introduction of

First, they allow us to repair a newly-discovered flaw in the best-known algorithm for

From the viewpoint of a theorist, it is appropriate to design a reduction from one’s source problem to

In practice, however, it is essential to avoid this latter step if there is to be any hope of acceptable running times.
Toward this end, in

In

Refining our Reduction

Our initial reduction from

Search for the optimal weight.

Find the minimum weight
\tilde{\ell}

such that there exists a closed walk from

with weight
\tilde{\ell}
,
collecting at least
t
colors.
We repeat the following steps until finding the optimal weight.

(a)

Construction of an arithmetic circuit.

Construct an arithmetic circuit
for a target weight
\ell
(
\ell_{\textnormal{lo}}\leq\ell\leq\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}
).

(b)

Evaluation of the arithmetic circuit.

Solve

for the constructed arithmetic circuit.
If the output for

is feasible

\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}
.
Otherwise, we repeatedly solve

\theta

.

(3)

Recover and output a solution walk with weight

.

.

Let

be a

.
We perform the following preprocessing steps in our implementation.
Given a graph

Remove all vertices

For every edge

6

For accuracy, round weights on the transitive closure.

Again, compute all-pairs shortest paths to make

7

7
Search Strategies

In this section, we describe three search algorithms.
We prove that each algorithm correctly finds the optimal weight
with probability
1-(1-p)^{\theta}
,
where
p
is the constant success probability from
Lemma

3.2
,
and
\theta
is the failure count threshold greater than
p^{-1}
.
Also, we analyze the expected running time under simplistic assumptions:
the optimal weight
\tilde{\ell}
is uniformly distributed between
\ell_{\textnormal{lo}}
and
\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}
;
a single run of construction and evaluation of a circuit takes (non-decreasing)
f(\ell)
time;
and the evaluation results in
True
if
\tilde{\ell}\leq\ell
with probability
p

and
False
otherwise.
We write
T(\ell_{\textnormal{lo}},\ell_{\textnormal{hi}})
for the expected running time with lower and upper bounds

\ell_{\textnormal{lo}}
and
\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}
, respectively.
We also define
\ell_{\textnormal{diff}}:=\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}-\ell_{\textnormal{lo}}+1
.

7.1
StandardBinarySearch
.

We first implemented the standard binary search.
Given
\ell_{\textnormal{lo}}
and
\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}
,
we examine the middle value
\ell=\lfloor(\ell_{\text{lo}}+\ell_{\text{hi}})/2\rfloor

to see if
\ell
is feasible.
If
\ell
is feasible,
then we update
\ell_{\text{hi}}
to
\ell
.
On the other hand, if the circuit evaluates only to
False
for

\theta^{\prime}
trials, then we set
\ell_{\text{lo}}
to
\ell+1
.
Here we set
\theta^{\prime}
as a number in
\mathcal{O}(\theta\log\log(\ell_{\textnormal{diff}}))
such that

{(1-(1-p)^{\theta^{\prime}})^{\lfloor\log_{2}(\ell_{\textnormal{diff}})\rfloor}\geq 1-(1-p)^{\theta}}
.
The algorithm terminates when
\ell_{\text{lo}}=\ell_{\text{hi}}
,
and outputs this number.

Lemma 7.1
(
\bigstar
)

Algorithm
StandardBinarySearch
correctly finds the optimal weight
in expected running time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\theta\cdot f(\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}))

with probability
1-(1-p)^{\theta}
.

7.2
ProbabilisticBinarySearch
.

The performance of the previous algorithm degrades when the optimal value is close to
\ell_{\text{lo}}

because concluding that the value
\ell
is infeasible requires
\theta
evaluations of a circuit.
To mitigate this penalty,
ProbabilisticBinarySearch
evaluates each circuit once at a time
and randomly goes higher
before
concluding that the value is infeasible.
We set this probability
p^{\prime}
to
1-p/2
.
We maintain midpoints and failure counts as a stack.
See
Algorithm

5
in
Section

A.4
.

Lemma 7.2
(
\bigstar
)

Algorithm
ProbabilisticBinarySearch
correctly finds the optimal weight
in expected running time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((\theta+\ell_{\textnormal{diff}})\cdot f(\ell_{\textnormal{hi}}))

with probability
1-(1-p)^{\theta}
.

Now, we assume that a circuit returns

As described in

8
Two Phase Solution Recovery

We introduced solution recovery algorithms for general circuits in
Section

4
.
Once we find a tree certificate for the output node corresponding to weight
\ell
,
it is trivial to construct a solution walk collecting at least
t
colors with
weight
\ell
(see the proof of
Lemma

6.3
).
However, finding a tree certificate necessitates many circuit evaluations,
often requiring more time than that for finding the solution weight.
To cope with this issue, we propose
TwoPhaseRecovery
, a solution recovery strategy specific to
Graph Inspection
.
In this section, we extend the definition of a walk to allow consecutively repeated vertices.
For example,
(v,v,u)
can be considered as a walk,
and we treat the weight from
v
to
v
as
0
.

For an instance
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{C},w,\chi,s,t)
of
Graph Inspection
such that
G
is a complete metric graph,
we say an ordered set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is
an
optimal color order
if
there exists an optimal closed walk
W=(s,v_{1},\ldots,v_{t},s)

with possibly (consecutively) repeated vertices in
G

satisfying
c_{i}\in\chi(v_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq t
.
We also say that the walk
W
is
consistent with
the color order
C
.
The following lemma guarantees that any instance has an optimal color order of size
t

and an optimal walk consistent with that color order.

Lemma 8.1
(
\bigstar
)

Let
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{C},w,\chi,s,t)
be an instance of
Graph Inspection
such that
G
is a complete metric graph and
|\mathcal{C}|\geq t
.
Suppose
\ell
is the weight of an optimal walk.
Then, there exist an ordered color set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}\}

and a walk
W=(s,v_{1},\ldots,v_{t},s)
with weight
\ell
that is
consistent with
C
.

Our strategy is based on the following observation:
if we fix an
ordered
set of
t
colors to be collected,
then we can recover a minimum-weight walk in polynomial time.

The
TwoPhaseRecovery
algorithm takes as input
a recoverable circuit for
Graph Inspection
with respect to an optimal weight
\ell

and proceeds with the following two phases.
First, it finds an optimal color order from the given circuit.
Second, the algorithm constructs a minimum-weight walk
W
in
G
consistent with the obtained color order.
From
Lemma

8.1
,
W
must be an optimal walk with weight
\ell
and collect at least
t
colors.

Phase 1: Finding an optimal color order.

We determine an optimal color order in reverse.
Intuitively,
instead of using binary search to identify the previous vertex in a walk,
we use it to identify the previous color.
This enables an asymptotic speed-up of
\log_{2}n/\log_{2}|\mathcal{C}|
.

Lemma 8.2
(
\bigstar
)

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a recoverable circuit of
m
edges for an instance
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{C},w,\chi,s,t)
of
Graph Inspection
where
G
is a complete metric graph.
There exists a Las Vegas algorithm that finds an optimal color order
with expected running time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{t}tm)
.

Specifics depend on the circuit constructions.
Take
CompactCircuit
, for example.
Let
Y
be candidates for the final color collected and initialize it to
\mathcal{C}
.
We know that there must exist such a color
y\in\mathcal{C}
.
Now, we modify the circuit so that only the variables for
Y
are directly fed into the last layer.
This can be done by removing edges from
X\setminus Y
to the auxiliary nodes in the last layer,
where
X
denotes the set of variable nodes.
We run binary search on
Y
until
|Y|=1
, i.e., we find
y
.

Lastly, we remove all edges from
y
to all the auxiliary nodes except those in the last layer.
This ensures that we collect (
t-1
) other colors before collecting color
y
.
We then proceed to the (
t-1
)-th layer,
and by continuing this process, we can determine an ordered set of
t
colors
collected in an optimal walk.

Phase 2: Reconstructing a walk from ordered colors.

Given ordered colors
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}\}
, we can find a walk with the minimum weight in polynomial time
by a reduction to the classic
Shortest Path
problem.

Lemma 8.3
(
\bigstar
)

Let
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{C},w,\chi,s,t)
be an instance of
Graph Inspection
where
G
is an
n
-vertex complete metric graph.
There exists a
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(tn^{2})
-time algorithm that,
for a given color order
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
,
finds a walk
W=(s,v_{1},\ldots,v_{t},s)
such that
c_{i}\in\chi(v_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq t
,
and its weight is minimized.

Using
Lemma

8.2
and
Lemma

8.3
together,
we obtain the following. We defer the proofs to
Section

A.5
.

Lemma 8.4

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a recoverable circuit of
m
edges for an instance
(G=(V,E),\mathcal{C},w,\chi,s,t)
of
Graph Inspection
where
G
is a complete metric graph, and the target weight is
\ell
.
There exists a Las Vegas algorithm that finds a closed walk from
s
in
G
with weight at most
\ell
,
collecting at least
t
colors,
with an expected running time of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{t}tm+tn^{2})
.

Now we are ready to formally state our main result.

Theorem 9.1

If the edge weights are restricted to integers
and there exists a solution with weight at most

Consider running

The preprocessing steps described in

Step
(1)

In Step

\mathcal{O}(\ell tn^{2}+t|\mathcal{C}|n)

\ell

in
G

contains a tree certificate for

at most
t

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell tn^{2}+t|\mathcal{C}|n+t|\mathcal{C}|)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell tn^{2}+t|\mathcal{C}|n)

Lastly, in Step

\tilde{\ell}

11
Conclusion

In this paper we address the exponential space complexity of dynamic-programming algorithms for robotic motion planning by
designing a memory-efficient algorithm based on a reduction to
Multilinear Detection
. We introduce the concept of tree certificates, which
have broad implications for the recovery of multilinear monomials from arithmetic circuits, as well as several carefully designed application-specific algorithmic subroutines.
Our experiments verify that our algebraic approach is memory-efficient in practice, and provide evidence that
algebraic alternatives to dynamic programming are not as far from practicality as one might suspect.
We hope to inspire further research on both the theory and practice of these techniques; in particular, we wish to highlight
the potential of GPGPU acceleration, which has proven effective (albeit very complicated) for a related class of algebraic algorithms
[
11
]
.

A.1

We start by stating the following property of a tree certificate.

Proposition A.1

Let

We prove by induction on the number

For the base case with

For the inductive step,
consider

for an output node
v
.
Notice that
v
must be either an addition gate or a multiplication gate.

If
v
is an addition gate, we know that there exists

v

Due to the minimality of a certificate,

If

is a multiplication gate,
then for every in-neighbor

for every

.
Notice that since the underlying graph of

a_{v}^{(j)}
to
T^{\prime}(v,j)
and
add new edges from
b_{v}^{(j)}
to
T^{\prime}(v,j)
.
Observe that if
\mathcal{F}
has a tree certificate
including
a_{v}^{(j)}
and
T_{i,v,d,j}
, then

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
must have a tree certificate
including
b_{v}^{(j)}
and
T_{i,v,d,j}
,
and vise versa.
Hence,
\mathcal{F}
contains a tree certificate
if and only if
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
also contains a tree certificate
for any construction type.
Also, since every monomial at layer
i
has degree
i
,
a tree certificate in
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
must contain
one multiplication gate at layer
i
,
one node from
Y
, and one variable connected to
Y
.

Once the algorithm finds a color
c
for layer
i
,

Algorithm

7
invalidates the edges from the variable node
x_{c}

for color
c
to the layers up to
i-1
for reducing the circuit size.
This does not affect the correctness
because any tree certificate in
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
with
x_{c}

must include an edge from
x_{c}
to a node in
Y
,
and then the other edges from
x_{c}
are never used.
Invalidating unused edges does not affect the existence of a tree certificate (
Lemma

A.4
).

The analysis of running time is very similar to that for
LasVegasRecovery
.
Inside the outer loop, any modification of the circuit can be done in
O(m)
.
The overall running time is dominated by the running time of
Algorithm

7
,
and the expected number of executions of
Algorithm

7
is
\mathcal{O}(t\log k)

as we binary-search a color for each layer from
|\mathcal{C}|=k
colors.
Notice that this value is smaller than that of
LasVegasRecovery
(
\mathcal{O}(t\log n)
) if
k<n
.
Each execution of
Algorithm

7
takes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{t}m)
time,
so the total expected running time is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t\log k\cdot 2^{t}m)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{t}tm)
.

A.5.2
Reconstructing Walk from Color Order.

See
8.3

We construct an auxiliary
directed
graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
with edge weights
w^{\prime}:E^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as follows.

V^{\prime}
consists of
t
layers,
where layer
i
consists of vertices
U_{i}=\{u_{i,v}\colon v\in\chi^{-1}(c_{i})\}
,
which are copies of the vertices in
V
having color
c_{i}
.
We add two more vertices,
s
for the source and its copy
s^{\prime}
for the sink.
We connect these to internal vertices by
N^{+}(s)=U_{1}
and
N^{-}(s^{\prime})=U_{t}

and set edge weights to
w^{\prime}(s,u_{1,v})=w(s,v)
and
w^{\prime}(u_{t,v},s^{\prime})=w(v,s)
.
For
1\leq i\leq t
, we add edges from all of
U_{i}
to all of
U_{i+1}
.
Edge weights are set to
w^{\prime}(u_{i,v},u_{i+1,v^{\prime}})=w(v,v^{\prime})
,
where we define
w(v,v)=0
.
We will show that a shortest path from
s
to
s^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
corresponds to
a minimum-weight walk
W=(s,v_{1},\ldots,v_{t},s)
in
G

such that
c_{i}\in\chi(v_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq t
.
From
Lemma

8.1
, there must be an optimal walk with
t

internal vertices, with possibly repeated vertices.

First, observe that there is a one-to-one mapping
from a path
P=(s,u_{1,v_{1}},\ldots,u_{t,v_{t}},s^{\prime})
in
G^{\prime}

to a walk
W=(s,v_{1},\ldots,v_{t},s)
in
G
that is consistent with the given color order.
By construction,
v_{i}\in\chi^{-1}(c_{i})
for every
i
, and hence
c_{i}\in\chi(v_{i})
.
Also, from

w(s,v_{1})+w(v_{t},s)+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}w(v_{i},v_{i+1})=w^{\prime}(s,u_{1,v_{1}})+w^{\prime}(u_{t,v_{t}},s^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}w^{\prime}(u_{i,v_{i}},v_{i+1,v_{i+1}})
,
the weight of
P
is equal to the weight of
W
.

Suppose
\tilde{P}=(s,a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},s^{\prime})
be a shortest path from
s
to
s^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
.
By construction,
a_{1}\in U_{1}
,
a_{k}\in U_{t}
,
and each of the other edges increases the layer index by one.
So,
\tilde{P}
must be in the form of
(s,u_{1,v_{1}},\ldots,u_{t,v_{t}},s^{\prime})
.
This implies that
P
has the minimum weight, then
W
also has the minimum weight.

For the running time, constructing
G^{\prime}
and running Dijkstra’s algorithm on
G^{\prime}
take

\mathcal{O}(|E^{\prime}|+|V^{\prime}|\log|V^{\prime}|)
,
which is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(tn^{2})
since
|V^{\prime}|\leq tn+2
and
|E^{\prime}|\leq(t-1)n^{2}+2n
.
