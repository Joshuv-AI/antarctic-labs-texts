Melanoma treatment in the era of nanotechnology and precision medicine
Authors: Anderson Fraga da Cruz, Francesco Colella, Giuliana Grasso, Valentina Onesto, Stefania Forciniti, Beatriz B. Ortiz, Federica Carnevali, Anna Chiara Siciliano, Roberta Bove, Jacqueline Gonçalves dos Santos, Helena Iuele, Emanuele C. Zub, Giuseppe Gigli, Loretta L. del Mercato, Carolina Camargo de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Imagine the future where melanoma, one of the most lethal types of skin cancer, is not a frightful diagnosis anymore but a challenge met with tailored precision medicine. Despite advances, its treatment remains hindered by the triple barrier of metabolic reprogramming, immune escape, and inefficient nano-delivery. This review explores the transformative intersection of nanotechnology methodologies and precision medicine, addressing the central question: how these barriers can be overcome to achieve durable and personalized responses? Beyond summarizing current progress, it introduces the Metabolism–Immune–Nano Collaborative (MIN-C) Framework as an integrated perspective, where molecular insights, patient-specific data, and innovative nanomaterials converge to redefine care. The analysis begins by dissecting melanoma genetic and molecular complexities as a systemic disease, revealing the interplay between driver mutations, immune escape mechanisms, and the many players of the tumor microenvironment. The discussion shifts to the pivotal role of nanotechnology as a powerful ally breaking delivery barriers, amplifying immune responses, and enabling real-time monitoring of disease progression. This article proposes using the TME metabolic reprograming as a starting point for the design of nanoimmunotherapy, rather than an endpoint, complemented by precision medicine guiding decisions through biomarkers and genomic profiling, ensuring that each intervention is as unique as the patient it serves.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Over the past few decades, numerous advances in cancer treatment have reshaped modern oncology and gave patients hope for increased survival and better quality of life. Discoveries in the field of immunotherapy, precision medicine, and combined therapies have completely changed how cancer is treated. Nevertheless, melanoma is still among the most aggressive and treatment-resistant human malignancies. Many environmental and hereditary factors are related with melanoma occurrence, but all of them lead to mutations in important genes that control characteristics such as proliferation, cell death escape, and migration [1, 2]. Melanoma originates from the transformation of melanocytes, which are cells originated from neural crest progenitors. The main physiological role of these cells is the production and accumulation of melanin, a dark-brown pigment that protects skin from ultraviolet (UV) irradiation harmful effects, as a result of sun exposure. However, the role of melanin in early carcinogenesis, tumor progression, and metastasis is still not clear and debated [3].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) around 330,000 new cases of melanoma were diagnosed globally in 2022, and almost 60,000 people died from this disease [4]. Melanoma occurrence can significantly vary across different regions of the world and ethnic groups, and is mainly related to skin pigmentation, genetic factors, and environmental exposures such as UV radiation. Australia and New Zealand are leading the incidence rank (around 37–45.9 per 100,000 people) due to the predominance of fair-skinned populations and to geographical positioning that results in heightened UV radiation exposure, as well the significant public awareness, which contributes to increased early diagnosis. Following closely, are Northern Europe (between 10 and 25 cases per 100,000 people annually) and North America (21.2 new cases per 100,000 individuals annually). Additionally, gender differences exist, with men being more likely to develop melanoma, especially as they age. In general, melanoma distribution on the body varies according to sunburns pattern and gender. It is known that women tend to develop lesions on arms and legs while men on the back. Patients with a family history of the disease tend to develop lesions on the limbs, and truncal sites are associated with patients with a previous high amount of non-tumoral nevi [5, 6].

The primary treatment for localized melanoma is surgical excision. The skin tumor is removed with between 0.5 and 2 cm margin, according to the tumor cells depth. This process is associated with a high cure rate, particularly in early detected tumors, before other tissues invasion, and in such cases, lymph node dissection can be performed. This approach is often curative with a low recurrence rate. However, the main problem with melanoma lies in its metastatic stage [7]. As a comparison, patients with locally detected melanoma present a 98% survival rate. When diagnosed with regional or distant melanoma lesions, these values drop to 64% and 23%, respectively [8].

Over the last 50 years, melanoma treatment has greatly improved with the development and approval of assorted therapeutic approaches. The first drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration agency (FDA) for metastatic melanoma treatment was dacarbazine, a chemotherapeutic agent, in 1975. This alkylating agent showed limited success, with an average survival that ranged from 5 to 11 months, and an overall 1-year survival of 27%. All the subsequent attempts to improve melanoma chemotherapy for the subsequent 20 years involved the coadministration of dacarbazine with other drugs, such as cisplatin and vinblastine [9]. In 1992, the FDA approved the administration of high Interleukin-2 dosage, which could be considered the first immunotherapeutic approach, showing a complete response in 6% of patients and high systemic toxicity. Nonetheless, the real turning point in melanoma treatment was obtained in 2011. In the same year three new drugs were approved: ipilimumab, the first checkpoint inhibitor was approved on March 25; pegIntron, a PEGylated recombinant interferon alfa-2b, was approved on March 29; and vemurafenib, the first BRAF kinase inhibitor specific for BRAF mutants, was approved on August 17. Vemurafenib proved its efficacy by promptly progressing through clinical phases and showing a striking result in a randomized phase III trial, with an overall response rate of 48% compared to the 5% obtained with dacarbazine [9]. Unfortunately, all that glitter was not gold, and almost all patients treated with the BRAF inhibitor rapidly showed resistance. The mechanism behind such resistance is still not clear and seems to be caused by multiple mutations and by the high heterogeneity of melanoma cells. Given the circumstances, the research on melanoma is currently focused on improving precision medicine, which is the medical approach that tailors disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment based on the individual characteristics of each patient. Instead of using a one-size-fits-all strategy, precision medicine takes into account factors such as genetics, environmental exposure and personal health data [10]. In the view of personalized treatment, nanotechnology offers great potential for the translation of this knowledge in clinical practice. Nanomaterials were widely explored for tailored drug delivery, in order to improve the efficacy and selectivity of a pharmacological treatment based on the distinctive profile of patients [11, 12]. However, the application of these materials is not only restricted to drug delivery; promising results were also obtained in the field of diagnostics and, by coupling both diagnostic and therapeutic application, in what is called theranostics [13, 14]. In this review article, we describe the latest updates on the implementation of nanotechnology in the precision medicine of melanoma, covering topics from preclinical to clinical experimentation, as well as many challenges, future applications, and concerns.

Melanoma: genetic, biological, and clinical aspects

Melanoma is characterized by a wide genetic diversity, metastatic capability, and clinical heterogeneity. The genetic landscape of skin melanoma reveals recurrent mutations in genes such as BRAF, the most common mutation in this case, that regulate essential cellular signaling pathways. Based on it, target therapy has been implemented and more recently, protocols combining BRAF and MEK inhibitors have improved patient progression-free survival. However, therapeutic resistance remains a significant challenge. This landscape becomes even more complex when examining rare melanoma subtypes, such as acral, uveal, and mucosal melanomas. They exhibit unique biological characteristics and clinical diversity leading to poorer prognoses and less effectiveness to conventional therapies.

Metabolic plasticity is another significant melanoma trait that contributes to disease progression. Melanoma tumor cells alternate between aerobic glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, adapting to adverse conditions in the microenvironment, such as hypoxia. These metabolic changes support the tumor energy demand, favoring cell survival, and contributing to resistance to conventional therapies, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy [15, 16].

Paradoxically to genetic and molecular challenges, the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in melanoma progression. Immunotherapy, with the programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) and CTLA-4 inhibitors, has significantly improved overall survival in advanced cases [17]. However, the immunosuppressive microenvironment, with the presence of regulatory T cells and tumor-associated macrophages, limits the effectiveness of therapies, impairing anti-tumor immune response and promoting therapeutic resistance. Combined strategies, such as immunotherapy and radiotherapy, show the potential to overcome these challenges. Radiation can help increase tumor antigens visibility and activate T cells, improving immune response. However, it also contributes to microenvironment immunosuppression the, which can limit the effectiveness of treatment [18].

Recent advances in genomics, tumor biology, and immunology have enabled a more comprehensive understanding of the disease, impacting clinical practice by influencing staging systems and prognostic indicators [19] and immensely contributing to more effective therapeutic strategies. Based on that, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) provides guidelines for melanoma classification, considering several aspects including tumor thickness, the presence of ulceration, and lymphatic involvement. In advanced stages, biomarkers such as LDH predict prognosis, enabling risk stratification and guiding more efficient personalized treatments [20].

Although the therapies and patients treatment regimen were greatly improved, recurrence and acquired resistance require continuous research and personalized approaches to improve therapeutic outcomes [21]. Current knowledge on melanoma genetic heterogeneity, metabolic signature, and TME will be further discussed on the next topics.

Genetic landscape of cutaneous melanoma

Melanoma genetic landscape is known as remarkably complex, due to the highest mutation burden among all cancer types. This significant mutation load is driven primarily by UV radiation exposure (in people with higher phenotypic predisposition), causing DNA alterations [22, 23]. Among the most common are C >T and CC >TT transition, commonly referred as a UV signature mutation, and are present in around 70% of melanoma cases [24]. Hereditary factors account for only 10% of the cases [25].

Melanoma susceptibility genes are pivotal in understanding the genetic basis of melanoma risk. They can be categorized into three groups: high, medium, and low penetrance genes, based on their impact on melanoma development. The high penetrance genes are associated to hereditary forms of the disease, while the medium with genes where susceptible alleles interact. Low penetrance genes are those related to most of the population, where melanoma develops through environmental and genotypic causes [25]. Key driver mutations in melanoma cells disturb the regulation of known and important pathways, especially those involved with cell proliferation and survival within tissues such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway (MAPK).

BRAF, NRAS, and NF-1 mutations, all related to MAPK pathway, are commonly seen as points of interest as driver mutations [26, 27]. Approximately half of all cutaneous melanoma cases are related to mutations in the BRAF gene, being the V600E substitution the most common, resulting in MAPK pathway activation culminating in sustained cell proliferation. NRAS mutation, present in roughly 20% of all melanoma cases, and NF-1 loss-of-function mutation, present in 10%, also contribute to abnormal MAPK [28, 29]. Cells that lack these mutations are considered triple wild-type (TWT) and are related to other genetic mutations, such as KIT, GNAQ, EZH2, MET, PARD3, SF3B1, GNA11, CTNNB1, CDKN2A, and TP53 [30–32]. In the case of familial melanoma syndrome, which is characterized by the presence of multiple atypical nevi, conferring a substantial risk of melanoma, 20 to 40% of cases are CDKN2A mutated together with other high-penetrance genes. Polygenic variants contribute to the emerging risk of melanoma, and it is possible that other high penetrant mutations are still unknown for these cases [25, 33, 34]. Given the advances in uncovering the complex genetic landscape of melanoma through next-generation sequencing, Mirek et al. consider that not only 3, but 22 genes should be mandatory in melanoma-specific genomic tests to personalize treatment based on patients’ genetic background, and proposed a gene panel comprising drivers, therapy-candidates, and predictive markers [35]. Some of these other genes can be used as predictive genes, like RAC1P29S, for BRAF/MEK inhibitors resistance and CTNNB1S45F that is related to lower responses to immunotherapy [36].

Beyond coding mutations, non-coding regions also play a role in melanoma development and progression. Non-coding alterations in the TERT promoter are of particular interest, as it is related to an increase in telomerase activity, enabling cellular immortality. Non-coding RNAs - that include microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs – also have been implicated in melanoma key pathways modulation, extending the importance beyond protein-coding regions [37, 38]. Additionally, some epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation, histone modification and chromatin remodeling [39] were described. Genes such as ARID1A and SMARCA4, involved in chromatin remodeling, are frequently mutated in melanoma, underscoring the significance of these characteristics. Recent studies emphasize the significance of personalized medicine in oncology, particularly through the clinical application of genetic panels based on Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) [40]. These panels help identify unique genetic variations that influence treatment responses in individual patients, enhancing therapeutic strategies. By understanding a patient genetic landscape, such as those outlined in the context of cutaneous melanoma, clinicians can offer more precise treatments tailored to the tumor genetic profile. This personalized approach has become a cornerstone in improving patient outcomes and advancing cancer treatment. Using NGS panel, a study identified alterations in 260 advanced melanoma samples, presenting alterations in genes as BRAF and TERT (44% and 59,9% of the samples, respectively). Finally, this panel showed only 1,2% failing analyzing 229 amplicons in 25 oncogene/oncosuppressor genes [41].

Distinct profile of rare melanoma subtypes

Treatment options for patients with rare types of melanomas, such as acral, mucosal and uveal melanoma, are still very limited [42, 43], given their atypical locations and distinct biological characteristics, in addition to a worse long-term prognosis [42].

Acral lentiginous melanoma (ALM) is a cutaneous melanoma subtype that appears on palmar and plantar surfaces as well as on nail beds, and its development is not associated with cumulative sun damage (CSD) [44, 45]. Common sites for ALM metastases include the lungs, distant lymph nodes, scalp, contralateral limbs, and liver [46]. While its exact cause is still unclear, mechanical stress is often considered a contributing factor [47]. ALM differs from other melanomas as it has a relatively low rate of point mutations and fewer UV-associated genetic changes, indicating that UV radiation plays little to no role in its development [44, 48], but in some cases can affect partially transformed cells that expand into sun-exposed regions and then receive UV radiation. The lack of point mutations in ALM is contrasted by a high copy number burden and structural aberrations, such as the presence of multiple amplifications, deletions, and chromosomal rearrangements [48]. ALM frequently involves mutations in NF-1 and the KIT proto-oncogene, along with amplifications in genes like CCND1, PAK1, GAB2, CDK4, MDM2, and TERT promoting cell proliferation and immortality [48, 49]. Mutations in BRAF and NRAS are less common, which makes most ALM patients unsuitable for BRAF or BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapies [44, 50]. While therapies targeting KIT and CDK4/6 offer some potential these strategies still have their effectiveness limited by the activation of resistance mechanisms [43]. Activating mutations in the MAP kinase pathway arise during ALM progression, and are not initiating mutations, as in the case of cutaneous melanomas. This manifestation leads to intratumoral heterogeneity, where mutations in BRAF V600E or NRAS Q61 are present in some areas of the tumor and absent in others [48].

It is known that cellular interactions that occur in the TME underlie tumor immunogenicity, being essential to determine tumor resistance or susceptibility to different therapeutic strategies [51]. In the case of ALM, TME is characterized by being extremely immunosuppressive and more heterogeneous when compared to cutaneous melanoma. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and exhausted CD8 + T cells, M2 macrophages, and cancer-associated fibroblasts are increased, with few natural killer (NK) cells, and almost no presence of γδ T cells. Oncogenic pathways activation converging to CXCL8 (or IL-8), a cytokine that recruits suppressor myeloid cells and related to the low efficacy of ICI therapies is also observed [52–56]. According to Li et al., the greater infiltration of CD4 + B and T cells and reduction of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, monocytes and macrophages is related to metastases occurrence in this type of melanoma [52]. Wei et al. demonstrated the occurrence of certain intratumoral heterogeneity, with less prominence of stromal cells, NK cells and effector CD8 + and CD4 + cells in metastatic than primary microenvironments, showing greater impairment of the antitumor response in metastatic sites [53].

Mucosal melanoma (MM) develops in mucous membranes of the nose, nasal sinuses, mouth, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital tract [43, 45, 57]. When compared to cutaneous melanoma, MM is more aggressive and has a worse prognosis [58, 59]. The detection of MM is challenging due to its hidden location, which makes visual detection difficult and often delays diagnosis until advanced stages. On average, MM is diagnosed around the age of 70. The sites where MM typically occur are less exposed to UV radiation, which influences its distinct pathogenesis and risk factors when compared to cutaneous melanomas [42, 60, 61]. In MM, tumors have a low load of point mutations and are characterized by genomic instability, presenting higher rates of structural variations with amplifications in CDK4, MDM2, KIT, and TERT genes [49], the latter being associated with telomeric changes [62]. MM is less likely to involve mutations in BRAF and tends to exhibit alterations in genes such as KIT and NRAS [59, 63]. Furthermore, mutations in SF3B1 are common in female genital and anorectal melanomas, and mutations seen in CTNNB1 are related to aberrant signaling in the WNT pathway [62]. Treatment often depends on tumor location, with surgical excision being the initial step [43]. Given its aggressive nature, MM typically requires a combination of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic treatments [63]. Furthermore, studies suggest that the use of c-KIT and BRAF inhibitors is beneficial for patients with mutations in these genes [59].

MM has a lower mutational load than cutaneous melanoma that contributes to low immunogenicity and, consequently, to the lower immunological infiltration observed in this type of tumor [64]. Thus, the TME of MM is characterized by decreased infiltration of immune cells and increased cellular components that contribute to angiogenesis and tumor mitogenic signaling [65]. The presence of CD8 + T cells and B cells are favorable prognostic factors in melanoma [66, 67], with better responses to immunotherapy. Furthermore, patients with greater infiltration of CAFs, macrophages, and higher levels of TGF-β respond less to ICI therapies, confirming the important role of the immune system in mechanisms of resistance and response to therapies [64].

Uveal melanoma (UM) develops from melanocytes within the eyes’ uveal tract and is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults [68–70]. The treatment for primary tumors includes brachytherapy, phototherapy, or surgical resection. Distant metastases are a frequent complication and tend to happen most commonly in the liver [71, 72]. Compared to cutaneous melanomas, UM has a lower overall mutation burden, except for iris melanomas, which exhibit a high mutational load and may be responsive to immunotherapy, in addition to being easier to detect [73]. Mutations in BRAF or NRAS, are rare, instead, the key driver mutations in UM usually occur in the G-protein related genes, GNAQ and GNA11, which can lead to aberrant MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [68, 69]. BAP1, PLCB4, CYSLTR2, SF3B1, and EIF1AX genes are also implicated in MM initiation as driver mutations [73]. A significant advancement in UM treatment came in 2022 with the FDA approval of tebentafusp, a bispecific fusion protein targeting gp100, used to treat HLA-A*02:01-positive adults with unresectable or metastatic disease [68].

In UM, high intratumoral immunological activity, such as high infiltration of TAMs and TILs, is associated with a worse prognosis. In the TME, infiltrating leukocytes are mostly activated CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages [74–76]. Patients with progressive disease have a high number of M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells, few CTL lymphocytes and a reduced stromal CD8/CD4 ratio, characterizing an impaired antitumor response. Patients responsive to immunotherapy have a lower percentage of Treg lymphocytes and a lower Treg/CTL ratio [75, 77].

The distinct genetic landscape of rare melanoma subtypes underscores the need for tailored therapeutic strategies. Advances in many fronts, such as KIT and CDK4/6 inhibitors for acral and mucosal melanoma, and tebentafusp for uveal melanoma, highlight the potential of advances in precision medicine [78]. However, resistance mechanisms and the immunosuppressive TME remain significant challenges. Further research is essential to refine targeted approaches and improve outcomes for these aggressive melanoma subtypes [79].

Metabolic adaptations in melanoma cells

Metabolic reprogramming is a common feature of most cancer cells, and it represents an obvious adaptation to survive in a hostile environment. The lack of vascularization in neoplasia generally means hypoxia, low nutrient perfusion, metabolite accumulation, and acidosis. These environmental conditions are the main trigger for metabolic rewiring of cancer cells, or at least the phenotypic selection for more adapted cancer cells [80, 81]. The major metabolic pathways involved in melanoma metabolic rewiring and their interrelationship can be seen in Fig. 1. Even though melanoma shares common metabolic features with other types of cancer, it also displays some tissue-specific characteristics, such as melanogenesis. One of the primary roles of melanin is to protect the skin from harmful UV radiation, which is also a major regulator of its biosynthesis, along with different hormonal factors at tissue, cellular, and subcellular levels [3]. However, several evidence shows how melanin could also play an ambiguous role in melanoma onset, by providing both a protective effect against radiation but also a mutagenic stimulus for further DNA mutations, due to highly reactive intermediates and oxygen species (ROS) [81].

The most known metabolic feature of cancer cells is called the Warburg effect, which implies the leverage of anaerobic glycolysis as the major source of ATP, instead of the oxidative phosphorylation that takes place in mitochondria. Warburg observed that cancer cells produced high amounts of L-lactic acid in the presence of oxygen, suggesting a dysfunction in oxidative phosphorylation. The NADH + H+ produced during glycolysis is then oxidized to regenerate NAD+ and produce L-lactic acid from pyruvic acid [82]. This pivotal reprogramming in glucose metabolism provides a fast source of energy and intermediates for the anabolism of highly proliferating cells in hypoxic conditions. One of the key mediators for this effect is the hypoxia-inducible factor 1 or HIF-1, which is a transcription factor that responds to the decreased concentration of oxygen in cells [83]. Indeed, exposure to UV radiation can trigger HIF-mediated adaptation in melanocytes [84]. Slominski et al. proved how the simple addition of the melanin precursor, L-tyrosine, in the cell culture medium was able to increase the HIF-1α expression in melanotic hamster AbC1 and human SKMEL-188 cells [3]. HIF-1α is responsible for the regulation of some of the key players in the glycolytic pathway, indeed, among the numerous hypoxia-response elements (HREs) there are: glucose transporter 1 and 3 (GLUT1 and GLUT3) [85–88], lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) [89, 90], hexokinase 2 (HK2) [90, 91], and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isozyme 1 (PDK1) [92–94]. LDH has been widely studied as a cancer biomarker and was the first prognostic blood biomarker included in the AJCC staging system for metastatic melanoma patients, since high LDH levels in the serum are associated with poor prognosis, cancer progression, and metastasis [95]. However, elevated LDH levels in the serum of melanoma patients primarily result from its release by necrotic glycolytic cells, limiting its specificity.

There are other proteins involved in the glycolytic pathway that are known to be dysregulated in melanoma, and some of the most important are: phosphofructokinase (PFK) [96, 97], pyruvate kinase (PK) [98, 99], mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPCs) [100, 101], that regulate the entrance of pyruvate inside mitochondria, monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) [89, 102] which regulate the passage of L-lactic acid across cell membranes, and the GPR81 or hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1 (HCA1), which is a membrane G-protein coupled receptor for lactic acid [103, 104]. MCT1 (that mediates lactate influx) and MCT4 (that mediates lactate efflux) may play a role in what is called “metabolic symbiosis”, which implies the presence of glycolytic cells in the tumor hypoxic region and oxidative cells in the normoxic tumor region. The concomitant existence of these two cell phenotypes should guarantee the survival of both, with glycolytic cells using glucose as the main source of energy while providing L-lactic acid to fuel the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) in oxidative cells. The symbiosis between heterogeneous cells inside the same tumor mass might change the outdated paradigm that confined lactic acid as a metabolic waste [89, 105].

The key role of glycolysis in melanoma was confirmed by the discovery of BRAF role in glucose metabolism regulation. Nonetheless, BRAF was found to regulate HK2, GLUT1 and 3, which could explain the decreased glycolytic rate found in BRAF V600E mutant compared to BRAF WT [106]. Glycolysis also seemed to be involved in the development of drug-resistance toward BRAF inhibitors, indeed, melanoma cells, which developed BRAFi-resistance, displayed an increased glycolytic rate compared to the original phenotype. Indeed, recently, the coadministration of vemurafenib and dichloroacetate (a glycolytic suppressor) to A375 melanoma cells improved the cytotoxic effect in a synergistic way [107].

Despite mitochondria are classically considered impaired or “broken” in cancer cells, they are not inactive at all [108]. However, the role of mitochondria in carcinogenesis and cancer progression is still unclear. These organelles are the main ROS producers, providing a mutagenic environment for both nDNA and mtDNA. Somatic mtDNA mutations commonly occur in human tumors, with an incidence rate greater than 50% and are usually associated with a more glycolytic phenotype [109]. The accumulation of mtDNA mutations in melanoma cells reduced the aggressive behavior of cancer cells and the ability to produce metastasis [110]. Recent studies have proved how melanoma cells are able to transfer their defective mitochondria to tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), via tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) and extracellular vesicles (EVs). This discovery highlighted a new way by which cancer cells can evade the immune system, producing anergic T cells by the direct poisoning of TILs with impaired mitochondria [111, 112]. Evidence shows how even alterations in the expression pattern of mitochondrial carriers are involved in cancer proliferation and invasiveness. For instance, the aspartate-glutamate carrier 1 (AGC1) proved to be involved in the formation of lung metastasis in B16-F10 mouse melanoma model [113, 114]. Melanoma cells exploit glutamine for both energy production, in a process called glutaminolysis, and for biosynthetic reaction via anaplerosis [115, 116]. Glutamine can enter the TCA as oxoglutarate and proceed in both directions, in the classical way for energy production or in the reverse TCA, to produce acetyl-CoA and thus lipid biosynthesis.

The metabolic rewiring of melanoma also implies increased lipid biosynthesis and uptake, which is essential as these cells require high lipid turnover to support rapid proliferation and invasive profile [117]. The accumulation of different fatty acids, especially in the form of lipid droplets, was able to affect melanogenesis in melanoma [118]. Palmitic acid displayed a stimulating effect on melanin production, whereas polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) like docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), reduced melanogenesis [119]. Besides, melanoma cells are also capable of escaping lipotoxicity typically associated with fatty acids accumulation, in a way not fully characterized yet [120]. The FASN enzyme was found upregulated in melanoma cells and proved how its pharmacological inhibition with orlistat or cerulenin reduced cell proliferation and activated apoptosis [121]. COX-2 is an enzyme responsible for the conversion of AA into eicosanoids, and it was proved to be overexpressed in melanoma. It seemed to be involved in cancer progression and metastasis [122, 123]. There are other lipids and sphingolipids that seem to be altered in cancer metabolism, however, their role still needs to be investigated [117]. A better understanding of the metabolic adaptations and the interplay between the heterogeneous population of cancer cells is crucial for the development of new treatments, especially in the perspective of precision medicine [124]. Metabolic changes occurring between cells present in the TME, whether tumor or immune cells, lead to nutrient scarcity, promoting acidification of the environment. This acidification promoted by the Warburg effect alters the behavior of immune cells within the tumor, potentially altering the infiltration and function of natural killer cells, dendritic cells, CD8 + T lymphocytes, TILS, and TAMs phenotypes, where metabolic reprogramming facilitates the immune evasion of melanoma, training immune cells in the TME to favor tumor progression. It is known that the TME plays an essential role in cellular responses to therapies, and the literature shows that metabolic pathways are a promising strategy for altering the immunosuppressive state of the TME and impairing tumor cell homeostasis [125–128]. Yang et al. developed a peroxynitrite nanogenerator, leading to a decrease in lactate and promoting M1 macrophage infiltration, a decrease in Tregs, and an increase in CD8 + T lymphocytes [128]. Luo et al. developed an effective Fe3O4 nanoimmunopotentiator for immunotherapy in the form of vaccines, which were able to activate mature BMDCs, stimulate T cells, and promote macrophage activation in vitro [129]. Brin et al. showed that an ADI-PEG 20 nanoplatform induces tumor T cell infiltration in mouse models of melanoma and colon cancer [130]. Thus, metabolic plasticity, together with microenvironmental limitations, must be considered in the development of anti-melanoma therapies in order to overcome resistance and increase antitumor efficacy.

TME and immune dynamics in melanoma

The accumulation of mutations is essential for melanoma cell survival and proliferation; however, the TME plays a significant role in both carcinogenesis and cancer progression [131, 132]. A deep understanding of the TME spatial heterogeneity is essential for discovering new treatment and for comprehending the mechanisms of resistance to targeted therapy. The TME is a complex and dynamic network of various cellular and non-cellular components, including tumor cells, cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial and immune cells, signaling molecules, and blood vessels. All these components, which have each distinct molecular characteristics, are embedded in a dense stroma of extracellular matrix (ECM) made of type I collagen, hyaluronic acid, fibronectin, and growth factors (Fig. 2a) [133, 134]. Tumor cells actively interact with the surrounding TME in a bidirectional manner, exchanging molecular signals that promote and modulate the malignant phenotype [135]. CAFs have been proven to be a key TME constituent that affects both tumorigenesis and response to therapies, particularly because of their intra-tumor heterogeneity [136]. A recent study identified for the first time in melanoma the presence of three distinct CAF subsets and their association with tumor malignancy [137]. By applying molecular and spatial single-cell analysis, the authors reveal the existence of myofibroblast-like RGS5 + CAFs, matrix CAFs (mCAFs), and immunomodulatory CAFs (iCAFs) that critically impact the TME. Both mCAFs and iCAFs display immunomodulatory properties. Notably, mCAFs contribute to the deposition of extracellular matrix in the tumor-stroma and play a role in restricting T cell invasion in early-stage melanomas. iCAFs, instead, are more abundant in advanced melanoma and promote immune cells activation by expressing elevated levels of cytokines and chemokines. On the other hand, CAFs play a critical role in remodeling the ECM by secreting metalloproteinases (MMPs) which activate pathways for promoting cell dissemination and create a tumor-supportive environment [138]. In addition to functioning as tumor cells support, the ECM drives and promotes cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions and it is also implicated in different molecular pathways that regulate cell proliferation and differentiation [139]. Beyond this high degree of heterogeneity and plasticity regarding CAFs, other studies focused on distinct subpopulations of cells within the TME that support melanoma growth and metastatic dissemination [80, 140]. Importantly, Karras et al. demonstrated through single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analysis that these abilities are limited to only a small pool of cells. In particular, the tumorigenic property is associated with a localized perivascular niche, established by an intercellular crosstalk between endothelial cells [141]. Moreover, temporal single-cell tracing of a fraction of melanoma cells with a mesenchymal-like state proved that these cells represent a part of metastatic initiating cells that change their features while spreading to secondary sites. These observations revealed the presence of dynamic changes and interactions within the TME that can activate several pro-invasive programs, such as an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, leading to migration and metastatic dissemination [142, 143]. The activation of these pathways enhances the release of a variety of cytokines and growth factors that have a potential involvement in either promoting or suppressing the various stages of tumor [144]. The main factors include the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), involved in angiogenesis, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) which modulates immune response and matrix remodeling, interleukins, chemokines, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) that mediate immune cell activity and inflammation [145]. In melanoma TME, melanocytes also secrete the basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) that autocrinally influences their behaviour by promoting proliferation and survival [146]. Indeed, the secretion of bFGF is related to various aspects of tumor progression, such as tumor growth and immune evasion [147].

One of the peculiar features of melanoma TME is its immunogenic profile, due to the presence of a consistent lymphocytic infiltrate that creates a dynamic crosstalk with cancer cells by activating or modulating the adaptive immune response [148]. This immune infiltrate consists of dendritic cells (DCs), tumor-infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs) including CD8 + cytotoxic T-cells, CD4 + Helper T cells, regulatory T-cells (Tregs), and natural killer cells (NKs), B-cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), that participate to create both pro- or anti-tumor environments [149–151]. However, the immunogenicity of melanoma and thus the induction of adaptive immune responses, which allow them to prevent tumor growth, is progressively lost and the balance between survival and proliferation is often deregulated [152].

During melanoma initiation, both immune and tumor cells go through the immunoediting event that consists of interconnected phases such as elimination (or immunosurveillance), equilibrium between immune and tumor cells, and escape or immune evasion [153, 154]. In this scenario, DCs are the main actors that indirectly contrast the growth and survival of melanoma cells [155]. Moreover, they are crucial for inducing adaptive immunity through antigen presentation to T cells via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules (Fig. 2b), but they are also involved in maintaining tolerance and preventing autoimmunity [156]. In the TME, DCs generally surround tumor cells, constituting the so-termed immune infiltrate that is considered a positive indicator to immunotherapy response. A key role in the anti-tumor activity of TME is accomplished by T-cells, particularly CD8 + T cells that directly target and kill melanoma cells by recognizing their specific tumor-associated antigens via MHC class I molecules [157].The function of CD8 + T cells is enhanced by CD4 + T cells that also contribute to the anti-tumor immunity by activating pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages through the secretion of several cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-12, and TNF-α [158–160]. However, macrophages undergo a polarization towards an anti-inflammatory M2 state and therefore to a pro-tumor phenotype that is referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [161]. TAMs, which are predominantly M2-like, contribute to immune suppression and tumor progression by producing cytokines (e.g. TGF- β, IL-10, and VEGF) which promote tumor growth and angiogenesis, and secrete MMPs, which facilitate invasion and metastasis (Fig. 2b) [162, 163]. Despite the accentuated anti-tumor immunity and the pro-tumorigenic role of TAMs, melanoma implements different strategies for immune evasion, such as the immune checkpoint regulation [164, 165]. For instance, tumor cells in melanoma TME upregulate programmed death ligand 1 (PDL-1) which interacts with its receptor PD-1 expressed on CD8 + T cells, leading to the inhibition of T-cell activation and then to immune evasion [166, 167]. Moreover, Tregs and MDSCs represent crucial contributors to immune suppression by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines or inflammatory mediators (e.g. TGF-β, IL-10, NO) in the TME and helping melanoma cells to escape immune detection (Fig. 2c) [168, 169]. Although various strategies targeting immune checkpoints, Tregs or MDSCs are becoming important for developing efficient immunotherapies, an unfailing clinical benefit remains confined to a subset of melanoma patients [170]. This therapeutic failure is mainly due to immune and genomic contributors, as well as intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity that significantly leads to tumor-immune evasion [171]. In this scenario, Mitra et al. revealed a pronounced relationship between genomic and immune heterogeneity through a spatially detailed analysis of 67 sub-areas sampled from one entire tumor and three metastatic lesions. The molecular and spatial analysis demonstrated an important correlation between a specific patient genetic feature, the copy number gain of chromosome 7, and an adverse immune component dominated by neutrophil activation [172]. Notably, this link is pronounced in metastatic melanoma patients who do not respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors therapy.

Current staging systems of melanoma

Older staging systems were based on the classification of melanoma thickness; the Breslow depth involves thickness starting from the skin surface to the deepest site of the tumor. Meanwhile, the Clark scale describes the anatomical invasion of melanoma, by using a staging system based on five invasion levels [20, 173]. The currently used cancer staging system was formulated by the AJCC and updated in its latest version, implemented in 2018 [174, 175]. It is based on the “TNM” classification, which relies on primary tumor thickness (T), tumor spread to nearby lymph nodes (N), and the presence of distant metastasis (M). Therefore, staging involves the combination of graded TNM characteristics ranging from T1, when the primary tumor is less than 1 mm thick to T4 being thicker than 4 mm; N0 when no regional lymph nodes affected to N3 when they are affected; and the absence (M0) or presence of metastasis (M1). The stage grouping process represents the combination of these specific categories and it is crucial for an overall staging of the patient [20, 176].

Finally, attention to prognostic factors is crucial in understanding the characteristics of primary and metastatic melanoma. Therapeutic challenges in late melanoma

Currently, tumor staging plays a crucial role in determining therapy decisions for melanoma. In the early stages of melanoma (stages I and II), surgery is often the primary treatment approach. To guarantee the complete removal of cancer cells, a wide local excision is performed, involving melanoma removal along with a margin of surrounding healthy tissue [178, 179]. For melanomas with a higher likelihood of spreading, a sentinel lymph node biopsy is commonly employed to determine whether cancer cells have migrated to neighboring lymph nodes. However, due to significant genetic heterogeneity, metastases, and drug resistance, late-stage melanomas are still challenging to treat [180]. Chemotherapy, immunotherapy and radiotherapy are also used after surgery for high-risk melanoma to reduce the chance of recurrence. All the drugs clinically approved for the post-surgery treatment of melanoma are listed in Table 1.

The causes of resistance to therapeutic treatment are multiple and associated with several factors. Melanoma cell plasticity relies on changes in ECM, on the influence of immune cells and CAFs, and on the metastatic cascade, which promotes treatment resistance [181]. Consequently, it has been proposed that alterations in gene expression and adaptive phenotypic cell plasticity are the main causes of melanoma resistance to treatment, in addition to genetic evolution [182]. However, not all the dynamic alterations seen at the transcriptional and phenotypic levels throughout the evolution of melanoma can be attributed to oncogenic mutations. During metastatic dissemination, a variety of cells with a distinctive stable phenotype may develop within a single melanoma [183]. These cell types differ in their molecular and functional profiles. Furthermore, melanoma can evolve into drug-tolerant phenotypes that are challenging to treat, and such phenotypic heterogeneity can promote interconversion between phenotypic states in response to pharmacological challenges [184].

For over 30 years, dacarbazine has been the first-line chemotherapeutic for advanced metastatic melanoma, since it has been the most effective drug. Several therapeutic approaches are currently available for melanoma metastatic disease (see Table 1) [185, 186]. Current pharmacological options are immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapy [187, 188]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors target checkpoint proteins, which are expressed by cancer cells and immune cells [187–189]. PD-L1 and CD80 are two examples of checkpoints used to evade the immune system by suppressing T-cell activation, which results in immunological resistance. Among this class of inhibitors, the most used are nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and ipilimumab. According to the CHECKMATE-037 study, nivolumab proved to be more effective than standard chemotherapy (dacarbazine or paclitaxel combined with carboplatin) in patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma who had previously received treatment [186, 190]. Pembrolizumab proved to be more effective than chemotherapy in patients with previously treated metastatic and unresectable melanoma (in the KEYNOTE-006 study) [191]. However, several molecular changes occurring during the development of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) resistance need to be investigated. Recent studies have demonstrated that about 25% of ICB-resistant cases have genetic mutations related to interferon gamma signaling pathways and antigen presentation [192]. Multiplex spatial analysis and T cell receptor (TCR) clonality studies indicate that anti-CTLA4 resistant lesions have a persistent immunological response, including immune-regulatory characteristics. Anti-PD-1 resistant tumors, instead, are typically immunologically deficient with non-expanded TCR clones. In particular, PD1 + CD8 + T cell fractions are lower in anti-PD-1 resistant tumors than in ICB naive-metastases [192]. Target therapy includes the class of BRAF inhibitors, which is a serine/threonine protein kinase that triggers the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway. Among this therapeutic class, dabrafenib is a kinase inhibitor used for the treatment of metastatic or unresectable melanoma with BRAF V660K or V600E mutation [186]. Trametinib, encorafenib, and binimetinib, instead, are MEK inhibitors approved for the management of metastatic BRAF V660K or V600E mutated unresectable melanoma or as adjuvant treatment [193]. In addition, vemurafenib is another targeted inhibitor used in monotherapy for the treatment of metastatic BRAF V600E melanoma with brain metastases, due to its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier [194]. Nevertheless, almost all patients treated with BRAF/MEK inhibitors relapse within two years. Melanoma cell proliferation and carcinogenesis require androgen receptor (AR) activation. AR overexpression has been observed in BRAFi-resistant melanomas, thus causing transcriptional alterations, such as increased expression of EGFR and SERPINE1, which are probably clinically significant. In fact, inhibition of AR expression or activity enhances the infiltration of CD8 + T lymphocytes and cancer cell death [195].

Numerous drugs are under investigation to address the acquired resistance to both immunotherapy and targeted therapy. Among these, talimogene laherparepvec (TVEC) was the first clinically approved oncolytic virus, utilized for the local treatment of stage IV melanoma and unresectable metastatic stage IIIB/C [196]. Today, these approaches provide much higher efficacy and longer-lasting outcomes for advanced cases, compared to traditional treatments.

Nanotechnology in precision medicine for melanoma

Nanotechnology, which involves the manipulation of materials at the nanoscale (typically 1–100 nanometers), has emerged as a groundbreaking tool in precision medicine. In particular, nanomedicine offers innovative strategies for targeted delivery, early detection, and personalized treatment, addressing the significant clinical challenges posed by melanoma, such as rapid metastasis.

One of the primary applications of nanotechnology in melanoma precision medicine is in the development of targeted drug delivery systems. Traditionally, systemic chemotherapy with agents such as dacarbazine or temozolomide has been used, but these drugs show limited response rates and significant systemic toxicity [197]. Melanoma cells overexpress specific molecular markers that can be exploited by nanoparticles to deliver therapeutic agents, such as chemotherapy drugs, small molecule inhibitors, or immunotherapy agents, directly to the tumor site [198]. This targeted approach not only enhances treatment efficacy but also reduces systemic toxicity commonly associated with traditional chemotherapy. For example, nanoparticles such as liposomes, dendrimers, and micelles can encapsulate hydrophobic drugs, facilitating their delivery through the skin or across the blood-brain barrier, which is crucial for treating melanoma metastases in the brain. Several studies showed that nano-formulations of chemotherapy drugs enhance therapeutic effectiveness while reducing side effects compared to conventional delivery methods [11]. Additionally, nanoparticles can be engineered with ligands or antibodies that specifically target melanoma cell markers, further improving the precision of drug delivery [199]. Nanotechnology also plays a pivotal role in the early detection of melanoma. Conventionally, diagnosis relies on dermoscopy, biopsy, and imaging methods such as CT, PET, or MRI, which often fail to detect micro-metastases at an early stage [200]. Nanoparticles can be designed to bind to specific tumor biomarkers, allowing earlier detection than traditional imaging methods. Magnetic nanoparticles, quantum dots, and gold nanoparticles have been investigated for their ability to enhance the sensitivity and resolution of imaging techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Computed Tomography (CT) scans, and fluorescence imaging [201, 202]. For instance, gold nanoparticles conjugated with antibodies have been shown to improve melanoma cell imaging by targeting surface markers [201, 203]. This approach not only facilitates early detection but also enables the monitoring of tumor progression and treatment response, allowing for real-time adjustments to therapeutic strategies.

Moreover, nanotechnology has proven valuable in boosting immunotherapy effectiveness, a cornerstone of melanoma treatment. While immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1), have revolutionized melanoma care [204], yet many patients still fail to respond to these therapies. Nanoparticles can enhance the delivery and efficacy of immune modulators, such as cytokines (e.g., interleukin-2, IL-2) or tumor-associated antigens, by concentrating them at the tumor site to stimulate a stronger, localized immune response [205]. Furthermore, nanoparticles can help counteract the immunosuppressive factors present in the TME, thereby improving the ability of immune cells to attack melanoma cells [206, 207].

Nanotechnology holds immense potential for advancing precision medicine in melanoma treatment. While surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy remain the backbone of current practice [197, 200, 208, 209], they are often limited by toxicity, resistance, and lack of specificity. By improving drug delivery, enabling early detection, and enhancing immunotherapies, nanomedicine aims to reduce side effects and improve treatment accuracy.

. However, the exorbitant number of materials that can be used for the production of NPs, their size, shape and chemical properties poses a huge problem regarding the standardization in the preclinical and clinical evaluation of their efficacy. Unlike small-molecule drugs, NPs add new layers of complexity to the assessment of a drug candidate, and unfortunately, not all the controls are uniformly reported across different studies. Nanotherapeutics must still follow randomized, controlled, blinded trial structures, but nanoparticle-specific standards are still emerging. Regulatory agencies (such as FDA, EMA, NCI’s Nanotechnology Characterization Lab) published several recommendations for the characterization and preclinical evaluation, but no universally accepted standards were promulgated for clinical trials controls unique to NPs [210, 211]. Moreover, clinical trials of nanotherapeutics also require to test the biocompatibility of empty NPs, usually in Phase I, in order to assess whether adverse effect could be caused by the drug-loaded NPs, the NPs or the cargo itself. Despite such controls could be critical for understanding nanoparticles’ contribution, empty carrier controls are often omitted beyond Phase I [211].

However, challenges related to nanoparticle stability, biocompatibility, and regulatory approval remain significant. Ongoing research and clinical trials will be essential to fully harness the transformative potential of nanotechnology in melanoma care (see Table 2).

Tailored drug delivery systems

Nanoparticles (NPs) can be meticulously designed to precisely target tumor cell markers, enabling the delivery of therapeutic agents while minimizing damage to healthy tissues [212]. Drug delivery strategies using NPs are generally classified as either passive targeting or active targeting. Passive targeting relies on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, where NPs naturally accumulate at tumor sites due to leaky vasculature [12]. In contrast, active targeting involves functionalizing NPs with ligands (e.g. antibodies, peptides, aptamers, or transferrin) that selectively bind receptors overexpressed on tumor cells (see Fig. 4a) [213]. For example, PD-L1 can be targeted using PD-L1 antibodies conjugated with doxorubicin-loaded lipidic NPs. These NPs interact with the tumor cell membrane, reducing its fluidity and functionality, thereby impairing cell metabolism and migration [214]. Similarly, PD-L1 antibodies linked to hollow gold nanoshells have been employed for chemo-photothermal therapy [215]. Additionally, PD-L1 antibodies combined with melanoma cell membranes have been used as functional groups on TiO2-based sonocatalytic nanoagents, promoting high tumor cell uptake and intracellular sonocatalytic killing of B16-F10 cells [216].

Preclinical and clinical trials have explored targeting other markers, such as CD20, overexpressed in melanoma-initiating cells, and CD47, an integrin-associated transmembrane protein present in highly metastatic patients. For instance, anti-CD47 antibodies conjugated to chitosan oligosaccharide-grafted nano-graphene oxide loaded with dacarbazine effectively induced early apoptosis in B16-F10 cells [221]. Similarly, anti-chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4) antibodies were conjugated to positively charged chitosan NPs to simultaneously deliver the oncosuppressor microRNA-126 and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) inhibitors, overcoming resistance to BRAF inhibitors like dabrafenib (see Fig. 4b). These experiments showed a decrease in tumor growth as well as reduced lung and liver metastases [218]. Due to the expensive cost and instability of antibodies, chemically synthesized aptamers (i.e., single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules) have emerged as viable alternatives. For example, AS1411, the first FDA-approved nucleic acid aptamer, binds nucleolin, a protein overexpressed on cancer cell membranes [222]. This aptamer has been used to modify polymeric NPs loaded with dacarbazine, reducing its toxicity to healthy [223]. Nanotechnology has also enabled advances in CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, where single-guide RNA (sgRNA) can be tailored to target specific genes.

Cationic lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have been shown to efficiently deliver Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes to melanoma cells, targeting the BRAF gene [224]. In vivo studies using transdermal microneedles demonstrated BRAF gene silencing efficiency of about 70% [225].

Other effective active-targeting ligands are peptides, which are smaller and simpler to be coupled with treatment molecules, compared to antibodies and aptamers. For example, arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) peptide-targeted NPs facilitate doxorubicin uptake by B16-F10 cells due to their strong affinity for αVβ3 integrins, which are overexpressed on melanoma cell membranes [226]. Cys-Arg-Glu-Lys-Ala with N-methylated Glu polypeptide nanodisks were selected to target fibronectin, related to cancer invasiveness being expressed on stromal and vascular components, to treat lung metastasis by delivering paclitaxel and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand [227]. Notably, the alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (αMSH) peptide can target the transmembrane protein melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1-R), overexpressed on melanoma cell membranes. The αMSH-functionalized Lutetium-177 (177Lu) radiolabeled core-shell silica nanoparticles (C’ dots) were used in melanoma radiotherapy, demonstrating selective tumor uptake in vivo (Fig. 4c) and a delay in tumor growth with increased survival [219]. Other targeting molecules include transferrin, folate and hyaluronic acid because of their good stability, low immunogenicity and strong penetration ability [217]. Folate, involved in cell proliferation and DNA replication, is overexpressed in rapidly proliferating cancer cells, and it has been explored for photodynamic therapy [228–230] and drug delivery of doxorubicin from liquid crystalline nanoparticles [231]. Hyaluronic acid binds CD44 receptors and has been used in chemo-photodynamic combination therapy for melanoma [232, 233] and for the topical delivery of metformin in the form of coating of chitosan-gelatin NPs [234]. Transferrin, a glycoprotein promoting iron uptake, has been conjugated to lipid polymer hybrid NPs (LPHNPs) to deliver the chemotherapeutic agent plumbagin naphthoquinone, resulting in a 40% tumor disappearance rate and 10% regression in vivo [235]. The lipidic shell of LPHNPs can also be functionalized by conjugating stimuli-responsive moieties to provide intelligent holding and active targeting of drugs [236]. For example, smart NPs have been synthesized to release nitric oxide (NO) under visible light, overcoming doxorubicin (DOX) resistance in melanoma. These NPs consist of a poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) core loaded with doxorubicin and a lipidic outer layer containing a nitric oxide photodonor (NOPD). NO release from NPs, upon irradiation with visible light for 30 min, inhibits the transporters drug efflux activity through nitration of critical tyrosines (P-gp, MRP1, and BCRP) in DOX-resistant human M14 melanoma cells (Fig. 4d-I, II), with consequent increase of intracellular DOX accumulation (Fig. 4d-II, red channel) and increase in the number of killed cells when compared with other formulations and free DOX (Fig. 4d-III, IV) [220].

Advances in targeted and immunotherapy for melanoma

Advancements in melanoma biology have significantly shaped the field of immunotherapy [237], now recognized as an innovative and effective cancer treatment. Immunotherapy harnesses the immune system against the tumor, through the recognition of specific antigens exposed on cancer cells surface [238, 239]. Over the past decade, therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) [240] (targeting PD-1/PD-L1, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CLTA-4)), adoptive cell therapy [241] (tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, CAR-T), cytokine therapy [242], tumor vaccines [243], and oncolytic viruses [244] delineated new routes in cancer treatment, achieving remarkable outcomes. However, many reports show the failure of melanoma treatments as a consequence of both immune escape mechanisms [245] and inadequate delivery of the therapeutics towards the cancer site. To address these challenges, nanotechnology has emerged as a powerful tool, offering nanoparticle-based platforms that enhance drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy [246].

In this context, organic (lipid-based [247] and polymeric NPs [248]) and inorganic particles (gold, silver, silica NPs) [201, 249, 250] are among the most studied tools. This section highlights the latest developments in nanomaterial-based drug delivery systems (DDSs) designed to optimize melanoma immunotherapy. For instance, the hydrophobic PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor BMS202 was successfully loaded onto PEGylated nanodiamonds (BMS202-NDs), enhancing pharmaceutical stability and delivery (Fig. 5a) [251]. Three isogenic melanoma cell lines (WM793, WM793P1, and 1205Lu) and human PBMCs were employed in an in vitro culture system to evaluate the efficacy of BMS202-NDs. The DDS promoted the inhibition of interaction between PD-L1 and its receptor, allowing immune response enhancement and melanoma cells death. In another approach, Barati et al. prepared a siRNA-PD-1 (siPD-1)-loaded liposomal formulation to silence PD-1 expression in T cells and study the antitumor activity in in vitro models [252]. Next, the authors injected siPD-1 into B16-F0 tumor-bearing mice to evaluate the efficacy of PD-1 suppression. The results showed how siPD-1 administration significantly delayed tumor growth, improving the mean survival rate in melanoma tumor-bearing mice, due to the activation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes within the cancer site.

In a different approach, Ma et al. presented multifunctional hybrid oncolytic virus-mimicking nanovesicles (HVMNVs), as a promising platform for coupling precise tumor-targeted delivery and cancer immunotherapy in melanoma (Fig. 5b) [253].

Here, functional nanoparticles (HVMNVs@Fe-Cs) incorporated iron oxide nanoparticles (ESIONPs) and a mitochondrial depolarizing agent carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP), meanwhile HVMNVs were designed with viral-envelope glycoproteins and macrophage membranes. The results obtained in in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that, upon recognition and binding of hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) HVMNVs@Fe-Cs to sialic acid, the ESIONPs and CCCP were distributed within the tumor cells cytosol, determining mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of the caspase cascades, and release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) via pyroptosis, a newly defined programmed cell death, with the concomitant stimulation of anti-tumor immunity. Another example of melanoma immunotherapy approach was represented by the targeting of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), an enzyme that contributes to TME immunosuppressive activity [254]. The highly potent and selective IDO inhibitor, epacadostat (EPA) was covalently attached via tumor-sensitive oxime-ester bond to the amphiphilic sphingomyelin (SM). The SM-EPA adduct, in contact with an aqueous medium, formed a self-assembled liposome nanovesicle delivery platform (epacasomes), that provided EPA encapsulation, improving its pharmacokinetics. The epacasomes were readily uptaken by tumor cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and the cytosolic release of EPA, due to hydrolysis of the oxime-ester bond run by intracellular hydrolases, enhanced IDO inhibitory activity, resulting in increased T cells proliferation in an in vitro melanoma model. Furthermore, because of the targeted tumor accumulation, epacasome showed also, in in vivo B16-F10 melanoma model of female mice, a tumor mass reduction, a prolonged survival rate, with a concomitant increase of IDO inhibition and anti-tumour CTLs activities.

Another promising strategy involves the exploitation of tumor-derived membranes for drug targeting, such as EVs. These are small, membrane-delimited particles that are naturally released by almost all types, they originate from late endosomes and multi-vesicular bodies (MVBs) in cells and are released into the extracellular space. These vesicles act as carriers of biological information between cells and usually contain proteins, lipids, RNA (mRNA, miRNA), DNA fragments, and metabolites. Due to the high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity and targeting properties, EVs, are actively explored as natural drug delivery systems. Engineered EVs were also explored for the design of vaccines for cancer immunotherapy, showing promising results for melanoma therapy [255, 256]. Ye et al. developed Golgi apparatus exosome-PLGA hybrid membrane coated nanoparticles (GENPs) capable of inhibiting PD-L1 synthesis and PD-L1-bearing exosome secretion within the Golgi of tumor cells, triggering the reversion of the immunosuppressive behavior of TME. GENPs were able to mimic exosomes and induce T cell activation through a vaccine-like effect. These NPS were formulated as a hydrogel-forming spray and tested on an incomplete tumor resection mouse model proving the inhibition of local residual tumor growth and metastasis, as well as significantly extended survival [257].

This type of strategy might provide a valid adjuvant therapy for the post-surgical treatment of melanoma, that combined with immunotherapy might reduce the risk of recurrences. However, one of the main challenges of this approach resides in the scalability and reproducibility of the production process.

The combination of immunotherapy with nano-DDS offers numerous advantages, including improved pharmacokinetics, reduced off-target toxicity, and enhanced immune activation. However, translating these promising preclinical results into clinical applications remains a significant challenge. Issues such as nanoparticle stability, biocompatibility, and regulatory approval must be addressed to realize the full potential of these cutting-edge therapies.

Targeting TME cells and ECM components

As it is well established, the TME is composed of a diverse and multicellular niche in which the interaction among cells (tumor, stromal, and immune cells) and ECM components (collagen, elastin, fibronectin, laminin, growth factors and enzymes) supports tumor growth, invasion, and resistance to therapies. A straightforward application of nanotechnology regards the possibility to develop nanosystems (e.g. polymeric NPs, lipid-based NPs) for targeting TME cells and the ECM components [258]. In this context, various drug-delivery systems (DDS) have been engineered to improve pharmaceuticals solubility, enhancing their penetration into the hostile TME and reducing systemic toxicity.

Several studies have demonstrated that TAMs are responsible for cancer recurrence after surgery by limiting the infiltration and activation of T cells within the TME [259, 260]. Li et al. developed a biocompatible alginate-based hydrogel functionalized with pexidartinib (PLX)-encapsulated nanoparticles (PLX-NP@Gel) (see Fig. 6a). Thanks to PDLX gradual release, the DDS was able to block the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptors (CSF1R), thus determining TAMs depletion from the TME. Tumor recurrence was inhibited during systemic and local delivery of anti-PD-1 antibody in mice bearing subcutaneous B16-F10 tumor implantation. In the last decade, other researches pointed out that innate and adaptive immunity could be activated by targeting checkpoint molecules expressed on both natural killer (NK) cells and T cells [261]. Exploiting this potentiality, Ye et al. designed a DDS based on an albumin/polyester composite nanoparticle (APCN) (see Fig. 6b) [262]. This system was functionalized with a tri-specific Nano-Antibody (Tri-NAb). The Tri-NAb was able to target PD-L1, NKG2A (an inhibitory member of the NKG2 family expressed in activated NK and T cells), and 4-1BB (a glycoprotein receptor expressed in activated NK and T cells) [263]. The Tri-NAb promoted proliferation and activation of NK and CD8 + T cells by simultaneously binding stimulatory and inhibitory pathways. The effective disease eradication was demonstrated in an in vivo murine melanoma model, in which Tri-NAb showed high affinity towards tumor cells, elicited via antigen-antibody binding mechanism. Another challenging approach for immune system stimulation and activation was represented by the production of specific immunomodulatory proteins via local delivery of mRNA-based therapy [264, 265]. In their work, Hamouda et al. formulated lipid-based NPs (LNPs) for the delivery of mRNA encoding for IL-21, IL-7, and the immunostimulatory molecule 4-1BB ligand [266]. The synergistic effect of the triplet LPNs resulted in the promotion of CD8 + T cells infiltration together with their activation, leading to granzyme B and IFN-γ production. The employment of high-dimensional flow cytometry, single-cell RNA, and surface protein co-profiling helped assess the nano-formulation therapeutic effect in the subcutaneous B16-F10 melanoma model. Moreover, the triplet LNPs treatment proved to protect against cancer relapse thanks to the development of long-term immunological memory.

Today, the induction of tumor immunogenic cell death (ICD) constitutes a novel therapeutic frontier in melanoma treatment [267, 268]. The working principle is based on the activation, upon stimuli, of the immune system in TME and the switch-on of the corresponding signaling pathways that lead to cancer cells [269]. In the last few years, many efforts have been engaged in the preparation of nanocarrier-mediated ICD [270]. An example is represented by the work from Guo et al., which focused on the fabrication of a pH-sensitive triblock copolymer immunofunctional nanoplatform via RAFT (reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer) polymerization. The NP surface NPs was decorated with the targeting ligand cyclic arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (cRGD) or mannose (Man) to selectively target B16-F10 melanoma cells and TAMs, respectively [271]. The immune adjuvant R848 was encapsulated within the polymeric NPs lumen. Once delivered, the ICD was directly induced by the NPs via oxidative phosphorylation upregulation with increased ROS production and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [272]. Upon treatment of a tumor model in C57BL/6 mice, the study highlighted that the formulation promoted dendritic cells (DC) maturation, M1-like TAM polarization and CD8+/CD4 + T cell infiltration, resulting in melanoma tumor mass growth inhibition.

Although the described nanotechnologies represent revolutionary therapeutic treatments exploiting more peculiar TME features to fight the disease, great potential will come from the translation of such novel methodologies into clinical practice for the improvement of melanoma patients’ outcomes.

Targeting melanoma microenvironment metabolic features with nanotechnology

The TME is a complicated orchestra where the interaction between cancer cells, stromal cells, and all the ECM components, creates a hostile milieu that hinders the effectiveness of pharmaceutical treatments [131, 274, 275]. A promising strategy to overcome these challenges involves exploiting the unique metabolic features of the melanoma TME, such as acidification, hypoxia, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and ion flux dysregulation, to develop stimuli-responsive nanotechnologies [274, 276–278]. These systems aim to enhance the precision and efficacy of drug delivery while minimizing off-target toxicity. In the last decades, significant advances in nanomaterials have led to the design of TME-responsive nanocarriers, including organic NPs (e.g., liposomes, lipid nanocapsules, micelles, polymeric nanoparticles) and inorganic formulations (e.g., metallic NPs, magnetic NPs, quantum dots, carbon nanotubes) [279]. These nanocarriers provide innovative platforms for drug delivery by leveraging TME characteristics to trigger drug release and improve therapeutic outcomes.

It is well established that acidification is one of the main tumor features and constitutes the first step towards intrinsic or acquired multidrug resistance (MDR) [280]. Guo et al. synthesized TME-responsive NPs (DNPs) using a self-assembly technique [281]. Here, pH-responsive NPs were obtained through the self-assembly between cationic oncolytic polypeptide (PLP) and a pH-sensitive anionic polypeptide (PLDA), while pH-insensitive NPs (SNPs) were obtained by self-assembly of anionic polypeptide (PLSA) and used as a control. The final goal was to exploit the pH-liability of the maleic acid amide (MAA) bonds in PLDA, to trigger PLP release at acidic pH. Through in vitro studies carried out in B16-F10 cells, inducing lysis of tumor cells, the mitochondrial damage, and intracellular ROS generation. Lastly, the DNPs implanted in the tumor site of C57BL/6J mice showed a strong inhibition of tumor growth compared to SNPs-treated mice.

An additional obstacle to therapeutic approaches efficacy is the hypoxia-induced drug resistance, which also supports immune response suppression in the tumor site [282]. Zang et al. developed an injectable, in situ forming, light-responsive hydrogel, based on manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanosheets and sodium alginate (SA) (see Fig. 7a) [283]. This hydrogel generates molecular oxygen from ROS present in the TME, addressing hypoxia while enabling. The aim was to reverse the hypoxic conditions while exploiting photodynamic therapy and improving the delivery of the cytotoxic drug doxorubicin (DOX) (see Fig. 7a). The optimized hydrogel (MD@SA) was tested in B16-F10 cells in vitro and in vivo in B16-F10 tumor xenografted mice. The results confirmed that the hydrogel was able to downregulate HIF-1α and the drug efflux pump P-glycoprotein (P-gp). The more favorable TME allowed the cellular uptake of MD@SA and the specific release of DOX within the melanoma cells. Moreover, the exposure to NIR irradiation triggered apoptosis by stimulating the presentation of tumor-specific antigens on cell membranes [283]. In another example, Guan et al. engineered a DDS to combine immunotherapy with radiotherapy (RT) to limit post-surgical cancer recurrence and metastasis. In this case, the formulation consisted of PEGylated hollow MnO2 particles (HMP), which served as a carrier for the loading of a small molecular PI3-kinase γ (PI3kγ) inhibitor (IPI549). The DDS was named IPI549@HMP. In the proposed experimental model, under acidic TME conditions, the HMP nanoparticles disassembled to achieve tumor-targeted therapeutics delivery. At the same time, HMP activated catalases towards the metabolic transformation of H2O2 into O2, enhancing the hypoxia-relieved postoperative RT. The synergistic approach determined the immunogenic cell death (ICD) effect and the remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME into a susceptible immunogenic phenotype able to respond to the ICB therapy, as demonstrated in B16-F10 melanoma models. Here, the tumor mass registered a significant reduction of its growth rate in comparison with controls [284]. Developing multi-stimuli responsive platforms presents an even greater challenge. Recently a novel therapeutic strategy was proposed for enhanced local tumor starvation and hypoxia-activated chemotherapy (see Fig. 7b) [285]. The authors prepared a pH- and redox-responsive scaffold based on a diselenide-crosslinked dextran-based hydrogel, for the release of glucose oxidase (GOx) and the hypoxia-sensitive prodrug tirapazamine (TPZ).

GOx enzymatic activity induces tumor starvation by consuming glucose and producing H₂O₂ and gluconic acid, which degrade the hydrogel to release therapeutic agents. Concurrently, the extreme hypoxic environment facilitates the activation of TPZ, which triggers cell apoptosis by its conversion into the highly cytotoxic benzotriazinyl radical (BTZ·). These dual effects were deeply studied in B16-F10 cell line in vitro and in C57BL/6 mice in vivo. Even though stimuli-responsive nanocarriers represent a challenging approach to melanoma treatment, they hold great potential in the future clinical application of nanomedicine.

Failure case analysis: nanotechnological and pharmaceutical challenges

The heterogeneity and the continuous evolution of the cancer niche determine the activation of immunosuppressive signaling pathways [286] and the overexpression of tumor-associated enzymes [287], which contribute to the onset of unique biochemical and physical barriers able to obstacle the success of the therapeutic protocols [288]. In the last years, researchers have exploited these TME features as triggers to implement the design of adaptive nanocarriers capable of sensing and responding to the dynamic metabolic switches within the tumor niche [289–291]. Therefore, these innovative engineered nanosystems represent the closest generation of nanotechnology intended as dynamic entities able to adapt to the surrounding and fluctuating TME to achieve precise drug delivery and enhanced therapeutic efficacy, through their activation and/or transformation. Su et al. reported on the design and application of a dual-sensitive polymeric micellar nanodrug with a pH- and MMP-2-responsive PEG sheddable PEG coating integrating both a PD-L1 antibody and the photosensitizer zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc) for synergistic immuno-photodynamic tumor therapy [292].

A peculiar characteristic of melanoma is represented by a high glycolytic activity emphasized by the upregulation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) leading to lactate accumulation and acidosis [293]. Recent pharmacological studies proposed the PDK inhibition via the administration of dichloroacetate (DCA, see Fig. 1), which has been shown to block pyruvate into the TCA cycle and to lower lactate concentrations, with a significant reduction of the melanoma cell growth [294]. A challenging therapeutic approach has been proposed by combining the inhibition of PDK to improve the anti-PD-1immunotherapy efficacy [295, 296].

Considering the shortcomings of previously explored nanotechnology strategies and conventional vaccination in targeting solid tumors, nanovaccines represent an advanced frontier in cancer immunotherapy, combining tumor antigens and adjuvants in nanoscale structures to enhance efficacy while reducing treatment toxicity [297]. These platforms offer promising results for melanoma immunotherapy, improving antigen presentation, modulating the TME, and potentiating cytotoxic T cell responses [298]. A prominent example is the Resiquimod-Induced Nanovaccine (RINV), developed for personalized cancer therapy. In this system, the Toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) were chosen for their ability to stimulate production of the cytokine IFN-inducible protein-10 (IP-10), while inducing reduced concentration of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), thereby showing considering antitumor effects [299]. Their agonist Resiquimod (R848) is covalently linked to a fifth generation (G5-PAMAM) dendrimer via a disulfide bond. This design not only promotes consistent nanovaccine assembly with the model antigen ovalbumin (OVA) but also enables redox-responsive intracellular release of R848, improving antigen presentation and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell activation, promoting dendritic cell maturation and effector cytokine secretion, slowing tumor progression, inhibiting metastasis, and exhibiting efficacy in both preventive and therapeutic contexts [300].

Innovative preclinical models for nanotherapeutics

To improve the understanding of underlying molecular mechanisms of melanoma and for the development of new treatment strategies, researchers have developed innovative preclinical models that mimic TME more accurately [301, 302]. These models include three-dimensional (3D) culture techniques, especially using patient-derived samples, melanoma-on-a-chip systems, and organotypic skin reconstructions.

Spheroids and organoids, 3D culture models, offer a more realistic representation of tumor architecture by incorporating cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, oxygen and nutrient gradients, and intratumoral heterogeneity [303, 304]. Melanoma spheroids can be generated from melanoma cell lines or patient-derived cells obtained from excised melanoma tissues. They are generated from single-cell suspensions, where the single cells aggregate under non-adherent conditions [305]. Zamora-Perez et al. explored the effect of plasmonic nanocapsules in melanoma spheroids for their potential application as a photothermal therapeutic agent. They observed the maintenance of nanocapsule geometry in the intracellular milieu with no impairment of cellular ultrastructure [247]. In spheroid models, co-culture with other cell types is used to analyze the interactions between tumor and non-tumor cells in the TME, as fibroblasts and immune cells [306]. For instance, Schäfer et al. demonstrated that 3D co-culture spheroids of melanoma cells, fibroblasts and keratinocytes can provide valuable insights and serve as in vitro models with improved predictive value for the in vivo activity of cancer nanotherapeutics [307]. By comparing lipoplex nanoparticle-mediated mRNA delivery to 2D and 3D (spheroid) cell co-culture models of melanoma, these authors found that mRNA lipoplex formulations distribution and transfection efficiency can be modulated by both coculture of distinct cell types and create differences in 3D cell growth characteristics. Current research favors multicellular over single-cell cultures to better mimic the TME, with 3D multicellular spheroids being especially useful for modeling cell interactions and extracellular matrix dynamics, thus offering a more accurate simulation of TME structure and function (see Fig. 8a) [308, 309]. Organoid models are becoming central to melanoma research. Derived directly from patient tumor samples, these 3D structures retain the original tumor genetic and phenotypic characteristics, making them highly valuable for personalized medicine (see Fig. 8b) [310, 311]. Moreover, organoids provide a TME that better reflects drug diffusion and cellular interactions, essential for evaluating nanotherapy responses [305]. They also contain other autologous cell populations from patient-derived tissue, such as lymphoid and myeloid cells. By coculturing organoids with autologous lymphocytes from peripheral blood or lymphoid tissue, researchers can study interactions between tumor and immune cells in the context of immunotherapeutic approaches [312, 313]. Although organoids have emerged as the most widely employed preclinical models in biomedical research, they continue to face inherent limitations that constrain their translational applicability. There are several closely related and significant efforts underway in organoid standardization, especially around manufacturing, quality control, and reproducibility. For instance, the International Society of Organoid Research (ISoOR) is advancing standardized protocols for organoid biobanking. Their platform aims to enhance reproducibility, consistency, and regulatory alignment in organoid research, by creating a framework focused on harmonized quality standards for organoid banking worldwide [314]. Some international efforts are ongoing with the ISO Technical Committee 276 that it is exploring standardization for organoid-based alternative testing methods, from a bioprocessing and analytics standpoint. Furthermore, the OECD is concurrently working on test guidelines for toxicity evaluation of chemicals using organoid technologies [315]. As of now, no formal initiative appears to exist that is specifically labeled for standardizing nanodrug screening in organoid systems. However, the existing frameworks in organoid manufacturing and quality control support the guidelines for consistent organoid development, endpoint quality evaluation, reproducibility and reliability improvements. In this context, the proposal of a multidimensional system, such as a Nano Organic Response Metrics framework, designed to evaluate the efficacy and biological impact of nanoparticle-based therapeutics in organoid and patient-derived models could be promising. The framework integrates three core components: the Penetration Depth Index (PDI), which quantifies the extent of nanoparticle infiltration within 3D organoid structures [316, 317]; the Immune microenvironment remodeling score (IRS) [318, 319], reflecting nanoparticle-induced shifts in immune cell composition and signaling; and patient-specific metabolic markers, such as the LDH/lactate ratio, which provide insight into altered metabolic states associated with treatment response [320, 321]. Another innovative approach for studying nanotherapeutics in melanoma is the melanoma-on-a-chip, a microfluidic device that mimics physiological conditions like blood and nutrients flow through micrometer-sized chambers. These chambers can be populated with various cell types, enabling the study of complex cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions within the TME [322]. Tumor-on-a-chip systems enable high-throughput, real-time testing of nanotherapeutics, facilitating observations of drug distribution, efficacy, and toxicity in a 3D environment (see Fig. 8c) [323]. Wang et al. developed a microfluidic tumor-vasculature-on-a-chip that mimics two important biological barriers: tumor leaky vasculature and 3D tumor tissue with dense extracellular matrix. With this system they were able to investigate nanoparticle extravasation through vasculature and subsequent accumulation in tumor tissues [323]. Tumor-on-a-chip systems can also model metastasis by incorporating vascular channels to observe cell migration, invasion, and extravasation through vessel-like structures [324]. In addition to melanoma cell lines, 3D structures such as spheroids, organoids and mini tissues can be integrated into these platforms, creating a more realistic representation of TME [325, 326]. Therefore, melanoma-on-a-chip offers a precise and reliable platform for testing nanotherapeutics, supporting the potential for clinical translation of nanomedicines [327].

Three-dimensional bioprinting can help in recreating more realistic models of melanoma, since the intrinsic layered structure of skin makes it particularly suitable to be reproduced by a layer-by-layer technology. In addition, 3D printing allows the use of multiple materials with precise control of tissue composition as well as on its thickness and geometry [328]. For example, epidermis and dermis bioinks were prepared with decellularized porcine skin, and the basement membrane with human-derived amniotic membrane, including melanoma cells and dermal fibroblasts, to recreate a model of early-stage melanoma cell migration (see Fig. 8d I-VII) that could help in evaluating drug response [329]. Another tri-layered 3D-printed system was developed for reproducing malignant melanoma in vitro and in vivo and testing the effects of the chemotherapeutic vemurafenib. Agarose-collagen type I hydrogels embedding tumor stem cells and human keratinocytes were used for the epidermal layer, fibroblasts and HUVECs for the dermis and mesenchymal stem cells for the hypodermis [330]. To better mimic the in vivo tumor environment, Fang et al. developed a composite scaffold including decellularized extracellular matrix from pig skin to emulate tissue-specific characteristics, together with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) for scaffold stability and biocompatibility, and sodium alginate for its printability and cross-linking with calcium ions. The 3D-printed scaffold was combined with quercetin NPs prepared through the self-assembly of Pluronic F127, which showed an inhibitory effect on A375 cells, reaching a killing rate of the 90% after 14 days [331]. In another application, 3D-bioprinted structures successfully induced an in situ post-surgical photothermal-chemotherapeutic effect thanks to a heterogeneous bioink composed of sodium alginate, gellan gum and polydopamine nanoparticles covered with a DOX-loaded thermoresponsive gelatin [332]. Interestingly, 3D-printed scaffolds could even maintain cryopreserved explants in culture. Fragments of patient-derived melanoma explants were loaded in 3D collagen scaffolds and incubated in transwells under hypoxic conditions, for a better in vitro mimicking of the TME than typical culture methods [333].

Toxicity and safety of nanotechnology-based approaches in melanoma

Nanotechnology has enabled advanced drug delivery strategies that enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce off-target effects. Nanoparticles, nanocarriers and nano formulations can overcome limitations of conventional therapies, such as low selectivity, multidrug resistance, and systemic toxicity [334]. Nevertheless, the increasing use of nanotechnology in biomedical field has also elevated concerns about their potential toxicity and long-term biosafety. Therefore, these issues must be addressed through rigorous preclinical and clinical evaluation. In a preclinical study, Bahamonde and collaborators investigated the intravenous administration of PEGylated gold NPs (GNPs) at doses up to 1,000 mg/kg in rats. Although most animals tolerated treatment, three deaths (total n = 19) occurred within 24 h following the administration. The authors believe that capillary blockage caused by nanoparticle aggregation is the cause of these deaths. Histological analysis revealed the development of microgranulomas in the liver and the accumulation of GNPs in the spleen and liver. Despite this accumulation, there were no obvious indications of hepatic, renal, or metabolic failure in the serum biochemical profiles [335]. In another preclinical research, Pu and collaborators developed an acetaminophen-loaded PEG/HMnO₂ nanosystem (APAP@PEG/HMnO₂) designed for synergistic chemotherapy and oxidative stress amplification in melanoma. APAP@PEG/HMnO2 was administered intravenously at dosages of 0, 10, and 20 mg/kg. There were no notable histopathological abnormalities, biochemical alterations, or weight loss, supporting its biocompatibility [336].

Additionally, encouraging safety evidence has been obtained from early clinical trials. A nanoformulation of paclitaxel nanoparticles in an anhydrous ointment base (SOR007) was investigated in patients with cutaneous metastases in a phase I/II study (NCT03101358). The nanomedicine was applied topically twice a day for a maximum of 56 days. All dose levels of SOR007 were well tolerated, and neither unusual laboratory safety data (chemistry, hematological, or urinalysis) nor changes in vital signs were considered to be related to the study drug. According to the researchers, the unexpected local skin reactions were mild to moderate in severity and were not or were not believed to be associated with the study drug [337]. Another phase I trial (NCT02820454) assessed the safety and dose tolerance for systemically administered gadolinium-based nanoparticle (AGuIX) in combination with whole brain radiotherapy in patients with multiple brain metastases. No dose-limiting toxicities were observed up to 100 mg/kg and increasing magnetic resonance signal enhancement was observed in the brain metastases with increasing administered dose [338]. Based on these findings, a double-blind phase II trial (NCT04899908) has been initiated and is currently recruiting volunteers. More recently, a phase I/II trial (NCT06870994) was designed to assess the safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy of gold nanorod–based targeted hyperthermia therapy (THT) in patients with metastatic melanoma refractory to checkpoint inhibitors. Two THT treatments will be administered separated by seven days. While clinical data are not yet available, preclinical models demonstrated good tolerability without systemic toxicity [339].

Despite encouraging preliminary data, most nano-based therapies remain in early development, and their administration protocols, dosage, and material composition deeply influence safety profiles. Careful protocol design, including tailored dose-escalation and rigorous biodistribution studies, is essential for their safe clinical translation.

Clinical trials and translation of nanotechnology-based approaches

While early detected melanoma can often be successfully treated by surgical treatment, patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma commonly undergo supplementary treatments such as radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Unfortunately, these options are often associated with poor prognoses. Moreover, a large heterogeneity of responses to treatments underscores the need for robust clinical trials to evaluate new therapies, drug combinations, or different dosages among a plethora of patients, emphasizing the importance of precision medicine. Nowadays, numerous clinical trials are underway to explore new treatments and therapeutic approaches for melanoma. According to ClinicalTrials.gov, since late the 1990 s, more than 340,000 clinical trials for melanoma have been registered, with nearly 500 trials based on immunotherapy [340]. A comprehensive list of the latest clinical trials, that involved nanotechnology for the precision medicine of melanoma, is reported in Table 3. Among these, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), along with vaccines, have been the most widely studied. Most of the trials explored PD-1 inhibition using nivolumab or pembrolizumab, which are gradually replacing traditional chemotherapy in the treatment of advanced skin melanoma [345, 347]. Notably, phase III trials such as CheckMate76K (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04099251, see Table 3) and KEYNOTE-716 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03553836, see Table 3), have demonstrated the effectiveness of PD-1 inhibitors in earlier-stage melanoma, significantly reducing recurrence in patients at high risk for progression. These studies mark a breakthrough for long-term survival in patients with resected stage IIB/C melanoma [348, 349]. Another prominent phase III study, the NADINA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04949113, see Table 3), investigated the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab as neoadjuvant therapy (i.e. administered before surgery) for patients with resectable, macroscopic, stage III melanoma. This approach showed greater results in extending event-free survival (EFS) when compared to the standard care for this stage: 12 cycles of adjuvant nivolumab post-surgery. The EFS rate in 1 year was 83.7% in the neoadjuvant group, compared to 57.2% for the adjuvant group. Improved effectiveness was highlighted among patients with BRAF-mutant melanoma, indicating the need for more personalized treatments based on tumor markers. Unfortunately, adverse events that were related to systemic treatment (grade 3 or higher) occurred twice as often in the neoadjuvant group compared to the adjuvant one, 29.7% and 14.7%, respectively [350]. Chimeric antigen receptor-expressing T (CAR-T) cell immunotherapy is one of the immunotherapeutic approaches that produced numerous clinical achievements in hematological malignancies; however, their use in solid tumors is restricted. Up to date, only 15 clinical trials involving CAR-T cell-based therapy for melanoma treatment were reported [351]. A Phase I/II study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01218867, see Table 3), conducted by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Surgery Branch tested the safety and effectiveness of cell therapy using anti-VEGFR2 gene modified tumor CD8 + lymphocyte cells to treat recurrent or relapsed metastatic cancer. Patients received CAR-T cells in different quantities with a low or high concentration of IL-2, but, unfortunately, the research was terminated due to a lack of objective response. Twenty-three patients out of 24, in fact, showed a progression of the disease and had serious side effects caused by the therapy [352]. Other CAR-T clinical trials terminated early or are ongoing with no published results yet. A promising strategy to overcome the limitations of CAR-T therapy is represented by the TILVANCE-301 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05727904, see Table 3), currently recruiting for phase III [353]. This study is enrolling patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma (Stage IIIC, IIID, or IV) who have had no prior therapy for metastatic disease to test the efficacy of lifileucel plus pembrolizumab regimen compared with pembrolizumab monotherapy. Lifileucel represents the first FDA-approved autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) immunotherapy. Compared to approved CAR T-cell therapies, where T cells are collected from the blood stream, for TIL therapy, the T cells are collected directly from the tumor and expanded to billions before being re-injected. Previous phase results showed that ICI-naive patients treated with the combination therapy present shrinkage of their tumors. In two-thirds of patients, tumors shrank by at least 30% and responses were long-lasting; additionally, in almost a third of patients, the primary unresectable tumor became undetectable. These results displayed encouraging efficacy and durability for the combination therapy [354].

A recent clinical trial phase I (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06783270, see Table 3), still in its recruiting phase, will use non-viral CRISPR-Cas9 technology to silence the expression of PD-1 on the surface of TILs to further prevent tumor-induced immune suppression in the treatment of inoperable or metastatic melanoma. Pre-clinical results showed an average reduction of 87.53% in cell surface PD-1 expression post-expansion and no off-target editing was detected [355].

Currently, other immunotherapy approaches involving new varieties of engineered immune cells, such as alpha-type-1 polarized dendritic cells (aDC1), Gammadelta T (γδ T) cells, and Natural killer (NK) cells, are approaching clinical translation. A terminated phase II trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04093323, see Table 3) investigated the effectiveness of autologous aDC1 cells-based treatment with tumor-selective chemokine modulation (CKM: Interferon Alpha 2b, Rintatolimod and Celecoxib) in preventing melanoma progression in refractory patients. Patient monocyte-derived aDC1 cells were loaded with tumor blood vessel-targeting antigenic peptides (TBVA) in final concentration of 1 µg/mL under cGMP guidance and injected intradermally as a vaccine [356]. Allogeneic γδ T cells from healthy donors were instead combined with Interferon-alpha1b (IFN-α1b) or PD-1 monoclonal antibody in an innovative neoadjuvant treatment of Stage III-IV resectable melanoma in an early phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06212388, see Table 3). Between novel immune cell-based therapies, universal donor expanded TGF-β-imprinted NK cells, successfully used for the treatment of hematological diseases, are nowadays actively used in clinical trials for metastatic melanoma. The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center is currently recruiting patients for testing this new therapy in combination with temozolomide drug in a phase I/II trial and assess its effectiveness in treating brain metastases (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05588453, see Table 3). Upon administration, TGF-β-imprinted NK cells may directly lyse cancer cells and also secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that further stimulate a systemic immune response. In this context, clinical trials are leveraging nanotechnology to minimize side effects and improve melanoma treatment outcomes. Via nanoparticles (NPs), drugs are delivered to the patient more efficiently and with fewer adverse effects.

The combination of NPs with chemotherapy has been the subject of several studies, in particular the nab-paclitaxel formulation: a 130-nM, albumin-bound (nab) particle form of paclitaxel. In 2010, the conclusion of a phase II trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00081042, see Table 3) established the antitumor activity of nab-paclitaxel both in previously treated and chemotherapy-naive patients with metastatic melanoma [341]. Its good tolerance and clinical activity have been further demonstrated in other clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00404235, NCT00626405, NCT00738361, NCT02020707 and NCT05664139, see Table 3) when used in combination with other regimens such as carboplatin, bevacizumab [342, 343], or recombinant human adenovirus type 5.

Another promising approach for melanoma treatment involves nanoparticles as carriers to stimulate immune response in patients, as in the case of tumor-specific RNA-nanoparticles vaccine. A recent phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05264974, see Table 3) is evaluating the feasibility of an autologous total tumor mRNA-loaded dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (DOTAP) liposome vaccine to prevent early recurrences in patients with stage IIB-IV melanoma after anti-PD1 adjuvant therapy [357]. Overall, nanotechnology-based approaches represent promising strategies for more efficient and personalized therapeutic options for melanoma, providing new hope for patients with high-risk or advanced-stage melanoma.

Beyond delivery: does nanotechnology redefine melanoma treatability?

While nanotechnology and precision medicine hold immense promises for advancing melanoma treatment, several disputes and open questions remain. One major controversy concerns the delicate balance between immunogenic cell death (ICD) and immune-related adverse reactions (irAEs).

Despite these perspectives, the field still faces considerable technological challenges. The TME heterogeneity limits the predictability of nanoparticle biodistribution penetration, and clearance, while issues such as off-target accumulation and long-term safety remain barriers to clinical translation. To address these limitations, novel strategies have emerged, including stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, multifunctional nanosystems and biomimetic coating [358]. In parallel, advances in computational modeling and artificial intelligence are beginning to guide the rational design of nanomaterials, enabling better prediction of their behavior in complex biological contexts. Together, these approaches illustrate a shift from conventional drug delivery toward dynamic, adaptative platforms that may ultimately expand the therapeutic landscape of melanoma.

Nanomaterials can be engineered to potentiate ICD, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity [212, 359]; however, their capacity to overstimulate immune responses raises the risk of irAEs, including autoimmunity and systemic inflammation [360]. The clinical relevance of ICD in melanoma and its interplay with immune checkpoint blockade has been increasingly discussed in recent years [361]. Moreover, the safety and translational potential of nanomaterials designed to modulate the immune system remain debated within preclinical and clinical research communities [362]. Future research must clarify whether nanomaterial design can selectively amplify tumor-specific immunity while minimizing collateral immune activation, or whether trade-offs are inevitable.

A second unresolved issue relates to metabolic interventions within the TME. Agents such as dichloroacetate (DCA) aim to reprogram tumor metabolism by shifting glycolytic pathways; nonetheless, metabolic plasticity often allows melanoma cells to compensate through fatty acid oxidation or alternative bioenergetic circuits [363, 364], potentially driving therapeutic resistance [365]. This raises the question of whether metabolic modulation should be employed as a standalone strategy or in combination with nanodelivery systems and immunotherapy to preempt adaptive resistance mechanisms.

These perspectives highlight that the future of nanomedicine in melanoma will depend not only on technological advances in drug delivery but also on a deeper mechanistic understanding of tumor immune metabolism interactions. Addressing these questions requires systematic evaluation of nanomaterial induced immune effects, identification of biomarkers predicting irAEs, and mapping of metabolic escape routes. Only then will it be possible to determine whether nanotechnology truly redefines the treatability of melanoma, or if its promise will be constrained by the same challenges faced by conventional therapies.

Conclusions

Cancer has been a profound challenge throughout human history, and its complexity suggests it will continue to be a major concern for future generations. Melanoma represents a challenge due to its intrinsic genetic landscape, high metastatic potential, and resistance to conventional therapies. From the first recorded description of a melanoma case, by Hippocrates in the 5th century B.C., to the development of targeted therapy, the journey of understanding and managing this disease has been long and arduous.

Nanotechnology in the past decades has emerged as a promising game-changer in general for the oncology field, driving the development of multi-purpose nanoplatforms for many fields, that can combine different characteristics such as imaging, drug delivery, and tumor microenvironment modulation. Nanocarriers such as liposomes, dendrimers, and natural/synthetic polymeric nanoparticles have been engineered to deliver chemotherapeutics, immunotherapies, and target therapies directly to melanoma cells, minimizing systemic toxicity symptoms in patients. For instance, nanoparticles functionalized with ligands specific to overexpressed melanoma markers, such as mutant BRAF or PD-L1 expression, allow for precise delivery, enhancing efficacy compared with the same substances alone. Moreover, nanostructures equipped with imaging agents offer real-time monitoring and evaluation of therapeutic responses, paving the way for adaptive treatment strategies in many cases.

Precision medicine complements these nanotechnological advancements by leveraging genetic and molecular data to inform treatment decisions. Breakthroughs in genomics have identified actionable mutations, such as BRAF V600E, that guide the use of targeted therapies. Additionally, the incorporation of liquid biopsies and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis into clinical practice enables the early detection of resistance mechanisms, allowing for the timely adjustment of therapeutic regimens.

The integration of nanotechnology and precision medicine offers a transformative approach to cancer treatment, creating a new paradigm for this challenging malignancy. The integration of these branches has opened the doors to innovative strategies, offering a close glimpse into a deeply tailored and effective future in oncological care. However, significant challenges remain. Tumor heterogeneity and the dynamic evolution of metastatic melanoma pose significant barriers to achieve sustained treatment efficacy. While nanotechnology offers potential solutions to overcome some of the biggest biological barriers, including those related to the tumor microenvironment, its translation into clinical practice is hindered by logistical and practical issues, including manufacturing, scalability, and regulatory approval. Similarly, the high cost of precision medicine tools and the need for comprehensive patient datasets limit accessibility, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Looking forward, the synergistic combination of nanotechnology and precision medicine holds huge promise. Emerging technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing and artificial intelligence (AI) could further refine these approaches. For instance, AI-driven algorithms can analyze vast datasets to predict therapeutic outcomes or identify novel biomarkers, while CRISPR-based systems integrated into nanocarriers could selectively edit oncogenes or modulate immune responses within melanoma cells in patients.

The role of TME in therapy resistance and disease progression is certainly a point of greater attention. Nanotechnology-based interventions targeting TME, such as reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages/fibroblasts or normalizing aberrant vasculature, could enhance the efficacy of current immunotherapies. The development of “smart” nanoparticles capable of responding to TME cues, represents an exciting frontier in melanoma research. Furthermore, as melanoma is a multifaceted disease, it will likely require a combinatorial approach deploying a pool of treatments targeting various pathways and mechanisms to achieve durable remission and potentially curative outcomes. There is no doubt that significant progress has been made in cancer treatment; however, the potential of precision medicine and nanotechnology in melanoma therapy is just at the beginning of its path. Interdisciplinary collaboration between oncologists, pharmacologists, biotechnologists, bioengineers, chemists, nanotechnologists, physicists, and computational scientists will be essential to overcome existing challenges and ensure equitable access to these transformative innovations.

Moreover, all the promising results in the application of nanotechnology in melanoma therapy faces several limitations that are still far from being overcame. Safety concerns also remain, as many nanoparticle formulations are engineered to be biodegradable, some nanomaterials accumulate in off-target tissues such as the liver, spleen, or skin, raising the risk of chronic toxicity. Immune recognition of nanoparticle surfaces can also trigger inflammatory responses or rapid clearance from circulation, limiting therapeutic exposure.

As already discussed, from a translational perspective, challenges include maintaining the reproducibility and standardization of nanoparticle synthesis, achieving batch-to-batch consistency, and scaling up production under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions. The complexity of nanoparticle formulations further complicates regulatory evaluation, as stability, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity must be extensively validated; presenting new practical challenges that hinder clinical adoption. Preclinical models often demonstrate great efficacy, that may not fully capture the complexity of human melanoma progression and immune interactions, leading to limited predictability in clinical trials.

In conclusion, the use of nanotechnology for the precision medicine of melanoma is obviously the path that modern medicine selected. However, the possibility to obtain a personalized treatment, based on a personalized in vitro model, and the potential to monitor the disease progression with unprecedented accuracy, might transform the common idea of cancer as a lethal disease. In the near future it could be possible to conceive cancer as a chronic disease that could be managed and coexist with, focusing on the improvement of the quality of life more than eradicating cancer cells.
