Lipidomic analysis of A1, A2, and heterozygous beta-casein genotypes in bovine milk using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry
Authors: Phelipe A.B.M. Peres, Milton J. Filho, Hebreia O.A. Souza, Mário M. Martins, Arlene B.S. Nossol, Douglas C. Caixeta, Arthur F. Demétrio, Thais F.M. Reis, Natieli Saito, Ana Graci Brito-Madurro, João Marcos Maduro, Vivian Alonso-Goulart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The consumption of A2 milk, which contains only the A2 type of beta-casein, has been increasing because it is considered healthier. In contrast, milk that contains the A1 type of beta-casein may cause gastrointestinal discomfort due to the release of the peptide beta-casomorphin-7 during digestion. The milk is composed of valuable proteins, liposoluble vitamins, and mineral salts, that could be essential source of dietary lipids for humans. The lipidomic analysis allows the characterization of important milk lipids. In this context, the objective of this work was to describe and determine the lipid profile and differential compounds in genotypes A1A1, A2A2, and A1A2 for beta-casein from bovine milk. Lipid extraction followed by gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry were performed on 54 bovine milk samples. As results, 12 differential metabolites were found, where the cholesterol; Cholesta-3,5-diene and Cholest-5-ene-3-ol(3. beta.)-carbonochloridate were the most abundant lipid compound in A2 milk. The identified lipids participate of the important pathways, such as the fatty acyl. The data obtained in this exploratory study provide insights into the main differences between the lipidomic profile of bovine milk present different beta-caseins in its composition.

Introduction

The food industry plays a significant role in the economy and public health, as food is essential for regulating metabolism and releasing hormones in the body [1]. In health area, an inappropriate diet can also represent a risk factor in the development of inflammatory and heart diseases, among others [2,3].

World milk production is projected to grow at 1.6% p. a. over the next decade (to 1085 Mt in 2033) supported by yield per animal. This rate of growth is faster than other main agricultural commodities. More than half of the growth in production is anticipated to come from India and Pakistan which will jointly account for over 30% of world production in 2033 [4]. It is estimated that, in 2025, global milk production reached approximately 992.7 million tonnes [5]. Specifically, milk production in North and South America, including the Caribbean, is mainly concentrated in the United States and Brazil, with annual volumes estimated at 102.994 and 36.650 million tonnes, respectively [5]. The milk is composed of valuable proteins, liposoluble vitamins, and mineral salts, that could be essential source of dietary lipids for humans [6]. It is composed of 87% water, 3.68% lipids, 4.98% lactose, 0.74% microelements, and 3.51% proteins, approximately, with beta-casein being one of the main components [7]. Nowadays, the physiological effects of the 15 beta-casein variants in human body have been studied [8], its being encoded by chromosome number 6 of the bovine genome. Molecular methods can determine the difference of a unique nucleotide in the beta-casein gene and thus identify the animal genotype [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. The main variants present in milk is the A1 and A2, where the first present a mutation at position 67 of the amino acids, replacing a proline for a histidine. This mutation triggers a difference in the digestibility of milk in human stomach, cleavage by histidine digestive enzymes releases a 7-amino acid peptide called beta-casomorphin-7 (BCM-7), known as an μ-opioid receptor agonist [[13], [14], [15]]. Some studies have concluded that the release of β-casomorphin 7 influences gastrointestinal motility as well as proinflammatory and immunomodulatory responses, based on findings from both in vitro models and in vivo animal experiments (Jianqin et al., 2015 [16]), the development of type 1 diabetes [17], cardiovascular problems (Shashank et al., 2018 [18]), schizophrenia [19], various autoimmune diseases [20], and even autism in children [21].

The progress of science and technology, particularly the development of mass spectrometry and omics technologies, has opened up a new frontier in the research of lipid composition [22]. The use of lipidomic has advances in the food industry, a subfield of metabolomics, considered a functional tool that analyzes simple samples, as cells, tissues, and even the most complex ones, as biological fluids, being widely used in food quality, safety, and traceability [23,24].

The presence of thousands of lipid species in milk makes it a highly complex food for lipidomic analyses [25]. Although there are studies on beta-casein variants and their physiological effects, there remains a significant gap regarding the detailed characterization of the lipid profile of bovine milk as a function of different genotypes (A1A1, A2A2, and heterozygous). To date, most research has focused on evaluating the protein or digestive aspects of these variants, leaving the relationship between beta-casein genotype and milk lipid composition largely unexplored. By addressing this gap, this study aims to expand the understanding of the genetic impact on milk lipid composition, contributing not only to scientific advancement but also to industrial applications in animal traceability, improving the efficiency of identifying A1 and A2 variants and ensuring quality and food safety in the dairy production chain. Therefore, the objective of this work was to investigate, through lipidomic approaches, the lipid profiles present in bovine milk from different beta-casein genotypes (A1, A2, and heterozygous), as well as to identify lipid metabolites that are differentially expressed among these groups.

Materials and methods

Sampling

A total of 54 raw bovine milk samples were collected from cows of different beta-casein genotypes (A2A2, n = 20; A1A2, n = 19; A1A1, n = 15). The animals were mainly crossbred Girolando (1/2, 3/4, and 5/8 genetic composition), with a small proportion of purebred Gir (5%). The lactation periods ranged from 3 to 6 months postpartum. The animals included in this study had been previously genotyped as part of the routine commercial bovine genotyping service providedby GENEAL Genética e Biotecnologia Animal SA, a company certified by the Brazilian Health Regulatory Agency. Genotyping of the β-casein (CSN2) gene was performed by detecting single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) using real-time PCR with allele-specific fluorescent probes. PCR reactions were carried out on a QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR System according to the manufacturer's instructions and the laboratory's validated standard operating procedures. All animals were fed exclusively using the Free Stall system, with a diet consisting of corn and grass silage and a 24% crude protein mix.

Milk samples were collected from bovine animals in accordance with a protocol approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Uberlândia (CEUA-UFU) (Approval No. 23117.067490/2024-06)from farms in the Uberlândia region, Minas Gerais, Brazil (Latitude: 18° 54′ 41″ S, Longitude: 48° 15′ 44″ W) and stored at −20°C for subsequent lipid extraction. Prior to sampling, all animals were genotyped by GENEAL Genética e Biotecnologia Animal SA to confirm their beta-casein genotype as expressed in the milk.

Lipidic extraction

Lipid extraction was performed in triplicate using the method developed at the Prof. Dr. Luiz Ricardo Goulart Filho Nanobiotechnology Laboratory (NANOS) (Uberlândia – MG – Brazil), through modifications of studies previously developed by Bligh and Dyer [26]; Folch, Lees and Sloane Stanley [27]. The samples were homogenized and 100 μL of milk were collected, adding 300 μL of dichloromethane (Biograde®, Anápolis, GO-Brazil) with 100 μL of methanol (Biograde®, Anápolis, GO-Brazil). After further homogenization, the samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, followed by centrifugation in a 5804R centrifuge (Eppendorf®, Hamburg-Germany) at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C for phase separation. The organic phase was then collected and dried in a SpeedVac Model DUC-22060-C00 (Genevac®, Ipswich, England) for 1 h at 40°C and subsequently resuspended in 50 μL of dichloromethane.

Analysis by gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC/MS)

Lipid extraction was performed in triplicate, and analysis was conducted using a gas chromatography system (Agilent GC/MS 7890B) coupled to a mass selective detector (MSD f5977B, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with an autosampler and operated in split mode (2:1). The mass spectrometer was operated in electron impact ionization mode (70 eV), with a high-vacuum pump and a quadrupole analyzer. Chromatographic separation of the compounds was performed on a DB-5HT capillary chromatographic column (30 m × 250 μm x 0.25 μm). The chromatographic conditions employed were: injector temperature of 290°C. The carrier gas was high-purity helium at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. Samples were injected in a volume of 6 μL. The initial temperature was 80°C; this temperature was maintained for 0min, increased to 300°C at 6°C/min, and maintained for 12 min. Then, the temperature was changed to 350°C at 25°C/min, totaling 50.67 min of analysis.

The following mass spectrometry conditions were used: the quad MS and MS source temperatures were 150°C and 280°C respectively. The Scan (MS) mode was scanned from m/z 50–550, with a threshold count of 150 and solvent delay of 4 min. The analysis methodology used for GC/MS was Scan monitoring (all ions present).

The spectra were analyzed in the NIST2018 library of EI spectra at 70 eV, with an integrated set of data collection, analysis, and interpretation management. Quantification was performed by SCAN mode.

Data processing and statistical analysis

A frequency filter of 80% (only compounds found in 80% of the biological samples within at least one group were considered) was used to assure quality data control. Differential statistical analysis consisted in analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a probability level less than or equal to 0.05 and Benjamini-Hochberghoc test using the MassHunter Profiler Professional program (Agilent Technologies®). The identification of compounds was performed using the software MassHunter Unknowns Analysis 10.0 (Agilent Technologies®) with the NIST 2018 library. Data from the general enrichment analysis were processed using Metaboanalyst 5.0 software. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using MatLab R2019a (MathWorks, USA) with PLS-toolbox (version 8.6, Eigenvector Research Inc, 2010). Chemometric analysis was carried out by processing the data by auto-scaling to give the same importance to all variables with different magnitudes.

Results and discussion

Screening and identification of lipid biomarkers in homozygous and heterozygous beta-casein genotypes from bovine milk

Bovine milk lipids were analyzed using lipidomics to investigate the differences in the lipidome between milk from 54 animals, comprising 20 cows with the A2A2 genotype, 19 animals with the A1A2 genotype, and 15 animals with the A1A1 genotype for beta-casein. The animals belonged to the Gir breed (5%) and Girolando (1/2, 3/4, 5/8 bloodlines). The lactation periods ranged from 3 to 6 months postpartum. Although this experimental design was advantageous for minimizing environmental and management-related variability, thereby allowing a more controlled evaluation of the association between β-casein genotypes and milk lipid composition, it may also limit the extrapolation of the findings to broader dairy production systems. Kaizhen Liu et al. (2024) showed that feeding dairy cows a diet consisting of alfalfa silage can increase the major phospholipid content and polyunsaturated fatty acid composition in raw milk. In addition to diet, the circadian cycle period during which the animal is fed may also influence the milk lipid profile, as demonstrated by the study conducted by Becchi PP et al. (2026), where lipidomics highlighted significant changes in morning milk that involved di- and triacylglycerols, ceramides, and sphingomyelins enrichment.

Initially, 1054 metabolites were identified (Supplementary Material 1). After applying a 75% frequency filter, the number was reduced to 12 metabolites. Subsequently, a statistical analysis of variance (p ≤ 0.05) was performed, considering a fold change (F·C) ≥ 2.0, which resulted in the identification of 12 differentially expressed metabolites across the three genotypes (A1A1, A1A2, and A2A2). It was possible to identify 10 compounds, while 2 compounds were unidentified (NI). Namely: Cholesterol; Cholesta-3,5-diene; Cholest-5-ene-3-ol(3. beta.)-carbonochloridate; cis-vaccenic acid; Hexadecanoic acid, 2-hydroxy-1-(hydroxymethyl)ethyl ester; 9-Octadecenoic acid (Z)-, oxiranylmethyl ester; Octadecanoic acid; Tetradecanoic acid; Methyl palmitate and Dodecane. These identified analytes correspond to four classes of compounds: sterol (3), fatty acids (5), methyl ester (1), and hydrocarbon (1).

Expressed lipid metabolism pathways

To gain more insights and further explore the lipid metabolism associated with the different types of milk studied, the identified lipid metabolites were mapped using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 software. By referencing a library of 400 metabolite sets based on normal human metabolic pathways, five key metabolic pathways were identified as being mediated by these lipids (Fig. 3). These include the metabolism of fatty acyls, prenol lipids, sterol lipids, glycerolipids, and other lipids and lipid-like molecules. As shown in the figure, the fatty acyls pathway displayed the largest bubble and the most intense color, indicating that it is the most significant metabolic (p < 0,05) pathway among those analyzed.Fig. 3Class analysis of the main significantly different lipid metabolic pathways in milk from cows with the A1A1, A1A2, and A2A2 beta-casein genotypes, using Metaboanalyst 5.0 software.

Among the fatty acyls in bovine milk, fatty acids are the most prominent compounds in the group. Milk fat is a natural source of certain bioactive fatty acids with potential benefits to human health [29]. Among them, short-chain fatty acids such as buritic acid can exert antimicrobial activity and affect the pathogenesis of various types of diseases [30]. Scientific studies have shown that fatty acids abundant in ruminants, such as 14:0 and 16:0, have specific properties associated with important cellular and biological functions [31,32]. Another fatty acid expressed in milk is conjugated linoleic acid, which has bioactive properties that modulate inflammation. This metabolite is also involved in the prevention of cancer, obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [29].

Conclusion

This research advances the understanding of the differences in lipid metabolites associated with beta-casein genotypes by demonstrating that A2 milk has a distinct lipidomic signature, with candidate biomarker compounds such as cholesterol, cholesta-3,5-diene, and cholesta-5-eno-3-ol(3. beta.)-carbonochloride, which can assist inspection bodies in authenticating this raw material. More than a descriptive comparison, this study establishes an analytical basis for integrating lipidomics into the quality assessment and differentiation of cow's milk, especially in the context of the growing demand for A2 products. Additionally, by highlighting the participation of lipids in relevant metabolic pathways, with fatty acids being the most prominent, the results open new perspectives for investigating possible nutritional and functional implications of these variations. For future research, it is recommended to expand the analyses to different regions, breeds, feeding strategies, and environmental conditions, are necessary to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of the lipid profile variations identified in the present work.
