Determinants of Steiner Distance Hypermatrices
Joshua Cooper, Zhibin Du

Abstract
Generalizing work from the 1970s on the determinants of distance hypermatrices of trees, we consider the hyperdeterminants of order- k k Steiner distance hypermatrices of trees on n n vertices. We show that they can be nearly diagonalized as k k -forms, generalizing a result of Graham-Lovász, implying a tensor version of “conditional negative definiteness”, providing new proofs of previous results of the authors and Tauscheck, and resolving the conjecture that these hyperdeterminants depend only on k k and n n – as Graham-Pollak showed for k = 2 k=2 . We conclude with some open questions.

Introduction

Graham and Pollak (
[
6
]
) showed that the determinant of the distance matrix
D(T)
of a tree
T
on
n
vertices only depends on
n
, and provided a formula for it:
(1-n)(-2)^{n-2}
. Later, Graham and Lovász (
[
7
]
) went further by exploiting a “nearly diagonalizing” congruence of
D(T)
as a quadratic form, i.e., choosing a matrix
P
so that
P^{T}D(T)P
is diagonal except for its first (or last) row and column. The present authors and Tauscheck (
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
) considered
Steiner distance hypermatrices
of order
k\geq 2
, a natural generalization of distance matrices (which correspond to the case of
k=2
), showing that the
hyperdeterminant
is
0
iff (a)
n\geq 3
and
k
is odd, (b)
n=1
, or (c)
n=2
and
k\equiv 1\pmod{6}
. They conjectured that the hyperdeterminant’s value – not just its nonvanishing – only depends on
n
and
k
. Here, we prove this conjecture and provide new proofs of some of the aforementioned results by showing that the Graham-Lovász near-diagonalization fully generalizes to
k>2
.

Throughout, we consider a tree
T
with vertex set
[|V(T)|]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
where
n=|V(T)|
and edge set
E(T)
. Let
T
be a tree and
S(U)
the Steiner distance of the set
U\subseteq V(T)
, i.e., the fewest number of edges in any connected subgraph of
T
whose vertex set contains
U
. We often suppress braces in this notation, i.e.,
S(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=S(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\})
. Given a vector
\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}
, we write
T_{\mathbf{i}}
for the Steiner tree of
\mathbf{i}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
, i.e., the (unique) subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
so that
|E(T^{\prime})|=S(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
. The interested reader is referred to
[
11
]
for much more on Steiner distance. Fixing
k\geq 1
, write
M
for the order-
k
Steiner distance hypermatrix of
T
, i.e., for
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V(T)^{k}
, define
M_{\mathbf{i}}=S(\mathbf{i})
, and for vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
, write

M(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}):=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}M_{\mathbf{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}x_{ji_{j}},

where we denote the
j
-th component of a vector represented by any bolded letter
\mathbf{u}
by the unbolded
u_{j}
, so that here the
j
-th component of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is written

. Note that
M(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k})
is a multilinear form, and setting
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{c}
for each

yields a
k

k
) in the coordinates of the vector
\mathbf{c}
.

The
hyperdeterminant
of a fully symmetric order-
k
hypermatrix (equivalently: a
k
-form) is, in analogy to determinants, the unique monic irreducible polynomial in the hypermatrix entries whose vanishing is equivalent to the existence of a nontrivial solution to the system of equations given by
\nabla M(\mathbf{x},\ldots,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
, i.e.,

\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k-1}}M_{a\mathbf{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}x_{i_{j}}=0

for each
a\in[n]
. See
[
13
]
for more on the properties of this hyperdeterminant, which parallels the classical theory of determinants and much of which is derived from the theory of resultants via
[
5
]
.

In Section
2
, we show that the action of
M(\cdot,\ldots,\cdot)
on the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{H}_{n}
of
\mathbb{J}
, the all-ones vector, has a particularly compact form. This implies that the map vanishes on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if
k\geq 3
is odd and
n\geq 2
, and it is negative on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if
k\geq 2
is even and
n\geq 3
. This latter negativity property for

is known as “conditionally (strictly) negative definite” in the matrix theory literature, and it has played an important role in explaining the relationship between kernels, metrics, and probability measures, especially in work of Kolmogorov, Schoenberg, and Löwner; see, for example,
[
1
,
10
,
12
]
. We then use a polarization identity due to Thomas to provide a diagonalization of
M
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. In Section
3
, we show that, by taking an appropriate basis of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and a vector other than
\mathbb{J}
not contained in this codimension-
1
subspace, it is possible to nearly diagonalize
M
to a hypermatrix which does not depend on
T
except via
k
and
n
. Since the change-of-basis matrix we employ is the zeta function of
T
considered as a poset rooted at some vertex, it has determinant
1
, implying that the hyperdeterminant of

also does not depend on
T
. We conclude with a conjecture about the sign of

and provide some evidence to support it.

\mathbb{J}^{\perp}

For an edge

, let

be the

Theorem 2.1
.

For a tree
T
on
n\geq 2
vertices and a vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, write
\alpha_{e}=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{e}
and
C=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbb{J}
. Then

M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\left(C^{k}-\alpha_{e}^{k}-(C-\alpha_{e})^{k}\right).

Proof.

First, write
\mathbf{b}_{e}=\mathbb{J}-\mathbf{a}_{e}
, the indicator vector of the set
B(e)
. Note that
\beta_{e}:=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{e}=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbb{J}-\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{e}=C-\alpha_{e}
.
Let
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V(T)^{k}
. Write

to denote
\prod_{j=1}^{k}c_{i_{j}}
if
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}S(\mathbf{i})c^{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}\sum_{e\in E(T_{\mathbf{i}})}c^{\mathbf{i}}.

Consider the inner sum above; its terms are functions of a pair
(\mathbf{i},e)
, where
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in V(T)^{k}
and
e\in E(T_{\mathbf{i}})
. We may decompose this set of pairs according to the coordinates of
\mathbf{i}
which belong to
A(e)
versus
B(e)
. That is, for each
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V(T)^{\ast}
, let
E(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
denote the set of edges

of
T
for which
u\in A(e)
for all
u
a coordinate of
\mathbf{u}
and
v\in B(e)
for all
v
a coordinate of
\mathbf{v}
. If
J=\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{t}\}\subseteq[k]
, write
\mathbf{i}_{J}
for
(i_{j_{1}},\ldots,i_{j_{t}})
. Note that
e\in E(T_{\mathbf{i}})
iff
e\in E(\mathbf{i}_{J},\mathbf{i}_{[k]\setminus J})
for some
J
with
1\leq|J|\leq k-1
. Then we may write

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}\sum_{e\in E(T_{\mathbf{i}})}c^{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[k]\\
1\leq|J|\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{e\in E(\mathbf{i}_{J},\mathbf{i}_{[k]\setminus J})}c^{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[k]\\
1\leq|J|\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}}\sum_{e\in E(\mathbf{i}_{J},\mathbf{i}_{[k]\setminus J})}c^{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\sum_{J\in\binom{[k]}{t}}\sum_{e\in E(T)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in V(T)^{k}\\
\text{s.t. }\mathbf{i}_{J}\in A(e)^{t}\\
\text{\& }\mathbf{i}_{[k]\setminus J}\in B(e)^{k-t}\end{subarray}}c^{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\sum_{J\in\binom{[k]}{t}}\sum_{e\in E(T)}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in A(e)^{t}}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in B(e)^{k-t}}c^{\mathbf{u}}c^{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in A(e)^{t}}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in B(e)^{k-t}}c^{\mathbf{u}}c^{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}\left(\sum_{u\in A(e)}c_{u}\right)^{t}\left(\sum_{v\in B(e)}c_{v}\right)^{k-t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}\alpha_{e}^{t}\beta_{e}^{k-t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\left((\alpha_{e}+\beta_{e})^{k}-\alpha_{e}^{k}-\beta_{e}^{k}\right),

from which the desired conclusion follows.
∎

We now deduce a few corollaries of Theorem
2.1
. The next result, which follows immediately from the preceding proof, states that

as a polynomial in
\{c_{i}\}_{i}
acting on

can be expressed in an exceptionally simple manner.

Corollary 2.2
.

For a tree
T
on
n\geq 2
vertices,
k\geq 2
even, and a vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, if
\alpha^{\prime}_{e}=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{e}
, then

M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})=-2\sum_{e\in E(T)}\alpha_{e}^{\prime k},

so the left-hand side is a diagonal form of Waring rank at most

.

Proof.

Note that
C=0
and
\beta_{e}=-\alpha_{e}
. Furthermore,
\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{e}=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{e}
for any
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Since

is even, then, applying Theorem

yields

M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})=-\sum_{e\in E(T)}\alpha_{e}^{k}-\sum_{e\in E(T)}\beta_{e}^{k}=-2\sum_{e\in E(T)}\alpha_{e}^{k}.

∎

Corollary 2.3
.

For a tree
T
on
n\geq 2
vertices,
k\geq 2
odd, and a vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, then

M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})=0.

Proof.

Note that
C=0
and
\beta_{e}=-\alpha_{e}
, so

\displaystyle M(\overbrace{\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c}}^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\left(C^{k}-\alpha_{e}^{k}-(C-\alpha_{e})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(T)}\left(-\alpha_{e}^{k}-(-\alpha_{e})^{k}\right)=0.

∎

Definition 1
.

An order-
k

\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbb{J}=0

We say that

Theorem 2.4
.

Let

Proof.

We apply the preceding corollary:

Near-diagonalization

Let

(T,\preceq_{v})

-linear form in a particularly simple way across all of

For any

\displaystyle=(U-2(\mathbb{I}_{n-1}^{k}\oplus[0]))(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{\prime})

(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}(-1)^{t-1}\sum_{w=1}^{n-1}c^{\prime t}_{w}c^{\prime k-t}_{n}\right)-2\sum_{w=1}^{n-1}c_{w}^{\prime k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=1}^{n-1}\left(-(c^{\prime}_{w}-c^{\prime}_{n})^{k}+(-c^{\prime}_{n})^{k}+c_{w}^{\prime k}\right)-2\sum_{w=1}^{n-1}c_{w}^{\prime k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=1}^{n-1}\left(c_{n}^{\prime k}-c_{w}^{\prime k}-(c^{\prime}_{n}-c^{\prime}_{w})^{k}\right).

Label the edges of
T
as
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}\}
, where
e_{j}
is the edge incident to vertex
j
whose other vertex is the neighbor of
j
closer to
n
. Write
\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{e_{j}}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha^{\prime}_{e_{j}}
for succinctness. Now,
P^{\prime}_{T}\mathbf{c}
is a vector whose
j
-th coordinate is
\mathbf{a}_{e_{j}}^{\prime T}\mathbf{c}=\alpha^{\prime}_{j}
for
j<n
and whose
n
-th coordinate is
\mathbb{J}^{T}\mathbf{c}
.

From the discussion preceding the statement of the present theorem, it is clear that the first
(n-1)\times(n-1)
principal submatrix of
-ZP_{T}^{\prime-1}
is the identity matrix. It remains to describe its last column, which is
-Z\mathbb{J}/n
. The
j
-th coordinate of
Z\mathbb{J}
is the sum of all entries in the
j
-th row of
Z
, i.e., the number of vertices
v
in
T
so that
j\preceq_{n}v
. Therefore,
-ZP_{T}^{\prime-1}
is the matrix whose first
(n-1)\times(n-1)
principal submatrix is the identity, and whose last column has entry
-|\{v\in T:j\preceq_{n}v\}|/n
in row
j
, which equals
-|B(e_{j})|/n
if
j<n
or
-1
if
j=n
.
Thus,
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}=Z\mathbf{c}=(-ZP_{T}^{\prime-1})(-P^{\prime}_{T}\mathbf{c})
is a vector whose
j
-th coordinate is
|B(e_{j})|C/n-\alpha^{\prime}_{j}
for
j<n
, or
C
for
j=n
, where recall that
C=\mathbb{J}^{T}\mathbf{c}
. Note that

\frac{|B(e_{j})|C}{n}-\alpha^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{(n-|A(e_{j})|)C}{n}-\alpha^{\prime}_{j}=C-\alpha^{\prime}_{j}-\frac{|A(e_{j})|C}{n}=C-\alpha_{j}.

That is,

c^{\prime}_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C-\alpha_{j}&\textrm{if $j<n$}\\
C&\textrm{if $j=n$.}\end{array}\right.

Therefore, we may rewrite (
1
) as

\displaystyle(U-2(\mathbb{I}_{n-1}^{k}\oplus[0]))(Z\mathbf{c},\ldots,Z\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(C^{k}-(C-\alpha_{j})^{k}-\alpha_{j}^{k}\right),

which agrees with the expression for
M(\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c})
in Theorem
2.1
. For any
k
, then

\displaystyle U(Z\mathbf{c},\ldots,Z\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=U(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}(-1)^{t-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c^{\prime t}_{j}c^{\prime k-t}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{t}(-c^{\prime}_{j})^{t}c^{\prime k-t}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left((c^{\prime}_{n}-c^{\prime}_{j})^{k}-c^{\prime k}_{n}-(-c^{\prime}_{j})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(C^{k}+(\alpha_{j}-C)^{k}-\alpha_{j}^{k}\right),

which agrees with the expression for
M(\mathbf{c},\ldots,\mathbf{c})
in Theorem
2.1
when
k
is odd.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

For

\det(M)=\det(U-2(\mathbb{I}_{n-1}^{k}\oplus[0])),

where

is the hypermatrix whose
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})

(-1)^{t-1}

for

. In particular,

only through

Proof.

It follows immediately from Theorem

\det(M)=\det(U-2(\mathbb{I}_{n-1}^{k}\oplus[0]))\cdot\det(Z)^{(k-1)^{n}}.

However,

The following conjecture appears in
[
2
]
, and has been checked numerically for
(k,n)=(4,4)
,
(4,5)
, and
(6,4)
, it is trivially true for
n\leq 3
and
k
odd, and it follows from Graham-Pollak when
k=2
. Write
\sgn(x)
to mean
1
if
x>0
,
-1
if
x<0
, or
0
if
x=0
.

Conjecture 1
.

For any tree on
n
vertices,
\sgn(\det(M))=(-1)^{n-1}
when this quantity is nonzero.

Here we also verify the conjecture for
n=2
and all
k
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Conjecture
1
is true for
K_{2}
and any
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

By
[
2
]
, the Steiner order-
k
hypermatrix eigenvalues of
K_{2}
are
-1
with multiplicity
k-1
, and
\lambda_{j}=[1+\cos(2\pi j/(k-1))+\mathrm{i}\sin(2\pi j/(k-1))]^{k-1}-1
for
j=0,1,\dots,k-2
. For
j=0
, we have
\lambda_{j}=2^{k-1}-1>0
. It is straightforward to see that
\Re(\lambda_{j})=\Re(\lambda_{k-1-j})
for
j=0,\ldots,k-2
. If
j=k-1-j
, then
j=(k-1)/2
, which implies that

\lambda_{j}=\left[1+\cos\pi+\mathrm{i}\sin\pi\right]^{k-1}-1=(1-1+0)^{k-1}-1=-1<0.

Thus, the positive eigenvalues consist of an even number of the
\lambda_{j}
along with
j=0
, i.e., there are an odd number of positive eigenvalues, an odd number of negative eigenvalues, and
\sgn(\det(M))=-1
.
∎

We now reprove the conjecture for
k=2
in order to suggest a route to generalization.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathbf{v}_{0}
be the projection of
\mathbf{v}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and let
\mathbf{v}_{1}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{0}
be its projection onto
\spn(\mathbb{J})
. The linear transformation that maps
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{1}
to
-\mathbf{v}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{1}
is represented by the matrix
A=2J_{n}/n-I_{n}
, where
J_{n}
is the
n\times n
all-ones matrix and
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. Furthermore,
A^{-1}=A
and
A
is orthogonal.

Proof.

Let
A=\frac{2}{n}J_{n}-I_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle A\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}J_{n}\mathbb{J}-\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}n\mathbb{J}-\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{J}.

Next, we show that each element of a spanning set of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is mapped to its negative. For
i\neq j
, let
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
denote the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose
i
coordinate is
1
, whose
j
coordinate is
-1
, and whose other coordinates are
0
. It is straightforward to see that that
\spn(\{e_{ij}\}_{i\neq j})=\mathcal{H}_{n}
and also that
A\mathbf{e}_{ij}=-\mathbf{e}_{ij}
. The rest of the claim follows from the fact that
A^{2}=I_{n}
and
A
is symmetric.
∎

Set

C:=M\left(\frac{2}{n}J_{n}-I_{n}\right)

and

D:=M\left(\frac{2}{n}J_{n}-I_{n}\right)+\left(\frac{2}{n}J_{n}-I_{n}\right)M\,,

where
M
is the distance matrix of an
n
-vertex tree. Clearly,
D
is symmetric, though
C
may not be.

Lemma 4.3
.

D
is positive definite.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be nonzero. Write
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}
where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\spn(\mathbb{J})
; and let
A=\frac{2}{n}J_{n}-I_{n}
. Note that
A\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}
and
A\mathbf{y}=-\mathbf{y}
by Lemma
4.2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{T}D\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{y}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{T})(MA+AM)(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{y}^{T}MA\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{z}^{T}MA\mathbf{z}+2\mathbf{y}^{T}(MA+AM)\mathbf{z}

using the fact that
A
and
M
are symmetric. Continuing the calculation,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{T}D\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{y}^{T}MA\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{z}^{T}MA\mathbf{z}+2\mathbf{y}^{T}MA\mathbf{z}+2\mathbf{y}^{T}AM\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{y}^{T}M\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{z}^{T}M\mathbf{z}+2\mathbf{y}^{T}M\mathbf{z}-2\mathbf{y}^{T}M\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{y}^{T}M\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{z}^{T}M\mathbf{z}>0,

since
M
is positive on
\spn(\mathbb{J})
and negative on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
by Theorem
2.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

The matrix
C
is positive definite.

Proof.

Clearly,
D=C+C^{T}
. It is known that
D
is positive definite in Lemma
4.3
.
That is to say, for any nonzero
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

{\bf x}^{T}D{\bf x}>0\,.

So

{\bf x}^{T}(C+C^{T}){\bf x}={\bf x}^{T}C{\bf x}+{\bf x}^{T}C^{T}{\bf x}=2{\bf x}^{T}C{\bf x}>0\,,

as desired.
∎

Since
C
is positive definite and
\det(2J_{n}/n-I_{n})=(-1)^{n-1}
,

\sgn(\det(M))=\frac{\sgn(\det(C))}{\sgn(\det(2J_{n}/n-I_{n}))}=(-1)^{n-1}.

Unfortunately, the above strategy does not easily generalize to
k>2
, although we obtain a weaker result as follows. Let
A
be the matrix mentioned above representing reflection through
\mathbb{J}
, i.e., so that
A\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{x}
if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
if
\mathbf{x}\in\spn(\mathbb{J})
. Then, for
k=4
, write

C_{1}=M(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,A\cdot)

and

C_{3}=M(\cdot,A\cdot,A\cdot,A\cdot).

Proposition 4.5
.

The order-
4
hypermatrix
C_{1}+C_{3}
is positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{x}\in\spn(\mathbb{J})
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Since
M
is symmetric,
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
and
A\mathbf{y}=-\mathbf{y}
, by multilinearity we have

\displaystyle C_{1}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+(3-1)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\qquad+(3-3)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})+(1-3)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-M(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+2M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-2M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-M(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})

and

\displaystyle C_{3}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+(1-3)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\qquad+(3-3)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})+(3-1)M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-M(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-2M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+2M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})-M(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}).

Thus,

(C_{1}+C_{3})(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})=2M(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-2M(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y})>0

(2)

by Theorem
2.4
and the fact that
M
is nonnegative.
∎
