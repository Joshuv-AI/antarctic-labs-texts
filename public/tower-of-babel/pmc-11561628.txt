Relationship between Xylene Solubles and Crystallization Elution Fractionation of Polypropylene
Authors: Mengxue Shi, Mei Hong, Lin Liu, Rongjuan Cong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Xylene Soluble (XS) is one of the most important parameters closely related to the performance of polypropylene (PP) materials. In order to obtain precise and accurate XS data, ASTM D 5492-17, based on the wet chemistry separation mechanism, strictly specifies each and every step of the procedure, such as a glassware setup, heating and cooling rates, etc. Meanwhile, crystallization elution fractionation (CEF), a newly developed technique, is capable of quantifying polyolefin structural regularity and comonomer distribution in a fast and automated manner. There have been a few pieces of preliminary work aiming to provide XS value from the CEF test with a limited number of samples. It is important to gain deep insights into the relationship between XS and the purge fraction of CEF by studying a large number of diverse polypropylene materials. In this paper, close to 300 commercial polypropylene and/or homemade polypropylene, both homopolypropylene (hPP) and propylene–ethylene copolymers (P/E) made with various catalysts under different conditions, were employed. XS values were obtained via CRYSTEX QC calibration. It was found that XS and the soluble fraction (SF) from CEF yielded a linear relationship with R2 = 0.9942. This linearity is applicable regardless of polymer types, which can differ significantly in terms of molecular weight distribution, tacticity, chemical composition, and melt flow rate. One of the prominent advantages of using the CEF test instead of ASTM D 5492-17 is the elimination of the time-consuming procedure for much-improved precision and high productivity. In addition, the catalytic system was found to have an influence on SF and crystalline fractions of copolymers.

Introduction

Propylene-based polyolefin is one of the most common synthetic polymers widely used in automotive, packaging, household appliances, etc. Its performance is closely related to polymer molecular structures.1−3 Xylene soluble (XS), standing for the amount of the material remaining in xylene at 25 °C, is one of the most critical structural parameters for polypropylene.4,5 ASTM D 5492-17, technically equivalent to the standard ISO 16152-2022,6 “standard test method for the determination of xylene solubles in polypropylene plastics”, is a classic test and also the industry gold standard.7 It consists of four steps: dissolve the polymer sample at a desired concentration under reflux conditions in xylene; cool down and then maintain at 25 °C isothermally for an extended period of time to facilitate the formation of the insoluble portion; separate the insoluble portion from the solubles by filtration; and gravimetrically measure the soluble fraction through evaporating xylene from filtrating and subsequently drying in a vacuum oven. The ASTM method has a few drawbacks: it is time-consuming, takes at least 8 h to complete one sample, and is difficult to automate and use a large quantity of a hazardous solvent. In addition, many polypropylene materials may have a variety of additives, fillers, and impurities that may interfere with XS measurement. Filtration of the insoluble fraction can be painstaking when the soluble fraction has a high molecular weight (high viscosity). Besides xylene, other solvents, such as n-heptane and diethyl ether, were used to provide the amount of soluble components in the specified solvent,8 which are likely related to very low molecular weight and/or very high comonomer contents.9

Various other techniques have been developed to overcome these drawbacks. Low-field benchtop nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) utilizes different NMR responses from amorphous and crystalline parts of the material.10,11 The NMR method is fast and nondisruptive, whereas the quantification of the xylene soluble is provided with a calibration curve constructed by preferably using at least six samples of real products with predefined xylene soluble values. In general, this NMR method applies well for XS equal to or less than 5%, in other words, mainly for some homopolymer polypropylene. Extreme caution must be paid when extending this method to different types of polypropylene materials.

Flow Injection Polymer Analysis (FIPA) was developed in early 2000 by Malvern USA by adopting similar dissolution and precipitation steps as the classic XS method.12 Differently, the solution of the soluble portion of the sample was injected directly and analyzed by a fast but low- or room-temperature gel permeation chromatography (GPC) unit. The amount of the soluble fraction in xylene was quantified with a mass detector. By eliminating the solvent evaporation and subsequent sample drying steps of ASTM D 5492-17, the FIPA test shortened the analysis time. Furthermore, FIPA increased the sample throughput when sample dissolution/precipitation steps were performed in a batch manner.13 When equipped with an autosampler, FIPA was fully capable of analyzing a few dozen of soluble fraction samples in a run queue. Unfortunately, the FIPA instrument was no longer commercially available due to the business model changes of Malvern USA approximately around 2012.

A revolutionary change appeared with the debut of an improved filter based infrared (IR) detector. The IR detector measures the infrared absorbance of carbon hydrogen bonds, deemed with high sensitivity and excellent temperature/pressure stability. CRYSTEX QC was developed by Polymer Char (Spain). With automation and procedure optimization, CRYSTEX QC shortened the classic XS test from 8 to 3.5 h.14 There is one caveat. By definition, CRYSTEX QC measures the amount of the soluble fraction in trichlorobenzene (TCB) or dichlorobenzene (ODCB). To provide an equivalent XS, a correlation between TCB solubles (or ODCB solubles) and XS is constructed by using six samples of real products with predefined xylene soluble values.15 CRYSTEX QC test has another advantage. The IR detector equipped with the composition IR band is capable of providing ethylene content in propylene–ethylene copolymers (P/E) by calibrating with comonomer content measured by 13C NMR.16,17

Crystallization elution fractionation (CEF) was invented by Monrabal in 2007 to measure comonomer content distribution (CCD) or short chain branching distribution (SCBD) of polyolefin materials.18,19 CEF shares a polymer fractionation mechanism similar to that of CRYSTEX QC. CEF recently pioneered providing an equivalent XS value with just a few samples of impact copolymer polypropylene.20

One of the key reasons for authors to choose CEF is that CEF generates superior repeatability, an order of magnitude higher than all of its predecessors.21,22 CEF uses one injector, one column, and one oven, and all of the samples are analyzed exactly the same with the same detecting systems installed.23 Another reason is that CEF takes time less than 2 h for sample analysis rather than 3.5 h from programmed crystallization using CRYSTEX QC. Conceptually, a technique similar to CEF, TREF, which stands for temperature rising elution fractionation, can be another choice.24 However, TREF, even with the commercial TREF instrument and a sample preparation unit with five reactors, is much inferior to CEF.25 Therefore, only highly automatic and highly reproducible CEF was used in this study.16,26

The legitimate question is whether the CEF approach reported by Antinucci et al.20 can be used to provide equivalent XS data for a wide range of propylene-based polymers in addition to the comonomer distribution profile. This paper summarizes a study using ∼300 propylene-based polymers, either commercial products purchased directly at the open market in China or synthesized in our laboratory. The study took approximately three years. In other words, this study provides an excellent summary of using CEF and CRYSTEX QC to compare SF obtained at different experimental conditions and its correlation with XS data.

Experimental Methods

Materials

Homopolymer polypropylene (hPP), propylene–ethylene copolymer (P/E), and propylene–ethylene impact copolymers (HIPP) were purchased from various suppliers at the open market in China. 1,2,4-Tricholorobenzene (TCB) was purchased from Honeywell (Muskegon, MI) without further purification. 2,6-Ditert-butyl-1-methylphenol (BHT) and n-heptane (Superdry grade) were purchased from J&K.

Results and Discussion

Conversion Factor for Xylene Solubles by CRYSTEX QC

CRYSTEX QC measures the soluble fraction remaining in TCB at 40 °C. The equivalent XS value was computed from a calibration curve according to Jeremić.14Figure 1 shows the correlation between SFCRYSTEX QC and the predefined xylene soluble values of the five reference materials provided by Polymer Char. An excellent agreement was obtained with an R2 of 0.9990. In other words, the equivalent XS of the sample can be calculated from SFCRYSTEX QC by multiplying a factor of 1.026 for all 293 polypropylene samples.

Calibration between XS by ASTM D 5492-17 and TCB soluble by CRYSTEX QC.

It is recommended that the conversion factor should be determined for each CRYSTEX QC instrument. Polymer crystallization is a kinetic-controlled process, where thermal rates affect the results.27 Each CRYSTEX QC instrument may have some variations in hardware, such as the heating and cooling rates. Variations in the oven performance, for example, may impact actual cooling/heating rates.

One of the important but fragile consumables in CRYSTEX QC is the IR lamp. Two different IR-4 lamps were evaluated with the same CRYSTEX instrument. The same conversion factor was obtained. This indicates that the conversion is linked to a function of thermal profiles, not the light intensity of the IR-4 lamp itself.

Xylene Soluble Measurement Using CEF

Both CEF and CRYSTEX QC were chosen to measure the soluble fraction for all of the 293 samples. Figure 2 plots the SF by the CEF method versus XS calculated from CRYSTEX QC.

SF by the CEF method versus XS calculated from CRYSTEX QC for all 293 propylene-based polymers prepared in-house or purchased commercially from the open market.

The plot clearly demonstrated a linear correlation between XS and SF by CEF. It provides the answer to the question proposed earlier in this paper, to which it is indeed grossly appropriate to use SF by CEF. The xylene soluble number can be obtained by multiplying the soluble fraction percentage by a factor of 0.8068.

Here, XS is the xylene soluble percentage of polypropylene, and SF is the soluble fraction percentage of polypropylene measured by the CEF method. The linear correlation was applicable to commercial grades, Ziegler–Natta (Z–N) catalyst random copolymers, and homopolymer polypropylene. It is easy to derive from eqs 1 and 2 that SFCRYSTEX QC is 78.6% of SFCEF for the 293 samples. The low SFCRYSTEX QC can be understood as the differences in the cooling rate and the extended amount of isothermal equilibrium time in the CRYSTEX QC method (40 min at 40 °C) versus 5 min at 35 °C for the CEF test, where 5 min of soluble time in CEF was optimized by analyzing the standard reference provided by the vendor at a series of soluble time settings. In other words, a general practitioner may adopt this approach according to his/her specific testing parameters by selecting a few samples with predefined XS with a range of XS (for example, from 0 to 25%). This may also reiterate the effects of cooling and heating rates on the crystallization behavior of polypropylene. Some crystallizable polypropylene chains may or may not form crystals depending on the time and temperature, especially at low temperatures such as 35 or 40 °C. Using the CEF method to extract XS data has various advantages, such as superior short-term reproducibility for consecutive injections.12 With the robust temperature calibration proposed by Cong et al.,28 a recent study demonstrated excellent long-term repeatability using six different instruments across different geographic locations over 8 years of time. The CEF instrument is equipped with a robust pump system to perform sample loading and sample elution processes. In addition, CEF also provides a feasible solution to further separate SF from isotactic polypropylene by introducing a higher flow rate during cooling so that the SF can be separated further away from other components to get baseline separation for improved accuracy and precision.

Figure 2 only covers the samples having XS less than 17%. The polypropylene materials containing higher XS have different performances; for example, impact copolymers were used to fabricate automotive parts, such as car bumps. With a limited experience of HIPP, ∼ a dozen of these types of materials were tested. The results showed a large one-way deviation of 12.26%. Herein, the authors may cast some doubts on how accurate XS measurement itself may be for HIPP samples having high XS with high viscosity. This disagreement may have at least 2-fold uncertainties. When HIPP has a high amount and a high-molecular-weight rubber phase, using ASTM D 5492-17 to measure XS can be tricky because of the challenges associated with the filtration step. The insoluble precipitates often block filter paper, making the filtration painstakingly long and messy; replacing filter paper a few times in order to collect enough filtrate happens often based on the authors’ first-hand experiences. This challenge in filtration not only is frustrating but also may change/extend the crystallization isothermal time for the soluble fraction. The crystallization process in CEF happens on the surface of inert substrates; this type of inorganic surface may affect the formation of the initial crystal seed; in other words, this may affect the overall crystallization process. The difference may lead to a substantial difference between the XS data by ASTM D5492-17 and the soluble fraction by the CEF instrument.

It took time to collect and measure these 293 samples. One question is whether the CEF instrument or CRYSTEX QC might have been changed during this period of time purposefully or unintentionally because the two instruments were not equipped with the capability of temperature calibration or pump performance validation. The robust temperature calibration method was not widely published until 2022 (it was published in some patents). To mitigate the possibility of instrumental change during the period, the first half of the data are plotted in Figure 3. Clearly, 147 samples yielded a consistent correlation between XS and SFCEF.

Here, XSFH is the xylene soluble percentage of first-half data, and SFFH is the soluble fraction percentage of first-half data from CEF. This result is indeed a piece of great news. First, the data speak for the reliability of both the CEF and CRYSTEX QC instruments. Second, using the dispenser to elute sample solution in CRYSTEX QC is indeed proven to be as reliable and precise as a high-performance liquid pump (with the experimental parameters set in this paper) with the flow rate setting specified in this paper. Also, it is reasonable to assume that TCB provided by the vendor has good product consistency or that possible impurities in TCB have an equivalent effect on CRYSTEX QC analysis and CEF analysis.

Xylene soluble and soluble fraction percentages CEF for first-half data.

Among the total 293 samples, 93 of them were propylene–ethylene copolymers. While we believe higher SF values require a certain amount of ethylene content, there was no limitation for P/E to reach lower SF values. As shown in Figure 4, XS correlated well with SFCEF. For the samples having SFCEF < 10%, data points are distributed evenly and close to the straight line. For the samples having SFCEF > 10%, the data scattered substantially more from the linear line. This may indicate that the cooling effect may be molecular structure-dependent. For ethylene–propylene copolymers, the soluble fraction stands for the material that remains soluble at low temperature, which can be lower molecular weight ethylene–propyplene copolymers and/or the polymers having a high comonomer content fundamentally different from homopolypropylene. In the homopolypropylene case, the soluble fraction is deemed to be mainly low-Mw atactic polypropylene chains. With a possible effect of Mw on the two methods, some deviation was observed. This volatility is likely to be generated from both CEF and XS. With the reported excellent reproducibility of CEF analysis, it is more likely that the CRYSTEX QC method contributes a higher level of error because the cooling and heating control in the CRYSTEX QC instrument is in no way as reproducible as the CEF instrument.

XS and SFCEF for propylene–ethylene copolymers.

In addition, recently, there have been some improved chromatographic techniques. A column packed with gold-coated nickel may promote the crystallization process and facilitate some crystallizable components to form depositions onto substrates and minimize the value of the SF fraction.29,30

Molecular Structure of hPP versus Propylene–Ethylene Copolymers Made by the Z–N Catalyst

To understand the differences in the molecular architecture of polypropylene, hPP, propylene–ethylene copolymers P/E-1% (sample with 1% ethylene by weight in P/E) and P/E-4% were chosen. For simplicity, P/E-1% stands for the propylene–ethylene copolymer having 1% ethylene (by weight) incorporated. Incorporating a low amount of ethylene into polypropylene chains can substantially modulate melting temperature while maintaining a high stiffness performance. Figure 5 shows the CEF elution profile with SF at 5.46%. The second y-axis is the number of methyl groups per 1000C. It can be quantified with methylene and methyl channels. An SF of 5.46% is pretty high for any commercial applications because SF is fully amorphous and does not contribute positively to any desired performance for homopolypropylene applications. Maintaining the lowest possible SF to minimize the content of small molecules is one of the common goals of polypropylene catalyst research studies. Both SF and crystalline fraction got CH3/1000C at 333CH3/1000C measured by IR-5, indicating the SF majorly being atactic polypropylene. For the content of SF, there are two possibilities, species from mainly atactic polypropylene and/or a minor amount of low-Mw isotactic polypropylene. Truly differentiating these two species is beyond the capability of this study. A thorough study of the Mw effect of the elution temperature is to be conducted in the future. CH3/1000C does not differentiate aPP from iPP; aPP and iPP have the same stoic chemical composition in theory. CH3 groups were oriented along the −CH–CH2– backbone randomly without preferred orientation. This results in a complete amorphous nature of aPP, thus appearing as a soluble fraction. A complete verification can be made through other tests, for example, NMR analysis of the atactic fraction. Figure 5b gives MWD, with a reported Mw of 140 kg/mol and an Mw/Mn of 4.4. The second y-axis in Figure 5b gives CH3/1000C measured by IR-6. CH3/1000C across MWD was consistent at 333 CH3/1000C. The slight uptick of CH3/1000C at approximately log M = 4 or less may suggest a possible end group effect on CH3/1000C. No chain-end deduction was used either manually or by GPC One data processing software. The uptick of CH3/1000C at logM larger than 5.5 points to testing error at low detector responses from both methylene and methyl channels, which is very minor (from 333CH3/1000C to 334CH3/1000C), well within the experiment error of the IR-5 detector. Composition CEF and composition GPC data are in good agreement.

hPP sample CEF (a), hPP sample molar mass distribution (b), P/E-1% sample CEF (c), P/E-1% sample molar mass distribution (d), P/E-4% sample CEF (e), and P/E-4% sample molar mass distribution (f).

Figure 5c shows the CEF elution profile of P/E-1% with a reported SF at 8.0%, typically indicating a copolymer with minimal ethylene content. Both ethylene and propylene produce semicrystalline polymers. An increase in ethylene to propylene reduces the crystallinity, and further, the increase in ethylene until a certain level of ethylene leads to the increase of crystallinity once again. However, for many commercial ethylene–propylene copolymers, having high ethylene content leads to different physical properties, for example, poor solid handling properties when ethylene is above a certain level or a low stiffness. The second y-axis gives CH3/1000C for the SF and crystalline fraction. Based on CH3/1000C data, it is estimated that the ethylene content of SF is 5.2%. In contrast, the crystalline fraction had ∼329CH3/1000C, suggesting the fraction being polypropylene with ∼1% ethylene. This difference in ethylene content of the soluble fraction and the main crystalline peak indicated a multicenter catalytic behavior of Z–N.31,32 As shown in Figure 5d, the composition of GPC by IR-6 provided a gradual increase in CH3/1000C at 327.8. This may be expected for a Z–N catalyst with multiple sites of different abilities for comonomer incorporation.33,34

The results of the P/E-4% sample are shown in Figure 5e. The second y-axis provides the soluble fraction with 15% ethylene content, while the crystalline fraction is yielded with 2% ethylene comonomer, which coincides with the same characteristics of the P/E-1% sample, whereas a much more drastic difference between soluble and crystalline fractions is observed. 15% SF indicates a fairly higher portion of ethylene being incorporated in the soluble fraction. The similarities between P/E-4% and P/E-1% may indicate an interesting observation that favored the comonomer in low-MW polymer chains, resulting in a significant rise in SF. Extra production of SF without putting ethylene in the crystalline fraction will stop the low-temperature behavior from improving as we gradually increase the ethylene content during synthesis. This complied well with our interpretation of the catalytic system, with the second y-axis of Figure 5f GPC showing the same increasing trend of the comonomer content. The slope change showed drastic differences between the SF and crystalline fraction in the GPC curve, which corresponded with the change we observed in the CEF curve. Moreover, the curve further testified that both atactic PP and low Mw parts are contained in SF. The slopes of high Mw and crystalline fraction are similar, indicating that the higher Mw part is naturally more difficult to crystallize. A molar mass of 367 kg/mol and a polydispersity of 5.9 confirmed the similarities with the P/E-1% sample.

For complex molecular structures of P/E made by the Z–N catalyst, two-dimensional cross-fractionation techniques, such as two-dimensional HTLC with a graphite column,35 would be very helpful. It may provide better comprehensive structural information such as the MWD of the soluble fraction with a possibility of achieving a superior resolution.36

This work has focused on polypropylene. It will be interesting to include other types of polyolefins, such as low-density polyethylene (LDPE), to further understand the SF from the point of view of ethylene sequences, as reported in ref (37).

In addition, sample homogeneity is worth noting. CEF uses a small quantity of materials. For some samples made in gas or slurry processes, powdered samples may have chemical compositional variation between particles. For some extreme cases, it is advised to make powdered samples into a plaque for homogenization purposes, and then the plaque is used in subsequent CEF analysis.38

Conclusions

Xylene soluble stands for the amount of the materials remaining in xylene at room temperature. This is a critical parameter for proplyene-based polymers. High amounts of SF in homopolymer polypropylene can substantially surrender the performance of hPP materials. It is a general commercial practice to lower SF as much as possible through catalyst technology, which is a desired outcome. On the other hand, the soluble fraction in the propylene copolymer is closely related to the rubbery performance, an improved impact performance; a high amount of the soluble fraction may lead to a challenge in the material fabrication process; therefore, a low soluble fraction with high ethylene content is a must for many copolymer products.

Using fast and highly reproducible CEF to quantify XS was extensively studied in this paper. Close to 300 Ziegler–Natta-catalyzed homopolypropylene and P/E samples were studied for their XS and SF by CEF. XS and SF were linearly related with R2 = 0.9942. This result indicates that CEF is fully capable of providing a fast approach to measure polypropylene XS, in addition to providing a full-scale elution profile, with a time of less than 2 h. This correlation is applicable to all of the polypropylene materials, either copolymers made with different catalysts or homopolymer polypropylene.

In the future, more samples synthesized by two reactor systems could be further deployed, especially for high-impact polypropylene, which will allow more ethylene content in P/E, yield more flexibility, and be better suited for more complex crystallization behaviors as well as impact resistance potentials. This type of study may help us better understand chain cocrystallization behavior made with specific catalytic systems, which promote material performance but negatively affect the analysis accuracy.39,40
