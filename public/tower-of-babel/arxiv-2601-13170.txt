Global stability of a Hebbian/anti-Hebbian network for principal subspace learning
David Lipshutz, Robert J. Lipshutz

Abstract
Biological neural networks self-organize according to local synaptic modifications to produce stable computations. How modifications at the synaptic level give rise to such computations at the network level remains an open question. 20 proposed a model of a self-organizing neural network with Hebbian and anti-Hebbian synaptic updates that implements an algorithm for principal subspace analysis; however, global stability of the nonlinear synaptic dynamics has not been established. Here, for the case that the feedforward and recurrent weights evolve at the same timescale, we prove global stability of the continuum limit of the synaptic dynamics and show that the dynamics evolve in two phases. In the first phase, the synaptic weights converge to an invariant manifold where the ‘neural filters’ are orthonormal. In the second phase, the synaptic dynamics follow the gradient flow of a non-convex potential function whose minima correspond to neural filters that span the principal subspace of the input data.



1
Introduction

Biological neural networks self-organize according to local synaptic interactions that produce stable computations at the network-level.
A challenge in theoretical neuroscience is to link these local interactions to stable network-level computations.

In a seminal work,
17
proposed a computational model of a neuron as implementing an online algorithm for learning the top principal component of its input data using local, Hebbian synaptic updates.
Oja’s algorithm thus establishes a link between local, Hebbian interactions at the synaptic level and principal components analysis (PCA) at the neuron level.
Moreover, the online (stochastic) algorithm is strikingly stable and data efficient—it is globally stable with a convergence rate that matches the information theoretic lower bound
[
4
]
—suggesting that neural networks with local synaptic updates may also be relevant in machine learning and neuromorphic computing applications.

In the multi-channel setting, much less is known about the stability of neural networks with local synaptic updates.
Following Oja’s work
[
17
]
, there were several extensions to principal subspace analysis (PSA) algorithms that can be implemented in neural networks with local Hebbian and anti-Hebbian synaptic updates
[
5
,
25
,
24
,
10
,
20
,
21
]
; however, these multi-channel networks include recurrent interactions that complicate their analyses and global stability of the networks’ dynamics has not been established.
In another line of work, networks with
non-local
synaptic updates for PSA have been proposed and analyzed
[
15
,
26
,
11
,
16
,
7
,
18
,
22
]
, but these networks do not explain how local interactions give rise to stable network level computations.

The focus of this work is to analyze the global synaptic dynamics of a multi-channel network for principal subspace analysis with Hebbian feedforward synapses and anti-Hebbian recurrent synapses introduced by
20
,
21
and depicted in Figure
1
. Specifically, we show that the ordinary differential equation (ODE) associated with the synaptic dynamics is globally stable in the sense that for almost every initialization, the synaptic weights converge to an optimal configuration associated with PSA. We further show that the dynamics evolve in two phases. In the first phase, the synaptic weights converge to an invariant manifold characterized by orthonormality of the neural filters. In the second phase, the synaptic dynamics follow the gradient flow of non-convex potential function.

2
Hebbian/anti-Hebbian network model

In this section, we first review the Hebbian/anti-Hebbian neural network model for PSA proposed by
20
.
A detailed derivation of the network from a so-called “similarity matching” objective can be found in
[
20
,
21
]
.
We then introduce the ODE associated with the synaptic dynamics, which will be the focus of our analysis in this work.

Consider a network with
k
primary neurons that receives
n>k
inputs, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Feedforward synapses
{\bf W}
connect the inputs to primary neurons, and recurrent synapses
-{\bf M}
connect the primary neurons.
At each timestep
t=1,2,\dots
, the network receives an input vector
{\bf x}_{t}=(x_{t}^{1},\dots,x_{t}^{n})
.
The network operates on two timescales: a fast neural dynamics timescale and a slow synaptic update timescale.

In the first phase, the neural activities of the
k
output neurons, which are denoted by the
k
-dimensional vector
{\bf y}_{t}=(y_{t}^{1},\dots,y_{t}^{k})
, evolve according to fast linear neural dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{\bf y}_{t}(\gamma)={\bf W}_{t}{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf M}_{t}{\bf y}_{t}(\gamma),

which converge to
{\bf y}_{t}={\bf F}_{t}{\bf x}_{t}
, where
{\bf W}_{t}
and
{\bf M}_{t}
respectively denote the states of the feedforward and recurrent synaptic weights at time
t
, and the row vectors of the
k\times n
matrix
{\bf F}_{t}:={\bf M}_{t}^{-1}{\bf W}_{t}
are referred to as the ‘neural filters’. After the neural activities converge, the synaptic weights are updated according to the slow synaptic plasticity rules

\displaystyle{\bf W}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf W}_{t}+2\eta({\bf y}_{t}{\bf x}_{t}^{\top}-{\bf W}_{t})

(1)

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf M}_{t}+\frac{\eta}{\tau}({\bf y}_{t}{\bf y}_{t}^{\top}-{\bf M}_{t}),

(2)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate for the feedforward synapses
{\bf W}
and
\tau
denotes the ratio between the learning rates for the feedforward synapses
{\bf W}
and the lateral synapses
-{\bf M}
. The plasticity rule for the feedforward synapses
{\bf W}
includes a term proportional to the product of the pre- and postsynaptic activities,
{\bf y}_{t}{\bf x}_{t}^{\top}
, so it is referred to as ‘Hebbian’. The plasticity rule for the lateral synapses
-{\bf M}
includes a term inversely proportional to the product of the pre- and postsynaptic activities,
{\bf y}_{t}{\bf y}_{t}^{\top}
, so it is referred to as ‘anti-Hebbian’.

To analyze the stability of their algorithm,
21
considered the continuum limit of the updates. Formally, when the input data
\{{\bf x}_{t}\}
are independent and identically distributed samples with zero mean and fixed
n\times n
covariance matrix
{\bf A}
and the step size
\eta>0
is infinitesimally small, the synaptic dynamics can be approximated by the ODE

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}

\displaystyle={\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}-{\bf W}(t)

(3)

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf M}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}

\displaystyle={\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}-{\bf M}(t).

(4)

The relationship between the online algorithm and the ODE can be made precise for certain time-dependent learning rates under appropriate regularity conditions (e.g., the spectrum of
{\bf M}
is uniformly bounded away from zero)
[
1
,
8
]
.

21
proved that every equilibrium point of the ODE corresponds to an eigen-subspace of
{\bf A}
, and, when
\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, all linearly stable equilibrium points correspond to the
principal
(eigen-)subspace of
{\bf A}
. While their theoretical analysis is informative about the synaptic dynamics near the equilibrium points, it is not informative about the synaptic dynamics away from the equilibrium points, which corresponds to most random initializations.
20
,
21
provide empirical evidence that both the online algorithm and ODE are globally stable, and they benchmark both against comparable algorithms; however, a theoretical analysis of the global dynamics remains an open problem. The focus of this work is to prove global stability of the ODE.

The ODE (
3
)–(
4
) is naturally viewed as the gradient descent-ascent flow (with timescale separation
\tau
) for solving the nonconvex-concave minimax problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bf W}\max_{\bf M}f({\bf W},{\bf M}),

\displaystyle f({\bf W},{\bf M}):=\tr\left(-{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}+{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf M}^{2}\right).

(5)

where the minimization is over the set of
k\times n
matrices
\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
and the maximization is over the set of
k\times k
positive definite matrices
\mathcal{S}_{++}^{k}
. Analogously, the discrete algorithm (
1
)–(
2
) is naturally interpreted as a stochastic gradient descent-ascent algorithm for solving the minimax problem. In general, proving convergence of gradient descent-ascent algorithms for nonconvex-concave minimax problems is challenging and existing results
[
1
,
12
,
3
]
rely on a separation of time-scales (i.e., letting
\tau\to 0
).

, suggesting that a separation of time-scales is unnecessary for proving global convergence and that
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
may be a parameter of particular interest.

3
Global stability of the synaptic dynamics

For the case
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
, we prove global convergence of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) to the desired principal subspace. We assume
{\bf A}
is a positive definite
n\times n
matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
and
k<n
is fixed. The following theorem is our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
.
For every
\tau>0
and
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}:=\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\times\mathcal{S}_{++}^{k}
, there exists a unique solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) with initial condition
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, suppose
\lambda_{k}>\lambda_{k+1}
and
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
. Then there is a set
\mathcal{Z}
with Lebesgue measure zero such that if
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, then the solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
converges, as
t\to\infty
, to the set of equilibrium points
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})
of the ODE such that the neural filters (i.e., the row vectors of
{\bf F}_{\ast}:={\bf M}_{\ast}^{-1}{\bf W}_{\ast}
) are orthonormal and span the principal subspace of
{\bf A}
.

The next two sections and appendix
A
are devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
.
In appendix
A
we prove existence and uniqueness of solutions, which ensures that solutions that are initialized in
\mathcal{D}
remain in
\mathcal{D}
for all
t\geq 0
.
In section
4
, we review results from
[
21
]
on local linear stability of equilibrium points, denoted
\mathcal{E}
. Global convergence is proved in section
5
. We show that the synaptic weights evolve in two phases. In the first phase, for any initialization outside of a null set
\mathcal{N}
, the synaptic weights first converge to the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}
corresponding to orthonormal neural filters; that is, the set of
({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}
such that
{\bf F}:={\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}
has orthonormal row vectors (section
5.1
and Figure
3
). This convergence is captured by a convex Lyapunov function
L({\bf W},{\bf M})
. Then, in the second phase, starting on (or near) the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}
, the synaptic dynamics are approximated by the gradient flow of a non-convex potential function
V({\bf W})
(section
5.2
and Figure
4
). As a result, for almost any initialization, the synaptic weights converge to an equilibrium point such that the neural filters correspond to the desired principal subspace projection.

.

4
Local stability of equilibrium points

Next, we characterize the equilibrium points of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) and recall results by
21
on their linear stability. To this end, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:{\bf G}({\bf W},{\bf M})={\bf 0}\}

denote the set of equilibrium points, where
{\bf G}:\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathcal{D}
is the vector field defined by

\displaystyle{\bf G}({\bf W},{\bf M}):=\left(2{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}-2{\bf W},\frac{1}{\tau}({\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}-{\bf M})\right).

4.1
Characterization of equilibrium points

The following lemma, whose proof is given in appendix
B
, characterizes the equilibrium points in terms of their singular value decompositions (SVDs).

Lemma 1
.

Assume
\tau>0
and
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
. A pair
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if
{\bf W}_{\ast}={\bf U}{\bf S}{\bf V}^{\top}
and
{\bf M}_{\ast}={\bf U}{\bf S}{\bf U}^{\top}
, where
{\bf U}
is any
k\times k
orthogonal matrix,
{\bf V}
is a
d\times k
matrix whose column vectors are orthonormal eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
{\bf A}
, and
{\bf S}
is a
k\times k
diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
{\bf A}
corresponding to the column vectors of
{\bf V}
.

As a consequence of Lemma
1
, if
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}
, then
{\bf W}_{\ast}
is full rank (since we assume the eigenvalues of
{\bf A}
are positive) and
{\bf F}_{\ast}:={\bf M}_{\ast}^{-1}{\bf W}_{\ast}={\bf U}{\bf V}^{\top}
, where
{\bf U}
is a
k\times k
orthogonal matrix and the column vectors of
{\bf V}
are orthonormal eigenvectors of
{\bf A}
. In other words, the row vectors of
{\bf F}_{\ast}
(i.e., the neural filters) are orthonormal and span an eigen-subspace of
{\bf A}
.
In addition, if
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}
, then
({\bf Q}{\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf Q}{\bf M}_{\ast}{\bf Q}^{\top})\in\mathcal{E}
for every
k\times k
orthogonal matrix
{\bf Q}
. In particular, each equilibrium point is an element of a
\frac{k(k-1)}{2}
dimensional manifold of equilibrium points corresponding to an eigen-subspace of
{\bf A}
.

4.2
Local linear stability analysis

For our purposes, we say an equilibrium point
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}
is ‘linearly stable’ if all of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian of
{\bf G}
evaluated at
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})
have nonpositive real part and it is ‘linearly unstable’ if it is not linearly stable; that is, at least one of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian of
{\bf G}
evaluated at
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})
has positive real part. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{E}:\text{the rows of ${\bf W}$ span the principal subspace of ${\bf A}$}\}.

The next result follows immediately from
[
21
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
0<\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
. An equilibrium point
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}
is linearly stable if and only if
({\bf W}_{\ast},{\bf M}_{\ast})\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
.

5.1
Asymptotic orthonormality of the neural filters

In this section, we show that for almost every initialization, the solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) converges to the following invariant subset of matrices in
\mathcal{D}
that correspond to orthonormal neural filters
{\bf F}={\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}:=\left\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}={\bf I}_{k}\right\}.

(6)

To show this convergence, we define the following convex Lyapunov function on
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle L({\bf W},{\bf M}):=\lVert{{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}-{\bf M}^{2}}\rVert^{2}=\tr\left[({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}-{\bf M}^{2})^{2}\right].

(7)

Note that
L({\bf W},{\bf M})
is nonnegative everywhere and equal to zero if and only
({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{O}
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
and
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is a solution of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) with
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
and initial condition
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
. Then

\displaystyle L({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))=L({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})e^{-8t},\qquad t\geq 0.

(8)

Proof.

First, note that the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}-\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf M}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}{\bf M}(t)

\displaystyle=2{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}-2{\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle\qquad-\left[2{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf M}(t)-2{\bf M}(t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-2\left[{\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}-{\bf M}(t)^{2}\right].

Therefore, by the chain rule, the product rule, the cyclic property of the trace rule, and the previous display,

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}L({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))

\displaystyle=4\tr\left[\left({\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}-{\bf M}(t)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}-\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf M}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}{\bf M}(t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-8L({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t)).

Solving the differential equation by separation of variables yields equation (
8
).
∎

To prove that the neural filters are asymptotically orthonormal, we need a technical lemma that states conditions under which the solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
does not converge to zero.
To this end, define the set

\mathcal{N}=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:\exists\,{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\,\lambda>0,\text{ such that}\;{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf v}={\bf 0}\text{ and }{\bf M}{\bf v}=\lambda{\bf v}\}.

(9)

Then
\mathcal{N}
is the set of pairs
({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}
such that
{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}
is singular
and
there is an eigenvector of
{\bf M}
in the null space of
{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}
.
Note that if
{\bf W}
is full rank and
{\bf M}
is positive definite, then
({\bf W},{\bf M})\not\in\mathcal{N}
.
The set
\mathcal{N}
corresponds to the orange vertical line in Figure
3
. The following technical lemma establishes that if for any initialization not in
\mathcal{N}
, the solution to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) remains bounded away from zero and infinity.
The proof is provided in appendix
C
.

Lemma 3
.

The set
\mathcal{N}
has Lebesgue measure zero. Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
and
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is the solution of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) with
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
and starting at
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
. If
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\not\in\mathcal{N}
, then
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))\not\in\mathcal{N}
for all
t\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\left\{\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}\|+\|{\bf W}(t)\|\right\}<\infty.

On the other hand, if
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{N}
, then
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))\in\mathcal{N}
for all
t\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\det({\bf M}(t))=0.

The following corollary of Lemmas
2
and
3
states that almost every solution of the ODE converges exponentially to the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
and
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is a solution of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) with
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
and initial condition
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{N}
.
Then
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
converges to
\mathcal{O}
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3
,
K:=\sup\{\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}\|:t\geq 0\}<\infty
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}-{\bf I}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bf M}(t)^{-2}\|^{2}\|{\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}-{\bf M}(t)^{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K^{4}e^{-8t}\|{\bf W}_{0}{\bf W}_{0}^{\top}-{\bf M}_{0}^{2}\|^{2},

where the first inequality is due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the second inequality follows from Lemma
2
.
∎

5.2
Convergence to equilibrium points

Having shown that
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
converges to
\mathcal{O}
as
t\to\infty
, we now analyze the dynamics when
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is near the set
\mathcal{O}
. To begin, consider the case
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{O}
so that
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))\in\mathcal{O}
for all
t\geq 0
. Then we can rewrite the right-hand side of the ODE (
3
) as a function of
{\bf W}(t)
only:

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}=2({\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}-2{\bf W}(t)=-2\nabla V({\bf W}(t)),

(10)

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the nonconvex potential function

\displaystyle V({\bf W}):=f\left({\bf W},({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\tr\left[-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}+\frac{1}{2}{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}\right],

(11)

where
f({\bf W},{\bf M})
is defined in (
5
).
Therefore, we can interpret the
{\bf W}(t)
dynamics on
\mathcal{O}
as the gradient flow of the potential function
V
. (Note that
V
is only differentiable on the subset of full-rank matrices
{\bf W}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
.) In Figure
4
, we plot the vector field
-\nabla V({\bf W})
in the case
d=2
and
k=1
to illustrate the dynamics of
{\bf W}(t)
on the set
\mathcal{O}
.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
. The function
V
is bounded below. Furthermore,
\nabla V({\bf W})
exists and satisfies
\nabla V({\bf W})=0
if and only if
{\bf W}
is full rank and
({\bf W},({\bf W}{\bf W})^{\frac{1}{2}})\in\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Let
{\bf W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
and let
{\bf W}={\bf U}{\bf S}{\bf V}^{\top}
denote its SVD. Then

\displaystyle V({\bf W})

\displaystyle\geq\tr\left[-\sigma_{\max}({\bf A})({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\sigma_{\max}({\bf A})\tr({\bf S})+\frac{1}{2}\tr({\bf S}^{2})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{k}{2}\sigma_{\max}^{2}({\bf A}),

where
\sigma_{\max}({\bf A})
denotes the spectral norm of
{\bf A}
. Since this holds for all
{\bf W}
, the function
V
is bounded below. Next, suppose
{\bf W}
is full rank and
\nabla V({\bf W})=0
. Then
2({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}=({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}}
and so
{\bf V}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf V}={\bf S}
. Since
{\bf S}
is diagonal, it follows that the column vectors of
{\bf V}
are eigenvectors of
{\bf A}
and the diagonal entries of
{\bf S}
are the corresponding eigenvalues of
{\bf A}
. Thus, by Lemma
1
,
({\bf W},({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}})\in\mathcal{E}
. The converse is readily verified by substitution.
∎

It follows from LaSalle’s invariance principle
[
9
]
, the ODE (
10
) and Lemma
4
that when initialized with
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{O}
, the solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
converges to a fixed point of
V
, which corresponds to an equilibrium point in
\mathcal{E}
. Next, for the general case
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{N}
, we can rewrite the right-hand side of the ODE (
3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}

\displaystyle=-2\nabla V({\bf W}(t))+\left[{\bf M}(t)^{-1}-({\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]{\bf W}(t){\bf A},

where we recall that
{\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top}
is non-singular for all
t\geq 0
by Lemma
3
. By the chain rule,

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!V({\bf W}(t))}{\operatorname{d}\!t}

\displaystyle\leq-2\|\nabla V({\bf W}(t))\|^{2}+\|\nabla V({\bf W}(t)){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}\|\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}-({\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|.

We claim that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|\nabla V({\bf W}(t)){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}\|\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}-({\bf W}(t){\bf W}(t)^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=0.

Assuming the claim holds, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!V({\bf W}(t))}{\operatorname{d}\!t}\leq 0.

Therefore, by LaSalle’s invariance principle,
{\bf W}(t)
converges to the set of fixed points of
V
, which correspond to the set of equilibrium points
\mathcal{E}
. We summarize this result in the following lemma, and provide a detailed proof in appendix
D
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
and
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is a solution to ODE (
3
)–(
4

\tau=\frac{1}{2}
and initial condition

5.3
Proof of Theorem
1

Existence and uniqueness of solutions is shown in Theorem

of appendix

and Theorem

to prove global convergence. Define the subset

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}:({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))\text{ converges to $\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{0}$ as $t\to\infty$}\right\}

of initializations whose trajectories converge to the set of linearly unstable equilibrium points. If

7
Global stability for general synaptic learning rates

Our analysis focused on the special case
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
, where feedforward and recurrent synapses evolve at equal rates.
The choice simplified the structure of the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}
and Lyapunov function
L
, enabling a transparent proof of global convergence.
However, the assumption
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
may appear restrictive and raises the question of whether the same two-phase convergence holds for other
0<\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

3
recently established global stability in the limit
\tau\to 0
, where the recurrent synapses evolve on a separate timescale than the feedforward synapses.
In this regime, the synaptic dynamics also exhibit a two-phase structure, but with separate timescales.
In the first phase, the feedforward weights
{\bf W}
are fixed while the recurrent weights
{\bf M}
evolve towards the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{0}=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}={\bf M}\}
.
As shown in appendix
E.1
, this convergence can be described by the convex Lyapunov function
L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})=\|({\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top})^{2}-{\bf M}^{3}\|^{2}
.
In the second phase, as shown in
[
3
, section 5.3]
, the synaptic matrices
({\bf W},{\bf M})
evolve within the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{0}
and the feedforward synapses
{\bf W}
follow the gradient flow of the non-convex potential function
V_{0}({\bf W})=\frac{3}{2}\|({\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{3}}\|^{2}-\|{\bf W}\|^{2}
.
Notably, both the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{0}
and functions
L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})
and
V_{0}({\bf W})
are distinct from those analyzed here for the case that
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
.

We conjecture that global convergence holds for all
0<\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, with dynamics exhibiting a similar two-phase structure: (i) rapid convergence to a generalized invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
with convergence described by a generalized convex Lyapunov function
L_{\tau}({\bf W},{\bf M})
, and (ii) slower convergence along this manifold to the principal subspace that follows the gradient flow of a generalized non-convex potential function
V_{\tau}({\bf W})
.
Moreover, we conjecture that for each
0\leq\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, there is a function
\Phi_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\to\mathcal{S}_{+}^{k}
mapping feedforward weights to recurrent weights and
p_{\tau}>0
such that

•

the invariant manifold satisfies
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:\Phi_{\tau}({\bf W})={\bf M}\}
,

•

the convex Lyapunov function satisfies
L_{\tau}({\bf W},{\bf M})=\|\Phi_{\tau}({\bf W})^{p_{\tau}}-{\bf M}^{p_{\tau}}\|^{2}
,

•

and the potential function is
V_{\tau}({\bf W})=f({\bf W},\Phi_{\tau}({\bf W}))
, where
f({\bf W},{\bf M})
is defined in (
5
).

Furthermore,
\Phi_{\tau}
and
p_{\tau}
satisfy
\Phi_{\frac{1}{2}}({\bf W})=({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
p_{\frac{1}{2}}=2
, and
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\Phi_{\tau}({\bf W})=({\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{3}}
and
\lim_{\tau\to 0}p_{\tau}=3
.
We anticipate that the conjecture can be proved for
\tau
in a small neighborhood of
\frac{1}{2}
using an application of the following implicit function theorem for dynamical systems (Persistence of Normally Hyperbolic Invariant Manifolds under Perturbations
[
6
]
). The challenge is extending the proof beyond a neighborhood of
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
.
In appendix
E.2
, we provide a numerical method for estimating the invariant manifolds
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
and illustrate the manifolds in the scalar setting
n=k=1
.

8
Discussion

We proved global convergence of solutions to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) when
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
. Our analysis revealed a two-phase structure to the convergence: (1) rapid convergence to an invariant set where the neural filters are orthonormal, and (2) slower evolution along this manifold following the gradient of a non-convex potential function whose minima correspond to the principal subspace. This result provides a rigorous link between local Hebbian/anti-Hebbian interactions and stable network-level computations. Practically, the results suggest that initializing synaptic weights on the invariant manifold (e.g., setting
{\bf M}_{0}={\bf I}_{k}
and
{\bf W}_{0}
to have orthonormal row vectors) could accelerate convergence.

This work is an important step towards proving convergence rate guarantees for the online algorithm analogous to the results established by
4
for Oja’s PCA model of a neuron. The analysis may also provide insight into the global dynamics of related algorithms that can be implemented in neural networks with local Hebbian synaptic learning rules for solving non-negative matrix factorization problem
[
19
]
and networks with local non-Hebbian synaptic learning rules for solving symmetric generalized eigen-subspace problems such as canonical correlation analysis
[
13
,
14
]
.

While the assumption
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
simplifies the analysis and enables closed-form Lyapunov functions, it is also restrictive and may not be biologically realistic.
Future work should extend the proof of global stability to the range
0<\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, as supported by numerical evidence (section
7
and appendix
E
) and analytical results for the regime
\tau\to 0

[
3
]
.

Appendix A
Existence and uniqueness of solutions

In this section, we prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) for any
\tau>0
. A solution of the ODE is a continuously differentiable function
t\mapsto({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathcal{D}
whose derivative satisfies equations (
3
)–(
4
). Recall that
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Frobenius norm.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}
. For any
\tau>0
and
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists a unique solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) with initial condition
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})
.

Proof.

For each
K<\infty
, define the set

\mathcal{D}_{K}:=\left\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:\|{\bf M}^{-1}\|+\|{\bf W}\|<K\right\}.

Since
{\bf G}
is analytic on
\mathcal{D}
and, for
K<\infty
, the closure of
\mathcal{D}_{K}
is compact, it follows that
{\bf G}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{D}_{K}
. Let
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
. Then for each
K<\infty
sufficiently large such that
({\bf W}_{0},{\bf M}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
, there exists a unique solution
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) on the interval
[0,T_{K})
, where

T_{K}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}\|+\|{\bf W}(t)\|\geq K\right\}

is the first time
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
exits the set
\mathcal{D}_{K}
. We are left to show that
T_{\infty}:=\lim_{K\to\infty}T_{K}=\infty
.

Let
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be an arbitrary unit vector and define
a(t):={\bf v}^{\top}{\bf M}(t){\bf v}
for all
t\in[0,T_{\infty})
. Then

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\operatorname{d}\!a(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}={\bf v}^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf v}-a(t)\geq-a(t),

and so
a(t)\geq a(0)e^{-t/\tau}
for all
t\in[0,T_{\infty})
. Since this holds for all unit vectors
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have
\sigma_{\min}({\bf M}(t))\geq me^{-t/\tau}>0
for all
t\in[0,T_{\infty})
, where
m:=\sigma_{\min}({\bf M}_{0})
and
\sigma_{\min}({\bf M})>0
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
{\bf M}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}\|=\sqrt{\tr({\bf M}(t)^{-2})}\leq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\sigma_{\min}({\bf M}(t))}\leq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}e^{t/\tau},\qquad t\in[0,T_{\infty}).

(12)

Next, we have, for all
t\in[0,T_{\infty})
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}(t)}{\operatorname{d}\!t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|{\bf M}(t)^{-1}\|\|{\bf W}(t)\|\|{\bf A}\|+2\|{\bf W}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}\|{\bf A}\|e^{t/\tau}+1\right)\|{\bf W}(t)\|.

By Gronwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|{\bf W}(t)\|\leq\|{\bf W}_{0}\|\exp\left[2\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}\|{\bf A}\|e^{t/\tau}+1\right)t\right],\qquad t\in[0,T_{\infty}).

(13)

Let
T<\infty
be arbitrary. Setting

\displaystyle K:=\max\left\{\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{m}e^{T/\tau},2\|{\bf W}_{0}\|\exp\left[2\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}\|{\bf A}\|e^{T/\tau}+1\right)T\right]\right\}<\infty,

it follows from equations (
12
)–(
13
) that
T_{K}\geq T
. Therefore,
T_{\infty}=\lim_{K\to\infty}T_{K}=\infty
.
∎

.

Appendix C
Bounding the synaptic weights

In this section, we prove Lemma
3

\displaystyle=2\left({\bf v}^{\top}{\bf W}(t){\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf A}-{\bf v}^{\top}{\bf W}(t)\right)=0

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf M}(t){\bf v}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}

\displaystyle=2\left({\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf W}(t){\bf A}{\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf M}(t)^{-1}{\bf v}-{\bf M}(t){\bf v}(t)\right)=-2\lambda{\bf v}.

It follows that for
t>0
,
{\bf W}(t)^{\top}{\bf v}=0
and
{\bf M}(t){\bf v}=\lambda e^{-2t}{\bf v}
,
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))\in\mathcal{N}
. Therefore, for each
t>0
,
{\bf v}
is an eigenvector of
{\bf M}(t)
with eigenvalue
\lambda e^{-2t}
.
It follows that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\det({\bf M}(t))=0
.
∎

Appendix D
Convergence of neural filters to an eigen-subspace

In this section, we prove Lemma
5
.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Suppose
({\bf W}(t),{\bf M}(t))
is a solution to ODE (
3
)–(
4
).
By the definition of
V({\bf W})
in equation (
11
) and the ODE (
3
), we have
(suppressing the dependence on
t
)

\displaystyle\tr\left[\nabla_{{\bf W}}(V({\bf W}))\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}^{\top}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}\right]

\displaystyle=2\tr\left[\left({\bf W}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}\right)\left({\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}-{\bf W}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\tr\left[\left({\bf W}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}\right)\left({\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf W}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\tr\left[\left({\bf W}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}\right){\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}\left({\bf M}^{-1}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-2\|\nabla_{{\bf W}}(V({\bf W}^{\top}))\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tr\left[\left({\bf W}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bf W}{\bf A}\right){\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}\left({\bf M}^{-1}-({\bf W}{\bf W}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right].

3

Appendix E
Stability of the ODE for general timescales

In section
7
we conjectured that the global convergence result proved in the main text for
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
(Theorem
1
) extends to all
0<\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and that the dynamics retain the same two-phase structure: (i) rapid convergence towards a
\tau
-dependent invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
, captured by a convex Lyapunov function
L_{\tau}
, and (ii) slow evolution along
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
governed by the gradient flow of a
\tau
-dependent nonconvex potential
V_{\tau}
, whose minima correspond to the principal subspace.
Here we provide some limited empirical evidence in support of our conjecture.

E.1
Convex Lyapunov function for the case
\tau\to 0

In the
\tau\to 0
regime, the recurrent weights first converge to the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{0}=\{({\bf W},{\bf M})\in\mathcal{D}:{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}={\bf M}\}
while the feedforward weights remain fixed, after which both weights evolve within the invariant manifold.
Here we sketch out an argument showing exponential convergence in the first phase, where we assume that the feedforward weights
{\bf W}
remain fixed; that is,
\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\bf W}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}=0
.
Recall the convex Lyapunov function
L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})=\|{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}-{\bf M}^{3}\|^{2}
from section
7
.
Suppose
{\bf W}
is fixed and
{\bf M}(t)
is a solution of the ODE (
4
).
By the product rule and the ODE (
4
), and suppressing the dependence on
t
, we have

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}{\bf M}^{3}

\displaystyle={\bf M}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}+{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}+{\bf M}^{-1}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}-3{\bf M}^{3}.

Next, by the chain rule, the previous display and the cyclic property of the trace operator, we have

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})

\displaystyle=-2\tr\left[\left({\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}-{\bf M}^{3}\right)\left(2{\bf M}{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}{\bf M}^{-1}+{\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}-3{\bf M}^{3}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-4\left\|{\bf M}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\bf W}{\bf A}{\bf W}^{\top}-{\bf M}^{3}\right){\bf M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|^{2}-2L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})

\displaystyle\leq-2L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M}).

Therefore, in the first phase, the Lyapunov function
L_{0}({\bf W},{\bf M})
converges exponentially to zero.

E.2
Estimation of invariant manifolds in the scalar setting

For each
\tau
, the arrows show the streamlines for the ODE (
3

4
). The solid blue line is a numerical estimate of the invariant set
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}

\mathcal{O}_{0}

Consider the scalar setting
n=k=1
, in which case the critical points are
\pm(a,a)
.
Figure
6
shows the streamlines of the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) for
w>0
, computed using Mathematica’s built-in function
Streamline
function, converging for different
\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
The invariant manifolds
\mathcal{O}_{0}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\frac{1}{2}}
are shown in orange and green, respectively. For intermediate
\tau
, we numerically estimate
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
using the method described in the next paragraph. Note that
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
appears to interpolate between
\mathcal{O}_{0}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\frac{1}{2}}
as
\tau
varies from 0 to
\frac{1}{2}
, and this is supported by further simulations (not shown).

Numerical estimation of
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
for
w>0
.

To estimate
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
when
a<w<2a
, we define a function
h:(0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
as follows. Given a point
(w_{0},m_{0})
, let
(w(t),m(t))
denote the solution of the ODE with initial condition
(w_{0},m_{0})
and set
h(w_{0},m_{0})=\lim_{t\to\infty}(\dot{w}(t),\dot{m}(t))
. A point
(w_{0},m_{0})
is in
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
if
h(w_{0},m_{0})
is parallel to the eigenvector of the Jacobian of the vector field
{\bf G}
with smallest associated eigenvalue. We then perform a numerical search to find a point
(w_{0},m_{0})\in\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
on the line
w=2a
, and then compute the solution of the ODE starting at
(w_{0},m_{0})
to estimate
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
for
a<w<2a
. We implemented this procedure with the help of Mathematica’s built-in functions
NDSolve
and
FindRoot
. To estimate
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
for
0<w<a
, we could use a similar argument starting with points
(w_{0},m_{0})
near the origin.
However, there is an alternative approach that does not require a numerical search.
Rather, we assume that the origin is in the closure of the invariant manifold
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
and the invariant manifold can be linearly approximated when
m
is small; that is the invariant manifold near the origin is well-approximated by
\{(v_{0}m,m):m\approx 0\}
for some
v_{0}>0
. Under this assumption, when
(w_{0},m_{0})
are small, the derivatives of the solutions to the ODE (
3
)–(
4
) at
t=0
are approximately
\dot{w}(0)\approx\frac{2aw_{0}}{m_{0}}
and
\dot{m}(0)\approx\frac{aw_{0}^{2}}{\tau m_{0}^{2}}
. Using the fact that
w_{0}\approx v_{0}m_{0}
and
\dot{w}(0)\approx v_{0}\dot{m}(0)
, we see that
2av_{0}\approx\frac{a}{\tau}v_{0}^{3}
and so
v_{0}=\sqrt{2\tau}
. Therefore, we estimate
\mathcal{O}_{\tau}
by computing a solution starting at
(\sqrt{2\tau}\alpha,\alpha)
for
\alpha>0
small.
This numerical procedure can also be generalized to estimate the invariant manifolds when
d,k>1
; however, visualizing the manifolds is more difficult.
