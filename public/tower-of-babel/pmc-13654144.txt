Multidimensional institutional learning curve of ultrasound-guided percutaneous transthoracic lung biopsy: a cumulative sum analysis with case-mix assessment
Authors: Xiubin Tang, Ming Chen, Chunrong Zhong, Xiao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltrasound-guided percutaneous transthoracic lung biopsy (US-PTLB) provides real-time sampling of peripheral or subpleural lesions, but institutional adoption requires coordinated improvements in efficiency, targeting precision, and safety. We evaluated these domains using sequential cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from a prospectively maintained institutional registry, supplemented by routine procedural records, for 120 consecutive ultrasound-guided percutaneous transthoracic lung biopsies performed from August 2024 to October 2025. Total procedure time, needle trajectory deviation requiring redirection, and overall complications represented workflow efficiency, targeting precision, and patient-centered safety. Cubic polynomial smoothing identified descriptive candidate transitions, and Chow tests provided supportive within-cohort evidence of structural changes. Adjusted models evaluated associations with case sequence and measured case-mix factors.ResultsCandidate transition points occurred at Case 40 for procedure time, Case 64 for trajectory deviation, and Case 75 for complications; all corresponding Chow tests yielded P<0.001. Across three descriptively defined phases, median procedure time was 22.2, 18.2, and 19.4 minutes (P<0.001), trajectory deviation decreased from 22.5% to 14.3% and 2.2% (P=0.017), and complications decreased from 30.0% to 20.0% and 6.7% (P=0.020). Case sequence remained associated with shorter procedure time after adjustment [coefficient, −0.037 minutes per case; 95% confidence interval (CI), −0.051 to −0.024; P<0.001].ConclusionsInstitutional learning was asynchronous across efficiency, targeting precision, and safety. Procedure time alone did not capture procedural maturity. Reductions in trajectory deviation and complications, with sampling quality preserved, support multidimensional registry-based monitoring. These data-derived transitions are institution-specific and require external validation.

Introduction

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous transthoracic lung biopsy (US-PTLB) provides real-time tissue sampling of peripheral or subpleural pulmonary lesions without ionizing radiation during guidance (1-3). Its implementation nevertheless requires continuous coordination of image interpretation, respiratory motion, needle control, specimen handling, and complication surveillance (4). Learning-curve studies of computed tomography (CT)-guided biopsy have evaluated diagnostic and safety outcomes, and cumulative sum (CUSUM) methods have been applied to ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy (5-7). Institutional adoption of US-PTLB remains less well characterized.

CUSUM analysis tracks sequential deviations and can identify candidate within-cohort transitions without predefined phase boundaries (8). Because each CUSUM trajectory is metric-specific, workflow efficiency, targeting precision, and safety may improve at different times. Measured case-mix factors may also influence these patterns and should be considered when interpreting accumulated institutional experience (9).

We evaluated institutional learning in US-PTLB using three complementary outcomes. Total procedure time captured the complete workflow from localization and planning to needle withdrawal; trajectory deviation requiring deliberate redirection measured maintenance of the planned target and safe access route; and complications represented patient-centered safety. Needle passes, maximum specimen length, and diagnostic yield provided complementary information on sampling quality. We also examined whether case sequence remained associated with performance after adjustment for measured patient- and lesion-related factors. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1185/rc).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was a retrospective analysis of data from a prospectively maintained institutional registry supplemented by routine procedural records for consecutive US-PTLB procedures performed in routine clinical practice. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The analysis was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (approval No. 2025LWB622). Written informed consent was obtained for the clinical US-PTLB procedure. The requirement for informed consent for the retrospective analysis was waived because of the observational design, de-identified data use, and minimal additional risk. All patient data were de-identified before analysis. Written informed consent was obtained for publication of anonymized clinical images where applicable.

Consecutive US-PTLB procedures performed between August 2024 and October 2025 were reviewed for eligibility. Eligibility required sonographic visibility of the target lung lesion, a clinical indication for biopsy, and complete procedural and clinical data. Prespecified exclusion criteria included inability to safely access the lesion using the planned ultrasound-guided approach or incomplete key procedural, pathological, or complication data. All 120 consecutive US-PTLB procedures performed during the study period met the eligibility criteria, and no procedure was excluded after eligibility review. No formal sample-size calculation was performed; the study size was determined by the number of consecutive eligible procedures performed during the predefined study period. No analyzed procedure had missing key procedural, pathological, or complication data. These procedures were analyzed in chronological order to reflect the institutional learning trajectory.

Procedural setting and US-PTLB workflow

During the study period, five physicians from the institutional interventional ultrasound team participated in US-PTLB procedures within the departmental workflow. All five remained members of the procedural team throughout the study period, and no physician joined or left the team. Individual procedural contributions and case allocation were not analyzed at the operator level. Most participating physicians had prior experience in diagnostic ultrasonography and basic ultrasound-guided interventions but limited experience with the institutional US-PTLB workflow before study commencement. No dedicated senior lung-biopsy operator with extensive prior US-PTLB experience participated in the workflow. Individual years of experience, prior US-PTLB volumes, and operator-specific case sequences were not collected as structured variables and could not be analyzed.

Lesion localization, trajectory planning, real-time ultrasound guidance, and needle manipulation were generally performed by the physician operator. A separate sonographer was not consistently assigned. Nursing and other supporting personnel assisted according to routine staffing arrangements with patient preparation, sterile assistance, equipment and instrument preparation, and postprocedural monitoring. Nonphysician staffing, team familiarity, and occasional staff rotation varied according to routine scheduling and were not recorded at the case level. A senior interventional ultrasound physician was available to provide general preprocedural planning advice, consultation, or safety oversight when clinically required. This seniority reflected broader interventional ultrasound experience rather than extensive procedure-specific experience in US-PTLB. The frequency, duration, and intensity of these interactions were not systematically recorded; therefore, their effects could not be separated from the institutional learning trajectory. The analysis consequently reflects team-based institutional learning rather than isolated operator learning.

Preprocedural imaging was reviewed to determine lesion size, lesion location, and the planned needle trajectory. Lesion size was defined as the maximum lesion diameter measured on preprocedural imaging. Needle path length was measured from the planned skin entry point to the proximal margin of the target lesion along the intended puncture trajectory. When clinically indicated, contrast-enhanced ultrasound was used to identify viable enhancing regions and avoid necrotic or non-enhancing areas during puncture planning.

US-PTLB procedures were performed using a color Doppler ultrasound system (Resona 8, Mindray, Shenzhen, China). Probe selection was based on lesion depth and the available sonographic window. A convex-array transducer was used for deeper lesions or when a wider sonographic window was required, whereas an 8–12 MHz linear-array transducer was used for superficial subpleural lesions to improve near-field spatial resolution. Core tissue specimens were obtained using an 18-gauge, 10-cm automated core biopsy needle (ABN18G; Zhejiang Ganeiwei Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) with a 1.9-cm sampling notch through a 17-gauge, 11-cm coaxial introducer needle.

US-PTLB was performed under real-time ultrasound guidance using a coaxial technique. During institutional adoption, extrapleural chest-wall positioning of the coaxial introducer needle was introduced into the procedural workflow as a technical strategy. However, it was not consistently implemented in every procedure, and its use and consistency were not systematically documented at the case level. When used, the stylet-inserted coaxial introducer needle was advanced to and maintained within the extrapleural chest-wall musculature before stylet withdrawal. Its proposed rationale is illustrated as a hypothesis in Figure 1; its independent association with complications was not evaluated.

Data collection and outcome definitions

Total procedure time and number of needle passes were obtained from the institutional registry and routine procedural records. Total procedure time extended from the start of ultrasound localization and trajectory planning after patient positioning to final withdrawal of the biopsy needle.

Needle trajectory-related corrective maneuvers were documented in routine procedural records generated during clinical care. For the present retrospective analysis, needle trajectory deviation was abstracted from these records using a standardized operational definition. All 120 procedural records were reviewed using the same operational criteria. Minor real-time needle adjustments inherent to ultrasound guidance were excluded. A trajectory-deviation event was coded only when the procedural documentation indicated deliberate redirection or repositioning of the coaxial access route or biopsy needle because the planned target trajectory or safe access route could not be maintained, irrespective of whether a new skin puncture was required. For brevity, this retrospectively abstracted variable is referred to as needle trajectory deviation throughout the manuscript.

Number of needle passes was defined as the number of core biopsy needle passages into the target lesion during one procedure.

Patient demographics, lesion characteristics, pathological category, complications, and maximum specimen length were retrieved from medical records, imaging, and follow-up documentation. Pathology was prospectively categorized in the registry as malignant, benign, or non-diagnostic. Detailed histological subtypes and a standardized primary-vs.-metastatic malignancy classification were not consistently captured as structured variables. Diagnostic yield was the proportion of malignant or benign results. Complications were assessed during the procedure and within 7 days, with complete follow-up for all procedures; each affected procedure was counted once. Major complications required an invasive intervention, prolonged treatment, or a clinically significant management change. Minor events required no invasive intervention and were managed with observation or supportive care according to symptoms and imaging. When multiple minor events occurred, the predominant clinically relevant event was used for subtype tabulation.

CUSUM learning curve analysis

CUSUM analysis evaluated three complementary performance domains: operational efficiency, targeting precision, and safety. Total procedure time was continuous and captured the complete workflow; needle trajectory deviation requiring redirection was used as a process-level indicator of targeting precision; and overall complications represented patient-centered safety. CUSUM series were constructed separately for total procedure time, needle trajectory deviation, and overall complications. Sampling quality was assessed separately using needle passes, maximum specimen length, and diagnostic yield. where n is the sequential case number, xi is the observed value for the i-th case, and μ is the overall mean for continuous variables or the overall event rate for binary variables. Needle trajectory deviation and overall complications were coded as 0 or 1 for each case. For plotting, the initial cumulative value was set at zero. Because the CUSUM reference value was derived from the overall cohort mean or event rate, the candidate transition points should be interpreted as within-cohort structural changes rather than externally defined proficiency standards.

Raw CUSUM trajectories were smoothed using cubic polynomial regression, and the dominant peak or turning point was treated as a candidate transition. The cubic polynomial was selected as a parsimonious descriptive global smoother for this 120-case sequence. It allowed one dominant transition to be summarized without prespecifying the number and location of knots required by restricted cubic splines or an initial breakpoint required by segmented regression. More flexible methods could yield multiple local extrema that would be difficult to interpret as a single institutional transition in this modest cohort. The raw trajectories were retained in the figures, and the cubic fit was not used as a confirmatory breakpoint estimator or universal proficiency threshold. Chow tests compared piecewise linear fits of the original CUSUM values before and after each candidate point, with K=2 for intercept and slope. Because candidate selection and testing used the same within-cohort trajectories, the Chow results were supportive rather than independent validation.

The three domain-specific candidate points were summarized in an integrated, data-derived framework used only for descriptive comparisons. Phase 1 comprised Cases 1–40, Phase 2 Cases 41–75, including the targeting transition at Case 64, and Phase 3 Cases 76–120. This framework was not the primary inferential analysis and did not assign greater clinical importance to procedure time than to targeting precision or safety. Continuous case-sequence models reduced dependence on the selected phase boundaries. Procedure time was also interpreted cautiously because it can reflect team organization and equipment preparation as well as operator experience.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Between-phase comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance, the Kruskal-Wallis test, the Chi-squared test, or exact tests, according to variable type, distributional characteristics, and expected cell counts.

For case-mix assessment of total procedure time, a multivariable linear regression model included body mass index (BMI), lesion size, needle path length, target lobe, and case sequence. Case sequence was forced into the model as a chronological proxy for cumulative institutional experience. Lesion size and needle path length were entered in centimeters, and target lobe was coded as lower vs. middle or upper lobe. Exploratory adjusted logistic regressions were performed for trajectory deviation and complications. Only 15 trajectory-deviation events and 22 complication events were available. Parsimonious models were therefore used without data-driven variable selection. Case sequence was entered per 10-case increase, with lesion size, needle path length, and lower-lobe location as covariates. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Given the limited event counts, coefficients may be unstable and were interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory evidence.

The interval between consecutive procedures was calculated from procedure dates, and its association with the change in total procedure time between adjacent cases was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Key performance indicators across learning phases

Total procedure time differed significantly across the three learning phases (P<0.001; Table 3). Median total procedure time was 22.2 minutes in Phase 1, 18.2 minutes in Phase 2, and 19.4 minutes in Phase 3. In the targeting precision domain, the frequency of documented needle trajectory deviation requiring redirection decreased progressively across phases, from 22.5% (9/40) in Phase 1 to 14.3% (5/35) in Phase 2 and 2.2% (1/45) in Phase 3 (P=0.017). The number of needle passes did not differ significantly across phases (P=0.851). Maximum specimen length was also similar across phases, with median values ranging from 1.4 to 1.5 cm (P=0.549). The diagnostic yield was 99.2% (119/120). Only one procedure was categorized as non-diagnostic, corresponding to a non-diagnostic rate of 0.8% (1/120), and this occurred in Phase 1.

Safety-related performance and complications

Overall complications occurred in 22 of 120 procedures (18.3%) and decreased across the descriptive phases from 30.0% to 20.0% and 6.7% (P=0.020; Table 4). Nineteen events were minor and required no invasive treatment. Minor pneumothorax was managed with clinical observation and symptom- and imaging-guided supportive care. Mild hemoptysis and limited pulmonary hemorrhage were managed conservatively with close monitoring, and the single vasovagal reaction received routine supportive measures and observation. Three major pneumothoraces required drainage, including two in Phase 1 and one in Phase 2; no major complication occurred in Phase 3. Pathology comprised 99 malignant, 20 benign, and one non-diagnostic lesion. Diagnosis-stratified complication analysis was not performed because final diagnosis was not included as a preprocedural case-mix factor, detailed tumor origin was unavailable, and the marked imbalance would result in sparse, unstable cells.

Adjusted analyses of procedural performance

In the multivariable linear regression model, cumulative case sequence remained associated with total procedure time after adjustment for BMI, lesion size, needle path length, and target lobe (Table 5). Each additional case was associated with a 0.037-minute decrease in total procedure time (unstandardized β=−0.037, 95% CI: −0.051 to −0.024; P<0.001). No significant associations were observed for BMI, lesion size, needle path length, or lower-lobe location.

Discussion

This study used a multidimensional CUSUM framework to characterize institutional learning in US-PTLB. Candidate transitions occurred at Case 40 for procedure time, Case 64 for trajectory deviation, and Case 75 for complications. The asynchronous timing of these transitions indicates that early efficiency gains did not represent complete procedural maturity. The integrated phases were a data-derived descriptive summary, not an independently validated classification or competency threshold.

Previous learning-curve studies have focused mainly on CT-guided transthoracic biopsy, using diagnostic and complication outcomes at the operator level (5,6). CUSUM methods have also been applied to ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy (7). Our institutional framework extends this work by monitoring efficiency, targeting precision, safety, and sampling quality in parallel. These domains reflect the combined development of operator performance, team coordination, and procedural standardization.

The early change in procedure time is consistent with workflow familiarization. Median time decreased after Phase 1 but was slightly longer in Phase 3 than in Phase 2, arguing against a strictly monotonic, operator-only learning effect. Procedure time reflected both physician performance and unit-level organization. Ultrasound localization and guidance were generally performed by the physician operator, while nursing and other supporting personnel contributed according to routine staffing arrangements through patient preparation, sterile assistance, equipment and instrument preparation, and postprocedural monitoring. Because operator-specific case allocation, nonphysician staffing, team familiarity, staff rotation, equipment preparation, and consultation intensity were not recorded at the case level, their individual contributions to the observed temporal trend could not be quantified.

Targeting precision improved later. Trajectory-deviation events decreased across phases, whereas needle passes and specimen length remained stable and diagnostic yield was 99.2%. Trajectory deviation therefore captured a process-level aspect of planned access that the number of sampling attempts alone did not reflect. Because this variable was retrospectively abstracted from routine procedural documentation, it represents documented corrective redirection or repositioning rather than a prospective record of every minor intra-procedural needle adjustment. The later transition may indicate that consistent needle control, respiratory-motion coordination, and sonographic visualization required more cumulative institutional experience. Stable sampling measures suggest that this improvement did not compromise tissue acquisition, although molecular adequacy depends on additional lesion and procedural factors (10,11).

Coaxial access can stabilize a trajectory but cannot ensure that the initial route is appropriate. Pleural and tract features are relevant to pneumothorax risk, particularly for small subpleural lesions (12,13). Extrapleural chest-wall positioning was incorporated into the institutional workflow during the adoption period, but it was not consistently implemented in every procedure, and its use and consistency were not recorded at the case level. Although the technique has a theoretical rationale, its independent effect was not evaluated. The observed reduction in complications therefore cannot be attributed to extrapleural chest-wall positioning. Prospective case-level documentation and evaluation are required before any preventive effect can be inferred.

Complications declined later than procedure time, but individual subtypes were sparse and only three major events occurred, all of which were pneumothoraces requiring drainage. Known risk factors include lesion size, pleural contact, needle characteristics, and other patient- and technique-related features (14,15). Population-level data further indicate that complication rates after transthoracic needle biopsy vary across clinical settings and patient populations (16). Comparative safety evidence and clinical guidance emphasize structured risk assessment and complication management (17,18). Pneumothorax risk remains influenced by patient-, lesion-, and procedure-related factors (19), while hemoptysis, pulmonary hemorrhage, and early postprocedural pneumothorax surveillance are also clinically important (20,21). Diagnosis-stratified inference was not performed because the registry contained 99 malignant, 20 benign, and one non-diagnostic lesion. Detailed histology and primary-vs.-metastatic status were unavailable as consistent structured variables; therefore, tumor-origin effects on complication risk could not be evaluated.

The adjusted analyses reduced, but did not eliminate, concerns about case mix. Case sequence remained associated with shorter procedure time after adjustment, and inter-procedural spacing was not correlated with adjacent changes in procedure time. Continuous case-sequence models also reduced dependence on the descriptive phase boundaries. Nevertheless, case sequence is a chronological proxy that may incorporate unmeasured changes in workflow, equipment availability, team familiarity, and procedural emphasis. The findings therefore describe temporal institutional learning rather than causal effects.

For quality assurance, procedure time alone is insufficient because efficiency can improve while trajectory control or safety remains unstable. Monitoring should therefore pair efficiency metrics with targeting events, complications, and sampling quality, recognizing that each CUSUM curve is outcome-specific (8). Similar parallel assessment has been used in other procedural fields (22). A staged quality-improvement approach may emphasize workflow consistency and team coordination early, then trajectory control, respiratory-motion management, specimen quality, and complication recognition. Centers should develop monitoring frameworks suited to their staffing, case mix, and workflow rather than apply these candidate points as universal thresholds (23). Extrapleural chest-wall positioning remains hypothesis-generating until evaluated with prospective case-level documentation.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this single-center registry reflects one population, equipment setting, workflow, and team; the candidate transition points are cohort-dependent and require external validation. Second, baseline operator experience was not quantitatively documented. Most participating physicians had limited experience with the institutional US-PTLB workflow, and no dedicated senior lung-biopsy operator with extensive prior US-PTLB experience participated. Operator-specific years of experience, prior procedure volumes, case allocation, and individual learning curves were not modeled. Nonphysician staffing, staff rotation, team familiarity, equipment preparation, and consultation intensity were also not recorded at the case level. Third, needle trajectory deviation was retrospectively abstracted from routine procedural documentation rather than captured in a dedicated prospective registry field. Although all 120 records were reviewed using the same standardized operational criteria, subtle corrective maneuvers may not always have been fully documented at the time of clinical care; therefore, some under-ascertainment or misclassification cannot be excluded. Fourth, the exploratory logistic models included only 15 trajectory-deviation events and 22 complication events, and the estimates may be unstable. Fifth, detailed histological subtypes and primary-vs.-metastatic status were not consistently structured, and diagnosis-stratified analysis was limited by sparse cells. Consequently, the potential influence of tumor origin on complication risk could not be evaluated. Finally, extrapleural chest-wall positioning was not consistently implemented or recorded at the case level, and cannula position and tissue apposition at stylet withdrawal were not systematically captured. Its independent association with complications could therefore not be evaluated. Multicenter prospective studies are needed for external validation and technique-specific evaluation.

Conclusions

Institutional learning in US-PTLB was multidimensional, with distinct within-cohort candidate transitions in efficiency, targeting precision, and safety. Procedure time alone did not capture procedural maturity. Within this single-center cohort, trajectory deviation and complications decreased across descriptively defined phases without an apparent loss of sampling quality, and case sequence remained associated with procedure time after adjustment. These findings support team-based registry monitoring. The transition points are institution-specific and require external validation; complication reduction cannot be attributed to extrapleural chest-wall positioning, which was not consistently implemented or documented at the case level.
