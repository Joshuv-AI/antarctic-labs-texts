Weak solutions to a model for phase separation coupled with finite-strain viscoelasticity subject to external distortion
Thomas Eiter ,, Leonie Schmeller

Abstract
We study the coupling of a viscoelastic deformation governed by a Kelvin–Voigt model at equilibrium, based on the concept of second-grade nonsimple materials, with a plastic deformation due to volumetric swelling, described via a phase-field variable subject to a Cahn–Hilliard model expressed in a Lagrangian frame. Such models can be used to describe the time evolution of hydrogels in terms of phase separation within a deformable substrate. The equations are mainly coupled via a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into both contributions and via a Korteweg term in the Eulerian frame. To treat time-dependent Dirichlet conditions for the deformation, an auxiliary variable with fixed boundary values is introduced, which results in another multiplicative structure. Imposing suitable growth conditions on the elastic and viscous potentials, we construct weak solutions to this quasistatic model as the limit of time-discrete solutions to incremental minimization problems. The limit passage is possible due to additional regularity induced by the hyperelastic and viscous stresses.

Introduction

Mathematical models for coupling multi-phase systems with nonlinear elastic deformation
are fundamental for describing many phenomena in soft matter physics and biology, such as the wetting of soft substrates or the formation of patterns during swelling or deswelling
of hydrogels.
Hydrogels are cross-linked networks of (hydrophilic) polymers dissolved in a liquid,
and they are ubiquitous in nature and have particular applications in medical technology,
for example as scaffolds for cell growth
[
25
]
and mini-cell encapsulations
[
13
]
.
Due to the formation of coexisting regions with different magnitudes of swelling,
mathematical models for hydrogels
have to
take into account phase-separation processes and nonlinear elastic behavior
[
24
,
8
]
.

In this paper we show existence of weak solutions to
such a model.
The system is formulated in a Lagrangian configuration,
and the local deformation
\boldsymbol{v}

arises from the combination of plastic deformation due to liquid absorption
with a viscoelastic deformation
subject to a finite-strain model that allows the consideration of large deformations.
We consider a two-phase diffuse-interface model,
where the phase-field variable
\psi
represents regions with different magnitude of swelling,
are energetically preferred.
The phase-field evolution is subject to a Cahn–Hillard equation,
and the mechanical deformation is described by
a Kelvin–Voigt material model,
thus containing elastic and viscous stresses.
Since the mechanical evolution towards
equilibrium usually happens
at a much faster time-scale than the phase-field evolution,
we consider a quasistatic approximation
and neglect inertial effects in the mechanical equation.
The elastic potential will be chosen in a way that ensures the absence of
local self-penetration,
and we consider a second-grade nonsimple material,
that is, the stored elastic energy depends on the elastic strain and its gradient.
Such elasticity models were first introduced by Toupin
[
48
]
,
and have now become widely accepted in the community
[
16
,
34
,
39
,
49
,
7
,
32
,
5
,
27
]
.
The viscous stress potential will be formulated as a function
in terms of the right Cauchy–Green tensor in order to ensure frame-indifference,
see also Remark
2.7
below.

The phase field and the deformation are coupled via a decomposition of the deformation gradient
into elastic and plastic parts.
Instead of an additive decomposition,
which is suitable for the framework of small strains,
we use a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient.
This concept was first introduced by Kroner
[
26
]
and Lee and Liu
[
28
]
in the finite-strain setting
and has become a common approach employed in diverse applications,
also suitable for modeling (isotropic) swelling
[
1
,
14
,
26
,
29
,
30
,
52
,
31
,
44
,
27
,
20
]
.
The plastic deformation depends on the phase field
in such a way that the effect of swelling is purely volumetric and isotropic
[
20
, Subsect. 14.2]
.

Further,
we impose time-dependent Dirichlet boundary conditions for the deformation field
on some part of the boundary.
These boundary conditions can be used to investigate the influence of a variable distortion on pattern formation
during wetting and dewetting of the substrate.
To treat this time dependence,
we follow
[
47
,
15
]

and express the deformation
\boldsymbol{v}
in
terms of a composition with an
auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}
with time-independent boundary values.
As the mechanical equation is expressed in terms of the deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
,
this leads to another multiplicative structure in our system.

The analytical investigation of models
coupling the Cahn–Hilliard equation with elasticity
started few decades ago with the works by
Carrive, Miranville, and Piétrus

[
9
]
,
Garcke
[
17
]
,
and Miraville
[
35
]
.
Since then, the properties of such models were studied
in various configurations, see
[
18
,
4
,
38
,
23
,
22
,
19
,
37
]

for example.
While all these articles deal with the small-strain setting
and thus with linear elasticity,
the case of large-strain elasticity, which we are interested in,
seems to have been treated in only a few articles.
In
[
42
]
,
related static as well as dynamic models are studied,
where the latter case
requires higher-order regularization terms
and no Dirichlet boundary conditions are considered.
More involved models
considering additional thermodynamic effects were studied in
[
45
,
43
]
.
While these works investigate the systems in a Lagrangian framework,
there are also articles using an Eulerian approach, see the recent articles
[
3
,
2
,
44
]
and references therein.
For a numerical analysis of large class of biological models
related to the one we study here,
we refer to
[
12
,
46
]
.

To show existence of weak solutions to the model considered here,
we first construct approximate solutions
by a monolithic time-incremental scheme.
We obtain solutions
to the time-discretized problem
as solutions to minimization problems
using the direct method of calculus of variations.
Since we consider a hyperelastic stress, which serves as a second-order regularization,
polyconvexity assumptions for the elastic potential are not required.
Moreover, the consideration of a second-grade nonsimple material
ensures sufficient spatial regularity of the mechanical variable
as well as a uniform lower bound for the determinant of the deformation gradient
using the theory by Healey and Krömer
[
21
]

and by Mielke and Roubíček
[
32
]
.
Since the time-dependent boundary conditions and the coupling to the phase-field lead to
a non-stationary problem, the mathematical analysis also requires increased time-regularity of the mechanical variable,
which is derived from the viscous stress terms.
This framework allows us to apply a generalized Korn-type inequality,
originally due to Neff
[
36
]
and Pompe
[
40
]
,
which implies the temporal compactness of the mechanical variables, see also
[
50
,
32
]
.
In summary, this means that the hyperelastic and the viscous stress
serve as a regularization,
which is crucial in order to obtain the weak formulation as the limit
of Euler–Lagrange equations for the discrete minimization problems.
Although these discretized problems will be solved in terms of the
variables
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\psi
,
the formulation of the (time-discrete) Euler–Lagrange equations
and the weak formulation of the problem will be given in terms of the physical variables
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\psi
, or rather the deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
and
\psi

and their derivatives,
see (
2.25
), (
2.26
), (
2.27
) below.

2
Main results

We first introduce the specific model we investigate
in this article,
and we collect the general assumptions on the energy and dissipation potentials, the measure of swelling and the time-dependent Dirichlet data.
After introducing the functional framework,
we state the result on existence of weak solutions.

2.1
The model

Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, describing
the viscoelastic body in a reference configuration,
and a time horizon
T>0
.
The deformation of the body is described by the field
\boldsymbol{v}\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and the field
\psi\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

describes the different phases of liquid absorption that result in swelling or shrinking.
The deformation field
\boldsymbol{v}
and the phase field
\psi

are coupled via a multiplicative decomposition of the
deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
into
an elastic part
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}
and a plastic part
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}
.
More precisely, we let the plastic and the elastic deformation gradients be defined by

\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}(\psi)=g(\psi)\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{v},\psi)=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{g(\psi)}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(2.1)

where
g
is a function that acts as a measure of swelling,
and
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
is the
(d\times d)
-identity tensor.
In particular, swelling is modeled as a purely volumetric and isotropic effect.
Based on this decomposition,
the free energy density is given by

W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)=W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})+W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F})+W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F}),

(2.2)

where
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}
and
\boldsymbol{F}
are related by (
2.1
).
The first two terms represent elastic contributions,
where
W_{\mathrm{el}}
corresponds to a nonlinear elastic energy
depending on the elastic deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}
,
and
W_{\mathrm{hy}}
depends on
\nabla\boldsymbol{F}
,
which leads to a hyperelastic stress in the evolution equations.
The last term
W_{\mathrm{pf}}
is a Ginzburg–Landau free energy,
which combines a double-well potential for
\psi

with a term quadratic in
\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi

that corresponds to a Korteweg stress in the deformed configuration.
For the detailed properties of these potentials, we refer to
Subsection
2.2
.

The evolution of the phase-field variable
\psi
is governed by the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi

\displaystyle=\Delta\mu\,

\displaystyle\text{in }[0,T]\times\Omega\,,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\partial_{\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)-\operatorname{div}\big[\partial_{\nabla\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)\big]

\displaystyle\text{in }[0,T]\times\Omega\,,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\nabla\mu\cdot\nu

\displaystyle=\nabla\psi\cdot\nu=0

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega\,.

(2.3c)

This constitutes
a Cahn–Hilliard equation,
which can be regarded as an
H^{-1}
-gradient flow
[
17
,
22
]
.
More precisely, (
2.3a
) is a diffusion equation
for the phase-field variable
\psi

with constant mobility equal to
1
and chemical potential
\mu
,
which is subject to the constitutive equation (
2.3b
),
given as the variation of the free energy
W
with respect to
\psi
.
Moreover, (
2.3c
) describes Neumann boundary conditions,
which ensures mass conservation.
The evolution of the deformation field
\boldsymbol{v}
is determined by the equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\left[\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}-\Div\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}\right]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }[0,T]\times\Omega\,,

(2.3d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T]\times{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\,,

(2.3e)

\displaystyle[\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}-\dv_{\mathrm{s}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}]\nu

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega\backslash{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\,,

(2.3f)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}:(\nu\otimes\nu)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega\,.

(2.3g)

Here, (
2.3d
) describes a
Kelvin–Voigt-type viscoelastic material at mechanical equilibrium.
In particular, we consider a quasistatical approximation
that neglects inertia terms.
The total stress is
composed of the first-grade elastic stress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}
, the viscous stress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}
, and the elastic hyperstress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}

given by

\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})+\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F}),\quad\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\dot{F}}}V(\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi),\quad\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}=\partial_{\nabla\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}),

(2.3h)

where
V
is a viscous stress potential
that depends on the defomration gradient
\boldsymbol{F}
, its time derivative
\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}
and the phase-field variable
\psi
.
By (
2.3e
) we prescribe the deformation at the
Dirichlet part
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\subset\partial\Omega
of the boundary
via a (time-dependent) function
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
,
while by (
2.3f
) we have natural boundary conditions
for the total stress on the remaining part
\partial\Omega\setminus{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}
.
Here,
\dv_{\mathrm{s}}
is the surface divergence, defined as the trace
of the surface gradient
\nabla_{\mathrm{s}}u=(\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-\nu\otimes\nu)\nabla u
.
Equation (
2.3g
) is a second-order Neumann-type condition
for the hyperstress.
For a justification of these boundary conditions,
we refer to
[
32
]

or
[
27
, Subsect. 2.5.1]
.
The system is completed with initial conditions

(\psi,\boldsymbol{v})(0)=(\psi^{0},\boldsymbol{v}^{0})\qquad\text{in }\Omega

(2.3i)

for the phase field and the deformation.

2.2
Assumptions

For the whole article, we consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
,
with Lipschitz boundary.
Let the Dirichlet part
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\subset\partial\Omega
of the boundary
have positive surface measure
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}({\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})>0
.
We further assume the following:

(A1)

Let
p,q,\beta\in(1,\infty)
satisfy

p\geq 2\beta,\quad\beta>d\quad\text{and}\quad q\geq\frac{\beta d}{(\beta-d)}\,.

(A2)

The elastic energy density
W_{\mathrm{el}}:{\mathrm{GL}}_{+}(d)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is twice continuously differentiable
and satisfies:

(i)

Growth condition
: There are
\alpha,c>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{F}\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{+}(d)
it holds

W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{A})\geq\alpha|\boldsymbol{A}|^{p}+c\det(\boldsymbol{A})^{-q}\,.

(2.4)

(ii)

Control of the stress
:
There is
C>0
such that for
\boldsymbol{F}\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{+}(d)

it holds

\displaystyle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{A}^{T}|\leq C\left(1+W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{A})\right)\,.

(2.5)

(iii)

Uniform continuity of stress
: For
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
there exist
\delta>0
such that for
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{+}(d)
and
|\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{I}_{d}|\leq\delta
it holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A})(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A})^{T}-\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{A}^{T}|\leq\overline{\varepsilon}(1+W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{A}))\,.\end{split}

(2.6)

(A3)

The elastic hyperstress potential
W_{\mathrm{hy}}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a strictly convex
and continuously differentiable function
that satisfies:

(i)

Growth conditions
: There are constants

\gamma,C>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
it holds

\displaystyle\gamma|\boldsymbol{G}|^{\beta}-C\leq W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{G}|^{\beta})\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{G}|^{\beta-1})\,.

(2.8)

(ii)

Uniform continuity of the hyperstress
:
For
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
, there are
\delta>0
and
C>0
such that for
\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
with
|\boldsymbol{G}-\boldsymbol{H}|\leq\delta
it holds

\displaystyle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{H})|\leq\overline{\varepsilon}C(1+|\boldsymbol{G}|^{\beta-1})\,.

(2.9)

(A4)

There are constants
a,b>0
such that the phase-field energy density
W_{\mathrm{pf}}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F})=\frac{a}{4}(\psi^{2}-1)^{2}+\frac{b}{2}|\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi|^{2}\,.

(2.10)

(A5)

The
viscous stress potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is given via a reduced potential
\hat{V}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

V(\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi)=\hat{V}(\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}+\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi)
,
where
\hat{V}
is quadratic in the second argument with

\forall\psi\in\mathbb{R},\ \forall\boldsymbol{C},\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\quad\hat{V}(\boldsymbol{C},\dot{\boldsymbol{C}},\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}:\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{C},\psi)\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}

(2.11)

for a continuous forth-order tensor
\boldsymbol{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
,
and such hat there are constants
c,C>0
with

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in\mathbb{R},\ \forall\boldsymbol{C},\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\quad c|\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}|^{2}\leq\hat{V}(\boldsymbol{C},\dot{\boldsymbol{C}},\psi)\leq C|\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}|^{2}\,.

(2.12)

(A6)

The measure of swelling
g
is a nondecreasing function
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle\exists\,\underline{g},\overline{g}\in\mathbb{R}:\ \forall z\in\mathbb{R}:\quad 0<\underline{g}\leq g(z)\leq\overline{g}\,.

(2.13)

(A7)

The time-dependent boundary data

\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}

are given as a function

\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the regularity

\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathrm{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}))

(2.14)

such that there is
C>0
with

|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})|+|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})|+|\partial_{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})|+|\partial_{t}\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})|\leq C

(2.15)

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,T]
,
and with pointwise invertible spatial derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})

such that

\forall(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ |\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})^{-1}|\leq C.

(2.16)

Remark 2.1
.

One example that satisfies the conditions in
(A2)
and
(A3)
is given by the Ogden-type elastic energy potential
W_{\mathrm{el}}
and the hyperstress potential
W_{\mathrm{hy}}
defined as

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F})=\frac{\alpha}{p}|\boldsymbol{F}|^{p}+\frac{c}{q}(\det(\boldsymbol{F}))^{-q}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}|\boldsymbol{G}|^{\beta}

(2.17)

with
\alpha,c>0
and exponents
p,q,\beta\in(1,\infty)
as in assumption
(A1)
.
Additionally, this choice satisfies static frame-indifference,
see Remark
2.7
for more information.
A natural candidate for the swelling function
g
could be given by
a non-decreasing function
with

g(t)=\begin{cases}\overline{g}\quad&\text{if }t\geq 1+\delta\,,\\
1+at\qquad&\text{if }t\in[-1,1]\,,\\
\underline{g}\quad&\text{if }t\leq-1-\delta\end{cases}

for some
a,\delta>0
and
0<\underline{g}<1<\overline{g}
.
Then
the volumetric swelling effect is linear in
\psi

for
\psi\in[-1,1]
,
it vanishes for
\psi=0
,
and the energetically preferred states
\psi=\pm 1
correspond to
saturated and dry phases.

Remark 2.2
.

By the lower bound (
2.4
),
the energy blows up
if the determinant of the deformation gradient approaches
0
.
This ensures that local self-penetration is avoided.
Since in our analysis the deformation field
\boldsymbol{v}

is continuously differentiable, this further implies its local invertibility.
However, (
2.4
) does not prevent from
global self-penetration of the material.

Remark 2.3
.

In (
2.5
) and (
2.6
)
we formulate assumptions on the classical derivative
\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}

of the elastic potential,
which can also be regarded as the derivative with respect to the elastic part of the deformation gradient,
which will occur as the argument of
W_{\mathrm{el}}
.
However, in our model the elastic stress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}
is determined by
\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{el}}
,
that is, the derivative with respect to the full deformation gradient.
In virtue of (
2.1
), these terms are related by

\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})=\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-T}=\frac{1}{g(\psi)}\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})

(2.18)

Similarly, we observe

\partial_{\psi}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})=\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\partial_{\psi}\left(\tfrac{1}{g(\psi)}\right)=-\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\tfrac{g^{\prime}(\psi)}{g(\psi)^{2}}\,.

Remark 2.4
.

The upper bounds and continuity properties of the
derivatives of the elastic stress and hyperstress potentials
in (
2.5
), (
2.6
),
(
2.8
) and (
2.9
)
will be used to identify minimizers of the time-discretized problem
as solutions of associated Euler–Lagrange equations.
In the time-continuous limit, this leads to a weak formulation
of the mechanical force balance (
2.3d
)
in a Lagrangian frame, see (
2.27
) below.
Observe that the condition (
2.5
)
was used by Ball
[
6
]

for a similar purpose
in the framework without a second-grade elasticity term.
However, the admissible test functions used in the weak formulation in
[
6
]

depend on the weak solution of the problem.
Instead, the formulation in a Lagrangian frame
and with test functions independent of the solution,
which we use here,
seems favorable
from the perspective of numerical implementation.

Remark 2.5
.

The phase-field energy density
W_{\mathrm{pf}}
in (
2.10
)
is composed of a double-well potential and a gradient term
associated to a Korteweg stress.
The first term favors pure phases
\psi=\pm 1
,
while the second one penalizes transitions between these phases
thus modeling capillarity effects.
In order to take into account the phase-field gradient
in the deformed configuration,
we consider here
\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi

instead of
\nabla\psi
.
Choosing
a=o(\varepsilon^{-1})
and
b=o(\varepsilon)
,
the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
formally leads to a sharp-interface model
with surface tension.

Remark 2.6
.

Instead of
(A5)
,
we could choose
the viscous potential
V
quadratic in
\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}

in order to simplify the analysis.
However, for a physically reasonable model,

V
has to satisfy

dynamic frame-indifference
,
which means that for all
\boldsymbol{F},\,\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and all smooth functions
t\mapsto\boldsymbol{R}(t)\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
it holds

V(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F}+\boldsymbol{R}\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi)=V(\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi),

see also
[
27
, Sect. 9]
.
This is equivalent to the existence of a reduced potential
\hat{V}
in terms of the right Cauchy–Green tensor

\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}
and its time derivative
\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}+\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}

as we assume in
(A5)
.
In this regard, the quadratic structure of
\hat{V}
in
2.11
,
which leads to a viscous stress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}

linear in
\dot{\boldsymbol{C}}
,
is the simplest choice that is physically reasonable.
Observe that we allow the forth-order tensor
\boldsymbol{D}
to depend on
\boldsymbol{C}
,
\psi

in an arbitrary (smooth) nonlinear fashion,
but even if
\boldsymbol{D}
is constant,
the viscous potential
V
explicitly depends on
\boldsymbol{F}
.

Remark 2.7
.

To obtain a physically reasonable model,
the elastic stress potential
W_{\mathrm{el}}

and the hyperstress potential
W_{\mathrm{hy}}

has to satisfy
static frame-indifference
:
For all rotation matrices
\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
it holds

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F},\psi)=W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F},\psi)\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{G})=W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})\,

for all
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
,
where
(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{G})_{jk\ell}:=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{R}_{jm}\boldsymbol{G}_{mk\ell}
.
Note that one can also formulate the static frame-indifference of the elastic potentials
and the dynamic frame-indifference of the viscous potential
(see Remark
2.6
)
in terms of the associated stresses
\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}
,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}
, which are given in (
2.3h
), in the following way:
For all rotation matrices
\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
it holds

\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F},\psi)=\boldsymbol{R}\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F},\psi)\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{G})=\boldsymbol{R}\Sigma_{\mathrm{hy}}(\boldsymbol{G})\,,

and for all smooth functions

t\mapsto\boldsymbol{R}(t)\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
it holds

\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}(\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{F}+\boldsymbol{R}\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi)=\boldsymbol{R}\Sigma_{\mathrm{vi}}(\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}},\psi)

for all
\boldsymbol{F},\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
.

2.3
Functional setup

In view of the time-dependent Dirichlet conditions (
2.3e
)
and the growth condition (
2.4
)
for the elastic energy,
the set of admissible deformations at time
t\in[0,T]
is given by

\mathcal{U}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in W^{2,\beta}({\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t)\text{ on }{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}},\,\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{w})>0\text{ in }\Omega\}\,,

(2.19)

which is a closed subspace of
W^{2,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
To avoid this time-dependent function space, we introduce

\mathcal{Y}=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in W^{2,\beta}({\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{id}\text{ on }{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}},\,\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})>0\text{ in }\Omega\}\,,

(2.20)

which is again a closed subspace of
W^{2,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Under the assumption
(A7)
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
induces a bijective mapping between
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{U}(t)
by

\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}(t,\cdot):=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)),

(2.21)

see Lemma
3.1
below.
In particular,
\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,x)
for
x\in{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
and
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t,\cdot)
has positive determinant if and only if
\nabla\boldsymbol{y}
does.

The set of admissible phase fields
\psi
is given by

\mathcal{Z}=H^{1}({\Omega})\,.

Observe that from (
2.3a
) and (
2.3c
), we can conclude conservation of mass, precisely,

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T]:\ \int_{\Omega}\left(\psi(t)-\psi^{0}\right){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}=0\,.

In particular,
\partial_{t}\psi
has vanishing mean value,
and we can formulate the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
2.3a
) as
an
H^{-1}
-gradient flow. To this end, we introduce the mean-free function space
V_{0}
and its dual space by

V_{0}=\big\{\phi\in H^{1}({\Omega})\mid\int_{\Omega}\phi{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}=0\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{V}_{0}=\big\{\phi\in(H^{1}({\Omega}))^{*}\mid\langle\phi,{1}\rangle_{(H^{1})^{*}\times H^{1}}=0\big\}\,.

(2.22a)

This allows us to define the inverse operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}:\tilde{V}_{0}\to V_{0}
of

-\Delta:V_{0}\to\tilde{V}_{0}\,,\quad\phi\mapsto(\nabla\phi,\nabla\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(2.22b)

The space
\tilde{V}_{0}
is endowed with the scalar product

\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\rangle_{\tilde{V}_{0}}=(\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\phi_{1},\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}\phi_{2})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for }\phi_{1},\,\phi_{2}\in\tilde{V}_{0}\,

(2.22c)

and the corresponding norm.
Note that the definition in (
2.22c
) is suitable for constant Cahn–Hilliard mobility as assumed for our system, but it has to be adapted for other mobilities.
Moreover, each function
f\in V_{0}
can be interpreted as an element of
\tilde{V}_{0}
via the mapping

\xi\mapsto\int_{\Omega}f\cdot\xi{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\quad\text{for }\xi\in V_{0}\,,

such that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\tilde{V}_{0}}
is also defined for elements in
V_{0}
.

The free energy density given by (
2.2
)
gives rise to the total free energy

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)=\int_{\Omega}\big[W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}(\psi))+W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y}))+W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y}))\big]{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\,.

Further, the viscous dissipation
\mathcal{V}
is defined via
the viscous dissipation potential
V

and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\colon[0,T]\times\mathcal{Y}\times H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R},

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\psi)=\int_{\Omega}V(\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\boldsymbol{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{2}),\psi){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\,.

Usually, we will consider the case
\boldsymbol{y}_{2}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
,
but other choices will be necessary in the time-discrete setting.

2.4
Existence of weak solutions

We can now define the notion of weak solutions to (
2.3
).
For given initial data
(\boldsymbol{y}^{0},\psi^{0})\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
,
a pair
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi):[0,T]\to\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}

is called a
weak solution

to (
2.3
) if

(i)

\boldsymbol{y}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,

(ii)

\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{*})
,

(iii)

(\boldsymbol{y}(0),\psi(0))=(\boldsymbol{y}^{0},\psi^{0})
,

(iv)

there exists
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
such that for all
{\zeta\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\Omega}))}
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}\psi,\zeta\rangle_{(H^{1})^{*}\times H^{1}}{\,{\mathrm{d}}s}=-\int_{{\Omega}_{T}}\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\zeta\,{\mathrm{d}}(s,x)\,,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{T}}\mu\,\zeta\,{\mathrm{d}}(s,x)=\int_{{\Omega}_{T}}\partial_{\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)\zeta+\partial_{\nabla\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)\cdot\nabla\zeta\,{\mathrm{d}}(s,x)\,,

(2.26)

(v)

for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in L^{2}(0,T;W_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}^{2,\beta}({\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d}))
it holds

\displaystyle 0=\int_{{\Omega}_{T}}\big[

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{e}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F})

(2.27)

\displaystyle+\partial_{\boldsymbol{\dot{F}}}V(t,\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{F}):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F})\mathrel{\text{\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\,}}\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{w}\big]\,{\mathrm{d}}(s,x)\,,

where
\boldsymbol{F}(t,x):=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(t,x))
,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}(t,x):=g(\psi(t,x))\boldsymbol{I}_{d}

and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}}:=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}
.

Here the initial data
\boldsymbol{y}^{0}
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{0}
are related by
\boldsymbol{v}^{0}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(0,\boldsymbol{y}^{0})
.
In this sense, a weak solution
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)
satisfies the initial conditions (
2.3i
).

The weak formulation (
2.25
), (
2.26
) and (
2.27
)
is in a compact form.
Due to (
2.1
) and (
2.10
),
more detailed expressions for the derivatives in (
2.26
)
are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\boldsymbol{A}}W_{\mathrm{el}}\left(\frac{1}{g(\psi)}\boldsymbol{F}\right):\boldsymbol{F}\frac{g^{\prime}(\psi)}{g(\psi)^{2}}+a(\psi^{3}-\psi),

(2.28)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nabla\psi}W(\boldsymbol{F},\nabla\boldsymbol{F},\psi,\nabla\psi)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\nabla\psi}W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F})=b\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi.

(2.29)

In (
2.27
) we replaced the derivative of
W_{\mathrm{el}}

with respect to
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
,
which appears in the elastic stress
\Sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W
,
with a term involving the classical derivative of
W_{\mathrm{el}}
,
see also Remark
2.3
.
The corresponding derivative of
W_{\mathrm{pf}}
is given by

\partial_{\boldsymbol{F}}W_{\mathrm{pf}}(\psi,\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{F}):\boldsymbol{G}=-b(\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi):(\boldsymbol{G}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi),\qquad\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(2.30)

Clearly, (
2.25
) and (
2.26
)
are weak formulations of (
2.3a
)
and (
2.3b
)
in virtue of the boundary conditions in (
2.3c
).
The corresponding statement can also be shown for (
2.27
),
see
[
32
]
for details.

As the main result of this article,
we show existence of
weak solutions to (
2.3
)
globally in time
that satisfy an energy-dissipation estimate.
Here we require a smallness condition that involves
the boundary velocity
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
and the
constants
\alpha
and
\gamma

occurring in the growth conditions for the elastic potential
and the hyperstress potential from
(A2)
and
(A3)
.

Theorem 2.8

(Existence of weak solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
let
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}({\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}})>0
,
and let
the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A7)
hold true.
Then there is

\delta_{0}>0
such that if

\alpha^{-1}\gamma\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{\beta}<\delta_{0},

(2.31)

then there exists a weak solution
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)
to system (
2.3
) such that
the energy-dissipation estimate

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(t),\psi(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\mu|^{2}

\displaystyle{{\displaystyle\mathrm{d}}x}{\,{\mathrm{d}}s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{V}(s,\boldsymbol{y}(s),\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{y}(s),\psi(s)){\,{\mathrm{d}}s}

(2.32)

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{F}(0,\boldsymbol{y}^{0},\psi^{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}\mathscr{F}(s,\boldsymbol{y}(s),\psi(s)){\,{\mathrm{d}}s}\,

holds for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
.

The proof of Theorem
2.8

will be given in Section
5
.

If
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)
is a weak solution as in Theorem
2.8
,
then we can use the transformation (
2.21
),
to obtain the actual deformation

\boldsymbol{v}(t,x):=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(x))

from the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}
.
Then
(\boldsymbol{v},\psi)
has the same regularity as
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)

and satisfies (
2.25
), (
2.26
) and (
2.27
) with
\boldsymbol{F}:=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
.
Thus,
(\boldsymbol{v},\psi)
can also be considered a weak solution to (
2.3
).
Nevertheless, we chose the formulation of Theorem
2.8

in terms of the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}

since it
enables us to identify the term
\partial_{t}\mathscr{F}

in the energy-dissipation inequality (
2.32
)
as the power of external forces
due to the time-dependent boundary conditions.
Moreover, (
2.32
) reveals that the energetic principles behind the
time evolution of the system:
It combines energy storage via the free energy
\mathscr{F}

with energy dissipation due to two effects,
one arising from the chemical potential
\mu
,
and the other due to the viscosity potential
V

via (
2.24
).

Observe that the smallness condition
(
2.31
) is trivially satisfied if
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
is affine linear for every
t\in[0,T]

since then
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}=0
.
In particular, the prescription of time-dependent dilatation or shearing
of the boundary part
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}
is admissible without size restrictions.
If
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}=0
is not satisfied,
the second-order gradient
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}
of the deformation
also contains first-order derivatives of the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}
,
see (
3.1
) below.
These first-order terms thus appear in the hyperstress potential

and
the smallness condition ensures their compensation by the elastic potential

due to the growth condition in assumption

,
so that the free energy
\mathscr{F}
is coercive
in terms of the variables
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)
,
as shown in Lemma
3.4
below.

3
Preliminary results

In this section, we first study how convergence properties
can be transferred
from the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}

or the phase-field
\psi
to convergence of associated functions.
Afterwards, we
derive several properties of the energy functional
\mathscr{F}
.

3.1
Convergence properties

By the construction in (
2.21
), the deformation gradient has a multiplicative structure when expressed in the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}
,
namely

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}(t,x)=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(t,x))\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(t,x)\quad\text{for }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\,,

(3.1)

where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
denotes the derivative of
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
with respect to the second argument
\boldsymbol{y}
.
The second-order gradient of
\boldsymbol{v}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we denote by
\nabla\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}
, is a third-order tensor
with components

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}\right)_{ijk}=\left(\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{ijk}=\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}&={(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{j})}^{T}{(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}},i})}{(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}+(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}},i})^{T}{\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{y}}\,\end{split}

(3.2)

for
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

In the following lemma, we compare Lebesgue and Sobolev norms
of
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})
.
In particular, we conclude that the relation (
2.21
) induces a homeomorphism between

\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{U}(t)

defined in (
2.19
) and (
2.20
),
and that strong convergence transfers from one space to the other.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
be as in
(A7)
.
Then
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t.\cdot)
is bijective for all
t\in[0,T]
,
and there is a constant
C>0
, independent of
t
, such that
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(x))

and all
p\in[1,\infty)
,
we have

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{2p}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}).

(3.6)

Moreover,
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})

defines a homeomorphic mapping from
\mathcal{Y}
to
\mathcal{U}(t)

if
\beta\in(1,\infty)
with
\beta\geq d/2
.

Proof.

Bijectivity of
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
follows directly from
the invertibility of
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
in all points
and Hadamard’s inverse function theorem.
Since
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
and its matrix inverse are bounded,
from (
3.1
) we directly obtain

\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)},\qquad\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

(3.7)

Moreover, we can use Poincaré’s inequality
to conclude

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}})\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C(1+\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)})

and

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{id}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{id}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\nabla(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{id})\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\lVert\boldsymbol{id}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C(1+\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}).

Combining these estimates yields (
3.3
) and (
3.4
).
Similarly, (
3.2
) and (
2.15
)
directly yield (
3.5
).
By multiplying with the inverse matrix
(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}})^{-1}

we further deduce from (
3.2
) that

\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{y}=(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}})^{-1}\big(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{v}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\otimes\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k}):\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\big),

(3.8)

which allows to conclude (
3.6
) from (
2.15
)
and the first-order estimate (
3.7
).

If
\beta\geq d/2
, we have
W^{1,\beta}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2\beta}(\Omega)
.
Hence, the previous estimates show that
\boldsymbol{y}\in W^{2,\beta}(\Omega)

if and only if
\boldsymbol{v}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in W^{2,\beta}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, since

\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t))=\det(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}))\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}),

we see that
\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t))>0
if and only if
\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})>0
.
Therefore, we obtain the bijectivity of the mapping

\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{U}(t)
,
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})
.
To show its continuity, consider a convergent sequence
(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{Y}

with (strong) limit
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
and let
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{v}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})
.
We can choose a subsequence
(\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{m}})_{m}
converging a.e.,
and using the continuity properties of
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
,
we obtain
\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{m}}(t,x)\to\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)
,

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{m}}(t,x)\to\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)

and
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{m}}(t,x)\to\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)

a.e. as
m\to\infty

from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).
In virtue of the Sobolev embeddings
W^{1,\beta}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2\beta}(\Omega)
,
we can use the previous estimates and
Pratt’s theorem (see
[
11
, Ch. 6, Satz 5.1]
for instance)
to conclude that
\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{m}}(t)\to\boldsymbol{v}(t)
in
\mathcal{U}(t)
as
m\to\infty
.
Since the limit is the same for any choice of subsequence,
we obtain convergence of the original sequence
(\boldsymbol{v}_{k})_{k}
.
To show continuity of the inverse mapping
from
\mathcal{U}(t)
to
\mathcal{Y}
,
we can proceed analogously and make use of (
3.8
)
instead of (
3.2
).
This completes the proof.
∎

The following result is fundamental to transfer weak convergence properties of the auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}

to the deformation
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathcal{U}(t)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let assumption
(A7)
hold true.
Let
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, and let
{(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{Y}
be weakly convergent with
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{y}
in
W^{2,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

for
\beta>d
.
Then, for all

it follows

\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{v}(t)\quad\text{in}\quad\mathcal{U}(t)\,.

(3.9)

Proof.

Due to the compact embedding
W^{2,{\beta}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\Subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by Rellich’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\to\boldsymbol{y}\,,\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\to\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\quad\text{ uniformly in }\overline{\Omega}\text{ as }k\to\infty\,.

(3.10)

Since
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\cdot)
are continuous by
(A7)
,
we conclude the convergence

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

\displaystyle\to\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{v}(t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})\,,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

\displaystyle\to\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\boldsymbol{y}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\,C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})\,

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Moreover, for
i,j,\ell\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we have

\displaystyle{\left(\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t,x)\right)_{ij\ell}}

\displaystyle={(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}_{j}^{T}{(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}},i}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}))}{(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}_{\ell}+{(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}},i}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}))_{\ell}}^{T}{(\nabla_{j\ell}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}\,.

The first term converges strongly in
C(\overline{\Omega})
by the regularity assumptions on
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}
and the uniform convergence
from (
3.10
),
and weak convergence of the second term in
L^{\beta}(\Omega)

follows from (
3.11
) and the weak convergence
\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\rightharpoonup\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}
.
This shows the weak convergence

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(t)\rightharpoonup\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}(t)\quad\text{in }L^{\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d})\,

(3.12)

for
t\in[0,T]
.
Summarizing (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) yields (
3.9
).
∎

Next we study the convergence of the plasticity term
g(\psi)

when we have weak convergence in the phase-field variable
\psi
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(\psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\psi_{k}\rightharpoonup\psi
in
H^{1}(\Omega)

as
k\to\infty
.
Let

satisfy assumption
(A6)
,
and define

and
g_{\infty}(x):=g(\psi(x))
.
The following properties are valid:

(i)

For all

it holds

g_{k}\to g_{\infty}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{g_{k}}\to\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\quad\text{in }L^{q}(\Omega)\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty

(ii)

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
(\boldsymbol{G}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and

\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\to\boldsymbol{G}
in
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
. Then

{\frac{1}{g_{k}}}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\to\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\boldsymbol{G}\quad\text{ in }L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})\,.

(3.14)

(iii)

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and let
(\boldsymbol{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
and
\boldsymbol{F}\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
such that
\boldsymbol{F}_{k}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{F}
in
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})

\frac{1}{g_{k}}\boldsymbol{F}_{k}\rightharpoonup\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\boldsymbol{F}\quad\text{ in }L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})\,.

(3.15)

Proof.

From Rellich’s compactness theorem, we first conclude that
\psi_{k}\to\psi
in
L^{q}(\Omega)
for
q\in[1,6)
, with
d\in\{2,3\}
.
Since
|g^{\prime}|
is bounded due to assumption
(A6)
,
we can apply the mean-value theorem to conclude that

\|g(\psi_{k})-g(\psi)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|g^{\prime}(z)|\|\psi_{k}-\psi\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty\,.

Moreover, using that

is bounded from below by (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{g(\psi_{k})}-\frac{1}{g(\psi)}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\underline{g}^{2}}\|g(\psi_{k})-g(\psi)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty\,,

which shows
(i)
.

Concerning
(ii)
,
we estimate

\lVert g_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}-g_{\infty}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert g_{k}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{k}-\boldsymbol{G})\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\lVert(g_{k}^{-1}-g_{\infty}^{-1})\boldsymbol{G}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty\,,

where the first term converges due to the boundedness of

and the second term converges by Lebesgue’s theorem.
We thus conclude (

).

To show

\boldsymbol{H}\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})

.
From (

that
g^{-1}_{k}\boldsymbol{H}\to g_{\infty}^{-1}\boldsymbol{H}
in

.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{g_{k}}\boldsymbol{F}_{k}{-}\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\boldsymbol{F}\right){:}\boldsymbol{H}{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{k}{-}\boldsymbol{F}\right){:}\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\boldsymbol{H}{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{F}_{k}{:}\left(\frac{1}{g_{k}}\boldsymbol{H}{-}\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\boldsymbol{H}\right){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\to 0

as

.
This implies the weak convergence asserted in (

) and concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Properties of the energy functional

We next prove additional properties of the energy functional

defined in (

At first, we show weak sequential compactness of the sublevel sets
provided that the smallness condition (

Lemma 3.4

(Coercivity and weak lower semiconituity of
\mathscr{F}
)
.

Let the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
,
(A6)
and
(A7)

hold true.
Then there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that if

\alpha^{-1}\gamma\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{\beta}<\delta_{0},

(i)

in such a way that there are constants

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)\geq c\big(\alpha\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}+\|\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})^{-1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\gamma\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{\beta}_{W^{2,\beta}(\Omega)}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-c_{1}\big)

(3.16)

for all
(\boldsymbol{y},\psi)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
.

(ii)

For every
t\in[0,1]
,
the functional
\mathscr{F}(t,\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}

is weakly lower continuous
and its sublevel sets are weakly sequentially compact.

Proof.

At first, we show coercivity.
With the notation in (
2.1
), with (
2.21
) and with the definition of
W_{\mathrm{el}}
,
W_{\mathrm{pf}}
,
W_{\mathrm{hy}}
in (
2.17
) and (
2.10
), and for

as in (
2.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)=\int_{\Omega}\Big(W_{\mathrm{el}}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{e}})+\frac{a}{4}(\psi^{2}-1)^{2}+\frac{b}{2}|\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi|^{2}+W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F})\Big){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\\
&\quad\geq\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2p\overline{g}^{p}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{p}+\frac{c\underline{g}^{dq}}{q}J^{-q}+\frac{a}{8}\psi^{4}-\frac{a}{4}+\hat{c}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\Big){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}+\frac{c\gamma}{\beta}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{F}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}^{\beta}\,,\end{split}

(3.17)

with
\boldsymbol{v}(t,x)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}(x))
,
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
and
J=\det(\boldsymbol{F})
,
where we used Young’s inequality to estimate

(\psi^{2}-1)^{2}\geq\frac{\psi^{4}}{2}-1
as well as

|\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla\psi|^{2}\geq{\varepsilon}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}|\boldsymbol{F}|^{2}\geq{\varepsilon}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-\frac{c_{p}\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(1+|\boldsymbol{F}|^{p})

for some
c_{p}>0
,
and we choose
\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{2\alpha}{c_{p}p\overline{g}^{p}}
.
For the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.17
) we
use (
3.1
), (
2.16
) and Poincaré’s inequality
to estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\geq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geq C(\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{id})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}-\|\nabla\boldsymbol{id}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\geq C(\|(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{id})\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}-\|\nabla\boldsymbol{id}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\geq C\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}-\tilde{c}\,.

Moreover,
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(t)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\boldsymbol{y}
implies

J=\det\big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\big)\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})\leq C\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})

by (
2.15
),
and by the boundedness of
\Omega
and Young’s inequality we deduce

\|\psi\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{4}\geq C\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}\geq C\big(\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-1\big).

Using the last three inequalities in (
3.17
) leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)&\geq c\big(\alpha\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}^{p}+\|\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})^{-1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\|\psi\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}^{2}-c_{1}\big)+\frac{c\gamma}{\beta}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{F}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}^{\beta}\,.\end{split}

(3.18)

To treat the second-order term, we set

B\coloneqq\lVert\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)}
and use
(
3.2
)
to observe

\displaystyle\|\nabla\boldsymbol{F}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{T}\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}-\|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}}}\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geq C\|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega)}-B\|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\|_{L^{2\beta}(\Omega)}^{2}\,.

Due to
p\geq 2\beta

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2\beta}_{L^{2\beta}(\Omega)}\leq C(1+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)})\,,

and similarly,

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}+c\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{\beta}_{W^{1,\beta}(\Omega)}-\tilde{c}\,.

Combining these inequalities with (
3.18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)

\displaystyle\geq(c\alpha-C\gamma B^{\beta})\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+c\big(\|\det(\nabla\boldsymbol{y})^{-1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+\|\psi\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{\beta}_{W^{1,\beta}(\Omega)}-c_{1}\big),

which yields (
3.16
)
if

is sufficiently small.

To show weak lower semicontinuity,
we consider
\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)
as a functional
in
(\boldsymbol{F},\psi)
.
Since
W
is a convex function in
(\nabla\psi,\nabla\boldsymbol{F})
,
and weak convergence of a sequence

\mathcal{Y}

yields weak convergence of
(\boldsymbol{F}_{k})=(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(t,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

in

we obtain weak lower semicontinuity of

see
[

for instance.
The weak sequential compactness of sublevel sets now follows from the coercivity
property by a standard argument.
∎

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(t+\theta,\cdot)\right)_{ijk}&={(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{j})}^{T}{\partial_{t}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}}_{i}}(t+\theta,\boldsymbol{y}))}{(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}+\partial_{t}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\mathrm{D}}_{i}}(t+\theta,\boldsymbol{y}))^{T}{(\nabla_{jk}^{2}\boldsymbol{y})}\,.\end{split}

Due to the assumptions

and

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(t+\theta,x))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\text{\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\,}}\partial_{t}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(t+\theta,x))\big|\leq C(1+|\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(t+\theta,x)|^{\beta-1})(|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}+|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}|)

\displaystyle\leq C(|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}(x)|+|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2\beta}+|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{\beta})\,,

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2\beta}+|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{\beta})\,,

which is an integrable bound
since

with

implies

and

by Sobolev embeddings.
Hence, we can apply dominated convergence to conclude time differentiability of
\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{hy}}

with

given in (

).
Using the previous estimate, we further find that

|\partial_{t}\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{hy}}(t,\boldsymbol{y})|\leq\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{\boldsymbol{G}}W_{\mathrm{hy}}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F})\mathrel{\text{\put(0.0,-2.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,0.0){$\cdot$}\put(0.0,2.0){$\cdot$}\,}}\partial_{t}(\nabla\boldsymbol{F})|{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}\leq C\int_{\Omega}1+|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2\beta}+|\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{\beta}{\,{\mathrm{d}}x}.

Since

In total, we conclude the full estimate (

) for

.

By Gronwall’s inequality applied to the functions

\displaystyle|\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)-\mathscr{F}(s,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)|\leq\left(\exp{(c_{1}|t-s|)}-1\right)(\mathscr{F}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)+c_{0})

for all

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{{\mathrm{e}}}(t)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(t)

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}\mathscr{F}_{\mathrm{el}}(t,\boldsymbol{y},\psi)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(|\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{F}_{{\mathrm{e}}}(t))-\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{F}_{{\mathrm{e}}}(s))|\,|\boldsymbol{L}(s)|+|\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{F}_{{\mathrm{e}}}(t))|\,|\boldsymbol{L}(t)-\boldsymbol{L}(s)|\right){\,{\mathrm{d}}x}

For the whole section, we assume that the assumptions

as

Arguing similarly for
(\underline{\boldsymbol{y}}_{M}(t))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

Next we address the interpolants for the phase field.

L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))

).
From the Aubin-Lions lemma
we now conclude

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{M}\to\psi\quad\text{in }C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\quad\text{as }M\to\infty\,.

(5.7)

From (
4.12e
) we further obtain elements
\overline{\psi},\underline{\psi}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))

and a subsequence
such that there exist

\overline{\psi},\underline{\psi}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))

To show that the limits
\psi
,
\overline{\psi}
and

we argue as above to first conclude

Next we show (

and
\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{M}\to\nabla\boldsymbol{y}
