Inpatient Recovery of Function After Knee Replacement: A Longitudinal Evaluation Across Four Sequential Care Pathway Redesigns
Authors: Jelmer Jager, Wim van Houtert, Wim P. Krijnen, Richard Bimmel, Nico L. U. van Meeteren, Thomas J. Hoogeboom, Reinier P. Akkermans, Geert van der Sluis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionInpatient recovery of activities (IRoA) is a key outcome after total knee replacement (TKR), reflecting the patient's ability to regain independence. Preoperative prediction can support shared decision‐making and care planning, but its accuracy may decline as healthcare contexts evolve. This study evaluated: Stability of the predictors used for estimating IRoA across sequential care pathway redesigns.Temporal transportability of prediction models for IRoA over different time periods. MethodsA retrospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted at Nij Smellinghe Hospital (the Netherlands) using routine clinical data from 1853 patients opting for TKR surgery between 2009 and 2020. Four consecutive care pathway periods were studied: Joint‐Care, Function Tailored, Fast Track, and Prehabilitation. IRoA was assessed using the modified Iowa Levels of Assistance Scale (mILAS). Multinomial regression and minimum Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) guided selection of preoperative predictors (e.g., age, ASA score, TUG, DEMMI). Model performance and temporal transportability were evaluated across periods.ResultsPredictor stability was limited across care pathways. Age was consistently retained in all periods for both slow and regular recovery groups. DEMMI was retained in all but the Function Tailored period, while BMI was not retained in any model. ASA and ISAR were variably retained, mainly in later periods and predominantly for slow recovery. TUG was inconsistently retained and primarily selected in earlier periods. Model performance declined when models were applied to later care pathways, with predicted correct proportions decreasing from 0.70 to 0.59, 0.66 to 0.43, and 0.51 to 0.36 across successive transitions. The Prehabilitation model achieved a predicted correct proportion of 0.65 within its own period.ConclusionsPrediction of IRoA after TKR is sensitive to changes in care pathways. Predictor relevance and model performance varied over time, limiting temporal transportability. Ongoing model updating and local recalibration are required to maintain clinical usefulness in evolving care contexts.

Introduction

Total knee replacement (TKR) is considered the most common surgical treatment for end‐stage knee osteoarthritis. In 2024 28.708 TKR procedures were registered in the Netherlands alone [1], and the number of primary TKR procedures is projected to reach 1.26 million procedures worldwide by 2030 [2]. As procedural volumes continue to rise, optimizing perioperative care pathways to ensure safe discharge for every individual and efficient use of healthcare resources remains a key healthcare challenge.

In this study, we present a case study from Nij Smellinghe Hospital in the Netherlands, where in 2009 a cyclic approach to care optimization for persons opting for TKR was initiated [3]. The rationale behind this optimization was to improve the TKR care pathway using person‐centered care as its guiding principle, optimally engaging patients in their perioperative process [3, 4, 5, 6]. A central element of this approach was the systematic collection and use of routine healthcare data on inpatient recovery of activities (IRoA), reflecting patients' ability to independently perform essential daily activities such as walking, transferring in and out of bed, and stair climbing [7, 8, 9]. Regaining independence in these activities is essential for safe discharge to the preoperative living situation [9, 10] and helps reduce risks of social isolation, falls, and subsequent need for rehabilitation and community services [9]. IrOA was used for determining whether a person was ready for discharge from the hospital to the own living situation after surgery.

Routine IRoA data was used to make a preoperative prediction model to support shared decision‐making between the person opting for TKR and healthcare professionals [4]. Based on predicted time to IRoA, individualized discussions were held regarding preoperative preparation, timing of surgery, and planning of the postoperative care trajectory. In this way, routinely collected clinical data was translated into actionable knowledge to discuss personalized care decisions related to a meaningful outcome: to function independently.

Over time, the TKR care pathway at Nij Smellinghe Hospital was influenced by changes in patient characteristics, treatment regimes, reimbursement structures, insurance policies, and broader healthcare regulations. Some of these changes were driven by local insights derived from routinely collected data, while others were externally imposed. Leveraging a rich longitudinal dataset spanning 2009–2020, we retrospectively examined how these transitions in clinical protocols, perioperative strategies, and support systems affected both the time to IRoA and the predictive performance of models estimating time to functional recovery.

To the best of our knowledge, limited research has examined how healthcare system transitions influence the long‐term performance of preoperative prediction models for functional recovery after TKR. This study presents a longitudinal case from Nij Smellinghe Hospital as an example of how routinely collected clinical data can be used within a Learning Health System to support iterative learning, adaptation of care pathways, and evaluation of predictive tools over time [11].

Methods

This retrospective longitudinal cohort study evaluated the stability and temporal transportability of prediction models and was conducted in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [12]. The TRIPOD checklist is available in Table S1.

Learning Health System Context

Since 2009, perioperative care optimization for people opting for TKR in Nij Smellinghe Hospital was guided by a multidisciplinary learning community consisting of physical therapists, nursing staff, orthopedic surgeons, hospital management, and an embedded researcher. Over time, this community expanded to include nurse practitioners, physician assistants, anesthesiologists, and patient representatives. This study was conducted within a local Learning Health System designed to support iterative learning across four interconnected pathways (see Table 1), as described by van Houtert et al. [6, 13].

Performance to Data (P2D)

Routine medical and daily physical function data from patients undergoing a perioperative TKR trajectory were obtained since 2009 from electronic health records. All patients included opted for TKR surgery at Nij Smellinghe Hospital and underwent preoperative screening by a physical therapist.

Data to Knowledge (D2K)

Routinely collected perioperative data was analyzed to generate insight into functional recovery trajectories after TKR and to make prediction models estimating time to IRoA. These analyses informed understanding of factors associated with delayed recovery and supported evaluation of prediction model performance across different care pathway configurations.

Knowledge to Performance (K2P)

Based on the outcomes of data monitoring and evidence‐based insights, TKR care in Nij Smellinghe Hospital underwent incremental and more substantial adaptations, resulting in four care pathways (see Table 1).

Practice to Data (P2D)

The present study represents the P2D phase of this learning cycle, in which accumulated real‐world data was used to evaluate the stability of predictors and the temporal transportability of prediction models across successive care pathway redesigns.

Study Population and Data Sources

The data of all patients opting for primary TKR in the period 2009–2020 were included in this study. Patients who underwent revision surgery or unicondylar knee replacement were not included. Preoperative screening was the standard treatment for patients who opted for TKR surgery. The routine medical and functional data used in this study were obtained from the healthcare administration system. The study population was included based on the knee joint replacement reimbursement codes used for administrative purposes in the Netherlands.

Description of Outcome Measure and Predictors

Outcome Measure

IRoA, defined as the time in days from the day of surgery to the day of full recovery of independence on functional activities was measured using the modified Iowa Levels of Assistance scale (mILAS) [7, 8, 10] and valued with a mILAS total score of 0. The mILAS scores the patients' ability to safely perform five activities of daily life (i.e., supine to sit, sit to supine, sit to stand, walking (with walking aids), and stair climbing) and the degree of assistance needed to perform the activities. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with a total mILAS score of 0 reflecting sufficient IRoA to be safely discharged home [7, 8, 10]. Stair climbing was only assessed if the patient needed to climb stairs at their preoperative living situation. Trained physical therapists assessed the mILAS twice daily a as part of the clinical rehabilitation routine with patients. The physical therapists were not affiliated with the research team and were unaware of the research intentions regarding data collection. The time required for IRoA was calculated during data analysis. IRoA was predefined as fast (< 2 days), regular (2 to < 3 days), or slow recovery (> 3 days). The fast recovery group was the reference group. Throughout the study, the day of surgery is considered Day 1.

Predictors

The patient characteristics used as predictors were age (in years), body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score. The latter is an assessment of a patient's overall health expressed in five ordinal classes, ranging from 1 to 5. A higher score indicates that the patient is less fit [13]. Age, BMI, and ASA score were routinely assessed by an anesthesiologist during preoperative medical screening. The anesthesiologist was not affiliated with the research team and was unaware of the research intentions regarding the data.

Further included variables were: Identification of seniors at risk (ISAR); ISAR is a self‐report screening tool to identify increased risk of adverse health outcomes (it ranges from 1 to 6; scores > 2 points indicate frailty) [14]. The timed up and go test (TUG) measures the time required by a patient to get up out of a chair, comfortably walk 3 m, turn around, walk back again, and get seated in the chair again [15]. The DeMorton mobility index (DEMMI) is a 15‐item mobility instrument that measures static and dynamic balance, walking capacity, transfers, and functional muscle function. The 15 items are normalized to a 0–100 points scale, where a higher score represents better mobility [16]. The DEMMI has good validity with minimal detectable change and minimal clinically important difference of 9 and 10 points [16]. It has also been demonstrated to be an independent predictor of delayed IRoA [4]. During preoperative screening, a physical therapist guided and administered the ISAR screening tool and TUG‐ and DEMMI tests. The physical therapists were not affiliated with the research team and were unaware of research intentions regarding the data collection.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics and perioperative outcomes of individuals undergoing TKR between 2009 and 2020 (n = 1853) were summarized using descriptive statistics. Due to missing values, 230 patients (12%) were excluded from the analysis, leaving 1623 patients for the final study cohort. Missingness was assessed using Little's Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) test [17], which indicated that data were missing completely at random; therefore, no additional statistical corrections or imputations were applied.

Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Preoperative characteristics (age, ASA, BMI, ISAR) and functional mobility measures (TUG, DEMMI) were included as independent variables in the multinomial regression model, with IRoA as the dependent variable.

Patients who did not achieve predefined functional recovery criteria prior to hospital discharge and were transferred to inpatient rehabilitation facilities were classified as having delayed recovery.

A p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 [18].

Predictor Stability Across Care Pathways (RQ1)

To investigate RQ1 univariable multinomial regression was performed to determine the association between individual variables and IRoA. Multivariable multinomial regression analyses were performed to compare the effect sizes of different variables in each specific care pathway period. Minimum AIC was used to select the best‐performing prediction model per care pathway period for the three outcome levels [19]. Model coefficients and predictor inclusion were estimated separately for each care pathway period.

Temporal Transportability of Prediction Models (RQ2)

To evaluate temporal transportability (RQ2), models from each care pathway period were validated on data from subsequent periods. When performance deteriorated, models were re‐estimated using data from the next period. This process was repeated sequentially across all periods.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patient representatives actively advised on perioperative care transitions on a voluntary basis, starting from the Fast‐track period. Several group sessions were conducted with patients to explore their needs, preferences, and desired outcomes for future perioperative care, aiming to benefit the implementation. Our focus and continuous monitoring of functional recovery were influenced by these sessions with the patients. Perceived barriers and suggestions for improvement within perioperative care were discussed and documented in sessions with patients who experienced the full perioperative trajectory. Patients participating in the group sessions were compensated for their time (on average 2 h per patient) and effort with a gift voucher (€ 25).

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Commission of Nij Smellinghe Hospital. The Medical Ethics Commission determined that a full review was not required under the Dutch Law for Medical Research with Human Subjects (WMO) (reference ID: 16‐107/JS/AB, 3 December 2013 and BK/AB/ID 17568, 1 February 2021). Additionally, this study adheres to the Medical Treatment Agreement Act (WGBO) and the Data Protection Act (AVG, aligned with the EU General Data Protection Regulation effective May 28, 2018).

Temporal Transportability of Prediction Models (RQ2)

Models from each care pathway period showed reduced performance when applied to data from subsequent periods. The predicted correct proportion for the Joint‐Care population was 0.70 and for the next period (Function Tailored period) was 0.59. For the Function Tailored population, the predicted correct proportion of IRoA categories was 0.66 and that of the next period (Fast Track period) was 0.43. For the Fast Track population, the estimation procedure showed a predicted correct proportion of 0.51 versus 0.36 when tested on the dataset of the next period (Prehabilitation period). Finally, the predicted correct proportion of IRoA categories for the Prehabilitation population was 0.65.

These results indicate limited temporal transportability of prediction models across successive care pathway improvements.

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this longitudinal retrospective cohort study based on routinely collected perioperative data, we found that age remained a consistent predictor of IRoA across successive care pathway redesigns, while preoperative functional performance measures (DEMMI and, to a lesser extent, TUG) showed variable but clinically relevant predictive value. The relative contribution of these predictors changed over time as the perioperative care process evolved, indicating that predictor effects were not static (RQ1). In addition, prediction models based on earlier care pathway periods demonstrated worse performance when applied to subsequent periods, underscoring limited temporal transportability in the context of system‐level adaptations (RQ2). Together, these findings highlight the necessity of regular model evaluation and updating when prediction tools are used in evolving clinical pathways.

Interpretation in Light of System Change

The predictive importance of preoperative and functional variables should be interpreted in the context of evolving perioperative care pathways over the study period. Between 2009 and 2020, iterative optimization of the TKR care pathway was accompanied by changes not only in clinical protocols, but also in organizational mindset and expectations among patients and healthcare professionals. Since the introduction of the function‐tailored pathway in 2010, the formal discharge criteria—absence of medical reasons for prolonged hospital stay and achievement of IRoA remained unchanged. However, the degree of attention devoted to actively optimizing these criteria increased substantially. This shift was reflected in interventions spanning the full perioperative trajectory, including personalized preoperative training in the home setting of patients at risk for prolonged postoperative hospital stay [4, 9, 20, 21], optimization of medication and surgical techniques, and increased alignment of expectations and behaviors among clinical staff [22, 23]. As a result, the observed associations between functional measures and inpatient recovery do not only represent static patient characteristics, but rather the dynamic interaction between patient capacity and system‐level organization of care. The variation in predictive performance observed across successive care pathways underscores that recovery outcomes such as IRoA are inherently system‐dependent, reinforcing the importance of interpreting predictive models within the temporal and organizational context in which they are applied.

Implications for Learning Health Systems

From a learning health system perspective, this study demonstrates how routinely collected clinical data can be transformed into actionable knowledge to support shared decision making about the full perioperative process without the ambition to make “perfect” predictions. Using functional performance data and IRoA aligns the prediction with meaningful goals, focusing on recovery. Providing patients and clinicians with insight into likely recovery trajectories supports shared decision‐making across the perioperative process, focusing on recovery rather than administrative endpoints such as length of stay. Importantly, the modeling approach is designed to support iterative learning. As care pathways evolve, updated data can be used to recalibrate and refine prediction models, enabling continuous alignment between clinical practice and decision support. In this way, the model does not represent a static tool, but a low‐complex mechanism for embedding feedback loops between data, knowledge, and practice. Such an approach may support hospitals in adapting recovery‐oriented decision‐making during transitions in care delivery, while preserving local relevance and responsiveness to system changes.

Methodological Considerations and Limitations

This study presented has several strengths. First, the dataset of routinely collected care data spans from 2009 to 2020. Despite the midsized data per period, the dataset was sufficient for meaningful analyses, providing insights into variables predicting IRoA over four treatment policy periods. Second, using the minimum AIC criterion allowed us to select the best predictive model based on the data, thereby facilitating quick update [24]. Third, focusing on IRoA rather than length of stay—an outcome influenced by numerous clinical and non‐clinical factors [25]—allowed functional recovery to be evaluated independently of discharge logistics. As prolonged hospital stay is associated with adverse patient outcomes and substantial economic impact [20, 25, 26, 27, 28], monitoring IRoA provided a clearer signal of recovery while simultaneously highlighting non‐functional reasons for delayed discharge, supporting iterative reflection and pathway optimization. Fourth, the study's clinical relevance is notable. Hospitals and rehabilitation centers can draw inspiration by using this method to predict inpatient functional recovery adapted to their specific clinical context, especially during transitions towards new clinical care pathways [29, 30, 31]. The statistical method can also serve as a starting point for developing and validating prediction models on inpatient functional recovery with patients in elective medical trajectories recovering from other conditions [9, 32, 33].

There are also several limitations to be noted. First, throughout the study period, the same routinely collected care parameters were included in the modeling process. It is conceivable that incorporating additional variables, such as preoperative EuroQol‐5 Dimension scores, 10‐m walking test results, or Oxford Knee Scores, could have further improved predictive performance [9]. Second, this study was conducted using data from a single hospital, which may limit generalizability of the predictive models to other healthcare settings. The broadly comparable patient characteristics observed relative to larger European cohort studies [34, 35] suggest that similar functional variables and modeling strategies may be relevant in other settings, but local validation and adaptation remain essential before clinical implementation. Differences in local care pathways, discharge criteria, (p)rehabilitation availability, and perioperative protocols will influence both recovery trajectories and model performance when applied elsewhere. Rather than aiming for universal applicability, this study should therefore be interpreted as demonstrating a locally valid, data‐driven approach for predicting IRoA. Importantly, such local specificity can also be explained as a defining characteristic instead of a weakness. The modeling framework is intended to be retrained, recalibrated, and iteratively improved using data within each institution's own clinical context.

Third, the categorization of IRoA into fast, regular, and slow recovery was based on clinically meaningful thresholds at the time of data collection. However, as perioperative care pathways and discharge practices evolved over the study period, these categories may no longer fully reflect contemporary patterns of hospital stay or care delivery. The labels “fast,” “regular,” and “slow” thus reflect historical clinical intent rather than invariant recovery states. This misalignment does not only represent a methodological limitation but also illustrates a core challenge for learning health systems: even the dependent variable may require recalibration over time as clinical practice changes, rather than being treated as a fixed construct.

Future Recommendations

As perioperative pathways continue to evolve, future research should re‐evaluate recovery benchmarks and explore prediction models tailored to emerging care models, including selection for outpatient or day‐case surgery. Such models may benefit from incorporating additional variables, including sex, smoking status, and metabolic comorbidities, to support safe and effective patient stratification, as identified by Yeramosu et al. (2021) [36]. Future research should explore automated model updating using newly accumulated data, requiring systematic data collection and longitudinal monitoring. Advances in digitalization, data governance, and FAIR data principles [37] may facilitate the development of adaptive AI‐based prediction models that continuously recalibrate as care pathways evolve [38]. These approaches may further enhance predictive performance over time, while complementing transparent decision‐support tools used in shared decision‐making [39].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the predictive value of preoperative variables for IRoA after TRK varied across sequentially redesigned care pathways within a single hospital. Although age and preoperative functional performance (DEMMI and TUG) remained important predictors of IRoA, their retention and relative influence varied as perioperative care processes evolved. Prediction models based on the data of one care pathway period showed reduced performance when applied to subsequent periods, indicating limited temporal transportability in the context of system‐level adaptations. These findings underscore the importance of regular evaluation and local recalibration of prediction approaches when used in dynamic clinical environments. Treating prediction tools as adaptive rather than static supports their continued relevance in guiding recovery‐oriented decision‐making as care pathways evolve.
