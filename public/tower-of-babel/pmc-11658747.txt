Complications Following Surgical Treatment of Haglund's Syndrome With and Without Flexor Hallucis Longus Tendon Transfer
Authors: Albert T. Anastasio, Alexandra N. Krez, Katherine M. Kutzer, Kevin A. Wu, Kali J. Morrissette, Zoe W. Hinton, Andrew E. Hanselman, Karl M. Schweitzer, Samuel B. Adams, Mark E. Easley, James A. Nunley, Annunziato Amendola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:In the surgical treatment of Haglund syndrome, combining a flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon transfer with Achilles tendon may improve plantarflexion strength and reduce strain on the repaired Achilles tendon. However, there is limited research comparing complication rates between surgical approaches with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer.Methods:This was retrospective study of patients who underwent surgical management for Haglund syndrome between January 2015 and December 2023. Surgical management included open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer. Patient demographics, comorbidities, and postoperative complications were collected. Statistical analysis was conducted to compare outcomes between the two groups.Results:Among the 390 patients who underwent surgical intervention for Haglund syndrome, 52 individuals received FHL tendon transfer. The average follow-up duration was 10.1 ± 12.9 months. Overall, complications included persistent pain (10.3%), wound breakdown (8.2%), infection (1.8%), plantar flexion weakness (1.5%), and Achilles tendon rupture (0.3%). No notable difference was observed in complication rates between the two groups. However, wound breakdown was higher in the FHL tendon transfer group compared with the non-FHL group (P = 0.5). No patients who received a FHL tendon transfer experienced a postoperative Achilles tendon rupture.Conclusion:In the largest study of patients who underwent surgical treatment for Haglund syndrome, with or without FHL tendon transfer, the overall complication rate was comparable. However, the FHL tendon transfer group exhibited a higher tendency for wound breakdown.

Haglund syndrome encompasses the concomitant presence of a posterior superior calcaneal prominence, retrocalcaneal bursal irritation, and insertional Achilles tendinopathy.1-5 Conservative measures such as shoe wear modifications,6 rest, activity adjustment, gastrocnemius stretching,7 and orthotic use8 often prove successful in alleviating symptoms. However, in refractory situations, surgical treatment of Haglund syndrome can be considered.9,10

Although many surgical techniques have been described for the treatment of Haglund syndrome, three approaches are commonly employed: (1) endoscopic procedures through posteromedial and posterolateral portals adjacent to the Achilles tendon,11 (2) open central-splitting or Achilles-adjacent approaches to carry out bony prominence resection, Achilles débridement, and subsequent Achilles fixation,12-15 and (3) Zadek osteotomy, often through minimally invasive surgery techniques with the use of a burr.16-19 In Haglund syndrome accompanied by substantial insertional tendinopathic disease of the Achilles tendon, thorough débridement of mucoid degenerative tissue is often warranted.20 Thus, many authors have described the use of a concurrent flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon transfer in addition to Achilles tendon repair to act as a bolster to plantarflexion strength and to offload the Achilles tendon repair.21,22 Despite a relatively scant body of literature evaluating FHL tendon transfer in conjunction with Haglund prominence resection for insertional Achilles tendinopathy in Haglund syndrome, FHL tendon transfer has been shown to be a viable treatment of chronic Achilles tendon rupture,23-25 thus providing an evidentiary basis for the use of this procedure in Haglund resection cases involving substantial Achilles débridement.

Further evidence is needed to support the use of an FHL transfer in Haglund syndrome to evaluate differential complication rates and to assess whether incidence of debilitating plantarflexion weakness is reduced with the adjunctive application of FHL transfer. Thus, the purpose of this study was to compare patients undergoing open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer based on the rate of complications such as wound breakdown, Achilles tendon rupture, or plantarflexion weakness, and infection. These findings will provide valuable insights into the outcomes of repair with and without FHL tendon transfer, helping to further our understanding and manage patient expectations.

Methods

Study Cohort

This study was conducted retrospectively with approval from the Institutional Review Board at the Duke University. A cohort of 496 surgical interventions for Haglund syndrome consisting of open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer, were studied. The procedures were performed by one of six different surgeons at an academic hospital in North Carolina over the course of 8 years from January 2015 to December 2023. Inclusion criteria encompassed diagnoses of Haglund deformity, retrocalcaneal bursitis, or insertional Achilles tendinopathy, identified by corresponding current procedural terminology codes for Haglund resection. Specific surgical techniques and postoperative protocols and are detailed in Table 1. Achillies repair techniques included titanium knotted suture anchors, knotless suture anchors and SpeedBridge. Diagnoses were then manually verified through surgical note review. Patients were excluded if the surgery was a revision (51), the surgeon had performed less than 40 Haglund resections (35), the patient had a prior Achilles tendon rupture (12), a heel ulcer was present at the time of procedure (3), the surgery was initially an open reduction and internal fixationof a avulsion-type calcaneal fracture (2), the surgery was canceled (2), or the surgery was performed endoscopically (1). No constraints were found on the minimum follow-up period. The 52 patients with current procedural terminology code corresponding to specifically FHL transfer (27691) were highlighted for this study. With regard to FHL transfer technique, several surgical specifics were adhered to across the cohort. Namely, concomitant gastrocnemius recession was not employed, biotenodesis screw was used as the sole form of fixation, and no platelet-rich plasma was applied to the wound.

Data Acquisition

Patient data were sourced through a direct export from the electronic medical records system and corroborated through manual chart reviews conducted by two independent reviewers. All research activities complied with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations. Recorded patient demographic information, included age, sex, body mass index, smoking habits, and comorbidities, which encompassed the American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) classification and diabetic status.

Postsurgical Weight-bearing Protocol

Following surgery, patients were advised on weight-bearing limitations, varying from non–weight-bearing to partial or full weight-bearing as tolerated in a supportive boot (Table 1). These instructions were initially provided in the surgical notes and any subsequent modifications were recorded in follow-up documentation. The standard protocol entailed removal of sutures and progression to regular footwear by the second week postoperation, with the initiation of range of motion exercises upon satisfactory wound healing. A return to athletic activities was generally expected by the third month, progressing to a complete recovery by the 6th month postoperation.

Complication Documentation

The study investigated complications such as pain, weakness, superficial wound breakdown, rupture, and deep infection as noted in the postoperative medical records. Patient-reported outcome metrics were heterogeneously reported, and the decision was ultimately made to remove patient-reported outcome reporting from this article, preferring instead to emphasize complication rates in this large patient sample. Pain was categorized as ongoing discomfort persisting between 6 months to a year postsurgery, relative to the final follow-up. Weakness was defined as a lasting inability to perform plantarflexion movements against body weight beyond 6 months after the removal of the walking boot. Occurrences of wound breakdown, rupture, or infection were recorded because they were recorded in the postoperative notes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for each subgroup within the study. Patient demographics and postoperative results were comparatively analyzed between the subgroup of patients who had an FHL transfer and those who did not undergo FHL transfer in conjunction with Haglund débridement. The assessment of data normality was done visually using plots. The statistical evaluation was carried out using the R software, version 3.6.1 (R Foundation), with a predetermined significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Results

This retrospective cohort study included a total of 390 patients diagnosed with Haglund syndrome, of whom 52 patients underwent FHL transfer in addition to open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, and 338 patients received the abovementioned procedures without FHL transfer. The average age of patients with FHL transfer was higher compared with those without (57.73 ± 9.24 vs. 54.75 ± 11.95 years; P = 0.04). In the total cohort, most patients were female (n = 259; 66.4%) and had no smoking history (n = 270; 69.2%). Sixty-eight patients (17.4%) had a history of DM. The most common preoperative ASA score was III (n = 186; 47.7%) and II (n = 172; 44.1%). No notable difference was observed in sex, body mass index, ASA score, DM, or smoking status between patients with or without FHL transfer (Table 2).

Intraoperative and Postoperative Weight-bearing Characteristics

Surgeons demonstrated diverse preferences regarding both surgical techniques and the postoperative weight-bearing protocol, as indicated in Table 1. Speedbridge was the most common technique, accounting for 57.9% of cases. No notable difference was observed in the distribution repair type used between the two groups. However, a notable difference was observed in postoperative weight-bearing status between those who received FHL tendon transfer and those who did not (P = 0.014). Initial non–weight-bearing status was implemented in 66.9% of patients in the non-FHL group and 80.8% in the FHL group. Furthermore, initial weight-bearing as tolerated was observed in 15.7% of patients in the non-FHL group and 1.9% in the FHL group (Table 1).

The average follow-up duration was 10.1 ± 12.9 months. At least one complication was experienced in 21.2% patients who underwent FHL transfer and in 19.8% patient who did not. Complications included persistent pain (n = 40; 10.3%), superficial wound breakdown (n = 32; 8.2%), deep infection (n = 7; 1.8%), plantar flexion weakness (n = 6; 1.5%), and Achilles tendon rupture (n = 1; 0.3%). Superficial wound breakdown was higher in the FHL transfer group (11.5%) compared with the non-FHL group (7.7%), but this finding did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.5). No patients in the FHL transfer group experienced a postoperative Achilles tendon rupture. Seven patients (1.8%) in the total cohort underwent revision surgery surgery. Two patients in the FHL group underwent revision surgery for deep infection (n = 1) and superficial wound breakdown (n = 1). Of the five patients in the non-FHL group who underwent revision surgery, the most common indication was persistent pain (n = 2) and deep infection (n = 2), followed by rupture (n = 1). No notable differences were observed in complications or revision surgery rates between patients with or without FHL transfer (Table 3).

Discussion

In the largest single-institution sampling of patients undergoing open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer, several notable trends were identified. Although the overall complication rates between patients undergoing FHL transfer and those receiving surgical treatment of Haglund syndrome without FHL transfer treatment are comparable, there are differences in the types of complications experienced. Although none of these findings achieved statistical significance, a higher rate of wound breakdown in the FHL group was observed (11.5% vs. 7.7%). Furthermore, no ruptures were reported in the FHL transfer group, whereas the non-FHL transfer group experienced a postoperative Achilles tendon rupture rate of 0.30%. Although these data are not conclusive, the findings herein warrant further discussion.

High rates of wound-related complications plague Achilles tendon surgery given poor blood supply to the region.26-31 In this study, a trend toward a higher rate of wound breakdown in the FHL group was observed. However, this finding did not reach statistical significance. It is important to note that all incisions were closed in similar fashion with vertical sutures. We prefer avoiding the use of absorbable Vicryl for closing the subcutaneous layers, as we believe that it increases suture bulk and could potentially serve as a source for infection. Furthermore, a notable difference was observed in postoperative weight-bearing protocol. A higher distribution of patient in the FHL group were non–weight-bearing compared with that in the non-FHL group. Hunt et al32 published a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in 2015 comparing open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent double-row suture anchor Achilles tendon reattachment either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer. These authors reported 12 wound-related complications in 39 total cases, consisting of minor superficial wound dehiscence (6), skin blistering or cellulitis (2), delayed wound healing (2), and peri-incisional maceration (2). No surgical intervention was necessary in any of these cases, and all wounds eventually healed. When comparing the FHL transfer group with the nontransfer group, no notable was found difference in the rate of wound complications due to small sample size, despite a twofold higher incidence of wound complications in the FHL transfer group (8 of 12 in the FHL transfer group versus 4 of 12 in the non-FHL transfer group).

Although the nature of the study by Hunt et al as a RCT provides the highest-level evidence to date comparing the treatment of Haglund syndrome with or without FHL transfer, the study is limited by inclusion of only 18 patients in the control group without FHL transfer and 21 patients in the FHL transfer group. Thus, the ability to achieve statistical significance is limited. Moreover, an a priori power analysis was not done for the abovementioned RCT, indicating that the study is not equipped to definitively comment on wound-related complications when Haglund syndrome treatment is done either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer. However, given that twice as many of the wound-related complications occurred within the FHL transfer group, these data can be interpreted as strongly indicating that with a larger sample size; this finding may have reached statistical significance, and these authors would have been able to provide strong evidence that the addition of an FHL transfer may lead to higher rates of wound breakdown. This finding is in line with our results, where the FHL transfer group experienced a higher rate of wound complications, but the magnitude of difference did not reach statistical significance. Other authors have also demonstrated a high rate of wound-healing issues after Haglund débridement with the addition of FHL transfer, with 3 of 20 patients (15%) experiencing minor wound complications in a single-surgeon retrospective review by Singh et al.21

Taken together and in the absence of a large sample size RCT with an appropriate a priori power analysis designed to determine the effect of addition of FHL transfer to wound breakdown rates, surgeons should understand that the available evidence points toward a higher rate of wound breakdown when FHL transfer is done with open treatment of Haglund syndrome. This finding could be considered intuitive, given the increased surgical time and excessive retraction that may be required to effectively dissect out the posteromedial aspect of the hindfoot, harvest the FHL, and prepare the FHL tendon and the tendon insertion site. Of note, systematic review and meta-analysis of various forms of surgical treatment for Haglund syndrome concludes that endoscopic surgery results in fewer wound-related complications,2 but direct comparison of endoscopic and open techniques through RCT is limited in the existing literature.

With regard to postoperative rupture of the Achilles tendon after Haglund débridement surgery, Hunt et al. reported no ruptures in either the FHL transfer group or the non-FHL transfer group in the RCT discussed previously. Similarly, in Singh et al report, no ruptures were reported in their series of 20 patients undergoing Haglund débridement with FHL transfer. In our cohort of 390 patients undergoing either Haglund resection and repair alone or accompanied by FHL transfer, only one patient experienced a confirmed postoperative Achilles tendon rupture, and this patient was in the non-FHL transfer group. Moreover, incidence of debilitating plantarflexion weakness was rare in both cohorts. It may be the case that FHL transfer can bolster the Achilles tendon suture anchor repair after Achilles débridement and reduce the risk of postoperative rupture. However, future research would be needed to confirm this finding, given the low reported incidence of postoperative rupture and the resulting need for large sample sizes in each treatment arm to effectively differentiate the rate of rupture.

This study is not without several limitations. Given the retrospective design, this study has limitations with regard to selection bias for the use of concurrent FHL transfer. In addition, intraoperative assessment of the severity of tendinopathy and surgical time was not recorded. The decision to perform FHL tendon transfer in conjunction with Haglund resection and Achilles tendon repair was made based on the surgeon's discretion based on the criteria of when greater than 50% of the tendon width had been débrided given tendinopathic changes. In these cases, the FHL transfer was added to provide mechanical strength and restore functional load-bearing capacity in the setting of an aggressively débrided Achilles tendon. Thus, we were unable to control for disease-specific factors leading to the choice in whether to transfer the FHL. For example, in a patient with more extensive tendinopathic disease warranting the removal of a notable portion of the Achilles tendon, a surgeon may be more likely to add an FHL transfer to the Haglund débridement procedure. These patients with more extensive tendinopathic involvement may be more likely to also suffer from cooccurring conditions, which could lead to variable complication rates. Moreover, these patients may have been more likely to suffer from postoperative rupture had they not undergone FHL transfer during the index procedure. Attempts were made at controlling for the degree of tendinopathic disease, but only select patients had received a preoperative magnetic resonance imaging test, and so appropriate assessment of degenerative disease proved difficult. Furthermore, given the trend toward a higher rate of wound breakdown in the FHL group despite uniform closure and predominantly non–weight-bearing protocols, future studies should focus on identifying specific risk factors for wound breakdown in this patient population. Additional limitations include the retrospective design and lack of PROs. In our opinion, current PROs fail to capture the presence of clinically meaningful plantarflexion weakness and may confer minimal utility for the purposes of comparing Haglund débridement with FHL transfer to isolated Haglund débridement.

Conclusion

In a large sample of patients undergoing open central-splitting Achilles tendon débridement, Haglund prominence resection, and subsequent Achilles tendon reattachment, either with or without concurrent FHL tendon transfer, higher rates of wound breakdown were noted in the FHL transfer group, although this result did not reach statistical significance. No meaningful difference in debilitating plantarflexion weakness, postoperative rupture rate, or presence of persistent pain were appreciated between the groups.
