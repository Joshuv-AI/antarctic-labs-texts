Non-thermal plasma treatment as a sustainable approach for wastewater reuse in hydroponic cultivation of Lactuca Sativa
Authors: Ludmila Čechová, Plamena Marinova, Evgenia Benova, Yovana Todorova, Ivaylo Yotinov, Yana Topalova, Jakub Buday, Pavel Pořízka, Jozef Kaiser, Todor Bogdanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The challenge in developing modern agricultural practices is to reduce irrigation water use, identify alternative water sources, improve plant growth, increase crop yields, and, finally, reduce the use of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides. Plasma treatment and wastewater decontamination may enable the safe reuse of nutrient-rich water in agriculture. In this study, municipal drinking water and wastewater after treatment with plasma were used for the irrigation of Lactuca sativa plants grown in hydroponic systems. Variations in micro- and macro elements content in plants were determined. Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) was used to determine the elemental distribution in the plant’s parts. Plasma treatment was performed using a novel plasma system, the Beta device—a microwave Argon plasma torch operating at 2.45 GHz, developed by the Clean & Circle Centre of Competence at the University of Sofia. Plasma treatment achieved more than a tenfold reduction in the total bacterial count in wastewater. Wastewater application in hydroponic farming resulted in a statistically significant increase in fresh mass of L. sativa by 27%. Plasma-treated wastewater was found to enhance chlorophyll concentrations in plants by 25% compared to the hydroponic fertilizer. The application of wastewater and plasma-treated wastewater provided essential nutrients for plant development, including K, Mg, Ca, and Na.

Introduction

Water scarcity and climate change are among the most pressing environmental issues of our time. Due to climate change, an increase in municipal wastewater volumes, and a high demand for freshwater, there is a growing interest in recovering nutrients and energy from wastewater1,2. Studies estimate that 380 billion m³ of wastewater are produced annually, and the production of wastewater is predicted to increase by 51% by 20503. Therefore, recycling wastewater to recover nutrients is becoming increasingly important, especially for nutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). It is estimated that up to 16.6 million tons of nitrogen can be recovered from wastewater, followed by 3.0 million tons of phosphorus and 6.3 million tons of potassium3. The nutrients in wastewater can cover up to 13.4% of the global demand for these nutrients3. Wastewater may also be a source of rare earth elements (REE), and the potential of REEs recovery in the circular economy has been discussed4. Recycling wastewater offers a promising strategy for the circular economy and sustainable agriculture; however, it is not without its challenges.

Water contamination with heavy metals, microplastics, organic pollutants such as antibiotics, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), or microbiological and pathogen contamination may limit the reuse of wastewater5–7. These contaminants are a threat to human health and the environment8. Therefore, several treatment steps need to be applied to solve this problem and reuse wastewater for irrigation safely9. Excessive chlorine and sodium levels contribute to soil salinity. Moreover, high concentrations of chlorine in wastewater could be harmful to plants, causing chlorosis and leading to a decrease in crop yield10.

Several technologies have been developed for treating wastewater. These technologies can be divided into 3 categories: physical, chemical, and biological methods11–13. Physical methods, including sedimentation, filtration, or membrane separation, are intended for the removal of suspended solids14–16. Coagulation, flocculation, and advanced oxidation processes (AOP) are used to remove colloidal particles and degrade organic pollutants (such as antibiotics)17,18. To remove organic matter, nutrients, biological methods are used. The biological methods are based on the metabolic activities of microorganisms19,20.

Non-thermal plasma is an AOP that offers a promising approach for the degradation of organic compounds, such as textile dyes or pharmaceuticals21–23 by the simultaneous effect of reduction processes, UV, charged particles, reactive species and metastable particles. Several studies have been published on the plasma decontamination properties24–26. Studies also show that non-thermal plasma is promising in antibiotic degradation and even PFOA degradation27. Non-thermal plasma is also known to generate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species when it comes into contact with water28. This means additional nitrogen enrichment in wastewater.

Studies have shown positive results on the effects of Plasma Activated Water (PAW) on plant growth and vigour29,30. However, PAW in the studies is mostly prepared from distilled or tap water. While distilled water is a perfect medium for studying the effect of non-thermal plasma on water properties (change in pH, conductivity, RONS concentration, and stability), real-life applications require treating natural water used in agriculture, such as tap or wastewater. Recent studies have demonstrated the application of PAW in hydroponic and aeroponic systems. It was shown that PAW influences plant growth, as well as nitrate and nitrite accumulation31,32. While plasma may increase the concentration of nitrogen and oxygen species, other essential nutrient elements (P, K, Mg, Ca, etc.) are also important. Therefore, treating wastewater, which is already rich in nutrients, in order to increase nitrogen levels and to eliminate bacteria and organic pollutants, is very promising33,34.

To assess the impact of plasma treatment on nutrient availability and plant uptake, an elemental analysis is essential. Traditional techniques, such as inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), provide highly sensitive bulk measurements of elemental composition but require destructive sample preparation35. In contrast, laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) enables a rapid, spatially resolved, and direct analysis of elemental distribution in various biological matrices, including plant tissues36, teeth37, or cancerous tissue38. The LIBS is a spectroscopy technique, based on the interaction of a laser with matter. When the laser beam interacts with the sample, a microplasma is created, and the plasma radiation is collected by optical fibres. The LIBS offers fast and straightforward elemental analysis of samples in all 3 states of matter39,40. The most significant benefit of LIBS is its ability to perform large-scale imaging, which provides information on the spatial distribution of elements41. This information is crucial for the proper evaluation of element location and its potential toxic effects.

Familiar with the possibility of plasma technologies to support water purification, scientists are considering the possibility of implementing them at various stages of this process42,43. This work traces the effect of plasma treatment of wastewater after its purification in the wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs).

In this study, a novel discharge type was developed and applied to treat nutrient-rich wastewater for potential reuse in hydroponic farming. The treated wastewater was analysed for changes in physicochemical properties and nutrient composition. Its effects on plant growth and nutrient accumulation were then evaluated under hydroponic cultivation conditions using Lactuca sativa as a model species. The combination of LIBS and ICP-OES provides complementary insights into both nutrient accumulation and distribution in plant tissue, offering a comprehensive understanding of how plasma-treated wastewater affects plant nutrition. Lactuca sativa was selected as the model crop due to its high global consumption, fast growth rate, and well-established suitability for hydroponic cultivation. In addition, lettuce is highly sensitive to water quality and nutrient availability, making it an ideal model crop for evaluating the effects of plasma treated water and wastewater. These characteristics make it effective bioindicator for evaluating the safety of wastewater reuse, including potential accumulation of nutrients and contaminant in edible tissue44–46.

While plasma treatment has been widely used in agriculture for generation of plasma activated water (PAW), most studies use clean water such as distilled or tap water. In contrast, the reuse of plasma treated wastewater in hydroponic systems remains largely unexplored. The study addresses this knowledge gap and provides new insights into the potential of plasma technology as a sustainable and nutrient-preserving wastewater treatment process for agricultural applications.

Materials and methods

Wastewater analysis

Wastewater

The wastewater treatment plant of the city of Sofia, the capital of the Republic of Bulgaria, is located near the village of Kubratovo (Sofia Municipality) and serves the capital’s sewerage system (domestic, industrial, and stormwater). It represents one of the largest wastewater treatment facilities on the Balkan Peninsula. Some of the parameters of the treatment plant are: Design capacity: ~500,000 m³ of wastewater per day; European sources indicate treatment of approximately 484,000–500,000 m³/day; It serves approximately 1,628,399 PE (population equivalent) according to current modernization projects. The technological treatment scheme includes the removal of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus. The treatment process achieves the required parameters of the treated water (COD, BOD₅, suspended solids, total nitrogen, and phosphorus) which comply with EU requirements and Ordinance No. 6 of 9 November 2000 for discharge into the Iskar River.

Treatment at Kubratovo is designed so that biological treatment and sedimentation ensure a sufficient effluent quality for discharge into the Iskar River in accordance with regulatory requirements. Therefore, continuous UV or chlorine disinfection of the entire flow is generally not used as a standard tertiary treatment stage. The possibility of chlorination is provided (1) in case of epidemiological risk; (2) in case of deteriorated effluent indicators; (3) upon special orders from regulatory authorities. The water quality after secondary/advanced biological treatment usually meets discharge requirements without additional disinfection. Such water was also used in our experiments.

In our studies, water from the Kubratovo treatment plant was sampled at the outlet during periods without critical situations, when it was neither chlorinated nor UV-treated. Cold plasma treatment aims to reduce the number of bacteria in the treated wastewater—namely, the total microbial count and the number of bacteria from the family Enterobacteriaceae (coliforms). Microbiological analysis of wastewater

For the assessment of changes in the microbiological quality of wastewater after plasma treatment, both untreated and treated samples were analysed. Two indicator groups of microorganisms, the total microbial count (TMC) and the number of coliforms, were determined ат 37 °C using the standard count-plate technique on Nutrient agar and Lactose TTC Agar with Tergitol 7 (Merck Millipore), respectively. The TMC is an indicator for general microbial contamination; the number of coliforms is an indicator for fecal contamination and the potential presence of other high-risk pathogenic bacteria. The data from the analyses were presented as Colony Forming Units per 1 mL (CFU/mL).

In its routine monitoring, the treatment plant determines the microbiological parameters of the treated wastewater by enumeration of the total microbial count using the standard plate count method in accordance with ISO 6222:2002. The average values are 46,800 ± 25 991 CFU/mL at 22 °C and 38,400 ± 15 478 CFU/mL at 37 °C after appropriate incubation periods.

Physical-chemical properties of wastewater

The physical-chemical properties of plasma-treated wastewater were determined by the measurement of pH, conductivity, the concentration of RONS (hydrogen peroxide, nitrates, nitrites), and the presence of nutrients by ICP-OES (Plasma Quant 9100 Elite High-Resolution Array ICP-OES, Analytik Jena, Germany). The ICP-OES analysis provided total dissolved element concentration and does not differentiate between specific chemical species present in the solution.

Hydrogen peroxide concentration was determined spectrophotometrically using a colourimetric titanium reagent (titanium sulphate Ti(SO4)2 dissolved in 27–31% sulphuric acid, Sigma-Aldrich) and a UV-VIS spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Hydrogen peroxide reacts selectively with titanium sulphate to form the peroxytitanyl acid yellow complex, which can be determined spectrophotometrically at 407 nm. Concentrations of nitrates and nitrites were determined by selective spectrophotometric reagents (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) based on the Griess reaction. The coloured complexes were measured at the appropriate wavelength (540 and 380 nm for nitrites and nitrates, respectively) using a UV-VIS spectrometer.

Plasma treatment of wastewater

Beta device—is a novel plasma system (developed by the scientists from the Clean & Circle Centre of Competence, University of Sofia). Usually, the plasmas produced at atmospheric pressure (including microwave discharges) are with high gas temperature. To avoid the thermal damage of biological system treatment the afterglow is used (plasma jets). Microwave discharges sustained by travelling electromagnetic wave have been widely studied over the years and their advantages have been proven. In optimised operational conditions it is possible to produce cold plasma (with gas temperature bellow 45 °C) by a Surfatron type discharge47 with active plasma region (not afterglow) in the open space—cold plasma torch. Two main problems arise in using this plasma torch: (i) The plasma properties (including gas temperature) strongly depend on too many discharge parameters—not only the electromagnetic wave power and the type and flow rate of the working gas but also geometrical ones like dielectric tube inner and outer diameters and its permittivity, the length of the tube outside the Surfatron, ets. (ii) In treating the liquids, the plasma torch cannot penetrate inside the liquid but only can modify the liquid surface48. In order to solve these problems new microwave plasma source (called Beta device) was constructed. More details can be found in42. The device was designed for treating liquids in two modes: underwater mode (when plasma is inside the treated liquid) (Fig. 1a) and surface mode (when plasma is generated above the liquid surface and is in contact with the liquid) (Fig. 1b). The plasma system consists of a disposable 3D printed applicator, a GMS 200 W solid-state microwave generator (SAIREM, Décines-Charpieu, France) operating at a frequency of 2.45 GHz, a coaxial cable, and an Argon gas supply system with a VÖGTLIN (Unna, Germany) RED-Y compact® mass flow controller to control gas flow speed. The input microwave power is in the range of 10–30 W, which provides the low plasma gas temperature required by biological applications and also energy efficiency. The working gas was chosen to be Argon as Argon plasma is proven to simultaneously be cold and with a high concentration of reactive particles49. Plasma treatment was performed under atmospheric conditions, allowing interactions between excited argon species and ambient air molecules (O2, N2). This results in generation of RONS. The Beta device was specifically designed with a disposable applicator when used for wastewater treatment, as this helps to prevent contamination being carried between samples from the tip of the device when it is in contact with the liquid. A significant challenge in applying microwave discharges to treat biological systems is their typically high temperature. This problem is usually addressed by primarily using the afterglow of the discharge to treat objects. The Beta device allows the creation of plasma with a gas temperature close to room temperature and high electron energy, necessary for the processes in the plasma to occur. When considering conventional microwave sources, as MW plasma torch, in contact with liquids the challenge is the possibility of the active part of the plasma being in contact with the liquid. The innovative advantage of the Beta device is that it works stably not only in contact with liquids, but also when immersing the applicator below the surface of the liquid. For optimization of plasma treatment effects, the discharge conditions, such as the treatment time and the treatment mode, were varied. Treatment times of 5, 10, and 15 min were used in both surface mode (SM) and underwater mode (UW). The wave power and Argon flow were fixed at 20 W of power and 5 l/min Argon flow. For the hydroponic cultivation of the plants, 10 min of treatment time and UM of 200 ml batch sample were chosen.

Plant growth

Salad Lactuca sativa seedlings were pre-grown in seedling trays using commercially purchased seeds in a soil substrate until they reached the phase with two primary leaves. Selected plants of similar size were then cleaned and transferred for cultivation in hydroponic systems, where they were grown for 3 weeks. Experimental 3D-printed hydroponic systems with water circulation, a 1 l liquid volume, and artificial lighting were used to group the plants (Fig. 2). The plants were divided into five groups in different hydroponic systems with separate water circulation, based on the growth media: conventional hydroponic fertiliser (F) as control, tap water (TW), plasma-treated tap water (TTW), wastewater (WW), and plasma-treated wastewater (TWW). A secondary control group was kept growing in soil (S) with tap water irrigation. A water-soluble 20-20-20 fertiliser, containing 20% nitrogen, 20% phosphorus, and 20% potassium, was used as the growing solution at a concentration of 1 g/L in tap water. The group grown in the fertiliser solution, together with the one grown in soil, was considered as a control variant. After 3 weeks, plants were collected for analysis.

Chlorophyll content

The main plant photosynthetic pigments were determined. Chlorophyll a, and Chlorophyll b, as well as the Carotenoids, were measured by a destructive method using organic solvent 80% Acetone. After the extractions, the pigment concentrations were calculated by Vernon’s formulas by the absorption at 665 nm, 649 nm, and 440 nm50.

Coliform bacteria

Microbiological analysis of the grown plants was also carried out for all growing variants. The Lactose TTC Agar with Tergitol 7 was used as a differential medium for detecting coliforms. The samples from lettuce plants were preliminarily processed before microbiological analysis; 1 g of fresh plant material was weighed, and 9 mL of saline was added. It was mechanically crushed and sonicated (3 times, 15 s each). From the resulting suspension, serial dilutions were prepared, and the work was carried out according to standard cultivation methodology. The data from the analyses were presented as Colony Forming Units per 1 g (CFU/g) fresh weight (for lettuce plants).

Elemental analysis

Elemental analysis was focused on the determination of nutrient concentration in plant bulk material by ICP-OES, and elemental distribution in salad leaves was performed by LIBS.

ICP-OES bulk analysis

The plants grown in hydroponic systems from all variants were analysed using ICP-OES (Plasma Quant 9100 Elite High-Resolution Array ICP-OES, Analytik Jena, Germany) to determine nutrient concentrations. Elemental analysis was performed on composite samples from 8 plants, and only a single concentration value per treatment was obtained.

LIBS bioimaging

For the LIBS analysis, the leaves of the salad were dried and molded to ensure the samples were as flat as possible for bioimaging. The dried flat leaves were then fixed to a microscopy slide with epoxy glue and photographed using an optical microscope before measurement. At least 3 samples from each group were selected for LIBS measurements. The 2D elemental maps were recorded using the LIBS FireFly system (Lightigo, Czech Republic) with the diode-pumped solid-state laser (1064 nm, 10 ns, 50 Hz, 5 mJ). Characteristic radiation was collected by a wide-angle (37.5 mm focal length) and optical fibre bundle (400 μm core diameter), where each fibre collected plasma emission from slightly different parts of the plasma plume. The plasma emission was transferred to a series of four Czerny-Turner spectrometers covering a spectral range from 180 to 690 nm (0.035 nm resolution). Samples were placed on a motorised stage, where each laser pulse was delivered to a fresh spot (100 μm spot size) with a step size of 100 μm. Spatially resolved LIBS bioimaging was carried out by raster scanning the sample surface and reconstructing two-dimensional elemental maps from the emission intensity of selected lines, as described by Kopřivová et al. in a recent study on melanoma tissues51.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student´s t-test to evaluate differences between experimental groups. The levels of significance were accepted at *p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001. Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 2604, Microsoft Corporation, USA).

Results and discussion

Water treatment and analysis

The physical and chemical properties of plasma-treated tap water and wastewater were determined immediately after plasma treatment. The two operational modes of the Beta device were used: surface mode (SM) and underwater mode (UM). Both modes were used to treat the water for 5, 10, and 15 min. The discharge parameters used were an input power of 20 W and an argon flow of 5 l/min.

Fig. 3H2O2, NO2− and NO3− concentrations in Tap water and Wastewater treated by the Beta device for 5, 10, and 15 min in Surface mode (SM) and Underwater mode (UM).

H2O2, NO2− and NO3− concentrations in Tap water and Wastewater treated by the Beta device for 5, 10, and 15 min in Surface mode (SM) and Underwater mode (UM).

The generation of selected RONS (NO3−, NO2−, and H2O2) in water by the Beta device is time-dependent (Fig. 3). In both SM and UM, the generation of RONS increases over time in both treated tap water and wastewater. However, the UM is more efficient at generating RONS, especially in wastewater. This trend is most noticeable in the H2O2 concentration. The concentration of H2O2 increases significantly, especially with the 10 min UM treatment. The concentration is stagnant after 10 min of SM treatment of wastewater. Because the SM operates above the water surface and does not cause mixing of the water, it can be inferred that the water surface is saturated with H2O2 after 10 min of treatment and remains saturated after 15 min. It can also be hypothesized that the H2O2 reacts with NO2− to form NO3−, and therefore, the concentration of H2O2 decreases during the treatment52. However, this effect is not applicable to tap water. The H2O2 plays a vital role in plasma decontamination properties. Previous research comparing plasma-liquid systems has already shown that the properties of plasma-treated water depend on both the treatment time and the type of discharge53. Trebulová et al. (2025) report that the decontamination effect of plasma-treated liquids (PTL) increases when the underwater plasma treatment is introduced. Moreover, they report that the decontamination effect of PTLs is a synergistic effect of H2O2, NO3−, and lowered pH. PTLs with a high concentration of NO3− showed the best decontamination efficacy54.

Microbiological analyses of the water

The positive effect of non-thermal plasma treatment on the inhibition of bacterial and yeast growth has been reported in numerous studies25,26,46. Treatment with the Beta device also resulted in a significant reduction in the total number of microorganisms in wastewater, as shown in Fig. 4.

In the present study, the untreated wastewater exhibited a relatively high microbial load, expressed as a total microbial count of 62,000 ± 11,000 CFU/mL. Comparison with the average value of total microbial count measured at the treatment plant (38400 CFU/mL) indicates that microbial abundance increased during storage of the treated wastewater. This increase explains the nearly threefold decrease in nitrate concentration and the approximately fourfold decrease in nitrite concentration observed after storage prior to plasma treatment (Fig. 3). Treated wastewater represents a living and dynamic system containing microorganisms and enzymes. Despite refrigerated storage, denitrification-related transformation processes continue, resulting in increased bacterial abundance and decreased nitrate and nitrite concentrations.

In plasma-treated wastewater variants, more than a tenfold reduction in the total bacterial count was observed. The reduction was time-dependent, with a more effective decontamination achieved under the surface treatment mode. This microbial decontamination also reduced the rate of microbiological transformation processes. Nevertheless, nitrate and nitrite concentrations increased due to reactions occurring during plasma treatment, and this post-treatment nitrate enrichment exerted a beneficial stimulatory effect on plant growth.

The reduction in total microbial count following plasma treatment also involved an almost complete elimination of coliform bacteria. Coliforms were detected only in plasma-untreated wastewater samples at low levels (20 ± 2 CFU/mL), whereas treatment with the Beta device eliminated coliform bacteria from the water used for experimental plant irrigation (Fig. 4).

Plant biomass

The effects of plasma-treated water and wastewater on the growth of L. sativa were evaluated by measuring fresh and dry weight. The plants were grown in the soil-grown plants were considered the control group in the statistical analysis. For the hydroponic cultivation of the plants, 10 min of treatment time in the underwater mode were chosen. This was based on the results of the water analysis, especially the concentrations of H2O2 and NO3− in wastewater.

The results of fresh weight measurements show that, when using tap water (TW) and plasma-treated tap water (TTW) in the hydroponic system, the fresh weight was significantly lower than that of the soil-grown group at the p < 0.001 level of significance (marked as *** in Fig. 5a). Plants grown in wastewater (WW) had a higher fresh weight at the p < 0.05 (marked as *) level of significance compared to the soil-grown samples. In plasma-treated wastewater (TWW), the difference from the control group was not statistically significant. The highest difference in plant fresh weight was in the fertiliser (F) group, with the weight significantly higher than the control soil samples at the p < 0.01 (marked as **) level of significance. This increase in fresh weight could be due to the increased water content (Fig. 5b). The highest water content was found in the fertiliser group, followed by the WW and TWW groups. The increase in water content might be attributed to the hydroponic growth of most groups. Plants grown with fertiliser have the highest water content. A similar trend has been reported by Li et al. (2025), focusing on the application of domestic wastewater for irrigation and its effects on the growth and yield of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and green beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) under varying treatments in a hydroponic system. The results also show that tomato yield increased significantly with untreated wastewater, whereas yield decreased with increasing levels of wastewater treatment55. Treated wastewater was also reported to increase the fresh weight of lettuce when partially mixed with conventional fertilizer56. Previous research also showed that plasma-activated water (PAW) can enhance plant growth and development. The PAW, especially when with high concentrations of nitrates, enhanced the growth of salad (Lactuca sativa)57,58, radish29, and enhanced germination and plant growth of pea59.

Chlorophyll content

Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b concentrations are among the most important plant parameters indicative of plant health and vitality. For statistical analysis, soil-grown plants were again considered the control group. Growth in tap water (TW) and fertiliser (F) decreased chlorophyll content in L. sativa plants (Fig. 5c). When grown in treated tap water and wastewater, chlorophyll concentration was comparable to that in the control group. Significantly higher chlorophyll concentration was observed not observed in any of the variants. However, the data show increased concentration of Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b in the plasma-treated wastewater (TWW) group, which was approximately 25% higher compared to the hydroponic solution (F) group. These results indicate that not only is plasma-treated wastewater suitable for reuse in agriculture, but it can also increase chlorophyll content and has no negative effects on plant health and vitality. Positive effects of PAW on chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were previously reported57,58.

Microbiological analyses of the plants

The results presented in Fig. 5d show the number of coliforms detected per gram of fresh weight of lettuce grown under different experimental conditions. The highest number of coliforms was registered for plants grown on soil (control variant) − 2150 ± 350 CFU/g. This result is in accordance with the reported data in the literature, as the summary of Greve et al.60. shows that the most lettuce samples (96.4%) have coliform counts of < 101 to 103 CFU/g60. The plants grown in hydroponic systems with fertilizer and untreated wastewater have similar reported coliform counts—450 ± 350 and 500 ± 100 CFU/g, respectively. Plasma treatment of wastewater results in a 50% reduction. The variants with tap water (untreated and plasma-treated) have the fewest coliforms. Vergine et al. (2017) report that the use of treated agro-industrial wastewater did not result in any bacterial contamination of selected crops61. Treated wastewater irrigation elicited a positive effect on the bacterial diversity in soil; however, further studies on the impacts of wastewater on soil microbes need to be performed62.

LIBS bioimaging

The spectra recorded by the LIBS FireFly (Fig. 6) show differences in the nutrient intensity, especially in the potassium line K I 766. 49 nm. The LIBS bioimaging results in Fig. 7, 6 show distribution maps of potassium K, magnesium Mg, calcium Ca, and sodium Na. Bioimaging reveals that plants grown in fertilizer (F) exhibit a high accumulation of potassium in their leaves, accompanied by low accumulation of other elements. Plants grown in wastewater (WW) and plasma-treated wastewater (TWW) show a medium to high accumulation of potassium, while the other groups show a low accumulation of the same element. While potassium is an essential macronutrient responsible for proper plant growth and development, an excessive accumulation of potassium can cause significant problems with plant growth and the proper functioning of photosynthesis. The excess potassium in samples cultivated in hydroponic solution is evident in LIBS bioimaging of the leaves and in ICP-OS results. As a possible result of excess potassium, this variant shows a lower concentration of photosynthesizing pigments63, as shown in Fig. 5 , 6. All the groups show comparable accumulation of calcium and magnesium. There is a high accumulation of sodium in plants grown in wastewater and plasma-treated wastewater. This can cause salt stress, leading to low yields and stunted growth. The application of LIBS for the analysis of nutrient elements in plant samples was previously reported64,65, as well as the application of LIBS for the determination of total soil carbon66. Moreover, the effect of plasma activated water (PAW) on the uptake of heavy metals (Cd, Pb) as well as nutrient elements was previously studied30,53. This research shows that LIBS has the potential to be used in agricultural practices to control nutrient uptake and optimize fertilization strategies. The application of LIBS for controlling environmental contamination is also a promising strategy (Fig. 7).

Although LIBS results provide the spatial distribution of elements, it is necessary to quantify these results and compare them with those obtained using a quantitative method. The ICP-OES is the gold standard for elemental analysis and quantification. The results from the ICP-OES method are presented in Fig. 8. Elemental analysis was performed on composite samples, therefore statistical analysis could not be provided. There is a much higher accumulation of potassium from the hydroponic solution (F), compared to other growth mediums, including wastewater and treated wastewater. This overaccumulation of potassium can have implications for other findings, such as low concentrations of chlorophyll in plants or elongated leaves (Figs. 5c and 7)67. Other media supplied the amount of potassium compared to the soil control group. On the other hand, plants grown in wastewater and plasma-treated wastewater show a high accumulation of magnesium, calcium, and even sodium. Magnesium and calcium are essential nutrients required for proper plant development, photosynthesis, and the uptake of other nutrients and minerals68–71. Higher calcium in the growth medium may improve plant growth under low potassium or high sodium conditions72. Sodium is not particularly important for this process; however, lower supplies of sodium can be essential for certain plant species73. On the other hand, high concentrations of sodium can cause salt stress to plants, resulting in stunted growth74. In plants grown in wastewater and treated wastewater, no effects of salt stress or stunted growth were observed. Potassium is the third most important microelement, responsible for the regulation of photosynthesis, osmoregulation, and even resistance to abiotic stress75. Based on the previous studies, the presence of potassium in wastewater can mitigate the negative effects of sodium in plants76. Differences in elemental accumulation in plant tissues among the tested variants indicate that at least part of the dissolved nutrients in the water variants was available for plant uptake. However, it is essential to note that in this pilot study, the plants were grown in wastewater for only 3 weeks, and a longer exposure could have had negative effects on plant growth and development.

Conclusion

This work demonstrated the effect of plasma treatment of urban wastewater for its subsequent reuse in plant cultivation. A novel type of discharge was developed and applied to treat a nutrient-rich wastewater. Its low cost opens the possibility of larger-scale treatment. With an appropriate monitoring of composition and after an additional decontamination, treated wastewater was shown to be suitable for hydroponic farming. Plasma treatment introduced RONS in the water, affecting plant growth. Wastewater application in hydroponic farming resulted in a statistically significant (p < 0.05) increase in fresh mass of L. sativa by 27%. In addition, plasma-treated wastewater increased the concentrations of photosynthetic pigments—chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, by 25% when compared to conventional hydroponic fertilizer.

Plasma treatment proved to be an effective and reliable method for decontaminating wastewater and recovering nutrients. Plasma treatment of wastewater resulted in a more than 10-fold reduction in bacterial contamination. Alongside microbial safety, careful control of nutrient and sodium content remains essential. Elemental analysis by ICP-OES and LIBS shows that macronutrient uptake is directly affected by the growth medium and its treatment. Although a higher sodium accumulation was observed in wastewater-treated plants, no negative effects on plant growth were detected under the experimental conditions.

Future studies should focus on long-term cultivation of plants using plasma treated wastewater, and more detailed characterization of nutrient bioavailability in treated water. Assessment of possible contaminant accumulation should be studied as well. In addition, scalability of the plasma discharges and their economic feasibility and cost-effectiveness will be crucial for future large-scale agricultural applications.

Overall, the results indicate that properly treated wastewater can be safely and effectively reused in agriculture, providing an additional water and nutrient source. This approach can help reduce the fertilizer use in agriculture and lead to a cleaner environment.
