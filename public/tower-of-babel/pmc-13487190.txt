Knowledge, attitude, practice and training of inhalation therapy among respiratory nurses: a cross-sectional study on the current status and influencing factors
Authors: Xia Yang, Huan Liang, Zhen Huang, Jian Sun, Bo Wang, Diandian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective inhalation therapy is critical for managing chronic airway diseases, yet improper technique remains prevalent and contributes to poor clinical outcomes. In China, nurses play a central role in patient education. However, the relationship between nurses’ own training and their competency in delivering inhalation therapy education remains underexplored. A cross-sectional online survey was conducted from September to October 2025 among 387 respiratory nurses from 32 hospitals across 25 provinces. A validated 61-item questionnaire assessed their knowledge, attitudes, practices (KAP), and training status regarding inhalation therapy. Mean scores were 33.78 ± 2.76 for knowledge, 35.01 ± 7.98 for attitude, and 61.37 ± 10.2 for practice. Only 50.13% achieved a “good” level of theoretical knowledge. Practice correlated more with attitudes (Rho = 0.57, P < 0.001) than knowledge. Years of experience in specialty nursing, practical workshops and specialist nurse training were markedly associated with the knowledge and practice of inhalation therapy, respectively (P < 0.05), while educational video utilization was significantly correlated with better performance in both areas (all P < 0.05). In summary, respiratory nurses demonstrate positive attitudes and practices but have insufficient theoretical knowledge towards inhalation therapy. Introduction

Chronic airway diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma represent a major and growing global health challenge, significantly contributing to both morbidity and mortality worldwide1. In China, national cross-sectional studies have highlighted the scale of this burden: asthma affects 4.2% of adults, which corresponds to approximately 45.7 million individuals2. The prevalence of spirometry-defined COPD is even higher, at 8.6% nationally, equating to nearly 99.9 million people3. Notably, among those aged 40 and above, the COPD prevalence has risen sharply from 8.2% in 2007 to 13.7%4. However, only 5.6% asthma patients had been treated with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)2, and the utilization of peak flow meters showed no significant improvement5. Furthermore, management of COPD remains suboptimal, as evidenced by a multi-province survey which found that only 7.9% of patients received regular medical treatment6. Therefore, enhancing clinical awareness of chronic airway diseases and implementing standardized treatment protocols are essential to reduce the disease burden.

While inhaled drugs remain the cornerstone of managing obstructive lung diseases, inadequate inhalation knowledge and technique persist as a major challenge. This problem undermines clinical efficacy by preventing the effective delivery of medication to the airways7,8. Vanoverschelde et al. noted that half of the patients with obstructive lung diseases in their study made critical errors in using at least one of their inhalers, which contributed to poor disease control9. A previous study showed that non-adherence to treatment was associated with higher mortality among moderate-to-severe COPD patients treated in the Brazilian public health system10. Given this serious consequence, the role of healthcare professionals becomes essential for instructing patients in proper inhaler use and promoting treatment adherence. Particularly in China healthcare system, this educational responsibility falls predominantly on nurses, who serve both out-patient and hospitalized patients. Consequently, nurses’ proficiency in providing effective inhalation therapy education may be a pivotal, yet often under-evaluated, determinant of patient outcomes.

The knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) theory is widely accepted for the investigation of health-related behaviors and health-seeking practices11. Based on the classic KAP sequence where knowledge shapes attitudes and attitudes subsequently drive behavior12, this framework provides a conceptual foundation for nursing researchers to identify areas for development. The resulting insights can also be applied to nursing practice, future research, and evidence-based policy-making. To the best of our knowledge, existing literature often focus on patients’ errors or simply examining nurses’ knowledge of inhaler technique, without specifically investigating the linkages between nurse training characteristics, knowledge acquisition, attitude formation, and practical performance in real‑world clinical settings13–15. In this research, we aimed to evaluate the current knowledge, attitudes, practices, and training patterns of respiratory nurses concerning inhalation therapy, and to determine the factors influencing these dimensions. The findings are intended to provide strategies for governments and medical institutions to further improve the quality of and adherence to inhalation therapy among patients with chronic airway diseases in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a cross-sectional observational study conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE (strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology) statement. The target population was registered nurses employed in the department of respiratory and critical care medicine. A convenience sampling strategy was employed. From September to October 2025, electronic questionnaires were distributed via the Questionnaire Star platform16 to 32 comprehensive hospitals across 25 provinces in China. To diversify recruitment sources, we invited nurses from tertiary, secondary, and primary hospitals, representing geographic and institutional diversity. The survey link was disseminated by the respiratory department coordinators of each participating hospital via WeChat groups. To reduce non-response bias, all items were set as “mandatory” to guarantee response completeness and validity. Furthermore, each IP address was restricted to a single submission. Two researchers (X.Y. and H.L.) independently reviewed all completed questionnaires. Questionnaires with response time less than 120 s or logically inconsistent answers were deemed invalid and excluded. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher (B.W.). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approved No.: 2024-165). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants, who read the consent information and clicked an “I agree” button before accessing the questionnaire. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged over 18 years; (2) holding a valid nursing license issued by the Chinese Ministry of Health and currently employed in the department of respiratory and critical care medicine. Nurses who were on external training programs, retired, or undertaking internships were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies17, with the following parameters: α = 0.05, Z(1−α/2) = 1.96, p = 0.5 (the assumed degree of variability, which maximizes the sample size), and δ = 5% (the allowable margin of error). The calculation yielded a minimum theoretical sample size of 385 participants. To account for potential invalid responses, we aimed to recruit at least 400 participants.

Questionnaire development and validation

A Chinese version questionnaire on nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practice of inhalation therapy was developed according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2025 Report18, Chinese expert consensus on standardized inhaler device application in stable chronic airway diseases (2023)19, and The 2025 Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) strategy20. The initial questionnaire was reviewed by an expert panel consisting of five respiratory physicians and five nursing educators. A pilot study was conducted with 30 respiratory nurses from two non-participating hospitals in Sichuan province. Based on the pilot results, ambiguous items were reworded for clarity. The pilot data were not included in the final analysis. The final questionnaire comprised the sociodemographic information (age, gender, highest education, hospital classification, department, professional title, specialist nurse training status, and years of specialty nursing) and three KAP dimensions with a total of 61 items. The knowledge section consisted of three sub-domains: theoretical knowledge (14 items), device-related (15 items) and skill-related knowledge (10 items), and was assessed using 39 objective questions (“Right” or “Wrong”), with participants awarded one point for each correct answer and zero point for the incorrect. Attitudes were measured using 8 items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree,” with higher scores reflecting more positive attitudes. Practices were assessed through 14 items on a 5-point Likert scale from “Never” to "Always," where increased scores indicated more proactive practice. The total score of the questionnaire ranged from 22 to 149. For each dimension, the average score rate was calculated as the average score/theoretical maximum × 100%, with percentages ≤ 60%, 61–80% and > 80% considered poor, moderate and good, respectively. The internal consistency of the final questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and sampling adequacy for dimensional analysis was examined using the Kaiser‑Meyer‑Olkin (KMO) test.

The training information of inhalation therapy questionnaire was designed based on a review of the literature. The questionnaire comprised two sections: one assessing training frequency (with four options: “Once per year”, “Twice per year”, “Once every three months”, and “Once per month”) and the other evaluating learning sources (“Textbook”, “Prescribing information”, “Educational videos”, “Online training”, and “Practical workshops”), with “Yes” or “No” responses for each source.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by SPSS 25.0 for Windows (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. To compare the differences in scores related to knowledge, attitudes and practices independent t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed. The relationships among knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were analyzed using Spearman correlation. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyses were subsequently entered into multivariate linear regression models to identify influencing factors of KAP. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and KAP scores

Anonymous questionnaires were sent to 400 nurses, and 387 valid questionnaires were collected with an effective return rate of 96.75%. As shown in Table 1, the study participants were predominantly female (88.1%), with a mean age of 33.4 ± 6.8 years. Most held a bachelor’s degree or higher (76.2%) and were employed in public tertiary hospitals (81.7%). Among them, 222 nurses (57.4%) had more than five years of specialty nursing experience. A majority of the clinical nurses (52.5%) held junior professional titles and had not undergone specialist nurse training (68.2%).

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total scale was 0.921, indicating a good internal consistency. The KMO test verified sufficient sampling adequacy for dimensional analysis (all KMO values ≥ 0.65 and P < 0.001). The mean total KAP score of inhalation therapy was 130.30 ± 15.57. Participants demonstrated mean scores of 33.78 ± 2.76 for knowledge, 35.01 ± 7.98 for attitude, and 61.37 ± 10.2 for practice. Within the knowledge domain, the average scores were 10.89 ± 1.92 for the theoretical dimension (knowledge (T)), 13.29 ± 1.55 for the inhalation device-related dimension (knowledge (D)), and 9.60 ± 0.90 for the inhalation skill-related dimension (knowledge (S)). Participants who had longer working experience of specialty nursing and received specialist nurse training showed higher knowledge and practice scores, respectively (all P < 0.05). Notably, higher education level and professional title only were associated with higher theoretical knowledge scores, while hospital classification and specialist nurse training could be associated with device-related knowledge scores (all P < 0.05).

Response to KAP dimensions

As presented in Fig. 1, the proportions of respiratory nurses who were rated “good” were much lower for knowledge (T) (50.13%) and practice (65.37%) than attitudes (75.3%). The theoretical knowledge assessment revealed significant disparities, with 4 poor-scoring and 8 high-scoring items. The lowest-scoring items were “Inhaled corticosteroids are the preferred initial treatment for sudden asthma attacks.” (K4, 0.18 ± 0.39) and “Compared with the combination of different bronchodilators, an increased dosage of a single bronchodilator yields enhanced therapeutic outcomes with a reduced incidence of side effects.” (K6, 0.46 ± 0.50). Regarding the inhalation device- and skill-related knowledge dimensions, consistent accuracy was observed across items, with 24 items achieving high scores (range: 0.80 ± 0.40 to 1.00 ± 0.051) (Table S1). In the attitude dimension, over 90% of participating nurses expressed positive attitudes toward nearly all 8 items (Table S2). All 14 items in the practice dimension also received uniformly positive responses, with mean scores ranging from 4.28 ± 0.886 (P12) to 4.51 ± 0.763 (P4 and P5) (Table S3).

Impact of inhalation therapy training on KAP scores

As presented in Table 3, all hospitals where the participants worked provided training on inhalation therapy, but the frequencies were different. 28.9%, 26.9% and 28.2% respiratory nurses received annual training, bi-annual training and quarterly training, respectively, while monthly training was notably less common, with only 16% participants reporting this frequency. The data also revealed that multiple learning sources are utilized for inhalation therapy training, with varying degrees of adoption. Prescribing information (87.3%), educational videos (79.6%) and practical workshop (78.3%) were the most widely used resources, while textbooks (65.1%) and online training (62.5%), though common, were less employed.

The analysis of training patterns revealed different associations with nurses’ KAP performance. Specifically, training frequency was significantly correlated with both theoretical knowledge and practice scores: bi-annual training was associated with the highest knowledge (T) scores (11.26 ± 2.08), whereas monthly training correlated with the lowest (10.40 ± 1.93) knowledge (T) scores and highest practice scores (62.58 ± 14.26). Regarding the learning source, textbook use was associated with significantly lower theoretical but higher skill-related knowledge scores (10.66 ± 1.90 and 9.69 ± 0.67, respectively). Learning prescribing information was negatively linked to attitude but positively linked to practice scores. Video utilization was markedly associated with higher total scores in knowledge (33.99 ± 2.25) and practice (62.47 ± 9.42), while online training only showed a significant association with higher practice scores (62.30 ± 9.85). Workshops were associated with significantly higher scores in knowledge (34.01 ± 2.21), including device- and skill-related scores, and practice scores (61.95 ± 10.03).

Multivariate linear regression analysis of the influencing factors on nurses’ KAP

In this study, variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate analyses were selected as independent variables in the multivariate linear regression model. The findings revealed that years of experience in specialty nursing, educational video utilization and practical workshops were markedly associated with the knowledge of inhalation therapy (P < 0.05). Whether nurses have engaged in specialist nurse training and educational video utilization were significantly associated with nurses’ practice towards inhalation therapy (P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

The results of this study revealed that despite exhibiting positive attitudes and proactive practices, respiratory nurses had moderate levels of knowledge with regard to inhalation therapy (only 50.13% achieved > 80% correct), particularly in the theoretical knowledge section. Consistently, a Chinese survey showed that only 20.10%, 8.20%, and 13.10% of nurses working in community, secondary, and tertiary hospitals, respectively, had fully mastered the operational key points of inhaler devices15. Even among respiratory nurses, knowledge levels regarding inhaled steroids in COPD remains low, as reflected by the Bristol COPD Knowledge Questionnaire (BCKQ)21. However, inhaler use errors were common issues among COPD patients mainly due to the lack of adequate basic knowledge13. Therefore, a solid theoretical foundation is critical for boosting nurses’ competence and confidence in educating patients about inhaler use. Notably, while nurses with higher education and professional titles only scored better on theoretical knowledge, this advantage did not extend to their overall knowledge scores, indicating that advanced education and titles enhance core theoretical understanding, but do not necessarily translate into comprehensive, practice-integrated knowledge required for effective patient education. As expected, longer specialty nursing experience was positively associated with overall knowledge scores, largely through its effect on theoretical understanding. Also, device-specific knowledge mastery was more strongly linked to having received formal specialist nurse training. The findings collectively revealed that clinical experience along with specialized training might be the critical determinant for improving overall knowledge mastery.

Moreover, the observed knowledge-attitude-practice gap indicated that favorable attitudes and self-reported behaviors might not necessarily guarantee an accurate or sufficient mastery of inhalation therapy. This finding aligns with reports from other clinical areas, where nurses have demonstrated favorable attitudes towards conditions like sarcopenia or frailty even in the absence of substantive knowledge22,23. In contrast, nursing practices correlated more strongly with professional attitudes than with knowledge levels, implying that positive professional attitudes may serve as the primary mediator in overcoming the “know-do gap” rather than knowledge acquisition alone. Therefore, effective interventions integrating cognitive training with attitudinal development, including recognition of clinical significance and potential risks of inhalation therapy may be essential17.

In our study, despite higher mean practice scores among nurses with specialist training, regression analysis revealed an independent, negative relationship between training and clinical practice performance after accounting for other factors. This finding differs from conventional assumption from other specialties, such as ophthalmology, where systematic training has been shown to enhance core competencies and the quality of specialized care24. This apparent discrepancy suggests that the independent contribution of specialist training to practice might be negligible or negative, possibly because the training did not focus on skill application or translate into an additive improvement in behavioral change. Still, fewer than 50% participants would always monitor the patient’s adherence to inhalation therapy, evaluate their inhalation technique and work with them to identify the reasons when the adherence was poor or treatment was not working well. It implied that most nursing interventions stop at initial instruction, failing to establish a continuous cycle of monitoring, assessment, and feedback. Close monitoring of inhaler technique is a key component of good practice in asthma and COPD care25. Generally, nonadherence is categorized as intentional or unintentional. Unintentional nonadherence may stem from forgetfulness, poor inhaler technique, or insufficient understanding of instructions—all of which may be addressed through improved education delivered by nurses to patients26. In contrast, intentional nonadherence often stems from patients’ beliefs about their disease or medication, including concerns about side effects or perceived lack of therapeutic need27. It is therefore vital for nurses to engage in open communication with patients to better understand their motivations, concerns, and preferences28,29, which should not be a one-time event but an ongoing process, integrated into routine follow-up visits.

Swami et al. suggested that nurses with adequate training can improve their competency in assessing and teaching correct inhaler techniques to their patients and provide personalized education. This could help reduce the incidence of inhaler use errors and effectively manage COPD30. While over 60% of nurses engaged in quarterly inhalation therapy training using diverse sources, our study found that KAP improvements were closely tied to the exact training frequency and methods employed. Notably, high-frequency training may not be associated with improved practical behavior or knowledge if the methodology is inappropriate. Specifically, educational videos were directly correlated with both knowledge and practical performance, while practical workshops were also significantly linked to knowledge acquisition. This finding is supported by precious evidence: for instance, educational video was proved to improve nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes toward opioid use disorders31. Such effectiveness underscores the value of a blended learning model incorporating video-assisted resource, which is useful for teaching clinical skills to students of health including nursing and is often preferred by students due to its flexibility32. Videos ensure standardization of the core educational content, eliminating variability inherent in live lectures. This consistency ensures all nurses receive the same high-quality instruction on critical steps. The repeatability of videos is crucial for mastering psychomotor skills; nurses can review intricate steps (e.g., inhaler actuation-coordination) at their own pace, pausing and rewinding as needed until the procedure is internalized. This self-paced, on-demand learning accommodates different learning speeds and schedules, promoting better consolidation of both declarative knowledge and procedural skills. Thus, to ensure the quality of the blended learning video resource the length of time for the video should be short, authentic situations used and supporting information provided to enable students to understand the context and engage with video resource.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although efforts were made to mitigate biases related to convenience sampling—through a pilot study, diversifying recruitment sources, and increasing sample size—regional variations may still exist due to the participation of nurses from different cities across China. The sample overrepresented tertiary hospitals and nurses from urban settings, while primary care and rural facilities were underrepresented, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to respiratory nurses in primary healthcare settings or rural areas. Second, voluntary participation may have introduced selection biases, potentially leading to an overestimation of the findings33. Third, the cross-sectional design lacks randomization, which may limit the ability to establish causal relationships among the variables34. Future studies employing random sampling methods with larger sample size are needed to improve accuracy and validate the reported results. Also, to accurately evaluate current training approaches for respiratory nurses in inhalation therapy, enhanced monitoring and follow-up of clinical nursing practices are required. In addition, this study primarily assessed self-reported knowledge and practices, which may not fully correspond to actual clinical performance. Observations of clinical practice would provide a more objective measure of competency in inhalation therapy.

Conclusions

Taken together, our study investigated the knowledge, attitudes, practices, and training status of respiratory nurses regarding inhalation therapy across multiple regions in China, yielding a comprehensive report which suggest that although respiratory nurses have positive attitudes and proactive practices towards inhalation therapy, their knowledge need to be improved. The discrepancy between knowledge acquisition and its translation into practice may be bridged by more practice-focused educational formats such as educational videos and practical workshops. The quality, relevance, and design of the training method are more consequential than its mere frequency. To enhance inhalation therapy management, formal specialist nurse training programs should adopt more integrated learning strategies that combine theoretical instruction with visually engaging tools like educational videos, skill practice, and incorporate modules designed to cultivate positive professional attitudes. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to trace the long-term effect of training on objective patients’ outcomes, thereby closing the loop between nurse education, competent practice, and improved patient health.
