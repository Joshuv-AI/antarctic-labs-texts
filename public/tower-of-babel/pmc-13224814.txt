Renal blood flow and progression to kidney failure in ADPKD
Authors: Kristen L Nowak, Timothy P Copeland, Elaine Ku, Mark J Sarnak, Zhiying You, Berenice Gitomer, Kaleab Z Abebe, Kyongtae T Bae, Arlene Chapman, Ronald Perrone, Frederic F Rahbari-Oskoui, Vicente Torres, Alan S L Yu, Michel Chonchol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA decrease in renal blood flow (RBF) precedes estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline among patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) and inversely associates with disease severity and progression. However, it is unknown whether RBF independently predicts progression to kidney failure in patients with ADPKD.MethodsA total of 379 participants with early-stage ADPKD (mean age 36.6 ± 8.5 years; eGFR: 92.9 ± 17.1 ml/min/1.73 m2) who participated in the Halt Progression of Polycystic Kidney Disease Study A (HALT PKD A; NCT00283686) with baseline RBF (phase contrast magnetic resonance imaging) data were included in the primary analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were used to determine the association of baseline RBF as a continuous and categorical variable with kidney failure risk (during the HALT study and subsequently linked to the US Renal Data System) over a median follow-up period of 13.1 years (interquartile range 7.5–14.5) (primary outcome). All-cause mortality was also considered as a competing risk (Fine and Gray method). The associations of RBF with eGFR slope and the change in height-adjusted total kidney volume (∆htTKV) were evaluated with linear and logistic regression models.ResultsThe kidney failure incidence rate was the highest among those with the lowest RBF tertile {1.90 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.31–2.78] versus 0.34 [95% CI 0.14–0.83] events/100 person-years in tertile 3}. In the fully adjusted model, the lowest RBF tertile was associated with a higher risk of progression to kidney failure [hazard ratio (HR) 3.19 (95% CI 1.05–9.67] versus the highest tertile]. The association of RBF with kidney failure risk was slightly attenuated in adjusted models. All associations remained similar when considering death as a competing risk [HR 0.82 (95% CI 0.67–1.00) per 100 ml/min/1.73 m2 higher RBF]. Higher RBF was associated with a slower eGFR decline and lower odds of rapid annual ∆htTKV.ConclusionsLower baseline RBF independently associates with a greater risk of progression to kidney failure in early-stage patients with ADPKD.

INTRODUCTION

In contrast to most other organs, arterial blood flow to the kidneys meets not only metabolic demands but is also critical for filtration of the blood [1]. Notably, the kidneys receive about one-fifth of cardiac output at rest in healthy individuals [1], and oxygen delivery is proportional to total renal blood flow (RBF) [1]. RBF is reduced in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2–5], and resulting hypoxia may exacerbate CKD progression [6].

RBF also decreases with disease progression in patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), with declines occurring in parallel with the increase in total kidney volume (TKV) but preceding the decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [7]. Combining multiple magnetic resonance features beyond TKV alone, including the addition of RBF, enhanced sensitivity to predict eGFR decline among a longitudinal cohort of patients with ADPKD [8]. However, it is currently unknown whether RBF predicts progression to kidney failure in patients with ADPKD or in patients with CKD secondary to other aetiologies.

Accordingly, the goal of this analysis was to determine whether RBF in patients with early-stage ADPKD is associated with progression to kidney failure independent of eGFR and other known risk factors. Through the recent linkage of the Halt Progression of Polycystic Kidney Disease Study A (HALT PKD A; NCT00283686) data to national kidney failure and death registries, we determined whether baseline RBF was associated with progression to kidney failure over a median follow-up of 13.1 years. We hypothesized that lower RBF at baseline would be independently associated with a greater risk of kidney failure. We also evaluated whether baseline RBF was associated with a change in kidney function (eGFR slope) and kidney growth [annual change in height-adjusted TKV (htTKV)] during the HALT study period.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

The design of the HALT PKD Study A, a prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre trial, has been described in detail previously [9–11]. Briefly, 558 participants were randomized between February 2006 and June 2009 across seven sites in the USA. All participants provided written informed consent and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants had a known diagnosis of ADPKD, were 15–49 years of age at baseline, had an eGFR (using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation) of >60 ml/min/1.73 m2, either hypertension or high-normal blood pressure (BP) and did not have diabetes. The study was a 2 × 2 factorial design with randomization to 1) an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) + angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) compared with an ACEi + placebo and 2) low (95–110/60–75 mmHg) compared with standard (120–130/70–80 mmHg) BP control. The primary outcome of HALT Study A was the percent change in TKV, assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). RBF was not measured in HALT Study B.

Study variables

Renal blood flow

Phase contrast magnetic resonance (MR) images were obtained in a 1.5-T scanner at participating centres using a protocol developed by the HALT PKD Imaging Subcommittee based on the Consortium for Radiologic Imaging Studies of Polycystic Kidney Disease (CRISP) study [12] and transferred to the Image Analysis Center at the University of Pittsburgh for central analysis [10, 13]. In brief, as a reference image, thick section oblique axial two-dimensional phase-contrast breath-hold MR angiograms were obtained along the course of each renal artery. MR blood flow sequences utilized a two-dimensional cine phase-contrast breath-hold pulse sequence perpendicular to the renal arteries and flow rates were calculated utilizing FLOW software. Of the 558 participants in the HALT PKD Study A, 379 had baseline RBF data available and were included in the kidney failure analysis. A total of 347 and 338 participants had baseline RBF data and follow-up eGFR and htTKV data, respectively, and were included in the eGFR slope and change in htTKV analyses. There was a high number of missing RBF measurements in the trial due to technical issues with acquisition of high-quality phase contrast images. All analyses of RBF were performed by a single radiologist, the measurement process required on average 10–20 minutes per participant, all acquisition sites were trained using a test dataset to ensure precision and reproducibility and the intra- and interreview variability was <5%.

Kidney failure and death

We previously linked former enrolees from five HALT PKD study sites to the US Renal Data System (USRDS; the national kidney failure registry) [14] and the National Death Index to obtain longer-term follow-up for kidney and mortality endpoints (through 31 December 2020) [14, 15]. Five sites provided institutional review board approval, in addition to the data coordinating centre, for linkage of data. Two sites (Cleveland Clinic and Mayo Clinic) elected not to participate in this analysis. For these two sites, kidney failure and death events from the parent trial were included in the current analysis and follow-up was censored after the final visit, which occurred in 2014. Among the 407 patients linked to the USRDS and National Death Index (two of the seven sites were not linked), there were 58 new cases of kidney failure and 11 new deaths in HALT Study A.

Kidney function decline and kidney growth

Kidney growth was quantified as the annual percent change in htTKV using the baseline and final value (month 60). htTKV was determined by MRI and analysed by a central analysis centre at the University of Pittsburgh, as described previously [9, 10]. The 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation was used to calculate eGFR (this differed from the equation used for study inclusion) [9] and a slope was calculated using regression models including three or more time points from the HALT study period.

Covariates

Potential confounders related to RBF and kidney failure, all measured at baseline, were selected a priori as potential covariates. As required by the funding agency, race was determined by self-report. Systolic BP was measured in the seated position as the average of three readings following at least 5 minutes of rest, as described previously [10]. Glucose level was measured in fasted serum samples collected during screening using a standard methodology. Urine albumin excretion was measured by 24-hour urine collections [10]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using baseline body weight (in kg) divided by baseline height (in m2) and rounded to the nearest tenth. Participants were categorized based on BMI using the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute’s criteria [16] as normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2) or obese (≥30 kg/m2). Genotype analysis was performed previously, with mutation class categorized as PKD1 mutations, PKD2 mutations, no mutation detected and unknown [17]. Missing data for systolic BP (n = 3), htTKV (n = 11) and urine albumin (n = 6) were imputed using single imputation with chained equations, incorporating age, sex and race as covariates, with 20 burn-in iterations in the kidney failure analysis. This methodology was also applied to the eGFR slope and change in htTKV analyses.

Statistical analyses

The incidence rate was calculated as the number of kidney failure outcomes per 100 person-years. The association of baseline RBF [as a continuous and categorical variable (tertiles)] with time to kidney failure was analysed using Cox proportional hazard models. We checked for proportional hazards assumptions using Schoenfeld residuals. Time in all models began at randomization and follow-up ended at kidney failure, death or 31 December 2020 (for the sites with data linked to the USRDS and National Death Index) or end of trial closure (for the two sites in the parent trial that did not participate in long-term linkage). All-cause mortality was considered as a competing risk in a sensitivity analysis using the Fine and Gray method.

Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, race and randomized treatment arm (model 1), model 1 plus baseline systolic BP and serum glucose (model 2) and model 2 plus baseline eGFR, urine albumin (log-transformed) and htTKV (log-transformed) (model 3). As there was no univariate association of genotype with RBF (Table 1; no difference in genotypes across RBF tertiles), it was not included as a covariate. In a final model (model 4), BMI classification was added separately, as we recently reported that higher BMI was associated with lower RBF in patients with ADPKD (thus BMI could be a mediator rather than a confounder) [18]. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to visualize unadjusted kidney failure rates.

Linear regression models were used to evaluate the association of baseline RBF [as a continuous and categorical variable (tertiles)] with eGFR slope, using the same covariates as in the Cox proportional hazards analyses. A subgroup analysis for participants with Mayo class 1C was also performed (n = 116), given that TKV does not perform well in this category, as there is a broad range of disease progression [19]. Multinomial logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios for three categories of annual change in htTKV (<5%, 5–7% and ≥7%), with the <5% change in htTKV, the lowest RBF tertile, serving as the reference, as described previously [20]. RBF was considered as both a categorical variable (tertiles) and continuous variable.

Baseline characteristics were summarized by RBF tertiles and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Any cells <11 were suppressed per USRDS guidelines, with categories combined as necessary. This limited presentation of certain data, such as the number of kidney failure events by tertile. The Pearson chi-squared test, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA; kidney failure analysis) and SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA; eGFR slope and htTKV analysis) and two-tailed values of P < .05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics at baseline

Among the 379 participants included in the primary analysis (kidney failure outcome), age at baseline was 36.6 ± 8.5 years, 50% were male (n = 189), 91% (n = 346) were non-Hispanic White, baseline eGFR was 92.9 ± 17.1 ml/min/1.73 m2 and baseline RBF was 595 ml/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 461–728). Individuals with a lower baseline RBF were more likely to be older, have lower baseline eGFR and have a larger baseline htTKV (Table 1). Participants who had RBF data available had similar participant characteristics to those with missing RBF data, with the exception of a higher baseline eGFR in those with RBF data available (Supplemental Table 1).

Relation between RBF and kidney failure

The median follow-up time was 13.1 years (IQR 7.5–14.5). There were a total of 41 kidney failure events. Kidney failure events stratified by tertile cannot be presented, per USRDS guidelines (any cells <11 must be suppressed). The incidence rate of kidney failure was higher in individuals in the lowest tertile of baseline RBF (Table 2). Lower RBF tertile was associated with a shorter time to occurrence of kidney failure (logrank P < .001; Fig. 1). This association remained statistically significant across all adjusted models (Table 3). As compared with the highest tertile, participants with the lowest tertile of RBF were 319% (IQR 5–967) more likely to reach kidney failure in the fully adjusted model. In unadjusted models, for every 100-unit (ml/min/1.7 3m2) higher RBF, participants were 32% (IQR 19–43) less likely to reach kidney failure. When considering RBF as a continuous variable, the association was slightly attenuated in adjusted model 3 and slightly more attenuated when including BMI in the model. However, all associations remained similar when considering death as a competing risk (Table 4).

The median follow-up time for the eGFR slope was 6.0 years (IQR 1.0–7.0). Baseline RBF was positively associated with eGFR slope in all models when considered as a continuous variable or comparing the highest to lowest tertile (Table 5). In the fully adjusted model (including baseline eGFR and htTKV), for every 100-unit (ml/min/1.73 m2) higher RBF, the eGFR slope was 0.29 units (IQR 0.14–0.45) greater. Results were similar in the Mayo class 1C subgroup, albeit slightly attenuated due to lower statistical power [eGFR slope 0.25 units greater per 100-unit higher RBF (IQR −0.025–0.52)]. In the fully adjusted model, higher RBF was modestly associated with slower kidney growth [odds ratio ≥7% versus 5% annual change in htTKV: 0.85 (IQR 0.73–1.00) per 100-unit (ml/min/1.73 m2) higher RBF] (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

We have shown that among patients with ADPKD early in the course of their disease, low RBF increases the risk of progression to kidney failure over long-term follow-up. To our knowledge, an association of RBF with progression to kidney failure has not been demonstrated previously in patients with ADPKD (or in other populations). Although RBF and eGFR may provide overlapping information, importantly, the observed association persisted after adjustment for baseline eGFR. In addition, higher baseline RBF was associated with slower kidney function decline (eGFR slope) and modestly associated with slower kidney growth (change in htTKV) over the HALT study period.

The phase-contrast MR protocol implemented in HALT Study A was based on the CRISP study. Cross-sectionally in CRISP, RBF correlated positively with measured GFR, which remained statistically significant after adjustment for covariates and accounted for 80% of the variance in the model [12]. Longitudinally, over a 3-year period, RBF declined, which paralleled the increase in TKV and preceded the decrease in eGFR [7]. This is consistent with evidence that young patients with ADPKD already have reduced effective renal plasma flow as compared with healthy controls, despite having comparable GFR [21]. At baseline in HALT Study A, RBF correlated inversely with TKV and positively with eGFR. The association of RBF with eGFR persisted after covariate adjustment, including TKV [13]. Renal vascular resistance increased more in the standard BP arm over the HALT Study A duration, such that RBF declined similarly in both arms despite the difference in BP between treatment groups [9].

More recently, combining multiple MR features beyond TKV alone, including RBF, enhanced sensitivity to predict eGFR decline among a cohort of 186 patients with ADPKD [8]. Our current findings confirm an association of RBF with eGFR decline and expand prior knowledge regarding this association by relating RBF to the clinically meaningful endpoint of kidney failure. Furthermore, results were similar in a subgroup analysis of individuals with Mayo class 1C, suggesting that RBF could potentially be a valuable biomarker in this category where TKV does not perform well. Additionally, we report a modest association of baseline RBF with the change in htTKV in the fully adjusted model, suggesting that the mechanism by which RBF promotes progression to kidney failure may be in part via kidney growth.

RBF is regulated by both intrinsic mechanisms (myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback) and extrinsic mechanisms (neurohormonal factors) on a day-to-day basis [1, 22]. In ADPKD, sympathetic hyperactivity and hypoxia at the juxtoglomerular apparatus can promote renin release and subsequent activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [23], which plays a key role in alterations in renal haemodynamics in ADPKD [24]. Furthermore, long-term decline in RBF in patients with ADPKD is likely related to structural changes resulting from dramatic vascular remodelling [25–27].

In an observation cohort of 61 patients with ADPKD and measurements of kidney haemodynamics, renal plasma flow decreased over a 2-year follow-up, but afferent resistance increased only in patients with Mayo class 1C–1E (not 1A or 1B). The greater eGFR decline observed in the faster-progressing group (1C–1E) was caused by the increase in afferent resistance resulting from a decrease in efferent resistance [28]. This supports a high likelihood that an increase in renal afferent resistance contributes to the decline in eGFR with progressive ADPKD. Additionally, reductions in RBF can lead to hypoxia and reduced oxygen delivery to tissue, potentially promoting tubular injury, inflammation and kidney disease progression [1]. We have shown that in early-stage patients with ADPKD, both kidney oxidative metabolism and cortical perfusion are reduced as compared with healthy controls, which correlates inversely with TKV [29]. As both factors contribute to the development of hypoxia, this supports the hypothesis that kidney hypoxia may exacerbate cyst growth, although hypoxia could not be assessed in the current study.

There are several important strengths of this study, including linkage to a clinically important outcome (kidney failure) over a long-term follow-up period, a large sample size and protocolized assessments of RBF and covariates in the setting of a clinical trial. Several limitations also merit discussion. RBF can be technically challenging to accurately measure, which may limit utility outside of a research setting. Methodologically, phase contrast MRI has been shown to accurately measure RBF in patients with ADPKD, although feasibility is reduced in patients with greater disease severity due greater technical challenges [30]. Accordingly, assessment of RBF was only performed in HALT Study A (early-stage patients, and not successfully obtained in all patients in the original study) and not HALT Study B (late-stage patients), limiting the generalizability of our results. Additionally, trial sites varied in the technical expertise necessary for high-quality phase contrast MRI scanning, which may have introduced site bias in data acquisition.

There were also a small number of kidney failure events. Thus there is a possibility of type 2 error in the analyses, and we were unable to conduct subgroup analyses. An additional limitation was that it was beyond the scope of current analysis to compare the prognostic utility of RBF to other established predictors, including Mayo imaging classification and the PROPKD score, which would be an important next step. Additional limitations include assessment of RBF and covariates at a single baseline time point, exclusion of two sites from linkage to the USRDS and National Death Index [31, 32], that ACEi/ARB can increase RBF (randomization in HALT) and a lack of mechanistic insight, such as hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha as a marker of hypoxia. Finally, information is not available from participants after HALT Study A ended, including whether they began tolvaptan and subsequent BP control.

Our results have important clinical implications, as they support that RBF can be both utilized as a biomarker to predict progression to kidney failure in patients with ADPKD and that a reduction in RBF should be further studied as a potential physiological mechanism contributing to disease progression. Whether reduced RBF is independently associated with progression to kidney failure in patients with other aetiologies of kidney disease should also be assessed. Additionally, there is utility in continuing to integrate assessment of RBF into future clinical trials and observational cohorts with ADPKD patients. Although implementing assessment of RBF in the clinical setting is more challenging, this should be considered in the future, as the feasibility and reproducibility of this methodology continues to advance.
