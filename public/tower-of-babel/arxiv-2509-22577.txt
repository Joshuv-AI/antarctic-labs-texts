Abstract
. Let A ∈ ℝ n × n A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} be a random matrix with independent entries, and suppose that the entries are “uniformly anticoncentrated” in the sense that there is a constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 such that each entry a i ​ j a_{ij} satisfies sup z ℙ [ a i ​ j = z ] ≤ 1 − ε \sup_{z}\mathbb{P}[a_{ij}=z]\leq 1-\varepsilon (for example, A A could be a uniformly random n × n n\times n matrix with ± 1 \pm 1 entries). Significantly improving previous bounds of Tao and Vu, we prove that the permanent of A A is exponentially anticoncentrated: there is c ε > 0 c_{\varepsilon}>0 such that sup z ℙ [ per ( A ) = z ] ≤ exp ( − c ε n ) \sup_{z}\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]\leq\exp(-c_{\varepsilon}n) . Our proof also works for the determinant, giving an alternative proof of a classical theorem of Kahn, Komlós and Szemerédi. As a consequence, we see that there are at least exponentially many different permanents of n × n n\times n matrices with ± 1 \pm 1 entries, resolving a problem of Ingram and Razborov.



1.
Introduction

Two important matrix parameters are the
determinant

and
permanent
: for an
n\times n
matrix
A=(a_{ij})
,
they are defined as

(1.1)

\det(A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\operatorname{sign}(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i\pi(i)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{per}(A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i\pi(i)}.

Determinants are omnipresent throughout mathematics and the sciences. Permanents are of fundamental importance in combinatorics and computational complexity theory, and also play an important role in quantum physics and statistics (see for example the books and surveys
[
2
,
27
,
17
,
20
,
16
]
).

In combinatorics, computer science and random matrix theory, it is of great interest to understand statistical properties of determinants and permanents, among various classes of discrete matrices.
In particular, one of the most intensive directions of
research is the study of the singularity probability of random sign matrices. Let
A_{n}

be a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix with
\pm 1
entries; what
is the probability that
\det(A_{n})=0
? Perhaps the foundational
theorem in combinatorial random matrix theory, due to Komlós
[
12
]
,
is that
\mathbb{P}[\det(A_{n})=0]=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
. A subsequent
breakthrough by Kahn, Komlós and Szemerédi
[
10
]
(simplified
by Tao and Vu
[
22
]
) showed that the singularity probability
is in fact
exponentially small
: that is to say,
\mathbb{P}[\det(A_{n})=0]=\exp(-cn)

for some constant
c>0
. Quantitative aspects were later improved
by Tao and Vu
[
23
]
and by Bourgain, Vu and Wood
[
3
]
,
culminating in a celebrated result of Tikhomirov
[
26
]
proving the essentially optimal bound
\mathbb{P}[\det(A_{n})=0]\leq(1/2+o(1))^{n}
.

It is reasonable to expect similar results for the permanent. In fact one may expect that
even stronger
bounds
should hold; for example, Vu (see
[
29
, Conjecture 6.12]
and

[
28
]
) conjectured that
\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})=0]
decays faster than exponentially.
However, the permanent is much more difficult to study than the determinant,
and much less is known: until now, the only theorem in this
direction is due to Tao and Vu
[
25
]
, who proved that

\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})=0]\leq n^{-c}

for some constant
c>0

(see also
[
24
]
for an earlier, simpler version of their argument that
gives a bound of shape
\exp(-c\sqrt{\log n})
). Although Tao and Vu did not attempt to optimise the constant
c
, they note that their argument cannot hope to obtain a stronger bound than about
1/\sqrt{n}
, owing to their use of the so-called
Erdős–Littlewood–Offord inequality
(they write that “in principle, one can obtain better results by using more advanced Littlewood-Offord inequalities, but it is not clear to the authors how to restructure the rest of the argument so that such inequalities can be exploited”).

In this paper, we overcome this barrier, obtaining the first exponential bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
A_{n}
be a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix with
\pm 1
entries. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})=x]\leq\exp(-cn).

Here, we stated a bound for the probability that
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})=x
for general
x
, not just for
x=0
, but this should not be viewed as a new feature of our work: it is easy to make the same generalisation with all previous work on determinants and permanents of discrete random matrices.

It is worth remarking that our proof works in exactly the same way
for the determinant instead of the permanent, so as a byproduct this
also gives a new proof of the Kahn–Komlós–Szemerédi theorem.
All previous work on determinants of random matrices fundamentally used linear-algebraic properties of the determinant; since such properties are unavailable
for the permanent, our proof is necessarily very different. In fact, our proof is also very different to the previous approach of Tao and Vu in
[
25
]
(which was until now the only known approach to study permanents of random matrices).

1.1.
General entry distributions

Some of the previous work on determinants and permanents of discrete random matrices readily generalises to other entry distributions. For example, shortly after Komlós’ foundational paper showing that
\mathbb{P}[\det(A_{n})=0]=o(1)
, in a second paper
[
13
]
he proved that the same result holds for a random matrix with independent
\mu
-distributed entries, for any non-degenerate distribution
\mu
(fixing
\mu
and sending
n\to\infty
).

Bourgain, Vu and Wood
[
3
]
observed that in fact one does not need to fix the entry distribution; it suffices for the entries to be independent and “uniformly anticoncentrated” in the sense that there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that each entry
a_{ij}
satisfies
\sup_{z}\mathbb{P}[a_{ij}=z]\leq 1-\varepsilon
. Specifically, they observed (see
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
) that some of their ideas could be combined with an approach of Tao and Vu
[
22
]
, to prove that
\mathbb{P}[\det(A_{n})=0]
decays exponentially in this very general setting. Here, we prove the same statement for the permanent.

For a random variable
X
, we write
Q[X]=\sup_{x}\mathbb{P}[X=x]
for the maximum point probability of
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
0<p<1
there is
c_{p}>0
such that the
following holds. Let
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a random matrix with independent entries, such that every entry
\xi
of
A_{n}
satisfies
Q[\xi]\leq p
.
Then

Q[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})]\leq\exp(-c_{p}n).

Note that
Theorem

1.1
is an immediate corollary of
Theorem

1.2
for
p=1/2
. We remark that while
Theorem

1.1
might be improvable to a super-exponential bound (as conjectured by Vu), in the more general setting of
Theorem

1.2
an exponential bound is best-possible. Indeed, if the entries in the first row of
A_{n}
are each equal to zero with probability
p
, then with probability
p^{n}
the entire first row is zero, in which case
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})=0
.

1.2.
The range of the permanent

For any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\Phi_{S}(n)
for the set of all permanents of
n\times n
matrices with entries in
S
.

For any discrete random variable
X
, it is easy to see that
X
must be supported on at least
1/Q[X]
different values. So, by applying
Theorem

1.2
to random matrices whose entries are uniformly sampled from any pair
\{a,b\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we obtain an exponential lower bound on
|\Phi_{\{a,b\}}(n)|
.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of size at least 2, taking
c_{1/2}
as in
Theorem

1.2
, we have

|\Phi_{S}(n)|\geq\exp(c_{1/2}n),

This result is not new when
S=\{0,1\}
. Indeed, in this case a classical theorem of Brualdi and Newman
[
4
]
says that
\Phi_{\{0,1\}}(n)\supseteq\{0,\dots,2^{n-1}\}
(see also the improvements in
[
7
]
). However,
Corollary

1.3
significantly improves on previous results when
S=\{-1,1\}
. Indeed, in 1993 Kräuter
[
14
]
showed that
|\Phi_{\{-1,1\}}(n)|\geq n+1
. This has remained essentially the state of the art
1
1

1

One can slightly improve Kräuter’s bound using Tao and Vu’s results on permanents of random matrices, but it is unclear how to get more than
o(n)
additional permanents this way.
until very recently, when Ingram and Razborov
[
9
]
showed how to use ideas from additive combinatorics and Diophantine geometry to prove that
|\Phi_{\{-1,1\}}(n)|\geq\exp(c(\log n)^{2}/\log\log n)
for some constant
c>0
. Actually, they explicitly raised the problem (see
[
9
, Problem 1]
) of proving that
|\Phi_{\{-1,1\}}(n)|
grows exponentially in
n
; this problem is solved by
Corollary

1.3
.

1.3.
Proof ideas

The full proof of
Theorem

1.2
is not very long, but it nonetheless seems worthwhile to give a brief glimpse of the ideas. The starting point is a “relative anticoncentration inequality” (
Theorem

3.1
), proved using a theorem of Kesten
[
11
]
(which appears as
Theorem

3.2
, and is the only aspect of our proof which is not self-contained). Very roughly speaking,
Theorem

3.1
says that, under certain assumptions, sums of independent random variables are more anticoncentrated than “thinned-out versions of themselves” obtained by taking a randomly subsampled subset of the summands. This inequality is related to certain inequalities of Halász
[
8
]
that played a crucial role in the work of Kahn, Komlós and Szemerédi
[
10
]
on the singularity probability (mentioned earlier in this introduction). However, the way we use
Theorem

3.1
is rather different to all previous work in this area: we use it to prove a
recursive
anticoncentration bound, taking advantage of the recursive properties of the formula in
Equation

1.1
.

In a bit more detail: in the setting of
Theorem

1.2
, it is not hard to see that
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})
can be interpreted as a weighted sum
a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\xi_{n}
,
where
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are the entries of the last row, and

a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are permanents of
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrices of the first
n-1
rows. If we condition on the first
n-1
rows of
A_{n}
(so the only remaining randomness is in the last row) then this is a sum of independent random variables, to which we can apply our relative anticoncentration theorem. It turns out that if we average the resulting bound over all possible outcomes of the first
n-1
rows, we get a bound on the anticoncentration of
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})
, in terms of the anticoncentration of the permanents of certain “smaller” random matrices.

To see why this is useful, suppose that we were able to prove a bound of the form
Q[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})]\leq\alpha_{p}Q[\operatorname{per}(A_{n-1})]
, for some
\alpha_{p}<1
. We would then be able to iterate this bound to prove that
Q[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})]\leq\alpha_{p}^{n}
; i.e.,
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})
is exponentially anticoncentrated, as desired. Unfortunately, the situation is not quite this simple: it turns out that for our smaller random matrices,
the point probabilities of the entries are not bounded by
p
anymore,
and also there are “error terms” corresponding to certain assumptions we need to make to effectively apply
Theorem

3.1
. Nonetheless, with various additional ideas, we can prove a sufficiently strong recursive anticoncentration bound (see
Theorem

2.2
)
for a certain more general class of random matrices (which are allowed to contain some fixed deterministic rows in addition to their random rows).

It is worth remarking that our proof is “purely combinatorial”; in particular, it features no geometry, no linear algebra and no Fourier analysis.

1.4.
Further directions

The most obvious direction for further research is to improve the
bound in
Theorem

1.1
, towards the super-exponential
bound conjectured by Vu.

Also, it would be very interesting to generalise these ideas to
symmetric
random matrices. For a random symmetric
n\times n
matrix
S_{n}
with
\pm 1
entries, the second and third author
[
15
]
proved that
\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(S_{n})=0]\leq n^{-c}
for some constant
c
, but this method cannot prove a bound stronger than
1/\sqrt{n}
. Even for the determinant, it is much more difficult to prove exponential bounds in the symmetric setting: this was accomplished only recently by Campos, Jenssen, Michelen and Sahasrabudhe
[
5
]
.

Another direction is to consider the “small-ball” setting: namely, instead of just studying the probability that
\operatorname{per}(A_{n})
is equal to a given point, we could study the probability that it lies in an interval of given length. Actually, Tao and Vu
[
24
]
already considered this small-ball setting: their proof incorporates a “growth” step, which allows them to prove that
\mathbb{P}[|\operatorname{per}(A_{n})-x|\leq n^{(1/2-\varepsilon)n}]\leq n^{-1/10}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and any constant
\varepsilon>0
(assuming
n
is sufficiently large). By incorporating this growth step into our own proof scheme, one can prove the same result with an exponential probability bound. (This introduces some additional technicalities, so in the interest of keeping the arguments simple, in this paper we decided to restrict our attention to the point-probability setting.)

In the latter direction it is worth mentioning the
permanent anticoncentration conjecture
of Aaronson and Arkhipov
[
1
]

(a key ingredient in the analysis
of the hardness of the
BosonSampling
problem in quantum computing).
This is a precise conjecture about the typical size of the permanent of a Gaussian random matrix,
seemingly well outside the reach of current techniques. See

[
1
]
for some discussion of the relevant obstacles; some of
these obstacles can be overcome by ideas in this paper, but the critical
obstacle is that existing techniques are not sufficiently sensitive
to small differences in the permanent.

1.5.
Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
be the set of non-negative integers. We use asymptotic notation throughout: for functions
f=f(n)
and
g=g(n)
, we write
f=O(g)
or
g=\Omega(f)
or
f\lesssim g
to mean that there is a constant
C
such that
|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|
for sufficiently large
n
,
and we write
f=o(g)
to mean that
f/g\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Subscripts on asymptotic notation indicate quantities that should be treated as constants. Also, all random variables throughout this paper are assumed to be real-valued.

2.
A recursive bound

We will actually prove a slight strengthening of
Theorem

1.2
, where we consider matrices which have a random part and a fixed part. This is necessary for our recursive approach.

Definition 2.1
.

For
0<p<1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{p}(n)
be the supremum of
Q[\operatorname{per}(A)]
, over
all random matrices
A
of the following form.

•

For some
T\in\mathbb{N}
, let
A_{\mathrm{fix}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times(n+T)}
be a fixed
T\times(n+T)

matrix containing at least one
T\times T
submatrix with nonzero
permanent.

•

Let
A_{\mathrm{rand}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n+T)}
be a random matrix with independent entries, such that every entry
\xi
of
A_{\mathrm{rand}}
satisfies
Q[\xi]\leq p
.

•

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+T)\times(n+T)}
be the
(n+T)\times(n+T)
matrix obtained by
concatenating
A_{\mathrm{fix}}
and
A_{\mathrm{rand}}
(i.e.,
the first
T
rows are the rows of
A_{\mathrm{fix}}
, and the last

n
rows are the rows of
A_{\mathrm{rand}}
).

In
Definition

2.1
, we think of
n
as being the “size” of
A
(the random part
A_{\mathrm{rand}}
has
n
rows, and in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, we will only really use the randomness of
n
of its columns). We will prove a recursive bound for
f_{p}(n)
, see
Theorem

2.2
below. To state this bound, for any
0<p<1
, let us define

(2.1)

\tau_{p}=1-\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}(1-p^{s}),

and observe that
\tau_{p}<1
, no matter how close
p
is to
1
. To see this, let
C_{p}
be sufficiently large in terms of
p
such that
\exp(-C_{p}p)\leq 1-p
, so for every
0\leq x\leq p
we have
1-x\geq\exp(-C_{p}x)
, and hence
1-\tau_{p}=\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}(1-p^{s})\geq\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}\exp(-C_{p}p^{s})=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big(}\!-C_{p}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}p^{s}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big)}=\exp(-C_{p}p/(1-p))>0
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
0<p<1
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0

and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
we have

f_{p}(n)\leq\exp(-\delta n)\;+\;\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)\;+\;(1+\varepsilon)\tau_{p}\cdot f_{p}(n-t)\;+\;(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s).

In the course of proving
Theorem

2.2
, we will also prove the following easier bound (which shall serve as a base case when proving
Theorem

1.2
by induction).

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
p<1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

f_{p}(n)\leq 1-\prod_{s=1}^{n}(1-p^{s})\leq\tau_{p}<1.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
given
Theorem

2.2
and
Proposition

2.3
.

As in the theorem statement, fix
0<p<1
. First, note that it suffices to prove that
f_{p}(n)\leq\exp(-c_{p}n)
for some
c_{p}>0
. Indeed, any random matrix
A_{n}
as in
Theorem

1.2
trivially satisfies the conditions in
Definition

2.1
for
T=0
, so
Q[\operatorname{per}(A_{n})]\leq f_{p}(n)
.

Recalling that
\tau_{p}<1
, choose
\mu>0
such that
\tau_{p}<1-5\mu
. Then, choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon<(1-\sqrt{p})\mu
and
(1+\varepsilon)\tau_{p}<(1+\varepsilon)(1-5\mu)<1-4\mu
. Also, choose
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p^{t/2}<(1-\sqrt{p})\mu
. Now, let
\delta>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
be as in
Theorem

2.2
. By making
N
larger if needed, we may assume that
\exp(-\delta N/2)\leq\mu
.

By
Proposition

2.3
we have
f_{p}(n)<1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, we can choose some
c_{p}>0
such that
f_{p}(n)<\exp(-c_{p}n)
for
n=0,1,\dots,N
, and also
c_{p}<\delta/2
and
\exp(c_{p})<p^{-1/2}
and
(1-4\mu)\exp(c_{p}t)<1-3\mu
.

Let us now prove by induction that
f_{p}(n)\leq\exp(-c_{p}n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
n\leq N
this is automatically guaranteed by the choice of
c_{p}
. So let us now consider
n>N
, and assume that we have already shown
f_{p}(n^{\prime})\leq\exp(-c_{p}n^{\prime})
for all
n^{\prime}<n
. Then, using
Theorem

2.2
, we have

\displaystyle f_{p}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\delta n)+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)+(1+\varepsilon)\tau_{p}\cdot f_{p}(n-t)+(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\delta n)+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}\exp(-c_{p}(n\!-\!s))+(1-4\mu)\exp(-c_{p}(n\!-\!t))+(1+\varepsilon)\!\!\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}\exp(-c_{p}(n\!-\!s)).

This yields

\displaystyle f_{p}(n)\cdot\exp(c_{p}n)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-(\delta-c_{p})n)+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}\exp(c_{p}s)+(1-4\mu)\exp(c_{p}t)+(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}\exp(c_{p}s)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\delta n/2)+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}p^{-s/2}+(1-3\mu)+(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}p^{-s/2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-3\mu)+\exp(-\delta N/2)+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{n}p^{s/2}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s/2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-3\mu)+\exp(-\delta N/2)+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\sqrt{p}}+\frac{p^{t/2}}{1-\sqrt{p}}\leq(1-3\mu)+\mu+\mu+\mu=1,

as desired.
∎

3.
A relative anticoncentration inequality

A key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

2.2
is the following “relative anticoncentration inequality”, stating that under certain assumptions, a sum of independent random variables is more anticoncentrated than a “randomly thinned-out version of itself”. This is similar to
[
21
, Lemma 7.14]
and related inequalities of Halász
[
8
]
, used in previous work on the singularity probability of random matrices.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider independent random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, and for each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
X_{i}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
X_{i}
. For some integer
1\leq k<n/4
, let
K\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a uniformly random subset of exactly
k
indices (independent from the variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{n}^{\prime}
). Now, define
X=X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
and
X_{K}^{*}=\sum_{i\in K}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})
, and assume that
\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]<1-4k/n
.
Then we have

Q[X]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}{1-\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}.

The main ingredient to prove
Theorem

3.1
is the following (special case of a) theorem of Kesten
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
11
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Consider independent random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
and
0<p<1
, such that
Q[X_{i}]\leq p
holds for each
i=1,\dots,m
. Then

Q[X_{1}+\dots+X_{m}]\lesssim\frac{p}{\sqrt{(1-p)m}}.

We note that
Theorem

3.2
is the only part of this paper that is not self-contained. It was proved with a simple (but quite ingenious!) combinatorial argument, via an anticoncentration inequality of Rogozin
[
19
]
(which, in this context, is essentially equivalent to the so-called
Erdős–Littlewood–Offord theorem

[
6
]
). See also
[
18
]
for an alternative Fourier-analytic proof.

In our proof of
Theorem

3.1
, we will actually need the following slight generalisation of
Theorem

3.2
, which compares
Q[X_{1}+\dots+X_{m}]
to probabilities that the random variables
X_{i}
are equal to independent copies of themselves.

Corollary 3.3
.

Consider independent random variables

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
0<p<1
. For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
X_{i}^{\prime}
be an independent
copy of
X_{i}
. Suppose that we have
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}]\leq p
for
at least
m\geq 2
different indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

Q[X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}]\lesssim\frac{p}{\sqrt{(1-p)m}}.

To deduce
Corollary

3.3
from
Theorem

3.2
we will need a few more general inequalities. First, we record the (near-trivial) fact that a sum of independent random variables is at least as anticoncentrated as its summands.

Fact 3.4
.

For independent random variables
X,Y
we have
Q[X+Y]\leq Q[X]
.

Proof.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathbb{P}[X+Y=z]=\mathbb{P}[X=z-Y]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}\mathbb{P}[X=z-Y\,|\,Y]\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}Q[X]\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}=Q[X].\qed

Next, we record a simple “duplication” inequality for a sum of independent random variables.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider independent discrete
random variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. Letting
X_{1}^{\prime}
and
X_{2}^{\prime}
be independent copies of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, we have

Q[X_{1}+X_{2}]\leq\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=X_{1}^{\prime}]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{P}[X_{2}=X_{2}^{\prime}]^{1/2}.

In particular (applying the above inequality to the case where
X_{2}
has the distribution of
-X_{1}
), we have

Q[X_{1}-X_{1}^{\prime}]\leq\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=X_{1}^{\prime}].

Proof.

We have

Q[X_{1}+X_{2}]=\sup_{x}\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=z]\mathbb{P}[z+X_{2}=x]\leq\sup_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=z]^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[z+X_{2}=x]^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. But note that (for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
)

\displaystyle\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=z]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=z]\mathbb{P}[X_{1}^{\prime}=z]=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=X_{1}^{\prime}],

\displaystyle\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[z+X_{2}=x]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\mathbb{P}[X_{2}=x-z]\mathbb{P}[X_{2}^{\prime}=x-z]=\mathbb{P}[X_{2}=X_{2}^{\prime}],

and the desired result follows.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Corollary

3.3
.

Proof of
Corollary

3.3
.

We may assume that
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}]\leq p
for
i=1,\dots,m
. By
3.4
we may furthermore assume that
n=m
and that
m\geq 2
is even. Also, note that we may assume that
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are discrete random variables
2
2

2

Formally: for each
i
, write
A_{i}=\{x:\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=x]>0\}
as the “atomic part” of
X_{i}
, and note that for any
z
we have
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}=z\text{ and }X_{i}\notin A_{i}\text{ for some }i]=0
. So, if we alter the behaviour of
X_{i}
when
X_{i}\notin A_{i}
(to make
X_{i}
discrete), this cannot decrease
Q[X_{1}+\dots+X_{m}]
. We can make these alterations in a way that has an arbitrarily small impact on probabilities of the form
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}]
.
.

By the first part of
Lemma

3.5
, we see that
Q[X_{1}+\dots+X_{m}]
is at most

\mathbb{P}[(X_{1}+\dots+X_{m/2})=(X_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+X_{m/2}^{\prime})]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{P}[(X_{m/2+1}+\dots+X_{m})=(X_{m/2+1}^{\prime}+\dots+X_{m}^{\prime})]^{1/2}.

Then, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[(X_{1}+\dots+X_{m/2})=(X_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+X_{m/2}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[(X_{1}-X_{1}^{\prime})+\dots+(X_{m/2}-X_{m/2}^{\prime})=0]

\displaystyle\leq Q[(X_{1}-X_{1}^{\prime})+\dots+(X_{m/2}-X_{m/2}^{\prime})].

By the second part of
Lemma

3.5
, for each
i=1,\dots,m
, we have
Q[X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}]\leq\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}]\leq p
, so
Theorem

3.2
yields

\mathbb{P}[(X_{1}+\dots+X_{m/2})=(X_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+X_{m/2}^{\prime})]\lesssim\frac{p}{\sqrt{(1-p)(m/2)}}\lesssim\frac{p}{\sqrt{(1-p)m}}.

By exactly the same argument, we obtain

\mathbb{P}[(X_{m/2+1}+\dots+X_{m})=(X_{m/2+1}^{\prime}+\dots+X_{m}^{\prime})]\lesssim\frac{p}{\sqrt{(1-p)m}},

and the desired result follows.
∎

Finally, we use
Corollary

3.3
to prove
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
h=\lfloor n/k\rfloor
and let
I_{1},\dots,I_{h}\in\{1,\dots,n\}

be uniformly random disjoint subsets of exactly
k
indices (independent from all other random objects in the statement of
Theorem

3.1
). For
each
j=1,\dots,h
, define

X_{j}^{*}=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}),\quad\rho_{j}=\mathbb{P}[X_{j}^{*}=0\,|\,I_{j}]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{j}}X_{i}=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}X_{i}^{\prime}\,\Big|\,I_{j}\Big],

and let
\lambda=1-\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]>4k/n>2/h
. Note that for each
j=1,\dots,h
, the random variable
X_{j}^{*}
on its own has exactly the same distribution as
X_{K}^{*}
. Thus, we have
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{1}+\dots+\rho_{h}]=h\cdot\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]=(1-\lambda)h
. So, we can fix an outcome of
I_{1},\dots,I_{h}
satisfying
\rho_{1}+\dots+\rho_{h}\leq(1-\lambda)h
.

Now, we can have at most
(1-\lambda/2)h
indices
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
with
\rho_{j}\geq(1-\lambda)/(1-\lambda/2)
. Thus, there are at least
(\lambda/2)h
indices
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
satisfying
\rho_{j}<(1-\lambda)/(1-\lambda/2)
.
Then,
3.4
and
Corollary

3.3
(applied with
m=\lceil(\lambda/2)h\rceil\geq 2
and
p=(1-\lambda)/(1-\lambda/2)<1-\lambda/2
, with the random variables
\sum_{i\in I_{j}}X_{i}
and their independent copies
\sum_{i\in I_{j}}X_{i}^{\prime}
for
j=1,\dots,h
) give

Q[X]\leq Q\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{1}}X_{i}+\dots+\sum_{i\in I_{h}}X_{i}\Big]\lesssim\frac{(1-\lambda)/(1-\lambda/2)}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)\cdot(\lambda/2)h}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\cdot\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}{1-\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}.\qed

Note that
Theorem

3.1
only applies under the assumption that
\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]
is sufficiently far away from 1. We record a corollary with an additional condition that allows us to easily verify this assumption.

Corollary 3.6
.

Consider
\gamma>0
and
0<p<1
, and in the setting of
Theorem

3.1
, assume that
2/\gamma\leq k<(1-p)n/8
and that we have
Q[X_{i}]\leq p
for at least
\gamma n
indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then
\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{K}=0]\leq(1+p)/2<1-4k/n
and consequently

Q[X]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}{1-\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0]}\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\mathbb{P}[X_{K}^{*}=0].

Proof.

Let
I
be a set of
\lceil\gamma n\rceil
indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
satisfying
Q[X_{i}]\leq p
. Then
|K\cap I|
has a hypergeometric distribution, with mean
\mathbb{E}[|K\cap I|]=|I|\cdot k/n\geq\gamma k
and variance
\operatorname{Var}[|K\cap I|]\leq|I|\cdot k/n=\mathbb{E}[|K\cap I|]
. So by Chebyshev’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{P}[K\cap I=\emptyset]\leq 1/\mathbb{E}[|K\cap I|]\leq 1/(\gamma k)\leq 1/2
. Thus, we have
K\cap I\neq\emptyset
with probability at least
1/2
. For any such outcome of
K
we have
\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{K}=0\,|\,K]\leq p
by
3.4
. So, we can conclude that
\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{K}=0]\leq(1+p)/2
. Using
k<(1-p)n/8
, it is easy to check that
(1+p)/2<1-4k/n
, and the rest of the statement is obtained by applying
Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Fact 4.2

We collect various estimates on probabilities

Conditioning on the given outcome of

For any

For

depends on the randomness of
\xi_{i}
, and at most one of the possible outcomes of
\xi_{i}
will cause
\operatorname{per}(A[-(I^{*}-i)])=z
(here we are using that
\operatorname{per}(A[-I^{*}])\neq 0
as
I^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
). We need
\operatorname{per}(A[-(I^{*}-i)])=z
for all
i\in J^{*}
for
I^{*}
not to be parental, and we recall our assumption
Q[\xi_{i}]\leq p
.

Combining
Equation

4.1
and
Equation

4.2
yields

(4.3)

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\leq 2p^{s-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])=z\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\Big]

for any outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)
.

Note that this in particular implies
\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\,|\,A^{\uparrow s}]\leq 2p^{s-1}
. If
s\geq n/2
, then averaging this over the possible outcomes of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)
already yields the bound

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]\leq 2p^{s-1}\leq 2p^{n/2-1}=\exp(-\Omega_{p}(n)).

Therefore for the rest of the proof we may assume
s<n/2
.

Now, note that if we reveal
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})
,
then in the resulting conditional probability space,
\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])

is a linear function of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n+T}
(by
4.2
). For every
i\in\{1,\dots,n+T\}\setminus J^{*}
, the coefficient of
\xi_{i}
in this linear function is precisely
\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])
.
We will apply our relative anticoncentration result in
Corollary

3.6
to this linear function (or, more precisely, to the linear function obtained by omitting the last
T
terms).

Step 2: Applying the relative anticoncentration inequality.

Let
k=\lfloor\nu(n-s+1)\rfloor
, and let
A^{*}
be a random matrix obtained from
A[-J^{*}]
as follows. First, for every entry
\xi_{i}
in the
(n+T-s+1)
-th row of
A[-J^{*}]
, replace this entry with
\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\prime}
, where
\xi_{i}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
\xi_{i}
. Then, choose a uniformly random subset
K\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}
of exactly
k
of the first
n-s+1
column indices of
A[-J^{*}]
, and set all the entries in the
(n-s+1)
-th row to zero except those indexed by
K
.

Claim 4.7
.

For any fixed outcomes of
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])=z\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Big]\leq C_{p}\cdot\sqrt{\nu}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Big].

Proof.

As in the claim statement, let us fix outcomes of
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
, and consider the conditional probability space obtained by conditioning on these outcomes.
For a random variable
Y
, we write
Q[Y\,|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})]=\sup_{y}\mathbb{P}[Y=y\,|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})]
.

As
(I^{*},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
, there are at least
(\alpha/2)(n-s+1)
different sets
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
with
(I^{\prime},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
. For all of these sets
I^{\prime}
we have
J^{*}\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{per}(A[-I^{\prime}])\neq 0
. In other words, there are at least
(\alpha/2)(n-s+1)
indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}
such that
\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])\neq 0
.

Now, let us define
X_{i}=\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])\cdot\xi_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n+T\}\setminus J^{*}
. Here, the coefficient
\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+j)])
is already determined by
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})
, so after revealing
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})
, the random variable
X_{i}
is a rescaling of
\xi_{i}
. Thus, the random variables
X_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n+T\}\setminus J^{*}
are independent in the conditional conditional probability space obtained by revealing
A^{\uparrow s}
and
(I^{*},J^{*})
. Furthermore, by
4.2
we have
\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])=\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,n+T\}\setminus J^{*}}X_{i}
. We can now apply
Corollary

3.6
with
\gamma=\alpha/2
and the random variables
X_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}
. At least
(\alpha/2)(n-s+1)
of these random variables satisfy
Q[X_{i}\,|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})]\leq p
, since this holds for all indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}
such that
\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])\neq 0
(also noting that
k=\lfloor\nu(n-s+1)\rfloor\geq\lfloor\nu n/2\rfloor\geq 4/\alpha
if
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
p
,
\alpha
and
\nu
). This yields

\displaystyle Q\Big[\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Big]

\displaystyle=Q\Bigg[\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,n+T\}\setminus J^{*}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!X_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq Q\Bigg[\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!X_{i}\,\Bigg|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n-s+1}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},(I^{*},J^{*})\Big].

Here, for the first inequality we used
3.4
, and for the second inequality we used
Corollary

3.6
, noting that
\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=\sum_{i\in K}\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])(\xi_{i}-\xi^{\prime}_{i})=\sum_{i\in K}(X_{i}-X^{\prime}_{i})
if we define
X^{\prime}_{i}=\operatorname{per}(A[-(J^{*}+i)])\cdot\xi^{\prime}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus J^{*}
(then
X^{\prime}_{i}
is an independent copy of
X_{i}
in this conditional probability space). Recalling
k\leq\nu(n-s+1)
, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

Averaging the inequality in
4.7
over all possible outcomes of
(I^{*},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
yields

(4.4)

\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])=z\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},\,\mathcal{E_{H}}\Big]\leq C_{p}\cdot\sqrt{\nu}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},\,\mathcal{E_{H}}\Big].

Now, for every fixed outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)
, recall that
|\mathcal{H}|\geq(\alpha/2)s\binom{n}{s}
, and that
(I^{*},J^{*})
is independent from
A^{\uparrow s}
. This implies

\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{E_{H}}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[(I^{*},J^{*})\in\mathcal{H}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]=\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{s\binom{n}{s}}\geq\alpha/2.

Therefore we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\geq\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\text{ and }\mathcal{E_{H}}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A[-J^{*}])=z\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},\,\mathcal{E_{H}}\Big].

Combining this with
Equation

4.3
and (
4.4
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{p}\sqrt{\nu}p^{s-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s},\,\mathcal{E_{H}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{p}\sqrt{\nu}}{\alpha}p^{s-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\leq\alpha p^{s}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]

for every outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)
, where for the last inequality we used
\sqrt{\nu}\leq(\alpha^{2}/4)\cdot(p/C_{p})
.

Summing over the possible outcomes of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)
,
we deduce that

(4.5)

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]\leq\alpha p^{s}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]\leq\alpha p^{s}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\Big].

Step 3: Recursion.

Now, note that the permanent is unaffected by rearranging rows. Let
A^{\prime}
be obtained from
A^{*}
by moving row
(n+T-s+1)
(the row we modified to get
A^{*}
from
A[-J^{*}]
) directly after row
T
, to become the new
(T+1)
-th row. Note that the last
T
entries of the
(n+T-s+1)
-th row of
A^{*}
are always zero, hence the last
T
entries of the
(T+1)
-th row of
A^{\prime}
are always zero (and also note that the
T\times T
submatrix of
A^{\prime}
in the first
T
rows and last
T
columns has a non-zero permanent, since this is precisely the submatrix in the last
T
columns of
A_{\mathrm{fix}}
).

Suppose we reveal an outcome of
J^{*}

and an outcome of the
(T+1)
-th row of
A^{\prime}
which is not
all zero. Then, the first
T+1
rows of
A^{\prime}
contain a
(T+1)\times(T+1)

submatrix with nonzero permanent (in the last
T
columns, together
with any column for which there is a nonzero entry in the
(T+1)
-th
row). So, conditional on revealed information,
A^{\prime}
has the form in
Definition

2.1
, with
n-s
random rows (and
T+1
fixed rows), so the conditional probability that
\operatorname{per}(A^{\prime})=0
is at most
f_{p}(n-s)
. This shows

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}\operatorname{per}(A^{\prime})=0\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}\text{the }(T+1)\text{-th row of }A^{\prime}\text{ is all-zero}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}+f_{p}(n-s).

Now, note that the
(T+1)
-th row of
A^{\prime}
is all-zero if and only if all entries of the
(n+T-s+1)
-th row of
A^{*}
are zero. The probability of this is at most
p^{k}\leq p^{\lfloor\nu n/2\rfloor}=\exp(-\Omega_{p,\alpha}(n))
(since
Q[\xi_{i}-\xi^{\prime}_{i}]\leq Q[\xi_{i}]\leq p
for every
i\in K
).
Thus, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big[}\operatorname{per}(A^{*})=0\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big]}\leq f_{p}(n-s)+\exp(-\Omega_{p,\alpha}(n)),

and together with (
4.5
) we can conclude

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(s-1,\alpha/(4s))}\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]\leq\alpha p^{s}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big(}f_{p}(n-s)+\exp(-\Omega_{\alpha,p}(n))\mathopen{}\mathclose{\big)}.\qed

Now, we deduce
Theorem

2.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

Recalling that the event
\mathcal{E}(n)
holds deterministically, we can observe that

(4.6)

\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(n)\Big]\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(t)\Big]+\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap\mathcal{E}(s)\Big].

We can further break down the above terms, as follows. Let
\alpha=\alpha_{t}=\varepsilon/3
, and for all
1\leq s\leq t
let
\alpha_{s-1}=\alpha_{s}/(4s)
. Then for
s=t+1,\dots,n
, we have

(4.7)

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap\mathcal{E}(s)\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap(\mathcal{E}(s)\setminus\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha))\Big]+\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big],\\

and furthermore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(t)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap(\mathcal{E}(t)\backslash\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha))\Big]+\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha)\Big]

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap(\mathcal{E}(t)\backslash\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha))\Big]\!+\!\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(1,\alpha_{1})\Big]\!+\!\sum_{s=2}^{t}\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s\!-\!1,\alpha_{s\!-\!1})}\cap\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha_{s})\Big]

holds. We can directly substitute
Lemma

4.5
into some of the
terms in
Equation

4.7
and
Equation

4.8
. To bound the other terms, let us write
\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{F}\,|\,A^{\uparrow s}]

and
\inf_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{F}\,|\,A^{\uparrow s}]
to
indicate a supremum or infimum over all outcomes of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying an event
\mathcal{E}
. Then, by
Lemmas

4.3
and
4.4
, for
s=t+1,\dots,n
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap(\mathcal{E}(s)\setminus\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha))\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)}\Big]\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}(s)\setminus\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)\Big]

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s,\alpha)}\Big]\cdot\sup_{\mathcal{E}(s)}\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\leq(1+2\alpha)f_{p}(n-s)\cdot p^{s},

as well as (recalling the definition of
\tau_{p}
in
Equation

2.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap(\mathcal{E}(t)\backslash\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha))\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha)}\Big]\cdot\Bigg(1-\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}(t)\setminus\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha)\Big]\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(t,\alpha)}\Big]\cdot\Bigg(1-\inf_{\mathcal{E}(1)}\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\cdot\prod_{s=2}^{t}\inf_{\mathcal{E}(s)}\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{E}(s-1)\,\Big|\,A^{\uparrow s}\Big]\Bigg)

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\alpha)f_{p}(n-t)\cdot\Bigg(1-\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-p^{s})\Bigg)\leq(1+2\alpha)\tau_{p}\cdot f_{p}(n-t).

Substituting
Lemma

4.5
and
Equation

4.9
into
Equation

4.7
, for
s=t+1,\dots,n
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}(s-1)}\cap\mathcal{E}(s)\Big]\leq(1+3\alpha)p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)+\exp(-\Omega_{\varepsilon,p}(n)),

and substituting
Lemma

4.5
and
Equation

4.10
into
Equation

4.8
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big[\overline{\mathcal{E}_{z}(0)}\cap\mathcal{E}(t)\Big]\leq(1+2\alpha)\tau_{p}\cdot f_{p}(n-t)+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\alpha p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)+\exp(-\Omega_{\varepsilon,p,t}(n)).

Substituting the above two estimates into
Equation

4.6
yields

\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]\leq(1+2\alpha)\tau_{p}\cdot f_{p}(n-t)+\alpha\sum_{s=1}^{t}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)+(1+3\alpha)\sum_{s=t+1}^{n}p^{s}f_{p}(n-s)+\exp(-\Omega_{\varepsilon,p,t}(n)),

as desired (recalling
\alpha=\varepsilon/3
).
∎
