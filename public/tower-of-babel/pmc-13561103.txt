Artificial Intelligence Is a Useful Tool in Exercise Science and Sports Medicine
Authors: BRADLEY C. NINDL, KARL E. FRIEDL
Published: 2026

The initial concept of using machine technology to perform learning and analytical functions to augment human functions came out of a 1956 summer workshop at Dartmouth; now, artificial intelligence (AI) applications are indispensable across industry and society. AI is increasingly recognized as a decision-support technology in exercise science and sports medicine, rather than a replacement for expert judgment. AI has become sufficiently influential that Professor Karim Lakhani at Harvard Business School has asserted that “AI won’t replace humans—but humans with AI will replace humans without AI” (1).

Not replacing human thinking but enhancing human performance, AI can rapidly perform a large number of computations in the analyses of massive amounts of information, with important applications such as the identification of complex patterns that exceed unaided human capabilities (2). Powerful machine-learning algorithms such as logistic regression, random forests, and support vector machines are already widely used “shallow” learning algorithms to analyze moderately sized datasets to predict injury risk or for workload modeling. More advanced forms of deep learning such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) learn features automatically and excel with high-dimensional data such as images and video in large datasets. These deep learning algorithms are useful for a wide range of applied sports applications such as wearable sensor signal interpretation (e.g., accelerometry), technique assessment (e.g., swim stroke analyses), player tracking in team sports, and jump landing analysis. Reinforcement learning (e.g., Q-learning models) learns through trial-and-error interaction to optimize long-term outcomes, with human supervision. These are useful for tactical decision optimization using simulations such as digital twin models, perhaps suggesting training load adjustments. They can also support postsurgical rehabilitation planning (3).

The value of AI to the mission of the American College of Sports Medicine to educate and empower professionals to advance the science and practice of health and human performance is substantial, and herein, we provide some of these examples.

AI in Clinical Sports Medicine: Diagnosis, Prevention, and Rehab

In sports medicine, deep learning algorithms augment a provider’s diagnostic ability in the interpretation of multimodal imaging technologies. CNNs are particularly useful in edge detection and other aspects of medical imaging analysis, assisting radiologists by highlighting abnormalities, assigning probability scores, and suggesting diagnoses. CNNs trained on large repositories of musculoskeletal imaging are being used to identify soft tissue injuries, joint pathology, or stress fractures, often approaching or, in selected tasks, matching human diagnostic performance (4–6).

AI algorithms can draw on well-curated patient databases to provide rehabilitation recommendations, integrating multimodal data streams that exceed clinical synthesis (6). Reinforcement learning algorithms can also be trained on experimental datasets that include combinations of exercise intensity, frequency, and progression rates to provide optimal recovery protocols. For example, a database built from longitudinal assessment of various knee pathologies (KneE-PAD) offers a posture and movement assessment tool for knee rehabilitation based on surface electromyogram and inertial measurement unit wearables (7). Most of these systems are still experimental and will require intensive ethical validation but show great promise. These systems will, in concept, personalize rehabilitation programs by developing precision diagnostics and recommendations for return to play based on integration of a large number of variables from the individual patient, including their exercise results, pain reports, and performance monitors and markers (8,9). Even larger data frameworks that start with a biological basis have been proposed, such as biomechanically informed neural network and the Adaptive Sports Medicine Strategy to expand the tools available to clinicians, coaches, and rehabilitation experts (6).

AI also enables predictive injury modeling through the analysis of sequential data. RNNs can model time-series data (e.g., gait asymmetry, ground contact time variability, and subtle declines in power output) to forecast overuse injury risk (10). Such tools can help clinicians develop strategies to mitigate injury risk. The development of wearable sensor-based databases for the prediction of physical performance and injury is a current high priority in military recruit training for several countries including Switzerland, Finland, Israel, and the United States (11). The US Marine Corps has embraced AI technologies to augment Marines to do jobs faster and better, providing a competitive advantage in modern warfare (12). Development of physiological metrics and datasets will form the basis of AI-assisted personalized strength and conditioning training programs that enhance capability and reduce musculoskeletal injuries in Marine training (13). The US Army’s Load Carriage Decision Aid algorithm is another example of the physiological metrics and models needed to support AI-based physical training, in this case, providing a predictive model to plan periodized training and prevent overuse injuries in a wider range of conditions than previously available (14). Military training environments provide unusually large, standardized, and longitudinal datasets that are well-suited for AI-assisted injury and performance modeling.

AI and the Performance Edge: Optimization at Scale

Predicting performance has been an age-old goal of scientists and coaches. Deep learning models such as CNNs and RNNs can be used to predict marathon performance with increasing accuracy based on analysis of longitudinal training logs, biomechanical and physiological signals, and environmental factors (15). With advances in human activity recognition, the more challenging complexities of monitoring and predicting team performance are also being rapidly overcome (16,17). Catapult Vector 8 (Boston, MA), STATSports (Newry, N. Ireland, UK), and Firstbeat Sports (Jyvaskyla, Finland) are example machine-learning systems that integrate complex data (e.g., from inertial sensors, GPS, heart rate, and position and movement data) to identify performance and injury risk factors tailored to specific sports. While independent peer-reviewed validation of proprietary “AI” claims remains limited, multiple vendors in the sports performance domain explicitly describe the inclusion of machine learning or AI components in their analytics pipelines. Vendor documentation and industry reports indicate that machine learning is used to support pattern detection, movement classification, and other data-driven insights in systems such as Catapult and STATSports. This usage reflects the broader trend in sports analytics research, where AI/ML methods are increasingly applied to extract performance metrics and predict outcomes from high-dimensional sensor and video data.

Published studies validate selected sensor-derived metrics from commercially available systems, whereas proprietary analytic pipelines remain largely opaque to independent evaluation (18–20). This validation challenge is compounded by the speed at which industry develops new systems and software upgrades, which far outpaces traditional study designs. One conceptual inspiration might be the Food and Drug Administration’s approach to anticipate biotechnology advances that they will have to evaluate for safety and efficacy. They do this, in part, by maintaining in-house expertise and research in the evolving fields. There is a similar need in the sports community for independent research teams on the cusp of rapidly evolving AI technologies to provide objective guidance on new developments to the community of users.

Commercial athlete-monitoring platforms increasingly incorporate machine-learning analytics, yet their scientific value derives less from proprietary algorithms than from the structured aggregation of large-scale physiological datasets. Independent validation of vendor-specific AI claims remains limited; however, military cohort studies using survival-based machine-learning models illustrate how similar analytical approaches can stratify injury risk while maintaining transparency and clinical interpretability (21). Collectively, these developments suggest that AI’s principal role in exercise science is not automated decision-making, but the amplification of expert pattern recognition within complex, multivariate physiological systems. By leveraging AI-based training models, coaches can move beyond static periodization to dynamic training prescription. For example, individualized models may detect subtle signs of overtraining based on declining heart rate variability, poor sleep quality, changes in gait and movement, depressed mood state, or decreased power output and exercise tolerance (22). Early detection of maladaptation would permit earlier intervention before an athlete experiences significant performance decrements. AI has been successfully applied in tennis performance analyses, including talent identification, psychological state monitoring, and match outcome prediction (23). This type of causal inference is an important higher level validation beyond performance correlations.

A novel use of AI is in the codification of expert coaching knowledge. By crowd-sourcing decisions from elite practitioners and feeding these into supervised learning models, coaching heuristics, which are often treated as intangible, can be translated into data-informed strategies, thus democratizing access to high-quality training insights. Data for these models can be derived using standardized virtual human interactions, taking advantage of other rapidly developing technologies in human simulation and affective computing (24).

Wearables and the Digital Twin: The Future of Personalized Monitoring

The explosion of wearable technology has created opportunities for real-time, continuous monitoring of physical activity, physiological stress, recovery, and sleep. Sensors embedded in smartwatches, shoes, and textiles now collect data that can be used for physiological status tracking and useful predictions (11). AI enables these systems to adapt to individual baselines and make context-sensitive recommendations. A study of over 8000 participants with over 1.3 million hours of noisy wearable sensor data revealed response patterns that signaled COVID-19 infection at least 3 days sooner than a positive test (25). Similar studies with IMU sensors and markerless motion capture evaluating changes in gait might forecast injury, fatigue, or overtraining (26). The massive amounts of data made available through wearable sensors require deep learning AI tools to analyze and interpret, with the potential to inform ever-improving health and performance predictions (11).

A particularly promising application of AI is the “digital twin,” a virtual replica of a person’s physiological profile that evolves with their activity and training, age, health events, and fitness status. This started in engineering, with digital models of the vital processes of a nuclear power plant, which could then be rapidly and safely tested for predicted outcomes based on many different emergencies or interventions. In youth sports, such twins could be constructed using movement data, biometric trends, and injury history. Over time, this model could simulate various what-if scenarios, projecting how changes in training load, sleep, or diet might impact performance or injury risk. As individuals age, digital twins could support clinical decision-making by simulating optimal rehabilitation paths after joint replacement or injury, recommending evidence-based protocols adjusted for comorbidities, medication history, and healing capacity (27). Sel et al. (28) have proposed a mechanistic physiological model of a digital twin that continuously forecasts optimal management of cardiovascular health using personalized data systems. This could provide individualized healthcare drawn from a lifetime of the model learning its human twin while using data analytics to contextualize individual patients. Of course, ethical issues will have to be addressed, including obtaining data on minors, long-term data retention, and other privacy security issues. While digital twins remain an emerging framework, their promise lies in combining data-drive inference with physiological constraints to support individual hypothesis testing (27). EDITH is an even more ambitious framework of the human phenome that is supported by the European Commission, using high-speed computing and AI tools for a fully integrated multiscale and multiorgan whole-body twin (29). Nevertheless, these powerful new tools are models and not oracles.

Digital twin frameworks offer an opportunity to present physiological systems that are otherwise intractable to model using first-principles methods alone. Many biological processes—such as cardiovascular regulation, tissue perfusion—are governed by multiscale dynamics with hidden or unmeasurable variables, required for personalization. The complexity of these interactions makes traditional mechanistic modeling insufficient for personalization. Scientific machine learning, including physics-informed neural networks, provides a powerful proxy by starting with known physiological rules and principles that are embedded into data-driven models. Automation in Research and Education

Another important benefit of AI is the automation of labor-intensive tasks in exercise science and sports medicine research. For example, deep learning models can analyze video recordings of movement tasks, identifying joint angles, velocities, and compensatory patterns without the need for manual annotation (30). This accelerates data processing and reduces human error, allowing researchers to focus on hypothesis testing and theory development. Sports medicine is data-rich but interpretation-heavy, which makes generative AI a valuable tool, contextualizing or synthesizing information on top of predictive AI estimates.

In educational settings, AI-driven platforms can simulate complex physiological interactions such as the effects of temperature or hydration status on endurance performance (31). These simulations would enhance learning by allowing students to interactively explore systems physiology, adapting their input to explore many different conditions and outcomes (32). Any of the new and emerging AI-based large language models (LLMs) (e.g., Galactica, PaperQA, DeepReview, and general purpose LLMs such as GPT-5 and Claude 3) are becoming essential tools to help identify new research hypotheses based on rapid analysis of large volumes of scientific literature and extraction of patterns, contradictions, and gaps in current knowledge; however, these powerful tools still require careful human verification and augment but do not replace human judgment.

Limitations and Ethical Considerations

Several limitations must be addressed before AI can be fully and ethically integrated into practice. Medical-grade data collected by wearables, imaging systems, or digital health platforms can reveal private health information that is not related to the intended purpose of performance monitoring. For example, analyses of movement patterns can now effectively identify disease states (33). Interpretability is equally critical. Many deep learning models function as “black boxes” producing results that are difficult to explain, or they may be simply wrong for the unaware user; for example, large language models tradeoff between generalization and precision and can make up nonexistent facts that only sound correct. Efforts to develop explainable AI (XAI) are crucial to ensure that outputs can be contextualized and understood by practitioners. There is a risk of over-reliance on AI at the expense of human judgment. While algorithms offer probabilistic insights, they may miss context-specific subtleties best identified by experienced clinicians or coaches. This requires a balanced, hybrid approach, where AI supports but does not replace expert decision-making.

CONCLUSIONS

Artificial intelligence is no longer a future technology in sports medicine and exercise science; it is already shaping the present, whether optimizing elite performance, predicting injury risk, or guiding personalized rehabilitation. AI tools offer unprecedented capabilities that align closely with the field’s longstanding goals of individualization. The vision of digital twins and lifelong fitness companions is within reach, if we ensure these tools are used to augment, not replace, the expertise and empathy of human professionals. For American College of Sports Medicine members, the immediate challenge is not whether AI will be adopted, but how to ensure its responsible validation, interpretation, and integration into practice.
