On the Suboptimality of Linear Codes for Binary Distributed Hypothesis Testing
Adway Girish, Robinson D. H. Cung, Emre Telatar

Abstract
We study a binary distributed hypothesis testing problem where two agents observe correlated binary vectors and communicate compressed information at the same rate to a central decision maker. In particular, we study linear compression schemes and show that simple truncation is the best linear scheme in two cases: (1) testing opposite signs of the same magnitude of correlation, and (2) testing for or against independence. We conjecture, supported by numerical evidence, that truncation is the best linear code for testing any correlations of opposite signs. Further, for testing against independence, we also compute classical random coding exponents and show that truncation, and consequently any linear code, is strictly suboptimal.

Preliminaries and Problem Setup

To fix ideas, we first describe the general hypothesis testing framework in a one-shot fashion, i.e., with only one sample. Naturally, this subsumes our setting with
n
i.i.d. samples as the single general sample is allowed to be a vector drawn from a product distribution.

2.1
General one-shot setup

Under hypothesis
\mathcal{H}=i
, we have
(X,Y)\sim\mathsf{P}^{i}_{XY}
. We may drop the subscripts when the random variables are clear from context.

We first consider a co-located version of the problem:
suppose the decision maker
\mathsf{C}
observes both
X
and
Y
. The goal of
\mathsf{C}
is to obtain an estimate
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
of
\mathcal{H}
from
(X,Y)
, i.e., a
decision rule

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(X,Y)
. Associated with each decision rule
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
are two kinds of errors, called the type-I and type-II errors, with probabilities respectively given by

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle=\Pr\{\hat{\mathcal{H}}=1\mid\mathcal{H}=0\}=\mathsf{P}^{0}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}(X,Y)=1),

\displaystyle e_{2}

\displaystyle=\Pr\{\hat{\mathcal{H}}=0\mid\mathcal{H}=1\}=\mathsf{P}^{1}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}(X,Y)=0).

Let
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{XY},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{XY})\subset[0,1]^{2}
denote the set of all pairs
(e_{1},e_{2})
achieved by some decision rule
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
. An
optimal
decision rule is one that lies on the lower-left boundary of this region. As is well-known, these optimal decision rules are the randomized Neyman–Pearson likelihood ratio tests—pick a threshold
t\geq 0
, and decide
\hat{\mathcal{H}}=0
if
\mathsf{P}^{0}(X,Y)/\mathsf{P}^{1}(X,Y)>t
,
1
if
\mathsf{P}^{0}(X,Y)/\mathsf{P}^{1}(X,Y)<t
, and randomly in case of equality. Sweeping
t
from
0
to
\infty
gives the lower-left boundary of
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{XY},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{XY})
.
Hence, the likelihood ratio is a sufficient statistic, i.e., no decision rule computed using
(X,Y)
can have both type-I and type-II error probabilities lower than the optimal decision rule computed using
\mathsf{P}^{0}(X,Y)/\mathsf{P}^{1}(X,Y)
.

More generally, let
U=u(X,Y)
and
V=v(X,Y)
be two statistics computed from
(X,Y)
using possibly randomized functions
u
and
v
respectively (in all randomized functions we discuss, the randomness cannot depend directly on the hypothesis). We say that
U
is
better
than
V
for testing
(X,Y)\sim\mathsf{P}^{0}
versus
\mathsf{P}^{1}
, denoted by
U\succeq_{\text{B}}V
, if the optimal decision rule using
U
has uniformly better type-I and type-II error probabilities than that using
V
. Clearly, this happens if and only if

\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{U},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{U})\supset\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{V},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{V})
.
We trivially have
(X,Y)\succeq_{\text{B}}u(X,Y)
for any
u
.
As the likelihood ratio is a sufficient statistic, we also have
\mathsf{P}^{0}(X,Y)/\mathsf{P}^{1}(X,Y)\succeq_{\text{B}}(X,Y)
.
The following classical result due to Blackwell
[
3
]
,
[
20
, Ex. III.1]
characterizes exactly when this partial order relation is satisfied.

Fact 1

(Blackwell order)
.

Let
T
have distribution
\mathsf{P}^{i}
under hypothesis
\mathcal{H}=i
, and let
U=u(T)
and
V=v(T)
be two statistics computed from
T
using possibly random functions
u
and
v
. Then, we have
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{U},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{U})\supset\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{V},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{V})
if and only if there exists a conditional distribution
\mathsf{P}_{V|U}
such that for all
v
,

\mathsf{P}^{i}_{V}(v)=\sum_{u}\mathsf{P}_{V|U}(v\mid u)\mathsf{P}^{i}_{U}(u)

for
i=0,1
, i.e., inclusion of
\mathcal{R}
is equivalent to channel degradation.

Note that the same conditional distribution
\mathsf{P}_{V|U}
must generate
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{V}
from
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{U}
for both
i=0
and 1. Hence, to show that a statistic
U
is better than
V
, it suffices to construct a channel from
U
to
V
that maps
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{U}
to
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{V}
for both
i=0,1
.

A distributed version of this problem is as follows: the decision maker
\mathsf{C}
no longer observes
X
and
Y
directly. Instead, agents
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
respectively observe
X
and
Y
, and then compute and send
g(X)
and
h(Y)
to
\mathsf{C}
, where
g
and
h
are possibly random functions, and together referred to as a code for
(X,Y)
.
The set of all possible type-I and type-II error probabilities of this distributed hypothesis test is given by the region
\mathcal{R}\big(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{g(X),h(Y)},\mathsf{P}^{1}_{g(X),h(Y)}\big)
. For a given code
(g,h)
, the optimal decision rules are given by the likelihood ratio test as earlier, but using the induced distributions
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{g(X),h(Y)}
.
The code
(g,h)
has uniformly better type-I and type-II error probabilities than
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
if and only if
(g(X),h(Y))\succeq_{\text{B}}(g^{\prime}(X),h^{\prime}(Y))
.

2.2
n
i.i.d. samples of correlated binary data

As described in Section
1
, we now suppose that the agents observe

i.i.d. samples drawn from a doubly symmetric binary source whose correlation is determined by the hypothesis.
We write

to mean

and

are
\Bern(\frac{1}{2})
and

. The correlation between

and

\DSBS(p)

\rho_{i}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\sqrt{\var(X)\var(Y)}}=1-2p_{i},

and hence, we have positive correlation if
p_{i}<\frac{1}{2}
, negative correlation if
p_{i}>\frac{1}{2}
and independence otherwise. Also note that
\rho_{1}=-\rho_{0}
if and only if
p_{1}=1-p_{0}
.
Hence, under hypothesis
\mathcal{H}=i
, we have that
(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})
,

is independently drawn according to
\DSBS(p_{i})
, i.e.,
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{X^{n}Y^{n}}=\DSBS(p_{i})^{\otimes n}
.
In the co-located version where
\mathsf{C}
observes both
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
, the modulo 2 sum
X^{n}\oplus Y^{n}\coloneqq(X_{\ell}\oplus Y_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{n}

(X^{n},Y^{n})
(in fact, the Hamming weight of

is sufficient).

In the distributed setup,
\mathsf{C}
observes only the coded observations
g(X^{n})
and
h(Y^{n})
, for some functions
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{k_{X}}
and
h:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{k_{Y}}
, with
k_{X},k_{Y}\leq n
. Let
|g|\coloneqq|\{g(x^{n}):x^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}|
denote the number of possible outputs of the function
g
, then we define the
rate
of
g
as the number of output bits per input bit, i.e.,
R_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}|g|
, which is at most
k_{X}/n
. Similarly, we define
R_{Y}=\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}|h|
, which is at most
k_{Y}/n
.

In particular, we study
linear codes
, where
g
and
h
be linear functions, i.e.,
G
and
H
are matrices of dimensions
k_{X}\times n
and
k_{Y}\times n
respectively, and
g(X^{n})=GX^{n}
and
h(Y^{n})=HY^{n}
, with matrix multiplication modulo 2, and
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
taken as column vectors. Without loss of generality, we may assume that the matrices are full-row-rank, as we can always convert a rank-deficient
k\times n
matrix
G
with
k\leq n
to a
\rank(G)\times n
full-rank matrix by a one-to-one transformation. Hence, the rates of
g
and
h
are respectively given by
k_{X}/n
and
k_{Y}/n
.

Our motivation for studying linear codes is the following: Since
Z^{n}\eqqcolon X^{n}\oplus Y^{n}
is a sufficient statistic in the co-located problem, one possible approach is to choose
g
and
h
so that
\mathsf{C}
is able to compute an estimate

that is “close” to

[

showed that for computing the modulo 2 sum of correlated binary vectors in such a distributed fashion, linear codes can be used to obtain the same performance as classical random quantization but at a lower sum-rate. This suggests that the Körner–Marton coding scheme might also be well-suited to this DHT problem, as is indeed shown by Haim and Kochman
[
10
]
when
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
are both smaller than

.

Before proceeding to our technical results, it will be convenient to represent
(X,Y)\sim\DSBS(p_{i})
,

using the following equivalent notation. Assume, without loss of generality (by re-labelling the hypotheses, if necessary) that
p_{1}
is closer to

is (i.e.,
|p_{0}-1/2|\geq|p_{1}-1/2|

Let

,
Z\sim\Bern(p_{0})
and
\tilde{Z}\sim\Bern(\tilde{p})
be independent, with
\tilde{p}
such that
p_{1}=p_{0}*\tilde{p}\coloneqq p_{0}(1-\tilde{p})+\tilde{p}(1-p_{0})
—such a

is guaranteed to exist as

. Then, we clearly have

.

In particular, for

, we have
\tilde{p}=1
, and hence

. Likewise, for
p_{0}\neq p_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
, we also have
\tilde{p}=\frac{1}{2}
.
For the case of
n
i.i.d. samples, we have

Y^{n}=X^{n}\oplus Z^{n}\oplus i\tilde{Z}^{n},

(1)

where
X_{\ell}
are i.i.d.
\Bern(\frac{1}{2})
,
Z_{\ell}
are i.i.d.
\Bern(p_{0})
and
\tilde{Z}_{\ell}
are i.i.d.
\Bern(\tilde{p})
, and these are also independent of each other, with
\tilde{p}
such that
p_{1}=p_{0}*\tilde{p}
. Note that

and

are on opposite sides of

3
Best choices of linear codes

Recall the partial order
\succeq_{\text{B}}
on statistics defined in Section
2
.
We now use the characterization in Fact
1
to obtain order relations involving statistics computed using linear codes. First, we show that it is always better to use the same linear code to compress
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
, and that it is sufficient to base the decision rule on the modulo 2 sum of the compressed messages.
That is, the optimal decision rule based on the statistic
G(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})
has uniformly better type-I and type-II error probabilities than the optimal rule based on the statistic
(GX^{n},HY^{n})
. Similarly, the optimal rule based on
H(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})
also has uniformly better type-I and type-II error probabilities than the optimal rule based on the statistic
(GX^{n},HY^{n})
.

Theorem 1

(Same linear code is (always) better)
.

For any
p_{0},p_{1}\in[0,1]
, consider the hypothesis test
\DSBS(p_{0})
versus
\DSBS(p_{1})
using
n
i.i.d. samples. For any
n
-column matrices
G,H
, we have
G(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})\succeq_{\text{B}}(GX^{n},HY^{n})
and
H(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})\succeq_{\text{B}}(GX^{n},HY^{n})
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by constructing a channel to simulate
(GX^{n},HY^{n})
from
G(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})=G(Z^{n}\oplus i\tilde{Z}^{n})
under
\mathcal{H}=i
. Let
Y^{\prime}_{\ell}
be i.i.d.
\Bern(\frac{1}{2})
, independent of
X^{n},Y^{n}
. Set
U=G(X^{n}\oplus Y^{n})
,
V_{1}=U\oplus GY^{\prime n}
and
V_{2}=HY^{\prime n}
, then
(V_{1},V_{2})
has the same distribution as
(GX^{n},HY^{n})
and we are done by Fact
1
. The other side holds similarly.
∎

It is worth noting that this is not true if non-linear codes are allowed. For example, if we take
n=2
,
g(x^{2})=x_{1}x_{2}
and
h(x^{2})=\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
(these are the logical AND and OR operations on the two bits), then
(g(X^{2}),h(Y^{2}))
and
(g(X^{2}),g(Y^{2}))
cannot be compared by
\succeq_{\text{B}}
.

We say that a code
(g,h)
is the
best
in some class
\mathcal{C}
of codes if
(g(X^{n}),h(Y^{n})\succeq_{\text{B}}(g^{\prime}(X^{n}),h^{\prime}(Y^{n}))
for all
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{C}
. It is possible that a given class
\mathcal{C}
may not have such a greatest element. We show that when
\mathcal{C}
is the class of all linear codes of a given rate, there is indeed a greatest element for some choices of
(p_{0},p_{1})
.

First, note that by Theorem
1
, when looking for the best linear codes, we may assume without loss of generality that
G=H
, i.e., both agents use the same full-row-rank
k\times n
matrix
G
to encode
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
respectively to
GX^{n}
and
GY^{n}
. We now show that for certain values of
(p_{0},p_{1})
, the best linear code at rate

is the most straightforward code possible—simply truncating the observation

to
X^{k}
by picking the matrix
G=[\mathbf{I}_{k}\,\mathbf{0}_{k\times(n-k)}]
(more generally, any one-to-one transformation of any
k
of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
will do).
This is in analogy with the dictator functions maximizing the mutual information of binary vectors
[
6

19
]
,
though there does not appear to be a technical connection (indeed the “multivariate” generalization is known to be false
[
5
]
). The values
(p_{0},p_{1})
for which we are able to show this result are: (1)
p_{0}=1/2
(testing for independence,
\rho_{0}=0
), (2)
p_{1}=1/2
(testing against independence,
\rho_{1}=0
), and (3)
p_{1}=1-p_{0}
(testing positive against negative values of the same magnitude of correlation,

).

(Truncation is (sometimes) the best linear code)

4
Comparison with random quantization

Given that truncation is the best linear code for a set of
(p_{0},p_{1})
pairs, it is now easy to check that a linear code is suboptimal for such
(p_{0},p_{1})
—simply find a random code that does better than truncation and we are done.
It is usually difficult to evaluate the exact type-I and type-II error probabilities of random codes, so we focus on the asymptotic Stein regime, as is common in the DHT literature. That is, we take
n\to\infty
, and consider the best achievable type-II error
exponent

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log e_{2}
while requiring that the type-I error exponent is smaller than some constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We denote this best achievable type-II exponent when both agents operate at rate
R
by
E(R)
. In the co-located case, the Stein exponent is
E(\infty)=D(\mathsf{P}^{0}_{XY}\,\|\,\mathsf{P}^{1}_{XY})
.
In the binary setting that we consider with
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{XY}=\DSBS(p_{i})
, we have
E(\infty)=E(1)=d_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1})
, i.e.,
e_{2}=\exp(-nd_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1})+\mathsf{o}(n))
, where
d_{2}(a\,\|\,b)
denotes the KL divergence between
\Bern(a)
and
\Bern(b)
distributions.

Hence, in the distributed case using truncation to encode
(X^{n},Y^{n})
to
(X^{k},Y^{k})
, the best achievable type-II error is
e_{2}=\exp(-kd_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1})+\mathsf{o}(k))
. The Stein exponent is then simply

E_{\text{tr}}(R)=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\tfrac{1}{n}(-kd_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1})+\mathsf{o}(k))=Rd_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1}),

or equivalently, the exponent-rate trade-off is given by a straight line joining
(0,0)
to
(1,d_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,p_{1}))
as shown in Fig.
3
.

We now consider Han’s random coding scheme
[
13
]
. It requires the agents to pick “test channels”
\mathsf{P}_{U|X}
and
\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}
, which allows them to communicate with the decision maker at rates
I(U;X)
and
I(V;Y)
respectively (see
[
12
,
14
]
for a detailed description of the scheme).
Given these test channels, let
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{UXYV}
denote the induced joint distribution given by
\mathsf{P}^{i}_{XY}\mathsf{P}_{U|X}\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}

The Stein exponent achieved by Han’s random coding scheme is given by

E_{\text{Han}}(\mathsf{P}_{U|X},\mathsf{P}_{V|Y})=\min_{\pi_{UXYV}}D(\pi_{UXYV}\,\|\,\mathsf{P}^{1}_{UXYV})

(2)

with the minimum taken over distributions
\pi_{UXYV}
having the same
UX
,
UY
, and
UV
marginals as
\mathsf{P}^{0}_{UXYV}
.
By the theory of I-projections
[
7
]
, the optimal
\pi^{*}
in the above minimization has the following structure:

\pi^{*}(u,x,y,v)=\mathsf{P}^{1}_{UXYV}(u,x,y,v)f_{1}(u,x)f_{2}(v,y)f_{3}(u,v)

for some functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
. This gives us the Markov relations
X\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}(U,V)\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}Y
and
U\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}(X,Y)\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}V
under
\pi^{*}
. For testing against independence, i.e., when
\mathsf{P}^{1}_{XY}=\mathsf{P}^{0}_{X}\mathsf{P}^{0}_{Y}
, this simplifies to
X\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}U\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}V\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}Y
.

For our DSBS setup, since we consider symmetric rate constraints, we choose
\mathsf{P}_{U|X}=\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}
to both be binary symmetric channels (BSC) of the same parameter, say
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. To operate at rate
R
, we require
I(U;X)=1-h_{2}(\alpha)=R
, where
h_{2}(a)
denotes the entropy of
\Bern(a)
. This is not known to be the optimal choice of test channel, but experiments suggest that this is indeed the case for any
(p_{0},p_{1})
on opposite sides of
1/2
.
In Fig.
3
, we plot
E_{\text{Han}}
for a representative
(p_{0},p_{1})
by numerically solving (
2
) for different choices of
\mathsf{P}_{U|X}
and
\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}
that are randomly picked from a Dirichlet distribution. Our choice
(p_{0},p_{1})=(0.1,0.9)
satisfies
p_{1}=1-p_{0}
, since it is only in this case (and testing for/against independence) where we know that truncation is better than other linear codes, letting us meaningfully compare linear and random codes via truncation, but the plots are qualitatively identical for other
p_{0},p_{1}
on opposite sides of
1/2
as well.

Let
E_{\text{Han}}(R)
denote the Han exponent on taking
\mathsf{P}_{U|X}
and
\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}
to be the same BSC at rate
R
.
We see that
R\mapsto E_{\text{Han}}(R)
is not concave and there is a value of
R
such that
E_{\text{Han}}(R)>E_{\text{tr}}(R)
. This allows us to improve on both
E_{\text{Han}}
and
E_{\text{tr}}
in a trivial manner by appropriately truncating the quantized vectors
(U^{n},V^{n})
, which yields an improved random coding exponent
E_{\text{com}}
given by the upper concave envelope of
E_{\text{Han}}
(Fig.
3
). First generate a random code with
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
so that we are at the point on the
E_{\text{Han}}(R)
curve where the tangent from the origin touches it, and then truncate to obtain all points on the line to the origin.

Figure 3
:
Comparison of
E_{\text{tr}}
and
E_{\text{Han}}
for
(p_{0},p_{1})=(0.1,0.9)
. Choosing
\mathsf{P}_{U|X}
and
\mathsf{P}_{V|Y}
to be the same BSC seems to be the best choice of test channels in Han’s scheme. At sufficiently large rates,
E_{\text{Han}}(R)>E_{\text{tr}}(R)
, and hence we have an improved exponent
E_{\text{com}}(R)
which is strictly better than
E_{\text{tr}}(R)
at all
R
and strictly better than
E_{\text{Han}}(R)
for small
R
.

For testing against independence in particular, i.e.,
p_{1}=1/2
, we can arrive at this conclusion analytically.
Since
\pi^{*}
has the same
UX,UV,VY
marginals
\mathsf{P}^{0}
, we can identify the transition probabilities at each step of the
X\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}U\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}V\mathrel{-\mkern-3.0mu{\circ}\mkern-3.0mu-}Y
Markov chain as
\pi^{*}_{X|U}=\BSC(\alpha)
,
\pi^{*}_{V|U}=\BSC(\alpha*\alpha*p)
, and
\pi^{*}_{Y|V}=\BSC(\alpha)
. We can then explicitly compute Han’s exponent as
E_{\text{Han}}=I(U;V)=1-h_{2}(\alpha*\alpha*p)
at rate
R=I(U;X)=1-h_{2}(\alpha)
. This gives us the exponent-rate trade-off curve parametrized by
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
. Note that
E_{\text{Han}}(1)=E_{\text{tr}}(1)=d_{2}(p_{0}\,\|\,\frac{1}{2})
. It is also easy to see that the derivative
E_{\text{Han}}^{\prime}(1)=0
by checking that

\lim_{\alpha\to\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}1-h_{2}(\alpha*\alpha*p)}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}1-h_{2}(\alpha)}=0,

and hence, at a sufficiently large rate, Han’s quantization scheme achieves a strictly higher Stein exponent than truncation.

It might be of interest to note that for testing against independence with a rate-constraint only on
X^{n}
(i.e.,
R_{X}\leq R
,
R_{Y}\leq\infty
), this quantization is known to be optimal
[
1
]
. The exponent-rate pair is
(I(U;Y),I(U;X))=(1-h_{2}(\alpha*p),1-h_{2}(\alpha))
; this curve is concave and always lies above the truncation exponent given by the straight line (also called the
F_{I}
-curve
[
25
,
4
]
or the information bottleneck function). In our setting with a symmetric rate constraint, the exponent-rate pair is given by a different “bottleneck”
(I(U;V),I(U;X))=(1-h_{2}(\alpha*\alpha*p),1-h_{2}(\alpha))
, which turns out to not be concave.
