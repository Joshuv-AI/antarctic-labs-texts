Study on hydraulic fracturing prevention and control of rock burst in roof of deep extra-thick coal seam roadway group
Authors: Huayong Lv, Zhanbo Cheng, Fei Xie, Junfeng Pan, Fei Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRock bursts in roadway groups of deep mining workfaces are more likely to occur due to concentrated static loads, posing significant threats to mining safety and efficiency. In this study, the coal mine roadway groups in western China are taken as an engineering case to investigate the occurrence mechanism of rock bursts in deep mining workfaces caused by concentrated static loads. A novel prevention and control method based on hydraulic fracturing is proposed, and a sensitivity analysis is conducted on key parameters, including in-situ stress, roof firmness coefficient, flow increment, and borehole spacing, to assess their influence on the hydraulic fracturing effect. The results reveal that the failure energy and peak stress of coal pillars within the roadway group gradually increase due to the continuous pressure exerted by the overlying roof, eventually reaching the conditions necessary to trigger rock bursts. The application of a super-long horizontal staged hydraulic fracturing technology transforms the thick, hard roof into a plastic cushion, which absorbs energy and weakens the stress transfer from the overlying roof. This process effectively reduces the stress concentration in the coal pillars. Furthermore, the hydraulic fracturing effect improves with increasing in-situ stress difference and decreasing borehole spacing, while the effect of flow increment is relatively limited. The study also highlights that higher roof firmness coefficients hinder the effectiveness of hydraulic fracturing, as greater water pressure is required for fracture propagation. Field application of the hydraulic fracturing technique, with parameters including an in-situ stress difference (λ), rock strata firmness coefficient, flow rate of 50 m3/day, borehole spacing of 60 m, and a borehole horizontal level of 40–50 m above the roadway, led to a significant reduction in microseismic events and ground audio frequency, demonstrating a remarkable anti-impact effect on-site. This research provides a theoretical framework and practical insights for the prevention and mitigation of concentrated static load-induced rock bursts in similar mining roadway groups.Keywords: Deep extra-thick coal seam mining, Roadway group, Rock burst, Hydraulic fracturingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Under the influence of both in-situ rock stress and mining-induced stress, the elastic energy accumulated within the coal and rock masses around roadways or working faces can be suddenly released, resulting in a dynamic phenomenon characterized by severe damage, known as a rock burst. Rock bursts are often accompanied by loud sounds and intense vibrations, which can lead to the rapid destruction of roadways or working faces, affecting areas from several meters to hundreds of meters in length. This sudden failure can cause structural support collapse, equipment damage, roadway blockages, and significant casualties. The occurrence of rock bursts is influenced by factors such as coal-rock properties, geological structures, mining layouts, and coal pillar design. Consequently, the prevention and control of rock bursts are critical challenges in ensuring coal mine safety and operational efficiency1–4. Numerous theories have been developed to explain the mechanism of rock bursts, laying a solid foundation for prevention and control efforts. These include strength theory5, stiffness theory6, energy theory7, impact tendency theory8, three-criteria theory9, deformation instability theory10, three-factor theory11, strength weakening and impact reduction theory12, stress control theory13, impact initiation theory14, dynamic and static load superposition induced impact theory15, creep instability theory16, disturbance response instability theory17. These theoretical frameworks have significantly advanced the understanding of rock bursts and provided insights into effective control measures.

China experiences the highest number of rock bursts worldwide, with over 150 rock burst-prone mines across 15 provinces and autonomous regions1. As coal mining activities shift toward deeper and western regions, rock bursts in roadways are becoming increasingly frequent. A significant body of research has focused on the occurrence mechanisms and prevention technologies for rock bursts. For example, Shi et al18. studied delayed coal bursts through monitoring fracturing and deformation processes in deep roadway excavations, enhancing the understanding of delayed rock bursts in deep coal mines. Guo et al19. investigated the stress characteristics and rock burst mechanisms in roadway groups under composite structures, providing valuable reference data for the prevention of rock bursts in mines with similar geological conditions. Zhou et al20. analyzed the static and dynamic stress evolution of deep roadway groups, proposing roof blasting and pre-splitting as effective pressure relief measures to mitigate the risk of rock bursts. Pan et al21. analyzed the mechanism of concentrated static load rock burst in deep roadway group and the influence of bottom coal thickness, fold structure and coal pillar between roadways. The proposed deep hole interval blasting method can effectively prevent and control rock burst disasters. Wang et al22. revealed the evolution mechanism of different types of rockburst, proposed caving mining, deep-hole blasting and large-diameter hole prevention measures and verified their effectiveness, which can provide important theoretical basis for rockburst prevention. Kang et al23. used large-scale hydraulic fracturing technology to fracture the hard roof above the working face, effectively released the concentrated stress and reduced the number of microseismic events, which can be used as an effective measure to prevent coal burst. Aiming at the dynamic disasters such as rock burst, coal and gas outburst caused by bending and breaking of hard roof, Huang et al24. put forward the theory and complete set of technology of hydraulic fracturing control of hard roof, which can solve many problems of surrounding rock control and safety, and achieve remarkable economic benefits. Zheng et al25. revealed the principle of rock burst caused by synergistic fracture of composite hard roof, and put forward the technical mode of preventing and controlling rock burst disaster in advance area of directional long borehole in underground coal mine, which can reasonably and effectively prevent and control rock burst disaster of composite hard roof, and provide technical reference for regional weakening control of roof under similar geological conditions. Sun et al26. proposed a long-hole hydraulic fracturing anti-impact technology, and analyzed its pressure relief effect on medium and high thick and hard roofs, which can provide a guarantee for the safe mining of the working face. Li et al27. established a combined hydraulic fracturing control technology system on the ground and underground, and verified the effectiveness of the technology through industrial tests, which can provide reference for mine strata control and rock burst prevention and control under similar conditions. Wu et al28. established a three-dimensional prevention and control mode of regional and local combination and full coverage of coal seam and rock stratum on the basis of combined hydraulic fracturing on the ground and underground, combined with pressure relief measures such as large diameter borehole drilling and coal seam blasting in coal seam, and achieved good engineering application results. In addition, hydraulic fracturing technology is also widely used in Europe29, Polish scholar Jendrý et al30. validated the effectiveness of directional hydraulic fracturing for preventing rock bursts by analyzing microseismic data before and after fracturing.

While most prior studies have focused on rock bursts during active mining operations, a distinct type of rock burst has emerged in recent years. These bursts occur without direct mining activities around roadway groups, as observed in incidents such as the Liangbaosi Coal Mine on August 15, 2016, Gaojiapu Coal Mine on February 3, 2017, and Mengcun Coal Mine on May 24, 2020. During the early stages of mine construction, many roadway groups were retained within impact-prone coal seams for expediency. Over time, the problem of rock bursts in these roadway groups has worsened. Unlike traditional rock bursts, this type exhibits characteristics such as a lack of precursors, large scale, absence of dynamic load participation, concealment, and extreme difficulty in prevention.

Given the unique challenges posed by these bursts, there is an urgent need to improve understanding and develop targeted control strategies. This study focuses on the rock bursts experienced in the central roadway group of a coal mine in western China. Through a combination of theoretical analysis and numerical simulation, the mechanism of rock bursts in deep, thick coal seam roadway groups caused by concentrated static loads is investigated. A novel super-long hole horizontal staged hydraulic fracturing technology is proposed, and its pressure relief and impact mitigation mechanisms in hard roofs are examined. A sensitivity analysis of in-situ stress, roof firmness coefficient, flow increment, and borehole spacing on the hydraulic fracturing effect is also conducted. Finally, an optimal fracturing scheme is determined and verified through engineering application, demonstrating its effectiveness.

Research site and its geological conditions

The study was conducted at a coal mine located in the central-western region of the Binchang mining area in Shaanxi Province, China. The primary mining seam, No. 4 coal seam, is characterized by a buried depth of 736 m, an average thickness of 22 m, and a gentle dip angle of 3°. The in-situ stress field follows the pattern of σH > σh > σv, indicating a horizontal stress-dominated environment. The immediate and basic roofs are primarily composed of dense and hard sandstone, known for their high strength and rigidity. In contrast, the direct floor consists of aluminum mudstone, which exhibits a propensity to expand when exposed to water.

The mining layout in No. 4 coal seam includes five central roadways. The isolated coal pillars between these roadways are 35 m wide, while the protective coal pillar of the main roadway is 200 m wide (Fig. 1). In 2020, a rock burst incident occurred within the central roadway, causing extensive damage and leading to a mine shutdown that lasted 15 months. The geological structure of the accident area is complex, featuring the DF29 fault, the Yuankouzi syncline, and the Xiejiazui anticline. Since the construction of the mine, more than ten rock burst events have been recorded in the central roadway, resulting in significant issues such as spalling, roof collapses, and floor heave (Fig. 2). These recurring incidents have severely hampered both production and safety operations within the mine.

Mechanism of rock burst in roadway group

The numerical simulation results indicate that shear stress, horizontal stress, and vertical stress in the coal pillars significantly increase following excavation, with vertical stress being particularly pronounced. Additionally, stress concentration is notably observed in the coal pillars on both sides of the roadway group. As the service life of the roadway group progresses, the stress concentration and extrusion damage become more severe, leading to a marked increase in the damage energy around the roadway. This energy accumulation provides the fundamental force and energy sources necessary to trigger rock bursts.

Investigations reveal that no mining operations occurred within a 500 m radius when multiple rock burst incidents occurred in the central roadway. This indicates that rock bursts in this area are primarily driven by static loads. The energy criterion for the rock burst can be expressed as follows14.

In summary, the mechanical mechanism of spontaneous rock bursts is illustrated in Fig. 7. The central roadway exhibits several key characteristics, including a large cross-sectional area, extensive excavation space, and high stress concentrations within the roadway group. These factors enable the occurrence of spontaneous rock bursts even without significant mining disturbances. Moreover, the vertical stress peak in the coal pillar increases from an initial value of P0 to P0 + ΔP, while the horizontal stress in the floor rises from N0 to N0 + ΔN due to the continuous pressure exerted by the overlying roof. These stress increments surpass both the critical abutment pressure (Pc1) and the ultimate bearing capacity (Pc2) of the surrounding rock, thus meeting the conditions necessary for rock burst initiation and causing impact damage to the roadway.

Hydraulic fracturing anti-impact mechanism of hard roof

Based on the analysis of the rock burst mechanism, it is evident that the primary cause of rock bursts in deep, extra-thick coal seam roadway groups is the excessive concentration of lateral abutment pressure and floor horizontal stress within the coal pillars on either side of the roadway. To address this, a method of staged hydraulic fracturing using super-long horizontal boreholes in underground mines is proposed. This method aims to disperse the concentrated vertical, horizontal, and shear stresses in the roadway group, thereby preventing rock bursts. The principle is similar to surface hydraulic fracturing techniques, which have been shown to effectively mitigate rock bursts32.

Pressure relief and anti-impact principle

From the evolution patterns of stress and acoustic emissions in the roadway group, it is evident that boreholes designed for hydraulic fracturing above the coal pillars provide direct pressure relief and energy dissipation within the coal pillars. Figure 8 illustrates the principle behind the fracturing and anti-impact effects of the hard roof. Post-hydraulic fracturing, a complex fracture network forms in the rock layer, significantly reducing the strength of the fractured rock. This fractured layer acts as a plastic cushion, absorbing energy and weakening the stress transfer from the overlying roof. Consequently, the stress in the upper rock layers shifts from “hard transfer” to "soft transfer," reducing the overall static load on the coal seam beneath the fractured zone. As a result, the peak stress in the coal pillars decreases from P0 + ΔP to Ph, which is lower than the critical load Pc1 required to trigger a rock burst, thereby preventing its occurrence.

Hydraulic fracturing anti-impact principle of hard roof (Ph is coal pillar abutment pressure after hydraulic fracturing, MPa).

Discussions

Hydraulic fracturing effect

Figures 15–16 compare the effects of hydraulic fracturing on the stability of the roadway and coal pillar, using simulation results with and without hydraulic fracturing. In the absence of fracturing in the overlying strata, the coal pillars exhibit high values of σx, σy, τxyand stress concentration coefficients, with peak stress points located closer to the roadway. Consequently, the roadway experiences more severe damage. In contrast, after hydraulic fracturing is applied to the overlying siltstone, these stress values and the concentration coefficient in the coal pillar are significantly reduced, and the peak stress point shifts farther from the roadway. The overall deformation of the roadway is minimal, and the surrounding rock remains stable, consistent with findings from previous studies33–35. Thus, hydraulic fracturing effectively reduces concentrated stress in the coal pillar, preventing rock bursts and enhancing the safety of coal mine operations.

Borehole spacing

Figure 28 shows that the primary hydraulic fractures propagate along the direction of maximum ground stress. When the borehole spacing is 60 m, the rock strata between the holes are fully fractured, and the fractures connect completely. At a spacing of 80 m, the rock strata between the holes are nearly fractured, and cracks penetrate, though fracturing is less effective. At 100 m spacing, the rock formation is poorly fractured, and though inter-hole fractures eventually connect, the fracturing effect is significantly weakened. Figures 29–31 display the initiation and instability pressures and vertical stresses along monitoring lines H and L under different borehole spacings. Both water pressure and fracturing effectiveness decrease as borehole spacing increases, with a V-shaped initiation pressure distribution and an inverted V-shaped instability pressure distribution. The rock pressure relief effect also diminishes as borehole spacing increases. Thus, a borehole spacing of 60 m is recommended for optimal hydraulic fracturing on-site.

Borehole horizontal level

Four borehole horizontal levels, 30 m, 40 m, 50 m, and 57 m above the coal seam, are considered (Fig. 32). Figure 33 shows that the main fractures propagate horizontally. When H = 30 m, seepage flow is large, but the high water pressure gradient near the coal seam reduces roadway support capacity and causes abnormal watering. As H increases to 40 m and 50 m, compression-shear failure increases, but the high water pressure gradient moves away from the coal seam, reducing the adverse effects on the roadway. At H = 57 m, however, roadway stability decreases significantly, and fracturing effectiveness weakens. Figure 34 shows that as H increases from 30 to 50 m, the peak stress point moves farther from the roadway, with a large stress drop. Beyond 50 m, stress concentration increases, leading to deformation and failure in the roadways. Thus, for optimal permeability and roadway stability, the borehole layer should be positioned 40–50 m above the roadway.

Engineering applications and validation

Fracturing scheme

In this study, an ultra-long horizontal borehole staged fracturing technology was developed for the roof. Based on the previous analysis, the horizontal drilling level was set 45 m above the main roadway. Five boreholes were arranged across a 600 m span along the central transportation roadway, each with an aperture of 153 mm. These boreholes radiate in a fan-shaped pattern from the drilling site towards the overlying roof of the coal pillars in the main roadway group. The spacing between boreholes 1–4 is approximately 40 m, while borehole 5, located above the protective coal pillar of the roadway group, is 70 m away from borehole 4. The specific hydraulic fracturing layout is shown in Fig. 35 (a).

The specific hydraulic fracturing scheme and technology.

A high-flow, high-pressure pump (BRW500, 3BZ9.8/70–250) was used for staged hydraulic fracturing. The designed fracturing stages were spaced 15 m apart, with a constant flow increment of 0.6 m3/min, and each fracturing stage lasted no less than 30 min. The detailed construction process included fixing the kilometer drilling rig, drilling, connecting the fracturing tool string, starting the fracturing pump, measuring pipeline pressure, sealing the borehole, injecting water at stable pressure, stopping the pump, discharging water, and withdrawing the pipe string. Fracturing was also performed in the borehole’s bending section, as shown in Fig. 35 (b).

Fracturing and anti-impact effect

A total of 45 hydraulic fracturing operations were carried out across five boreholes. The typical pressure–time curve is shown in Fig. 36. After approximately 7 min, fracturing began, with the initiation pressure reaching 20.2 MPa. A sharp pressure drop followed, indicating the opening of the fracture. As fluid filled the fracture, pressure stabilized until another sudden drop occurred, signaling the formation of macro-fractures and repeated tensile failures in the rock layer. The appearance of water in the roadway after a certain period confirmed that the fractures had fully extended and connected, marking a key indication for stopping the fracturing process.

To evaluate the roof hydraulic fracturing’s pressure relief and impact mitigation effects, four microseismic sensors and seven ground sound sensors were installed in the structural area. A comparison of the microseismic data for the 50 days before and after fracturing, as shown in Fig. 37, revealed a 44.3% reduction in microseismic frequency and an 83.3% reduction in microseismic energy. These results indicate that the thick, hard roof was effectively fractured. The No. 8 ground sound probe recorded an 85.6% reduction in detected ground sounds and a 76.3% decrease in total energy. Similarly, the No. 9 ground sound probe showed a 90.4% reduction in ground sounds and a 75.6% decrease in total energy.

Conclusion

Based on the study of rock bursts in deep mine roadway groups, the mechanisms of rock bursts, the effectiveness of hydraulic fracturing as a prevention method, and the key influencing parameters were investigated through numerical simulations, theoretical analysis, and engineering validation. The following conclusions are drawn:

(1) When the hard roof remains intact, the coal pillars exhibit large values of σx, σy and τxy with a high stress concentration coefficient, and the peak stress point is located near the roadway, resulting in severe damage. However, hydraulic fracturing significantly reduces the concentrated stress within the coal pillars, as evidenced by the decrease in acoustic emissions and reduced roadway damage energy, achieving effective pressure relief and impact mitigation.

(2) Hydraulic fractures consistently initiate and propagate along the direction of maximum ground stress, regardless of whether λ is less than or greater than 1. When λ = 1, the primary fracture direction becomes randomly distributed at various angles to the vertical or horizontal. Regardless of ground stress conditions, hydraulic fracturing is capable of fully destroying the rock mass.

(3) The effectiveness of hydraulic fracturing improves with increasing in-situ stress difference and decreasing borehole spacing, while the effect of flow increment on fracturing performance is minimal. Additionally, when the overlying strata have a high firmness coefficient, greater water pressure is required to extend fractures, making hydraulic fracturing less effective in such conditions.

(4) For optimal on-site hydraulic fracturing, the following parameters are recommended: a large in-situ stress difference λ, a low firmness coefficient for the rock stratum, an initial flow rate Q0 of 50 m2/d, a borehole spacing of 60 m, and a borehole horizontal level 40–50 m above the roadway. These parameters have been successfully validated in engineering applications to prevent rock bursts. The ultra-long horizontal borehole staged fracturing technology for hard roofs in underground mines has proven to be an effective method for reducing microseismic energy and ground audio frequency, making it a valuable tool for rock burst prevention and control.
