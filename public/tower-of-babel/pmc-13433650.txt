Perceived Sleep Quality as a Mediator Between Occupational Stress and Depression: The Moderating Role of Work Engagement
Authors: Jiwan Moon, Sang-Won Jeon, Sra Jung, Sung Joon Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is growing interest in the role of sleep-related factors, particularly perceived sleep quality, in the relationship between occupational stress and depression. Work engagement has been proposed as a potential protective factor against depression. This study aimed to examine the relationships among occupational stress, perceived sleep quality, and depression, as well as the role of work engagement in these relationships.MethodsA total of 2,425 Korean employees participated in this study. The participants completed a series of questionnaires, including the Korean Occupational Stress Scale, the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, a single-item measure of perceived sleep quality, and the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-9. The mediating and moderating effects were analysed using the PROCESS macro in the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences.ResultsPerceived sleep quality mediated the association between occupational stress and depressive symptoms (b = 0.051; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.040–0.062). Work engagement moderated the effects of both occupational stress and perceived sleep quality on depressive symptoms.ConclusionThis study highlights the role of perceived sleep quality and work engagement as mediators and moderators of occupational stress and depressive symptoms, respectively. Improving perceived sleep quality and promoting work engagement may help reduce the risk of depression among employees experiencing occupational stress.Keywords: Occupational Stress, Sleep Quality, Work Engagement, Depression, Workplace

INTRODUCTION

Depression is common among employees in the workplace and comes at a high cost. For individuals with depression, one major cost is a functional disability that reduces quality of life.1 Furthermore, employee depression impacts organisations through unemployment, job turnover, and productivity loss related to presenteeism or absenteeism among depressed employees.2,3 This can also be linked to healthcare costs and business outcomes that affect the national economy.4 Occupational stress, such as high demands or job insecurity, is a common predictor of depression at work.1 Previous studies on Korean employees have compared the predictability of specific occupational stressors, finding that inadequate social support and discomfort in the work climate are the strongest predictors of depressive symptoms.5 However, reducing occupational stress to prevent depression is challenging. If we find an indirect pathway from occupational stress to depression, we might obtain clues to mitigate the effects of occupational stress.

Several studies have investigated the role of sleep quality in the association between occupational stress and depression. Some studies have reported poor sleep quality and insomnia mediate the relationship between perceived stress and depression.6,7 Although fewer studies have focused on the association between occupational stress and sleep quality, one study reported that occupational stress predicts depression via sleep quality.8 However, a longitudinal study revealed that components of sleep quality have differential effects on the association between occupational stress and psychosomatic health complaints, with only sleep fragmentation mediating this relationship, whereas other components of sleep quality, such as sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency, and perceived sleep quality, did not.9 There is also evidence that perceived sleep quality, which refers to an individual’s subjective satisfaction with the quality of their sleep, is a better predictor of sleepiness than sleep quantity,10 and is more closely related to sleep-dependent processes.11 Moreover, perceived sleep quality, unlike sleep quantity, is associated with self-reported general well-being.12 Given that sleep parameters, such as sleep duration and timing, which are expected to affect sleep quality, have individual variability in their optimal range and effects on sleep quality.13 Perceived sleep quality may hold particular importance. Additionally, even self-rated normal sleepers differ in their perceived sleep quality.13 Therefore, this study investigated the independent role of perceived sleep quality. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have focused on perceived sleep quality rather than sleep quality as measured using the total score of assessments, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

Meanwhile, the potential role of work engagement as a protective factor against depression in employees is increasingly recognised. Previous findings have shown that low work engagement predicts depressive symptoms14 and high work engagement prevents burnout,15 which has a positive association with depression.16 Work engagement refers to a positive and fulfilling mental state related to one’s work and consists of three components,17 vigour, dedication, and absorption, which correspond to the behavioural-energetic, emotional, and cognitive components of work engagement, respectively.18 Vigour refers to increased energy and resilience during a task; dedication involves enthusiastic involvement in one’s work; and absorption is characterised by full concentration and immersion in work while remaining distinct from work addiction,18,19 Several studies have shown a positive correlation between sleep quality and work engagement.20,21,22 Work engagement is negatively associated with sleep problems.23 Therefore, we expected that work engagement would moderate the relationships among occupational stress, perceived sleep quality, and depression. Additionally, studies have shown that the three components of work engagement act differently related to depression,24 and we investigated the role of each component in this study.

This study aimed to examine whether perceived sleep quality mediates the relationship between occupational stress and depressive symptoms and whether work engagement, including its three components, moderates this relationship. METHODS

Study participants

Study participants were Korean employees who underwent workplace mental health screening at the Workplace Mental Health Institute of Kangbuk Samsung Hospital (Seoul, Korea) between May 2016 and December 2016. After excluding two participants with missing data, a total of 2,425 employees were included in the final analysis. Because the missing rate was very low and there was no indication of systematic missingness, listwise deletion was applied. The selected study participants ranged in age from 19 to 63 years and were from 10 organisations, including both private and public organisations.

Assessments

Information on sociodemographic factors, including sex, age, education level (college graduate or below, bachelor’s degree, and master’s degree or higher), and marital status (single, married, and other, including separated, divorced, or widowed), was collected.

We accounted for participants’ level of alcohol dependence, which is known to be a risk factor for both poorer perceived sleep quality25 and depression,26 was assessed using the Korean version of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT). The AUDIT is a 10-item self-reported assessment in which each item is scored from 0 to 4.27

To evaluate perceived sleep quality of each participant, we employed the single assessment item from the Korean version of PSQI. PSQI is a self-rated assessment of last month’s sleep quality and disturbances.28 Participants were asked to answer the question, ‘During the past month, how would you rate your sleep quality overall?,’ with scores ranging from 0 (very good) to 3 (very bad). Participants were also instructed to report their total sleep time in minutes, which was later used as a covariate to adjust for the effect of sleep duration when assessing the effect of perceived sleep quality.

To assess occupational stress, we used the Korean Occupational Stress Scale-Short Form (KOSS-SF), which focuses on unique and specific occupational stressors among Korean employees. The KOSS-SF consists of a 24-item self-reported questionnaire scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much) and includes seven subscales. The corrected subscale score is adjusted to 100 and the KOSS-SF total score is calculated as the sum of the corrected KOSS-SF subscale scores divided by seven.29

We assessed the severity of depressive symptoms using the Korean version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). The CES-D consists of 20 items scored on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, with higher total scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.30

We evaluated work engagement using the Korean version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-9 (UWES-9).31 It is a three-dimensional questionnaire that includes three components of work engagement: vigour, dedication, and absorption. Each component comprises three self-rated items scored on a frequency scale ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (always).18

Statistical analyses

We performed descriptive analyses for sociodemographic, sleep-related, job-related and clinical characteristics. We also conducted mediation analysis using Model 4 and moderation analysis using Model 15 in the PROCESS macro for Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), developed by Hayes.32 First, we examined whether perceived sleep quality mediates the association between occupational stress and severity of depressive symptoms. After identifying the mediating effect of perceived sleep quality, work engagement was introduced as a moderator to examine whether it mitigated the negative impact of occupational stress and poor perceived sleep quality on depressive symptoms. We demonstrated the moderating effect of work engagement using simple slope analysis. These analyses examined how the relationship between occupational stress, perceived sleep quality, and depressive symptoms varied at low (one standard deviation [SD] below the mean), average, and high levels (one SD above the mean) of work engagement. Based on previous studies, control variables including sex, age, education level, marital status, total sleep time, and alcohol use (AUDIT total score) were introduced in both analyses.6,33,34 Additionally, we conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether the three components of work engagement–vigour, dedication, and absorption–also showed similar moderating effects, using each component’s subscale score as a moderator. The number of bootstrap samples was 5,000 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. To account for potential clustering effects at the organizational level, we additionally estimated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for key variables (KOSS-SF and CES-D) and conducted a sensitivity analysis using random-intercept linear mixed models (Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimation) with organization as a random effect. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v23.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.).

Ethics statement

The requirement for consent from the participants was waived owing to the use of anonymised data. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangbuk Samsung Hospital (KBSMC 2019-01-042) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Mediation analysis: partial mediation effect of perceived sleep quality

The results of the mediation analysis examining the mediating role of perceived sleep quality are presented in Tables 2 and 3. The direct effect of occupational stress on depression and the indirect effect via perceived sleep quality were both significant (b = 0.290 and 0.051; standardized β = 0.35 and 0.06, respectively), indicating small-to-moderate magnitudes of association. Approximately 15% of the total effect of occupational stress on depressive symptoms was mediated through perceived sleep quality. Occupational stress was positively related to depressive symptoms (b = 0.290, P < 0.001), while it was positively related to poor perceived sleep quality (b = 0.014, P < 0.001), which in turn was positively related to depressive symptoms (b = 3.736, P < 0.001). Thus, perceived sleep quality partially mediated the association between occupational stress and depressive symptoms.

Moderated mediation analyses: moderation effect of work engagement

The results of the moderated mediation analyses that introduced work engagement as a moderator are presented in Tables 4 and 5. Work engagement is negatively related to depressive symptoms (b = −0.233, P < 0.001), and the interaction between occupational stress and work engagement was negatively related to depressive symptoms (b = −0.006, P < 0.001), while the interaction between perceived sleep quality and work engagement was also negatively related to depressive symptoms (b = −0.089, P < 0.001). These results suggest that work engagement moderates the association between occupational stress, depression, and the mediator (perceived sleep quality). Furthermore, we compared the differences in the effect of occupational stress on depressive symptoms according to the level of work engagement using a simple slope analysis (Fig. 2A). Depressive symptoms were more severe among employees with low work engagement (b = 0.258, P < 0.001) than among those with high work engagement (b = 0.126, P < 0.001). This suggests the role of work engagement in ameliorating the negative effects of occupational stress on depressive symptoms. Simple slope analyses also revealed the protective role of work engagement in the association between poor perceived sleep quality and depressive symptoms (Fig. 2B). The severity of depressive symptoms was greater among employees with low work engagement (b = 4.284, P < 0.001) than among those with high work engagement (b = 2.413, P < 0.001). In further exploratory analyses, where each component of work engagement was introduced as a moderator, the three components – vigour, dedication, and absorption – also exhibited significant moderating effects, with each showing a consistent effect of −0.003 (Supplementary Table 1). These results were generally consistent with the findings using the total UWES score. In a multilevel sensitivity analysis accounting for organizational clustering, the key interaction effects of occupational stress × work engagement and perceived sleep quality × work engagement remained consistent in both direction and statistical significance (ICC = 0.163 for KOSS-SF; 0.128 for CES-D), indicating that clustering effects did not materially change the primary findings.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the role of perceived sleep quality as a mediator between stress and depressive symptoms, assessing its unique effect while controlling for sleep duration. Simultaneously, this study also examined work engagement as a potential moderator in the association between occupational stress, depressive symptoms, and perceived sleep quality. These findings are significant because they suggest that improving perceived sleep quality or modifying the workplace to enhance employees’ work engagement, rather than focusing solely on reducing occupational stress, may be beneficial. Considering that work engagement and perceived sleep quality represent subjective evaluations of work and sleep quality, respectively, the results of this study underscore the critical role of subjective perceptions in the associations between occupational stress, sleep quality, and depression.

The results of our study suggest that occupational stress is indirectly associated with depressive symptoms through perceived sleep quality, with a direct effect independent of sleep quantity. This finding suggests that subjective assessments of sleep, which reflect restfulness, depth, and emotional recovery,35,36,37 may play a more direct role in depressive symptoms than sleep duration alone. For instance, an individual may sleep for a sufficient duration yet still experience poor rest due to persistent hyperarousal or impaired emotional recovery. The relationship between occupational stress and perceived sleep quality in this study is consistent with previous studies that reported that occupational stress induces poor sleep quality.38,39,40 Poor sleep quality is the result of hyperarousal when an individual is trying to fall asleep, which is related to the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis,41 and can be triggered by occupational stress.42 The finding that perceived sleep quality was indirectly associated with both occupational stress and depression is consistent with previous studies on healthcare workers,8 although it was not a study that focused on perceived sleep quality. Poor sleep quality can trigger depressive symptoms by affecting the perception and experience of emotional stimuli.43,44,45,46 In addition, self-reported sleep difficulties predict difficulties in withdrawing attention from negative stimuli, which in turn is associated with severe depressive symptoms.47,48 Poor sleep quality is associated with depressive symptoms resulting from maladaptive emotional processes. From a neurobiological perspective, chronic activation of the HPA axis in insomnia49 is associated with a risk of depression.50 The finding that occupational stress can predict depressive symptoms via poor perceived sleep quality ultimately emphasises the importance of interventions to increase perceived sleep quality to prevent depression in employees. In the context of the Korean workplace, characterised by long working hours, hierarchical structures and collective norms, employees may encounter challenges in achieving psychological detachment from their work, even outside of working hours.51,52,53,54 This phenomenon can potentially contribute to lower perceived sleep quality and increased susceptibility to depression.55 Targeted interventions at both individual and organizational levels may therefore be essential to mitigate these risks. Previous studies have recommended the use of cognitive behaviour treatment (CBT) for insomnia to improve sleep quality by managing stress and improving resilience.41 Internet-based CBT also improves depressive symptoms by improving sleep quality.56 At the organisational level, measures such as flexible scheduling, job autonomy, and workload adjustment may help employees recover more effectively and maintain a healthier work–rest balance.57,58 Given these cultural characteristics, it is advisable to exercise caution when generalising our findings to employees in different cultural settings.

In addition, this study suggests that work engagement may serve as a potential moderator that mitigates the negative effects of occupational stress. Given that whether a particular stressor is recognised as challenging or hindering depends on one’s stress mindset rather than the stressor itself,59 the way in which individuals deal with the stressor is important. As engaged employees have a positive view of their work and energetic connections to it,17 they are expected to perceive the same stressor as less challenging than their colleagues, which, in turn, reduces the risk of depression. Indeed, low work engagement predicts depressive symptoms14 and the onset of major depressive episodes.60 By contrast, in a longitudinal study, vigour was found to prevent depressive symptoms.24 In our study, not only vigour, but also dedication and absorption acted as moderators of the association between occupational stress and depression. Work engagement was also observed as a potential moderator in the relationship between poor perceived sleep quality and depression. There is no definitive evidence of the specific role of work engagement in this context; however, several plausible explanations exist. A previous study reported that engaged employees’ HPA axis function differs from that of those who are burned out.61 Therefore, work engagement may counteract chronic activation of the HPA axis, which is associated with poor perceived sleep quality. Another possibility is that work engagement prevents the onset of depression by affecting emotional states. Prior research has demonstrated that work engagement lets engaged employees experience more positive emotions than those who are not engaged.17,62 Indeed, emotional regulation is related to work engagement.63 However, the role of work engagement in this context remains inconclusive, and future research should clarify whether it acts as a protective moderator or represents a marker of personal and organizational resources.

Work engagement as a whole and its three components–vigour, dedication, and absorption–may function as potential moderators associated with a lower risk of depression among poor sleepers and those with occupational stress. Thus, increasing work engagement could potentially be promising intervention for preventing depression via both pathways. Previous studies have provided clues on ways to improve work engagement. For example, job resources, such as autonomy, guidance from supervisors, performance feedback, and personal resources, including self-esteem and self-efficacy, predict work engagement.17 Workplace social capital, which is described as a resource obtained through social networks, is also known to increase work engagement.64 In other words, these resources should be provided to improve employees’ work engagement. Referring to a previous study, resources that are not functional in the task are not helpful.65 Therefore, professional individual-level interventions suitable for job and task characteristics seem to be effective regardless of the type of intervention.

Our study has some limitations. First, because of the cross-sectional design of our study, in which all variables were measured simultaneously, the findings should be interpreted with caution rather than as evidence of causal relationships. For example, although work engagement was found to potentially moderate the association between occupational stress and depression, the direction of this relationship remains uncertain. Therefore, a longitudinal study is required to replicate the results of this study. Second, we used a single item from the PSQI instead of the total score, which makes it difficult to compare our findings with those of previous studies. However, considering that several studies have questioned the usefulness of the PSQI total score owing to the difference in factorial structures reported in some studies,66,67 using the total score is not always the best option. In addition, the focus on perceived sleep quality, adjusted for sleep duration, was a unique aspect of our study. Meanwhile, because perceived sleep quality was measured with a single ordinal item, its continuous treatment should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, future studies employing a generalized structural equation modeling approach could provide a more comprehensive validation of these findings. Third, because we used a self-rated questionnaire, the results may have been affected by a recall bias. However, because we wanted to assess perceived sleep quality and work engagement, which refers to how individuals feel about their work, the use of a questionnaire was unavoidable. Nevertheless, it is still necessary to consider accessory assessments to complement these limitations. Finally, as the participants were employees who took part in a health screening program, a potential healthy worker effect should be considered when interpreting our findings.68 Subgroup analyses based on employees’ mental health status would have been informative in this regard, but such analyses were not possible because past psychiatric history data were not collected.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that a decline in perceived sleep quality due to occupational stress contributes to depressive symptoms independent of sleep duration. Enhancing perceived sleep quality and work engagement could be considered as potential strategies to prevent depression among employees with occupational stress, especially when accompanied by individualised supports.
