Improved Locoregional Recurrence‐Free Survival With Carbon Ion Versus Photon Radiotherapy in Locally Advanced Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Yonglong Liu, Ningyi Ma, Yaqi Li, Jing Mi, Xingwen Fan, Jian Chen, Jingfang Mao, Guoliang Jiang, Kailiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCarbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) delivers high linear energy transfer radiation with superior dose conformity compared with photon radiotherapy (PRT). However, whether these physical advantages translate into clinical benefit for locally advanced non‐small cell lung cancer (LA‐NSCLC) in the era of modern systemic therapy remains unclear.MethodsWe performed a retrospective, propensity score‐matched cohort study of patients with Stage III LA‐NSCLC treated with definitive CIRT or PRT as part of multimodality therapy between January 2016 and December 2023. The primary endpoint was locoregional recurrence‐free survival (LRFS). Secondary endpoints included progression‐free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS), and acute grade ≥ 2 radiation‐related adverse events (AEs).ResultsA total of 292 patients were matched (146 per group). With a median follow‐up of 32.5 months, CIRT group demonstrated significantly longer median LRFS (57.7 vs. 25.0 months; HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.39–0.88; p = 0.01) and median PFS (17.7 vs. 16.7 months; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.53–0.94; p = 0.02) compared with PRT group, whereas OS and DMFS were similar between groups. CIRT group also had fewer acute grade ≥ 2 radiation‐related AEs.ConclusionsCIRT demonstrated improved locoregional control and fewer acute toxicities compared with PRT in LA‐NSCLC in this retrospective matched cohort. Prospective randomized trials are warranted to validate these findings.

Introduction

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy followed by consolidation immunotherapy is the standard of care for unresectable, locally advanced non‐small cell lung cancer (LA‐NSCLC) [1, 2]. However, survival remains unsatisfactory, and locoregional recurrence continues to account for a substantial proportion of treatment failures [3, 4]. Further dose escalation with photon radiotherapy (PRT) is often not feasible because many patients already approach heart and lung dose constraints, and additional toxicity may compromise the delivery and benefit of systemic therapy [5].

Carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) offers a promising alternative by combining superior dose distribution with high linear energy transfer and enhanced relative biological effectiveness (RBE) [6, 7]. These features allow more effective killing of hypoxic and radioresistant tumor cells while better sparing critical structures [7]. Preclinical and translational data also suggest that carbon ions may enhance antitumor immune activation, providing a strong biological rationale for integrating CIRT with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [8]. Growing clinical experience in lung cancer, including early‐stage and selected Stage III disease, has shown excellent local control and favorable toxicity, supporting a potential role for CIRT in multimodality treatment [9, 10, 11].

Despite this emerging evidence, direct comparisons of CIRT with state‐of‐the‐art PRT remain limited. Consequently, it is still unclear whether CIRT improves survival and toxicity outcomes and which patients are most likely to benefit in the immunotherapy and targeted therapy era [12]. To address these questions, we conducted a retrospective, propensity score‐matched (PSM) study directly comparing survival outcomes, disease control, and acute toxicities between definitive CIRT and intensity‐modulated PRT in patients with Stage III LA‐NSCLC, and explored prognostic indicators that may guide patient selection for CIRT.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with Stage III LA‐NSCLC treated at our institution between January 2016 and December 2023. Eligible patients met the following criteria (Table S1): (1) Stage III disease according to the AJCC 8th edition; (2) treatment with definitive photon radiotherapy (PRT) or carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT); (3) age 18–80 years; (4) completion of the planned radiotherapy course; and (5) Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) ≥ 90. Patients with recurrent or metastatic disease, prior thoracic radiotherapy, severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities, or insufficient follow‐up were excluded. The institutional review board approved the study before retrospective data collection and analysis began (approval No. 251007EXP‐01). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

Photon Radiotherapy

PRT was delivered using 6‐MV X‐rays with intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) [13]. Simulation CT scans were acquired with patients immobilized in the supine position, covering the neck, chest, and upper abdomen with 5‐mm slice thickness. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included the primary tumor and involved lymph nodes based on CT and/or PET‐CT. The clinical target volume (CTV) was generated by expanding the GTV by 1.2–1.5 cm, and a further 0.7‐cm margin was added to form the planning target volume (PTV). IMRT plans were created using the Pinnacle [3] system (Philips), and cone‐beam CT was performed for setup verification. Most patients received 60 Gy in 30 fractions. For photon IMRT, conventional QUANTEC‐based dose constraints were applied, including a lung V20 ≤ 35%, a mean lung dose ≤ 20 Gy, a maximum spinal cord dose ≤ 45–50 Gy, and a maximum esophageal dose ≤ 60–66 Gy. Additional institutional constraints for low‐dose lung and heart exposure were also considered where applicable.

Carbon Ion Radiotherapy

Intensity‐modulated CIRT was delivered using a pencil beam‐scanning technique with beam energies of approximately 86–430 MeV/u on an IONTRIS system (Siemens) [9]. Four‐dimensional CT (4DCT) was obtained for motion assessment. Free breathing was allowed if tumor motion was ≤ 5 mm; otherwise, respiratory gating or active breathing control was used to limit motion to ≤ 5 mm within the gating window. GTVs were delineated on all 10 phases of 4DCT and combined to generate an internal GTV (IGTV), with a 0.6–0.8‐cm expansion for the CTV and a 0.5–0.7‐cm expansion for the PTV, modified along beam paths according to water‐equivalent depth. Treatment plans were generated using the Syngo system with the local effect model, and doses were prescribed in gray equivalents (Gy[RBE]). Patients received 3–4 Gy(RBE) per fraction, 5 fractions per week, to a total dose of 69.0–83.6 Gy(RBE). OAR constraints included a mean lung dose ≤ 15 Gy(RBE), lung V20 ≤ 28%, heart V30 ≤ 30%, and maximum spinal cord dose ≤ 45 Gy(RBE). These constraints were developed with reference to published thoracic radiotherapy guidelines and consensus recommendations, together with institutional experience with CIRT [14, 15, 16, 17]. Dose limits for the esophagus and proximal bronchial tree (≤ 80 Gy[RBE]) were adopted in later cases to reduce the risk of high‐dose exposure to these structures. Phantom verification and weekly repeat CT were performed to allow adaptive replanning.

Systemic Therapy

All patients received either intensity‐modulated CIRT or IMRT. Most patients underwent four to six cycles of systemic therapy. Chemotherapy with platinum‐based doublets, typically combining cisplatin or carboplatin with taxanes or pemetrexed, was delivered as neoadjuvant, concurrent, or adjuvant treatment according to individual tolerance and clinical decision‐making. Patients who were unable or unwilling to receive concurrent chemotherapy were treated with sequential chemotherapy instead. Immunotherapy included PD‐1 or PD‐L1 inhibitors, and pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, durvalumab, and camrelizumab were the most frequently used in this cohort. Targeted therapy was introduced for patients with actionable oncogenic alterations, primarily with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) against EGFR or ALK.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was locoregional recurrence‐free survival (LRFS), defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to recurrence in the primary tumor or regional lymph nodes. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS), and acute grade ≥ 2 radiation‐related adverse events (AEs). OS, PFS, and DMFS were measured from radiotherapy initiation to death from any cause, documented disease progression, and first distant metastasis, respectively. Radiation‐related and ICI‐related AEs occurring within 3 months of radiotherapy were graded using CTCAE version 5.0.

Propensity Score Matching

To reduce potential confounding, PSM was performed based on age, sex, histologic subtype, TNM stage (IIIA‐IIIC), tumor location (central vs. peripheral), and receipt of chemotherapy, ICIs, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Patients who received CIRT were matched 1:1 to those treated with PRT using nearest‐neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. LRFS, PFS, OS, and DMFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Subgroup analyses for LRFS and PFS were performed using stratified Cox models across baseline clinical characteristics. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp) and Python 3.12.

Results

A total of 292 patients were included in the matched CIRT and PRT cohorts (Table 1). Although the overall TNM stage distribution was balanced between the two groups, the CIRT group had a higher proportion of T3‐4 tumors than the PRT group (p = 0.001). Chemotherapy was administered in over 90% of patients in both groups, whereas approximately 60% received ICIs and about 20% received TKIs.

Survival Outcomes

The last follow‐up date was November 22, 2024. The median OS was 32.3 months (95% CI, 26.4 to not reached [NR]) in the CIRT group and 31.1 months (95% CI, 23.1 to 59.8) in the PRT group (Figure 1A). The median PFS was 17.7 months (95% CI, 13.2 to 24.7) versus 16.7 months (95% CI, 14.0 to 19.5), respectively (Figure 1B). For LRFS, the median was 57.7 months (95% CI, 28.8 to NR) in the CIRT group and 25.0 months (95% CI, 19.5 to 42.2) in the PRT group (Figure 1C). The median DMFS was 54.2 months (95% CI, 28.6 to NR) versus 33.4 months (95% CI, 22.7 to 45.5), respectively (Figure 1D). At 36 months, OS, PFS, LRFS, and DMFS rates were 49.7% versus 44.9%, 35.1% versus 13.6%, 58.8% versus 41.1%, and 55.1% versus 44.2% in the CIRT and PRT groups, respectively. The median follow‐up for OS, PFS, LRFS, and DMFS was 33.8 versus 26.2 months, 33.2 versus 32.0 months, 20.4 versus 19.6 months, and 24.8 versus 16.4 months, respectively.

Survival Outcomes by Systemic Therapy

Among patients treated with ICIs, 53 received a PD‐1 inhibitor and 8 received a PD‐L1 inhibitor in the CIRT group; the ICI class was not documented for 26 patients. The corresponding numbers in the PRT group were 48, 22, and 13, respectively. Targeted therapy was administered to 29 patients in the CIRT group and 24 patients in the PRT group; the agent type was not documented for 4 and 3 patients, respectively. In patients who did not receive ICIs or TKIs, median LRFS was 28.6 months with CIRT versus 17.5 months with PRT (HR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.21–0.87; p = 0.019), whereas OS, PFS, and DMFS were similar between groups (Figure S1). Among patients without ICI use, median LRFS was 57.7 months with CIRT and 21.6 months with PRT (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.27–0.90; p = 0.021), with no significant differences in OS, PFS, or DMFS (Figure S2). In patients who did not receive TKIs, median LRFS was 57.7 months with CIRT versus 22.7 months with PRT (HR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.34–0.83; p = 0.005), while median PFS was 18.2 versus 17.1 months (HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.52–0.99; p = 0.044). OS and DMFS remained comparable between modalities (Figure S3).

Radiation Therapy‐Related Acute Adverse Events

As summarized in Table 2, grade ≥ 2 radiation‐related acute AEs occurred in 21.9% of patients treated with CIRT compared with 58.2% of those treated with PRT (p < 0.001). CIRT was also associated with significantly lower rates of grade ≥ 2 esophagitis (6.8% vs. 22.6%; p < 0.001) and grade ≥ 2 dermatitis (0% vs. 4.1%; p = 0.03), while rates of grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis were comparable between groups (12.3% vs. 17.1%; p = 0.25). Grade ≥ 2 hematologic AEs included leukopenia (11.0% vs. 38.4%; p < 0.001), neutropenia (9.6% vs. 23.3%; p = 0.002), anemia (11.0% vs. 19.9%; p = 0.04), and thrombocytopenia (0.7% vs. 17.1%; p < 0.001). Grade ≥ 2 pyrexia occurred in 6.2% versus 0.7% of patients (p = 0.01). Other systemic events were infrequent in both groups.

Discussion

In this PSM cohort of patients with unresectable Stage III LA‐NSCLC, CIRT was associated with significantly longer LRFS and PFS compared with intensity‐modulated PRT, while OS and DMFS remained similar between groups. Notably, the advantage of CIRT in locoregional control was also observed in patients who did not receive ICIs or TKIs, a group in which outcomes are more dependent on radiotherapy effectiveness. These observations, together with prior CIRT experiences in lung cancer, suggest that the physical and radiobiological properties of carbon ions may translate into improved local tumor control and support the continued evaluation of CIRT in the definitive treatment of selected patients with locally advanced disease. CIRT also demonstrated a more favorable acute toxicity profile, with a substantially lower incidence of moderate‐to‐severe treatment‐related adverse events.

Clinical evidence for CIRT in early‐stage NSCLC has shown excellent local control with low toxicity, supporting the theoretical advantages of carbon ions [18]. The greatest unmet need, however, remains in locally advanced disease, where photon‐based chemoradiotherapy continues to face limitations in dose intensification and locoregional control [4]. Several single‐arm studies of LA‐NSCLC have reported that hypofractionated or pencil beam‐scanned CIRT can achieve 2‐year local control rates of approximately 60% with manageable pulmonary and airway toxicity [9, 19]. Standard‐dose IMRT, despite refinements in planning and delivery, has produced 5‐year OS rates of only 30%–35% in Phase III trials such as RTOG 0617, where attempts at photon dose escalation increased cardiopulmonary exposure without improving outcomes [4]. In this context, our study extends prior CIRT experience by directly comparing CIRT with state‐of‐the‐art IMRT in a balanced Stage III population treated in the era of immunotherapy and targeted therapy and by incorporating detailed toxicity and systemic therapy‐stratified analyses.

The marked LRFS advantage observed with CIRT (median 57.7 vs. 25.0 months) likely reflects a combination of its superior physical dose distribution and enhanced radiobiological effectiveness. Carbon ions exhibit high LET and greater RBE compared with photons or protons, generating dense and complex DNA double‐strand breaks that are more difficult to repair and therefore more lethal to tumor cells [6]. They are especially effective against hypoxic, slowly proliferating, and stem‐like tumor cell subpopulations, features commonly seen in advanced NSCLC and strongly associated with photon resistance [7, 20]. Preclinical data also suggest that carbon ions may suppress hypoxia‐driven signaling and repopulation pathways, thereby enhancing long‐term tumor control [7]. Together, these mechanisms provide a strong biological rationale for the nearly twofold improvement in median LRFS observed in our cohort and reported in prior CIRT series, despite the CIRT group in this study having a higher proportion of T3‐4 tumors and centrally located lesions [19].

Notably, the LRFS benefit of CIRT over PRT persisted even in patients who did not receive ICIs or TKIs, suggesting that the advantage of CIRT is not solely dependent on modern systemic therapies. In this setting, outcomes are likely driven more directly by the intrinsic tumoricidal efficacy and spatial precision of radiotherapy. The LRFS improvement observed with CIRT in ICI/TKI‐naïve patients is consistent with pre‐immunotherapy carbon ion studies, which reported favorable local control despite limited systemic treatment options [19]. These findings suggest that the physical and radiobiologic advantages of carbon ions may translate into meaningful gains in locoregional disease control when systemic therapy is less intensive.

Within the CIRT cohort, the addition of ICIs or TKIs was associated with improved OS, PFS, and DMFS compared with CIRT alone, whereas LRFS was similar between patients who did and did not receive systemic therapy [2, 21]. This pattern suggests that modern systemic treatments may provide limited incremental benefit for locoregional control beyond what is achieved with standard chemotherapy and CIRT, while contributing more substantially to distant disease control and overall outcome. These observations imply that CIRT may be particularly valuable in patients who are unable to receive intensive systemic therapy, in whom maximizing the intrinsic efficacy of local treatment is especially important.

The PFS benefit associated with CIRT was likely driven mainly by improved locoregional control, because LRFS was longer whereas DMFS did not differ significantly between the two groups. Although sustained intrathoracic control may delay subsequent disease progression, distant failure remains an important determinant of outcome, consistent with previous CIRT and photon‐based chemoradiotherapy studies [19, 22]. However, the improvement in locoregional control did not translate into longer OS. Distant progression, postprogression treatment, and competing causes of death may have influenced OS. In addition, the follow‐up duration and number of OS events may have limited the statistical power to detect a significant difference. Because cause‐of‐death data were not consistently available, we could not determine whether noncancer‐related mortality differed between the two groups.

The toxicity profile observed in this study aligns with the expected dosimetric advantages of carbon ions. Owing to the pronounced sharper lateral penumbra, carbon ions enable escalation of tumor dose with improved homogeneity while minimizing irradiation of surrounding thoracic organs [23]. Dosimetric studies in locally advanced NSCLC have shown significant reductions in lung, heart, and esophageal exposure with CIRT compared with optimized photon plans, particularly for bulky T2b‐4 or centrally located tumors where photon dose intensification is limited by normal tissue tolerance [24]. The lower rates of acute grade ≥ 2 esophagitis and dermatitis with CIRT compared with PRT are consistent with a sharper lateral penumbra and reduced entrance and exit doses, which lower exposure of the esophagus and anterior chest wall [24]. The similar incidence of radiation‐induced pneumonitis between CIRT and PRT cohorts suggests that lung dose‐volume constraints were respected for both techniques and that high‐LET radiation does not inherently increase lung parenchymal sensitivity at the prescribed doses used [9, 19]. The absence of a clear reduction in radiation‐induced pneumonitis with CIRT may also be related to the slightly larger fraction size [25].

The substantially lower rates of leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia in the CIRT cohort likely reflect reduced low‐ and intermediate‐dose exposure of hematopoietic structures in the thorax, including vertebral bone marrow and large blood pools. Prior photon studies have linked higher lung and heart V5‐V10 and larger target volumes with more severe lymphopenia and worse survival in LA‐NSCLC [13, 26]. By reducing the integral dose, CIRT may help preserve immune and hematologic function during and after chemoradiotherapy, which is important for the safe delivery of consolidation immunotherapy and subsequent systemic treatments.

Among patients receiving ICIs, rates of grade ≥ 2 immune‐related AEs, including immune‐mediated pneumonitis, were comparable between CIRT and PRT. This observation is consistent with early clinical experiences combining particle therapy and checkpoint blockade, which have not shown unexpected immune‐related toxicity [27, 28].

Notably, the CIRT group received a higher nominal prescribed tumor dose (69.0–83.6 Gy[RBE]) than the PRT group, in which most patients received 60 Gy in 30 fractions, yet had fewer acute grade ≥ 2 radiation‐related adverse events. This finding may reflect the improved dose conformity and reduced normal‐tissue exposure achieved with CIRT. However, direct comparison of the prescribed doses should be interpreted cautiously because the fractionation schedules differed and CIRT doses were expressed in Gy(RBE). Clinically, CIRT may offer advantages for patients with bulky or centrally located tumors, for whom photon dose escalation is limited by organ‐at‐risk tolerance, and for patients in whom preservation of bone marrow reserve and organ function is important for continued systemic therapy [13, 19]. Exploratory subgroup analyses showed that the estimated treatment effects favored CIRT among patients aged < 65 years, never‐smokers, and those with centrally located tumors, T1‐2 disease, or Stage IIIA disease. However, these findings are hypothesis‐generating because of the limited subgroup sample sizes and require prospective validation [9, 12].

This study provides several important contributions. It represents one of the largest PSM comparisons of CIRT versus state‐of‐the‐art IMRT for LA‐NSCLC in the contemporary era of immunotherapy and targeted therapy. The findings indicate that CIRT is associated with improved locoregional control and PFS while reducing clinically meaningful acute moderate‐to‐severe toxicities. In addition, this work offers a detailed exploration of outcomes according to ICI and TKI use for patients receiving CIRT or PRT, supporting the biological rationale for optimizing radiotherapy modality within multimodality treatment.

Study Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. This was a retrospective, single‐institution study, and although PSM minimized imbalances across many key covariates, unmeasured confounding may still exist. Cause of death and the sequence of treatment failure were not consistently documented, precluding cause‐specific mortality analysis and reliable classification of the first recurrence as locoregional, distant, or simultaneous. In addition, although information on late adverse events was collected when available, these events were incompletely and inconsistently documented, preventing a reliable comparison between the two groups. Therefore, our safety findings are limited to acute adverse events. Importantly, the CIRT group in this study presented with more locally advanced disease (e.g., higher proportion of T3‐4 tumors), yet CIRT still demonstrated significantly superior LRFS, supporting the robustness of the observed treatment effect. In addition, this study did not include a formal dosimetric comparison between modalities, which limits mechanistic interpretation of the observed differences in toxicity and disease control. Nevertheless, our prior work incorporating normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) and tumor control probability (TCP) modeling has provided complementary evidence supporting the dosimetric and radiobiological advantages of CIRT over photon therapy with respect to lung and esophageal toxicity as well as tumor control probability [25]. Systemic therapy regimens varied over time, and subgroup analyses by ICI or TKI use were exploratory with limited sample sizes, reducing statistical power. Finally, CIRT is currently available only in specialized centers; therefore, generalizability to broader clinical practice and across different carbon ion platforms remains uncertain.

Conclusions

In patients with unresectable LA‐NSCLC, CIRT demonstrated significantly improved locoregional control and progression‐free survival compared with PRT, while achieving similar overall survival and a more favorable acute toxicity profile. These results highlight the potential role of CIRT as a valuable treatment option for patients with locally advanced NSCLC, and prospective randomized trials are warranted to validate these findings.
