Use of hair relaxers and hair dyes during pregnancy and breast tissue composition in mothers and adolescent daughters
Authors: Marissa Chan, Lothar Lilge, Jane Walter, Parisa Tehranifar, Rachel L. Miller, Anhthu Hoang, Peggy Shephard, Frederica Perera, Julie B. Herbstman, Mary Beth Terry, Jasmine A. McDonald, Rebecca D. Kehm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlack and Hispanic women are more likely to present with advanced-stage breast cancer and have lower survival rates compared to white women. Hair products are a source of exposure to environmental chemicals (e.g., formaldehyde, phthalates) that may contribute to these persistent inequities, particularly when used during sensitive exposure windows for breast tissue development (i.e., pregnancy, gestation). In this study, we examined the association between maternal hair product use during pregnancy and breast tissue composition (BTC) later in life among mothers and their adolescent daughters.MethodsWe analyzed data from a prospective cohort of self-identifying non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic mother-daughter pairs in New York City (n = 186 daughters and 180 mothers). Mothers reported on their use of hair relaxers and hair dyes during and prior to pregnancy at a visit occurring in the third trimester. One to two decades later, the BTC of mothers and daughters was measured using optical spectroscopy, which estimates BTC properties that are positively (water, collagen, and optical index [Oindex]) and negatively (lipid) correlated with mammographic breast density, an intermediate marker of breast cancer risk. Adjusted multivariable linear regressions were used to examine the associations between hair product use (categorized as “never,” “before pregnancy only,” and “during pregnancy”) and BTC.ResultsMaternal hair relaxer use during pregnancy (versus never use) was associated with several BTC measures among daughters and mothers, whereas hair dye use during pregnancy (versus never use) was only associated with Oindex among mothers (Adjusted βmother for Oindex = − 0.38; 95% CI = − 0.70, − 0.05). Consistent associations were reported between hair relaxer use and BTC among daughters and mothers. Specifically, hair relaxer use during pregnancy (versus never use) was associated with lower lipid content (Adjusted βdaughter = − 0.36, 95% confidence interval (CI) = − 0.65, − 0.07; Adjusted βmother = − 0.35, 95% CI = − 0.65, − 0.04), higher collagen content (Adjusted βdaughter = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.64; Adjusted βmother = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.04, 0.63), and higher Oindex (Adjusted βdaughter = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.77; Adjusted βmother = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.21, 0.79).ConclusionsMaternal hair relaxer use during pregnancy was associated with BTC later in life for both mothers and daughters. The consistency of associations across generations suggest pregnancy and gestation are potentially sensitive periods for this exposure, capturing both maternal and prenatal influences on BTC with potential implications for breast cancer risk.

Introduction

Increasing incidence rates of early-onset breast cancer are reported in the United States (US) and globally [1]. Notably, women of color are more likely to be diagnosed with early-onset breast cancer and have higher rates of more aggressive breast cancer subtypes (e.g., triple negative) and mortality compared to white women [2]. Personal care products, including hair products, are hypothesized to be one source of environmental chemical exposure that may contribute to persistent inequities in these and other health outcomes [3, 4].

Personal care products are a well-documented source of exposure to chemicals of concern, including endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) and formaldehyde-releasing preservatives [3, 5–8]. Inequities in exposure are reported in the US, with Black and Hispanic women more commonly using products (such as leave-in conditioners and gels) with these chemicals of concern (e.g., phthalates and parabens), resulting in higher urinary concentrations compared to other racial/ethnic groups [5, 7, 9, 10]. Hair product use patterns differ by race/ethnicity based on hair texture, cultural use patterns, and upstream drivers including beauty standards, with Black women more commonly using chemical hair relaxers and Hispanic women more commonly using hair dyes compared to other racial/ethnic groups [4, 10–15].

A growing body of literature is examining the association between hair product use and hormonal-mediated cancers, including breast cancer [16–19]. The evidence suggests that certain hair product use (e.g., hair relaxers, dyes), may be associated with a greater risk of breast cancer [19, 20]. Several biological mechanisms are plausible, since EDCs have the ability to bind to cell receptors to alter hormonal pathways and can impact cancer cell migration and proliferation [21–23]. In particular, there is growing interest in the relationship between EDCs and mammographic breast density (MBD), which reflects the amount of fibroglandular tissue in the breast and is one of the strongest risk factors for breast cancer [24, 25].

A previous study in a population of non-Hispanic white women in Wisconsin demonstrated that higher serum levels of certain phthalate metabolites and phenols, specifically monoethyl phthalate and bisphenol-A, are associated with higher MBD in postmenopausal women [26]. In contrast, another study in New York examining self-reported hair product use during childhood and adulthood in relation to percent breast density and dense area among adult women found only weak associations [27]. The inconsistent findings may reflect differences in exposure assessment, as neither study evaluated exposures across the life course or specifically during critical windows such as pregnancy and the prenatal period.

Pregnancy and the prenatal period represent two critical windows of susceptibility, during which exposure to toxic chemicals in hair products may have lasting effects on breast tissue in both mothers and offspring. These periods are marked by rapid fetal development and substantial hormonal, functional, and structural changes to the breast in the mother [28]. In vivo studies among rodents support this biologic plausibility, demonstrating that prenatal exposure to certain EDCs can alter mammary gland development and morphology in the offspring [29]. However, human evidence remains limited. In particular, few studies have examined whether prenatal exposure to maternal hair product use during pregnancy influences breast tissue composition (BTC) during adolescence, another important developmental period [30, 31]. As a result, there is limited epidemiologic evidence examining hair product use during pregnancy and the association with BTC in mothers and their daughters later in life.

To address this gap, we examined the association between maternal hair product use during pregnancy (capturing two distinct windows of susceptibility for mother and daughter) and BTC, measured by optical spectroscopy (OS), one to two decades later in a prospective cohort of non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic mothers and their adolescent daughters.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the Columbia Center for Children’s Environmental Health (CCCEH) birth cohort, a prospective study with recruitment from 1998 to 2006 that aimed to examine the impacts of environmental exposures, notably air pollution, on children’s health outcomes among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic communities in Northern Manhattan and the Bronx in New York City [32–34]. Women in their third trimester of pregnancy, aged 18–35, and not active cigarette smokers were recruited from prenatal clinics at New York Presbyterian Hospital, Harlem Hospital, and satellite clinics. Additional information on the CCCEH cohort is reported elsewhere [32–34]. In total, 725 women were recruited and followed from pregnancy through their children’s early adolescence. In 2016, 216 of the 285 still-active mother-daughter pairs enrolled in a follow-up study through the Breast Cancer and the Environment Research Program (BCERP). Mother-son pairs were not eligible to participate. Mothers and daughters were aged 29–55 and 11–20, respectively, and when enrolled, were asked to attend at least one clinic visit. The clinic visit included interviewer-guided questionnaires (collecting information such as self-reported race and ethnicity), biospecimen collection, anthropometric measurements, and OS measurements. At the time of the OS measurement, the mothers were free of breast cancer. In total, 180 mothers and 186 daughters had complete data for this analysis. Written informed consent and teen assent were obtained from all mothers and daughters enrolled. This study was approved by Columbia University’s Institutional Review Board.

Measurement of hair product use

We used mothers’ self-reported hair product use collected during the third trimester of pregnancy via questionnaire. Mothers reported on their use of hair dyes, hair relaxers, and permanent solutions. For each product, participants reported in the following order whether they used it during pregnancy (yes or no), whether the exposure was direct exposure (i.e. Doing it yourself or direct contact with the substance), frequency of use (daily, 2–3/week, 1/week, 1/month, < 1/month, 1 or 2 times during pregnancy), exposure at work (i.e., occupational), trimester(s) of use, ever use (i.e., including before pregnancy), and date of last use.

For this analysis, we used these responses to create a three-level variable to reflect never use, use only before (but not during) pregnancy, and use during pregnancy. Due to low use patterns for permanent solutions (94–96% never use) and low response rates for the other exposure variables (e.g., direct exposure, trimester of use, frequency of use questions; 56–69% missing responses) among the study population, we focused our analysis on hair dye and relaxer use during or before pregnancy.

Measurement of breast tissue composition using optical spectroscopy

We used OS to measure BTC in mothers and daughters during the BCERP clinic visit. Briefly, OS is a minimally invasive, non-imaging method that quantifies the biochemical composition of breast tissue by capturing red and near-infrared light absorption by chromophores (650 to 1060 nm spectral range) [35–37]. It also captures the optical parameters related to light scattering power and amplitude, which reflect breast tissue anatomy, including connective tissue related to cell density and size. Spectra were collected for multiple source-detector distances placed at different locations, optically interrogating different but overlapping tissue volumes, covering up to 90% of the entire breast volume. A fitting algorithm, informed by Monte Carlo-based light propagation simulations (FullMonte) [38], was developed to estimate chromophore concentrations and light-scattering properties of breast tissue from measured optical spectra. For this analysis, we focused on the three chromophores that most strongly correlate with percent MBD (% water, % collagen, and % lipid) [35, 39]. Water and collagen are positively correlated with MBD, and lipid is negatively correlated with MBD. Furthermore, we calculated a combined optical index (Oindex) [(collagen*water*scattering power)/lipid] [36], which has been shown to be positively correlated with MBD. More information about the OS measures and methods to derive the chromophores among the CCCEH-BCERP cohort is presented elsewhere [39].

Statistical analysis

We used multivariable linear regression models to examine the associations between maternal use of hair dyes and hair relaxers (never, before pregnancy only, and during pregnancy use) and BTC composition (% water, % collagen, % lipid, and Oindex) in mothers and adolescent daughters, analyzed separately. BTC measures were modeled as continuous, log-transformed variables that were standardized to a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Models were adjusted for covariates or confounders selected a priori. Specifically, age at OS measurement, body mass index (BMI), and parity were to improve the precision of OS measurements, consistent with our prior studies. We also adjusted for maternal education as a potential confounder. For both mothers and daughters, we fit unadjusted and adjusted models. For mothers, the fully adjusted models included the following: age at OS measurement (continuous variable), BMI at OS measurement, educational attainment (less than high school, high school or GED, some college, bachelor’s degree or higher, missing), and number of live births (1, 2, 3, 4+, unknown). For daughters, the fully adjusted models included the following: age at OS measurement, BMI at OS measurement, and maternal education. Daughter’s age at OS measurement was modeled as a categorical variable (quartiles), based on previous research demonstrating a nonlinear relationship between age and BTC [39]. Additionally, we fit models adjusting for age at pregnancy and parity at pregnancy; however, the estimates were similar, and so these variables were dropped from the final model for parsimony. We also fit models including an interaction term between age and BMI to assess whether associations between hair product use and BTC differed by these factors, which are known to influence endogenous hormone levels; however, conclusions were the same, thus we present the models without the interaction term for model parsimony. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0).

Results

Study characteristics

The mean age at OS measurement was 16.1 years (standard deviation (SD = 2.4) and 42.0 years (SD = 5.5), for daughters and mothers, respectively (Table 1). The mean BMI at OS measurement was 25.6 kg/m2 (SD = 7.1) for daughters and 30.7 kg/m2 (SD = 7) for mothers. At the time of OS measurement, 86% of the girls were in Tanner Stage 4 or 5. Nearly half of mothers reported never using hair dyes and relaxers (42.2%, n = 76 for dyes and 44.4%, n = 80 for relaxers). During pregnancy, 46.7% (n = 84) reported hair relaxer use, and 36.7% (n = 66) reported hair dye use. Hair product use patterns differed by race/ethnicity (Fig. 1). Hispanic mothers more commonly used hair dyes (47.01% during pregnancy versus 17.46% during pregnancy among non-Hispanic Black mothers). In comparison, non-Hispanic Black mothers more commonly used hair relaxers (73.02% during pregnancy versus 32.48% during pregnancy among Hispanic mothers). In terms of OS measurements, the mean (standard deviation) lipid, water, collagen, and Oindex values in daughters were 37.4% (11.6%), 19.8% (6.4%), 25.6% (5.8%), and 0.5 (0.8), respectively. Among mothers, the mean (standard deviation) lipid, water, collagen, and Oindex values were 43.4% (11.6%), 16% (6.5%), 24.6% (6.2%), and 0.2 (0.8), respectively. OS measurements were correlated in daughter-mother pairs (n = 180) (Spearman correlation coefficients for lipid, water, and collagen: 0.47, 0.35, 0.19, respectively). Maternal hair product use and BTC among daughters

Maternal hair relaxer use during pregnancy versus never use was associated with lower lipid content in the breast tissue of daughters across models (Adjusted β: − 0.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): − 0.65, − 0.07) (Table 2). We found significant associations between maternal hair relaxer use before and during pregnancy versus never use with higher collagen content (Before pregnancy adjusted β: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.01, 1.08; During pregnancy- adjusted β: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.64). Additionally, maternal hair relaxer use, both before and during pregnancy versus never use, was associated with an increase in Oindex (Before pregnancy- adjusted β: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.05, 1.09; During pregnancy- adjusted β: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.77). Maternal hair relaxer use was not significantly associated with water content among daughters. No associations were found between maternal hair dye use, either during pregnancy or before pregnancy, and BTC in daughters.

Hair product use and BTC among mothers

Hair relaxer use before pregnancy and during pregnancy versus never use was associated with lower lipid content among mothers (Before pregnancy- adjusted β: − 0.72, 95% CI: − 1.25, − 0.18); During pregnancy- adjusted β: − 0.35, 95% CI: − 0.65, − 0.04) (Table 3). Hair relaxer use during pregnancy versus never use was also associated with higher collagen content (Adjusted β: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.63). Additionally, hair relaxer use before and during pregnancy versus never use was associated with higher Oindex (Before pregnancy- adjusted β: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.20, 1.21; During pregnancy- adjusted β: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.21, 0.79). Hair relaxer use was not significantly associated with water content; however, trends indicated a decrease in water content. Hair dye use during pregnancy versus never use was associated with lower Oindex (Adjusted β: − 0.38, 95% CI: − 0.70, − 0.05). No other associations were found between hair dye use and BTC measures among mothers.

Discussion

This study presents some of the first evidence of associations between maternal hair product use before or during pregnancy, and subsequent BTC measured approximately 10–15 years later among daughters and mothers. Notably, maternal hair relaxer use during pregnancy was associated with lower lipid content and higher collagen content in the daughters’ breast tissue. These findings support the hypothesis that the prenatal period is a window of heightened susceptibility, during which in utero exposure to environmental chemicals may be associated with long-term alteration in BTC. When examining the composite Oindex measure of BTC, which integrates individual tissue chromophores (water, collagen, and lipid) and accounts for tissue scattering properties, maternal hair relaxer use, both before and during pregnancy, was associated with higher Oindex in daughters. The association with pre-pregnancy use may suggest that exposures occurring before gestation may also be relevant, potentially reflecting longer-term biological consequences that extend into pregnancy and gestation. More mechanistic research is needed to understand pathways linking pre-pregnancy exposures to breast tissue composition later in life. Lower lipid content, higher collagen content, and higher Oindex are associated with higher MBD [35], one of the strongest predictors of breast cancer risk [24, 25]. Therefore, these findings may have implications for breast cancer risk. However, it remains unclear whether adolescent MBD is correlated with adult MBD [40], underscoring the need for longitudinal studies to determine whether these early-life alterations in BTC persist and influence later breast cancer risk.

Among mothers, hair relaxer use, both before and during pregnancy, was associated with lower lipid content. Higher collagen levels were also reported among mothers using hair relaxers during pregnancy. Additionally, hair relaxer use was associated with higher Oindex among mothers who reported using it before, but not during pregnancy, as well as among those who reported use during pregnancy. The patterns observed in BTC measurements were highly consistent between daughters and mothers, suggesting that exposure to hair relaxers during different windows of susceptibility (pregnancy and the prenatal period) [41] may impact BTC through similar mechanisms and have lasting impacts at different life stages (childhood/adolescence vs. adulthood). Further research is needed to fully explore the mechanisms by which hair relaxer use during pregnancy may impact breast tissue morphology and composition at different life stages.

No associations for maternal hair dye use, other than between pregnancy use and the mother’s Oindex, were found. While significant findings were reported mainly for hair relaxers, interestingly, the direction of the associations was different between hair dye and relaxer use during pregnancy. This finding may be driven by the chemical composition of these products, given their functional roles. Specifically, hair dyes can contain a variety of chemicals of concern, including aromatic amines (e.g., p-phenylenediamine), phenolic compounds (resorcinol), and EDCs [42]. In comparison, hair relaxers can contain sodium/calcium hydroxide, formaldehyde, and EDCs [6, 8]. Furthermore, these chemicals may interact with each other and be transformed by heat [43], resulting in potentially differing downstream biological signaling pathways. Given that we could not assess the chemical composition of the hair products used in the CCCEH-BCERP cohort, future research is needed to examine potential differences in pathways across larger study populations.

This study addresses a major gap in evidence by examining hair product use during the pregnancy window of susceptibility and its association with BTC across two generations. Some of our previous work reported by McDonald et al. aimed to examine the association between hair product use during childhood and adulthood with age at menarche and adult MBD in two New York City (NYC)-based cohorts [27]. While associations were reported for age at menarche, childhood hair product use was not associated with MBD and weak associations were reported for adult use of other hair products (Percent density: β: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.79; Dense area: β: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.78) [27]. This previous study did not specifically consider exposure during pregnancy. As supported by the findings of this analysis, if exposure to chemicals of concern occurs during sensitive windows, such as pregnancy or the prenatal period, they may impact BTC for both mothers and daughters. These findings add to our research among the BCERP-CCCEH cohort examining environmental exposures during pregnancy on BTC. We have previously reported significant additive interactions between exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and household tobacco smoke with higher water content and Oindex in daughters and mothers [39]. This current analysis reported significant associations for lipid content, collagen content, and Oindex, but not water, highlighting how different environmental chemicals may impact BTC in distinct ways.

This study has several strengths and limitations worth noting. Specifically, a key strength is our use of a prospective birth cohort to examine hair product use during pregnancy and BTC decades later among both mothers and daughters. Next, to our knowledge, this study is one of the first to examine the association between hair product use during pregnancy and different breast tissue chromophores using OS, a safe and minimally invasive method for measuring BTC. Measures of BTC were made without knowledge of previous exposures, and all exposures were measured well over a decade before measurement of BTC limiting the role of information and selection bias in these findings. Additionally, through this work, we examined hair dye and hair relaxer use—two personal care product types that have been examined in previous research in the context of reproductive cancers [18–20]. Finally, we conducted this analysis among a sample of Hispanic and Non-Hispanic Black women in NYC. Since Hispanic and Non-Hispanic Black women are high users of dyes and relaxers [10–12, 14], respectively, we were able to conduct this analysis among populations who are highly exposed to chemicals from these products.

In terms of limitations, we used self-reported hair product use data, which may be subject to exposure misclassification. However, hair product use patterns were collected prospectively during pregnancy, and the objective BTC outcomes were collected a decade later, reducing potential information bias. Thus, if there were misclassification, these results would be biased towards the null. Self-reported product use is also commonly used in epidemiological studies. Next, while we have data on hair product use patterns, we do not have the chemical composition of the products used by each participant. We were also unable to adjust for potentially important hormonal factors, such as time since the last menstrual period and hormonal contraceptive use, which may have reduced the precision of our BTC estimates. Additionally, we used OS as a measure of BTC, which provides related but specific information about breast density compared with mammograms, which are considered the gold standard [44]. We also conducted this study among a small analytic sample, and these findings should be replicated among larger study populations. Lastly, we did not have data on maternal hair product use post-pregnancy or on hair product use among daughters. These exposures may also influence BTC, particularly if they occurred during other sensitive windows of vulnerability (e.g., postpartum, puberty). Therefore, further research is needed to better understand how exposure patterns across the life course influence BTC across generations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides some of the first evidence that hair product use during pregnancy may be associated with BTC in both mothers and their daughters, supporting the hypothesis that pregnancy and the prenatal period are critical windows of susceptibility for breast development and potentially for breast cancer risk. These findings highlight the need for additional research on exposure to environmental chemicals during pregnancy among both mothers and daughters on breast cancer risk across the life course, notably for exposures and health outcomes where inequities persist. Furthermore, this research contributes to the growing body of literature reporting associations between personal care product use and adverse health outcomes, particularly in settings where health inequities persist. This work emphasizes the need to develop solutions (e.g., policy, retailer interventions) to ensure equitable access to safer hair products and improve health for populations at risk.
