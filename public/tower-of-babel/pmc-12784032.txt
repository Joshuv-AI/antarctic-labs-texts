Effects of a 12-week combined exercise-nutrition intervention on body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with normal weight obesity
Authors: Dasom Heo, Man-Gyoon Lee, Won-Sang Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose]This study investigated the effects of 12 weeks of nutrition education and combined exercise training on body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with normal weight obesity (NWO).[Methods]Twenty-six women with NWO in their 30s (BMI 18.5-22.9 kg/m2, body fat ≥ 30%) were randomly assigned to either a diet education and exercise training group (DE+TR; n = 13) or a diet education only group (DE; n = 13). The DE+TR group performed 80 minutes of combined exercise (40 min aerobic + 40 min resistance) three days per week for 12 weeks with nutrition education. The DE group received nutrition education only. Body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators were measured pre- and post-intervention using two-way repeated measures ANOVA.[Results]Body fat mass and body fat percentage decreased significantly in both groups, while fat-free mass increased only in the DE+TR group (p < .05). Physical fitness parameters (grip strength, sit-ups, flexibility, standing long jump, VO2max) and all 1-RM tests improved significantly only in the DE+TR group. Lifestyle disease indicators improved significantly in the DE+TR group: hypertension markers (heart rate, systolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, rate pressure product), diabetes markers (fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR), lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C), and atherosclerosis risk ratios all showed favorable changes (p < .05).[Conclusion]Twelve weeks of combined nutrition education and exercise training effectively improves body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with NWO, providing more comprehensive health benefits than nutrition education alone. From a public health perspective, these findings support the implementation of combined exercise-nutrition programs in community health centers and workplace wellness programs for women identified with NWO.Keywords: normal weight obesity, combined exercise, nutrition education, body composition, lifestyle diseases

INTRODUCTION

Due to urbanization and mechanization in modern civilization, the obese population is rapidly increasing with decreased physical activity and Westernized dietary habits. The World Health Organization reported that approximately 640 million adults aged 19 and over are obese worldwide [1]. In Korea, 38.3% of the total adult population was found to be obese, and the prevalence of obesity increases with age, with obesity prevalence continuing to increase every year [2].

Recently, the problem of normal weight obesity (NWO) has been highlighted among women in Korea. Based on the 2009-2010 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the prevalence of NWO among Korean women aged 20 and over with normal BMI was approximately 30%, indicating that one in three normal-weight women had NWO [3]. NWO is defined as having a normal body mass index (BMI < 25 kg/m2) but a body fat percentage that exceeds obesity criteria (BF ≥ 30%), and it frequently occurs in women aged 30 and over [4]. Women in their 30s are particularly vulnerable to developing NWO due to lifestyle changes associated with career demands, childbirth, and childcare responsibilities, which often lead to irregular exercise habits and dietary patterns.

This phenomenon is attributed to incorrect diet methods that focus only on external appearance among women who try to reduce weight simply by reducing dietary intake without exercise. This leads to long-term nutritional imbalance, lack of exercise, stress, and increased incidence of lifestyle diseases such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease [5].

In particular, NWO is characterized by decreased muscle mass and increased visceral fat. Visceral fat acts as an endocrine organ that secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, inducing chronic inflammation and increasing the incidence of various lifestyle diseases such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia [6]. Decreased muscle mass causes not only physical fitness decline [7] but also metabolic dysfunction including reduced glucose disposal capacity and decreased basal metabolic rate. These metabolic problems are closely associated with insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease, thereby increasing the incidence of lifestyle diseases [8,9].

Previous studies have shown that NWO had higher triglyceride (TG) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels compared to normal weight individuals [10]. Additionally, NWO showed higher blood pressure, lipid levels, and cardiovascular risk along with increased body fat, and the development of atherosclerosis increased due to high inflammation and lipid levels [4].

For effective prevention and management of obesity, pharmacotherapy, surgical therapy, diet therapy, and exercise therapy are applied. However, drug treatment and surgical therapy are not widely used due to risks and cost burdens [11,12]. Previous studies have reported that regular aerobic exercise and resistance exercise are very effective in eliminating obesity [13-16], and combined treatment of diet and exercise has also shown positive effects [17,18]. However, studies specifically examining the combined effects of exercise training and nutrition education in women with NWO remain limited, and there is a need to compare the additional benefits of exercise when combined with nutrition education versus nutrition education alone.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of 12 weeks of nutrition education and combined exercise training on body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with NWO in their 30s. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) compare the effectiveness of combined exercise-nutrition intervention versus nutrition education alone on body composition changes; (2) examine the effects of combined training on multiple physical fitness components; and (3) evaluate comprehensive improvements in lifestyle disease indicators including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and atherosclerosis risk markers.

METHODS

Participants

Twenty-six women with NWO in their 30s participated in this study. Participants had a BMI of 18.5-22.9 kg/m2 and body fat percentage of 30% or higher. They were randomly assigned to either the diet education and exercise training group (DE+TR, n = 13) or the diet education group (DE, n = 13) using a computer-generated random number sequence. An independent researcher who was not involved in the intervention performed the randomization process using block randomization with a block size of 4 to ensure balanced group allocation. All participants provided written informed consent before participation, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (KHGIRB-21-187).

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Based on a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 1-β = 0.80, and a medium effect size (f = 0.25), the minimum required sample size for a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was calculated to be 24 participants in total. Considering a potential dropout rate of 10%, 13 participants per group (total n = 26) were recruited.

Intervention Program

Diet Education and Exercise Training Group (DE+TR). The combined exercise training consisted of 80 minutes per session (40 min aerobic exercise + 40 min resistance exercise), performed 3 days per week for 12 weeks. Aerobic exercise involved walking/running on a treadmill with progressive intensity: weeks 1-4 at 50-55% heart rate reserve (HRR), weeks 5-8 at 55-60% HRR, and weeks 9-12 at 60-70% HRR. Resistance exercise included 8 exercises (chest press, lat pull down, shoulder press, bicep curl, tricep extension, leg press, leg curl, leg extension) with progressive loading: weeks 1-4 at 40-50% of 1-RM intensity with 15 repetitions for 3 sets; weeks 5-8 at 50-60% 1-RM with 12 repetitions for 3 sets; and weeks 9-12 at 60-70% 1-RM with 10 repetitions for 3 sets. The 1-RM was reassessed every 4 weeks to adjust exercise intensity. Nutrition education was provided concurrently as described below.

Diet Education Group (DE). This group received only nutrition education and was instructed to maintain their usual lifestyle during the same treatment period.

Nutrition Education Program. Both groups received nutrition education once per week (30 minutes per session) for 12 weeks. The education was conducted in small group sessions (5-7 participants) and consisted of three phases: weeks 1-4 focused on understanding macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) and their roles in health; weeks 5-8 covered portion control and meal planning strategies; weeks 9-12 addressed sustainable dietary habits and behavioral modification techniques. Compliance was monitored through attendance records (average attendance rate: 92% for both groups) and weekly food diaries, which were collected and reviewed by a registered dietitian.

Measurements

Body Composition. Body weight, body fat mass, body fat percentage, and fat-free mass were measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 770, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). Measurements were taken in the morning after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours, with participants wearing light clothing and removing all metal accessories.

Physical Fitness. Physical fitness tests included grip strength (TKK 5401, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), sit-ups (60 seconds), sit and reach, standing long jump, and maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max). VO₂max was assessed using a motorized treadmill (Case8000, GE marquette Co., UAS) with a metabolic gas analyzer (Quark CPET, COSMED, Rome, Italy). The Modified Bruce protocol was employed, with participants exercising until volitional exhaustion while meeting at least two of the following criteria: (a) a plateau in VO₂ despite increasing workload, (b) respiratory exchange ratio (RER) ≥ 1.10, and (c) heart rate within 10 beats/min of age-predicted maximum. One-repetition maximum (1-RM) tests were conducted for five exercises (chest press, lat pull down, leg press, leg curl, leg extension) using standard protocols.

Lifestyle Disease Indicators. Blood samples were collected after 12 hours of fasting. Hypertension indicators (heart rate, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], mean arterial pressure [MAP], rate pressure product [RPP]), diabetes indicators (fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR), hyperlipidemia indicators (total cholesterol [TC], triglyceride [TG], LDL-C, HDL-C), and atherosclerosis indicators (TC/HDL-C ratio, TG/HDL-C ratio, LDL-C/HDL-C ratio) were measured.

Statistical Analysis

Two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the interaction effects between group (DE+TR vs. DE) and time (pre vs. post). When significant interactions were found, post-hoc analyses were performed using paired t-tests for within-group comparisons (pre vs. post) and independent t-tests for between-group comparisons (DE+TR vs. DE). Bonferroni correction was applied to control for the increased risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. All data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0.

Changes in Physical Fitness

All physical fitness parameters improved significantly in the DE+TR group (p < .05). Left and right grip strength increased by approximately 15%, sit-ups improved from 18 to 26 repetitions, sit and reach improved from 14.2 to 17.8 cm, standing long jump increased from 152 to 168 cm, and VO₂max improved from 28.5 to 32.4 mL/kg/min. All 1-RM tests (chest press, lat pull down, leg press, leg curl, leg extension) increased significantly only in the DE+TR group. No significant changes were observed in the DE group for any physical fitness parameters.

Changes in Lifestyle Disease Indicators

Table 4 shows the changes in lifestyle disease indicators. In the DE+TR group, all measured lifestyle disease indicators improved significantly (p < .05). Hypertension indicators including heart rate (76 to 68 bpm), SBP (118 to 108 mmHg), MAP, and RPP decreased significantly. Diabetes indicators including fasting glucose (92 to 85 mg/dL), fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR improved significantly, indicating enhanced insulin sensitivity. Hyperlipidemia indicators showed improvements with TC decreasing from 195 to 178 mg/dL, TG from 145 to 112 mg/dL, LDL-C decreasing, and HDL-C increasing. Atherosclerosis indicators including TC/HDL-C ratio, TG/HDL-C ratio, and LDL-C/HDL-C ratio all improved significantly in the DE+TR group. The DE group showed minimal or no significant changes in these indicators.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the effects of 12 weeks of nutrition education and combined exercise training on body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with NWO. The findings demonstrate that combined exercise-nutrition intervention provides more comprehensive health benefits than nutrition education alone.

Body Composition

Both the DE+TR and DE groups experienced significant reductions in body fat mass and body fat percentage. Importantly, only the DE+TR group showed a significant increase in fat-free mass (approximately 2.6 kg, representing a 6.6% improvement). This differential response highlights the critical importance of exercise training in conjunction with nutritional intervention for optimal body composition changes in women with NWO. These findings are consistent with recent systematic reviews demonstrating that combined exercise and nutrition interventions are more effective than diet-only approaches for managing NWO [19,20].

The body fat reduction observed in the DE group warrants discussion. Analysis of food diaries revealed that both groups reduced their daily caloric intake by approximately 200-250 kcal following the nutrition education, with decreased consumption of processed foods and refined carbohydrates and increased intake of vegetables and protein-rich foods. These dietary modifications likely contributed to the fat loss observed in the DE group. However, without the mechanical stimulus provided by resistance exercise, the DE group failed to increase fat-free mass, underscoring a critical limitation of diet-only interventions [17,18].

The preservation and enhancement of fat-free mass in the DE+TR group has important implications for long-term metabolic health. Skeletal muscle is the primary site of glucose disposal and plays a crucial role in maintaining insulin sensitivity. The 2024 ACSM consensus statement emphasizes that adequate exercise volume is essential for optimal weight management [21], supporting our findings that combined exercise training provides superior outcomes compared to nutrition education alone [22-24].

Physical Fitness

The DE+TR group demonstrated significant improvements across all measured physical fitness parameters. The 13.7% improvement in VO₂max (28.5 to 32.4 mL/kg/min) is particularly noteworthy, representing a transition from “poor” to “fair” cardiorespiratory fitness classification. These comprehensive fitness improvements are consistent with previous research demonstrating the efficacy of combined training protocols in improving multiple fitness components [20,25].

The lack of fitness improvements in the DE group highlights the specificity principle of training adaptations—nutritional interventions alone cannot induce the physiological adaptations necessary for fitness enhancement. This finding emphasizes the importance of physical activity as an irreplaceable component of comprehensive health promotion programs for women with NWO.

Lifestyle Disease Indicators

The comprehensive improvement in lifestyle disease indicators in the DE+TR group is clinically significant. The reductions in resting heart rate, blood pressure, and rate pressure product indicate improved cardiovascular function. These cardiovascular improvements are particularly important for women with NWO, who often present with cardiovascular risk factors despite having a normal BMI [4,10].

The improvements in glucose metabolism markers (fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR) indicate enhanced insulin sensitivity, which is of critical importance as insulin resistance is a key feature of NWO. According to previous research, both aerobic and resistance exercise contribute to improved glucose disposal capacity [15,17], and our findings support the synergistic effects of combined training.

The favorable changes in lipid profiles, particularly the 22.8% reduction in triglycerides, indicate marked reduction in cardiovascular disease risk. The improvements in atherosclerotic risk ratios (TC/HDL-C, TG/HDL-C, LDL-C/HDL-C) are perhaps even more clinically relevant than individual lipid parameters, as these ratios are superior predictors of cardiovascular disease risk [4,10].

Twelve weeks of combined nutrition education and exercise training effectively improves body composition, physical fitness, and lifestyle disease indicators in women with NWO, providing more comprehensive health benefits than nutrition education alone. The main findings include: (1) improvement in body composition through decreased body fat and increased fat-free mass, (2) enhancement of all physical fitness components, and (3) improvement in lifestyle disease indicators related to hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and atherosclerosis.

From a public health perspective, these findings support the implementation of combined exercise-nutrition programs in community health centers and workplace wellness programs for women identified with NWO. Clinically, routine screening for NWO using body composition analysis, rather than relying solely on BMI, may help identify at-risk individuals who could benefit from early intervention. The accessible nature of the intervention (moderate-intensity exercise combined with basic nutrition education) makes it feasible for widespread implementation in both clinical and community settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 26) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, long-term follow-up was not conducted to verify the lasting effects after the 12-week intervention period. Third, dietary intake was assessed using self-reported food diaries, which may be subject to under- or over-reporting bias. Fourth, additional biomarkers related to lifestyle diseases, such as inflammatory markers and hormones, were not measured. Future studies should address these limitations by employing larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and objective dietary assessment methods.
