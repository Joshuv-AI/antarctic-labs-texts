1.
Introduction

1.1.
The model

We study flows of incompressible fluids and constant density with elastic properties governed by the incompressible generalised Navier-Stokes-Voigt (NSV) equations in a bounded domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, with its boundary denoted by
\partial\Omega
, during the time interval
[0,T]
, with
0<T<\infty
.
The momentum equation is assumed here to be perturbed by a relaxation term
\kappa\Delta{\partial_{t}\bm{v}}
which makes the problem posed by the following system of equations:

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} {\partial_{t}(\bm{v}-\kappa\Delta\bm{v})+\mathbf{div}\,(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v})+\nabla\pi-\nu\mathbf{div}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})}}\right)}&={\bm{f}},\quad\text{ in }Q_{T},\\
\mathbf{div}\,\bm{v}&=0,\quad\text{ in }Q_{T},\\
\bm{v}&=\bm{v}_{0},\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times\{0\},\\
\bm{v}&=\bm{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{T},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Gamma_{T}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
.
Here,
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
and
\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
are vector-valued functions,
\pi
is a scalar-valued function, whereas
\nu
and
\kappa
are positive constants.
In real-world applications, space dimensions of interest are
d=2
and
d=3
, and in that case
\bm{v}
accounts for the velocity field,
\pi
is the pressure (divided the constant density),

\bm{f}
represents an external forces field.
Greek letters
\nu
and
\kappa
stay for positive constants that, in the dimensions of physics interest, account for the kinematics viscosity and for the relaxation time, that is
the time required for a viscoelastic fluid relax from a deformed state back to its equilibrium configuration.
The power-law exponent
p
is a constant that characterizes the flow and is assumed to be such that
1<p<\infty
.
Capital letters stay for tensor-valued functions, in particular
\mathbf{I}
is the unit tensor and
\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, i.e.
\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla\bm{v}+(\nabla\bm{v})^{\top}}}\right)
.
The notation
{\partial_{t}\bm{v}}
stays, as usual, for the partial time derivative of
\bm{v}
.

Equation (
1.1
)
1
can be derived from the principle of conservation of momentum by considering the deviatoric part of the Cauchy stress tensor of the form

(1.2)

\bm{\tau}=2\nu\,|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}\,\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})+2\kappa\,\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}),

which represents a nonlinear viscous response with an additional linear elastic contribution.
When
\kappa=0
, this extra stress reduces to the pure power-law (Ostwald–de Waele) relation

(1.3)

\bm{\tau}=2\nu\bigl(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|\bigr)\,\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}),\qquad\nu(s)=\nu_{0}\,s^{p-2}\quad(p>1,\ \nu_{0}>0),

so that the apparent viscosity scales like
|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}
.
In this setting, shear-thinning (or pseudo-plastic) fluids correspond to exponents
1<p<2
, shear-thickening (or dilatant) fluids to
p>2
, whereas the special case
p=2
recovers the classical Navier-Stokes equations for Newtonian fluids.
When
p=2
and
\kappa>0
(with
\nu>0
) in the constitutive law (
1.2
),
the deviatoric stress tensor reflects a combined effect of
Newton’s law of viscosity and Hooke’s law of linear elasticity.
For this case, equation (
1.2
) generalizes the one-dimensional Kelvin-Voigt model to higher dimensions and has been successfully applied to describe the rheology of viscous fluids with elastic properties, such as polymer solutions and certain biological tissues
[
46
,
17
]
.
The classical one-dimensional Kelvin-Voigt model,

(1.4)

\tau=\nu\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\kappa\,\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x\,\partial t},

provides a simple constitutive relation for linear viscoelastic materials.
This model can be obtained either from the Boltzmann superposition principle or through mechanical analogs consisting of elastic springs and viscous dashpots
[
9
]
.
A natural power-law generalization of (
1.4
), accounting for non-Newtonian viscous fluids endowed with (linear) elastic properties, is given by

(1.5)

\tau=\nu\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}}}\right|^{p-2}\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\kappa\,\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x\,\partial t}.

In fluid mechanics, the choice of a constitutive equation
is largely directed by the specific application under consideration.
For describing the rheological behavior of many incompressible viscoelastic
fluids, equations of the type (
1.4
) and (
1.5
)
are widely regarded as a natural starting point
[
12
]
.
Since a great variety of stress–strain relations may be postulated,
it is customary to impose admissibility criteria in order to restrict
attention to physically meaningful models. For incompressible fluids,
Oldroyd
[
44
]
proposed that constitutive laws should be:
(a) frame-invariant, (b) deterministic, and (c) local. However,
the elastic tensor
\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
fails to be objective and thus
does not satisfy the principle of frame indifference.
To illustrate this, let
\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})^{\ast}
and

\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})^{\ast}
denote the representations of

\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
in another reference frame,
and let
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}(t)
be an arbitrary orthogonal tensor. Since

\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
is objective, we have

\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})^{\ast}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
.
Objectivity of
\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
would then require

\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})^{\ast}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}+\mathbf{Q}\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}+\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}^{\top},

so that

\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}+\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=\bm{0},

a condition not generally satisfied. To obtain an admissible model,
the partial time derivative in (
1.3
) can be replaced by
the convected time derivative
[
12
]

(1.6)

\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})^{\triangledown}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})-\Big(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\nabla\bm{v}+(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\nabla\bm{v})^{\top}\Big).

However, the PDEs obtained from constitutive relations involving
(
1.6
) are highly nonlinear and analytically difficult,
so certain simplifications are required to make a rigorous
mathematical study feasible.

1.2.
Literature review

The mathematical analysis of pure (
p\not=2
) power-law fluids was initiated by Ladyzhenskaya
[
33
,
34
]
, followed by Lions
[
40
]
.
In her celebrated papers
[
33
,
34
]
, Ladyzhenskaya proposed studying what she termed the modified Navier–Stokes equations, in which the deviatoric stress tensor is prescribed by (
1.3
). Models of this kind had already been investigated experimentally since the early twentieth century in connection with polymer melts, colloidal suspensions, and other non-Newtonian fluids (see e.g.
[
9
]
). By adopting this form of the stress–strain relation, Ladyzhenskaya naturally placed her mathematical framework in line with experimentally observed non-Newtonian behavior, even though her main goal was to establish solvability results rather than to develop a detailed physical theory.
In
[
33
,
34
]
, it was proved
the existence of 3d weak solutions for

p\geq\frac{11}{5},

and their uniqueness for

p\geq\frac{5}{2},

Subsequently, Lions
[
40
]
extended these results to the case of a general space dimension
d\geq 2
, and where the diffusion depends on the full velocity gradient
\nabla\bm{v}
in place of its symmetric part
\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
. He established the existence for

(1.7)

p\geq\frac{3d+2}{d+2},

and uniqueness for

p\geq\frac{d+2}{2}.

The existence results in
[
33
,
34
,
40
]
, in addition to employing Galerkin approximations combined with compactness argumnets and the theory of monotone operators, rely (directly or indirectly) on the continuous embedding

(1.8)

\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{H})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\frac{p(d+2)}{d}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\frac{p(d+2)}{d}}(\Omega)^{d}),

which ensures that
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}:\mathbf{D}(\bm{\varphi})
is bounded in
\mathbf{L}^{1}(Q_{T})

for all
\bm{v}
and
\bm{\varphi}
in the same function spaces whenever (
1.7
) holds.
See the next section for the definition of the function spaces appearing in (
1.8
).

More than forty years later, Zhikov
[
54
]
succeeded in improving the existence results
[
33
,
34
,
40
]
to the case of

p\geq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{3d}{d+2},\frac{d+\sqrt{3d^{2}+4d}}{d+2}}}\right\}.

The main idea of his proof consisted in a regularization of the deviatoric stress tensor followed by the application of the classical existence results
[
33
,
34
,
40
]
.
The passage to the limit in the regularized problem is justified using arguments from Measure Theory, which allow one to handle the weak convergence of nonlinear terms.
It should be stressed that this lower bound for
p
had already been obtained by Nečas and his collaborators
[
41
]
, although their analysis was carried out in the setting of periodic boundary conditions instead.

Even prior to Zhikov’s result, Wolf
[
51
]
established the existence of weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes system with a power-law viscosity for exponents

p>\frac{2(d+1)}{d+2}.

In his proof, Wolf employed a decomposition of the pressure into a regular (measurable) part and a singular part, which allowed him to handle the nonlinear convective term effectively. This was combined with the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
–truncation method, a technique that enables control of the nonlinearities and provides compactness necessary to pass to the limit in the weak formulation.

Finally, Diening et al.
[
21
]
extended and improved the existence result
[
51
]
to cover smaller values of the power-law exponent
p
, specifically

\frac{2d}{d+2}<p<2.

In this work, the main tool was the successful combination of the pressure decomposition technique
[
51
]
with the Lipschitz–truncation method, which provides strong control over approximate solutions. This approach enabled the authors to extend the existence theory of weak solutions to a wider range of power-law exponents, particularly in the shear-thinning case.

Concerning existence, the range

1<p<\frac{2d}{d+2},\quad\mbox{for}\ \ d\not=2,

remains out of reach with the techniques currently available.
The main difficulty lies in the lack of an appropriate Gelfand triple that would allow the application of the Aubin-Dubinskiĭ compactness lemma to extract strong convergence of approximate solutions.

With regard to uniqueness for the Navier-Stokes system with a power-law viscosity, the classical results of
[
33
,
34
,
40
]
have only been improved (for
d\geq 3
) up to the range

p\geq\frac{3d+2}{\,d+2\,}.

In particular, see the work by Du and Gunzburger
[
22
]
as well as the contributions by Nečas and his collaborators
[
41
]
, where refined energy methods were used to establish uniqueness of weak (or suitably regular) solutions.

The particular case of the momentum (
1.1
)
1
with
p=2
is usually referred to as the Navier-Stokes-Voigt (NSV) equations.
Over the past several decades, the corresponding problem and related models have attracted considerable attention in the mathematical literature due to their analytical tractability and relevance to applications.
The first rigorous study of existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for the NSV equations is due to Oskolkov
[
45
]
, who coined the name Kelvin-Voigt (initially misspelled Kelvin-Voight) for this type of regularized fluid model.
As noted by Zvyagin and Turbin
[
55
]
, neither Kelvin nor Voigt themselves proposed a constitutive stress–strain law or a complete set of governing equations for viscoelastic fluids.
Nevertheless, the name Navier-Stokes-Voigt has become widely accepted, since this model naturally extends the equations originally suggested by Voigt (for elastic media with relaxation effects) to encompass viscous fluids with viscoelastic characteristics.
From the viewpoint of applications, one of the earliest uses of this model appears to be due to Pavlovsky
[
46
]
, who employed a simplified form of the equations to describe weakly concentrated water–polymer mixtures.
More recently, Ebrahimi et al.
[
23
]
explored the two-dimensional NSV equations as a tool in numerical algorithms, including applications to image inpainting, illustrating the flexibility of the model beyond classical fluid dynamics.
Since the pioneering contributions of Oskolkov
[
45
]
, the NSV equations have been investigated extensively with respect to existence, uniqueness, regularity, and asymptotic properties of solutions.
A key analytical feature, first emphasized by Ladyzhenskaya
[
32
]
and later by Oskolkov himself, is that the additional Voigt term
-\kappa\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
acts as a regularizing mechanism for the Navier-Stokes system: the three-dimensional transient problem possesses a unique global weak solution.
Before the year 2000, most of the research on this model was carried out by the Russian School of Analysis (see the survey
[
55
]
and references therein).
In the following years, the NSV equations have been further examined by many authors in various directions.
Cao et al.
[
15
]
proposed the system as a physically motivated regularization (for small
\kappa
) of the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations for purposes of direct numerical simulation, both under periodic and no-slip Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Several works have addressed the long-time dynamics of solutions:
Titi and his collaborators
[
30
,
29
]
, and Coti Zelati and Gal
[
16
]
established existence and regularity of global attractors,
while Dou and Qin
[
47
]
and Garcia-Luengo et al.
[
26
]
analyzed uniform and pullback attractors.
Berselli and Bisconti
[
10
]
analyzed NSV structural stability under viscosity and relaxation variations, while Berselli and Spirito
[
11
]
constructed suitable weak solutions for the 3D Navier-Stokes equations via the Voigt approximation.
The decay rates of solutions in relation to the initial data have been characterized by Niche
[
43
]
and Zhao and Zhu
[
52
]
.
Connections to turbulence modeling have also been highlighted:
Titi and his collaborators
[
36
,
37
]
related the NSV equations to the Bardina turbulence models,
and similar observations were made by Lewandowski et al.
[
38
,
39
]
.
Damázio et al.
[
18
]
establish an
\mathrm{L}^{q}
–theory for the NSV equations in bounded domains, proving existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions, while Baranovskii
[
8
]
analyzes the NSV equations with position-dependent slip boundary conditions, addressing well-posedness and the impact of such boundaries.
Mohan
[
42
]
provided a comprehensive study of global solvability, uniqueness, asymptotic behavior, and certain control problems for the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes-Voigt equations with memory effects.
Antontsev et al.
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
studied NSV equations with variable density, proving existence, uniqueness, and regularity, including cases with unbounded density, and extended the analysis to long-time behavior, anisotropic viscosity, relaxation, and damping.
De Oliveira et al.
[
19
]
established the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions for the NSV equations with non-negative (possibly vanishing) density, providing detailed regularity and well-posedness results.
Zvyagin and Turbin
[
56
,
57
]
also established weak solvability for NSV equations with non-constant density, including finite-order models, even when the initial density is not strictly positive.

The power-law case, with the exponent
p
varying over the entire interval
(1,\infty)
, has been investigated by relatively few authors to date.
Antontsev et al.
[
2
,
3
,
7
]
studied the Navier-Stokes-Voigt equations incorporating a
p
–Laplacian or anisotropic diffusion (allowing the power-law exponent to differ along distinct spatial directions), as well as relaxation and damping terms, and provided a detailed analysis of existence, uniqueness, conditions for finite-time blow-up, and the large-time behavior of solutions.
In a stochastic setting, Kumar et al.
[
31
]
established existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for the generalised (power-law) stochastic Navier-Stokes-Voigt equations, thereby providing a rigorous mathematical framework for studying this model under random forcing and highlighting the impact of stochasticity on the solution dynamics.

1.3.
Objectives and novelties of the paper

The main goal of this article is to demonstrate the existence of weak solutions to the generalised Navier–Stokes–Voigt equations. The literature on this model appears to be limited, and to the best of our knowledge, the analysis presented here has not yet been explored.

•

As mentioned above, there are few works in the literature addressing power-law fluids
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
21
,
51
]
. In
[
51
]
, the authors studied power-law fluids and established existence results for the exponent
p\in\big(\frac{2(d+1)}{d+2},\infty\big)
using the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-truncation, while
[
21
]
extended these results to
p\in\big(\frac{2d}{d+2},\infty\big)
via Lipschitz-truncation.
In our analysis, the presence of the Voigt term
-\kappa\partial_{t}\Delta\bm{v}
allows us to establish existence and uniqueness of the generalised Navier–Stokes–Voigt equations for the optimal range
p\in\big(\frac{2d}{d+2},\infty\big)
using compactness and monotonicity arguments.

•

Another novelty of this article is that we do not employ the Helmholtz–Hodge projection, which would restrict our analysis to the case
p\in[2,\infty)
. Although the Helmholtz–Hodge projection is well-defined for
p\in(1,2)
, the analysis in this range is highly delicate and creates significant difficulties in controlling the convective term in the momentum equation (
1.1
)
1
. By avoiding the Helmholtz–Hodge projection, we are able to treat the full range
p>\frac{2d}{d+2}
directly, without encountering these technical obstacles.

(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)
is invertible, and, because
P^{n}
acts on a finite-dimensional space,
(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)^{-1}P^{n}
is bounded.
Therefore it is sufficient to estimate the right-hand side of (
4.16
) in
\mathrm{W}^{-s,2}(\Omega)^{d}
to control
\partial_{t}\bm{v}_{n}
.

The convective term can be estimated, for any

\bm{\phi}\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)^{d}
, as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\big\langle-\mathbf{div}\,(\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}),\bm{\phi}\big\rangle}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{v}_{n}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}_{n}\cdot\bm{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right|\leq\|\bm{v}_{n}\|_{2^{\ast}}\,\|\nabla\bm{v}_{n}\|_{p}\,\|\bm{\phi}\|_{2^{\ast}_{s}},

where
2^{\ast}=\frac{2d}{d-2}
(for
d>2
, or arbitrarily large if
d=2
) and
2^{\ast}_{s}=\frac{2d}{d-2s}
for
s<\frac{d}{2}
.
Let us focus on the case
d>2
, once for
d=2
the analysis is simpler.
According to (
4.2
),
s>1+\frac{d}{2}
, and thus we can take
2^{\ast}_{s}=\infty
. Then, Hölder’s inequality requires

\frac{d-2}{2d}+\frac{1}{p}<1\Leftrightarrow p>\frac{2d}{d+2}.

The viscous term
\nu\,\mathbf{div}\,(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}))
is naturally controlled in
\mathrm{W}^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)^{d}
and the same is true for the forcing term

\bm{f}
in view of assumption (
3.4
).

Hence, a simultaneous control of all terms is achieved by taking
s=p
.
As a result, for any dimension
d\geq 2
, it holds that

(4.17)

\partial_{t}\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;\mathrm{W}^{-s,2}(\Omega)^{d}).

4.3.
Passing to the limit as
n\to\infty

In view of (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), the Banach–Alaoglu theorem guarantees the existence of functions
\bm{v}
and
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
such that, for some subsequences (still denoted by the same indices), we have:

(4.18)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{n}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[n\to\infty]{\ast}\bm{v},\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V}),

(4.19)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{n}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[n\to\infty]{\ast}\bm{v},\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p}),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{n}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[n\to\infty]{}\bm{v},\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p}),

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{n})

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[n\to\infty]{}\bm{\mathcal{A}},\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)^{d\times d}),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{v}_{n}

Assuming
d>2
(since the case

) and (

\mathrm{W}^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{W}^{-1,r}(\Omega)^{d},\quad p>\frac{2d}{d+2},\quad r\leq\frac{2d}{d-2},\quad d\neq 2,

we may apply Lemma
2.2
-(2) with

,

,

, and

.
This yields the existence of a function

such that, for some subsequence (again not relabeled),

By the Riesz–Fischer theorem, there exists a subsequence, still denoted by the same index, such that

\bm{v}_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\bm{v},\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{T},

and consequently,

\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v},\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{T}.

Now, in view of (

\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}\xrightharpoonup[n\to\infty]{}\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v},\quad\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{\frac{\rho}{2}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\frac{\rho}{2}}(\Omega)^{d\times d}).

4.4.
Application of the Minty trick

Multiplying (
4.4
) by
c_{k}^{n}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(0,T)
, with
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and adding up the resulting equation from
k=1
to
n
, and integrating over
(0,T)
, one has

(4.27)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big({\partial_{t}\bm{v}_{n}}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{j}+\kappa\nabla{\partial_{t}\bm{v}_{n}}:\nabla\bm{\psi}_{j}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\nu\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi}_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}_{n}\otimes\bm{v}_{n}:\nabla\bm{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

for any function

\bm{\psi}_{j}(\bm{x},t)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}{c_{k}^{j}(t)}\bm{\psi}_{k}(x),\qquad\bm{\psi}_{k}\in\mathbf{X}^{j},\quad k\in\{1,\dots,j\},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

In particular, taking
\bm{\psi}_{j}=\bm{v}_{n}
in (
4.27
), we have

(4.28)

\begin{split}&\|\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|_{2}^{2}+2\nu\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\|\bm{v}_{0,n}\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{0,n}\|_{2}^{2}+2\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{v}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

On the other hand, using the convergence results (
4.18
)-(
4.22
) and (
4.26
), we can pass to the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
4.27
) so that

(4.29)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big({\partial_{t}\bm{v}}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{j}+\kappa\nabla{\partial_{t}\bm{v}}:\nabla\bm{\psi}_{j}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\nu\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{A}}:\nabla\bm{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}:\nabla\bm{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

for any such function
\bm{\psi}_{j}
.
Since the functions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\bm{\psi}_{j}}}\right\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are dense in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})
, we can put
\bm{\psi}_{j}=\bm{v}
in (
4.29
), which yields

(4.30)

\begin{split}&\|\bm{v}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}(T)\|_{2}^{2}+2\nu\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{A}}:\nabla\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\|\bm{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+2\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Now, we subtract (
4.30
) from (
4.28
), and thus we have

(4.31)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t-\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t=:\frac{1}{2\nu}\big(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\big),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle I_{1}:=\|\bm{v}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}(T)\|_{2}^{2}-\big(\|\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|_{2}^{2}\big),

\displaystyle I_{2}:=\|\bm{v}_{0,n}\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{0,n}\|_{2}^{2}-\big(\|\bm{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big),

\displaystyle I_{3}:=2\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\big(\bm{v}_{n}-\bm{v}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t.

By the monotonicity of the operator
\mathbf{A}(\bm{v})=|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
(see (
2.3
)), there holds

(4.32)

\begin{split}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t&\geq\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}-\bm{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

for any function
\bm{\psi}\in\overline{\mathrm{span}\{\bm{\psi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\psi}_{n}\}}
.
Then, combining (
4.31
) with (
4.32
), we have

(4.33)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}-\bm{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}):\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad-\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}}}\right).\end{split}

Using the convergence results (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), we can pass (
4.33
) to the limit, as
n\to\infty
, so that

(4.34)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}-\bm{\psi})\leq C\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}}}\right),\end{split}

for some positive constant
C=C(\nu)
.
By (
4.18
), the properties of
\limsup
and
\liminf
, and the lower semicontinuity of the norm, one has

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}I_{1}\leq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}I_{1}\leq{\|\bm{v}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}(T)\|_{2}^{2}}-\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\big(\|\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|^{2}_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}_{n}(T)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\leq 0.

By construction (see (
4.5
)-(
4.7
)),

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}I_{2}=0.

Due to assumption (
3.4
) and (
4.20
),

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}I_{3}=0.

The above convergences allow us to write (
4.34
) as follows,

(4.35)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{\psi})-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}-\bm{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\leq 0.\end{split}

Taking
\bm{\psi}=\bm{v}\pm\delta\bm{u}
, with
\delta>0
and
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})
, in (
4.35
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\mp\delta\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}\pm\delta\bm{u})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}\pm\delta\bm{u})-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{u})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\leq 0.\end{split}

Letting
\delta\longrightarrow 0
, we see that it must be

\mp\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})-\bm{\mathcal{A}}\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{u})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t=0,

for all
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})
.
Hence

(4.36)

\bm{\mathcal{A}}=|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})|^{p-2}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}),\quad\mbox{a.e. in}\ \ Q_{T}.

4.5.
Conclusion

We can see that (1)-(2) of Definition
3.1
are easily verified due to (
4.18
), (
4.20
) and (
4.22
).
On the other hand, multiplying (
4.4
) by
\psi\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(0,T)
, with
\psi(T)=0
, integrating over
(0,T)
, using integration by parts in time, using (
4.5
), and the convergence results (
4.7
), (
4.18
), (
4.21
), (
4.26
) (observing here (
3.8
) for the choice of
\rho
in (
3.7
)) and (
4.36
), we obtain (
3.2
) for any
\bm{\varphi}=\psi\bm{\psi}_{k}
, with
\bm{\psi}_{k}\in\mathbf{X}^{n}
,
\psi\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(0,T)
, and
\psi(T)=0
. Thus, by continuity and density, (
3.2
) holds true for any
\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}_{0,\operatorname{div}}(Q_{T})
.
Finally, since
\partial_{t}\bm{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{V})
and
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{V})
, we have

\bm{v}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\subset\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbf{V})
.
Consequently, the initial condition
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0}
, stated in (3) of Definition
3.1
, is meaningful.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.2
under assumption (
4.1
).

In the rest of this work, we focus on the most challenging case in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, that is,

(4.37)

\frac{2d}{d+2}<p<\frac{3d-4}{d}.

This intermediate range of the power-law exponent
p
is significantly more delicate because the embedding stated in (
3.7
) no longer holds.
As a consequence, additional analytical tools are required to handle the nonlinear terms. In particular, it becomes necessary to perform a refined decomposition of the pressure into multiple components in order to compensate for the loss of compactness and to secure the weak convergence properties essential to the existence argument.
The next section is devoted to developing the technical aspects of this pressure decomposition, which will play a crucial role in the construction of weak solutions for the case of
p
satisfying (
4.37
).

5.
Revisiting the pressure decomposition technique

In this section, we look at the pressure term that appears in our model and will decompose it so that each part matches a specific term in the momentum equation.
The time derivative (inertia) and Voigt terms are grouped into one part of the pressure, while the viscous and convective terms are linked to two other pressure parts that are similar but have different roles.
The key idea behind the following result was originally introduced in
[
51
]
and later used in
[
21
]
, building on the original insight.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{C}_{\rm{w}}([0,T];\mathbf{V})
, and, for
m_{1},\,m_{2}\in(1,\infty)
, let

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{G}_{1}\in\mathrm{L}^{m_{1}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{m_{1}}(\Omega)^{d\times d}),

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{G}_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{m_{2}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{m_{2}}(\Omega)^{d\times d}),\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \mathbf{div}\,\mathbfcal{G}_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{m_{2}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{m_{2}}(\Omega)^{d}).

Assume further that condition (
3.3
) holds.
In addition, suppose the following identity is satisfied,

(5.1)

\begin{split}&-\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t-\kappa\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bm{v}:\nabla\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\mathbfcal{G}_{1}+\mathbfcal{G}_{2}):\nabla\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t=0,\end{split}

for all
\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}_{0}(Q_{T})
with
\operatorname{div}\bm{\varphi}=0
.

Then, there exist
\pi_{1}\in\mathrm{L}^{m_{1}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{m_{1}}(\Omega))
and
\pi_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{m_{2}}(0,T;\mathrm{W}^{1,m_{2}}(\Omega))
, and there exists
\pi_{h}\in\mathrm{C}_{\rm{w}}([0,T];\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(\Omega))
such that

(5.2)

\begin{split}&-\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t-\kappa\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bm{v}:\nabla\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\mathbfcal{G}_{1}+\mathbfcal{G}_{2}):\nabla\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad=\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\pi_{1}+\pi_{2})\,\mathbf{div}\,\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\pi_{h}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad{+}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{v}(0)\cdot\bm{\varphi}(0)+\kappa\nabla\bm{v}(0):\nabla\bm{\varphi}(0)\big)\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

for all
\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}(Q_{T})
with
\operatorname{supp}(\bm{\varphi})\Subset\Omega\times[0,T)
.

In addition, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle-\Delta\pi_{h}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\pi_{h}(0)=0,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|\pi_{h}(t)\|_{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{h}\big(\|\bm{v}(t)-\bm{v}(0)\|_{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}(t)-\nabla\bm{v}(0)\|_{2}\big),

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi_{1}(t)\|_{m_{1}}^{m_{1}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbfcal{G}_{1}(t)\|_{m_{1}}^{m_{1}}\mathrm{d}t,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi_{2}(t)\|_{m_{2}}^{m_{2}}\,\mathrm{dt}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbfcal{G}_{2}(t)\|_{m_{2}}^{m_{2}}\mathrm{d}t,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\pi_{2}(t)\|_{m_{2}}^{m_{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\int_{0}^{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mathbfcal{G}_{2}(t)\|_{m_{2}}^{m_{2}}+\|\mathbf{div}\,\mathbfcal{G}_{2}(t)\|_{m_{2}}^{m_{2}}}}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

for some positive constants
C_{h}=C(d,\Omega)
and
C_{i}=C(m_{i},d,\Omega)
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

It should be noted that if we combine all the components of the decomposed pressure, we obtain

\displaystyle\pi(t)=\pi_{h}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\pi_{1}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\pi_{2}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

which implies that
\pi\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\gamma}(\Omega))
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The proof has been divided into several steps.

Step 1:

Let us define a bilinear form
\mathcal{Z}
on the domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{Z}):=\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}\subset\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\bm{v},\bm{u}):=\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\kappa\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bm{v}:\nabla\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where
\kappa>0
is the relaxation time constant in the Navier-Stokes-Voigt model.

We begin by observing that
\mathcal{Z}
is well-defined and bilinear on
\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
, since both the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-inner product and the (Frobenius) inner product of gradients are well-defined for functions in
\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Next, we consider the norm induced by this bilinear form, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\bm{v},\bm{v})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This defines a norm on
\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
equivalent to the standard
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}
-norm, due to the Poincaré inequality (which applies because of the zero boundary conditions). Hence,
\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
is complete with respect to the
\mathcal{Z}
-norm. This completeness implies that the bilinear form
\mathcal{Z}
is closed. Moreover, it follows immediately from its definition that
\mathcal{Z}
is a symmetric and positive form.

Given that
\mathcal{Z}
is a densely defined, closed, symmetric, and positive bilinear form on the Hilbert space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, we can apply
[
49
, Lemma II.3.2.1]
to conclude the existence of a unique positive self-adjoint operator
\mathrm{A}:\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, with dense domain
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})\subset\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{A}(\bm{v})\cdot\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\mathcal{Z}(\bm{v},\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\kappa\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bm{v}:\nabla\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

for all
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}),\ \bm{u}\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Fixing an arbitrary test function
\bm{u}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
, we observe that the relation

\mathrm{A}(\bm{v})=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)(\bm{v})=(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\mathbf{div}\,\nabla)(\bm{v})

holds in the sense of distributions. Based on this (formal) identity, we define the operator
\mathrm{A}:=\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta
. Thus, the operator
\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta:\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{I}-\kappa\Delta)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
is characterized by the relation

(5.8)

(6.67)

\displaystyle\frac{2d}{d+2}\leq r_{0}\leq\frac{d}{d-1},\quad{d\leq 4}.

Using integration by parts, Hölder’s and Korn’s inequalities, and still (
6.51
), (
6.52
) and (
6.55
) (to be more specific (
6.8
), (
6.20
) and (
6.31
)), we have

(6.68)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}-\varpi_{1,n,j}\mathbf{I}\big)\cdot\bm{w}_{n,j}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}-\varpi_{1,n,j}\mathbf{I}\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{w}_{n,j})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}(t)-\varpi_{1,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{p}^{p}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
&\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big(\|\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}(t)\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}+\|\varpi_{1,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}\big)\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{p}^{p}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
&<\infty.\end{split}

Using Hölder’s and Sobolev’s inequalities, together with (
6.51
), (
6.53
) and (
6.56
) (to be more specific (
6.8
), (
6.32
) and (
6.53
)), we obtain

(6.69)

\begin{split}&\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}-\varpi_{2,n,j}\mathbf{I}\big)\cdot\bm{w}_{n,j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\big\|\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}(t)-\varpi_{2,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\big)\big\|_{r_{0}}\|\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{r_{0}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big\|\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}(t)-\varpi_{2,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\big)\big\|_{r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r_{0}}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{2}^{r_{0}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r_{0}^{\prime}}},\quad d\leq 4,\\
&\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big\|\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}(t)-\varpi_{2,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\big)\big\|_{r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r_{0}}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{2}\\
&\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\big\|\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}(t)\|_{r_{0}}^{r_{0}}+\|\varpi_{2,n,j}(t)\mathbf{I}\big)\big\|_{r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\Big)\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r_{0}}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(t)\|_{2}\\
&<\infty.\end{split}

The application of the Minty trick in this case is stated in the following claim.

Claim 3
.

(6.70)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}).

Proof of Claim
3
.

Keep in mind that (
6.66
) holds for all
\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}(Q_{T})
with
\operatorname{supp}(\bm{\varphi})\Subset\Omega\times[0,T)
.
We can select our test functions
{\bm{\psi}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})
(the class of admissible solutions) by using a standard density argument (see (
6.67
)–(
6.69
)).
This indicates that there is a sequence of test functions
\bm{\varphi}_{m}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}(Q_{T})
with
\operatorname{supp}(\bm{\varphi}_{m})\Subset\Omega\times[0,T)
, such that
\bm{\varphi}_{m}
converges to
{\bm{\psi}}
, in

\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p})
.
Given this information, we test (
6.66
) with
\bm{w}_{n,j}
(defined in (
6.59
)) to get

(6.71)

\begin{split}&\|\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\bm{w}_{n,j}^{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}^{0}\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)+2\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}-\varpi_{1,n,j}\mathbf{I}\big)\cdot\bm{w}_{n,j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+2\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbfcal{H}_{2,n,j}-\varpi_{2,n,j}\mathbf{I}\big):\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=:\sum_{i=1}^{3}J_{i,n,j},\end{split}

where we have used (
6.59
)–(
6.60
).
Using (
6.4
) together with (
6.60
), we deduce that

(6.72)

J_{1,n,j}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}0.

By the strong convergence (
6.62
), we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{n,j}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}\mathbf{0},\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{r}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)^{d}),

for
r
satisfying (
6.36
).
Combining (
6.53
), (
6.56
), and the above convergence, we conclude that

(6.73)

J_{3,n,j}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}0.

From (
6.71
), we have

\begin{split}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}:\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t=&-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(J_{1,n,j}+J_{3,n,j}}}\right)\\
&+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varpi_{1,n,j}\cdot\bm{w}_{n,j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Using (
6.47
), (
6.58
) and (
6.64
)–(
6.65
), and performing integration by parts in the resulting second left-hand side term, we get

(6.74)

\begin{split}&\nu\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{j})\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}-\bm{v}_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t-\underbrace{\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\mathbfcal{H}_{1,n,j}\cdot(\nabla\varpi_{h,n}-\nabla\varpi_{h,j})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t}_{\mathscr{I}_{n,j}}\\
&=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla\bm{w}_{n,j}(T)\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)}_{\leq 0}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(J_{1,n,j}+J_{3,n,j}}}\right).\end{split}

According to (
6.72
) and (
6.73
),

J_{1,n,j}+J_{3,n,j}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}0.

A standard density argument combined with (
6.54
) lead to

\mathscr{I}_{n,j}\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}0.

More specifically, we use the strong convergence (see (
6.54
)) of the harmonic pressure term
\varpi_{h,n}
on compact subsets of
\Omega\times[0,T)
to approximate the solution by a sequence of test functions with support compactly embedded in
\Omega\times[0,T)
that converges to the solution.
Therefore, we have

(6.75)

\displaystyle\limsup_{j\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{j})\big):\big(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t\leq 0.

Employing the monotonicity of the nonlinear operator
\mathbf{A}(\cdot)
(see (
2.3
)) and (
6.75
), we find

(6.76)

\displaystyle\limsup_{j\to\infty}\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{j})\big):\big(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}t=0.

Now, set

\mathcal{F}_{n,j}:=\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{j})\big):\big(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j})\big).

Combining (
6.76
) with Fatou’s lemma, there exists a subsequence
\mathcal{F}_{n,j_{k}}
such that

\mathcal{F}_{n,j_{k}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0,\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda^{d+1}\text{-a.e. }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

Using the strict monotonicity of
\mathbf{A}(\cdot)
, we conclude that

\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j_{k}})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n}),\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda^{d+1}\text{-a.e. }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

Finally, for any
k,\ell
,

|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j_{k}})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j_{\ell}})|\leq|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j_{k}})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})|+|\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})-\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j_{\ell}})|,

and both terms on the right-hand side converge to zero
\lambda^{d+1}
-a.e.
Therefore,
\{\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence
\lambda^{d+1}
-almost everywhere.
Thus, the limit function exists
\lambda^{d+1}
-a.e. but has to be equal to
\mathbf{D}(\bm{v})
on account of (
6.51
), that is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}),\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda^{d+1}\text{-a.e. }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

We address the reader to
[
20
, Eqn. (1.6)]
or
[
24
]
for more details.

With the help of above facts, we validate the limit process and assure that

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}),\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda^{d+1}\text{-a.e. }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

The proof of Claim
3
is therefore over.
For a detailed proof of the approach used here, we address the reader to
[
51
, Appendix A]
or
[
14
, pp. 324]
).

∎

With the above result, the proof of Theorem
3.2
is complete.

7.
Uniqueness

The final task in this work is to prove Theorem
3.3
, which asserts the uniqueness of weak solutions to the problem (
1.1
).

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}
be two weak solutions to the problem (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
, with the same initial data
\bm{v}_{0}
and forcing term
\bm{f}
.
Define

\bm{w}:=\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2},

and note that
\mathbf{div}\,\bm{w}=0
in
Q_{T}
and
\bm{w}(0)=0
in
\Omega
.
Since
\bm{w}
is not admissible as a test function in (
3.2
), we introduce a mollification
\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}*\bm{w}
, where the convolution is taken with respect to the time variable.
In particular,
\rho_{\varepsilon}(t)
is supported in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.
Then
\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}([0,T];\mathbf{V}_{p})
,
\mathbf{div}\,\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}=0
,
\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,T)=0
for
\varepsilon
small enough, and

(7.1)

\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\bm{w},\quad\text{in}\quad\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{p}).

Using
\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}
as a test function in (
3.2
) and subtracting the two weak formulations gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\tau}\bm{w}\cdot\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau+\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\partial_{\tau}\bm{w}:\nabla\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau\

\displaystyle\ \ +\nu\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{1})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{2})\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{w}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}):\nabla\bm{w}^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau.

Passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, using for this purpose (
7.1
), this yields

(7.2)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\nabla\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{1})-\mathbf{A}(\bm{v}_{2})\big):\mathbf{D}(\bm{w})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}):\mathds{\nabla}\bm{w}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

Combining the identity

\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}=\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\bm{w}+\bm{w}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}

with the monotonicity of
\mathbf{A}(\cdot)
(see (
2.3
)), we obtain

(7.3)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\nabla\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\bm{w}:\mathds{\nabla}\bm{w}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\bm{w}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}:\mathds{\nabla}\bm{w}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\tau.\end{split}

Using integration by parts over
\Omega
, the facts that
\mathbf{div}\,\bm{v}_{1}=0
in
Q_{T}
and
\bm{v}_{1}=0
on
\partial\Omega
in the sense of trace, the first right-hand side term vanishes.
For the second right-hand side term, we use integration by parts, the fact that
\mathbf{div}\,\bm{w}=0
in
Q_{T}
, Hölder’s and Sobolev’s inequalities, and the fact that
\bm{v}_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{V})
.
After all, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\bm{w}\otimes\bm{v}_{2}:\nabla\bm{w}\mathrm{d}\xd\tau}}\right|&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\bm{w}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}_{2}\cdot\bm{w}\mathrm{d}\xd\tau}}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\bm{v}_{2}(\tau)\|_{2}\|\bm{w}(\tau)\|_{4}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\bm{w}(\tau)\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad d\leq 4,\end{split}

for some positive constant
C
.
Using this information in (
7.3
), we get

(7.4)

\|\nabla\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\bm{w}(\tau)\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau,

for a distinct positive constant
C
.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality to (
7.4
), we obtain

\|\nabla\bm{w}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\nabla\bm{w}(0)\|_{2}^{2}\exp(Ct),\ \text{ for all }\ \ t\in[0,T].

As
\bm{w}(0)=\bm{0}
, Sobolev’s inequality implies that
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{v}_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
and for all
t\in[0,T]
and hence the weak solution to the system (
1.1
) is unique.
∎
