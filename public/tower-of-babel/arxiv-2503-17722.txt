Investigation into the role of the Bessel function order in the Fourier-Bessel series and the Hankel Transform
Suketu P Patni, Vikram M Gadre

Abstract
This work focuses on estimating the number of terms of a Fourier-Bessel series of order p ′ p^{\prime} required to get within a certain error of a Bessel function of a fixed order p p where p ≠ p ′ p\neq p^{\prime} . Our approach consists of two steps: one, constructing an invariant over n n of the n th n^{\text{th}} order Hankel transform; and two, observing the effect of expanding a suitably scaled Bessel function of a fixed order p p in its Fourier-Bessel series of order p ′ p^{\prime} . We demonstrate a new error metric to simplify the error computations. Further, we generate an empirical model using numerical simulations and examine its capabilities in predicting the number of terms required.



I
Introduction and Applications

Real world signals need to be represented as deterministic signals to extract useful information from them. This can be done by expressing them as linear combination of orthogonal basis functions. Many practical signals like seismic, speech and image signals are highly redundant and require compact representation for economic storage and transmission bandwidth
[
1
]
. The frequency information in these signals is packed in fewer samples by a Fourier series representation than by time domain representation. In
[
2
]
it has been shown that the Fourier-Bessel representation is a good basis for expressing non-stationary signals, because:

1.

Non-stationary signals are represented in a better way using non-stationary bases
[
3
]
. Since Fourier-Bessel series use non-stationary Bessel functions as their basis set, it can better represent non-stationary signals.

2.

Wideband signals are more compactly represented as a Fourier-Bessel series expansion
[
4
]
.

Fourier-Bessel series use Bessel functions of the first kind in the same way that the Fourier series uses sinusoids and cosinusoids. For aperiodic signals, the Hankel transform presents an analog for the Fourier transform, using Bessel functions instead of phasors. The Hankel transform is equivalent to the Fourier transform written in hyperspherical coordinates
[
5
]
, so in multidimensional signals with radial symmetry, it has an obvious advantage.

Fourier-Bessel series have been applied to many signal processing problems. Gurgen and Chen
[
6
]
approximated noise in transmitted signals as a Fourier-Bessel series. This approximation could be subtracted from the signal to enhance speech synthesis, with the Fourier-Bessel series acting as a low pass filter. Suresh et al.
[
7
]
used Fourier-Bessel series in conjunction with fractional Fourier transforms to decompose data collected by radars to capture micro-Doppler target signatures. The micro-Doppler signatures could then be analyzed to estimate the motion of detected targets. He et al.
[
8
]
used Fourier-Bessel series for signal decomposition from various sources. The decomposition process helped to identify the instantaneous frequency of the sources, which is an important parameter to separate sources from each other and from the recording frequency. However, even very recent work on Fourier-Bessel series and Hankel Transforms like in
[
9
]
is largely silent on the role of Bessel function order in the series and the Transforms.

In this work we introduce a new metric to compute the error between a Bessel function and its Fourier-Bessel series. We provide code for numerical simulation of the behaviour of this error. We also construct a new invariant of the Hankel transform.

II
Notation and Basics

II-A

Bessel functions

The Bessel functions are an important class of functions satisfying Bessel’s differential equation

x^{2}\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+x\frac{dy}{dx}+(x^{2}-n^{2})y=0

(1)

where
n\in\mathbb{C}
is called the order of the Bessel function. The above equation allows for two canonical linearly independent solutions,
J_{n}(x)
and
Y_{n}(x)
. In this paper we deal exclusively with
J_{n}(x)
, called the
Bessel function of the first kind of order
n
. It is well-behaved and smooth (i.e. infinitely differentiable) over
\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}
.

II-B

Fourier-Bessel series expansion

Given a fixed order
\alpha
, our basis for the vector space
V
of smooth, real-valued functions
f
with domain
[0,R],R\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f(R)=0
over the field
(\mathbb{C},+,\cdot)
is the sequence

B=\left\{J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{\alpha,n}x}{R}\right)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

Throughout the paper,
u_{p,q}
will denote the
q^{\text{th}}
positive root of
J_{p}(x)
. How we express a vector
x(t)\in V
using this basis is given by its Fourier-Bessel series:-

x(t)=\sum\limits^{\infty}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{\alpha,n}t}{R}\right)

(2)

where

c_{n}=\frac{2\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits\limits^{R}_{0}rx(r)J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{\alpha,n}r}{R}\right)dr}{\left(RJ_{\alpha+1}(u_{\alpha,n})\right)^{2}}

(3)

This expression for
c_{n}
can be derived trivially by considering that the binary operation on
V
defined as

\langle f,g\rangle\coloneq\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits\limits^{R}_{0}xf(x)g(x)dx

(4)

is an inner product, and that

\displaystyle\left\langle J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{\alpha,m}x}{R}\right),J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{u_{\alpha,n}x}{R}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&m\neq n\\
\frac{R^{2}}{2}\left(J_{\alpha+1}(u_{\alpha,m})\right)^{2},&m=n\end{cases}

(5)

So
B
forms an orthogonal (albeit not orthonormal) basis over
V
(note that the above relation holds only for
\alpha>-1
, so we will enforce this condition hereafter).

II-C

Hankel Transform

Let
n\in[-1/2,\infty)
.The
n^{\text{th}}
order Hankel transform is the continuous analog for the Fourier-Bessel series, and is given by

H_{n}(f(r))=F_{n}(\alpha)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}f(r)J_{n}(\alpha r)rdr

(6)

for some function
f(r)
(provided that the integral exists), and its inverse is

H^{-1}_{n}(F_{n}(\alpha))=f(r)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}F_{n}(\alpha)J_{n}(\alpha r)\alpha d\alpha

(7)

III
The invariant

We try to get some arbitrary function
f(t)
in a similar form as the LHS of (
1
). So, in (
7
),

f^{\prime}(r)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\alpha^{2}F_{n}(\alpha)J^{\prime}_{n}(\alpha r)d\alpha

(8)

and

f^{\prime\prime}(r)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\alpha^{3}F_{n}(\alpha)J^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\alpha r)d\alpha

(9)

Scale and add as

r^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(r)+rf^{\prime}(r)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\alpha F_{n}(\alpha)(\alpha^{2}r^{2}J^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\alpha r)+\alpha rJ^{\prime}_{n}(\alpha r))d\alpha

But from (
1
),

\alpha^{2}r^{2}J^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\alpha r)+\alpha rJ^{\prime}_{n}(\alpha r)=(n^{2}-\alpha^{2}r^{2})J_{n}(\alpha r)

So

\displaystyle r^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(r)+rf^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\alpha F_{n}(\alpha)(n^{2}-\alpha^{2}r^{2})J_{n}(\alpha r)d\alpha

\displaystyle=n^{2}f(r)-r^{2}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\alpha^{3}F_{n}(\alpha)J_{n}(\alpha r)d\alpha

\displaystyle=n^{2}f(r)-r^{2}H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{2}F_{n}(\alpha))

(10)

Note that the LHS does not depend on
n
, but the RHS does. Consider that

\displaystyle n^{2}f(r)

\displaystyle-r^{2}H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{2}F_{n}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=(n+1)^{2}f(r)

\displaystyle-r^{2}H^{-1}_{n+1}(\alpha^{2}F_{n+1}(\alpha))

\Rightarrow\frac{f(r)}{r^{2}}=\frac{H^{-1}_{n+1}(\alpha^{2}F_{n+1}(\alpha))-H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{2}F_{n}(\alpha))}{2n+1}

(11)

The RHS of (
11
) is our invariant. For instance, say
f(r)=r^{s}
where
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. We have the following identity
[
10
]
:-

H_{n}(r^{s})=F_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{2^{s+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n+s+2)\right)}{\alpha^{s+2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-s)\right)}

(12)

and since we know from (
7
) that the Hankel transform is involutary,

H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{k})=\frac{2^{k+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n+k+2)\right)}{r^{k+2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-k)\right)}

(13)

A useful result is that

\displaystyle H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{2}F_{n}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=H^{-1}_{n}\left(\frac{2^{s+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n+s+2)\right)}{\alpha^{s}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-s)\right))}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{s+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n+s+2)\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-s)\right)}H^{-1}_{n}(\alpha^{-s})

\displaystyle=4r^{s-2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{s}{2}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{s}{2})}

\displaystyle=4r^{s-2}\frac{(n+s)}{2}\frac{(n-s)}{2}

\displaystyle=r^{s-2}(n^{2}-s^{2})

(14)

So the RHS of (
11
) is

\frac{r^{s-2}((n+1)^{2}-s^{2})-r^{s-2}(n^{2}-s^{2})}{2n+1}=\frac{r^{s}}{r^{2}}

Our invariant holds.

IV
Approximating Bessel functions

Consider
x(t)=J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)
, with domain
[0,R]
. Here
R\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Clearly,
x(t)\in V
, so, we may expand it in its Fourier-Bessel series in terms of
J_{p^{\prime}}
where
p^{\prime}\neq p
as in (
2
) as

x(t)=\sum\limits^{\infty}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)

(15)

Consider only the first
l
terms of this expansion i.e. consider the function

x_{l}(t)=\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)

Our objective is to try and determine the minimal
l\in\mathbb{N}
for which, given an
\epsilon>0
, the following inequality holds :-

||x(t)-x_{l}(t)||_{d}<\epsilon

where
d
is some norm.

V
A new norm

Let us see what happens if we use the usual
L^{m}
-norm. We need

||x(t)-x_{l}(t)||_{L^{m}}<\epsilon

\Rightarrow\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left|J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)-\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right|^{m}dt<\epsilon^{m}

For odd
m
we are not even able to remove the absolute value sign. Further for
m
even, say
m=2
, we require

\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)-\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)^{2}dt<\epsilon^{2}

(16)

The integral

\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)\right)^{2}dt

does not seem to yield itself to any nice expressions (although it can be expressed using hypergeometric functions
[
11
]
). However, if the integrand had just one more factor of
t
, we would have a nice expression for it (as given in (
5
)).

Theorem 1:
The function
||\cdot||_{L^{2^{\prime}}}
defined as

||x(t)||_{L^{2^{\prime}}}\coloneq\left(\int\limits^{R}_{0}(x(t))^{2}tdt\right)^{1/2}

is a norm over
V
.

Proof:
Recall that a norm must satisfy non-negativity and the triangle inequality and must respect scaling under the scalars of the vector field.
Non-negativity and scaling can be easily shown. For the triangle inequality, we need to show that

||x(t)||_{L_{2^{\prime}}}+||y(t)||_{L_{2^{\prime}}}\geq||x(t)+y(t)||_{L_{2^{\prime}}}

On squaring and expanding, we see that it is equivalent to showing that

\langle x(t),x(t)\rangle\langle y(t),y(t)\rangle\geq\langle x(t),y(t)\rangle^{2}

Notice that now we can directly use Cauchy-Schwarz, which states that for all vectors
u
,
v
of an inner product space,
\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle\geq\langle u,v\rangle^{2}
holds. And we have already shown in (
4
) that
V
is an inner product space.

VI
Using
L^{2^{\prime}}

Now we need

||x(t)-x_{l}(t)||_{L^{2^{\prime}}}<\epsilon

\Rightarrow\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)-\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)^{2}tdt<\epsilon^{2}

Consider only the LHS.

\displaystyle=\int\limits^{R}_{0}

\displaystyle\left(J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)\right)^{2}tdt+\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)^{2}tdt

\displaystyle-2\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)tdt

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\left(J_{p+1}(u_{p,q})\right)^{2}+\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}^{2}\frac{R^{2}}{2}\left(J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-2\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)tdt

using (
5
). Now consider only the last term.

\displaystyle\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}J_{p}\left(\frac{u_{p,q}t}{R}\right)J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}t}{R}\right)\right)tdt

\displaystyle=\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(rx(r)J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}r}{R}\right)\right)dr

\displaystyle=\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}c_{n}^{2}\frac{R^{2}}{2}\left(J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}\frac{R^{2}}{2}\left(J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{2}\frac{4\left(\int\limits^{R}_{0}\left(rx(r)J_{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{u_{p^{\prime},n}r}{R}\right)\right)dr\right)^{2}}{\left(RJ_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{4}}

\displaystyle=2R^{2}\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}\frac{1}{\left(J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{2}}\left(\int\limits^{1}_{0}J_{p}(u_{p,q}t)J_{p^{\prime}}(u_{p^{\prime},n}t)tdt\right)^{2}

where in the last step we substitute
t=\frac{r}{R}
. So the LHS turns out to be just

\displaystyle\frac{R^{2}}{2}\left(J_{p+1}(u_{p,q})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-2R^{2}\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}\frac{1}{\left(J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})\right)^{2}}\left(\int\limits^{1}_{0}J_{p}(u_{p,q}t)J_{p^{\prime}}(u_{p^{\prime},n}t)tdt\right)^{2}

Writing it out in full, given an
\epsilon>0
, our aim is to find the minimal positive integer
l
for which

\sum\limits^{l}_{n=1}\left(\frac{\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits\limits^{1}_{0}J_{p}(u_{p,q}t)J_{p^{\prime}}(u_{p^{\prime},n}t)tdt}{J_{p^{\prime}+1}(u_{p^{\prime},n})}\right)^{2}>\frac{\left(J_{p+1}(u_{p,q})\right)^{2}}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2R^{2}}

A small corollary is that the LHS is always positive, so if the RHS is negative i.e. if

\frac{(J_{p+1}(u_{p,q})^{2}}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2R^{2}}<0\text{ or }\frac{\epsilon}{R}>\frac{J_{p+1}(u_{p,q})}{\sqrt{2}}

then
l=1
works. One term itself is sufficient to get within the required error bound.

IX
Conclusion

Alongwith demonstrating the existence of an order-invariant for Hankel transform, we have conceived of a model to predict the number of terms required to get within a certain error of a Bessel function using a Fourier-Bessel series of different order. This shines light on the role of diversity in the Bessel functions. Further progress would involve explaining our simulation results analytically. In fact, one use case for the mentioned metric could be in computing fast approximations to signals in terms of the Bessel functions of the first kind, which would have applications in compression and transmission.
