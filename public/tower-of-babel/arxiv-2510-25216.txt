Abstract
This study investigates the origin of gas fueling secondary star formation, i.e., rejuvenation in nearby galaxies. From the MaNGA IFU survey, we use stellar absorption features D n 4000 and H δ A \delta_{A} to identify regions that started the rejuvenation within the last ∼ \sim 200 Myr. We compare the gas-phase metallicity, metallicity gradients, environments, and HI gas fractions of the rejuvenating galaxies (RJGs) to controlled star-forming and quiescent galaxy samples. We demonstrate that, for the majority of RJGs, the rejuvenating gas is originally in the galaxy rather than accreted gas. The evidence includes: (1) gas metallicities consistent with the mass-metallicity relation of SF galaxies; (2) metallicity gradients that are not flattened, arguing against radial inflows; (3) gas velocities in rejuvenating regions consistent with their surroundings, and (4) high HI gas fractions comparable to SF galaxies, indicating a pre-existing reservoir. Furthermore, we find no evidence that the rejuvenating events are triggered by tidal interactions with neighbors. While internal processes appear to dominate, we also present a clear example of rejuvenation triggered by gas accretion. The galaxy MaNGA 12080-12705 hosts a low-metallicity, kinematically distinct star-forming region in an overall old, massive galaxy, providing unambiguous evidence of an external origin, such as accretion or a minor merger. Our analysis demonstrates that using D n 4000 and EW(H δ A \delta_{A} ) provides a reliable way to identify current rejuvenation events in large spectroscopic surveys. The method will enable statistical studies to understand rejuvenation across cosmic time.

I
Introduction

Galaxies exhibit a bimodal distribution on color-color diagrams
(
Strateva et al. 2001
;
Baldry et al. 2004
)
, where blue galaxies are actively forming stars and quiescent galaxies have insignificant growth of in-situ star formation activities. Generally, galaxies evolve from being star-forming to quiescent, suggested by the increasing number density of quiescent galaxies with cosmic time
(
Ilbert et al. 2013
;
Muzzin et al. 2013
)
. However, not all galaxies follow this general trend at all time. Various observations show that some galaxies can have a secondary star formation after they once became quiescent
(
Kaviraj et al. 2007
;
Donas et al. 2007
;
Schawinski et al. 2007
;
Chauke et al. 2019
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
)
. Such revival of star formation activity is often referred as “rejuvenation”.

These rejuvenation galaxies indicate that the quiescent status can be a temporary state. At
z\simeq 0
, both cosmological hydrodynamical simulation and constraints from the stellar continua of galaxies suggest that
\sim 10\%
of galaxies had experienced at least one rejuvenation event
(
Nelson et al. 2018
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
)
. The unsolved question is: what is the origin of the gas that fuels this renewed star formation, and what physical processes trigger its collapse into new stars? On one hand, the star-forming gas could be obtained through accretion of gas from the circumgalactic or intergalactic medium or via mergers with smaller, gas-rich galaxies
(
Kaviraj et al. 2009
;
Mapelli et al. 2015
;
Diaz et al. 2018
;
Werle et al. 2020
;
Rathore et al. 2022
)
. On the other, rejuvenation can be fueled by pre-existing reservoir of gas within the galaxy that was not fully consumed
(
Thom et al. 2012
;
Woodrum et al. 2022
;
Paspaliaris et al. 2023
)
.

The gas-phase metallicity provides a powerful diagnostic to distinguish the origin of gas. Gas accreted from external sources is expected to be relatively metal-poor and should lead to a noticeable dilution of the gas-phase metallicity in the star-forming regions
(
Ceverino et al. 2016
;
Hwang et al. 2019
)
. The rejuvenating gas may also exhibit a distinct kinematics from the galaxy. On the contrary, if pre-existing gas is the fuel, the gas metallicity in the rejuvenation regions would not deviate much from the typical values. Furthermore, gravitational interaction with another galaxy can drive inflow of gas from the outskirts to center. The gas flow can elevate star formation and flatten the metallicity gradient at the same time
(
Rupke et al. 2010a
;
Bustamante et al. 2018
;
Moreno et al. 2019
)
. Probing the metallicity gradients of galaxies could serve a way to identify recent gas flows in galaxies.

In this work, we attempt to identify galaxies that are currently experiencing rejuvenation in the Mapping Nearby Galaxies at APO
(
Bundy et al. 2015
, MaNGA)
integral field unit (IFU) survey. The properties of these galaxies provide the best proxies to understand the mechanisms that drive rejuvenation. MaNGA provides precise measurements of stellar absorption features of galaxies, allowing us construct a clean sample of rejuvenation events in galaxies. We then can estimate gas-phase metallicities of each rejuvenation region, as well as the metallicity gradient of each host galaxy to investigate the origin of the rejuvenating gas. In addition, using the profound information in the IFU data, we will demonstrate that, as suggested by stellar population synthesis models, current rejuvenation events can be identified from only 2 strongest stellar absorption features, D
n
4000 and EW(H
\delta_{A}
)
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.

This paper is constructed as follows. We describe our data and sample selection in Section
II
. We present the properties of galaxies hosting rejuvenation regions in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, we show an unambiguous case that a rejuvenation event triggered by accretion is caught in the act. We then discuss the implication from our results in Section
V
and summarize this work in Section
VI
. Through out this work, we assume
H_{0}=70\text{ km s}^{-1}\text{Mpc}^{-1}
,
\Omega_{m}=0.3
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
.

II.3
The Rejuvenating Samples

Accurately recovering the true formation histories from observables is a challenging task, especially for galaxies with rising star formation rates, where the most recent star formation event can outshine older events. Therefore, instead of performing full-spectral fittings to identify regions with current rejuventation, we select recently rejuvenating regions using the equivalent widths of the H
\delta

\delta_{A}
)
(

and the 4000 Å break, D

n
4000 that is comparable to normal star-forming galaxies but relatively weak H
\delta
absorption due to the lack of A-type stars.

Figure
1
shows the distribution of EW(H
\delta_{A}
) and D

4000 of MaNGA spaxels. The majority of spaxels are located on a diagonal sequence. Younger, star-forming galaxies have low D
n
4000 and high EW(H
\delta_{A}

n
4000 and low EW(H
\delta_{A}
) region. The rejuvenation regions are located at the lower-left corner in Figure

Zhang et al. 2023
has demonstrated that combining D
n
4000 and EW(H
\delta_{A}
) is able to select a clean sample of current rejuvenation events. We first select spaxels that fulfill the following criteria:

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{n}4000<1.4

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{EW}(\mathrm{H}\delta_{A})<3\text{\AA}

(3b)

\displaystyle 10\times\mathrm{D}_{n}4000+\mathrm{EW}(\mathrm{H}\delta_{A})/\text{\AA}-16<0,

(3c)

and signal-to-noise rates (SNRs) larger than 3.

Then, we keep spaxels whose line emission is dominated by star-forming activities so that we can use emission line ratios to infer the gas metallcities (Section

(

We further require a minimum of 10 adjacent rejuvenation spaxels to be a solid detection of rejuvenating region. We identify 173 galaxies containing at least 1 candidate rejuvenation region.

Next, we visually inspect the spectra of these candidate rejuvenation region to exclude interlopers. We identify 23 regions whose spectra is dominated by QSO and broadline AGN which leads to incorrect spectral model in the DAP, 12 regions that are contaminated by foreground stars but not masked out; 5 M31 pointins, 3 globular clusters; 3 galaxy pairs with different redshifts; and 1 Type II supernova

3

Sobral et al. 2012

\delta_{A}

In this section, we first investigate whether rejuvenation is caused by in-falling gas from the outside galaxies by examining local gas metallicities and velocities. We then present the physical properties of RJGs to find out what kind fo galaxies that rejuvenation is likely to happen and investigate the possible physical mechanisms.

III.2
Properties of the Host Galaxies

III.2.1
Gas Metallicity Gradients

Radial transport of gas within a galaxy has the potential to induce star formation
(
Torrey et al. 2012
;
Moreno et al. 2019
)
. If gas flows from the galaxy’s outskirts toward its center, the metal-poor gas originating from the outskirts will dilute the metal-rich gas in the inner region, consequently leading to a flatter metallicity gradient across the galaxy
(
Rupke et al. 2010a
;
Rupke et al. 2010b
;
Kewley et al. 2010
;
Moreno et al. 2019
;
Bustamante et al. 2018
)
. Therefore, we compare the metallicity gradients of RJGs to those of controlled SF galaxies to seek for signs for radial transportation of gas.

The gas metallicity gradients of galaxies are estimated between 0.5 and 2 effective radius (
R_{e}
). We measure the median metallicity in each radial bin of 0.1
R_{e}
and perform an unweighted least-squares linear fit to obtain the gradient. Some galaxies do not have measurements over the full range of radii, we calculate their gradients from radii with available data. If a galaxy has less than 5 bins with validate data, we do not calculate the gradient.

Figure
6
shows the metallicity gradients in unit of
R_{e}
of RJGs (blue) and SFGs (gray). The errors of the gradients are estimated by bootstrapping. We find that the RJG and SFG samples have similar distributions of metallicity gradients. There is thus no clear supporting evidence for such a gas radial transport within the galaxies.

Figure 6:
Gas metallicity gradients of RJGs (blue) and SFGs (gray). Errorbars of metallicity gradients are estimated by bootstrapping. Blue and black dashed line represent medians of gas metallicity gradient in each stellar mass bin. The side-panels represent the probability density distributions of metallicity gradients. The metallicity gradient of RJGs are not flatter than those of SFGs.

III.2.2
Environmental Dependence

Gravitational interactions with neighbors may trigger star formation within galaxies
(
Kennicutt 1998
;
Woods & Geller 2007
;
Pearson et al. 2019
;
Pan et al. 2019
)
. Therefore, we investigate whether the rejuvenation is related to the environments they reside in.

We compare the tidal parameters,
Q
, and the 5th-nearest-neighbor projected densities (
\eta_{5}
) (see Section
II
) of RJGs to SFGs and QGs. To control the mass-dependence, for each RJGs, we select SFGs and QGs of similar masses (
\Delta\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})<0.2
) and calculate the distributions of
\Delta Q=Q_{\mathrm{RJG}}-Q_{\mathrm{control}}
and
\Delta\eta_{5}=\eta_{5,\mathrm{RJG}}-\eta_{5,\mathrm{control}}
. We then measure the medians of distributions and their uncertainties by bootstrapping.

Figure
7
presents the
\Delta Q
and
\Delta\eta
for the comparison of SFGs and QGs, respectively. The distributions of
\Delta Q
for SFGs and QGs center around zero and there is no clear mass dependence. On the other hand, the distribution of
\Delta\eta_{5}
for QGs are mostly negative and shows a mass dependence, where low-mass QGs are located at lower density regions than RJGs.

Overall, the RJGs neither experience a stronger tidal force from neighboring galaxies, nor are located at higher-density regions compared to QGs. The rejuvenation in our sample is thus unlikely triggered by gravitational interaction.

III.2.3
HI Fraction

By definition, the progenitors of rejuvenating galaxies are quiescent galaxies. Therefore, we compare whether our rejuvenating galaxies contain more gas than other quiescent galaxies.

Figure
8
plots the gas fraction
f_{\text{H{I}}}=\text{M}_{H{I}}/\text{M}_{*}
as a function of stellar masses for RJGs (blue) and comparison samples (gray) for which with HI measurements. Detections and non-detections are represented by dots and down arrows. For non-detections, we plot the 1
\sigma

We estimate the correlation between
f_{\text{H{I}}}
and stellar mass by performing a linear regression. To take the upper limits into account, we perform survival analysis
(
Stark et al. 2021
, Section 2.7)
. We use the
ATS
routine in
NADA2

(
Julian & Helsel 2021
)
package to estimate the correlation between
f_{\text{H{I}}}
and stellar mass. The
ATS
routine is based on the Akritas-Theil-Sen (ATS) estimator
(
Akritas et al. 1995
)
, a nonparametric method that extends Theil-Sen estimator to estimate the slope, intercept, and Kendall’s
\tau
in a linear regression when censored data (i.e., upper limits) points are present. The Kendall’s
\tau
is a correlation coefficient that used to quantify the statistical dependence between two variables. The
\tau
lies within the interval
(-1,1)
:
\tau=0
means no statistical correlation, while
\tau=1,-1
means perfect correlation and anti-correlation.

Figure
8
shows that the HI fractions of RJGs are significantly higher than the ATS fit of QGs, and are comparable to that of SFGs of the same stellar masses. RJGs are intrinsically gas-rich systems.

In summary, from the metallicity gradient and environments of RJGs, we do not find supporting evidence that the rejuvenating regions are induced by infalling gas from outside galaxies or triggered by interaction with neighboring galaxies. The high HI fractions hint that the gas was originally in RJGs.

IV
A galaxy in rejuvenation

In Section 3, we show that the gas metallicities of the rejuvenating regions and star-forming regions in RJGs are similar to those in normal SFGs. In addition, the gas velocities of rejuvenation regions are similar to their surrounding areas. Overall, we find no supporting evidence to support that the rejuvenation is triggered by gas infalling from outside of galaxies.

However, in Figure
4
, there is a small fraction of spaxels showing lower gas metallicities, deviating from the average
\Sigma_{*}-Z
relation. These low-metallicity regions may indicate that gas infall is responsible for the rejuvenation in a minor fraction of galaxies. We thus further inspect galaxies that host low-metallicity regions. We find an unambiguous case that the rejuvenation of a massive galaxy triggered by an infalling gaseous component is caught in act.

IV.1

IV.2
Stellar mass and star-formation rate

We take the
modelMag
in 5 SDSS bands and the spectroscopic redshift for each component, and use
kcorrect
version 5.0.0
(

to derive the stellar mass of
10^{10.85}M_{\odot}
and
10^{9.0}M_{\odot}
for the main component and the emission-line component, respectively. The stellar mass of the main component we derived is consistent with the stellar masses categorized in the NASA Sloan Atlas (NSA) at
\sim 0.1
dex level.

We then estimate the star-formation rate of the emission-line component from its H
\alpha
luminosity and correct for the dust attenuation using the Balmer decrement, following the prescription in
Brinchmann et al. 2004
. It it non-trivial to cleanly separate the line emission from the II region and LINER-like emission from old stars, therefore, we estimate two SFRs from two different apertures. First, we use a circular aperture centered at the emission-line component with a radius of 4”, i.e., the purple circle in Figure
9
a. The second estimate uses the line fluxes integrating over the entire galaxy
(
Wu 2021
)
. We take these two values as the lower and upper limits of our SFR estimates.

We thus obtain an
\mathrm{SFR}_{H\alpha}=2.7-3.6\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}
yr
-1
. Comparing to the star-formation main sequence obtained by the same prescription for stellar mass and SFR,
\log(\mathrm{SFR}/[\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\mbox{yr}^{-1}])=0.76\times\log(\mathrm{M_{*}}/M_{\odot})-7.64

(
Renzini & Peng 2015
)
, the SFR of the emission-line component is
\sim 20
times higher than typical SFGs of similar stellar masses. Assuming the current SFR has sustained in the past, the emission-line component has formed all its stellar mass within only
\sim 300
Myrs.

IV.4
MaNGA 12080-12705 as a rejuvenating galaxy

From the distinct kinematics and the low metallicity of the emission-line component, we conclude that MaNGA 12080-12705 is an old, massive galaxy experiencing rejuvenation triggered by infalling gas or a dwarf galaxy.

We stack the spectra from individual spaxels over the entire IFU plate to mimic the kind of data obtained by fiber or slit spectroscopy for distant galaxies
(
Wu 2021
)
. We measure
\mathrm{D_{n}4000=1.29}
and
\mathrm{EW(H\delta_{A})=1.24}
, which place MaNGA 12080-12705 in the rejuvenation corner (Figure
9
b). The integrated stellar mass (
\log(M_{*}/\mathrm{M_{\odot}})=10.85
) and SFR (
\sim 3\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\mbox{yr}^{-1}
) put MaNGA 12080-12705 well in the star-forming main sequence
(
Renzini & Peng 2015
)
.

Therefore, if MaNGA 12080-12705 were at a larger cosmological distance, our criteria will be able to identify its current rejuvenation event and distinguish it from other normal star-forming galaxies. Therefore, this kind of rejuvenation event, even though rare, should be able to identified in existing and future large scale deep spectroscopic surveys across redshifts, and help us understand the mechanisms (see discussion in Section
V
).

V
Discussion

V.1
Origin of Rejuvenating Gas

In Section
III
, we show that the gas metallicities of RJGs are not systematically lower than those in the controlled SFGs. In addition, the gas velocities in rejuvenating regions are comparable to their surroundings. These results together suggest that, for the majority of RJGs, the rejuvenating gas is inherent to the galaxies themselves rather than accreted from the intergalactic or circumgalactic medium.

The metallicity gradients of RJGs are comparable to SFGs. We thus infer that the rejuvenating gas is unlikely to be undergoing an inward transportation and likely has been at the location where star formation happens. Moreover, the environments of RJGs also suggest that rejuvenation is unlikely triggered by gravitational interaction with neighboring galaxies; we do not find that RJGs suffer stronger tidal influence or are located at higher-density regions than other galaxies. We thus conclude that these local rejuvenation is fueled by gas that was originally in gas-rich galaxies and triggered by internal mechanisms.

While most local rejuvenation events are internal events, external origin is not ruled out. A small fraction of abnormally low metallicity regions exist (Figure
4
), which could be fueled by gas accretion
(
Hwang et al. 2019
)
. An unambiguous case is MaNGA 12080-12705, where the low-metallicity SF region also exhibits as a distinct kinematic component, indicating the infalling origin of the rejuvenation event.

V.2
Toward understanding the origin of rejuvenation across cosmic time

Previous studies using full-spectral fitting claims rejuvenation events happen in galaxies by identifying a second peak in the star formation histories
(
Chauke et al. 2019
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
)
. The full-spectral fitting does not differentiate the mass gained from mergers or in-situ star formation. The stellar masses in the second peak can be brought by a dry merger event with a galaxy of an younger stellar population. Furthermore, these rejuvenation events happened in the past. There is little we can know about the mechanisms that trigger the rejuvenation from those galaxies. In concept, full-spectral fitting is able to identify galaxies with current rejuvenation events. However, in practice, quantifying the SFHs of SFGs are less reliable than QGs. Moreover, the qualities of spectra, such as S/N, wavelength coverage, spectral resolution, affect the precision and accuracy of the SFHs and the dependence is not understood.

MaNGA 12080-12705 demonstrates an alternative and viable method to catch rejuvenation events in action. Following the theoretical work of
Zhang et al. 2023
, we present that current rejuvenation can be identified by just two of the most prominent stellar absorption features, D
n
4000 and EW(H
\delta_{A}
). Current and future large-scale spectroscopic surveys such as WEAVE-StePS, LEGA-C, PFS SSP, and MOONRISE
(
Iovino et al. 2023
;
van der Wel et al. 2021
;
Greene et al. 2022
;
Maiolino et al. 2020
)
will provide a vast amount of high-resolution, high-S/N, rest-frame optical spectra of galaxies from the nearby Universe to
z>2
. The proximity in wavelength of D
n
4000 and H
\delta_{A}
further makes them an efficient combination; they are likely both in the wavelength coverage of the same spectrum. A large number of rejuvenating galaxies can be expected to found in these surveys for statistical studies.

Taking MaNGA 12080-12705 as an example, the intense local star formation triggered by infalling gas is easily identified by the morphology and spatially-resolved broadband SED. Beyond the nearby Universe, Euclid
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025
)
and Roman Space Telescope
(
Akeson et al. 2019
)
will provide wide-field high-resolution images in multiple wavelengths. The synergy of ground-based spectroscopic surveys and space-based imaging surveys will enable a systemic, statistic investigation of mechanisms that drive rejuvenation across cosmic time.

V.3
Caveats and Challenges

Here, we point out a few caveats and challenges in our analysis. Firstly, we exclude regions where the spectra exhibit broad-line or where the AGN continua are strong. In these regions, the absorption lines indices measured by MaNGA DAP do not represent the underlying stellar populations thus are excluded. Because they are AGN-related, we always find them at the center. However, in the scenario that galaxy interaction triggers gas inflow and star formation, the strongest star formation is expected to be at the galaxy center
(
Kewley et al. 2006a
)
. If the gas fueling AGN could at the same time triggers central rejuvenation, our analysis will not pick up this kind of rejuvenation events. In principle, simultaneously modeling the AGN and stellar continuum can help recover the underlying stellar population
(
Sexton et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, but the accuracy and precision depend on the fractional contribution from AGN emission. In this work, we choose to adopt the measurements from MaNGA DAP for all galaxies for internal consistency. Studies for central rejuvenation regions will need a special and focused attention.

Secondly, our selection can miss intense rejuvenation events. Our illustrative simple model SFHs show that the sSFRs of rejuvenation events selected by our criteria are comparable to normal star-forming regions (Section
II
).
Zhang et al. 2023
arrived at qualitatively the same conclusion after examining a more comprehensive library of SFHs. This is a natural consequence; if the sSFR is high enough, the light from older stellar populations is overshined by the newly-formed stars. In the D
n
4000-EW(H
\delta
) parameter space, the rejuvenating regions are indistinguishable from normal star-forming regions. Including more information in the spectrum can help
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, but this issue is fundamentally rooted in the evolution of stellar population and will always be difficult cases for observations.

More HI data should provide better information on rejuvenation. In this work, we investigate global HI fraction in Section
III.2.3
and conclude that the RJGs are HI-rich. However, we identify local rejuvenation events, the global HI masses are not representative for the local gas properties. Spatially-resolved HI surveys, such as WALLABY
(
Koribalski et al. 2020
)
, may help us better address the spatial distribution of HI gas within the RJGs.

VI
Summary and Conclusion

From the MaNGA survey, we identify 110 galaxies that host rejuvenating regions, referred as RJGs, based on two stellar absorption features, D
n
4000 and EW(H
\delta_{A}
). These rejuvenation regions are likely undergoing a recent secondary star formation event within the last
\sim 200
Myr.

We analyze the gas-phase metallicities, metallicity gradients, environments, and HI gas fractions of RJGs and compare them to controlled samples of star-forming and quiescent galaxies. We find the following properties of RJGs.

•

Gas metallicity in RJGs is overall similar to controlled SFGs.

•

The metallicity gradients of the RJGs are not shallower than controlled SFGs.

•

The gas velocity in rejuvenating regions shows no obvious difference from that in their surrounding areas.

•

RJGs experience similar strength of tidal force from neighboring galaxies to controlled SFGs and QGs, but are located at lower-density regions than QGs when the environment is quantified as the projected 5th-nearest-neighbor density.

•

RJGs have higher HI fraction than that of other QGs, and are comparable to SFGs.

We thus conclude that the local rejuvenation events are fueled mostly by gas originally in galaxies rather than being brought from accretion. Furthermore, the rejuvenation is unlikely triggered by interaction with neighbors.

Meanwhile, gas accretion is still a viable mechanism. MaNGA 12080-12705 presents an unambiguous case of rejuvenation triggered by infalling gas or a dwarf galaxy onto a massive (
\log(M_{*}/\mathrm{M_{\odot}})=10.85
), old, quiescent galaxy. The infalling nature of the emission-line component in the galaxy is evident by the abnormally low gas metallicity and distinct kinematics. The intense and localized star formation puts MaNGA 12080-12705 in the star-forming main sequence. If MaNGA 12080-12705 were at a large cosmological distance and observed by a fiber or slit spectrograph, the D
n
4000 and EW(H
\delta_{A}
) will identify it as a rejuvenating galaxy and distinguish it from other star-forming galaxies.

The simplicity of the selection method makes it easily applicable to several existing and coming large-scale spectroscopic surveys. A large number of rejuvenating events should be captured by various survey data sets across a wide range of redshifts. Combing data from ground-based spectroscopic and space-based imaging surveys, we are toward understanding the mechanisms driving galaxy rejuvenation from the nearby Universe to the cosmic noon.
