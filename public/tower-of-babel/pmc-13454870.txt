Gastric Outlet Obstruction from Brunner Gland Hyperplasia Mimicking Malignancy: A Case Report
Authors: Khadar Hassan Ibrahim, Abdukadir Mohamed Hassan, Fathi Yasin Yusuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric outlet obstruction (GOO) is a clinical syndrome caused by mechanical narrowing at the distal stomach, pylorus, or proximal duodenum. Although malignancy remains an important concern in adults, rare benign duodenal lesions may produce a similar clinical and radiologic picture.Case PresentationA 55-year-old man presented with an approximately 8-month history of recurrent upper abdominal pain, postprandial vomiting, early satiety, epigastric fullness, and progressive unquantified weight loss. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed a lower esophageal mucosal break greater than 5 mm, retained gastric contents despite fasting, an apparently open pylorus, and inability to advance the endoscope into the second part of the duodenum because the patient began vomiting; complete visualization and biopsy were therefore not feasible. Laboratory investigations demonstrated hypernatremia followed by worsening creatinine and hypokalemia, consistent with dehydration-associated prerenal kidney injury. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed marked gastric dilatation with retained intragastric contents and distal gastric/pyloroduodenal narrowing, without a discrete mass or obvious lymphadenopathy on the available images. Because malignancy could not be excluded and endoscopic tissue diagnosis or dilation was not feasible, exploratory laparotomy with distal gastrectomy was performed. Histopathology localized the diagnostic abnormality to the duodenal mucosa at the distal/pyloroduodenal end of the resected specimen and showed chronic duodenitis with Brunner gland hyperplasia, fibrosis, muscle hypertrophy on the gastric side, and no atypia or malignancy. The patient recovered uneventfully and remained clinically well at the available short-term postoperative follow-up; the exact duration of follow-up was not documented.ConclusionThis case highlights Brunner gland hyperplasia as a rare benign cause of GOO that can closely mimic malignancy, particularly when endoscopic access and preoperative tissue diagnosis are limited. Integration of endoscopic, radiologic, operative, and histopathologic findings is essential to guide timely management.

Introduction

Gastric outlet obstruction is a syndrome of impaired gastric emptying caused by mechanical narrowing at the distal stomach, pylorus, or proximal duodenum. Patients commonly present with nausea, recurrent vomiting, early satiety, epigastric fullness, abdominal pain, dehydration, and progressive weight loss.1,2

The causes of GOO in adults include both benign and malignant conditions. Historically, peptic ulcer disease was a leading benign cause, whereas contemporary adult presentations require careful exclusion of gastric, pancreatic, periampullary, duodenal, and metastatic malignancy. Diagnosis generally relies on clinical suspicion supported by upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and cross-sectional imaging.1–3

Brunner glands are mucus-secreting glands located predominantly in the proximal duodenum. Brunner gland hyperplasia is an uncommon benign proliferative lesion and is usually asymptomatic; however, larger or strategically located lesions may produce bleeding, duodenal narrowing, or GOO. Reported frequency varies because hyperplasia, hamartoma, and adenoma are sometimes defined differently, but symptomatic Brunner gland lesions are rare and have been estimated to account for a small minority of benign duodenal tumors, with very low incidence in large series.4–6

This case is clinically notable because the patient developed severe chronic obstruction with metabolic derangement despite the absence of a discrete mass lesion on available imaging and operative assessment. It also illustrates the diagnostic uncertainty that can arise in a resource-limited setting when retained gastric contents, procedural intolerance, and limited access beyond the narrowed segment prevent complete endoscopic visualization or preoperative histologic confirmation.2,3,7,8

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man presented to the emergency department with an approximately 8-month history of recurrent upper abdominal pain, repeated postprandial vomiting, early satiety, epigastric fullness, and progressive weight loss. The exact amount of weight loss was not documented in the available clinical record. He reported long-standing gastritis. There was no history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, previous abdominal surgery, or known family history of gastric, pancreatic, colorectal, or other gastrointestinal cancer.

The symptoms were chronic and progressive. He described increasing intolerance to meals, persistent upper abdominal discomfort, and a sensation of epigastric fullness. Over time, these symptoms were associated with reduced oral intake and clinically noticeable weight loss.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed on 08 January 2026. The report documented a lower esophageal mucosal break greater than 5 mm, a stomach full of residual food despite the last meal reportedly being 6 hours earlier, an apparently open pylorus, inability to pass the scope into the second part of the duodenum, and termination of the procedure because the patient started vomiting. Retained food and procedural intolerance prevented complete visualization and safe preoperative biopsy. This raised concern for distal inflammatory narrowing or an infiltrative lesion that could not be adequately characterized endoscopically.

Given the chronicity of symptoms, progressive weight loss, worsening biochemical derangement, retained gastric contents, inability to obtain a definitive endoscopic tissue diagnosis, and persistent concern for malignancy, less invasive management such as endoscopic dilation was not attempted. Dilation without tissue confirmation was considered inappropriate because the obstructed region could not be safely traversed, malignancy could not be excluded, and the patient required definitive diagnostic and therapeutic management. The patient therefore underwent exploratory laparotomy with distal gastrectomy.

No intraoperative mass was identified. Gross examination of the resected distal gastrectomy specimen showed a specimen measuring 13×8 cm, with proximal and distal openings measuring 6 cm and 3 cm, respectively, and wall thickness of 1.2 cm. No gross mucosal lesion, ulcerated mucosa, or discrete fibrotic mucosal lesion was identified.

Microscopic examination showed gastric and intestinal wall tissue with mild chronic gastritis, fibrosis, and muscle hypertrophy on the gastric side. In the duodenal mucosa at the distal/pyloroduodenal end of the resected specimen, there was intense chronic inflammation with lymphoid aggregate formation and Brunner gland proliferation extending into the lamina propria. The muscle wall was unremarkable, and no atypia, dysplasia, or malignancy was identified. Immunohistochemistry was not performed because routine histologic features supported a benign inflammatory-proliferative process and showed no suspicious malignant features. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete clinical recovery before discharge. Follow-up was clinical and based on symptom assessment rather than repeat imaging or biochemical testing. At the available short-term postoperative clinical follow-up, the patient remained well without recurrent vomiting or obstructive symptoms; however, the exact duration of follow-up was not documented in the source record.

Discussion

This case illustrates a diagnostically challenging presentation of chronic GOO in an adult with progressive vomiting, early satiety, epigastric fullness, weight loss, dehydration-related biochemical abnormalities, and worsening renal function. Adult GOO warrants careful evaluation because benign and malignant disorders may produce similar symptoms, and prolonged obstruction can lead to dehydration, electrolyte derangement, renal dysfunction, and nutritional decline.1–3

The case contributes to the literature beyond rarity because severe obstruction occurred without a discrete mass lesion on available imaging or operative assessment. In many obstructive cases, a visible tumor, ulcer-related stricture, or polypoid duodenal mass provides an anatomic explanation before resection. In this patient, the final diagnosis depended on histopathology, which demonstrated chronic duodenitis with Brunner gland hyperplasia rather than malignancy. This sequence emphasizes that benign inflammatory-proliferative disease can mimic malignant obstruction even when tumor markers are normal and no definite mass is seen.

The differential diagnosis of adult GOO includes malignant causes such as distal gastric carcinoma, pancreatic head or periampullary malignancy, duodenal neoplasms, and metastatic disease. Benign causes include peptic ulcer disease-related scarring, historically the most common benign etiology, as well as inflammatory strictures, caustic injury, bezoars, postoperative adhesions, and rare proliferative duodenal lesions.1,3,7,9 The suspicion of malignancy in the present case was clinically reasonable because the patient had progressive obstructive symptoms, weight loss, marked gastric dilatation, and distal outlet narrowing.

The biochemical abnormalities were also important. Hypernatremia, hypokalemia, and rising creatinine were consistent with repeated vomiting, poor intake, extracellular volume depletion, and prerenal acute kidney injury. These abnormalities increased the urgency of intervention because ongoing obstruction could have led to further metabolic decompensation, aspiration risk, and nutritional deterioration.1,2

Endoscopy and CT were complementary but not definitive. Retained food despite fasting strongly supported impaired gastric emptying and prevented complete visualization. The apparently open pylorus did not exclude obstruction because the endoscope could not be advanced into the second part of the duodenum, suggesting narrowing beyond the pyloric channel or at the pyloroduodenal transition. CT confirmed marked gastric dilatation and a transition/narrowing region, while the absence of an obvious mass or lymphadenopathy did not eliminate the possibility of infiltrative or early malignant disease.2,3

Brunner gland hyperplasia is a rare benign proliferative lesion arising from mucus-secreting glands of the proximal duodenum. It is often incidental, but symptomatic lesions have been reported to cause upper gastrointestinal bleeding, duodenal intussusception, luminal narrowing, and GOO.4–6,10,11 Proposed mechanisms include chronic mucosal irritation, excessive acid exposure, and inflammatory stimulation. In this patient, the combination of chronic duodenitis, lymphoid aggregates, and Brunner gland proliferation supports a benign inflammatory-proliferative mechanism for obstruction.

Previously published cases of Brunner gland hyperplasia causing obstruction have commonly described vomiting, gastric dilatation, and diagnostic uncertainty, with treatment ranging from endoscopic resection in selected accessible lesions to surgery when obstruction is severe, tissue diagnosis is not feasible, or malignancy cannot be excluded.5,6,10,11 Compared with those reports, the present case is notable for a prolonged course, absence of a gross mass lesion, nondiagnostic preoperative endoscopy, dehydration-associated renal dysfunction, and definitive diagnosis only after distal gastrectomy.

Management of GOO depends on etiology, severity, patient stability, and available expertise. Endoscopic dilation, endoscopic resection, or stenting may be appropriate in selected benign or malignant cases, but these options require a safely accessible lesion, a reasonable diagnostic pathway, and careful exclusion of malignancy where indicated.3,7,9 In the present case, surgery was justified because endoscopic access and biopsy were limited, the patient had clinically significant obstruction with metabolic consequences, and malignancy remained an important unresolved concern.

The practical lesson from this case is that normal tumor markers and absence of a discrete mass do not reliably exclude malignancy in adults with progressive GOO. When the obstructed segment cannot be traversed and preoperative tissue diagnosis is not feasible, clinicians should integrate symptoms, endoscopic findings, CT findings, operative assessment, and histopathology. In resource-limited settings, timely surgical management may be both diagnostic and therapeutic when less invasive approaches are unavailable, unsafe, or unlikely to resolve diagnostic uncertainty.

This report has limitations. It describes a single patient and therefore cannot support estimates of frequency, risk, or comparative effectiveness of management strategies. The exact amount of preoperative weight loss and the exact duration of follow-up were not documented. Preoperative histologic diagnosis was not obtained because retained food, vomiting, and restricted endoscopic access prevented safe biopsy. Immunohistochemistry was not performed because routine histology showed no atypia or malignancy. Follow-up was clinical rather than radiologic or biochemical, and the findings may not be generalizable to patients with different anatomy, lesions that are endoscopically accessible, or settings with advanced endoscopic ultrasound and interventional endoscopy.

Conclusion

Brunner gland hyperplasia is a rare benign cause of GOO that may closely mimic malignancy when symptoms are progressive, obstruction is severe, and preoperative tissue diagnosis is not possible. This case is important because severe obstruction occurred without a discrete mass lesion, and the diagnosis was established only after surgical resection and histopathologic examination. Careful integration of clinical assessment, endoscopy, CT, operative findings, and pathology is essential. In resource-limited settings or diagnostically uncertain cases, timely surgery may be necessary to prevent clinical deterioration and to provide definitive diagnosis and treatment.
