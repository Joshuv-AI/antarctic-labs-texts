Association between early body weight reduction and treatment outcomes in participants with obstructive sleep apnoea and obesity: a post hoc analysis of SURMOUNT-OSA trials
Authors: Ying Ni Lin, Xian Wen Sun, Shao Jun Dai, Hao Xin Zhuang, Jun Jun Liu, Qing Yun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundObesity is a major modifiable risk factor for obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). Although previous studies have demonstrated that greater weight reduction is associated with larger decreases in apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) and improvements in cardiometabolic outcomes, the significance of early weight reduction is not fully understood.MethodsWe conducted a post hoc analysis of the SURMOUNT-OSA randomised trials (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05412004), which included 469 participants. Participants were divided by the percent of weight reduction at week 20: <5%, ≥5% to <10%, ≥10% to <15%, and ≥15%. The key endpoint was the change in AHI from baseline to week 52.FindingsParticipants who achieved ≥15% body weight reduction at week 20 demonstrated significantly greater AHI improvement at week 52 compared to those who achieved <5% weight reduction (p < 0·001), and the magnitude of AHI changes at week 52 was associated with greater early weight reduction in both trials. Participants in the ≥15% weight reduction group also had higher rates of clinically meaningful AHI reduction (≥50%), OSA remission, and greater improvements in sleep apnoea specific hypoxic burden, body weight, and cardiometabolic factors at week 52. Greater weight reduction at week 20 was associated with improvements in sleep apnoea-specific hypoxic burden, body weight, and cardiometabolic risk factors at week 52 in both trials.InterpretationThese findings indicate that early substantial weight reduction (≥15%) is associated with sustained improvements in OSA and related cardiometabolic outcomes, supporting early weight management as a key therapeutic strategy for OSA, regardless of positive airway pressure therapy use.FundingThe study was funded by Eli Lilly and Company.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception up to July 15, 2025, without language restrictions, using the terms “early weight reduction,” “obstructive sleep apnoea,” and “obesity” to assess the available evidence on the impact of early weight reduction on obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) outcomes and cardiometabolic benefits in individuals with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity. Although weight reduction is known to improve OSA, no studies have specifically assessed how the degree of early weight reduction relates to subsequent OSA outcomes. The minimum threshold of early weight-reduction required to achieve clinically meaningful OSA improvement or remission also remains unknown.

Added value of this study

This study demonstrates that achieving significant early weight reduction may be a key determinant of sustained improvement in OSA. Weight reduction of ≥10%, and ≥15%, by week 20 was associated with greater long-term reductions in apnoea-hypopnoea index, higher remission rates, and improved hypoxic burden, irrespective of positive airway pressure use.

Implications of all the available evidence

Available evidence supports weight reduction as a strategy for OSA management in individual with obesity but has not previously addressed the prognostic importance of early weight reduction. These findings suggest that early achievement of substantial weight reduction may serve as a clinically meaningful target and decision point, enabling timely optimisation of management strategies to improve long-term respiratory and cardiometabolic outcomes.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common sleep-related breathing disorder, affecting nearly 1 billion people worldwide, with over 425 million adults aged 30–69 years having moderate-to-severe OSA.1 OSA is characterised by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, and excessive daytime sleepiness.2,3 Beyond its impact on sleep quality, OSA is a major contributor to cardiometabolic morbidity and is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, and all-cause mortality.3,4 Untreated OSA has been associated with increased risk of hypertension, myocardial infarction, and stroke.4, 5, 6

Obesity is the most significant modifiable risk factor for OSA.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Excess adiposity, particularly in the upper airway and visceral compartments, increases pharyngeal collapsibility, impairs ventilatory control by reducing muscle contractility or tone, decreases lung volumes and caudal traction.10,12,13 Collectively, these changes cause or exacerbate OSA by promoting the collapse of the airway during sleep.12,13 More than 60% of moderate-to-severe OSA is attributable to obesity, and OSA severity is strongly correlated with body mass index (BMI).14,15 Despite the multifactorial nature of OSA, weight reduction remains a cornerstone of non-pharmacological management.16, 17, 18, 19

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that greater weight reduction is associated with significant reductions in the apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) and improvements in blood pressure, glycaemic control, and lipid profile.20 More recent trials have further shown that intensive weight reduction can result in clinically meaningful improvements and remission of OSA (AHI <5 events/h).21,22 However, these studies have primarily examined the effects of overall weight reduction on OSA outcomes, with limited attention to the prognostic significance of early weight-reduction magnitude. To date, no studies have explicitly evaluated the dose–response relationship between the magnitude of early weight reduction and subsequent OSA outcomes, nor clarified the minimum threshold of early weight reduction required to achieve clinically meaningful OSA improvement or remission. This knowledge gap limits the development of targeted, time-sensitive weight management strategies for OSA patients, as clinicians lack evidence on whether early weight reduction trajectories can predict long-term treatment success.

Tirzepatide, a long-acting dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, has been approved by United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity.23 In the SURMOUNT-OSA trial (NCT05412004), tirzepatide significantly reduced AHI, body weight, and cardiovascular risk factors in participants with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity.2 Despite these promising results, a critical evidence gap remains regarding the timing and magnitude of weight reduction necessary to achieve meaningful improvements in OSA severity. Although weight reduction has been associated with sustained improvements in AHI, limited data exist on the impact of early-phase weight reduction, particularly during the first 20 weeks of treatment, on OSA outcomes.

In this post hoc analysis of the SURMOUNT-OSA trials, we evaluated the relationship between the magnitude of early weight reduction and improvement of OSA in individuals with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity, providing evidence to inform and optimise early intervention strategies for OSA management.

Methods

Study design and participants

SURMOUNT-OSA trials were two 52-week, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 studies designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of tirzepatide in adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [≥27 kg/m2 in Japan]) and moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI ≥15 events per hour) without type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Trial 1 included participants who were not using positive airway pressure (PAP therapy) and trial 2 included participants who had been receiving PAP therapy. The participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either the maximum tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 mg or 15 mg) or placebo for 52 weeks. The trial designs, methods, statistical analyses, and results have been previously published.2

This was a post hoc analysis of SURMOUNT-OSA trials to evaluate the association between early weight reduction at week 20 and OSA improvement in participants with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity.

Ethics

SURMOUNT-OSA trial was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05412004) and has been completed. The trials were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki and its guidelines. The SURMOUNT-OSA protocols were approved by institutional review boards for each site (Supplementary). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Outcomes

The endpoints in this post hoc analysis were change in the AHI; percent change in AHI; the percentage of participants with clinically meaningful change (defined as ≥50% AHI reduction) in AHI; the percentage of participants with OSA remission with an AHI <5 events per hour or with an AHI = 5–14 events per hour and a score of 10 or less on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) (range, 0–24, with higher scores indicating greater daytime sleepiness); the percent change in sleep apnoea-specific hypoxic burden (SASHB), body weight; change in patient-reported outcome measures, including Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Sleep Disturbance and Sleep-Related Impairment scores, and Short-Form 36 version 2 (SF-36v2) Physical Component and General Health scores; the change in blood pressure, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) concentration, lipid profile, and safety. All endpoints were assessed from baseline to week 52, except for blood pressure, which was assessed at week 48.

Statistical analysis

In the original SURMOUNT OSA trials, approximately 206 participants were planned for enrolment in each trial (approximately 103 per treatment arm), providing at least 90% power to demonstrate superiority of tirzepatide over placebo for the primary endpoint (mean percent change in AHI from baseline to week 52) and key secondary hierarchical composite PRO endpoint.2 As the present study is a post hoc analysis of participants enrolled in the original trials, no formal sample size estimation or statistical power calculation was performed.

Participants were retrospectively categorised into four groups based on the percent of weight reduction at week 20: <5% (reference), ≥5% to <10%, ≥10% to <15%, and ≥15%. Endpoints were analysed using the modified Intent-to-Treat population under efficacy estimand, which included data from all randomised participants who received the assigned intervention as intended for the entire planned 52-week trial duration, excluding data collected after discontinuation of study treatment. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the endpoints: continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviation (SD), categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. A mixed model for repeated measures was used for continuous endpoints, and logistic regression model was used for categorical endpoints, with randomised treatment, strata, visit, treatment × visit interaction, baseline measure, and 4 weight reduction groups at week 20 as covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted by participants' sex, age and OSA severity at baseline. The significance level was set at 0·05. Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had a role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, writing of the report, and the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Participants

A total of 469 participants were included in the analysis, 234 from trial 1 and 235 from trial 2 (Fig. 1). Detailed demographic and baseline characteristics have been previously reported in the SURMOUNT OSA global study.2 Participants in trial 1 had an average age of 47·9 years. The majority were male (157/234; 67·1%) and white (154/234; 65·8%), with an average BMI of 39·1 kg/m2 and an average AHI of 51·5 (events/h). In trial 2, the mean age of the participants was 51·7 years. Most were male (170/235; 72·3%) and white (171/235; 73·1%), with an average BMI of 38·7 kg/m2 and an average AHI of 49·5. OSA remission at week 52

In trial 1, at week 52, 48·7% (OR 5·6) of participants in the ≥10% to <15% early weight reduction group and 50·7% (OR 6·6) of participants in the ≥15% early weight reduction group had OSA remission. OSA remission was observed in 29·8% (OR 1·9) of participants in the ≥5% to <10% and 12·8% of participants in the <5% early weight reduction groups, respectively (Fig. 3B).

In trial 2, 49·8% (OR 3·7) and 75·3% (OR 11·4) of participants in the ≥10% to <15% and ≥15% weight reduction groups, respectively, had OSA remission by week 52. While OSA remission was observed in 23·1% (OR 1·1) of participants in the ≥5% to <10% and 14·3% participants in the <5% weight reduction groups (Fig. 3B).

Patient reported outcomes

Across both trials, PROMIS Sleep Disturbance and Sleep Related Impairment scores improved from baseline at week 52 in all weight reduction categories, with significantly greater reductions observed in the ≥10% to <15% and ≥15% weight reduction groups compared with the <5% group (p < 0·05 for all; Supplementary Table S2).

In trial 1, SF-36v2 Physical Component and General Health Score improved significantly in all weight reduction categories; a significant difference vs the <5% group was observed only in the ≥10% to <15% group (p < 0·01). In trial 2, both scores improved significantly across all higher weight reduction categories (≥5% groups), with significant differences vs the <5% group observed in all comparisons (p < 0·001 for all; Supplementary Table S2).

Cardiovascular risk factors

In both trials 1 and trial 2, participants in the ≥5% to <10%, ≥10% to <15%, and ≥15% early weight reduction groups showed improvement in systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure at week 48, HDL-C, and triglycerides at week 52 compared to those in the <5% weight reduction group (Supplementary Table S3).

Subgroup analysis

Age

In trial 1, participants in both <50 years and ≥50 years age subgroups, a significant reduction in AHI at week 52 was observed in both the ≥10% to <15% and ≥15% early weight reduction groups compared to the <5% early weight reduction group (p < 0·01 for all) (Supplementary Table S4).

In trial 2, no significant reduction in AHI was observed in any of the weight-reduction groups at week 52 (p > 0·05) in the subgroup of participants with age <50 years. Whereas participants aged ≥50 years showed significant reductions in AHI at week 52 in both the ≥10% to <15% and ≥15% early weight reduction groups (p < 0·001 for all) (Supplementary Table S4).

OSA severity

In both trials 1 and 2, participants in the moderate OSA subgroup with ≥5% to <10%, ≥10% to <15%, and ≥15% early weight reduction showed significant reductions in AHI (p < 0·05 for all) at week 52. In the severe OSA subgroup in both trials 1 and 2, a significant reduction in AHI at week 52 was observed in the ≥10% to <15% and ≥15% early weight reduction groups (p < 0·05 for all) (Supplementary Table S4).

Safety

There was no difference in the incidence of serious adverse events (SAEs) between groups in both trials 1 and 2 (Table 3). There were no deaths related to adverse events (AEs). Discontinuations from the study due to AEs were rare, with no consistent pattern across groups. In Trial 1, discontinuations ranged from 0% to 3·9%, and in Trial 2 from 0% to 4·4%. Discussion

This post hoc analysis of the SURMOUNT-OSA trials demonstrated a positive association between the magnitude of early weight reduction and improvements in OSA severity, as measured by AHI, as well as cardiometabolic outcomes, regardless of PAP therapy use. In both trial 1 and 2, participants achieving ≥15% early weight reduction at week 20 experienced significantly greater reduction in AHI at both weeks 20 and 52, compared with those achieving <5% and ≥5% to <10% weight reduction, underscoring the durability of early intervention. The findings suggest that early and intensive weight management may be a key therapeutic strategy for participants with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity.

While weight reduction is a well-established determinant of OSA improvement, this analysis further examined the dose–response relationship between the magnitude of early weight reduction and subsequent OSA outcomes. A meta-analysis of 27 randomised controlled trials reported that BMI reductions of 20–30% were associated with AHI improvements of 57–69%, reinforcing the clinical relevance of weight reduction in OSA management.18 Although the underlying mechanisms are not fully elucidated,7,10,18 weight reduction likely enhances pharyngeal mechanics and reduces abdominal fat, thereby improving airway patency and ventilatory control.18,24 Our findings are consistent with prior evidence showing that weight reduction is associated with clinically meaningful improvements in OSA, with the most profound benefits observed among participants achieving early and intensive weight reduction. Participants who achieved ≥15% weight reduction by week 20 not only exhibited a high likelihood of long-term success in weight reduction, but also demonstrated the greatest improvements in AHI, with benefits evident as early as week 20 and sustained up to week 52. Approximately 50–75% of these participants achieved OSA remission, defined as post-treatment AHI <5 events/h, or AHI 5–14 events/h with ESS <10. This is clinically significant, given that OSA remission has been associated with reduced risk of all-cause mortality and major cardiovascular events.25 Collectively, these findings suggest that the magnitude of early weight reduction within the first 20 weeks of treatment could be considered as a pivotal point in OSA management. An early and robust response may encourage both patients and clinicians to pursue intensive weight management strategies, whereas a suboptimal response, particularly <5% weight reduction at week 20, may require timely reassessment of the treatment approach. In such cases, consideration could be given to intensifying lifestyle interventions, optimising or escalating pharmacotherapy where appropriate and tolerated, or switching to a different agent to improve OSA outcomes. In addition, adjunctive strategies such as initiation or optimization of PAP therapy may be appropriate for patients not already receiving it.

Beyond respiratory benefits, this analysis also identified a positive relationship between weight reduction and improvement in cardiometabolic risk factors. Participants achieving ≥10% weight reduction at week 20 showed significant reduction in body weight, blood pressure, and inflammatory markers at week 52, which are important cardiovascular risk factors in individuals with OSA and obesity. Greater weight reduction was also associated with improvements in lipid parameters, including HDL-C and triglycerides. These findings are consistent with previous studies, including a randomised clinical trial in 89 Spanish men,2,15 supporting that early and intensive weight reduction provides benefits for participants with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity because of significant improvement in important cardiometabolic end points involved in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases.

Subgroup analyses further support the primary findings. Among participants with age ≥50 years and any OSA severity, those who achieved ≥10% early weight reduction category exhibited significant AHI improvements compared to those with <5% early weight reduction. Participants with moderate-to-severe OSA exhibited sustained improvements with greater weight reduction (≥10%), whereas those with moderate OSA showed improvement in AHI at week 52 even in participants with ≥5% early weight reduction. Overall, the subgroup analysis underscores the importance of individualised treatment strategies in OSA management, as the impact of early weight reduction may vary according to patient characteristics, such as age and disease severity. These insights can inform personalised goal setting and enhance shared decision-making in clinical practice.

No significant difference in the incidence of safety events was observed across the early weight reduction groups in both trials 1 and 2. No deaths related to AEs occurred during the study. Safety findings were consistent with those reported in the Phase 3 SURMOUNT-OSA trials, in which GI events were the most frequently reported AEs and were generally mild to moderate in severity.

The current study has certain strengths. It included participants from 60 sites across 9 countries, ensuring a geographically diverse and adequately sized population across weight reduction groups. The observed benefits of early weight reduction reflected in improvement in AHI, body weight, and cardiovascular risk factors are consistent with findings from the primary studies and existing literature, thereby reinforcing the evidence supporting early weight reduction as a key strategy in OSA management. Furthermore, the post hoc analysis examining participants with and without PAP therapy provides evidence on early weight reduction in these patient populations that are prevalent in clinical practice. The study also assessed the improvement in cardiovascular risk factors across different weight reduction groups further underscoring the holistic benefits of early weight reduction in this population. The outcomes of subgroup analyses were aligned with those of the primary analyses, lending credibility and robustness to the findings.

However, these results should be interpreted in light of certain limitations inherent to post hoc analyses. First, the analyses were not pre-specified in the original SURMOUNT-OSA protocol, which limits the ability to draw definitive causal inferences and raises the potential for data-driven bias. Second, the retrospective stratification of participants by early weight reduction categories introduces a risk of selection bias, as these subgroups were defined based on observed outcomes rather than randomisation. Third, the generalisability of the findings may be constrained, as the trial population may not fully represent the broader clinical population with OSA and obesity. Lastly, the analysis was limited to a 52-week treatment period, leaving the long-term sustainability of weight reduction and its impact on OSA severity beyond this timeframe uncertain. Validation in prospective studies, including ongoing trials of incretin-based therapies in OSA and obesity, will be important to confirm these observations.

This study demonstrated a positive association between early weight reduction and improvements in OSA severity. Participants achieving ≥10% weight reduction by week 20 experienced significant and sustained reductions in AHI through week 52, with the greatest benefits observed in those achieving ≥15% early weight reduction. These findings underscore the importance of early and intensive weight management strategies for individuals with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity, irrespective of PAP therapy use.
