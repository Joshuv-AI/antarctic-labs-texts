Shared M0 macrophage infiltration-related gene signatures in osteoarthritis and osteomyelitis by integrating bulk and single-cell RNA transcriptome
Authors: Mingjie Wei, Xiangwen Shi, Wenbao Tang, Qian Lv, Yipeng Wu, Yongqing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) and osteomyelitis (OM) are prevalent orthopedic diseases, characterized by complex immune and inflammatory responses that impact bone regeneration. Macrophages play a crucial role in the immune regulation of osteoblast function, with M0 macrophages polarizing toward M1 and M2 phenotypes, influencing pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory responses. Despite evidence linking M0 macrophages with inflammation in OA and OM, the underlying pathogenic and immune regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsTo examine the immune microenvironment in OA and OM, we analyzed 6 OA datasets and 3 OM datasets from the GEO database. Shared M0 macrophages-related differentially Expressed Genes (M0R-DEGs) in OA and OM were identified by differential expression analysis, immune cell infiltration analysis, and correlation analysis. Characteristic M0R-DEGs were selected through random forest (RF), support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model. Subsequently, diagnostic models and molecular subtypes for OA and OM were established, further exploring the differences in M0R-molecular patterns and immune infiltration. In addition, the distribution, potential biological functions, and pathways of OA synovial cell types and M0R-DEGs were analyzed using scRNA datasets Finally, the expression of shared M0R-DEGs was validated through RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining.ResultsCombining immune infiltration analysis, correlation analysis, and machine learning models, five shared M0R-DEGs were identified, associated with the immune and metabolic processes of autoimmune diseases. Next, diagnostic models based on these genes were demonstrated to have strong diagnostic performance for OA and OM in both training and validation sets, effectively distinguishing between disease and healthy samples. Subsequently, two different M0R molecular patterns were further constructed, with OA cluster 1 and OM cluster 2 associated with immune activation and inflammatory responses in OA and OM, respectively. In OA synovial samples, TIMP1 was downregulated in M2 macrophages and may be involved in the function of M2 macrophages by downregulating TNF-α and NF-κB signaling pathways. The expressions of MMP9 and SPARC in OA and OM rat models were consistent with bioinformatics findings, confirmed by RT-qPCR and IHC.ConclusionThis study identified five shared M0R-DEGs between OA and OM, linked to immune activation and response in various immune diseases. The diagnostic model revealed shared M0R-DEGs have good diagnostic efficacy, with significant differences in immune and inflammatory factors such as IL4 and MMP2 between the two molecular patterns. These findings may provide valuable insights for identifying potential shared biomarkers in OA and OM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03342-4.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Osteomyelitis, M0 macrophage, Immune infiltration, ScRNA-seq, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is characterized by the destruction of articular cartilage and bone hyperplasia. Recognized as the most prevalent degenerative joint disease, the recent studies have increasingly identified it as associated with persistent low-grade inflammation and the activation of innate inflammatory responses [1–3]. Osteomyelitis (OM), in contrast to the low-grade inflammatory response seen in osteoarthritis (OA), is a prominent inflammatory bone disease caused by bacteria, fungi, or other pathogens. The inflammatory response in OM is intense, often resulting in more pronounced clinical manifestations [4, 5]. Severe damage to joint cartilage and surrounding osteophyte formation may induce chronic OM due to accumulated bacteria. As two prevalent and potentially comorbid chronic inflammatory diseases, OA and OM significantly impact global healthcare, exacerbating pain and medical costs as the aging population grows and incidence rates increase [6, 7]. In addition, treatment options for OA typically range from early-stage drug therapy to late-stage surgical replacement [8], osteomyelitis (OM) is treated with a combination of antibiotics and surgical debridement, high recurrence rates make early detection and treatment essential [9, 10]. However, the pathogenesis of both OA and OM is intricate, involving the cross-regulation of immune cells and inflammatory factors. Exploring the shared molecular mechanisms of these diseases could improve early diagnosis and patient prognosis.

In the immune and inflammatory microenvironment mediated by OA and OM, the crosstalk mechanism between osteoblasts and osteoclasts is affected, resulting in bone loss and impaired remodeling [11]. The progression of OA involves the recruitment of mononuclear cells, lymphocytes, and leukocytes, heightening the inflammatory response. Notably, macrophages play a pivotal role as immune cells, acting as key mediators of cartilage and synovial inflammation, defining a significant aspect of OA. These macrophages infiltrate various layers of the synovium, essential for the innate immune response. The pro-inflammatory M1-polarized macrophages exacerbate synovial inflammation and contribute to OA progression [12, 13]. Daghestani et al. [14] has demonstrated the predictive value of macrophage markers CD163 and CD14 in assessing OA severity, radiological features, and pain scores, with CD14 showing significant predictive ability for OA progression and pain. Furthermore, 83% of patients in advanced stages of OA exhibit varying degrees of synovitis, exacerbating the development of disease [15]. In addition, cartilage fragments and necrotic cells, recognized as risk factors, activate macrophages. This activation leads to the production of inflammatory factors, initiating the destruction of synovium and cartilage [16].

In the inflammatory microenvironment mediated by OM, macrophages serve as the first line of defense against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infection. In terms of pathogenesis, the virulence proteins of bacteria directly destroy cell membranes and destroy immune cells, including macrophages and neutrophils. Furthermore, during bone infection, the induction of naive macrophages (M0) towards to M2 polarization promotes fibrotic reaction and facilitates abscess formation [17]. In addition, S. aureus can survive within macrophages and inhibit their phagocytic activity by binding to the α5β1 integrin on the macrophage surface [18]. Thus, the polarization of M0 macrophages into M1/M2 phenotypes plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of both OA and OM. Understanding how immune regulation of M0 macrophages differs between patients with OA/OM and healthy individuals is crucial for developing immunological and targeted therapeutic strategies for the diseases.

The advancement of bioinformatics has greatly enhanced our understanding of the genetic foundations of OA and OM from a genetic perspective. In this study, Initially, shared M0 macrophages-related differentially Expressed Genes (M0R-DEGs) were selected through immune infiltration analysis, differential expression analysis, and correlation analysis. Diagnostic and molecular signatures associated with M0 macrophages were established based on these shared genes, and variations in inflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration across different molecular patterns were investigated. In addition, rat models of OA and OM were established, and the expression of shared M0R-DEGs was validated using qRT-PCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC). This study systematically investigated the potential shared biomarkers of OA and OM in diagnosis and clustering, which may provide clues for future research on the joint diagnosis of OA and OM (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Data selection and procession

OM and OA-related datasets and corresponding platform files were filtered and downloaded through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database, limiting the species to human. In the 6 OA-related datasets, 4 datasets were pooled and served as the training group. GSE51588 dataset included 40 OA samples and 10 healthy control samples, GSE55235 dataset included 20 OA samples and 10 healthy control samples, GSE55457 dataset included 23 OA samples and 10 healthy control samples, GSE77298 dataset included 16 OA samples and 7 healthy control samples. 2 datasets and served as validation groups, GSE82107 dataset included 10 OA samples and 7 healthy control samples, and GSE178557 dataset included 4 OA samples and 4 healthy control samples. In the three OM-related datasets, the GSE30119 dataset was used as the training group (39 OM samples and 44 healthy control samples), and the remaining two datasets were combined as the validation group, the GSE6269 dataset consisted of 15 OM samples and 6 healthy control samples, and the GSE16129 dataset consisted of 70 OM samples and 29 healthy control samples. The patient characteristics for all included datasets are presented in Table S4. Each dataset was processed individually: probe IDs were converted to gene symbols using the “perl” software (https://www.perl.org/) to ensure consistent gene annotation across platforms. Datasets were merged by retaining only genes common to all included studies. Each dataset was log2-transformed and normalized using the “limma” package. Batch effects arising from platform or study differences were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the “sva” package [19] with parametric priors. The effectiveness of integration and batch correction was visually confirmed through PCA plots (Fig. S1).

The single-cell transcriptomics GSE152805 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database, encompassing 6,652 cells from synovial samples of three selected OA patients. For processing the scRNA-seq data, the “Seurat” package [20] (Version 4.0) in R software was employed to import and integrate different OA synovial samples. Cell atlas filtering criteria were set as nCountRNA ≥ 1000, 200 ≤ nFeatureRNA ≤ 10,000, and percent.mt ≤ 20 to validate correlations among these indicators. Subsequently, data normalization was conducted using the LogNormalize method, and the “vst” method identified the top 2000 highly variable genes for downstream PCA and dimensionality reduction analysis, with visualization of the top 10 variable genes.

Immune infiltration analysis

Both OA and OM involve complex immune responses and immunoregulatory mechanisms, necessitating a comprehensive analysis of the immune microenvironment in both diseases. CIBERSORT is a method for characterizing the cellular composition of tissue clocks through large-scale analysis based on gene expression profiles and is commonly used to find disease biomarkers and therapeutic targets [21]. Specifically, this algorithm uses normalized gene expression data as input, with the LM22 gene signature set as the reference matrix. It utilizes a support vector regression-based deconvolution method to analyze cell composition. To ensure the reliability of the estimation results, we set the number of permutations to 1000. In addition, only the significant results with a P value < 0.05 from the CIBERSORT output were retained for further analysis. The final immune cell proportion matrix was visualized using a violin plot generated by the “vioplot” package to compare the infiltration level differences between the OA and OM disease groups and the healthy control group samples.

Identification of M0 macrophage-related differentially expressed genes (M0R-DEGs)

M0 macrophage-mediated M1 and M2 polarization play key roles in both OA and OM, with shared M0R-DEGs identified through differential expression analysis. The “limma” package [22] was used to screen the DEGs between the four OA training datasets and the OM training dataset. The “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” packages were used to visualize the DEGs between OA and OM in the form of heat map and volcano map. Adjusted P value 0.05 and |log2FC|≥ 0.585 was used as the screening criterion of DEGs. Then, the shared up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs in OA and OM were taken. Subsequently, Spearman's correlation analysis was used to assess the association between the expression of these shared DEGs and the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types. To preliminarily investigate the potential role of M0 macrophage-related biomarkers in OA and OM, DEGs significantly correlated with M0 macrophage infiltration were selected for further study, with the threshold set to P value < 0.05 and |r|> 0.30.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

After the shared M0R-DEGs of OA and OM were screened, the potential biological functions of M0R-DEGs were explored by GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The GO analysis was performed using the “Metascape” website (http://metascape.org) [23], and KEGG pathway analysis was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package [24]. Screening criteria set to adjusted P value 0.05.

Screening characteristic shared M0R-DEGs based on machine learning

To identify the most predictive biomarkers from the shared M0R-DEGs, we applied and compared several machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. These models were chosen because they have been widely validated for classification and feature selection in high-dimensional biological data. RF is an ensemble learning method that effectively prevents overfitting and provides intrinsic feature importance measures [25, 26]. SVM excels at finding complex nonlinear boundaries in high-dimensional space [27, 28]. LASSO regression is particularly suited for feature selection, constructing a sparse model by shrinking less important coefficients to zero [29]. RF and SVM models were trained using fivefold repeated cross-validation implemented via the caret package to ensure generalizability and avoid overfitting. This resampling process was repeated multiple times to obtain robust performance estimates of the models. Further, we used several quantitative metrics to rigorously compare the performance of the RF and SVM models and objectively determine the best algorithm for our dataset, including residual distribution and the area under the curve (AUC). Specifically, the "DALEX" package was used to generate and compare the inverse cumulative distribution plot and boxplot of absolute residuals to visually and quantitatively display which model had smaller and more concentrated prediction errors. In addition, the AUC values and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated and reported for each model.

Given the lower residuals and higher AUC values of the RF model, we chose the RF model for subsequent analysis. For RF, feature importance was calculated based on the Mean Decrease Gini (MDG) index, which reflects the contribution of each gene to the model's prediction accuracy across all decision trees [30, 31]. The top five genes ranked by MDG were retained as candidate biomarkers. To enhance the robustness of feature selection, we independently applied LASSO regression to the same set of M0R-DEGs and performed tenfold cross-validation. The optimal lambda value, which minimizes binomial deviation, was selected to filter a set of nonzero coefficient genes. The final characteristic biomarkers were defined as the intersection of the genes selected by LASSO and those selected by the RF model. This shared strategy ensures that the final biomarkers, whether considered individually or as a set, are strong disease predictors.

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (sgGSEA) and single-gene gene set variation analysis (sgGSVA)

To further investigate the potential biological functions and pathways of these characteristc M0R-DEGs, sgGSEA and sgGSVA enrichment analyses were employed. The correlation between the target gene and the rest of the genes in the training set was assessed using these analyses, which were conducted on each feature M0R-DEG. The “GSEABase” and “GSVA” packages in R were utilized for these analyses [32]. In GSEA, disease samples were grouped based on the expression levels of target genes, and gene sets significantly enriched were identified by ranking according to the logFC value between two group. After the control samples were removed, disease samples were categorized into high- and low-expression groups based on the expression levels of each gene. The functions and pathways with significant enrichment in GSVA scores were then analyzed further. All analyses were based on the predefined "c5.go.symbols.gmt" and "c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt" sets. The screening criterion for the enrichment analysis was set to P value < 0.05.

Diagnostic signature of OA and OM based on shared M0R-DEGs

Given the lack of biomarkers with good diagnostic efficacy for OA and OM, we investigated the diagnostic value of shared M0R-DEGs for OA and OM. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the selected characteristic M0R-DEGs for disease, ROC curves for OA and OM were constructed separately by the “pROC” package. The area under the curve (AUC) for each M0R-DEG was then evaluated. A logistic regression model was employed to predict whether each sample in the training set was a disease sample or a normal sample in OA or OM. The diagnostic performance was evaluated using the AUC value. To further verify the diagnostic ability of ROC curves, GSE82107 and GSE178557 datasets were combined as an OA validation set, and GSE6269 and GSE16129 datasets were combined to serve as an OM validation set. ROC curves and logistic regression models of characteristic M0R-DEGs were constructed to the accuracy of the training models for OA and OM. Furthermore, nomogram models for predicting the incidence of OA and OM in patients were constructed using the 'rms' package. The fit of these models was assessed against clinical decision curves and calibration curves to determine the benefit to patients [33].

Consensus clustering analysis based on shared M0R-DEGs in OA and OM

Clustering analysis of OA and OM from molecular and immunological perspectives facilitates stratification and personalized immune therapy for patients. Unsupervised cluster analysis is often used for dimensionality reduction to identify new molecular patterns or disease subtypes. The “ConsensusClusterPlus” package [34] of R software is utilized to allow the K-means algorithm to detect OA or OM samples with similar characteristics and group them into one cluster, thus identifying M0R-clustering patterns with different characteristics. The maximum K value for the number of clusters was set to 9, and the appropriate number of clusters was determined based on the consistency score, the consistency heatmap, and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) [35]. Once the number of clusters is determined, the classification results were visualized using heatmaps and boxplots. To verify the characteristics of the consensus clustering results, principal component analysis (PCA) and visualization were performed using the “ggplot2” package [36].

After different M0R-clustering patterns of OA and OM were established based on the M0R-DEGs, the abundance of immune cell infiltration between the different patterns was further explored. Specifically, the infiltrating abundance of 22 immune cells in OA and OM samples was calculated. The “reshape2” and “ggpubr” packages were utilized to compare the differences in immune infiltration between M0 macrophage patterns by plotting boxplots and bar charts in conjunction with disease typing results.

Since interleukin (IL)-related genes and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-related genes play key roles in regulating the development of OA and OM [37, 38], the differential expression of these genes was explored in different M0R-clustering patterns of OA and OM. Furthermore, to explore the molecular mechanisms between different M0 macrophage patterns, GSVA analysis was employed to identify key pathways with significant differences between the patterns in OA and OM. The top 10 enriched pathways were then presented in the form of bar graphs.

Cell clustering annotation and expression analysis of shared M0R-DEGs

Based on PCA, a KNN graph of distance distribution was constructed. Optimal resolution was selected using the “FindClusters” function, and cell distribution was displayed using “tSNE”. Subsequently, cell types were manually annotated based on specific markers described in previous studies (Table S1). Then, the top 10 upregulated DEGs across different cell types were visualized through heatmaps to validate their specificity.

Cell cycle analysis

The “tricycle” package [39] in R software was utilized for cell cycle inference and analysis, focusing on key biological and mathematical characteristics of cell cycles. Cell cycle positions were estimated to range from 0 to 2pi, with cell cycle stages being inferred as five phases: G1.S, S, G2, G2/M, and M.G1. Specifically, 0.5pi was associated with the start of the S phase, pi with the start of the G2.M phase, and 1.5pi with the middle of the M phase. Enrichment analysis of upregulated and downregulated DEGs across different cell types was then conducted using AUCell, UCell, singscore, and ssGSEA methods.

Cell–cell communication analysis

To explore potential communication patterns between different cell types at optimal single-cell resolution, the “CellChat” package [40] (version 1.6.1) was applied for cell–cell communication inference and analysis. Intercellular communication probabilities of cell types in each OA synovial sample were calculated and integrated at the signaling pathway level, with all significant interactions further analyzed.

Verification of shared M0R-DEGs expression based on OA and OM validation sets

To validate the expression levels of shared M0R-DEGs in the OA and OM training sets, the expression levels of key M0R-DEGs in the OA (GSE82107 and GSE178557) and OM (GSE6269 and GSE16129) validation sets were extracted using the "limma" package. The results were then visualized as violin plots.

Establishment of experimental OA and OM models

A total of 48 male SD rats, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing an average of 350 g, were acquired from Chushang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Kunming, China). Of these, 36 rats were designated for constructing OM and sham operation models, while the remaining 12 were designated for OA and sham operation models. The 36 rats were then randomly divided into two groups (OM and sham operation), with 18 rats in each group, according to our previously established protocol for creating rat tibial model of OM [41].

The remaining 12 rats were randomly allocated to either the OA model group or the sham operation group. Initially, anesthesia was administered via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital. A longitudinal incision was made along the medial side of the patella, followed by sequential dissection of the skin and joint capsule to expose the knee joint. The patella was gently dislocated laterally to expose the intra-articular structures. The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) was identified and completely transected transversely using micro-scissors. The surgical site was then irrigated with saline to remove tissue debris, and the joint capsule and skin were sutured in layers. The sham surgery group underwent the same surgical approach, including the longitudinal incision and joint exposure, but the ACL was left intact. After saline irrigation, the wound was sutured in layers. After 8 weeks, the rats were euthanized, and knee joint tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis. The study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Clinical sample collection

To validate the findings from bioinformatics and animal models, we collected clinical bone tissue samples. Articular cartilage tissue was obtained from patients with end-stage OA who underwent total knee arthroplasty (n = 3). OM bone tissue was collected from patients undergoing surgical debridement for OM (n = 3). A small amount of control bone tissue was obtained from healthy patients undergoing orthopedic surgeries (n = 3), such as those undergoing amputation for severe trauma or hip replacement for severe femoral neck fractures. After collection, all samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Joint Logistics Support Force 920 Hospital (2023–007-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RT-qPCR

For RT-qPCR analysis, following successful establishment of the OA and OM models, total RNA from knee joint tissues in the OA model group and tibial tissues in the OM model group was extracted. Total RNA was extracted from cartilage tissues (OA models and human OA samples) and tibial/bone tissues (OM models and human OM samples) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Inc, Carlsbad, CA). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SweScrip RT I First Strand Synthesis Kit (Service Bio, Guangzhou, China). Amplification was performed using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: 95 °C for 1 min for pre-denaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences for rat and human genes are listed in Table S2 and Table S3, respectively.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, knee joint tissues from the OA model and tibial tissues from the OM model were collected, along with corresponding tissues from the sham operation group. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and processed as detailed in our previous studies [42]. Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0) (Media Cybernetics Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) was used to assess the area and density of the stained region, and the integrated optical density (IOD) value of the IHC section was calculated. Subsequently, the value of mean density (mean density = IOD/area) was computed to reflect the concentration of the target protein per unit area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were conducted using t tests or Wilcoxon's rank-sum tests to compare significant differences between two groups, and the Kruskal–Walli’s test for comparing more than two groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were processed and analyzed using R software (version 4.3.1) and its packages.

Results

Study flowchart

The overall flowchart of this study is depicted in Fig. 1. Shared M0R-DEGs were identified from 4 OA datasets and 1 OM dataset obtained from the GEO database. The intersecting genes were then further screened as characteristic M0R-DEGs by machine learning models. Subsequently, a diagnostic model, clustering analysis of molecular subtypes were performed based on these characteristic biomarkers, and experimental validation of animal models in OA and OM were conducted, respectively.

Immune infiltration analysis in OA and OM

Both OA and OM mediate and regulate complex immune microenvironments, and the search for immune regulatory targets may facilitate the advancement of diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, the abundance of 22 typical immune cell infiltrates between OA and OM disease groups and healthy controls was evaluated using the CIBERSORT algorithm (Figs. 2A–D). In OA patients, significant differences in the abundance of infiltration of 11 out of the 22 immune cell types were observed. Plasma cells, T follicular helper cells, gamma delta T cells, activated NK cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages and resting dendritic cells were found to be significantly upregulated. In OM patients, significant differences in the infiltration abundance of 13 out of the 22 immune cells were noted, with regulatory T cells (Tregs), monocytes, M0 macrophages, resting mast cells, and neutrophils being significantly higher. Notably, higher infiltration abundance of M0 Macrophages was observed in both OA and OM patients. The immune infiltration analysis in this study provided preliminary evidence suggesting that M0 macrophages may play a role in the shared inflammatory environment of OA and OM.

Identification of M0R-DEGs in OA and OM

To explore the potential biological links in the pathogenesis of OA and OM, DEGs were screened through differential expression analysis between OA or OM samples and healthy control samples (Figs. 3A–D). The OA training set yielded 384 up-regulated genes and 291 down-regulated genes (Fig. 3E and F), while the OM training set yielded 1044 up-regulated genes and 893 down-regulated genes. Given that the results of immune infiltration analysis indicated a high proportion of infiltrating M0 macrophages in patients with OA and OM, we attempted to screen for DEGs significantly associated with M0 macrophages in 31 shared genes through correlation analysis (Fig. 3G–I). 7 M0R-DEGs eventually met the screening criteria “P value < 0.05 and |r|> 0.30”, and these M0R-DEGs may be involved in the immune regulation of OA and OM pathogenesis, in conjunction with M0 macrophages.

Results of enrichment analysis

31 shared DEGs may be involved in the developmental progression of both OA and OM, and the potential biological functions and signaling pathways of these DEGs were explored by GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The results of GO analysis by “metascape” online website suggested that shared DEGs of OA and OM were mainly involved in inflammatory responses, positive regulation of cell migration, cargo receptor activity, canonical Wnt signaling pathway, regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade and regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway, and these enriched biological functions are closely related to the immune-inflammatory response, apoptosis, cell differentiation and cell migration of the skeletal muscle system (Fig. 3J). The results of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that the shared DEGs were mainly enriched in PPAR signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway and cancer-related signaling pathway (including prostate cancer and proteoglycans in cancer) (Fig. 3K).

Screening characteristic M0R-DEGs based on machine learning

To further identify M0R-DEGs with diagnostic and typing features, the most appropriate machine learning was selected by comparing the residual values and diagnostic accuracies of both RF and SVM (Figs. 4A–C). RF was found to have lower residual error and higher diagnostic accuracy compared to SVM, and the top 5 characteristic M0R-DEGs with scores greater than 5 were obtained. In addition, 6 characteristic M0R-DEGs were screened using LASSO regression model and 10 times cross-validation (Fig. 4D and E), which were ultimately combined with the M0R-DEGs obtained by RF to identify five intersecting genes, three genes on chromosome 1 and two genes on chromosome 2 (Fig. 4F-I).

To further explore the potential molecular mechanisms of these shared M0R-DEGs in OA and OM, the potential biological functions of each M0R-DEG were analyzed by sgGSEA and sgGSVA (Fig. S2 and S3). In the GSEA analysis, MMP9 was mainly enriched in allograft rejection, SPARC and TIMP1 were primarily enriched in adipocytokine signaling pathway, SPRY1 and TMEM45A were mainly enriched in cell cycle. In the GSVA pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 5C and D), high expression of MMP9 was significantly correlated with enrichment of pathways related to autoimmune thyroid disease, allograft rejection and asthma. High expression of TIMP1 was significantly associated with tyrosine metabolism, histidine metabolism and drug metabolism cytochrome P450 pathways, while low expression of TIMP1 was associated with riboflavin metabolism, heparin sulfate through glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis and glycan biosynthesis pathways. Similarly, high expression of SPARC was associated with the activation of tyrosine and histidine metabolism, while low expression of SPARC was associated with ECM receptor interaction. In contrast, high expression of SPRY1 was significantly correlated with enrichment of pathways related to the glycan biosynthesis pathway and was associated with pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis. Low expression of SPRY1 was significantly correlated with the primary immunodeficiency pathway. Conversely, high expression of TMEM4A was associated with activation of the primary immunodeficiency pathway, while low expression of TMEM4A was significantly linked to chondroitin sulfate through glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-related pathways, which paralleled the pathway associated with low expression of TIMP1. These results suggested that the five shared M0R-DEGs may be associated with different immunometabolic pathways, providing clues to potential crosstalk regulation in OA and OM. However, it should be noted that ssGSEA/ssGSVA analyses reflect co-expression and enrichment correlations rather than causal activation, and further experimental studies are required to validate the mechanistic roles of these genes in OA and OM.

Diagnostic model based on shared M0R-DEGs in OA and OM

Early diagnosis of OA and OM is crucial for disease treatment, prompting exploration into the diagnostic value of shared M0R-DEGs for OA and OM patients and their potential role as early diagnostic biomarkers. In the OA training set (Fig. 5A-C), nomogram models were constructed to predict the incidence of OA, and while the fitted curves generally showed a good fit, the DCA curves suggested that clinical decisions based on the five shared M0R-DEGs might benefit OA patients. In the OM training set (Fig. 5D–F), it was observed that the calibration curves for the nomogram model were nearly identical to the ideal model, and the DCA curves also indicated that using the five shared M0R-DEGs to construct a nomogram might be beneficial for OM patients.

Furthermore, the logistic regression scores for OA and OM were obtained by multiplying the gene expression by the corresponding coefficients based on the coefficients of the five characteristic M0R-DEGs and summing them. The AUC values of the logistic regression model were found to be 0.882 in the OA training set and 0.812 in the validation set, indicating high diagnostic efficacy (Fig. 5G–J). ROC curves for the five feature genes to predict OA were further constructed, and it was shown that all five M0R-DEGs in the OA training set had good diagnostic efficacy (all AUC > 0.70), and four of the remaining M0R-DEGs in the validation set, except SPRY1, also displayed good diagnostic efficacy. In both the OM training set and validation set, the AUC values of the logistic regression model exceeded 0.90, suggesting good discriminatory ability for diagnosing OM patients (Fig. 5K–N). In addition, all five M0R-DEGs in the OM training set were found to have good diagnostic validity, and three of the remaining M0R-DEGs in the validation set, except SPARC and TIMP1, also demonstrated good diagnostic validity, with MMP9 being particularly significant (AUC = 0.914).

Identification of M0R-clustering patterns based on shared M0R-DEGs in OA and OM

Disease typing to identify molecular patterns with similar characteristics facilitates stratified diagnosis and targeted therapy for OA and OM. Consequently, M0R-clustering patterns for OA and OM were constructed based on 5 shared M0R-DEGs using an unsupervised clustering algorithm. For M0R-clustering patterns in OA (Fig. 6A–C), two different M0R-clustering patterns were identified based on the concordance score and CDF curves, which were presented in the form of a typing heatmap (Fig. 6D). The expression differences of the five shared M0R-DEGs between the two different M0R-clustering patterns were further explored (Fig. 6E), and significant differences were found in the expression of the 5 M0R-DEGs between the two patterns, with MMP9, TIMP1, SPARC and TMEM45A being highly expressed in OA cluster 1 and only SPRY1 being highly expressed in OA cluster 2. The PCA results suggested that significant differences were absent between the two different patterns of OA samples (Fig. 6F). Similarly, for M0R-clustering patterns in OM (Figs. 6G-I), two different M0R-clustering patterns were identified based on the concordance score and CDF curves, and these were presented in the form of a typing heatmap (Fig. 6J). Differential analysis revealed that the three M0R-DEGs exhibited significant differences between the two patterns, with SPRY1 highly expressed in OM cluster 1 and MMP9 and TMEM45A highly expressed in OM cluster 2 (Fig. 6K). The PCA results suggested that significant differences were also absent between the two different patterns of OM samples (Fig. 6L).

Identification of cell types in the OA synovial microenvironment

Previous studies have highlighted the crucial role of macrophages in the immune microenvironment of OA patients [43, 44]. To explore the role of macrophages in OA, synovial samples from three OA patients were downloaded and analyzed. Following the filtering and quality control of single-cell data, PCA dimensionality reduction was performed, and an appropriate resolution was selected. Cell distribution was then displayed using tSNE (Fig. 8A and Fig. S4-6). Eight cell clusters were eventually identified (Fig. 8B), seven distinct cell types were annotated manually: osteoclasts (OC), proliferative immune cells (ProIC), fibroblasts, M2 macrophages, monocytes, adipocytes, and endothelial cells (EC). The top 10 DEGs among these cell types were visualized using a heatmap (Figs. 8C).

Cell cycle and cell communication patterns

The cell cycle, a crucial regulator of cell replication and division, was analyzed in various cell types from OA synovial tissues using the “Tricycle” method (Fig. 8D and E). OCs were predominantly in the M.G1 phase, ProICs in the G1.S phase, fibroblasts in the G2 phase, while M2 macrophages, monocytes, and ECs were mainly in the G2.M phase. Subsequently, gene distribution of five shared M0R-DEGs (MMP9, TIMP1, SPARC, TMEM45A, and SPRY1) was displayed using UMAP (Fig. 8F). Comprehensive enrichment analysis was conducted using the “irGSEA” package, which revealed significant upregulation of myogenesis processes in OCs and downregulation in M2 macrophages and monocytes (Fig. 8G). Similarly, angiogenesis processes were upregulated in OCs and downregulated in M2 macrophages. These findings suggest that OCs and M2 macrophages may regulate myogenesis and angiogenesis in the OA synovial immune microenvironment. Notably, inflammatory responses were found to be significantly upregulated in M2 macrophages and monocytes but downregulated in fibroblasts. Consistently, the TNFA-SIGNALING-VIA-NFKB and IL6 − JAK − STAT3 pathways were significantly upregulated in monocytes, while TNFA-SIGNALING-VIA-NFKB was downregulated in fibroblasts. Overall, this study indicates that myogenesis, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses are crucial in the OA synovial immune microenvironment.

Previous research has indicated that cell–cell communication may influence cellular differentiation, suggesting a significant role in the progression of OA [45]. The most signaling activities were observed in OC, ProIC, and fibroblasts within the synovium of OA patients, with the primary communication being between OC and fibroblasts (Fig. 8H). Further analysis revealed that the CD99 signal originated from OC, M2 macrophages, and fibroblasts (Fig. 8I).

Functional analysis of TIMP1 in M2 macrophages

Based on previous differential expression analysis of 31 shared DEGs between OA and OM, the “AddModuleScore” method was employed to calculate the “sharedgene score” (Fig. S7A). Significantly higher scores were observed in OC compared to the other six cell types, and fibroblasts exhibited significantly higher scores than ProIC and M2 macrophages. An analysis of expression differences among the five shared M0R-DEGs across different cell types revealed that SPARC was significantly upregulated in OC, TMEM45A and TIMP1 in fibroblasts, ProIC, and OC, MMP9 in monocytes, and SPRY1 in ECs (Fig. S7B and C).

Given the significant correlation of TIMP1 expression with M0, M1, and M2 macrophages in OA patient immune infiltration analyses, the potential molecular mechanism of TIMP1 in M2 macrophages was further investigated (Fig. S7D). Genes were categorized into “high TIMP1-M2 macrophages” and “low TIMP1-M2 macrophages” based on average TIMP1 expression levels in M2 macrophages. The top 9 DEGs between these groups were extracted, with C1QB, CFD, S100A9, and NCF1 significantly overexpressed in the high TIMP1-M2 macrophage group, while ABL2, CCL3L3, INSIG1, PNRC1, and RGS1 were overexpressed in the low TIMP1-M2 macrophage group (Fig. S7E). KEGG pathway analysis suggested that TIMP1 in M2 macrophages was primarily enriched in pathways related to Staphylococcus aureus infection (Fig. S7F). GO analysis indicated that TIMP1 in M2 macrophages might be involved in amide (any derivative of an oxoacid in which an acidic hydroxy group has been replaced by an amino or substituted amino group) and peptide binding (Fig. S7G). GSEA analysis revealed that TIMP1 participated in the TNFA-SIGNALING-VIA-NFKB signaling pathway in M2 macrophages, with high expression of TIMP1 being associated with downregulation of this pathway (Fig. S7H). In summary, TIMP1 may regulate inflammation, playing a role in the immune modulation of the OA synovium.

In the OA validation set (Fig. 9A–E), MMP9 and TMEM45A were significantly highly expressed in disease samples, no significant differences were observed in the expression levels of SPARC, SPRY1 and TMP1 between the two groups, consistent with the expression trends in the OA training set. In the OM validation set (Fig. 9F–J), MMP9 and SPARC were highly expressed in disease samples, while SPRY1 and TMEM45A showed high expression in healthy control samples.

Discussion

As a low-grade inflammatory disease, OA may lead to the complication of chronic OM with the development of the disease and bacterial invasion. An increase in the aging population and the rising incidence of diabetes have led to higher incidences of combined OA and OM [46]. Previous studies have indicated that rare pathogenic bacteria such as Kingella kingae are now considered key pathogens in children with osteoarticular infections complicated by OM, and severe cases may result in bacteremia [47, 48]. However, in the recent years, the treatment of OA has relied on earlier conservative measures to delay disease progression and later replacement surgery to improve quality of life. This surgery primarily stems from difficulties in early diagnosis of OA, with treatment often commencing only after symptoms have appeared and diagnoses have been confirmed, resulting in suboptimal treatment outcomes [49, 50]. The clinical treatment of OM is also challenging, and despite complete surgery and antibiotic treatment, the recurrence rate has not improved in the recent years [51]. Therefore, beginning with the early inflammation and immune microenvironment, exploration of the early pathological mechanisms of OA and OM is considered beneficial for the development of diagnostic biomarkers and immune-targeted therapies. With the advancements in sequencing methods and the development of databases, the stratification of diseases, diagnosis, and treatment methods based on specific genes have become possible.

As an immune cell, macrophages are considered key mediator of tissue remodeling in innate immune responses [52, 53]. In the microenvironment of bone infection, macrophages also play a crucial role in defending against skeletal infections and maintaining the balance of osteoblast-osteoclast dynamics [54]. During the development of OA, synovial macrophages are activated and produce inflammatory responses through various pathways [55]. Kraus et al. [56] discovered evidence of activated macrophages in human OA through dual knee SPECT-CT imaging, and these macrophages were associated with the progression and pain of OA. Notably, targeting macrophages or related inflammatory signaling pathways may improve symptoms in OA patients. Similarly, experimental OA studies in rat models indicated that macrophages were activated throughout the entire process, especially in the early stages of OA progression [57, 58]. In the pathogenesis of OM, macrophage production is stimulated by S. aureus through the secreting toxins and subsequent evasion of the immune response, inducing macrophages to polarize toward the M2 phenotype, thereby creating favorable conditions for the formation of bacterial biofilms [59, 60]. These findings suggest the existence of shared macrophages with similar features in the inflammatory microenvironment of both OA and OM. However, the role of macrophages in the pathogenesis of OA and OM has been largely understudied.

Here, multiple bioinformatics methods were integrated to screen and identify DEGs significantly associated with M0 macrophages, and well-performed diagnostic models (all AUC values > 0.80) and molecular patterns of features were further constructed. Firstly, through immune infiltration, correlation analysis and machine learning model identification, five shared M0R-DEGs were identified. Based on these DEGs, diagnostic models for OA and OM were constructed, and the results from both the training set and validation set indicated good diagnostic efficacy. Compared with traditional inflammatory factors such as C-reactive protein [61], as well as novel diagnostic markers such as microRNA [62] and immune-related genes [63], the diagnostic model based on our M0R-DEGs demonstrated superior diagnostic efficacy. Subsequently, unsupervised clustering analysis was used to classify OA and OM samples and establish different molecular patterns. The M0R-pattern suggested that the five shared M0R-DEGs could differentiate OA and OM into two distinct molecular patterns.

Classifying diseases is beneficial for personalized and targeted therapy. Bondeson et al. [64] used anti-CD14-conjugated magnetic beads to remove macrophages and found that the expression of IL-1 and TGF-β in synovial cells was significantly downregulated, along with a decrease in MMP expression. This provides a new perspective on the mechanisms of inflammation and bone destruction in OA. Blom et al. [54] further confirmed the crucial role of macrophages in MMP-mediated cartilage degradation in vivo, as macrophage depletion resulted in a marked decrease in MMP3 and MMP9, especially in the early phase of MMP activity in OA. Interestingly, when we further analyzed the immune cell and inflammatory factor infiltration in the two different M0R-pattern of OA, IL1B, IL4 and IL7 were found to be more enriched in OA cluster 2, while MMP2, MMP9, MMP11 and MMP14 were more predominant in OA cluster 1. This may be due to the clusters being in different stages of OA, such as early and late stages. Meanwhile, we observed high levels of infiltration of IL-1B, MMP3 and MMP8 in the OA cluster 2. Given that MMP often mediates the activity of osteoclasts under inflammatory conditions [65], OM cluster 2 is likely to represent a cluster with severe inflammatory responses and bone destruction.

It is worth noting that, compared with the other 4 M0R-DEGs, MMP9 gene has a stronger correlation with M0 macrophages. As an important matrix-degrading enzyme, MMP9 can promote the destruction of the soft tissue around the cartilage and the occurrence of inflammatory reactions in the environment of OA [66]. According to previous studies, significant elevations in both gene and protein expression levels of MMP9 have been observed in patients with OA, with consistent results were observed in OA models of rat and horse [67–69]. Moreover, under OM conditions, S.aureus upregulates the expression of MMP9 in a dose-dependent manner, thereby promoting the formation of osteoclasts, which indicates the important role of MMP9 in OM [70]. In the recent years, MMP9 has been identified as a specific biomarker for OA and OM [63, 71] has been demonstrated by our ROC results. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of OA and OM targeting MMP9 may provide a basis for the development of novel therapeutic approaches.

However, certain limitations are still present in this study. Firstly, the sample size of public datasets was limited, and larger datasets for OA and OM comorbidity are needed to verify the value of diagnostic model. Secondly, although our bioinformatics findings have been validated by animal model expression, further in-depth mechanistic studies are needed to explore the regulatory roles of M0R-DEGs in OA and OM. This will help to provide clear evidence of the involvement of key genes in the regulation of M0 macrophages. Thirdly, the molecular patterns of OA and OM require validation from large-sample clinical cohort and prospective studies. Fourthly, the protein level changes observed through IHC reflect the overall tissue expression and lack the ability to analyze the contribution of specific cell subpopulations (such as TIMP1 in macrophage subgroups). This challenge may be further addressed in the future by utilizing spatial proteomics techniques.

In addition, although valuable insights into OA have been gained from our scRNA-seq analysis, it is important to acknowledge some inherent limitations of this technology. First, the tissue dissociation process required prior to sequencing may result in the complete loss of spatial context information [72]. While the CellChat tool can infer potential communication networks, it cannot recreate the precise physical proximity and anatomical microenvironment that cells rely on for communication within the synovial membrane. This may lead to an incomplete understanding of the true microenvironmental regulation of macrophages and other cells. Secondly, scRNA-seq is prone to technical noise and dropout events, and some low-abundance mRNAs may not be detected in individual cells, which could result in underestimating the expression levels and significance of such genes [73, 74]. In addition, the dissociation process itself may induce cellular stress and alter gene expression profiles, potentially introducing experimental bias. Finally, our analysis may not be sufficient to clearly identify the cell subpopulations that play a critical role in the pathogenesis of OA. Future research that integrates spatial transcriptomics or multiplex immunofluorescence techniques with scRNA-seq data will be crucial for validating predicted intercellular communications and precisely mapping the expression localization of key M0R-DEGs in the complete tissue architecture [75, 76].

Conclusion

This study represented the comprehensive and systematic screening of shared M0 macrophage infiltration-related genes in OA and OM. A robust model for diagnosing patients and stratifying treatment approaches has been established. Furthermore, the shared M0R-DEGs identified might play a role in the interaction of metabolic pathways in autoimmune diseases. Notably, more intense inflammatory responses and greater bone damage are linked to OA cluster 1 and OM cluster 2. This study revealed shared molecular characteristics between OA and OM and suggested that these genes may serve as directions for future functional studies.
