Unlocking the hidden pathways: river channel delineation and reservoir characterization in Shurijeh Formation, Kopeh Dagh Basin
Authors: Vali Mehdipour, Zahra Sadeghtabaghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Shurijeh Formation is the primary gas reservoir within the Kopeh Dagh-Amu Darya Basin, deposited in a fluvial and deltaic environment. Due to significant lateral variability associated with floodplain and river channel settings, detailed characterization of river channels is essential for optimal reservoir management. This study employs petrophysical data from 44 wells, complemented by seismic cube, thin section study, and production records, to investigate zone D of the Shurijeh Formation comprehensively. In this paper, we aim to innovatively apply the concept of coefficient of variation (CV) in fluvial type reservoirs. Final results show that zone D ranges from 40 to 50 m thick. The assessment indicates that the interior of the river channel forms a high-quality reservoir zone, with declining reservoir properties beyond its boundaries. Petrography analysis confirm the presence of different conditions inside and outside the fluvial channels. This research highlights the critical role of accurately identifying river channels to optimize well placement and improve hydrocarbon recovery in this basin. Additionally, using the CV parameter, a clear distinction can be understood between river channel sediments and floodplain deposits, which can play a significant role in determining the location of future wells and help prevent dry-hole drilling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27615-4.

Introduction

Shurijeh Formation is the most significant gas reservoir in the Kopeh Dagh Amu-Darya basin. This siliciclastic Lower Cretaceous formation is considered the main gas reservoir in Iran and Turkmenistan (Iranian fields of Khangiran, Gonbadli, and Turkmen fields of Bayram-Ali, Dauletabad, Chartak, Shatlyk, and Malay). “Shurijeh” is the Iranian name of this succession whereas, other names such as “Karabil”, “Almurad”, and “Shatlyk (Qezeltash)” are used in adjacent countries (Afghanistan and Turkmenistan)1–4. The Shurijeh succession consists of deltaic and fluvial lithofacies in the lower sections and fluvial to open marine lithofacies in the upper sections. This is overlain by carbonate Tirgan Formation. This siliciclastic formation was deposited on the other carbonate formation, Mozduran, which is the other significant reservoir in this basin5,6.

The high importance of Shurijeh Formation has caused this formation to be investigated by researchers from various aspects. Regarding the importance of the depositional environment and its reservoir properties, Moussavi-Harami and Brenner2,7, and Hosseinyar, Moussavi-Harami8 can be cited. They investigated the diagenetic history, sedimentological, and petrological aspects of the Shurijeh Formation in the eastern Kopeh Dagh, as well as depositional environment of the Shurijeh Formation in Iran and Turkmenistan (fluvial environment). Also, the equivalents of this formation in Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, and Afghanistan were completely assessed by Ulmishek4.

Moreover, regarding the issues already assessed for this formation, these researches can also be mentioned. Moussavi-Harami, Mahboubi6 determined the calcrete formation mechanism of the Shurijeh Formation using geochemical and petrographical studies. Mortazavi, Moussavi-Harami5 evaluated the oxygen and carbon isotope compositions of the Shurijeh Pedogenic carbonates. Moreover, weathering process and tectonic settings of the Shurijeh Formation were investigated using paleogeographical and petrographical data in the western and central parts of the Kopeh Dagh Basin9. Finally, Hosseinyar, Moussavi-Harami8 analyzed the facies of this siliciclastic succession in the Kopeh Dagh Amu-Darya Basin. Identification of fluvial channels using seismic attributes was done by Hosseinyar, Moussavi-Harami10, Hosseinyar, Moussavi-Harami11 and Mohebian, Kadkhodaei12.

Considering that this formation was deposited in a fluvial environment, it is obvious that it has facies changes laterally4,11,13. Given that the facies of the flood plain and river channels are very influential in the quality of reservoir rock14,15, the investigation of the condition of river channels plays a very vital role in better understanding the conditions of the reservoir and also predicting possible stratigraphic reservoirs. It should be noted that simply examining the fluvial environment is not sufficient, and determining the river channel location is also very important. In other words, if the river channel environment is not correctly identified, new wells may be drilled in the floodplain area and drilled wells will be dry. Floodplain sediments, unlike channel sediments, are very fine and their reservoir properties decrease significantly. Therefore, in fluvial systems, it is possible for one nearby well to be dry while another produces well. This underlines the importance of a more detailed examination of fluvial sedimentary environments. In this paper, despite valuable previous studies, we have attempted to assess this fluvial environment from several perspectives to achieve a better understanding and to correctly determine the location of future well drilling to prevent drilling dry wells.

This paper aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of zone D of the Shurijeh Formation. To achieve this, raw and interpreted petrophysical data from 44 wells within the area were utilized. Additionally, production data, seismic cube, and thin section studies were also incorporated into the analysis.

Geological settings

While closing of the Palaeotethys Ocean between Eurasia and Gondwana and along with the Cimmerian orogenic phase, the intracontinental Kopeh Dagh Amu-Darya Basin was formed. This basin which is located in the countries of Iran, Turkmenistan, Afghanistan and Uzbekistan (Fig. 1), is of global importance due to its abundant gas reserves4,16,17. The southern border of this basin is limited to suture zone of the Palaeotethys16,18. In this area, the sedimentation is almost uninterrupted from the middle Jurassic to the Miocene4,13. However, in the western and southern sections of this basin, there are hiatus events during cretaceous to Paleocene16,18. The thickness of sediments in Sarakhs area is approximately more than 5000 m1. Simultaneously with the closing of the Neotethys ocean and the Alpine orogeny (Neogene-Quaternary), the tectonic regime of the area was compressional18. This compressional regime in the region has caused the occurring of faults, anticline, and synclinal structures with northwest-southeast direction in the Iranian and Turkmen parts of this basin8,16,18.

With the expansive regression of the sea in the Late Jurassic - Early Cretaceous, the clastic facies of the Shurijeh Formation began to be deposited. The sedimentary environment of this formation is the types of braided and meandering rivers. In the Amu-Darya basin, this formation was deposited between the carbonate Gaurdak and Okuz Bulak formations (Fig. 2). The Shurijeh Formation is the most significant gas reservoir of the Iranian gas fields of Khangiran and Gonbadli, and the Turkmen field of Dauletabad4. The oldest sedimentary rocks of this area belong to the Devonian-Triassic (Fig. 2). Materials and methods

This study is based on data obtained from 44 wells, each of which provided both raw and evaluated petrophysical logs. The specific logs utilized in this analysis include computed gamma ray response (CGR), bulk density (RHOB), effective porosity, sandstone (Quartz) volume, shale volume, and water saturation. Additionally, a three-dimensional seismic cube from the study area was incorporated into the research. Importantly, inversion techniques were applied to derive acoustic impedance data, which further enhanced the analytical framework of the study. A comprehensive discussion regarding the application of this data and the methodologies employed is presented in the subsequent sections. Furthermore, well production data were utilized to better assess the location of the river channel and the production conditions. Additionally, efforts were made to improve the understanding of the depositional environment of the studied reservoir through petrography analysis. It is important to note that this study focuses exclusively on zone D to investigate the fluvial channels of the Shurijeh Formation. This specific zone is characterized by the presence of sandstone and fine-grained sediments. In this study, we analyzed maps pertaining to zone D. Utilizing both well data and seismic information, we generated maps that illustrate lateral variations within the zone.

Petrophysical-based maps

In current study, the petrophysical evaluation employed the probabilistic ‘Multimin’ method, which solves simultaneous equations to estimate mineral volumes. Using Geolog software, three key parameters were determined: effective porosity, water saturation, and quartz volume. Effective porosity was derived from neutron-density cross-plots, while water saturation was calculated using the Indonesian model. The results were validated based on graphic well logs, well test data, and core analysis.

To investigate the lateral variations of reservoir properties, maps were generated from petrophysical logs in the study area. The 2D maps generated from raw petrophysical log data are the density and CGR maps. As previously noted, the petrophysical log values for each well location within zone D were averaged, and the final maps were constructed from these averaged values. Effective porosity and sandstone (Quartz) volume logs derived from evaluated petrophysical logs were also utilized in the preparation of the maps. Given the intention to investigate the sandy channels within zone D of the Shurijeh Formation, the lateral sandstone alteration map is of significant importance.

Another map presented in this study is the net pay map, which was generated using data from evaluated petrophysical logs of effective porosity and water saturation. These interpreted petrophysical logs were modeled in three dimensions and subsequently combined with fluid contact information to create the net map. The cut-off values for porosity and water saturation were set at 4% and 80%, respectively. Following the application of these cut-off thresholds and the fluid contact data, the net map was developed to laterally identify the net pays. It should be noted that the cutoffs mentioned in this study were calculated using the cumulative hydrocarbon method (S-Curve), and additional figures and descriptions are provided in the supplementary file.

Another map analyzed in this study is the coefficient of variation (CV) map. The CV serves as an indicator of variability and is determined by taking the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of a set of samples, with the condition that the mean is not zero. The CV formula is as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CV=\raisebox{1ex}{$\sigma\:$}\!\left/\:\!\raisebox{-1ex}{$\mu\:$}\right.$$\end{document}

where, CV represents the coefficient of variation, σ denotes the standard deviation, and µ signifies the mean. According to Lake and Jensen20, permeability values have a CV of less than 0.5 in homogeneous materials, between 0.5 and 1.0 in heterogeneous materials, and greater than 1.0 in highly heterogeneous environments. In this study, CV has been calculated using porosity values from zone D. CV of log-based porosity can serve as a useful heterogeneity index when core data is unavailable. It should also be noted that CV values are normalized after calculation21.

Maps prepared from seismic attributes

Seismic attributes are mathematical functions obtained from seismic data, which can be analyzed in both time and frequency domains22. Seismic inversion methods are extensively employed in the identification of oil and gas reservoirs, as well as in the assessment of the physical properties of reservoir rocks. Analyzing acoustic impedance data offers deeper insights into geological structures and enhances the accuracy of subsurface interpretations23–25.

To address the challenges posed by more intricate geological conditions and the reduced risks inherent in hydrocarbon exploration, Whitcombe introduced a new methodology known as Extended Elastic Inversion (EEI) in 200226. This approach is particularly effective for differentiating fluid types and lithological characteristics, especially in zones where the acoustic impedances of gas-saturated sands and adjacent shales are nearly identical. The EEI technique involves the combination of intercept and gradient attributes at various angles to emphasize different parameters of the reservoir26–28. In this paper, to improve the differentiation of lithological types within sandy reservoirs, Extended Elastic Inversion (EEI) was derived using a model-driven inversion algorithm. Mu-Rho, Mu, and Brittleness were performed using a model-driven algorithm applied to a pre-stack time-migrated 3D seismic cube. The Shurijeh Formation is characterized by sediment deposition from river systems, making the identification of river channels particularly advantageous for resource exploration. To facilitate this process, a sand volume trend map has been developed applying smoothing techniques to log data (Fig. 7A), which allows for the detection of variations in sand distribution. It should be noted that the smoothing technique employed in this study is the “Mean” method within the smoothing operation. Under this method, every sample value is substituted by the average of its adjacent values plus itself, and increasing the neighborhood size leads to more pronounced smoothing. By analyzing the abundance of sand (based on sandstone volume map), it becomes possible to approximate the locations of river channels (Fig. 7B).

The comprehension of river channel dynamics is crucial in the context of oil and gas field development. Accurate identification of these channels enables more precise predictions of field development plans and helps minimize additional costs associated with dry drilling operations. It is essential to recognize that the strategies employed for development in reservoirs formed by river sediment deposition differ significantly from those applied in structural traps. While drilling in structural traps typically follows a systematic approach targeting anticline crest, this method proves inefficient for reservoirs of fluvial sediments.

Moreover, the inability to accurately identify river channels can obscure the spatial characteristics of both the channels and the surrounding floodplains, often leading to unsuccessful drilling attempts in floodplain sediments. In reservoirs formed by fluvial deposits, significant lateral variations in reservoir properties are anticipated. Generally, reservoir conditions within river channels are favorable due to the prevalence of sandy sediments, whereas conditions tend to decline in areas outside these channels, where finer-grained floodplain sediments dominate.

Porosity is a critical parameter in reservoir characterization; therefore, the subsequent step involves investigating porosity variations within this region. To construct an accurate porosity map, data derived from the interpretation of petrophysical logs were supplemented with acoustic impedance. Given the inverse relationship between acoustic impedance and porosity, the inverse values of acoustic impedance (Fig. 8A) were utilized as a trend for porosity map.

The resulting porosity trend map delineates the channel boundary, revealing that areas of elevated porosity are concentrated within this boundary while exhibiting a decline in porosity in the surrounding regions (Fig. 8B). This pattern suggests the presence of a sand channel characterized by high porosity, potentially encased by dense, fine-grained floodplain sediments. Such findings are significant for understanding the reservoir’s potential and optimizing hydrocarbon exploration and production strategies.

In the subsequent step, efforts were made to elucidate the relationship between raw petrophysical logs and the delineation of channel and floodplain boundaries. Figure 9A illustrates the variation map of the CGR log. The map indicates that high CGR values are predominantly located outside the channel, suggesting the presence of shale-rich floodplain sediments. Conversely, the areas within the channel exhibit lower CGR values, reinforcing the characteristics of clean river channel sands. Additionally, Fig. 9B presents the variation map of the density log (RHOB). This map reveals that the density values outside the river channel are significantly higher than those recorded within the channel, further supporting the differentiation between the floodplain and channel deposits.

Figure 10 depicts the net pay map for zone D of the Shurijeh Formation. The assessment incorporates porosity and water saturation logs, alongside data pertaining to the water contact levels. The figure demonstrates that the highest net pay areas are situated within the channel, exceeding 30 m in thickness. Outside the channel, there is a sharp decline in the net pay area, which diminishes sharply and ultimately approaches zero. The identification of channel boundaries, in conjunction with the assessment of the net pay map, is instrumental in guiding the planning and positioning of future drilling operations. This information is vital for optimizing resource extraction strategies and enhancing the understanding of the reservoir’s architecture.

Another valuable tool for analysis in this region is the Coefficient of Variation (CV) map, which was constructed in accordance with the methodology outlined in the Methods section. In this map (Fig. 11), areas delineated in white exhibit a CV of less than 0.5, while those highlighted in red display a CV ranging from 0.5 to 1. The concept of CV provides insights into sedimentary characteristics within the study area. Specifically, the white areas, characterized by a low CV, are indicative of homogeneous deposits. This homogeneity corresponds to the channel regions, where sediment size demonstrates minimal variation. In contrast, the red areas signify heterogeneous zones, typically associated with floodplain environments. These heterogeneous zones are characterized by a significant diversity in grain sizes, reflecting the complex depositional processes occurring in such environments. This CV map thus serves as an essential tool in understanding sediment distribution and texture variability within the channel and floodplain, contributing to a more comprehensive geological characterization of the area. Although in other studies, CV has been used to investigate heterogeneity in carbonate formations21,29, it is noted that CV results can also be usable for the clastic Shurijeh Formation as well. Changes in CV can effectively differentiate heterogeneity arising from fine-grained and shaly sediments outside the channel from sandy sediments inside the channel.

As previously mentioned, the EEI method was used to estimate the Mu, Mu-Rho, and brittleness parameters. Mu-Rho (Lamé impedances), which is commonly used AVO attributes, is applied to improve the identification of pore fluids and differentiate between various lithologies. EEI techniques can be utilized to distinguish between reservoir and non-reservoir areas. Figure 12 illustrates the maps of Mu-Rho, Mu, and Brittleness. These maps effectively differentiate between reservoir and non-reservoir areas. The parts of the channel exhibiting favorable reservoir conditions are associated with elevated values of all three parameters. However, it is important to highlight that Mu and Brittleness are particularly proficient at distinguishing reservoir sands from the finer sediments found in floodplains. In other words, in a simplified view that only considers sand and shale, one can conclude that high values of the Mu, Mu-Rho, and Brittleness parameters correspond to sand deposition. More precisely, Mu-Rho (Fig. 12A) demonstrates good dispersion and best delineates the channel location. The Mu parameter (Fig. 12B), at an overall view, can identify the channel position, but it only works in a general sense and is not helpful in the details. The Brittleness parameter (Fig. 12C) also plays a significant role in determining the central regions of the channel. The correlations that these parameters show with lithology changes are verifiable, in line with studies previously conducted in other regions30,31.

Gas production results

Data pertaining to gas production from five wells situated within the study area were extracted from pertinent reports and subsequently reviewed in the context of this research. The primary objective of this study is to examine the relation between gas production and the established locations of the river channel. Figure 13 illustrates the distribution of wells A to E within the study area, from which gas production data has been collected. This reveals that wells A and C are situated outside the delineated channel boundary, while the remaining three wells, B, D, and E, are located within this boundary. Notably, the test results for well A indicate that no fluid was recovered from the Shurijeh-D reservoir, a finding attributed in part to its comparatively lower porosity relative to the other wells in the region. The absence of gas production from this well reinforces two key conclusions: first, the correct identification of the channel location, and second, the critical importance of precise phenomena detection in stratigraphic reservoirs. It is evident that the reservoir properties outside the channel differ significantly from those within.

In contrast to well A, all four other wells have reported gas and condensate production. A review of the documentation suggests that well B may be juxtaposed to an aquifer, a hypothesis corroborated by the information presented in Fig. 10 of this paper. Additionally, well B is characterized by a low discharge rate. The findings of this paper propose that the low flow rate observed in this well may be attributable to either diminished effective porosity in the vicinity or its proximity to a water source.

Significantly, well D has recorded the highest volume of gas production, followed by well E. Unlike wells A and B, both of these wells are positioned in regions exhibiting high effective porosity, with well D identified as the most productive well in the study area. Regarding well C, despite its location outside the proposed channel boundary, it remains a producing well. However, it is noteworthy that existing reports indicate well C has the lowest volume of gas production among the wells assessed. This observation may be attributed to the positioning of the well along the boundary of the channel. Given the alignment between the findings presented in this study and the production reports, it can be concluded that the proposed delineation of the river channel is largely accurate and reliable. The channel described in this paper covers an area of nearly 98 km². If the thickness of the zone D is approximately 40 m, the approximate volume of this channel is 4 km³. Petrography study

For better understanding, several thin sections of the Shurijeh Formation (zone D) were studied. Among all available thin sections, four thin sections from different parts of the channel (Fig. 14) were selected. As shown, thin sections I and II correspond to the interior of the channel. Thin section III corresponds to the channel- margin area, and thin section IV is from the floodplain area. To gain a better and deeper understanding, Fig. 15 shows the samples of thin sections from the four regions separately. Thin section I belongs to the area with a high sand volume. In this figure, quartz crystals are labeled with the letter “Q”. It is evident that the sand grains are free of any clay, which supports a clean sandstone with suitable reservoir quality. In thin section II, in addition to quartz crystals, feldspar (Fsp) crystals, and lithic (L) fragments are observed. This thin section also corresponds to the interior of the river channel. Thin section III represents the channel margin, where quartz crystals and lithic fragments occur against a silty matrix. It might be that the low production in Well C (Fig. 13) is related to lithofacies changes in this region. Figure 9 also confirms a relative increase in CGR in this area. Finally, Thin section IV belongs to the floodplain; it is observed that quartz grains are dispersed in a shaly background. Considering Fig. 9, the highest CGR value is present in this region. The petrographic observations, together with the distribution of reservoir properties inside and outside the channel, and the production results, are in full agreement.

In conclusion, the results of this study are consistent with other worldwide investigations that evaluate sandstone reservoirs. For instance, in clastic deposits deposited in a deltaic setting, improvements in reservoir properties are directly related to an increase in sand fraction. As depth increases into deeper marine environments, the sand content tends to decrease32. Prior work employing seismic data in sandstone reservoirs also demonstrates that lithostratigraphic variations can alter reservoir properties, sometimes to the extent that reservoirs become isolated33. Moreover, additional factors—such as cementation, clay mineralogy, pressure, fluid contact, faults, and the structure of sandstone reservoirs—can contribute to reservoir compartmentalization34.

Conclusions

The clastic Shurijeh Formation is the most significant gas reservoir within the Kopeh Dagh-Amu Darya sedimentary basin. Its depositional environment is characterized as fluvial and deltaic. Reservoirs deposited under fluvial conditions often exhibit substantial lateral variability due to the distinct characteristics of floodplain and river channel environments. Consequently, detailed investigations of river channels are of paramount importance. In this study, zone D of the Shurijeh Formation has been thoroughly examined using a combination of raw and interpreted petrophysical data from 44 wells within the area. Results indicate that zone D has a thickness ranging from 40 to 50 m. A sand volume distribution map was employed to identify the location of the river channel. The net pay map further confirms that thickness of the net thickness inside the channel reaches more than 30 m, while outside the channel it can drop to zero. The coefficient of variation map shows greater heterogeneity in floodplain areas. Analysis of production data revealed that wells drilled within the channel exhibit markedly better performance, whereas wells outside the channel face production issues. In conclusion, this study underscores the importance of accurately identifying river channels, which is instrumental in optimizing well placement and enhancing production strategies in this basin.

It should be emphasized that there are always uncertainties in the applied calculations and constructed models. For instance, determining the net pay of the reservoir has a direct and strong dependence on the defined cutoff values, and any change in the cutoffs leads to a change in the final result. Likewise, petrophysical interpretations and seismic models are not exempt from this rule. In this paper, we tried to examine the topic from several different viewpoints to reduce the role of uncertainties and the high sensitivity of certain parameters to a reasonably acceptable level. Nevertheless, it is recommended that future studies use more complete data, more precise interpretations, and newer methods for accurate assessment of river channels.
