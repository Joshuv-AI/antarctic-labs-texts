Bridging factors within Ukraine’s mental health system during wartime: a qualitative study of stakeholders’ perspectives
Authors: Alyona Mazhnaya, Anna Meteliuk, Sergiy Bogdanov, Emma E McGinty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 dramatically increased mental health needs while simultaneously disrupting service delivery systems. This study examines bridging factors—elements that connect the outer system and inner organizational context—within Ukraine’s evolving mental health system during an emergency context.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with 18 mental health stakeholders from diverse professional backgrounds and organizational types across Ukraine between March and October 2023. Using deductive and inductive qualitative analysis approaches, we identified and categorized bridging factors according to established typology.ResultsUsing previously described categories of bridging factors, we identified and classified bridging factors described by stakeholders: coordination structures, regulatory and guidance and funding frameworks (formal arrangements), information exchange and synthesis, referral networks, knowledge transfer (process-oriented bridging factors), and collaborations, recruitment, and individual boundary spanners (relational ties). The interviewees perceived these factors as functioning with varying degrees of formalization, effectiveness, and sustainability in facilitating resource flow, knowledge exchange, and mental health service delivery. Although some bridging factors were deliberately planned (e.g., coordination mechanisms), others remained underdeveloped (e.g., referral systems). Notably, individuals who crossed the institutional boundaries played a critical bridging role by leveraging multiple affiliations to connect actors within mental health system.ConclusionsIn the described conflict-affected setting, bridging factors served as essential adaptation mechanisms, creating connections between fragmented system components while simultaneously supporting emergency response functions and transformation toward an evidence-based community-oriented mental health system. The strategic development of these bridging mechanisms, particularly through formalizing effective relational networks, strengthening local coordination capacity, and optimizing resource pathways, can enhance system integration when structural reform resources are constrained.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-025-00825-7.Keywords: Bridging factors, Implementation science, Mental health, Ukraine, War, Health system

Introduction

Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 has profoundly disrupted mental health services [1–3] while dramatically increasing psychological distress and mental health conditions among Ukrainians [3–6], compounding the trauma and service challenges that had already accumulated since the initial Russian aggression began in 2014 [3, 7].

International humanitarian organizations advocate for mental health services as a core component of emergency response [8]. Implementing these services during the humanitarian crises requires complex coordination between sectors, stakeholders, and organizational levels, yet these interdependencies are often disrupted during conflict.

Ukraine’s mental health system prior to 2022 operated within a framework largely inherited from the Soviet era, characterized by reliance on large psychiatric institutions, limited community-based services, and persistent stigma surrounding mental health conditions [9–13]. Despite reform efforts toward deinstitutionalization initiated in 2014, the system continued to face substantial challenges including outdated inpatient facilities, high mental health stigma [14, 15], excessive constraints on psychiatric practice, limited data transparency, and institutional inertia towards change [9–14, 16]. Pre-invasion literature highlighted critical gaps in community-based care, patient-centered funding mechanisms, collaborative service planning and delivery, sustainable financing structures, and coordination across sectors [9–12, 14, 16]. These longstanding systemic weaknesses were subsequently intensified by the Russian invasion in 2022, as documented needs for mental health services surged while service infrastructure experienced disruption and displacement [1, 2, 4–6, 17].

In response to both historical deficits and war-related challenges, Ukraine is developing a comprehensive, community-based mental health system that represents a fundamental departure from the institution-based approach [18–21]. The Lancet Psychiatry Commission on mental health in Ukraine outlined a transformation toward a stepped-care model with services spanning multiple settings—from primary care facilities to specialized community mental health centers and teams, with acute inpatient care integrated into general hospitals rather than isolated psychiatric institutions [18, 21]. Key elements of this envisioned system include: low-intensity interventions delivered by lay providers; establishment of mental health competencies among primary care physicians through Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) training [22]; transition of patients from long-term institutional to community-based services; legal reforms to protect patient autonomy and reduce coercive practices; reallocation of resources from institutional to community care; and development of a multidisciplinary mental health workforce aligned with European standards [18–21]. This comprehensive redesign requires not merely reorganization of services, but fundamental restructuring of professional roles, financing mechanisms, regulatory frameworks, and cross-sectoral coordination structures. Implementing this transformation during ongoing war creates a particularly complex environment where traditional implementation approaches may be insufficient.

The convergence of multiple factors—coordination across conflict-disrupted providers and sectors [1, 2]; managing diverse stakeholders spanning medical, social services, emergency response, military support, and international humanitarian actors [18–20]; and maintaining system functioning despite damaged infrastructure, displaced populations, and resource constraints [2, 8, 19] – demands theoretical frameworks capable of examining multilevel interactions within unstable contexts. Understanding how system components connect and influence each other becomes critical for both mainatinging emergency response functions and advancing strategic transformation toward evidence-based mocel.

Implementation science offers theoretical models designed to examine and improve the translation of evidence-based practices into real-world settings by considering contextual factors influencing system transformation initiatives and service delivery adaptations [23]. Among these, the Exploration-Preparation-Implementation-Sustainment (EPIS) framework provides a valuable lens to understand the implementation of mental health interventions while acknowledging the influence of contextual factors [24]. The EPIS framework systematically addresses both outer context factors—including funding mechanisms, policy environments, and inter-organizational networks—and inner context factors—such as organizational culture, leadership capacity, and workforce characteristics—that shape implementation processes [24].

A particularly valuable concept in the updated EPIS framework is that of “bridging factors,” which are defined as elements that connect outer and inner contexts [25]. These bridging factors enable the bidirectional exchange of resources, knowledge, and influence between system-level entities and individual implementers, providing an analytical concept for examining interactions in complex systems [25, 26], such as those providing mental health services. Building on recent theoretical development [25, 26], bridging factors can be categorized into three types: formal arrangements (such as contracts, funding mechanisms, and institutional agreements for structure and accountability); processes (including data sharing and quality assurance); and relational ties (including partnerships, professional networks, and collaborations). These bridging factors create dynamic interdependencies between system actors that ultimately influence implementation outcomes [25, 27–29]. It is important to understand these complex dependencies for effective implementation of mental health services. When properly identified and leveraged, bridging factors may enable a more effective selection of implementation strategies accounting for both system constraints and organizational realities [28]. They facilitate coordination between diverse stakeholders [30, 31] with varying priorities and resources [26, 32] while supporting sustainable service delivery [33] that can transition from emergency response to the envisioned model of care.

This study scopes bridging factors within Ukraine’s evolving mental health services system following the onset of Russian invasion in 2022 on February 24. By analyzing the dynamic interactions between outer and inner contexts during this period of rapid system adaptation, we aim to contribute to both theoretical understanding of bridging factors and practical knowledge for improving service implementation in crisis contexts.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative descriptive study, part of a larger effort to understand the landscape of mental health service provision in Ukraine following the 2022 invasion, aimed to explore the implementation context for mental health services within a humanitarian setting using the EPIS framework. Data was collected using semi-structured video-conference interviews with mental health service system stakeholders.

We developed an EPIS-informed instrument. The interview guide contained sections on: study overview; external context and organizational roles (e.g. ‘What is your organization’s role in mental health service provision in Ukraine?’ ‘How do you collaborate with other mental health service providers and institutions?’); bridging factors connecting outer and inner contexts; mental health service delivery, system coordination mechanisms, and evidence-based practice integration (intervention preparation and implementation: ‘How have service delivery for mental health been adapted since February 24, 2022?’ ‘What specific interventions have been introduced for war-affected populations?’, ‘How do different stakeholders coordinate in the mental health response?’); and reflections on wartime mental health system functioning.

Participants

The study employed a purposeful maximum-variation sampling to capture diverse perspectives across Ukraine’s post-February 2022 mental health service landscape. We conducted stakeholder mapping using a two-phase approach that combined systematic review of publicly available information (e.g. publicly available organizational websites, reports, and coordination meeting lists on mental health service coordination) with snowball sampling to identify key organizations, groups, and individuals across different organizational levels. Our approach drew on established implementation science methods, particularly the I-STEM framework [34, 35], and was operationalized through a mapping matrix that systematically recorded each stakeholder’s institutional position, influence, and relevance to the study themes. This approach facilitated representation across various institutions and allowed investigation of coordination mechanisms, technical support, funding streams, and resource allocation patterns. The recruitment prioritized organizational leaders, program managers, and administrators with comprehensive institutional knowledge and decision-making authority, while also including some direct service providers for ground-level perspectives.

Data collection

The research assistant (AM-2) contacted identified institutions via e-mail and through personal connections with study invitations. Interviews were scheduled at participants’ convenience. Data collection occurred between March-October 2023 through semi-structured video-conference interviews (recorded with audio only), addressing geographical constraints and safety considerations. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred language (Ukrainian or Russian). A single interviewer (AM-1) conducted all interviews, which lasted 43–99 min (averaging ~ 60 min). 20 potential participants were contacted; 18 agreed to participate. Two declined due to scheduling conflicts or non-response. Sample size was determined by data saturation—data collection continued until no new themes emerged for each of the sections of the topic guide.

Data management and analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim in the original language (Ukrainian or Russian), and systematically de-identified. Each transcript was assigned a unique alphanumeric identifier (e.g., “01_KYIV_N_A”), preserving organizational and geographical characteristics while maintaining confidentiality. Data was managed using MAXQDA [36] software, with password protection and restricted access. All audio recordings were deleted after transcript verification.

Analysis combined deductive and inductive coding strategies. The initial codebook drew from the EPIS framework constructs, particularly context, bridging factors, preparation, and implementation phases with emergent codes added during analysis.

Rather than using pre-defined bridging factors, coding emphasized interactions between actors (government agencies, international organizations, community organizations, academic institutions, service providers) and cross-institutional flow of elements including resources (e.g., financial, educational, human), knowledge, client referrals, and regulatory influences. These coded segments describing interactions by type of organization and flow of elements formed the foundation of the analysis. Analysis progressed through several phases: (1)developing descriptive summaries for each interview, focusing on key elements of bridging factors; (2) creating analytical summaries across interviews to identify patterns and mechanisms, and divergent perspectives; (3) and categorizing bridging factors according to the Lengnick-Hall typology (formal arrangements, process bridging, and relational ties).

This study follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [37]. A completed SRQR checklist is provided as an Additional File to ensure transparent reporting of our qualitative methodology.The ICF Alliance for Public Health Institutional Review Board approved the study (Meeting #11 from December 16, 2022). All participants received study information and provided oral, audio-recorded informed consent. No participation incentives were offered.

Results

Participants

The study included 18 participants (Table 1), with two-thirds females (n = 12) and the majority having more than 10 years of professional experience (n = 15). Participants had diverse professional backgrounds, with psychiatrists (n = 6) and psychologists (n = 5) comprising the largest groups. Most were affiliated with civil society organizations (n = 11) operating at the national level (n = 11). Interviews had a median duration of 55 min (Inter-Quartile-Range (IQR): 51.5–65.5 min). Interviews captured perspectives from representatives of governmental, academic, civil society, and medical institutions across local, national, and international levels of operation, geographically spanning multiple Ukrainian cities.

Importantly, while the largest participant group primarily self-affiliated with civil society organizations (CSO), they held multiple institutional roles, revealing civil society’s function as critical bridging entities connecting diverse stakeholders across Ukraine’s mental health system.

CSO in the sample operated through two primary mechanisms: delivering direct interventions to children, veterans and families, general public and internally displaced persons via individual and group modalities, in-person and online; and enhancing systemic capacity through knowledge transfer, training, and coordination activities across national, western oblasts of Ukraine, and frontline territories.

Summary of findings

Participants described bridging factors related to three interconnected implementation domains: (1) emergency mental health response coordination, (2) ongoing system transformation from institution-based to community-based care, and (3) integration of specific evidence-based practices (including mhGAP) (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of bridging factors identified in the study in relation to the outer and inner contexts of the mental health system actors in Ukraine after the Russian invasion in 2022

The findings reveal a complex ecosystem of bridging mechanisms operating across multiple system levels. Formal arrangements, process-oriented bridging factors, and relational ties were key for the system functioning in the context of urgency and strategic reconfiguration. The data demonstrated that these bridging factors did not operate in isolation, but interacted dynamically, with relational ties frequently preceding and facilitating the development of more formalized arrangements, particularly as emergency responses evolved towards structured coordination mechanisms.

Formal arrangement bridging factors

Formal arrangement bridging factors connected the strategic vision (outer context) — encompassing strategic documents such as mental health legislation, the Concept of Development of Mental Health in Ukraine until 2030, target model for MHPSS (Mental Health and Psychosocial Support), and implementation roadmap — with the work of individual actors (inner context). These formal arrangements manifested through the creation of a central coordination body, regulatory frameworks, funding mechanisms, cooperation agreements, and standardization efforts and created institutionalized pathways for the flow of resources between the outer and inner contexts of implementation agents. These formal bridging factors also provided structure and legitimacy to implementation efforts.

Coordination structures

Participants outlined the development and establishment of multi-level coordination efforts that functioned as bridging mechanisms between policy formulation and service delivery. A central component that emerged post-invasion was the Coordination Center for Mental Health (CCMH) established by the Ukrainian government on March 30, 2023, through Cabinet of Ministers Resolution №301 [38] to facilitate vertical and horizontal coordination among various stakeholders, including ministries, local governments, NGOs, international partners, and experts in implementing mental health initiatives in Ukraine (see quote CS1, Table 2).

Participants articulated how CCMH also structured mental health efforts through nested coordination activities at national, oblast, and local levels via technical working groups that connected diverse areas of expertise. For example, inter-sectoral coordination was operationalized through the development of standards for health, education and social services, the use of multidisciplinary teams, and the development of inclusive spaces and prioritizing mental health projects within each ministry (see quote CS2, Table 2).

Participants, however, were unclear on the overall architecture of the mental health system, identifying a gap regarding the system’s conceptual framework and operational boundaries (see quote CS3, Table 2).

The MHPSS technical working group (MHPSS TWG) platform was established in 2014 as part of the health cluster coordination mechanism, co-chaired by the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Medical Corps (IMC). Post-February 2022, this platform underwent substantial expansion and differentiation: participation increased, the platform was linked with national policymaking, and the platform was further segmented into multiple geographical and thematic working groups to localize and address diverse implementation needs within the mental health system in Ukraine. Regular meetings through these multi-stakeholder engagement platforms were designed to create spaces for dialogue and collaboration among diverse service providers and system stakeholders (see quote CS4, Table 2).

Ukraine’s decentralisation process, formalized in 2015 [39], established amalgamated territorial communities (hromadas) as official local governance institutions with delegated powers and resources. When functioning effectively, including the leadership capacity, these local structures served as institutional bridges connecting national mental health efforts with community-level implementation, enabling service adaptation to local contexts. Furthermore, participants believed that a formalized referral system could be most effectively developed at the hromada level. Many participants considered coordination mechanisms at this local level to be the most efficient approach to service delivery(see quote CS5, Table 2).

Regulatory and guidance frameworks

Policy and regulatory frameworks described by participants varied from foundational governance documents—including mental health legislation and the conceptual framework for Ukraine’s mental health system development—to standardization efforts as clinical protocols and licensing designed to facilitate implementation, formalized inter-organizational cooperation agreements, and systematized data collection frameworks that enable monitoring and evaluation processes.

During data collection, participants described the law on psychiatric care constituting the primary legislative framework governing mental health services within medical settings. Multiple participants characterized this legislation as antiquated and excessively constraining, with several respondents noting its incorporation of stigmatizing elements inherited from the Soviet era. Participants specifically highlighted the exclusive authority granted to psychiatrists for mental health diagnosis and treatment, alongside provisions that limited data transparency through prohibition to share data on mental health treatment in any form (see quote RGF1, Table 2).

Concurrently, respondents expressed optimism regarding emerging legislative developments, particularly referring to the draft law on psychotherapeutic care in the health sector. This proposed legislation was perceived to establish a more comprehensive regulatory framework that would either supersede or augment the existing psychiatric care law. Additionally, several participants advocated for the development of an integrated mental health legislative approach that would explicitly outline responsibilities across multiple sectors, including healthcare, education and social services thus facilitating cross-sectoral coordination in the provision of mental health services form (see quotes RGF2-3, Table 2).

Concurrently, participants characterized ongoing efforts to harmonize Ukrainian mental health legislation with the WHO’s action plan (2013–2030) [40] and other relevant international frameworks. While acknowledging the guidance provided by international partners in this alignment process, respondents pointed to a disconnect between international recommendations and the structures of the Ukrainian bureaucratic system and existing legislative frameworks.

The clinical protocols, developed and approved through Ministry of Health orders, aimed to bridge summarized evidence-based practices and service delivery, but implementation varied as these standards were subject to adoption and implementation by facilities and specialists. Participants noted that although healthcare institutions theoretically possess the autonomy to develop institution-specific protocols, this generates inconsistencies in care standards. Moreover, respondents indicated that few institutions demonstrated willingness to allocate the resources necessary for the development of local protocols (see quote RGF4, Table 2).

In addition, participants identified regulatory deficiencies regarding professional oversight mechanisms and the lack of a comprehensive licensing system for mental health practitioners, specifically psychologists and psychotherapists. Professional licensing discussions revealed deliberations regarding the optimal approach, specifically, whether psychological practice should be governed through centralized state mechanisms or through decentralized professional associations operating with delegated authority.

These regulatory gaps were related to quality assurance challenges, as respondents emphasized the paucity of formal mechanisms to authenticate practitioner qualifications, remove underqualified professionals, or systematically evaluate service delivery standards (see quote RGF5, Table 2).

The cooperation within the field was formalized through various memoranda of understanding and other types of strategic partnership documents between key stakeholders either for the specific projects or for more long-term cooperation.

For example, the WHO, the Ministry of Health, and various donors have established formal agreements to implement programs like the mhGAP. Similarly, United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) worked directly with the Ukrainian government to coordinate child-focused mental health initiatives. In some regions, like Lviv oblast, military administrations formalized relationships with international partners through established memoranda in August 2022, creating official coordination centers in each district (see quotes RGF6-7, Table 2).

The identified regulatory frameworks for institutional data collection functioned as formalized bridging factors that connected macro-level health system governance with micro-level service delivery through standardized reporting protocols. These encompassed several distinct examples: (1) national medical statistical reporting instruments designed to aggregate diagnosed mental health conditions; (2) performance-oriented monitoring systems implemented through the National Health Service of Ukraine (NHSU) that quantified service provision within contractually defined medical packages; and (3) heterogeneous accountability structures imposed by individual donor funding entities, each with distinct requirements. While these formal arrangements served as reporting and resource allocation bridges, it was not clear, based on participant understanding, how they were used for prospective mental health system design(see quotes RGF8, Table 2).

Funding frameworks

The resource bridges combined diverse funding streams from international and local sources and significantly varied depending on the type of organization. The health system financing mechanism included funding from the Program of State Medical Guarantees, which supported both multidisciplinary mental health teams and the implementation of mhGAP intervention by primary healthcare providers. This funding was administered through NHSU, which functions as a single-payer through established contract-based relationships with medical facilities using package-based funding systems (see quote FF1, Table 2).

The governmental financial pathways were supplemented by arrangements with international donors and academic partners, which created channels for conducting research and implementing evidence-based interventions throughout the country through civil society organizations.

In the case of donor funding, the arrangements were more sophisticated to account for the various limitations of academic state institutions and the opportunities of less regulated non-governmental organizations. While academic institutions in Ukraine primarily contributed expertise and research capabilities to focus on scientific research, clinical applications, and knowledge generation through various projects, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) served dual roles as financial intermediaries and direct service delivery agents. They functioned as essential bridges, enabling financial transactions that academic and other state institutions could not effectively implement. The complexity of international funding mechanisms has prompted the development of specialized NGOs explicitly designed to facilitate collaborations, addressing the fundamental limitation of state institutions to receive and efficiently use international funding for scientific and service-delivery projects (see quote FF2, Table 2).

These organisations provided unique operational advantages. NGOs were key recipients of funds through diverse grant mechanisms to offer service delivery outside the healthcare framework. Their structure allowed flexibility in meeting donor requirements and rapidly adapting to changing funding landscapes. However, such arrangements faced distinct challenges, including complex financial routing requirements and organizational structures requiring extensive coordination and bureaucratic processes. While this system enabled access to diverse funding streams (e.g., United Nations (UN), United States Agency for International Development (USAID), and private donors), it also created administrative inefficiencies and bottlenecks to funding with immediate field needs and sustainable services.

Processes

Our study identified process-oriented bridging mechanisms connecting implementation contexts through information exchange and synthesis, referral networks, and knowledge transfer mechanisms. These processes functioned as pathways facilitating implementation across organizations while addressing coordination challenges in complex service environments.

Information exchange and synthesis

Digital information exchange solutions identified through interviews represented significant process-oriented bridging mechanisms to overcome institutional boundaries, facilitating information flows between disparate elements of the system. They included dashboards and mapping systems, regular online meetings, and group chats. These technological infrastructures functioned as platforms, information repositories and as dynamic system navigation resources that provided representations of policy and service landscapes (e.g. CCMH and MHPSS TWG web pages) and cross-sectoral communication channels (MHPSS TWG group chat). The data suggested that these platforms were creating pathways for information transmission between organisational contexts. However, the data also revealed substantial variation in implementation and efficacy. While the technical infrastructure for information exchange has been established, its systematic utilisation remained contingent on voluntary individual engagement (see quote IES1, Table 2).

Analysis of interview data indicated that quality assurance also manifested primarily through information exchange within monitoring frameworks and institutional oversight arrangements. However, these bridging mechanisms were characterized by an over-emphasis on service volume metrics rather than quality outcomes. The focus on quantitative metrics was criticized for being less sensitive to the effectiveness or person-centered approaches (see quotes IES2-3, Table 2).

Information consolidation and synthesis initiatives functioned as epistemological bridges, integrating disparate information sources and data collection efforts to build a coherent understanding of the landscape of Ukraine’s mental health system. These processes were predominantly facilitated through formal academic collaborations or by international organisations with sufficient resource capacity to undertake comprehensive analytical work. The arrangements revealed a dependency on external institutional support rather than a sustainable infrastructure. Moreover, the described ad hoc nature of many knowledge integration efforts suggested the absence of embedded processes for evaluating and incorporating diverse data sources, particularly those generated through humanitarian assessments with varying methodological rigor and contextual validity (see quote IES4-5, Table 2).

Referrals

The referral processes described by the participants functioned as bridging factors connecting various actors in Ukraine’s mental health system, although with significant variability in implementation and effectiveness. The primary function of referrals was to facilitate the appropriate provision of services for clients and patients. Secondary functions included cross-organizational requests for professional support (training, education, supervision).

The interviews consistently revealed that inter-organizational referral pathways represent vulnerable points where service continuity experienced substantial disruption. Particularly vulnerable transition points included: referrals from primary care to specialized psychiatric services; transfers between civil society organizations and formal healthcare institutions; and transitions from short-term crisis interventions to sustained therapeutic services (see quote REF1, Table 2).

The interview data also demonstrated that referral processes largely lacked formal structures and relied heavily on preexisting professional relationships and trust networks, making an informal approach the predominant operational model (see quote REF2, Table 2).

Knowledge transfer

Knowledge transfer functioned as a multilayered bridging process connecting system-level policies with field-level implementation realities. These processes operated with dual functionality: as deliberate implementation strategies for evidence-based practices (e.g., the training was the main activity) and as enabling features that facilitated broader system transformation.

Formally required knowledge transfer operated through top-down channels when regulatory frameworks mandated specific training approaches (e.g., mhGAP certification requirements tied to contracting). Simultaneously, bottom-up knowledge transfer emerged as frontline providers articulated specific learning needs based on emerging clinical demands (e.g., crisis response competencies, veteran-focused interventions, and trauma-centered therapeutic approaches). Horizontal knowledge exchange networks facilitated cross-organizational and transnational transfer of theoretical frameworks and practical applications between professional communities (see quote KT1, Table 2).

Supervisory relationships and peer learning networks served as additional bridging mechanisms to facilitate knowledge transfer. Implementation projects with integrated supervisory components, especially those providing mandatory, subsidized clinical oversight at regular intervals, were highly regarded by practising participants (see quote KT1, Table 2). Transnational academic collaborations further enhanced knowledge transfer by providing structured educational resources, targeted technical assistance, and implementation support.

Knowledge transfer mechanisms emerged as a particularly prominent bridging factor in the context of workforce shortages and escalating service demands. Ultimately, knowledge transfer mechanisms were described to have optimal effectiveness as bridging factors when they addressed three critical dimensions simultaneously: (1) knowledge deficits among providers, (2) aspirational system transformation objectives, and (3) pragmatic implementation constraints, particularly regarding limited workforce capacity and organizational readiness (see quote KT3, Table 2).

Relational ties

In our data analysis, we identified three predominant categories of relational ties bridging factors: collaborative arrangements, relational ties facilitating recruitment, and individual boundary-spanning that connected various implementation contexts.

Collaborations

Participants described numerous collaborations in Ukraine’s mental health system serving various functions. While formal arrangements eventually emerged in some cases, the initial knowledge transfer, resource mobilization, and implementation support were primarily driven by interpersonal connections and shared professional interests. These relational ties developed through multiple pathways—including proactive outreach, crisis response, and historical connections—often transforming from simple transactional exchanges into trust-based collaborative projects, particularly as organizations adapted to the disruptions caused by the Russian invasion in 2022 (see quote COL1, Table 2).

Further, relational ties existed on a multidimensional continuum ranging from hierarchical structures to horizontal peer-to-peer collaborations. These often manifested as hybrid configurations where formal authority coexisted with informal collaborative networks. This complexity was particularly evident in partnerships between national governmental institutions, international NGOs, and local implementing organizations (see quote COL2, Table 2).

Relational ties furher served as crucial channels for knowledge exchange across institutional boundaries, enabling multiple forms of knowledge mobilization. For instance, collaborations with international organizations and academic institutions helped translate global evidence into local implementation practices. Meanwhile, horizontal relationships between practitioners with diverse experiences facilitated the sharing of implementation knowledge. These connections provided access to specialized expertise, addressing critical capacity gaps where local professional resources were insufficient (see quotes COL3-4, Table 2). Collaborations based on relational ties also constituted essential mechanisms for tangible resource mobilization. These relationships served as pathways for acquiring and distributing the financial and material resources necessary for service implementation and delivery (see quote COL5, Table 2).

Relational ties further served as legitimation mechanisms that conferred external validation on local implementation initiatives, enhancing their acceptance and credibility within professional communities. Such relationships provided internal validation when influential Ukrainian institutions and individuals endorsed international approaches, facilitating acceptance within local contexts. Collaborations with universities and research centers provided mental health initiatives with a scientific backing, and when supported by academic experts, these initiatives were perceived as evidence-based and reliable, fostering organizational adoption (see quote COL6, Table 2).

Recruitment

As the ongoing war exacerbated qualified staff shortages, recruitment processes, a specific type of relational tie, bridged different elements of the system. Participants described these recruitment processes as urgent, strategic headhunting, and dynamic interactions with varying levels of vetting and formalization.These recruitment-based relational ties served as effective bridging factors by transferring institutional knowledge and social capital across organizational boundaries. For example, professionals with previous government experience facilitated connections between international funders and local NGO implementers, creating pathways for resource flow and knowledge exchange (see quote REC1, Table 2).

Further recruitment processes functioned as a bridging mechanism for the flow of technical expertise. Recruitment is primarily initiated as part of new projects or scaling up existing projects due to increased demand, seeking specialists with implementation expertise. Participants described how organizations typically initiated recruitment through established professional networks and connections, strategically identifying specialists with the specific expertise needed for implementation efforts. The verification process for professional credentials varied considerably between institutions, as did the methods used to assess competencies. These relational recruitment pathways created opportunities to rapidly form implementation teams and challenges to ensure consistent qualification standards across different organizations (see quote REC2, Table 2).

Individual boundary spanners

The interview data revealed a distinct pattern where specific individuals functioned as boundary spanners – key nodes bridging different organizations and professional communities. Positioned at pivotal organizational intersections, professionals functioned as critical system elements facilitating resource flow, knowledge transfer, and collaboration. One mechanism described in the data is through simultaneous employment across multiple organizations, exemplified by one professional’s statement: “I’m counted in three [organizations].”

The interviews also demonstrated how individuals leveraged multiple institutional affiliations to establish connections between disparate stakeholder groups. Several professionals utilized positions in psychiatric institutions, professional associations, academic departments, civil society organizations and government working groups to facilitate interdisciplinary collaboration and system-wide coordination. These individuals functioned as knowledge brokers between sectors, mediating relationships between mental health services, emergency response organizations, educational institutions, military entities, and humanitarian aid providers. Their established professional reputations and personal connections appeared particularly significant in Ukraine’s mental health system, which is undergoing substantial transformation during wartime when formal institutional structures may be disrupted or still developing (see quote IBS1-2, Table 2).

Discussion

This study was inspired by developments in implementation science, specifically the EPIS framework focusing on bridging factors as elements connecting the outer system environment with the inner context of actors [25, 26]. A key finding is the dual functionality of bridging factors in Ukraine’s mental health system during wartime. Bridging factors simultaneously maintained operational functionality within a conflict-fragmented system while facilitating transformation toward an evidence-based, community-oriented model: concurrent emergency adaptation and reform. This dual role distinguishes crisis-context bridging factors from stable environments, where transformation envisioned as planned, sequential processes.

Bridging factors operated with varying formalization, effectiveness, and sustainability in facilitating resource flow, knowledge transfer, and service implementation. Formal coordination structures connected through informal relational networks, with boundary-spanning individuals leveraging multiple affiliations to connect system actors. Some bridging factors were deliberately planned (coordination mechanisms), while others were disrupted, such as referrals between service levels and sectors.

By applying the bridging factors construct to this conflict-affected setting, we extend its utility beyond stable implementation environments and illustrate how bridging factors function during an acute crisis accompanied by system disruption and transformation, an influx of multiple international and domestic actors, damaged infrastructure and displaced populations-conditions that fundamentally alter bridging factors development and operations.

Emergency settings shape bridging factors through time pressures, system instability, and specific communication needs [29, 33], influencing the function and form dimensions identified by Lengnick-Hall et al. [25]. Our exploration of stakeholders’ perceptions revealed how war urgency necessitated flexible communication channels and adaptive responses to maintain functionality despite service interruptions and workforce disruptions. This flexibility was particularly evident in the emergence of new coordination bodies like the CCMH and the evolution of MHPSS technical working groups to meet rapidly changing needs [9, 10, 41]. Unlike non-emergency settings where bridging factors can develop through systematic planning processes, formalized communication, and stable, long-term relationships between the outer and inner organizational contexts [26, 30, 31, 42–44] our results show how coordination mechanisms transformed within the crisis, relational ties evolved into formal arrangements, and existing structures influenced new bridging mechanisms, aligning with Lengnick-Hall et al.’s [25] dynamism dimension.

A critical finding how relational ties preceded and facilitated formal arrangements, with informal connections serving as foundational elements that were selectively formalized while preserving adaptive flexibility essential for crisis. This pattern paralles findings from other contexts: in the wildfire response networks in the American Northwest [22], bridging actors connected disconnected parts, enabling information and resource flow, though gaps existed between expected and actual bridging actors due to jurisdictional and operational circumstances. Similarly, boundary spanners at system intersections were key for resource flow, knowledge transfer, and collaboration in Ukraine’s fragmented mental health landscape.

The recent Lancet Psychiatry Commission on mental health in Ukraine [18–21] and Ukraine’s newly adopted Mental Health System Law [45] articulate an ambitious vision for transformation: developing community-based mental health services, reforming workforce training, rebuilding research capacity, restructuring financing and updating legal frameworks [18–20], with legal, organizational, economic, and social foundations that introduce the National Commission on Mental Health Issues, certification requirements, and a community-based approach. The reform framework emphasizes human-centered, evidence-based principles to ensure accessible, quality services, protect rights, and prevent mental health issues through psychosocial support, psychological assistance, psychotherapy, psychological rehabilitation, and psychiatric care [45].

Our identification of specific bridging mechanisms operating within this transformation reveals the concrete linkages that enable system change despite crisis conditions, extending previous documentation of system challenges [1, 11, 12, 14, 46] by showing how fragmented components actually connect in practice.

The comprehensive and ambitious vision for mental health system in Ukraine will require a multifaceted implementation approach, for which our study offers several empirically grounded considerations. We focus on the role of relational ties, balance between standardization and flexibility, resource management, knowledge transfer and quality assurance. Based on our findings, support for developing relational ties into sustainable institutional arrangements is needed by identifying effective networks that bridge service gaps or connect providers with coordination bodies. Implementing institutional agreements, flexible governance models, and supporting boundary spanners may strengthen bridging factors’ sustainability. Our findings highlight Ukraine’s decentralization process and local governance structures as strategically important. With adequate leadership, these structures connected national mental health initiatives with community implementation, enabling contextual service adaptation while facilitating coordination. However, referral pathways between organizations were vulnerable to disruption, with weaknesses occurring between primary care and specialized psychiatric services, civil society organizations and formal healthcare institutions, and transitions from crisis interventions to sustained support. Early identification of these vulnerability points, combined with targeted strengthening strategies such as support for local leadership development, dedicated liaison roles, and formalized handoff protocols, could prevent service disruptions.

Collaborations bridging resources need sustainability consideration to benefit from invested resources and to create sustainable systems. The operational differences between governmental institutions and NGOs require funding capacity redesign, addressing structural constraints limiting state institutions’ access to international funding while strengthening accountability of resource facilitators. Our findings suggest transitioning from volume-based toward quality outcome models that incentivize effective referrals and person-centered approaches. This connects with quality assurance development by supporting supervision relationships, learning networks, and professional oversight mechanisms. Knowledge transfer mechanisms most effectively bridged workforce shortages when addressing provider knowledge gaps, system transformation objectives, and implementation constraints. The sequence from informal relationships to partial formalization suggests that complete formalization may be counterproductive in crisis contexts by eliminating adaptive flexibility. Strategic partial formalization through agreements, coordination mechanisms, and flexible funding can provide structure while maintaining responsiveness. When reform resources are limited, strategic investment in community-of-practice models, supervision networks, and consultation systems can strengthen system integration while preserving adaptive capacity essential for crisis response.

This study of bridging factors contributes to understanding dynamic links between outer and inner contexts of mental health system actors in emergency settings, though several limitations and ideas for future research warrant acknowledgement. First, our sampling approach, while diverse, was limited to researchers’ experiences and networks, potentially missing unique bridging factor configurations. Despite follow-up efforts, some key stakeholders couldn’t be reached. This selection bias may have skewed understanding toward more accessible stakeholders’ perspectives while missing critical viewpoints. Second, data collection during the first year of Russian invasion in 2022 invasion likely influenced stakeholder availability, priorities, communication patterns, and potentially the bridging factors themselves. Further, video-conference data collection may have excluded participants without reliable internet access, though no contacted participants cited this reason for declining. Findings should be interpreted within this unique context, recognizing dynamics may differ at later stages of the war. The lack of longitudinal data tracking bridging factor evolution represents an opportunity for future research through prospective designs capturing dynamics across implementation phases. Third, our understanding relies on qualitative stakeholder insights. While these perspectives offer rich contextual information about bridging factor function and form, they would be enhanced through mixed-methods research designed to measure implementation outcomes linked to bridging factors (e.g. penetration, sustainability, etc.), though such instruments remain undeveloped. Further, the implementation research field should invest in comparative studies examining how political systems, funding mechanisms, and cultural norms influence bridging factor development and effectiveness to advance implementation science theory and practice.

Conclusion

Bridging factors in Ukraine’s mental health system during the first year of the Russian invasion in 2022 served as essential mechanisms with dual functionality: maintaining operational connections between fragmented system components while simultaneously enabling strategic transformation toward an evidence-based, community-oriented model of care.
