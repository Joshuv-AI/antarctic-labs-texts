Bayesian Empirical Bayes: Simultaneous Inference from Probabilistic Symmetries
Bohan Wu, Eli N. Weinstein, David M. Blei

Abstract
Empirical Bayes (EB) improves the accuracy of simultaneous inference “by learning from the experience of others” ( 34 ) . Classical EB theory focuses on latent variables that are iid draws from a fitted prior ( 29 ) . Modern applications, however, feature complex structure, like arrays, spatial processes, or covariates. How can we apply EB ideas to these settings? We propose a generalized approach to empirical Bayes based on the notion of probabilistic symmetry . Our method pairs a simultaneous inference problem–with an unknown prior–to a symmetry assumption on the joint distribution of the latent variables. Each symmetry implies an ergodic decomposition, which we use to derive a corresponding empirical Bayes method. We call this method Bayesian empirical Bayes (BEB). We show how BEB recovers the classical methods of empirical Bayes, which implicitly assume exchangeability. We then use it to extend EB to other probabilistic symmetries: (i) EB matrix recovery for arrays and graphs; (ii) covariate–assisted EB for conditional data; (iii) EB spatial regression under shift invariance. We develop scalable algorithms based on variational inference and neural networks. In simulations, BEB outperforms existing approaches to denoising arrays and spatial data. On real data, we demonstrate BEB by denoising a cancer gene–expression matrix and analyzing spatial air-quality data from New York City.

Introduction

Empirical Bayes (EB) is about
simultaneous posterior inference
. We observe related data
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, where each data point is associated with a latent variable
z_{i}
, and assumed drawn from
x_{i}\sim\mathrm{p}(x\,|\,z_{i})
. The form of the conditional likelihood is known. The goal of EB is to use Bayesian inference to estimate all the
z_{i}
together
(
101
;
37
;
29
)
.

In a traditional Bayesian analysis, we specify a prior
z_{i}\sim\textrm{g}(z)
and compute the posteriors
\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\,|\,x_{i})
. But in EB, we treat the prior
\textrm{g}(z)
as
unknown
and we estimate it from the data. EB thus blends Bayesian and frequentist thinking: we reason in a frequentist way about the prior in order to form a collection of Bayesian estimates.

The classical approach to EB assumes
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{g}
and estimates
\hat{\textrm{g}}
so that the marginal distribution of
x
maximizes the likelihood of the data. In the EB nomenclature, this is known as “
g
-modeling”
(
35
)
. Once the prior is fit, we calculate the posteriors
\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\,|\,x_{i},\hat{\textrm{g}})
to estimate the latent variables. Through the fitted prior, EB connects all
n
inferences: information from each observation improves all the others.

The assumption
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}g
is central to EB thinking. It says that the latent variables share a common prior, which justifies the EB procedure. But many modern datasets violate this iid assumption. Latent variables may come in rows and columns, they may lie on a spatial or temporal grid, or they may depend on known covariates. While these settings clearly violate the iid assumption, they still exhibit symmetries that tie the latent variables together. In this paper, we develop a general way to use such probabilistic symmetries to extend EB to non-iid settings.

Stepping back, the assumption that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are iid from
g
is a special case of a broader principle:
exchangeability
. If the joint distribution of the latent variables is invariant to permutations, then—under mild conditions—it can be viewed as part of an infinitely exchangeable sequence. The iid structure then arises through de Finetti’s famous representation theorem
(
41
)
. It states that any exchangeable sequence can be written as a mixture of iid laws,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\int\Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\mid\textrm{g})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}),

(1)

where
g
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mu
is a
distribution over such distributions. De Finetti’s result is
important because it connects a symmetry assumption—namely, exchangeability—to classical Bayesian reasoning
(
105
;
102
)
. If the
z_{i}
’s are exchangeable, then the prior
g
appears as the latent variable that renders the
z_{i}
conditionally independent.

Our insight is that empirical Bayes uses the same hierarchical structure as in
Equation

1
, where
z_{i}
are conditionally iid given
g
. However, in place of integration with respect to
\mu
, EB substitutes a fitted value
\hat{\textrm{g}}
obtained from data. Seen through de Finetti’s theorem, we can view EB as a Bayesian inference under exchangeability, but with the mixing distribution collapsed to a point estimate.

Using this perspective, we extend classical EB by relaxing its exchangeability assumption to other probabilistic symmetries. Latent variables often have structure, such as being organized in an array or indexed by space or time. Such settings are not fully exchangeable, but they may satisfy weaker invariances. An array may be invariant to permutations of its rows and columns; a spatial process may be invariant to shifts in location
(
66
)
. Each symmetry has an analogue of de Finetti’s representation theorem. We will use these representation theorems to extend EB to non-iid settings.

We call our approach
Bayesian empirical Bayes
(BEB). We begin with a dataset
\mathbf{x}
and an assumed probabilistic symmetry. We posit that each observation arises from a known likelihood conditioned on a local latent variable
\mathrm{p}(x_{i}\,|\,z_{i})
, and our goal is to infer all
z_{i}
simultaneously. The key idea is that our assumed symmetry implies an
ergodic decomposition
of the prior, i.e., a generalization of de Finetti’s theorem
(
90
)
. Specifically, if the latent variables are invariant under a group
\Phi
acting on its index set, then their joint distribution admits the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})=\int\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}),

(2)

where
g
indexes the
\Phi
-ergodic family. The chosen symmetry determines the form of
g
and the conditional distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
.

Following the EB recipe, we fit
g
by maximum marginal likelihood under the expanded model
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z},\bm{x})
,

\displaystyle\hat{\textrm{g}}\in\argmax_{\textrm{g}}\ \log\int\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{z})\,\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}.

(3)

Finally, we use
\hat{g}
to compute the empirical Bayes posteriors
\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\,|\,x_{i},\hat{\textrm{g}})
. In practice, we can use approximate posterior inference both to fit
\hat{\textrm{g}}
and estimate
\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\,|\,x_{i},\hat{\textrm{g}})
.

We illustrate BEB in several data analysis settings. For array data, the relevant symmetry is separate or joint exchangeability, and the ergodic decomposition is given by the Aldous-Hoover theorem
(
2
)
. The invariant variable
g
is a measurable function on
[0,1]^{3}
, which we parameterize with a neural network. For spatial data, the relevant symmetry is shift invariance, and we can flexibly model
g
with stationary ergodic Gaussian processes. In both settings, we can additionally condition on covariates and derive covariate-dependent EB procedures.

Thus, Bayesian empirical Bayes produces a generalization of EB to any probabilistic symmetry. The invariant variable
g
replaces the classical prior, and the ergodic decomposition supplies the structure needed for inference.

2
Bayesian Empirical Bayes

Consider a
structured dataset

\bm{x}=(x_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where each
x_{\omega}
is indexed by an element in an index set
\Omega
. For example, if
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
, then
\bm{x}
is an infinite sequence; if
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{2}
, then
\bm{x}
is an infinite array. (After describing a model of infinite data, we will reduce it to one of finite data.)

We posit that the data
\bm{x}
are noisy measurements of a matching set of
structured latent variables

\bm{z}=(z_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
. In the likelihood, each
x_{\omega}
is conditionally independent given its local latent
z_{\omega}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{z})=\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega}).

(4)

We assume the form of the noise distribution
\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})
is known.

We place a prior distribution on the latent variables
\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
. The joint distribution of
\bm{z}
and
\bm{x}
is now

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z},\bm{x})=\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega}).

(5)

Note, we do not assume independence in the prior.

Our goal is
simultaneous probabilistic inference
of all local latent variables across
\Omega
. That is, we want to calculate the posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\bm{x})
. From this posterior, we can calculate summary statistics, such as the posterior means
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\,|\,\bm{x}\right]
, or other quantities of interest, such as credible intervals, quantiles, posterior predictive distributions, or event probabilities. In short, we can denoise the data.

We now make the
empirical Bayes assumption
: there exists a prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
such that the true data-generating distribution of
\bm{x}
is expressed by marginalizing over
\bm{z}
.

Assumption 1

(Empirical Bayes)
.

There exists a prior
\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
such that the true data-generating distribution can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{\Omega}}\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}.

Of course, we do not know this true prior ahead of time - we just assume it exists.
We call this the empirical Bayes assumption because it links the Bayesian model to the true data-generating process.

2.1
Priors with Probabilistic Symmetries

How do we set the prior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
? We will use the idea of a
probabilistic symmetry
. A symmetry describes how we can relabel or transform the indices of structured random variables without changing their joint distribution.

Formally, let
\Phi
denote a group of transformations acting bijectively on the index set
\Omega
, where each element
\phi\in\Phi
reindexes the coordinates of
\Omega
. The action of
\phi
on
\bm{z}
is the
index transformation

\displaystyle(\phi(\bm{z}))_{\omega}=z_{\phi^{-1}(\omega)},\quad\omega\in\Omega.

(6)

As examples, if
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
, then
\phi(\bm{z})
reorders the elements of a sequence. If
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{2}
, then
\phi(\bm{z})
could permute rows and columns of a matrix. If
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
\phi(\bm{z})
could implement a spatial shift.

A distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
is
\Phi
-invariant
if it does not change under these transformations. For example, an exchangeable sequence is invariant under permutations; a stationary process is invariant under spatial shifts.

Definition 1

(
\Phi
-invariance)
.

A joint distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
of structured variables
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}
is
\Phi
-invariant
if

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{z})\overset{d}{=}\bm{z}\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\Phi,

(7)

where equality is in distribution for all finite-dimensional marginals.

A formal consequence of
\Phi
-invariance is the
ergodic decomposition theorem
, which states that any
\Phi
-invariant distribution can be expressed as a mixture of
ergodic distributions
.

Theorem 1

(Ergodic decomposition;
114
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a nice group acting on
\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}
.
There exists a family of distributions

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g}):\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega})

(8)

such that any
\Phi
-invariant distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
admits the unique decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}),

(9)

for some distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{G}
. The set
\mathcal{E}
is called the
\Phi
-ergodic family
and its index
g
is called the invariant variable.
1
1

1

It is also known as the directing random measure
(
66
)
.

A “nice” group is a technical condition; see
Appendix

A
for a formal definition. The
\Phi
-ergodic family is determined by the group
\Phi
and forms the set of extreme points of the convex set of
\Phi
-invariant distributions on
\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}

(
65
, Theorem 25.24)
. Once
\Phi
is fixed, so too is the form of
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
.

The ergodic decomposition theorem generalizes de Finetti’s representation
(
41
)
from exchangeable sequences to arbitrary group symmetries. Under exchangeability, the ergodic measures are precisely the i.i.d. product distributions.

The ergodic decomposition describes the structure of any
\Phi
-invariant distribution. We now apply this idea to the true prior.

Assumption 2

(True
\Phi
-invariant prior)
.

The true prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
is
\Phi
-invariant.

By
Theorem

1
, there exists an invariant variable
\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}
with distribution
\textrm{g}\sim\mu^{\star}
such that
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
is a
\mu^{\star}
-mixture of the
\Phi
-ergodic family,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g}).

(10)

Together,
1
and
2
state that among all models of the form in
Equation

5
, there exists a correctly specified version whose prior is
\Phi
-invariant. In the Bayesian empirical Bayes model, the latent variables
\bm{z}
capture the structured, invariant part of the world, and the observations
\bm{x}
are noisy realizations through the shared likelihood
\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})
.

An important consequence of Assumption
2
is that the data-generating distribution
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x})
inherits the same symmetry as the prior. To see this, we first note that applying any transformation
\phi\in\Phi
to both
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
leaves the joint distribution unchanged,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\phi(\bm{x}),\phi(\bm{z}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\phi^{-1}(\omega)}\,|\,z_{\phi^{-1}(\omega)})

(11)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})=\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x},\bm{z}).

(12)

Then we marginalize out
\bm{z}
to give
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\phi(\bm{x}))=\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x})
.

Alternatively, instead of assuming the true prior is invariant, we could assume that the marginal distribution of the data is invariant. Then, under mild regularity assumptions on the likelihood, the
\Phi
-invariance of the population distribution
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x})
also implies the
\Phi
-invariance of
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
.

2.2
Examples of
\Phi
-invariant priors

We present three examples of
\Phi
-invariant priors, which we will use throughout this paper. In each, we specify the index set
\Omega
, the symmetry group
\Phi
, the form of the ergodic decomposition, and the corresponding invariant variable
g
.

Exchangeable sequences.
Let
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
, so that
\bm{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
is a sequence of random variables. The symmetry group is
\Phi=\mathbb{S}
, the group of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
. The
\Phi
-invariant distributions on
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
are the
exchangeable sequences
.

In this case, the ergodic decomposition recovers de Finetti’s representation theorem
(
41
)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\mathbf{z})=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{{\rm perm}}}\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}(z_{\omega})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}).

(13)

The invariant variable
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{perm}}
is a random probability measure on
\mathcal{Z}
, and
\mu
is a distribution over such random measures.

Exchangeable arrays.
Let
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{2}
, so that
\bm{z}=(z_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
is an array of random variables. The array is
separately exchangeable
if its distribution is invariant under the independent permutations of rows and columns. That is, the symmetry group is
\Phi=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle(z_{ij})\overset{d}{=}(z_{\pi(i),\pi^{\prime}(j)})\quad\text{for all }(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\Phi.

(14)

Under separate exchangeability, the Aldous–Hoover theorem provides the ergodic decomposition
(
1
;
2
)
.

Theorem 2

(Aldous–Hoover representation for separate exchangeability)
.

A random array
\bm{z}=(z_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
is separately exchangeable if and only if its distribution can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{sep}}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}).

(15)

The invariant variable is a random function

\displaystyle\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}:[0,1]^{3}\to\mathcal{Z}

(16)

and the ergodic distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
is defined through uniform random variables

\displaystyle u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}(0,1)\quad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij})\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

(17)

Through the shared row and column variables
(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, the entries
z_{ij}
exhibit dependence within rows and within columns.

Variants of this representation capture other symmetry assumptions. If the array is
jointly exchangeable
—invariant when rows and columns are permuted together—then the same decomposition holds with

u_{i},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\quad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{ij})\quad\textit{for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

In a binary array with joint exchangeability, i.e., a random graph, the Aldous–Hoover representation yields

\displaystyle z_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{u_{ij}\leq\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{graph}}(u_{i},u_{j})\},

for a random symmetric graphon
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{graph}}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
. In
Section

D.3
, we discuss array exchangeability conditional on row- and column-specific covariates.
90
extends all these representations to
k
-arrays, where
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

Stationary processes.
We now consider variables in time or space. Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
(continuous space) or
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(lattice), and
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}
.
A distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
on
\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}
is
stationary
if it is invariant under spatial shifts

\Phi_{\mathrm{shift}}=\{\phi_{h}:\omega\mapsto\omega+h\}
with
h\in\Omega
:

\mathrm{p}(\phi_{h}^{-1}(A))=\mathrm{p}(A)\quad\text{for all measurable }A\subseteq\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega},\ h\in\Omega.

Each such
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})
is a
stationary probability measure
. Among these, a measure
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{erg}}
is called
ergodic
if it cannot be written as a nontrivial mixture of other stationary measures. Equivalently, it is ergodic if
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{erg}}(A)\in\{0,1\}
for every shift-invariant event
A
.

By the ergodic decomposition theorem
(
106
, Theorem I.4.10)
, any stationary measure can be written uniquely as a mixture of ergodic stationary measures,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{erg}}(\bm{z})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{erg}}),

(18)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the space of all ergodic stationary measures on
\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}
.
Compared with exchangeable models, stationary processes impose a weaker symmetry: the discrete shift group grows only polynomially with system size, while the permutation group grows super-exponentially
(
84
)
. As a result, representation theorems for stationarity are less restrictive, but also less tractable.

2.3
The finite population model

We have defined our model in terms of the entire set
\Omega
. But in practice we only observe finite data. We now specialize our population model to a finite domain.

Let
S_{n}\subseteq\Omega
be a finite subset, with
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots
forming a nested sequence that exhausts
\Omega
. We observe finite data
\bm{x}_{n}=(x_{\omega})_{\omega\in S_{n}}
, each associated with a local latent variable
z_{\omega}
. We write
\bm{z}_{n}=(z_{\omega})_{\omega\in S_{n}}
. Intuitively, this means we observe a finite subset of the full population
\bm{x}
at selected index locations, i.e. certain entries of an array or certain locations in time or space).

We first state the finite-data analog of the empirical Bayes assumption, that there exists a true prior that captures the population distribution.

Proposition 1

(Finite empirical Bayes model)
.

Under
1
, the marginal distribution of the finite data
\bm{x}_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{S_{n}}}\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n})\prod_{\omega\in S_{n}}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})\,d\bm{z}_{n},

for some prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n})
on the latent variables indexed by
S_{n}
.

We next connect this finite prior to the group symmetry, and write its ergodic decomposition.

Proposition 2

(Finite
\Phi
-invariant prior)
.

Suppose the population prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
is
\Phi
-invariant as in
2
. Then its marginal
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n})
also admits an ergodic representation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,d\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g}),

where

\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})=\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega\setminus S_{n}}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,d\bm{z}_{\Omega\setminus S_{n}}

is the marginal of the ergodic distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
on
S_{n}
.

Consider a dataset
\bm{x}_{n}
and assume it was drawn from
\bm{z}_{n}
with a
\Phi
-symmetric prior. As a consequence of
Propositions

1
and
2
, its generative process is a hierarchical model:

\displaystyle\textrm{g}

\displaystyle\sim\mu^{\star}

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})

\displaystyle x_{\omega}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{p}(x\,|\,z_{\omega})\quad\omega\in S_{n}.

The latent variables are
\bm{z}_{n}
and
g
, and the observations are
\bm{x}_{n}
. The only unknown factor is the prior
\mu^{\star}
, because the likelihood
\mathrm{p}(x\,|\,z)
is fixed and the form of
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})
is known and determined by the probabilistic symmetry.

2.4
Bayesian empirical Bayes

We are given a dataset
\bm{x}_{n}
and assume a probabilistic symmetry on its prior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n})
. Consequently, our data comes from the model in
Equation

19
, with the form of the invariant variable
g
and ergodic distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})
determined by the assumed symmetry. Our goal is to compute the posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})
.

We first write the posterior as an integral

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g},\bm{x}_{n})\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\textrm{g}.

(20)

Note the conditional distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g},\bm{x}_{n})
is proportional to the product of the likelihood
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\,|\,\bm{z}_{n})
and the ergodic distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g},\bm{x}_{n})\propto\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\,|\,\bm{z}_{n})\,\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g}),

(21)

where both these terms are known. The only unknown component in

Equation

20
is
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})
, which depends
on the unknown
\mu^{\star}
.

We next approximate
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})
by a point estimate
\delta_{\hat{\textrm{g}}}
at its value that maximizes the marginal likelihood of the data,

\displaystyle\hat{\textrm{g}}\in\argmax_{\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}}\log\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{S_{n}}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})\,\prod_{\omega\in S_{n}}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\,|\,z_{\omega})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}_{n}.

(22)

The form of
\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}
depends on the chosen symmetry. For example, in exchangeable data,
g
is a prior on
z
and
\mathcal{G}
is the space of such priors. In separately-exchangeable array data,
g
are the functions described in
Equation

16
; we will parameterize them with neural networks to capture a large space
\mathcal{G}
.

This approximation is motivated by both asymptotic and computational considerations. The observed dataset
\bm{x}_{n}
is generated from a single draw
\textrm{g}^{\star}
. So asymptotically, we expect the ideal posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})
to concentrate at
\textrm{g}^{\star}
as
n\to\infty
. In particular, by
Theorem

1
, the law
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
is
\Phi
-ergodic and hence identifiable (see, e.g.,
(
65
, Lemma 27.1)
and
(
80
, Theorem 1.2)
). Under a characteristic likelihood, such as the normal model with known variance, distinct priors
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
induce distinct marginals
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\,|\,\textrm{g})
, so
g
is identifiable from
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\,|\,\textrm{g})
. Posterior concentration then follows from Doob’s theorem
(
45
, Ch. 6)
. Computationally, although the marginal likelihood in
Equation

22
may be intractable, we can use variational inference to approximate it
(
12
)
.

Finally, we substitute the point-estimate approximation of the prior into the posterior of
Equation

20
. This gives the approximation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n}):=\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\hat{\textrm{g}},\bm{x}_{n}).

(23)

We estimate
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n},\hat{g})
, given in
Equation

21
, by exact or approximate methods. For example, we can use variational inference or MCMC.

Our procedure is called
Bayesian empirical Bayes (BEB)
. We are given data
\bm{x}_{n}
and assume a symmetry group
\Phi
for the latent variables
\bm{z}_{n}
. We use the ergodic representation theorem to form the expanded hierarchical model (
Equation

19
), which includes the invariant variable
g
and the corresponding ergodic family
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\,|\,\textrm{g})
. We parameterize
g
and estimate it from data via maximum marginal likelihood (
Equation

22
) or a suitable surrogate. Finally, we plug the estimate
\hat{\textrm{g}}
into the conditional posterior (
Equation

23
).
Algorithm

1
presents this recipe.

Input:

Data
\bm{x}_{n}
, likelihood model
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\,|\,\bm{z}_{n})
, group
\Phi
.

Steps:

1.

Derive the ergodic decomposition
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g})
from
\Phi
.

2.

Estimate
\hat{\textrm{g}}
by maximizing the marginal likelihood or a surrogate:

\hat{\textrm{g}}\in\arg\max_{\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}}\log\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n};\textrm{g}).

3.

Approximate the posterior of the latent
\bm{z}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})\propto\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\,|\,\bm{z}_{n})\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\hat{\textrm{g}}).

Output:

Approximate posterior
\hat{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})

New Models for Empirical Bayes

We now use BEB to design new methods for empirical Bayes inference. In our first example, we use BEB to recover the classical empirical Bayes
g
-modeling for the normal means model (
Section

3.1
). This recovers classical EB methods
(
36
;
107
)
. We then extend EB to additional probabilistic symmetries: separate/joint exchangeability in arrays (

3.1
Classical Example Revisited: Normal Means Model

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The observed variables
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{\Omega}
are generated from the heteroskedastic normal means model

\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}),\quad x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(z_{i},\Sigma_{i}\right),

(24)

where
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is known.

We assume the true prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
is exchangeable. By de Finetti’s theorem, we can write
Equation

24
as the following generative process:

\displaystyle\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}\sim\mu^{\star},\quad z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{g}_{\rm perm},\quad x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(z_{i},\Sigma_{i}\right).

(25)

Here
\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}
is a random prior over
z
. In short, de Finetti’s representation specializes
Theorem

1
to sequences, with
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\textrm{g}_{\rm\textrm{perm}}(z_{i})
the ergodic distribution.

Under the expanded model, the posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n}\mid\bm{x}_{n})
has the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n}\mid\bm{x}_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\mid\textrm{g}_{\rm perm},x_{i})\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}\mid\bm{x}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}.

(26)

In Bayesian EB, we approximate
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}\mid\bm{x}_{n})
with its maximum marginal likelihood estimator (MMLE),

\displaystyle\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\rm perm}\in\argmax_{\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathrm{p}(x_{i}\mid z_{i})\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}(z_{i})\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{z}_{n}.

(27)

If we restrict
\textrm{g}_{\rm perm}
to a parametric family, then this
recovers parametric empirical Bayes estimation, or type-II maximum
likelihood. If we consider all possible priors, then it recovers the
nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation (NPMLE) approach.

With
\hat{g}_{\rm perm}
in hand, we approximate the target posterior
from
Equation

26
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{n}\mid\bm{x}_{n})\approx\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\mid x_{i},\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\rm perm}),

where each posterior is

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(z_{i}\,|\,x_{i},\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\rm perm})\propto\mathrm{p}(x_{i}\mid z_{i})\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\rm perm}(z_{i}).

NPMLE and the normal means model have been thoroughly studied in numerous works
(
69
;
61
;
62
;
15
;
63
;
119
;
34
;
36
;
111
;
16
;
64
;
20
;
107
)
.

If the likelihood is non-Gaussian, the same reasoning yields other nonparametric EB
g
-modeling procedures. For example, with exponential data
x_{i}\mid z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(z_{i})
, this approach recovers
g
-modeling for the exponential sequence model
(
98
)
. As another example, when
x_{i}
is a sample variance and
z_{i}
the true variance, we obtain the
\chi^{2}
sequence model

x_{i}\mid z_{i}\sim(z_{i}/k)\,\chi^{2}_{k}
(for known
k
). The exchangeability assumption on
z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}
recovers the
g
-modeling setting studied in
58
.

(
75
)
. The
28\times 28
image is corrupted by i.i.d. Gaussian noise with variance equal to the empirical pixel variance of the clean image

\displaystyle x_{\omega}\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{\omega}^{\star},\sigma_{0}^{2}),\qquad\omega\in[28]^{2}.

The goal is to recover the clean image
\bm{z}^{\star}_{n,n}
from the observed noisy image
\bm{x}_{n,n}
. The maximum likelihood estimator is the simply the noisy image which has a MSE of
10.291
. The exchangeable sequence approach treats the problem as a simple normal means problem with known variance

and applies the NPMLE procedure. The resulting MSE is

3.2
Bayesian empirical Bayes for matrix recovery

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
. We observe a noisy array
\bm{x}_{n,p}=(x_{ij})_{i\in[n],\,j\in[p]}
with latent signal
\bm{z}_{n,p}=(z_{ij})_{i\in[n],\,j\in[p]}
generated by

\displaystyle x_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{ij},\,\tau_{ij}^{-1}),\qquad\tau_{ij}>0.

Our goal is to estimate
\bm{z}_{n,p}
from
\bm{x}_{n,p}
. We will use BEB to fit a structured prior that captures dependencies among entries.

We assume
\bm{z}
is
separately exchangeable
: its distribution is invariant to independent permutations of rows and columns. Recall by the Aldous–Hoover theorem (
Theorem

2
), we can write the conditional distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\,|\,\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}})
as

\displaystyle u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\qquad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}),

(28)

where the invariant variable is a function
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}:[0,1]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. This conditional representation fully characterizes the finite-dimensional marginals of the ergodic distribution
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}})
, in the context of
Theorem

1
.

The corresponding Bayesian empirical Bayes model is

\displaystyle\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}

\displaystyle\sim\mu^{\star},\quad

(29)

\displaystyle u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\quad\quad\quad i\in[n],j\in[n]

\displaystyle z_{ij}

\displaystyle=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}),\quad

\displaystyle x_{ij}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{ij},\,\tau_{ij}^{-1}).

We parameterize
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}
by a neural network

\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep},\theta}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NN}}
, a two–layer ReLU
feed-forward network mapping
[0,1]^{3}
to
\mathbb{R}
. We estimate
\theta

by marginal maximum likelihood, now writing the conditional
distribution of
x_{ij}
directly in terms of
\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}_{p},\bm{u}_{n,p}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}\in\argmax_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\log\int\int\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathrm{p}\!\left(x_{ij}\mid\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep},\theta}(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij})\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{p}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{n,p}.

(30)

Exact evaluation of
Equation

30
is intractable because the integral is high–dimensional. We approximate it by maximizing an evidence lower bound (ELBO) under a structured variational family. The estimate
\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep},\theta}
is the variational MMLE. See
Appendix

D
for details.

Given
\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep},\theta}
, we approximate the empirical
Bayes posterior for
\bm{z}_{n,p}
with samples, through the posterior of

\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}_{p},\bm{u}_{n,p}
. For each sample
s
,

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n}^{(s)},\bm{v}_{p}^{(s)},\bm{u}_{n,p}^{(s)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}_{p},\bm{u}_{n,p}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n,p},\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep}})

\displaystyle z_{ij}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep}}(u_{i}^{(s)},v_{j}^{(s)},u_{ij}^{(s)}).

The conditional posterior of the auxiliary latent variables is

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}(\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}_{p},\bm{u}_{n,p}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n,p},\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep}})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{ij};\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{sep}}(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}),\tau_{ij}^{-1}\right),

(31)

which we sample from using the No–U–Turn Sampler (NUTS;
55
) or a variational approximation.

Related symmetries.

If the array is
jointly
exchangeable
then it is invariant when rows and columns are
permuted together. In this case,
Equation

28
simplifies to

\displaystyle u_{i},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\qquad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{joint}}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{ij}),

with
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{joint}}:[0,1]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. We follow nearly the same procedure.

If the array is
relatively exchangeable
conditioned on row covariates
\bm{y}_{n}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
with
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
and column covariates
\bm{a}_{p}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{p}
with
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
, then
Equation

28
expands to

u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\qquad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}\bigl(y_{i},a_{j},u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\bigr),

with
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
. This yields the covariate-assisted Bayesian empirical Bayes procedure developed in
Section

D.3
.

If the array is
fully exchangeable
then it is invariant to shuffling all of its cells. This case reduces to the algorithms of
Section

3.1
, where
z_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{g}_{\textrm{perm}}
for a random measure
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{perm}}
.

Denoising an MNIST digit (continued).

We revisit the example of denoising an MNIST "5" from
Figure

1
. The image is treated as a noisy matrix and we apply the proposed empirical Bayes matrix recovery (EBMR) methods under separate and joint exchangeability. We report
100\times\textrm{MSE}
between the posterior means
\hat{\bm{z}}_{n,n}
and the true pixel value
\bm{z}^{\star}_{n,n}
. The fourth and fifth panels in
Figure

1
show the results. EBMR with a jointly exchangeable prior (EBMR–Joint) achieves the lowest error, followed by EBMR with a separately exchangeable prior (EBMR–Sep), then EBMR with a fully exchangeable prior (NPMLE). This ordering aligns with the symmetry hierarchy full
\Rightarrow
separate
\Rightarrow
joint: weaker (less restrictive) symmetry assumptions enlarge the class of admissible priors and increase flexibility. On the other hand, weaker symmetry assumptions also increase the sample and computational complexity for estimating
g
. This creates a trade-off. In our setting, joint exchangeability offers a sweet spot between expressiveness and efficiency.

3.3
Bayesian empirical Bayes for spatial regression

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
. We observe
\bm{x}_{n}=\{x_{\omega}:\omega\in S_{n}\}
at sites
S_{n}\subset\Omega
. Each site has covariates
a_{\omega}=(a_{\omega,1},\ldots,a_{\omega,p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The
spatial regression model
is

\displaystyle x_{\omega}=\beta^{\top}a_{\omega}+z_{\omega}+\epsilon_{\omega},\qquad\epsilon_{\omega}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_{\omega}^{-1}),\ \tau_{\omega}>0,

(32)

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are regression coefficients and
\bm{z}=(z_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
is a latent process that captures spatial dependence. We place a flat prior
\pi(\beta)\propto 1
, so the estimator of
\beta
coincides with weighted least squares. The resulting model along with
Equation

32
coincide with the standard model in Bayesian spatial regression

(
51
;
108
;
89
;
9
;
7
;
6
;
79
)
.

We assume
\bm{z}
is
stationary
: its distribution is invariant under translations
\phi_{h}:\omega\mapsto\omega+h
. Following the ergodic decomposition in
Equation

18
, any stationary process can be written as a mixture of
ergodic stationary measures
,

\textrm{g}\sim\mu^{\star},\qquad\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g}\sim\textrm{g},

(33)

where
g
is a stationary probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
. Following the BEB method, our goal is to estimate the invariant variable
g
from data.

Defining stationary measures with Gaussian processes.

The space of stationary measures is complex and so, in practice, we restrict the ergodic family
\mathcal{G}
to stationary ergodic Gaussian processes. A Gaussian process
\mathrm{GP}(0,k)
is stationary if its covariance function depends only on the displacement
k(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=f(\omega-\omega^{\prime}):=k(\Delta)
. Each stationary covariance function
k
with an atomless spectral density (as described below) defines an ergodic stationary Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}

(
86
)
. So we can write

\displaystyle k\sim\mu(k),\qquad\bm{z}\mid k\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,k),

(34)

where
\mu(k)
is a distribution over stationary kernels.

How can we characterize the stationary covariance functions?
Bochner’s theorem

(
13
)
states that
k
is stationary and positive definite if and only if it can be written as

\displaystyle k(\Delta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\,2\pi i\,s^{\top}\Delta}\,\psi(s)\,ds,

(35)

for some nonnegative integrable function
\psi
. The function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is called the
spectral density
. It describes how the variance of the process is distributed across spatial frequencies. The associated spectral measure is
atomless
if it assigns zero mass to every singleton, i.e.,
\Psi({s})=0
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(in particular, if
\Psi
has density
\psi
, it is atomless). Bochner’s theorem gives a one–to–one correspondence between stationary Gaussian measures and their spectral densities. We therefore identify each ergodic stationary measure
g
with its spectral density
\psi
:

\displaystyle\psi\sim\mu^{\star},\qquad\bm{z}\mid\psi\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,k_{\psi}),

(36)

where
k_{\psi}
is the kernel defined by (
35
). The empirical Bayes task is to estimate
\psi
from the marginal distribution of the observed data.

Bayesian empirical Bayes for spatial regression.

We have defined the invariant variable
g
as the spectral density of a stationary kernel for a Gaussian process. With this definition, the BEB model is

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,k_{\psi}),

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\sim\pi(\beta)\propto 1,

\displaystyle x_{\omega}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\beta^{\top}a_{\omega}+z_{\omega},\tau_{\omega}^{-1}).

(37)

We estimate the invariant variable
\psi
by maximizing the marginal likelihood,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}\in\argmax_{\psi\in\mathcal{G}}\log\int\Bigl[\prod_{\omega\in S_{n}}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\mid z_{\omega},\beta)\Bigr]\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n}\mid\psi)\pi(\beta)\,d\beta\,d\bm{z}_{n}.

(38)

This is the empirical Bayes estimate of the stationary measure. Given
\hat{\psi}
, we compute the posterior
\mathrm{p}(\beta,\bm{z}_{n}\mid\bm{x}_{n},\hat{\psi})
, which is Gaussian with known mean and covariance. The BEB method thus fits the spectral density
\psi
, or equivalently the kernel
k_{\psi}
, that best explains the marginal distribution of the observed data.

In practice, we model
\psi
as a mixture of Gaussians, and fit it by maximum marginal likelihood. The details of this procedure, as well as how we perform simultaneous posterior inference, are given in
Appendix

E
.

In the spatial regression example, we model the spectral density of the Gaussian-process kernel as a mixture of Gaussian distributions. Under this model, the empirical Bayes procedure reduces to selecting the GP kernel parameters by maximum likelihood
(
96
)
. This approach is a common practice in the GP literature. It comes with strong theoretical guarantees and retains the conjugacy of the GP posterior
(
83
;
5
;
110
;
120
)
. What we see here is that this approach can be interpreted as an instance of Bayesian empirical Bayes.

In each study, we generate observations
\bm{x}_{S_{n}}
on a finite domain
S_{n}
(e.g.
S_{n}=[n]
) from a known data-generating process with latent truth
\bm{z}^{\star}_{S_{n}}
. We report the relative MSE to assess how well the BEB posterior mean
\hat{\bm{z}}_{S_{n}}
recovers the true latent values
\bm{z}^{\star}_{S_{n}}
. The relative MSE measures the MSE of the estimator as a percentage of the MSE of the MLE (out of
100

\textrm{R-MSE}=\frac{1}{\left|S_{n}\right|}\sum_{\omega\in S_{n}}\left(\hat{z}_{\omega}-z_{\omega}^{\star}\right)^{2}\times 100\times\tau,

where
\tau
is the precision of the noise distribution.

A better method achieves lower R-MSE.

4.1
Empirical Bayes Matrix Recovery

In our first study, we simulate observations from the normal means model
x_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(z_{ij}^{\star},\tau^{-1}\right)
with known precision
\tau>0
. The true matrix
\bm{z}_{n,p}^{\star}
is a single draw from the Aldous–Hoover representation with a pre-specified function
T_{0}
, that is

u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\qquad z_{ij}^{\star}=T_{0}\left(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\right).

We consider five choices of
T_{0}
, and vary the precisions
\tau\in\{0.25,1,2,4\}
and the sample sizes
(n,p)\in\{(20,20),\ (20,50),\ (50,50)\}
. The different
T_{0}
are as follows:

•

Linear:

T_{0}(U,V,W)=U+V+W
;

•

Sine–log:

T_{0}(U,V,W)=\sin(\pi UV)+\log(1+W)
;

•

Sine–cos:

T_{0}(U,V,W)=\dfrac{\sin(\pi U)\cos(\pi V)}{1+W^{2}}
;

•

Tanh:

T_{0}(U,V,W)=\tanh(U+V+W)
;

•

Reciprocal:

T_{0}(U,V,W)=\dfrac{1}{1+\lvert U+V+W\rvert}
.

We examine all combinations of
(\tau,n,p,T_{0})
for a total of
60
settings. For each setting, we run the experiment 10 times to calculate the R-MSE. We compare EBMR with a separately exchangeable prior (EBMR–Sep,
Algorithm

2
) against three benchmarks:

•

Benchmark 1: MLE.
The MLE is
\hat{z}_{\omega}=x_{\omega}
; by construction its R-MSE equals
100
.

•

Benchmark 2: NPMLE.
NPMLE-based methods are state of the art for normal-means denoising problems
(
62
;
74
;
107
)
. In the context of matrix recovery, it is equivalent to EBMR with a fully exchangeable prior,
z_{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{g}
. We estimate
g
via the nonparametric MLE using sequential quadratic programming
(
70
)
, implemented with
ebnm
in R.

•

Benchmark 3: Empirical Bayes Matrix Factorization (EBMF).

EBMF
(
116
)
models the latent matrix as low rank with exchangeable loadings and factors,

\bm{z}_{n,p}=\sum_{k=1}^{C}\ell_{k}f_{k}^{\top},\qquad\ell_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

with
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k=1}^{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}
from an unknown prior
\textrm{g}_{\ell}
and
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}
from an unknown prior
\textrm{g}_{f}
. The priors
(\textrm{g}_{\ell},\textrm{g}_{f})
are fit by variational empirical Bayes under the normal-means likelihood for
\bm{x}_{n,p}
, and we use the posterior mean of
\bm{z}_{n,p}
as the estimator. Unless noted otherwise, we set
C=10
and implement the method using the
flashier
package in R.

To implement
Algorithm

2
, we set
K=10
and parameterize
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}:[0,1]^{3}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as a fully connected feed-forward MLP with two hidden layers, ReLU activations, and 5 units per layer. We choose a simple network configuration because the sample sizes are small.

T_{0}

Covariate-Assisted Empirical Bayes

We evaluate the posterior-mean estimator of covariate-assisted empirical Bayes (CAEB;
Section

D.3
) for matrix recovery with side information. Data are generated according to

z_{ij}^{\star}=T_{0}(y_{i},a_{j},u_{i},v_{j},w_{ij}),\qquad x_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{ij}^{\star},1),

where
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
are global row- and column-specific covariates,
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
are latent factors, and
w_{ij}
is an additional stochastic input.

We simulate
y_{i}
as iid draws from a standard
t
-distribution with
5
degrees of freedom and
a_{j}
from a beta distribution with
a=2
and
b=5
. We consider three choices of
T_{0}
:

•

Linear:

T_{0}(y,a,u,v,w)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}y[k]+\sum_{k=1}^{4}a[k]+uv+w

Nonlinear:

T_{0}(y,a,u,v,w)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}y[k]+\sum_{k=1}^{4}a[k]+\sin(\pi u)\cos(\pi v)+0.5w^{2}

Logistic:

T_{0}(y,a,u,v,w)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}y[k]+\sum_{k=1}^{4}a[k]+\text{sigmoid}(u+v)+w

where
y[k]
and
a[k]
denote the
k
th coordinates of
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

0.8
to sample from the posterior.

4.3
Empirical Bayes Spatial Regression

Finally, we evaluate empirical Bayes spatial regression with simulated data. We generate spatial data on an evenly spaced grid
-10=\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=10
. The observed variables follow the model

x_{\omega_{i}}=a_{\omega_{i}}^{\top}\beta^{\star}+z_{\omega_{i}}^{\star}+\varepsilon_{\omega_{i}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

with design vectors
a_{\omega_{i}}=(1,\xi_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
where
\xi_{\omega_{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p-1})
, and noise

\varepsilon_{\omega_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{-1})
.

We fix
p=3
and set
\beta^{\star}=(0.5,-1.2,0.3)^{\top}
. The latent field
z^{\star}=(z_{\omega_{1}}^{\star},\ldots,z_{\omega_{n}}^{\star})
is a stationary Gaussian process with
covariance kernel

\Sigma^{\star}_{ij}=k_{w,\sigma,\mu}\left(\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}\right),\quad w=(0.5,0.3,0.2),\quad\sigma=(1.0,0.2,0.35),\quad\mu=(0,0.15,-0.25),

where the formula for
k_{w,\sigma,\mu}
is in
Equation

64
. Equivalently, it is a stationary kernel with a three-component Gaussian mixture

0.5\mathcal{N}(0,1^{2})+0.3\mathcal{N}(0.15,0.2^{2})+0.2\mathcal{N}(-0.25,0.35^{2})
as the spectral density.
We draw a single
\bm{z}_{n}^{\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\star})
as the ground truth.

To simulate
\bm{x}_{n}
, we vary the precision
\tau\in\{0.25,1,4\}
and the sample size
n
from
100
to
3000
. We fit spatial EB regression (
Section

3.3
) with
K\in\{3,4,5\}
, draw posterior samples of
\beta
and
\bm{z}_{n}
, and report the posterior mean of
\bm{z}_{n}
and its R-MSE.

As a baseline, we apply the NPMLE to residuals under the true coefficient
\beta^{\star}
:

r_{\omega}=x_{\omega}-a_{\omega}^{\top}\beta^{\star},\qquad r_{\omega}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(z_{\omega}^{\star},\ \tau_{\omega}^{-1}\bigr),

and estimate
\bm{z}_{n}^{\star}
using the normal-means model with a fully exchangeable prior (
Section

3.1
). The MLE for
z_{\omega}
in this case is
r_{\omega}
.

Figure

3
shows the results. EB spatial regression uniformly dominates the regression-adjusted NPMLE, which indicates that stationarity is a more informative symmetry than full exchangeability for this example. At each noise level, the MSE of the EB spatial estimator of
\bm{z}_{n}^{\star}
decreases sharply with the sample size, while the MSE of the regression-adjusted NPMLE remains essentially flat as
n
increases. The performance of the two methods is comparable only for small samples (e.g.,
n=100
); for larger
n
, the spatial EB estimator achieves substantially lower error. In
Figure

9
of
Appendix

G
, we show that the MSE of the posterior-mean estimator for
\beta
also decreases with
n
under the EB spatial regression model, which suggests that the posterior of
\beta
satisfies posterior consistency.

In high-precision regimes, the NPMLE yields little improvement, with its R-MSE hovering around
80
. By contrast, the R-MSE of EB spatial regression drops below
20
once
n\geq 2000
, albeit with higher cross-replicate variability than in lower-precision regimes.

5
Real Data Examples

For applications, we consider (i) a semisynthetic study where we add Gaussian noise to a cancer gene–expression matrix with varying noise levels, and (ii) denoising and interpolating NYC air–quality data using EB spatial regression. In the first example, we evaluate our methods using R–MSE of the recovered matrix versus the ground truth. In the second example, we provide the full inference results to reason about the learned prior and posterior.

5.1
Cancer

We apply empirical Bayes matrix recovery methods to a gene–expression dataset under varying levels of noise corruption. The dataset is a
251\times 226
gene–expression matrix analyzed by
19
and
73
, containing measurements of 226 genes across 251 breast–cancer patients. The statistical goal is to recover the latent gene–expression matrix from a noisy observation. It is reasonable to assume
a priori
that genes are exchangeable and patients are exchangeable, so we apply the empirical Bayes matrix recovery method with a separately exchangeable prior to pool information across related gene-specific and patient-specific effects.

In the semisynthetic design, we add independent Gaussian noise with precision
\tau\in\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,10\}
to each entry and compare three empirical Bayes procedures: (i) EBMR with a separately exchangeable prior (EBMR–sep), (ii) nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation (NPMLE;
62
), and (iii) empirical Bayes matrix factorization (EBMF;
116
). We measure the performance by the relative mean–squared error (R–MSE) between the recovered and original matrices, averaged over 20 replicates and summarized by the median R–MSE.

For EBMF, we follow
116
by setting
K=40
. For EBMR–sep, we discretize
[0,1]
on a
K=10
grid and parameterize
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}
as a two–layer ReLU network with 20 hidden units per layer. We train
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{sep}}
with Adam (learning rate
0.01
) on the variational objective in
Algorithm

2
over 500 iterations with 50 gradient steps per iteration. The posterior sampling step uses NUTS with 300 warm–up and 300 retained samples (target acceptance probability
0.8
). Each EBMR–sep run requires approximately 5 minutes on a GPU; a full noise–level sweep takes two hours on two GPUs.

Figure

4
shows the results. EBMR–sep attains uniformly lower R–MSE than both NPMLE and EBMF, with the performance gap increasing under higher noise. The intuitive reason is that the separately exchangeable prior captures gene-level and patient-level sharing of information in a nonlinear way, whereas the other two methods either ignore the row/column-exchangeable structure (NPMLE) or assume a low-rank structure (EBMF).

5.2
Denoising and Interpolating Air Quality Data in NYC

We apply empirical Bayes spatial regression (SpatialEB) to a dataset on fine particulate matter
\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}
(in
\mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{m}^{3}
) in New York City. The data comprise hourly readings from 16 monitoring stations obtained from the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene’s Real-Time Air Quality portal.
2
2

2

https://a816-dohbesp.nyc.gov/IndicatorPublic/data-features/realtime-air-quality/
We focus on four sites—Herald Square, Chinatown, the Lower East Side, and Queens North—using records from January 2019 to May 2025.
3
3

3

We select these four sites because they have the most complete records; for most other sites (including those in Brooklyn, Staten Island, and the Bronx), more than
60\%
of weekly observations are missing.
To capture medium-term dynamics, we aggregate to weekly averages
x_{s,t}
at site
s
and week
t
and assign sampling precisionconsis
\tau_{s,t}
as the inverse within-week variances.

The weekly series contain gaps (outages/maintenance), occasional spikes, and heterogeneous noise. Our goals are to (i) denoise and interpolate the weekly average data for all four sites, (ii) estimate the latent weekly
\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}
levels with uncertainty quantification, and (iii) characterize the spatio–temporal dependence structure via the fitted spectral density.

We model the data using the spatial regression model

x_{s,t}\mid z_{s,t},\beta\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(z_{s,t}+\beta^{\top}a_{s},\tau_{s,t}^{-1}\bigr),

where
z_{s,t}
denotes the latent weekly mean and
a_{s}
is a site-specific covariate vector including an intercept and site indicators. The regression component
\beta^{\top}a_{s}
accounts for persistent location effects across monitoring sites. We fit the spatial regression model with
K=10
Gaussian–mixture components for the spectral density, then apply the procedure in
Appendix

E
to obtain posterior means
\hat{z}_{s,t}
, regression coefficients
\hat{\beta}
,
95\%
credible bands for
\{z_{s,t}\}
, and kriged imputations for missing weeks. We also report site-adjusted means
\hat{m}_{s,t}=\hat{z}_{s,t}+\hat{\beta}^{\top}a_{s}
with credible bands.

When we parameterize the spectral density,
\omega=(\omega_{\text{time}},\omega_{\text{space}})
denotes temporal and spatial frequencies. The model learns a spectral density
\psi(\omega)
; by Bochner’s theorem this induces a stationary covariance
k(\Delta)
in the
(t,\text{space})
domain. Peaks in
\psi(\omega)
correspond to dominant temporal cycles and spatial scales of variation in
z_{s,t}
.

We assess the imputation accuracy of EB spatial regression against three Gaussian–process regressors on the same site–week features as our EB model, standardizing inputs and using per–observation noise variances
\alpha_{i}=\tau_{i}^{-1}
from within-week precision. The models differ only by kernel—RBF (squared–exponential, smooth), Matérn
(\nu=3/2)
(rougher trajectories), and Rational Quadratic (a scale–mixture kernel capturing multi–scale variability)—each with a small white-noise nugget (
10^{-3}
). Kernel hyperparameters are learned by maximizing the marginal likelihood. On held–out data, we report the MSE of the imputed series against the true weekly averages.
Figure

6
shows that EB spatial regression achieves substantially lower MSE than all GP baselines because the fitted spectral density adaptively captures the bimodal spatio–temporal variation. By comparing the four methods, we see that fitting a more flexible prior family that respects the shift invariance yields performance gain in imputation.

Figure

5
shows empirical Bayes posterior means with
95\%

Where observations are dense, the posterior uncertainty around the denoised weekly averages is narrow; where data are missing, it is wider. Within Manhattan (Herald Square, Chinatown, Lower East Side), the model borrows heavily across sites—for example, when Chinatown is entirely missing from mid-2021 to early-2022, the model interpolates from Herald Square and the Lower East Side to produce tight bands; similarly, during mid-2023 to early-2024, the model imputes the Lower East Side using the other two Manhattan sites, which recovers a decreasing, cyclical trend. By contrast, Queens North contributes less to interpolating Manhattan gaps (e.g., mid-2022 to early-2023). The reason for this different shrinking behavior is that the EB model finds two spatio–temporal regimes–one for Manhattan and one for northern Queens; see
Figure

10
in
Section

.

6
Discussion

We have presented
Bayesian empirical Bayes
, a general empirical Bayes procedure to obtain accurate simultaneous posterior inference of latent variables when the prior respects a general probabilistic symmetry. Our approach enables the full power of empirical Bayes to be applied to problems with structured data, beyond exchangeable sequences. In this way, it expands the scope of application for EB substantially. Using Bayesian EB, we derived new EB models and inference algorithms for three settings: array data with separate and joint exchangeability, spatial data with shift invariance, and covariate-augmented data. We demonstrated the competitive performance of Bayesian EB over classical empirical Bayes.

There are several avenues for further research.

First, we have focused here on the derivation and methods behind Bayesian EB. Its theoretical analysis remains an open area. Natural theoretical questions include the identifiability, consistency, and sample complexity of learning the invariant variable
g
via maximum marginal likelihood and more broadly, estimation under symmetry constraints.

Second, in this paper, we studied symmetry groups based on arrays and spatio-temporal constraints. In many scientific domains, however, the relevant symmetry groups are more complex, such as those arising from physical constraints (e.g., molecular or spacetime symmetries). Another interesting direction is to leverage geometric deep learning
(
14
)
to design new BEB methods tailored to such applications.

Finally, while our initial evidence suggests that Bayesian EB remains competitive under heavy-tailed noise (see
Figure

8
in
Section

G.2
), it would be valuable to develop sensitivity analysis for these new methods under other types of model misspecification. Sensitivity analysis for empirical Bayes has been studied in
8
;
17
;
26
.

Appendix A
Conditions on Groups

We provide the regularity conditions on the group of transformations
\Phi
acting on the space
\mathcal{Z}^{\Omega}
. For
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi
, we write the composition
\phi_{1}\phi_{2}(\cdot):=\phi_{1}\bigl(\phi_{2}(\cdot)\bigr)
. If
\phi\in\Phi
and
A\subseteq\Phi
, we write
\phi A:=\{\phi\psi\mid\psi\in A\}
. If
K,A\subseteq\Phi
, we write
KA:=\{\phi\psi\mid\phi\in K,\psi\in A\}
.

We equip
\Phi
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\Phi)
. For every locally compact, second countable, and Hausdorff (lcscH) group, there exists a
\sigma
-finite measure
\lvert\cdot\rvert
such that

\bigl\lvert\phi^{-1}A\bigr\rvert=\lvert A\rvert,\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\Phi,\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\Phi),

called a
Haar measure

(
43
)
. The Haar measure is unique up to a positive scaling factor. If
A\subset\Phi
is compact, then
\lvert A\rvert
is finite.

A
Følner sequence
in
\Phi
is a sequence of compact sets
\bm{A}_{1},\bm{A}_{2},\dots
such that, for every compact subset
K\subset\Phi
,

\frac{\lvert K\bm{A}_{n}\cap\bm{A}_{n}\rvert}{\lvert\bm{A}_{n}\rvert}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

If
\Phi
is discrete, its compact sets are finite, and hence the condition specializes to the familiar finite Følner property. An lcscH group is called
amenable
if it admits a Følner sequence.

A Følner sequence
\{\bm{A}_{n}\}
is
tempered
if there is some constant
c>0
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\bigl\lvert\bigcup_{k<n}\bm{A}_{k}^{-1}\bm{A}_{n}\bigr\rvert\leq c\bigl\lvert\bm{A}_{n}\bigr\rvert.

Not every Følner sequence is tempered, but every lcscH group that has a Følner sequence also has a tempered one
(
80
, Proposition 1.4)
.

Finally, we give the definition of a "nice" group, which matches the standard definition in the literature of probabilistic symmetry
(
4
)
.

Definition 2

(Nice group)
.

We call
\Phi
a
nice group
if

(i)

\Phi
is a locally compact, second countable, Hausdorff (lcscH) topological group, and the group operations are continuous.

(ii)

\Phi
is amenable, i.e., it admits a Følner sequence
\left(\bm{A}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Appendix B
Empirical Bayes Beyond Index Transformations

In
Section

2
, we assumed
\Phi
to be a group of transformations acting on the index space
\Omega
. There are many interesting groups beyond index transformations, for example the rotation group or the Heisenberg group. We can develop Bayesian empirical Bayes methods for these groups under an equivariant assumption on the likelihood.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{\Omega}
be observed variables and
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{X}^{\Omega}
local latent variables. A general EB setup is as follows:

\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}),\quad\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z}).

The likelihood
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\cdot)
is assumed to be
\Phi
-equivariant in the sense of
Definition

4
. Specifically,

\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\phi(\bm{z}))=\mathrm{p}\left(\phi^{-1}(\bm{x})\mid\bm{z}\right),\quad\forall\phi\in\Phi.

As a special case, the product likelihood
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z})=\prod_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathrm{p}(x_{\omega}\mid z_{\omega})
is equivariant with respect to any group of index transformations. The Gaussian likelihood is equivariant to rotations.

By
1
and
2
, there exists a true prior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z})
that is
\Phi
-invariant. If
\Phi
is a nice group in the sense of
Definition

2
, the ergodic representation theorem holds for
\Phi
. Thus, there exists an invariant variable
g
such that the population distribution is

\textrm{g}\sim\mu^{\star},\quad\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g}),\quad\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z}).

(39)

The goal is to compute the posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})
, given by

\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})\propto\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z})\,\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g})\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g}).

The next result shows that both the population posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})
and the population empirical Bayes posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x},\hat{\textrm{g}})
satisfy the equivariance property.

Proposition 3
.

The population posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})
is a
\Phi
–equivariant conditional distribution, in the sense that for any
\phi\in\Phi
,

\mathrm{p}^{\star}\!\left(\phi^{-1}(\bm{z})\mid\bm{x}\right)=\mathrm{p}^{\star}\!\left(\bm{z}\mid\phi(\bm{x})\right).

For any
\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}
, the conditional posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x},\textrm{g})
is also
\Phi
–equivariant, including the population EB posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x},\hat{\textrm{g}})
.

This result shows that transforming the structured variable
\bm{z}
by
\phi
under the posterior is equivalent to computing the posterior of
\bm{z}
after applying the transformation
\phi
to the observed variables
\bm{x}
.

Proof.

We first consider the population posterior
\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})
. For any
\phi\in\Phi
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}^{\star}\!\left(\phi(\bm{z})\mid\bm{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}\!\left(\bm{x}\mid\phi(\bm{z})\right)\,\mathrm{p}\!\left(\phi(\bm{z})\mid\textrm{g}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}\!\left(\bm{x}\mid\phi(\bm{z})\right)\,\mathrm{p}\!\left(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{p}\!\left(\phi^{-1}(\bm{x})\mid\bm{z}\right)\,\mathrm{p}\!\left(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\star}(\textrm{g})=\mathrm{p}^{\star}\!\left(\bm{z}\mid\phi^{-1}(\bm{x})\right).

The second equality follows from the
\Phi
–invariance of
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\textrm{g})
, and the third from the
\Phi
–equivariance of the likelihood
\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z})
. An identical argument applies to
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x},\textrm{g})
for any fixed
\textrm{g}\in\mathcal{G}
, and therefore also to the EB posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x},\hat{\textrm{g}})
, where
\hat{\textrm{g}}
denotes the maximum marginal likelihood estimator.
∎

The result suggests that “transform then condition” and “condition then transform” commute for the posterior. The population posterior mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\mid\bm{x}\right]:=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{\Omega}}\bm{z}\,\mathrm{p}^{\star}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}
therefore satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\bm{z})\mid\bm{x}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\mid\phi(\bm{x})\right]
for any
\phi\in\Phi
when
\Phi
is a group of isometries. This suggests that, to learn the population Bayes estimator of transformed latent variables, it suffices to compute the posterior on transformed datasets. This observation connects empirical Bayes point estimation
(
62
)
to the common machine learning practice of enforcing symmetry via data augmentation
(
48
;
85
;
21
)
.

D.3
Covariate-Assisted Empirical Bayes

We extend empirical Bayes matrix recovery with a separately exchangeable prior to incorporate covariates on rows and columns. Let
\Omega=\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
. We observe a matrix
\bm{x}_{n,p}=(x_{ij})_{(i,j)\in[n]\times[p]}
with row covariates
\bm{y}_{n}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
with
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
and column covariates
\bm{a}_{p}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{p}
with
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
, generated via

x_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{ij},\tau_{ij}^{-1}),\qquad\tau_{ij}>0.

The statistical goal is to recover the latent array
\bm{z}_{n,p}
. On the population level, we impose
relative exchangeability
with respect to the observed covariates: the infinite array
\bm{z}
is said to be
\bm{y}
–
\bm{a}
exchangeable if its law is invariant under relabelings that preserve covariate values,

\Phi_{\bm{y},\bm{a}}:=\bigl\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}:y_{\sigma(i)}=y_{i}\ \forall i\bigr\}\times\bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{S}:a_{\pi(j)}=a_{j}\ \forall j\bigr\}.

Under
\bm{y}
–
\bm{a}
exchangeability, an extension of Aldous–Hoover theorem yields a measurable representation
(
22
)
: there exists a random function
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for iid
u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\sim\textrm{unif}[0,1]
,

u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\qquad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}\bigl(y_{i},a_{j},u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\bigr).

The covariate-assisted array model is

u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\textrm{unif}[0,1],\quad z_{ij}=\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(y_{i},a_{j},u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij}\right),\quad x_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(z_{ij},\tau_{ij}^{-1}\right).

(57)

We call the Bayesian empirical Bayes method for this model
covariate-assisted empirical Bayes
(CAEB). The estimation procedure mirrors EBMR with a separately exchangeable prior: we maximize a variational lower bound to the marginal likelihood over a structured family for
(u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij})
. We parameterize the invariant variable
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}
as a neural network
that takes
(y_{i},a_{j},u_{i},v_{j},u_{ij})
as inputs. With
\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{rel}}
in hand, we draw from the plug-in posterior
\mathrm{p}(\bm{z}_{n,p}\mid\hat{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{rel}},\bm{x}_{n,p})
. In the next section, we derive the variational lower bound to learn the invariant variable
\textrm{g}_{\textrm{rel}}
.

Related approaches include the covariate-assisted EB method of
59
,
which fits a normal-means sequence model with the prior’s mean and variance modeled
as functions of covariates, and
24
, which develops
covariate-assisted empirical Bayes matrix factorization. In the latter model, the loading and factor components each follow i.i.d. priors parameterized by row/column covariates.

Recall that the invariant variable is the spectral density

118

\psi
as a Gaussian mixture,

\psi_{\theta}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,\phi\!\left(s;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}\right),\qquad w_{k}\geq 0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1,

(63)

where each

Equation

63

(64)

\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,k_{\theta}),\qquad\beta\sim\pi(\beta)\propto 1,\qquad x_{\omega}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\beta^{\top}a_{\omega}+z_{\omega},\ \tau_{\omega}^{-1}\bigr).

Posterior Inference.

99

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{\bm{u},ik}\log w_{\bm{u},ik}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{\bm{v},jk}\log w_{\bm{v},jk}\Bigg\},

where
\bm{w}
collects all weight vectors

\bm{w}_{\bm{u},1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{\bm{u},n},\bm{w}_{\bm{v},1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{\bm{v},p}
and

\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}
is the discretized form of
\operatorname{LSE}
.

We fit
\bm{w}_{\bm{u}},\bm{w}_{\bm{v}}
and
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}
by alternating updates.
For
j\in[p]
and
k=0,\ldots,K
,

w_{\bm{v},jk}=\frac{\exp\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{K}w_{\bm{u},ik_{1}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k_{1}},u_{k},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\right)\right)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{K}\exp\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{K}w_{\bm{u},ik_{1}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k_{1}},u_{k^{\prime}},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\right)\right)}.

(73)

Similarly, for
i\in[n]
and
k=0,\ldots,K
,

w_{\bm{u},ik}=\frac{\exp\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{K}w_{\bm{v},jk_{2}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k},u_{k_{2}},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\right)\right)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{K}\exp\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{K}w_{\bm{v},jk_{2}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k^{\prime}},u_{k_{2}},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\right)\right)}.

(74)

Finally, we train the neural network
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}
by maximizing the weighted objective

\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\in\arg\max_{\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NN}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{K}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{K}w_{\bm{u},ik_{1}}\,w_{\bm{v},jk_{2}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k_{1}},u_{k_{2}},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{sep}}\right).

(75)

∎

Proof of
Proposition

5
.

By the Gibbs variational principle,

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{p}\!\left(\bm{x}_{n}\mid\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}\right)=\sup_{\textrm{q}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{\,n+n^{2}})}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\textrm{q}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{ij})}\left[\log\mathrm{p}\!\left(x_{ij}\mid\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{ij})\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\textrm{q}(\bm{u}_{n})}\left[\log\textrm{q}(\bm{u}_{n})\right]\Bigg\}.

(76)

Leveraging the structure of
\textrm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}
and rearranging yields

\textsc{ELBO}=\sup_{\textrm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\Bigg\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\textrm{q}_{\bm{u},i}(u_{i})\,\textrm{q}_{\bm{u},j}(u_{j})}\left[D_{\textsc{KL}}\left(\textrm{q}_{ij}(\cdot\mid u_{i},u_{j})\parallel\textrm{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid u_{i},u_{j})\right)\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\textrm{q}_{\bm{u},i}(u_{i})}\left[\log\textrm{q}_{\bm{u},i}(u_{i})\right]\Bigg\},

where
\textrm{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}(u_{ij}\mid u_{i},u_{j})\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau_{ij}}{2}\bigl[x_{ij}-\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{ij})\bigr]^{2}\right)
. Then, maximizing the ELBO over
\textrm{q}_{ij}
for fixed
\textrm{q}_{\bm{u}}
yields the stated objective.
∎

Proof of
Equation

67
.

Integrating over
\bm{z}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\theta})
gives

\bm{x}_{n}\mid\beta,\theta\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bm{A}_{n}\beta,S_{\theta}\bigr).

With
\pi(\beta)\equiv 1
, integrating over
\beta
yields

\log\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\mid\theta)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl\{\log\det S_{\theta}+\log\bigl|\bm{A}_{n}^{\top}W_{\theta}\bm{A}_{n}\bigr|+(\bm{x}_{n}-\bm{A}_{n}\hat{\beta}_{\theta})^{\top}W_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{n}-\bm{A}_{n}\hat{\beta}_{\theta})\Bigr\}+C,

where
C
is constant in
\theta
.
The objective
\ell(\bm{x}_{n};\theta)
equals
-\log\mathrm{p}(\bm{x}_{n}\mid\theta)
up to an additive constant.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1
.

The discretized version of (
54
) is

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{w}\in\Delta_{K+1}^{\,n}}\ \sup_{\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NN}}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{ik}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ii},\tau_{ii},u_{k},u_{k},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}\right)+\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{K}w_{jk_{2}}\,\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{k},u_{k_{2}},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}\right)-\log w_{ik}\Bigg],

where

\widehat{\operatorname{LSE}}\!\left(x_{ij},\tau_{ij},u_{i},u_{j},\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}\right):=\log\sum_{k=0}^{K}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau_{ij}}{2}\left[x_{ij}-\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}(u_{i},u_{j},u_{k})\right]^{2}\right)-\log(K+1).

Alternating maximization over
\bm{w}
(softmax updates) and over
\textrm{g}_{\mathrm{joint}}
(stochastic gradient step) yields the stated algorithm.
∎

Proof of
Equation

70
.

Conditional on
\beta
, the joint model for
\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{x}_{S_{n}}
is

\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\Sigma_{\hat{\theta}}\bigr),\qquad\bm{x}_{S_{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bm{A}_{S_{n}}\beta+\bm{z}_{S_{n}},D_{\tau}^{-1}\bigr).

By Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}\bigl(\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}\mid\bm{x}_{S_{n}},\beta\bigr)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\hat{\theta}}^{-1}\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\bm{x}_{S_{n}}-\bm{A}_{S_{n}}\beta-\bm{z}_{S_{n}}\bigr)^{\top}D_{\tau}\bigl(\bm{x}_{S_{n}}-\bm{A}_{S_{n}}\beta-\bm{z}_{S_{n}}\bigr)\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}^{\top}Q_{\hat{\theta}}\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}+\bm{z}_{S_{n}^{\prime}}^{\top}L\right),

where
Q_{\hat{\theta}}
and
L
are defined in the main text.
Hence the posterior is Gaussian with mean
Q_{\hat{\theta}}^{-1}L
and covariance
Q_{\hat{\theta}}^{-1}
.
∎

G.2
Covariate-Assisted Empirical Bayes with Model Misspecification

To assess the robustness of Bayesian EB methods, we compare covariate-assisted empirical Bayes to competing methods under a heavy-tailed noise. For each
(n,p,T_{0})
design, we generate independent noise
\varepsilon_{ij}
from a scaled Student–
t
distribution with
\nu=5
degrees of freedom:

\varepsilon_{ij}\sim c_{\nu}\,t_{\nu},\qquad c_{\nu}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}},

where
c_{\nu}
is chosen to ensure
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{ij})=\sigma^{2}
. Thus, the noise variance matches the target variance but the tail probabilities are heavier than Gaussian.

Figure

8
plots RMSE of
\hat{\bm{z}}_{n,p}
versus
p
; points show medians across replicates and bands indicate interquartile ranges for each method.

Curves compare CAEB, EBMF, EBMR–sep, and NPMLE; points are medians and bands are IQR (25–75%).
With noise following a
