Euler-Korteweg vortices: A fluid-mechanical analogue to the Schrödinger and Klein–Gordon equations
Daniël M.F. Bischoff van Heemskerck

Abstract
Quantum theory and relativity exhibit several formal analogies with fluid mechanics. This paper examines under which conditions a classical fluid model may reproduce the most basic mathematical formalism of both theories. By assuming that the angular momentum of an irrotational vortex in an inviscid, barotropic, isothermal fluid with sound speed c is equal in magnitude to the reduced Planck constant, and incorporating Korteweg capillary stress, a complex wave equation describing the momentum and continuity equations of a Euler–Korteweg vortex is obtained. When uniform convection is introduced, the weak field approximation of this wave equation is equivalent to Schrödinger’s equation. The model is shown to yield classical analogues to de Broglie wavelength, the Einstein–Planck relation, the Born rule and the uncertainty principle. Accounting for the retarded propagation of the wavefield of a vortex in convection produces the Lorentz transformation and the Klein–Gordon equation, with Schrödinger’s equation appearing as the low-Mach-number limit. These results demonstrate that, under explicit assumptions, a classical continuum can reproduce the mathematical formalism of quantum and relativistic theory in their simplest form, without assuming the postulates principal to those theories.



keywords
Euler-Korteweg systems, de Broglie–Bohm theory, quantum hydrodynamics, vortex dynamics, analogue relativity

Introduction

The analogy between relativity and fluid models is well known

This present paper extends upon previous work by showing there’s not only a formal resemblance between fluid mechanics and our most fundamental theories, but that we can actually derive quantum mechanics and relativity in their simplest forms through a fluid model, by postulating an inviscid, barotropic, isothermal fluid with

\hbar

K=\hbar^{2}/(4m^{2}\rho)
.

2
The equation of state

Let us postulate that space is filled with an inviscid, isothermal and
barotropic fluid, where the undisturbed speed of sound
c_{s}=c
.
Given these assumptions we have:

c_{s}^{2}=\frac{dP}{d\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho\beta}=c^{2}=\text{constant}

(1)

Where
P
and
\rho
are respectively the pressure and density of the
fluid,
\beta
its compressibility, and
c
the speed of light.
And so the equation of state of this fluid is:

P=\rho c^{2}

(2)

It seems natural to infer that the fluid has very low mass density and
compressibility relative to known fluids, but the individual numerical values of these quantities are presently left unspecified.

3
Quantized circulation and the Planck constant

Let us imagine an irrotational vortex as an excitation of the fluid. For an
irrotational flow the velocity field in polar coordinates is given by the
gradient of a potential:

v(r,\theta)=\nabla\Phi(r,\theta)

(3)

v_{r}=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r},\qquad v_{\theta}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}

(4)

\nabla\Phi=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}\,\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}\,\hat{\theta}

(5)

And we establish any radial flow as axisymmetric, being purely dependent on

r
and not
\theta
:

\frac{\partial v_{r}}{\partial\theta}=0

(6)

And thus, the vorticity satisfies:

\nabla\times v=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rv_{\theta})-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v_{r}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rv_{\theta})=0

(7)

Which ensures the vortex is irrotational, even though it can support a
radial flow, and ensures the circulation of the system
\Gamma
and the
mass-specific angular momentum
\ell
are:

\Gamma=2\pi v_{\theta}r=\text{constant}

(8)

\ell=v_{\theta}r=\text{constant}

(9)

For all
r
, as the velocity of the vortex diminishes in exact
conjunction with increasing
r
.

\ell=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta}

(10)

And integrating
\nabla\Phi
with respect to
\theta
, since

\partial v_{r}/\partial\theta=0
, we find:

\Phi(\theta)=\ell\,\theta

(11)

And:

\nabla\Phi(\theta)=\ell\,\nabla\theta

(12)

Any contour of the circulation
\Gamma
is the line integral of the
velocity around a closed loop enclosing the vortex:

\Gamma=\int v\cdot dl=\ell\int\nabla\theta\cdot dl=\ell\,\Delta^{t}\theta

(13)

Where
\Delta^{t}\theta
is the total coordinate distance. Because the
coordinate
\theta
must return to its initial value after an integer
number of turns, modulo
2\pi
, around the vortex, the circulation is
essentially quantized:

\Delta^{t}\theta=2\pi n

(14)

Where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is the integer winding number. Thus, we have:

\Gamma=\int v\cdot dl=2\pi n\ell

(15)

The simplest is the case where
n=1
, which we shall discuss at present.

First let us ascribe an effective mass
m
to the vortex. While this is
not a conventional procedure in classical fluid mechanics, it is well
established in theory for BEC vortices
23
;
54
;
36
;
32

With this, we ascribe a total angular momentum
J
to the vortex:

J=m\ell

(16)

Which is constant if
m
is. For the sake of clarity, we will henceforth assume:

J=\hbar,\qquad h=2\pi\hbar

(17)

Where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant and
h
Planck’s constant.
Of course, the specific value of
\hbar
is now inserted, which may seem
like we are forcing the analogy, but
\hbar
’s dimensions and its
constancy are physically motivated.

4
Korteweg stress as quantum potential

In this manner we already can see that a classical vortex can exhibit
‘quantized’ behavior akin to quantum systems. Furthermore, the periodicity of

\theta
allows us to define it as a single valued phase and so we can formulate
a complex field or wavefunction
\psi
describing the fluid excitation through
both the density and the phase:

\psi=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{i\theta}

(18)

Where
\rho
is the mass density,
\theta=\Phi/(\hbar/m)
is the azimuthal
angle and the phase,
e
Euler’s number and
i
the imaginary unit.
Inserting
\hbar=m\ell
into eq.
12
we have:

v=\nabla\Phi=\frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla\theta

(19)

Which is formally the same expression for a particle’s translational velocity
or the ‘guiding wave equation’ in Bohm’s
pilot wave theory
4
;
5
,
if we interpret the azimuthal coordinate
\theta
as the phase of the wavefunction.

Remembering the continuity equation:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho v)=0

(20)

And substituting eq.
19
, we find something similar to the quantum
probability current equation
38
:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\theta)=0

(21)

Although here, still,
\rho
is mass density, not probability density.

Euler‘s equation without external forces is:

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\nabla)v=-\nabla h_{\rho}

(22)

Where the enthalpy is:

\nabla h_{\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,\nabla P

(23)

But, if the vortex has a steep pressure gradient, implying a density defect
such as a spherical mass cavity or spike in the centre, we should account for
the free energy gradient
f_{0}
:

F=\int\left(f_{0}+\frac{K(\rho)}{2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right)dV,\qquad K(\rho)=\frac{\kappa}{\rho}

(24)

Where
K
is the capillary coefficient and
\kappa
a constant to be determined.
The Euler–Korteweg equation, accounting for capillary Korteweg stress, and any
possible external force per unit mass is:

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\nabla)v=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot\sigma^{K}+f_{\mathrm{ext}}

(25)

Where the Korteweg stress per unit mass expressed via
\kappa
is:

\frac{1}{\rho}\,\nabla\cdot\sigma^{K}=2\kappa\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right)

(26)

And the arbitrary external force per unit mass due to an external scalar
potential
V
is
f_{\mathrm{ext}}=-\frac{1}{m}\nabla V
.

Using the identity:

\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}=\frac{1}{2\rho}\nabla^{2}\rho-\frac{1}{4\rho^{2}}|\nabla\rho|^{2}

(27)

It follows that:

\frac{\kappa}{\rho}\nabla^{2}\rho-\frac{\kappa}{2\rho^{2}}|\nabla\rho|^{2}=2\kappa\,\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}

(28)

And if we set:

\kappa=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m^{2}}

(29)

Then we see the form of the Bohm quantum potential
Q
, which
Madelung
38
employed:

Q=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}

(30)

\frac{1}{\rho}\,\nabla\cdot\sigma^{K}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\,\nabla\!\left(\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right)=-\frac{1}{m}\nabla Q

(31)

Since
\hbar
is the constant angular momentum of the system, and

\hbar^{2}/m^{2}=\ell^{2}
, it is possible that
\kappa
’s form is physically
meaningful.

Following square gradient or mean field theory
9
;
10
;
48
;
52
;
12
;
41
,
the susceptibility or response function
\chi
near critical temperature of a fluid interface
T_{c}
is:

\chi=\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}

(32)

Where
\mu
is the chemical potential and:

a=a_{0}(T-T_{c})

(33)

Then:

\chi=\begin{cases}1/a,&T>T_{c}\\
1/(2|a|),&T<T_{c}\end{cases}

(34)

The characteristic correlation length
\xi
is:

\xi=\begin{cases}\sqrt{K/a},&T>T_{c}\\
\sqrt{K/(2|a|)},&T<T_{c}\end{cases}

(35)

Following the isothermal assumption:

\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial p}\right)_{T}=\frac{1}{\rho},\qquad a=\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\rho}\right)_{T}=\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial p}\right)_{T}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_{T}=\frac{c^{2}}{\rho}

(36)

Define a characteristic radius
r_{c}
where the vortex azimuthal velocity is
c
:

r_{c}=\frac{\hbar}{mc},\qquad\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m^{2}}=\frac{c^{2}r_{c}^{2}}{4}

(37)

If:

\xi=\frac{r_{c}}{\sqrt{q}}

(38)

Then:

\kappa=c^{2}\xi^{2}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m^{2}}

(39)

For
q=4
(
\xi_{+}
) or
q=8
(
\xi_{-}
) for
T>T_{c}
and
T<T_{c}
respectively.
This shows, at least, that there are possibly physical solutions to the setting of

\kappa=\hbar^{2}/4m^{2}
.

As a note of interest, the correlation length
\xi
is close to the healing length

\xi_{h}
in BEC vortices, and
\xi_{h}=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}/2m^{2}c^{2}}
if

gn=mc^{2}
. Also,
\xi_{+}/\xi_{-}=2
is analogous to the ratio between the
characteristic coherence length for the superconductive phase
T<T_{c}
and phase

T>T_{c}
in Ginzburg–Landau theory for superconductivity. If one identifies

m^{*}c^{2}=|\alpha_{\mathrm{GL}}|
, the Ginzburg–Landau coherence lengths for

\xi_{+}
and
\xi_{-}
are then described by eq.
38
with
q=4
and

q=2
respectively.

5
Schrödinger’s equation

Continuing, the full integrated Euler–Korteweg reads:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi)^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}+\frac{1}{m}V=-h_{\rho}

(40)

Let us consider an observer at rest with respect to a set of

x,y,z
coordinates and the far field fluid. The fluid has a
constant drift velocity
v_{d}
relative to the vortex, which is thus
seen to be in motion relative to the observer
(x,y,z)
with
v_{d}
.

In the vortex frame we then have the total velocity potential being
equal to the rest velocity potential plus the drift potential:

\Phi(x,y,t)=\Phi_{c}+\Phi_{d}

(41)

With:

x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad\Phi_{c}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\theta(x,y)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\arctan\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)

(42)

Since we have

\nabla\Phi_{d}=v_{d}=v_{d}\,\hat{x},\qquad\frac{\partial\Phi_{d}}{\partial x}=v_{d},\qquad\frac{\partial\Phi_{d}}{\partial y}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\Phi_{d}}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\Phi_{d}|^{2}

(43)

We can express:

\Phi_{d}=v_{d}x-\frac{1}{2}v_{d}^{2}t

(44)

In the drift case, the added phase becomes:

\theta_{d}=\frac{mv_{d}}{\hbar}\,x-\frac{1}{2}\frac{mv_{d}^{2}}{\hbar}t

(45)

Which added to the ‘rest’ phase becomes the total phase:

\theta=\theta_{c}+\frac{mv_{d}}{\hbar}\,x+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{mv_{d}^{2}}{\hbar}t\right)

(46)

Now, to formulate the complex vortex wave equation, recall that:

\psi=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{i\theta},\qquad\theta=\frac{m\Phi}{\hbar}

(47)

For the Schrödinger equation to appear we must neglect the azimuthal
flow field, which could be justified in a far field approximation where:

\Phi\approx\Phi_{d}

(48)

And ansatz
h_{\rho}=0
, which can be justified when the enthalpy is
approximately constant, such as in the far field and low Mach-limit regime.

Then in the same manner as Madelung has shown, we can insert the Euler
equation and the continuity equation into eq.
47
:

\psi=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{i\theta}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi)^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}+\frac{1}{m}V,\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho v)=0\end{cases}

(49)

And find:

i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V\right]\psi

(50)

Which formally resembles the Schrödinger equation. Here the vortex
wavefunction
\psi
plays the part of its quantum counterpart, mass
density that of probability density, and the coordinate
\theta
that
of the single valued phase.

One could object here, rightfully so, that the phase was derived through
the azimuthal flow field, and the Schrödinger equation emerges only after
suppressing
\theta_{c}
, leaving the kinematic plane wave phase

\theta_{d}
. Viewed from a fluid perspective, Schrödinger’s domain of
validity is then limited to the domain where these suppressions hold.

6
The Born rule

The results in the previous section show a correspondence that is purely
formal; the fluid Schrödinger does not make probabilistic predictions
like its conventional quantum counterpart. Madelung’s formulation employs
a probability density, whereas the classical Euler–Korteweg system
describes a mass density
\rho
. To see how the one and the other can be
related, consider the following construction.

Let
\rho_{v}(x,t)
denote the mass density of a single vortex and

\rho_{b}
the background fluid density, with the vortex mass sharply
localized:

\rho_{v}\gg\rho_{b}

(51)

Consider a double-slit experiment where the localization scale on the
screen is large compared to the vortex core size, so we may model the
vortex as a point mass:

\rho_{v}=m\,\delta(\zeta)

(52)

Where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function, and:

\zeta=x-X(t)

(53)

Where
x
is where the density is evaluated and
X(t)
is the trajectory
followed by the vortex, with:

\dot{X}=v_{d}

(54)

So, given an initial vortex centre
\mathbf{X}_{0}
, the later centre
\mathbf{X}
and density
\rho_{v}
are uniquely determined. But since we can in practice not control
\mathbf{X}_{0}
precisely, and moreover cannot determine both the vortex’s precise initial position and its drift velocity field (see section on the uncertainty principle), a hidden variable is introduced and with it an element of uncertainty.

Consider then a sequence of
n=1,2,\dots,N
trajectories with initial
positions
X_{0}^{(n)}
, trajectories

X^{n}(t)=X(t;X_{0}^{(n)})
. The distribution of mass density in the
limit
N\to\infty
is:

\overline{\rho}_{v}(x,t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}m\,\delta\!\left(x-X^{n}(t)\right)

(55)

And then the empirical distribution of positions of point vortices at
time
t
is described as:

\mathcal{P}_{N}(x,t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\!\left(x-X^{n}(t)\right)

(56)

In the continuum limit this converges to a smooth density

\mathcal{P}(x,t)
:

\mathcal{P}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{N},\qquad\int\mathcal{P}(x,t)\,dx=1

(57)

Where we see:

\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{m}\,\overline{\rho}_{v}

(58)

Thus
\mathcal{P}
is the probability distribution of finding a single
vortex at a given position. Since
\overline{\rho}_{v}
follows
the principle of local continuity, being a conserved quantity, and

\mathcal{P}
is separated from it only by a constant factor, probability
density must follow continuity as well. In this manner then, we can construct a continuity equation for
\mathcal{P}
, and insert
\mathcal{P}
in our Euler–Korteweg equation instead of
\rho
.

Following the Madelung procedure:

\psi_{\mathcal{P}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}\,e^{i\theta}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi)^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}}+\frac{1}{m}V,\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial t}+\nabla\!\cdot(\mathcal{P}v)=0\end{cases}

(59)

We retrieve exactly the Schrödinger wavefunction, and:

|\psi_{\mathcal{P}}|^{2}=\mathcal{P}

(60)

Exactly the Born rule
6
.

7
Einstein–Planck relation

Let us define the total specific energy:

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi)^{2}+h_{\rho}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}+V

(61)

Thus we have:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}=-\varepsilon=-\frac{E}{m},\qquad m\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}=-m\varepsilon=-E

(62)

Noting here we could define:

m\Phi=S

(63)

as the action of the system,
S
, and
E
as
H
, the real
Hamiltonian, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation of the vortex:

\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=-H

(64)

H=\frac{1}{2}\,m(\nabla\Phi)^{2}+mh_{\rho}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\frac{\nabla^{2}\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}}+V

(65)

Which is in a sense the mass-specific Bernoulli equation for the vortex.
Taking the time derivative of the phase:

\theta=\frac{m}{\hbar}\Phi=\frac{S}{\hbar}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}=\frac{m}{\hbar}\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}

(66)

We have:

\omega=\frac{v_{\theta}}{r}

(67)

And the material derivative of
\theta
:

\frac{D\theta}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}+v_{r}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial r}+v_{\theta}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}+\frac{v_{\theta}}{r}

(68)

For a steady vortex at rest, we have:

\frac{D\theta}{Dt}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}=-\frac{v_{\theta}}{r}

(69)

And thus we can express (rest) energy in terms of the vortex angular
frequency:

\omega=\frac{E}{\hbar},\qquad E=hf

(70)

Which matches the Einstein–Planck relation
42
;
19
, relating angular frequency to total energy via
\hbar
.

Utilizing the radius
r_{c}=\hbar/(mc)
and inserting into eq.
67
we can formulate:

\omega_{c}=\frac{c}{r_{c}}=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar}

(71)

And by equating these two expressions for the ’rest’ angular frequency we obtain:

E=mc^{2}

(72)

Like Einstein’s famous formulation relating the rest mass of a body to its energy content
18
.
Interestingly, multiplying the isothermal equation of state by a volume
u
:

Pu=\rho uc^{2}=E=mc^{2}

(73)

Also shows that the total pressure energy contained in a volume of the fluid
equals
mc^{2}
.

Taking these as the rest frequency and wavelength of the vortex:

\omega_{c}=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar},\qquad\lambda_{c}=\frac{h}{mc}

(74)

We should note that it seems we cannot allow a spatial component to the rest wave to have:

\theta_{c}=\frac{m}{\hbar}\Phi_{c}=-\omega_{c}t\quad\Rightarrow\quad m\,\frac{\partial\Phi_{c}}{\partial t}=mc^{2}

(75)

Hence it seems that the rest-case core wave should be a standing wave, only oscillating in time. Possible solutions could be a torsional breathing mode instead of a radial one.
Although this is an interesting inquiry, we will not investigate this further in this paper.

Finally we can note that for a counter-rotating vortex, the velocity, angular frequency and action change sign, and consequently the energy associated with the counter-rotating vortex becomes negative.

8
De Broglie wavelength

In what follows, the terms “rest” and “kinetic” are used in a fluid-mechanical sense: Rest refers to the frame comoving with the background fluid, while kinetic refers to the frame in which the background fluid has a drift velocity
v_{d}
. Investigating the kinetic, drift wavefield perturbations, we can see that

Ae^{i\theta_{d}}=Ae^{i(k_{k}x-\omega_{k}t)}

(76)

Takes the form of a plane wave ansatz, where

k_{k}=\frac{mv_{d}}{\hbar},\qquad\omega_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{mv_{d}^{2}}{\hbar}=\frac{\hbar k_{k}^{2}}{2m}

(77)

Where
k_{k}
and
\omega_{k}
are the ‘kinetic’ wavenumber and ‘kinetic’ angular frequency, and

mv_{d}
can be described as the momentum of the vortex
p
, for the observer at rest with the far field fluid.
And so the momentum and kinetic energy are described as:

p=\hbar k_{k},\qquad E_{k}=\hbar\omega_{k}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}

(78)

Analogous to the momentum and energy eigenvalues of QM’s Schrödinger equation.
So that:

E=E_{\text{rest}}+E_{k},\qquad\omega=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{mv_{d}^{2}}{\hbar}

(79)

If we compare the difference in kinetic phase between two points separated by
\Delta x
we have:

\Delta\theta_{d}=k_{k}\Delta x

(80)

So for the distance between maxima modulo
2\pi
:

2\pi=\frac{mv_{d}}{\hbar}\Delta x\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Delta x=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{mv_{d}}

(81)

We find the wavelength
\Delta x
is the de Broglie wavelength:

\lambda_{\mathrm{db}}=\frac{h}{mv_{d}}

(82)

These waves then, which are purely due to a drift velocity, come into the wavefield equation and will interfere with one another affecting the wave field, which could affect the path the vortex takes.
Then they are in essence a local pilot wave.
If a vortex passes a double slit, its kinetic wavefield traverses both openings, producing a phase shift and interference pattern, effectively allowing the particle to interfere with itself.

With linear acoustic perturbations satisfying:

\omega=ck,\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}

(83)

The same form as light, which could in this model be likened to its acoustic perturbations.

9
The uncertainty principle

Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle
30
is one of the fundamental
concepts in quantum mechanics, but it can be extended to classical systems.
Let us construct a kinetic wave-packet of sorts, by superimposing many plane
wave ansatzes around a given wavenumber, to construct a sharply defined
‘particle’. We would from the fluid perspective not really construct the particle
itself, but a wave-packet of kinetic waves emanating from the particle-vortex
in drift.

Taking the kinetic wavefunction:

\psi=\sqrt{\rho}\,e^{i\theta_{d}}

(84)

At
t=0
we expand in wavenumber
k=mv/\hbar
:

\psi(x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk

(85)

Where
\phi(k)
is the Fourier transform of
\psi
:

\phi(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\psi(x,0)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx

(86)

Defining the packet’s width in space
\Delta x
and wavenumber
\Delta k
by:

Lorentz transformation and Klein-Gordon

The equations we derived in previous sections can be noted to be incomplete because they neglect the retardation of the change of the scalar fields associated with a vortex in drift. It is purely the physical demand to account for this retardation that allows us to formulate the Lorentz transformation.

Let us consider the vortex as a source S of an acoustic wave in the non-convective case:

\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t^{2}}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi=S

(91)

Where
S=q\,\delta(x)
, and
q
is the source strength.
For the same perturbation, but in the fluid flowing with velocity
v_{d}

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{2}\psi-c^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi=S_{d}

(92)

Treating the core as a localized emitter with rest frequency
\omega{{}_{c}}=mc^{2}/\hbar
:

S_{d}(x,t)=q\delta(x-X)e^{-i\omega_{c}t}

(93)

Suppose we have the fluid in drift relative to the vortex source. In the frame attached to the fluid, the vortex is seen to be in motion along its x-axis, so that at the time t the vortex is at
X=v_{d}t
,
y=z=0
. Its position at the retarded time
\tau

\tau=t-r^{\prime}/c

(94)

Where
r^{\prime}
is the distance between the vortex and an observer stationary in (x, y, z) at the retarded time:

r^{\prime}=\sqrt{(x-v_{d}\tau)^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}

(95)

And so:

c^{2}(t-\tau)^{2}=(x-v_{d}\tau)^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}

(96)

Expanding, and collecting terms:

(v_{d}^{2}-c^{2})\tau^{2}-2(xv_{d}-c^{2}t)\tau+x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-(ct)^{2}=0

(97)

Solving for
\tau
:

(1-M^{2})\tau=t-\frac{M}{c}x-\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{(x-v_{d}t)^{2}+(1-M^{2})(y^{2}+z^{2})},\quad M=v_{d}/c

(98)

Where M is the mach number of the fluid flow. So, with drift velocity constant, if the vortex centre were at rest with the fluid the scalar field
\psi
would be:

\psi=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}}

(99)

In the convective case, it would become:

\psi=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(x-v_{d}t)^{2}+(1-M^{2})(y^{2}+z^{2})}}

(100)

And thus the correct transformation between these frames, preserving the wave equation and accounting for the retarded field cannot be Galilean, but must be the Prandtl-Glauert-Lorentz transformation

x^{\prime}=\gamma(x-v_{d}t),\quad y^{\prime}=y,\quad z^{\prime}=z,\quad t^{\prime}=\gamma(t-v_{d}/c^{2}\,x)

(101)

\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-M^{2}}}

(102)

If we utilize these coordinates to describe the phase:

\theta^{\prime}=-\omega_{c}t^{\prime}=-\omega_{c}\gamma(t-v_{d}/c^{2}\,x)=\omega_{c}\gamma v_{d}/c^{2}\,x-\gamma\omega_{c}t

And since in this transformed frame the fluid Planck - Einstein relation holds, we also find the fluid ‘relativistic’ energy, mass and momentum:

Furthermore we can in a usual manner start from:

11
Discussion

The results of this paper show that some of the mysterious entities and rules associated with quantum theory and relativity can arise naturally from a classical continuum model. The enigmatic Schrödinger equation is in this model a convenient linear combination of two more fundamental fluid equations: the continuity and momentum equations of a capillary vortex. We can also clearly see its limitations. The equation neglects the ‘rest’ rotational velocity field that is essential to its own derivation. It is only a weak-field approximation, breaking down at high Mach numbers where it is a mathematical necessity to employ a Lorentz transform, when transforming between two frames with different relative velocities to the fluid.

This paper is proof of nothing other than a formal equivalence between a very specific classical fluid model and Schrödinger’s and Klein–Gordon’s equations, but we have to admit it does invite speculation regarding physical interpretation. Could we possibly provide an explanation for the quantum behavior of elementary particles and the relativistic dilation of clocks, if we infer that our ‘elementary’ particles are these vortices – excitations of a more fundamental, material continuum? It is very much a question though, whether such a physical interpretation would survive closer scrutiny.

First of all, an exact correspondence between the conventional Schrödinger/KG and the fluid equations hinges on the implicit assumption that the laboratory where vortices are seen to be in motion with
v_{d}
, is at rest with the fluid. In this frame, the acoustic Lorentzian spacetime derived by Unruh and Visser is not only experienced by acoustic perturbations: If source and receiver, ruler, clock and observer are all (constellations of) these vortices, subject to the Lorentzian acoustic metric, then this experience should be ours as well. But then the rest frame of the fluid model is a preferred frame, which puts stringent bounds on the compatibility of the fluid model with tests of relativity.

Furthermore Schrödinger’s and Klein–Gordon describe spinless particles. Could the model be extended to a hydrodynamic Pauli equation in the low-Mach sector and the Dirac-equation for
M<1
by admitting a spinor structure into the vortex? A difficult challenge would be aligning multi-vortex dynamics with observed particle interactions, in manifold ways. To derive an analogue vortex analogy for antimatter and CP-symmetry, for the Pauli exclusion principle, tunneling, entanglement and Cooper pairing are some of the many challenges that such a fluid model would face.

Lastly it should be noted that while the fluid model and Bohmian mechanics are much aligned, both being a causal hidden variables theory, both supposing that the pilot wave of one particle or velocity field of one vortex depends on the position of all others, they differ in a crucial sense: Describing a multi-body field in configuration space, as is conventional in Bohmian/quantum mechanics
26
, would be physically incomplete and generally incorrect for the fluid model. Without a formulation in configuration space, even with the vortices all being excitations of the same global field, and Korteweg stress arising from a non-local kernel interaction
24
, the theory should be Bell-local: Any change of field should be constrained by the speed of sound. The only way a local, causal hidden variables theory passes Bell’s tests
2
is if the assumption of statistical independence is taken to be false, which is now commonly regarded as implausible or even conspiratorial.

12
Conclusions

In conclusion, when we postulate an inviscid, barotropic fluid with
c_{s}=c
, and assume this fluid sustains vortices that (1) are irrotational, (2) have an angular momentum equal to
\hbar
and (3) contain a steep pressure gradient at their core, causing capillary Korteweg-stress with
\kappa=\hbar^{2}/(4m^{2})
, we can derive Schrödinger’s equation describing the complex wavefield of a capillary vortex in uniform convection. Correctly accounting for the retardation of the wavefield of a vortex in drift yields the Lorentz transformations, from which one recovers the Klein–Gordon relations as the high-Mach limit. In this model, de Broglie wavelength, the Einstein–Planck relation, the uncertainty principle and other well-known quantum or relativistic relations are analogous hydrodynamic identities related to the capillary vortex.

These results demonstrate that, under explicit assumptions, a classical continuum may produce quantum and relativistic theory in their simplest form, without assuming the postulates principal to those theories. There is a deep correspondence between vortices and fundamental particles, that is sufficiently structured to warrant further inquiry. The correspondence may well come about due to common underlying geometric and conservation principles or something other still, but it seems of great interest to investigate the analogy, to see to where it extends, and where it breaks down both in terms of theory and experiment.
