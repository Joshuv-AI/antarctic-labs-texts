Handle-based Mesh Deformation Guided By Vision Language Model CCS: Computing methodologies Mesh models
Xingpeng Sun, Shiyang Jia, Zherong Pan, Kui Wu, Aniket Bera

Abstract
. Mesh deformation is a fundamental tool in 3D content manipulation. Despite extensive prior research, existing approaches often suffer from low output quality, require significant manual tuning, or depend on data-intensive training. To address these limitations, we introduce a training-free, handle-based mesh deformation method. Our core idea is to leverage a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to interpret and manipulate a handle-based interface through prompt engineering. We begin by applying cone singularity detection to identify a sparse set of potential handles. The VLM is then prompted to select both the deformable sub-parts of the mesh and the handles that best align with user instructions. Subsequently, we query the desired deformed positions of the selected handles in screen space. To reduce uncertainty inherent in VLM predictions, we aggregate the results from multiple camera views using a novel multi-view voting scheme. Across a suite of benchmarks, our method produces deformations that align more closely with user intent, as measured by CLIP and GPTEval3D scores, while introducing low distortion—quantified via membrane energy. In summary, our approach is training-free, highly automated, and consistently delivers high-quality mesh deformations.

1.
Introduction

Mesh deformation is a fundamental tool for many downstream applications in game and film production pipelines, including character rigging, terrain editing, and UV parameterization. Over the years, multiple research directions have enhanced the efficacy, accuracy, and usability of deformation techniques. For instance, various elastic energy formulations (e.g.,
(
Rabinovich et al. 2017
)
) have been introduced to model material behavior, enabling the automatic computation of mesh shapes from a sparse set of user-defined handles. In parallel, interpolation-based methods (e.g.,
(
Floater 2003
;
Lipman et al. 2008
;
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
) allow for rapid mesh shape computation without the need for numerical optimization. Over time, researchers have enhanced these methods by proposing flexible handle combinations
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
and joint-aware deformation techniques
(
Xu et al. 2009
)
. Nonetheless, the process of manually authoring mesh shapes remains labor-intensive and costly, which typically involves the manual labels of deformable mesh sub-part, the identification of handles, and the dragging of handles to pose the mesh.

The recent success of machine learning has opened new opportunities for automating the mesh deformation pipeline through data-driven approaches. Machine learning techniques have been applied to learn end-to-end energy-based deformations
(
Cao et al. 2023
)
, as well as cage- or skeleton-driven deformations
(
Yifan et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
)
. In parallel, several works
(
Xu et al. 2020
;
Jakab et al. 2021
)
have focused on learning to generate deformation rigs directly from mesh geometry.
While these methods can assist artists in setting up the deformation interface, they still often require extensive manual fine-tuning to manipulate handles and achieve desired poses. Moreover, many of these approaches are data-hungry, frequently necessitating retraining for each specific mesh category, which further limits their practicality and scalability.
Most recently, the rise of large generative models (e.g.,
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
) has sparked growing interest in text-driven 3D model generation
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
and deformation
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. Text-based interfaces offer a highly accessible and efficient means of interacting with 3D content, eliminating the need for expertise in modeling or rigging. However, the output quality of these methods often falls short.
For example, the text-driven deformation approach in
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
leverages the CLIP score
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
to guide the optimization of mesh vertices from screen space. Yet, this optimization process is prone to challenges such as view occlusion and the generation of noisy surfaces. Although the method incorporates elastic energy regularization to improve mesh quality, the resulting deformations remain suboptimal. In summary, none of existing text-driven methods can generate high-quality mesh deformations.

We present a Vision-Language Model (VLM) approach for text-guided 3D content creation by leveraging a handle-based deformation interface to modify 3D objects. Our method combines the strengths of traditional handle-based deformation
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
with the accessibility of text-driven interfaces
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. Rather than directly optimizing mesh vertices, we employ a VLM to interpret textual instructions and translate them into handle selections along with corresponding drag directions. By operating within the constraints of a handle-based framework, our approach preserves fine-grained details and produces high-quality, realistic deformations.
Our method takes as input an arbitrary 3D mesh and a text instruction specifying the desired deformation. First, we provide the VLM with multi-view renderings of the mesh and prompt it to break down the deformation task into a sequence of sub-instructions, each targeting a specific part of the mesh. For each sub-instruction, we implement a multi-view graph-cut algorithm that enables the VLM to segment the relevant deformable sub-part of the mesh.
Next, we apply a cone singularity detection algorithm
(
Li et al. 2023
)
to identify potential handle points within the segmented sub-part. The VLM is then tasked with selecting and dragging handle points under each camera view. These predicted handle manipulations are translated into mesh deformations using an optimization-based algorithm, with deformations averaged across views to mitigate the inherent uncertainty in VLM predictions and 2D-to-3D information loss.
Overall, our method enables text-guided 3D content generation. Unlike prior approaches
(
Jakab et al. 2021
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
, it eliminates the need for retraining on specific 3D object categories and consistently facilitates detail preservation. Our contributions are as follows:

•

A VLM-guided, few-shot, text-driven automatic 3D mesh deformation technique.

•

A VLM-guided 3D content generation technique with guaranteed output quality due to the handle-based interface.

•

Our experiments illustrated in Figure
1
show that our method generates deformed meshes that align better with text prompts in terms of CLIP and GPTEval3D scores and less distortion as measured by membrane energy.

Related Work

We review related works on 3D deformation models and text-driven 3D content generation techniques.

Deformation Models

A deformation model defines how an shape undergoes global deformation when specific parts are modified. By employing a deformation model, the number of degrees of freedom exposed to users is significantly reduced, simplifying the manipulation process. Deformation models have widespread applications in fields such as computer vision
(
Wen et al. 2019
)
, computer graphics
(
Nealen et al. 2006
)
, and medical imaging
(
Sotiras et al. 2013
)
.
Over the years, several deformation models have been developed specifically for mesh-based representations. These models can be broadly classified into two categories: optimization-based and interpolation-based models. Optimization-based models typically assume that the mesh is governed by an internal potential energy, with the deformed state corresponding to a local energy minimum
(
Rabinovich et al. 2017
;
Nealen et al. 2006
)
. As a result, when a sub-part of the mesh is altered, the entire structure deforms in a way that preserves detail. However, optimization-based models rely on numerical algorithms to compute the mesh shape, which can be computationally expensive. In contrast, interpolation-based models
(
Floater 2003
;
Lipman et al. 2008
;
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
define a closed-form interpolation scheme that can be efficiently evaluated across the entire mesh.
Most deformation models offer intuitive interfaces for selecting and modifying mesh sub-parts. The two most common interfaces are handle-based
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
and cage-based
(
Ströter et al. 2024
)
. We opt for the handle-based interface, as it exposes an even smaller number of degrees of freedom, making it easier for VLMs to manipulate.

In addition to analytic deformation models, learning-based models have been developed, mostly for other shape representations than meshes, such as point clouds
(
Yang et al. 2018
)
, NeRF
(
Park et al. 2021
)
, and Gaussian Splatting
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
. However, these models are still relatively immature compared to their mesh-based counterparts. Interestingly, recent works
(
Xu and Harada 2022
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
have leveraged mesh-based techniques to deform NeRFs, either by extracting a mesh
(
Xu and Harada 2022
)
or by employing a background grid
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
. These findings suggest that our method can adapt to newer appearance models with minimal adjustments in the future.

Text-driven 3D Content Generation

With the success of VLM-guided image generation, text-driven 3D content generation has gained significant attention. However, due to limited datasets and computational resources, text-to-3D generative models are still in their infancy
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. As an alternative, researchers have explored extracting 3D information from text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
.
Poole et al. 2022
derived NeRF representations from multi-view images using the SDS loss, ShapeWalk
(
Slim and Elhoseiny 2024
)
and ShapeTalk
(
Achlioptas et al. 2023
)
perform global point cloud 3D shape editing, while
Gao et al. 2023
and
Michel et al. 2022
deform 3D meshes by aligning them with textual descriptions via the CLIP model. Both approaches aim to recover fine details from multi-view images.
However, these methods often introduce artifacts due to discrepancies between 2D and 3D views, multi-view inconsistencies, and occlusion. To address this, our approach employs handle-based deformation, ensuring detail preservation of the input mesh. Another limitation of CLIP-based methods
(
Gao et al. 2023
;
Michel et al. 2022
)
is the difficulty in understanding complex text instructions
(
Wu et al. 2024a
)
. In contrast, our method leverages the more advanced VLM model (GPT-4o) to handle intricate textual guidance effectively.

Complementary to our work,
Wang et al. 2024
and
Yang et al. 2024b
use VLMs to organize diverse 3D models from pre-existing datasets but do not allow for geometry modifications. Our method bridges the gap between these approaches. It balances the detail-oriented mesh deformation of CLIP-guided methods
(
Gao et al. 2023
;
Michel et al. 2022
)
with the structural organization of VLM-guided layouts
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024b
)
. We enable modifications to low-frequency mesh geometries via handle-based manipulation, while preserving high-frequency details as much as possible.

3.
Problem Statement

In this section, we formalize the problem of VLM-guided handle-based mesh deformation. The input to our method is a mesh
\mathcal{M}
represented as a set of vertices
\mathcal{V}
and triangles
\mathcal{F}
, denoted as
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{F}\rangle
. Additionally, we assume the user provides a text description
\mathcal{T}
specifying the desired properties of the deformed mesh. The output of our method is a deformed mesh
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\rangle
, featuring updated vertex positions while preserving the original topology.
To ensure the quality of the deformed mesh, we adopt a strategy similar to
(
Jakab et al. 2021
)
, where the deformation is guided by adjusting the positions of a small set of keypoints, known as handles
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{V}
. The full shape
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is then determined from these handles using analytical deformation models, such as those proposed in
(
Yifan et al. 2020
;
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
. However, unlike previous approaches, our method is designed for open-vocabulary mesh deformation, meaning it can handle meshes from any category without requiring retraining.
In contrast,
Jakab et al. 2021
necessitates training separate keypoint predictors for each object category. To overcome this limitation, we propose a novel framework that leverages a VLM to detect keypoints and perform mesh deformations in a few-shot manner, enabling generalization across diverse mesh categories.

4.
Method

To compute the desired deformation in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, we introduce a novel framework that leverages the strengths of non-learning-based geometric analysis algorithms
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
;
Jacobson et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2023
)
and the 3D understanding capabilities of VLM
(
Hong et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024
)
. The key advantage of our approach lies in its open-vocabulary nature. Indeed, non-learning-based algorithms, which do not require training on specific 3D datasets, are immune to data scarcity and can handle meshes of arbitrary object categories. In contrast, the VLM, trained on vast text corpora, is capable of open-vocabulary comprehension.

Our framework consists of four stages as illustrated in Figure
2
, and we refer readers to our supplementary materials for all the detailed VLM prompts. In the first stage, the text instruction
\mathcal{T}
is decomposed into a set of
N
actionable deformation sub-instructions
{\mathcal{I}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{I}_{N}}
, each of which can be achieved by manipulating a single handle to deform a sub-part of the mesh. These sub-instructions are then recursively realized and applied to the mesh through the remaining three stages.
During the second stage, we employ a novel VLM-guided inside-outside segmentation algorithm, inspired by
(
Jacobson et al. 2013
)
, to identify the mesh sub-part associated with each sub-instruction. The third stage involves detecting potential handles using cone singularity detection
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, with the VLM tasked with deforming a single handle from each view. Finally, the fourth stage applies handle deformation to the entire mesh using an optimization-based deformation model
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
;
Sifakis and Barbic 2012
;
Liu et al. 2017
)
and combines the multi-view deformations through multi-view voting. All three stages are performed for each sub-instruction.
We denote the mesh deformed using the first
n
instructions as
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\prime}
, which serves as the reference mesh when applying the subsequent instruction
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}
. Ultimately, the fully deformed mesh is represented as
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}_{N}^{\prime}
. We provide details of each stage in the following subsections.

4.1.
Sub-instruction Extraction

Figure 3
.
In the example of Figure
1
(a), we use
\bar{M}=6
camera views (4 are shown in this figure). The VLM selects two views (left and right ticks), and we use Grounding-DINO and SAM to derive the pixel-wise mask (red). The two views are then merged to yield a per-face label
l(f)
on the mesh using graph cut.

During our first step, we need to extract the set of sub-instructions
{\mathcal{I}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{I}_{N}}
. Such decomposition is an essential step to reduce the workload of VLM. Indeed, as noticed by
(
Qi et al. 2025
)
, VLM can be rather inaccurate in predicting precise 3D information, such as coordinate values. Therefore, we propose to reduce the workload of VLM by first segmenting the mesh into a fixed part and a deformable part, and then task it with dragging only a few handles (typically 1-2 handle points) on the deformable part. As such, VLM needs to determine mesh segmentation prior to each deformation. Our sub-instruction decomposition ensures that each sub-instruction is only related to a single deformable part, leading to much more accurate segmentation result and eased handle selection. Taking the model cow in Figure
1
(c) for example, our text prompt is “raising its head with elongated horns”, which can be accomplished by first (
\mathcal{I}_{1}=
)“raise head” and then (
\mathcal{I}_{2}=
)“elongate horns”, where
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is only related to the heads and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
is related to the horns. In practice, we extract these sub-instructions by providing the VLM with a couple of text-based examples. These sub-instructions can be inferred from the text prompt without even looking at the actual 3D model.

4.2.
Mesh Sub-part Identification

For each sub-instruction
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, our next task is to segment the deformable sub-part of the mesh. As a major challenge to this end, existing open-domain segmentation models, such as Grounding DINO
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
and Segment Anything Model (SAM)
(
Kirillov et al. 2023
)
only works in 2D image space, while our handle-based deformation method requires a mesh segmentation. To bridge the gap, we borrow the idea of graph-cut-based inside-outside segmentation
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
, which uses a graph-cut algorithm to revise a rough estimation of inside-outside labels on the mesh surface. As illustrated in Figure
3
, we propose to use multi-view segmentation label as our rough estimation. Specifically, we maintain a set of
M
rendered images from different camera view angles, denoted as
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},\cdots,C_{M}\}
. From each image
C_{m}
, we invoke Grounding-DINO to detect the bounding box of the deformable part, and then use SAM to extract a pixel-wise mask. Next, for all the mesh faces
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we solve a multi-view-guided graph-cut formulated as the following mixed-integer programming:

(1)

\displaystyle\underset{l(f)\in\{0,1\}}{\argmin}

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}_{\text{mask}}(f,C_{m})[1-l(f)]+

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}_{\text{no-mask}}(f,C_{m})l(f)+

\displaystyle\sum_{f\cap f^{\prime}\neq\emptyset}|l(f)-l(f^{\prime})|w_{f,f^{\prime}},

which is solvable in polynomial time
(
Golovinskiy and Funkhouser 2008
)
. Specifically, we optimize the binary label
l(f)
for each face
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
l(f)=1
, meaning that
f
belongs to the deformable sub-part. If the face
f
is visible from
C_{m}
and SAM predicts that
f
intersects the masked region, as indicated by
\mathbb{I}_{\text{mask}}(f,C_{m})
, then our first term above encourages that
l(f)=1
and introduces a cost otherwise. Similarly, if the face
f
is visible from
C_{m}
and SAM predicts that
f
does not intersect the masked region, as indicated by
\mathbb{I}_{\text{no-mask}}(f,C_{m})
, then our second term above encourages that
l(f)=0
. Note that the first two terms are only related to faces visible to the given camera view, and we leave all the other invisible face labels to be determined by the optimizer. Finally, our third term is a smoothness term that encourages neighboring faces to take on similar values. We weight these terms using a coefficient
w_{f,f^{\prime}}
that is proportional to the dihedral angle
\theta_{f,f^{\prime}}
between the two faces. In this way, the mesh tends to use the same labels for relatively flat areas, while label boundaries tend to appear in sharp features, as suggested by the implementation in CGAL
(
Alliez and Fabri 2016
)
. Our last step converts the face
l(f)
to the vertex labels
l(v)
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
by labeling all vertices in a deformable face to be deformable as well.

The remaining task is to find a set of usable camera views, where label boundaries are clearly visible. Also, we need to provide a text prompt to Grounding-DINO and SAM, notifying the deformable part related to the sub-instruction
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. To this end, we propose to start from a super-set
\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\{C_{1},\cdots,C_{\bar{M}}\}
of
\bar{M}
images. In practice, we find that only using the 6 axis-aligned view angles suffice, i.e.,
\bar{M}=6
,
C_{1}
rendered from
+X
,
C_{2}
from
-X
, etc. More view angles could lead to better results at the cost of more images for VLM to digest. Given the super-set, we engineer our prompt to query VLM for the mesh sub-part name related to
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. For example, for
\mathcal{I}_{2}=
“elongate horns” in the example of Figure
1
(a), the related part is the “horn”. We further ask the VLM to choose a subset
\mathcal{C}\subset\bar{\mathcal{C}}
of
M\leq\bar{M}
images from which the deformable sub-part is clearly visible.

4.3.
Handle Detection and Selection

With the deformable sub-part segmented in the previous section, the VLM could deform the mesh by moving any vertex belonging the sub-part. However,
Jakab et al. 2021
has demonstrated that the selection of keypoint is crucial to the quality of such deformation. Therefore, they choose to train a neural model to predict useful keypoints. Regretfully, the model used by
Jakab et al. 2021
is re-trained for each 3D model category and cannot scale across multiple domains, which significantly compromise the benefits of VLM. During the early stage of this research, we have also tried to have the VLM mark the key points’ locations on the image. However, we found that VLM can struggle to select points on meshes without textures, especially for meshes with large, nearly flat areas.

Figure 4
.
For the two camera views selected during the sub-part identification stage, we show VLM a super-set of handles
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
(drawn in yellow) and ask it to select a desired set of at least one handles
\mathcal{H}
(arrow indicated red points).

To mitigate this problem, we observe that keypoints are typically placed at certain feature points, e.g., the tip of fingers, the foot of a human, and the tip of wings of an airplane. Fortunately, these feature points have distinguishing geometric properties of being the concentration points of Gauss curvature. In view of this, we propose to borrow the fast cone singularity detection algorithm
(
Li et al. 2023
)
to identify a small super-set of potential handles
\bar{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathcal{V}
. This formulation minimizes the distortion of conformal mapping as predicted by the Yamabe equation, and we refer readers to
(
Li et al. 2023
)
for more details. The number of cone singularity keypoints is regulated by the distortion bound, with a lower bound generating more singularities. If the distortion bound is set to zero, every mesh vertex theoretically becomes a cone singularity, as the condition for smooth parameterization becomes maximally restrictive
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2021
)
. To ensure viable keypoints for downstream deformation, our algorithm adaptively reduces the distortion bound if no singularities initially appear on segmented mesh regions. With the handle super-set detected, we then engineer the VLM to select a subset
\mathcal{H}\subset\bar{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathcal{V}
of one or more handles to drag for each select camera view in
\mathcal{C}
. Specifically, we draw all the detected handles
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
as small yellow dots in each image
C_{m}\in\mathcal{C}
. We then present these images to VLM and task it with selecting a set of handle coordinates that are useful to realize the sub-instruction
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. The final handles
\mathcal{H}
are selected to be the closest vertices to the selected coordinates in
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
in the image space, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

4.4.
Mesh Deformation via Multi-view Voting

With the desired handles
\mathcal{H}
identified, we then engineer VLM to predict the desirable target handle position in the image space. Our goal in this last stage is to produce a mesh deformation that realizes sub-instruction
\mathcal{I}_{n}
to deform
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{V}_{n-1}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\rangle
into
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{V}_{n}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}\rangle
. We face two major challenges during this stage. First, since the target handle position is predicted in the 2D image space, the information is under-determined and we have to recover the 3D coordinates. For example, the perspective camera distortion can lead to undesirable distortion in the deformed mesh
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\prime}
. Second, the predicted 2D position can be biased towards the selected camera view since the VLM is unaware of the full 3D geometry.

To deal with the first issue, we propose a distortion regularized optimization. Specifically, let us denote
\mathcal{H}(C_{m})
as the set of handles selected under camera view
C_{m}
and the target position for some
v\in\mathcal{H}(C_{m})
be denoted as
v^{\star}
in 2D image space. We optimize for the target positions for all the handle points in the super-set
\bar{\mathcal{H}}(C_{m})
, regularized by the finite-element-discretized membrane energy
(
Sifakis and Barbic 2012
)
. Specifically, our optimization formulation is written as follows:

(2)

\displaystyle\underset{v\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}}{\argmin}

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{V}_{n}^{\prime}}\|P_{m}v-v^{\star}\|^{2}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\underset{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{\prime}}{\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle\in\mathcal{F}}}\lambda E_{\text{membrane}}(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\mathcal{M}_{n-1}^{\prime}).

Here the first objective term penalizes the distance between the deformed handle position
v
and the VLM-predicted target handle position
v^{\star}
in the image space, with
P_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}
being the 3D-to-2D camera projection matrix associated with
C_{m}
. The second objective term is the membrane energy using
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}^{\prime}
as the reference mesh with
\lambda=0.01
being the regularization coefficient. Note that under the biharmonic deformation framework
(
Jacobson et al. 2011
)
, any vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{\prime}
is a linear function of all
v\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
. Therefore, the only nonlinear term in Equation
2
is the membrane energy
E_{\text{membrane}}
. Fortunately, the number of handles is very small and Equation
2
can be solved via a couple of iterations of Newton’s method.

To deal with the second issue, we propose to blend the results over multiple views. Specifically, we add additional subscript and denote the result of Equation

under camera view
C_{m}
as
v_{m}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
. The final handle positions are then derived by
v=\sum_{m=1}^{M}v_{m}/M
, and the other vertex positions are derived via biharmonic deformation. This operator is essentially doing voting over the VLM-predicted deformations over multiple views. As illustrated in Figure

, we find this technique very effective in dealing with the bias caused by 2D-to-3D transformation. Again, one could use more camera views to achieve better results at the higher VLM inference cost, while we find that
\bar{M}=6
is sufficient for all results in our evaluation. To mitigate the VLM hallucination in predicting the handle position, we self-collect a dataset to fine-tune the VLM. Particularly, for each data, we have an image with handles drawn in red dots, a deformation prompt, a list of handles as input, and reasoning, selected handles, handle directions as output (details in supplement material).

5.
Evaluation

We utilze a dataset from
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, originally from Thingi10K
(
Zhou and Jacobson 2016
)
, and focus on the 1,171 organic models due to their semantically meaningful parts aligned with natural language prompts. To reduce VLM cost, we filter out geometrically similar meshes, resulting in 300 diverse, semantic-rich shapes. We use GPT-4o as our VLM and will release the curated dataset upon acceptance.
All experiments were run on Apple M1 CPU and Nvidia A100 GPU. To profile computational times, we measured the cost of both CPU and GPU computations, including networking overhead. Under this configuration, generating a single result takes an average runtime of 8 minutes. Additional results can be found in supplementary material.

Metrics

We evaluate our method using two metrics: the CLIP score
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
and the GPTEval3D score
(
Wu et al. 2024b
)
. CLIP is a joint embedding model for text and images. To leverage this model, we embed the user input text
\mathcal{T}
and rendered images from
30
evenly sampled camera views, generated using the Fibonacci sphere algorithm. We compute the cosine similarity between the text and image embeddings, averaging the scores across all views to obtain the final CLIP score.
We evaluate performance using two different CLIP models: ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14.
GPTEval3D, on the other hand, leverages GPT-4V to evaluate text-to-3D models across several human-aligned criteria and can handle complex text prompts. Specifically, we assess performance based on four criteria: Prompt Asset Alignment, 3D Plausibility, Geometry Details, and Overall.

Ablation Study

In the initial study, we replace the VLM-guided handle selection mechanism with a random selection scheme. Specifically, instead of having the VLM choose the handle set
\mathcal{H}
, we randomly select a handle
v_{\text{random}}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
to form
\mathcal{H}
. All other factors are kept constant.
In our standard algorithm, a handle position
v\in\mathcal{H}
is moved from
P_{m}v
to
v^{\star}
in image space, where
P_{m}
is the 3D-to-2D camera projection matrix associated with
C_{m}
. To ensure consistency, we apply the same transformation to the randomly selected handle by setting
v_{\text{random}}^{\star}=P_{m}v_{\text{random}}+v^{\star}-P_{m}v
.
In the second study, we bypass the multi-view voting process and instead use a single, randomly chosen view for deformation.
The performance of our method, compared to these two variants, is summarized in Table
1
. The results show that our approach consistently achieves higher CLIP and GPTEval3D scores, highlighting the necessity of both the VLM-guided handle selection and multi-view voting.

We also study the benefits of VLM fine-tuning in Table

We additionally calculate the winning rate in displacing parts that need to be deformed in the correct direction. We use annotated directions in the test set of our fine-tuning dataset as the ground-truth direction. The results show that the fine-tuned model outperforms in all metrics, demonstrating greater robustness against LLM hallucinations. However, the standard VLM without fine-tuning already generates satisfactory results on all examples.

We further perform the qualitative experiment in Figure

Comparison with Baselines

TextDeformer
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
, another training-free framework for generating 3D content via mesh deformation, shares significant similarities with our method and serves as our baseline. We notice another relevant work, FusionDeformer
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, but its closed-source code prevents direct comparison. However, its results are likely similar to TextDeformer. TextDeformer directly optimizes mesh vertices by minimizing the CLIP score as a loss function across multiple views. However, a major limitation is that CLIP lacks an understanding of the plausible 3D shape space, leading to noisy surface details during optimization. To address this, TextDeformer incorporates elastic energy regularization to constrain shape deformation. Unfortunately, as shown in Figure
6
, this regularization still results in undesirable deformations, compromising 3D plausibility. In contrast, our method consistently produces more visually satisfactory results.
We present our quantitative results in Table
3
, comparing our deformed meshes to both the original mesh and the outputs of TextDeformer. Our method consistently outperforms the original mesh across all metrics, demonstrating our effectiveness. On the other hand, although TextDeformer achieves a higher CLIP ViT-B/32 score, which is expected, given its use of ViT-B/32 as a loss function, our method surpasses it when evaluated with ViT-L/14. Additionally, we outperform TextDeformer across all GPTEval3D metrics.
These results underscore the sensitivity of the CLIP score to model size and highlight its limitations in reflecting 3D plausibility. As illustrated in Figure
6
, the GPTEval3D score aligns more closely with human aesthetic judgments.
Further, we note that our method enjoys a lower computational cost, with the performance breakdown summarized in Table
5
. This is because TextDeformer requires many evaluations of the loss function by running CLIP model inference, while we only requires a network-independent optimization (Equation
2
) for mesh deformation that is very efficient to solve.
Finally, we compare the mesh distortion induced by both methods as measured by
E_{\text{membrane}}
. As summarized in Table
4
, we see that our method generates much smaller distortion on average. While the distortion variance of TextDeformer is also high, i.e. it can sometimes generate results corresponding to rather small distortion. This is because the optimizer in TextDeformer can sometimes get stuck in local minima, leading to very small overall deformation, which does not align well with
\mathcal{T}
.
We also compare with a part-based text-guided mesh editing method, MagicClay
(
Barda et al. 2024
)
. MagicClay generates regions of a mesh according to textual prompts using a hybrid mesh-SDF representation. We use the segmented region as input to evaluate its ability to evolve the shape toward a text prompt. According to Figure
6

User Control

As a major benefit, our method generates a sequence of explainable intermediary outputs, including segmentations, handles, and handle translations. Therefore, users could intuitively modify the results by tuning any of these intermediary outputs and continue running the remaining steps of our pipline. In Figure

6.
Conclusion & Limitation

We propose a training-free VLM-guided method for text-based 3D mesh deformation. Central to our approach is a handle-based deformation framework, which effectively regularizes the output mesh and ensures its quality. Our method carefully builds VLM into the pipeline of handle-based deformation. Specifically, VLM participates in the process of sub-part identification, handle identification, and mesh deformation. Through comprehensive evaluations, we demonstrate that our method consistently achieves high scores on CLIP and GPTEval3D metrics.

Despite its strengths, our method has some limitations. First, it is restricted to low-frequency modifications and cannot generate finer details, as achieved by methods like
Chen et al. 2021
. Second, our approach does not utilize any shape priors. Deformations are driven solely by handle manipulations and energy-based regularizations, such as membrane energy. Third, our method may result in self-intersection or unsymmetric deformation due to the fact that VLM has no 3D shape prior knowledge and, therefore, cannot always perfectly give intersection-free and symmetric handle movement prediction. Such limitations can be fixed by using a barrier function and symmetric-aware geometric processing tools
(
Golovinskiy et al. 2009
)
