Diagnosis and surgery for cervical synovial cysts: A review
Authors: Tayfun Hakan
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article by Epstein et al., which provides comprehensive insights into the diagnosis and surgical management of cervical synovial cysts.[3] Although rare, cervical synovial cysts represent a clinically significant pathology that may lead to progressive cervical myelopathy and irreversible neurological deficits if diagnosis and intervention are delayed.

In 2002, we published a case involving a 55-year-old male who presented with a 7-month history of progressive gait disturbance and upper motor neuron signs, including hyperreflexia and positive Hoffmann and Babinski responses.[4] Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a non-enhancing extradural synovial cyst originating from the left C5–6 facet joint. Gross total excision through laminectomy resulted in a marked neurological recovery, with the patient regaining independent ambulation by the 4th postoperative month. Our literature review at that time found only 27 cervical synovial cyst cases reported since the initial description by Cartwright et al. in 1985.[1]

This experience reinforced the notion that even isolated clinical reports contribute meaningfully to collective understanding and future clinical practice. What might seem clinically trivial at 1 time may later prove crucial in shaping diagnostic or therapeutic guidelines.

While aspiration or percutaneous interventions might temporarily alleviate symptoms, we concur with Epstein et al.[2] that complete surgical excision remains the definitive treatment, particularly in cases with significant mass effect. The evolution toward minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques is noteworthy and highlights the dynamic, non-dogmatic nature of surgical science, which continually refines its methods in pursuit of safer and more effective care.

We also appreciate the authors’ emphasis on preoperative imaging. Although MRI is the modality of choice, computed tomography holds particular value in identifying calcified cyst walls, which may adhere tightly to the dura. As highlighted in the review, the presence of a thick, calcified capsule raises the risk of inadvertent dural tears during resection.[2] This underscores the importance of meticulous preoperative planning and surgical technique.

Ultimately, Epstein et al.[3] aptly address both the challenges and evolving strategies in managing cervical synovial cysts. Their work further reinforces the need for early diagnosis, surgical precision, and multidisciplinary vigilance in preventing long-term disability.
