From Kinematic Motion Planners to Dynamic Autonomous Navigation with Obstacle Avoidance (Extended version)
Mayur Sawant, Abdelhamid Tayebi

Abstract
Kinematic motion planners are among the most widely used control approaches in robotic applications. By modeling the robot as a first-order system, they generate a feedback-based desired velocity field that guides the robot toward a target while avoiding obstacles. However, extending such feedback planners to systems with higher-order dynamics, while preserving safety and stability properties, is not a straightforward task. In the present work, we propose an approach that adapts existing feedback-based kinematic motion planners to second-order autonomous systems while retaining their safety and almost global asymptotic stability guarantees. We consider two general classes of kinematic motion planners: those derived from navigation functions and those defined directly through desired velocity fields without relying on an underlying navigation function. To validate the proposed methodology, two feedback-based kinematic motion planners are adapted to second-order systems and evaluated both in simulations and experimentally.

We consider two widely used classes of feedback-based kinematic motion planners: planners relying on a navigation function and planners specified directly by a desired velocity field without an underlying navigation function.
For the former class, we design a control strategy that relies on a damping term with a gain depending on the robot’s proximity to the obstacles. We show that the resulting second-order closed-loop system inherits the safety and convergence properties of the kinematic motion planner.
For the latter class, we design a control strategy that ensures a monotonic decrease in the norm of the error between the robot’s velocity and the desired velocity prescribed by the kinematic motion planner, and prove that the safety and convergence properties of the kinematic motion planner are preserved for the resulting second-order closed-loop system.

IV
Feedback control design

If the first-order planner
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}_{d}(\mathbf{x})
is applied to the second-order system (
2
) without incorporating damping, the
\mathbf{x}
-trajectory of the resulting closed-loop system may exhibit an overshoot. This overshoot may lead the system into unsafe regions, potentially resulting in collisions with obstacles.
To avoid such overshoots, a damping vector of the form
-k\mathbf{v}
, with a sufficiently high gain
k>0
, can be introduced.
However, excessively high damping significantly reduces the robot’s velocity, leading to slow convergence to the desired target state.

To address this trade-off, we propose a dynamic damping gain that adapts based on the robot’s proximity to obstacles.
Specifically, the damping gain remains low when the robot is far from obstacles, allowing for fast motion, and increases as the robot approaches obstacle boundaries.
This mechanism ensures safety by reducing the robot’s velocity near obstacles while maintaining efficient progress toward the target in obstacle-free regions.

In comparison with the first-order motion planner proposed in

VIII
Conclusion

The paper proposes two control strategies for adapting existing feedback-based kinematic motion planners that satisfy Properties
P1
-
P5
to systems governed by second-order dynamics, while retaining their safety and convergence properties.

For gradient-based kinematic motion planners, the DDF control strategy introduces a damping term whose gain increases as the robot approaches an obstacle.
The resulting increase in damping reduces the robot’s speed near the obstacle boundary, allowing the control input to eventually drive the robot’s velocity
\mathbf{v}
toward the desired velocity prescribed by the kinematic motion planner
\mathbf{v}_{d}
.
For feedback-based kinematic motion planners that are not derived from a navigation function, the VTF control strategy guarantees a monotonic decrease in the norm of the error between the robot’s velocity and the desired velocity, as illustrated in Fig.
7(d)
.

In the DDF control strategy, an increase in
\beta(d_{r}(\mathbf{x}))
increases the magnitude of the damping term, thereby reducing the robot’s velocity near obstacles.
Whereas in the VTF control strategy, the increase in
\beta(d_{r}(\mathbf{x}))
increases the rate at which the robot’s velocity tracks the desired velocity.
Consequently, as observed in Fig.
4
, the VTF-controlled trajectories generally converge to the target faster than the corresponding DDF-controlled trajectories.

Although the proposed control schemes provide rigorous safety and convergence guarantees under the stated assumptions, the present analysis does not account for control input constraints.
Incorporating control saturation while preserving these guarantees is an important direction for future work.
Furthermore, the analysis requires the obstacle boundaries to be sufficiently smooth so that the distance function
d_{r}
is at least twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of each obstacle boundary.
Extending the proposed framework to kinematic motion planners operating in environments with non-smooth obstacle boundaries is another important direction for future research.

Consider the map

)-(

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{d}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{z},

(85a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=-k_{d}\beta(d_{r}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{z}.

(85b)

2
)-(

) and (
85
) are equivalent under the diffeomorphism
T
, we proceed to show that, for every initial condition
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{z}(0)\in\mathcal{X}_{r}^{\circ}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the position trajectory
\mathbf{x}(t)

85
) is precompact in
\mathcal{X}_{r}^{\circ}

\mathcal{X}_{r}^{\circ}

, there exists
