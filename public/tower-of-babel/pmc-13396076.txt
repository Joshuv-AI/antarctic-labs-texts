Real-World Safety and Effectiveness of Lasmiditan in Patients with Migraine: A Post-Marketing Observational Study in Japan
Authors: Naoko Sanno, Kevin Y Urayama, Joji Mori, Kenichi Yoshizawa, Chie Hashimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMigraine is a chronic, disabling neurological disorder affecting the Japanese population, with a significant impact on quality of life and daily activities. Lasmiditan, a selective 5-HT1F receptor agonist approved for acute migraine treatment, provides an alternative for patients who cannot tolerate or do not respond to existing therapies, including triptans. The study aimed to evaluate the real-world safety and effectiveness of lasmiditan in Japanese patients with migraine as part of post-marketing pharmacovigilance requirements.MethodsThis single-arm, multicenter, non-interventional, prospective observational study enrolled 550 patients from 53 medical sites across Japan. Patients newly prescribed lasmiditan for acute migraine between June 2022 and September 2024 were included. Data were collected via electronic case report forms, eDiaries, and questionnaires. Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), serious AEs (SAEs), and treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs). Effectiveness was evaluated by pain freedom and pain relief at 2 h post-dose. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics.ResultsOf the 400 patients analyzed for safety (mean age 40.5 years; 79.5% female), 56.0% received 50 mg and 42.8% received 100 mg lasmiditan as the initial dose. About 41.8% experienced at least one TEAE, with no SAEs or deaths. The most common TEAEs were dizziness (8.4%) and somnolence (3.9%) with a median time to resolution of 3 and 4 h, respectively. In the effectiveness analysis set (n = 328), for moderate-to-severe headache, 21.5% and 53.9% achieved pain freedom or pain relief, respectively, 2 h post-first dose. For specific daily activities, 45.9% of work or school tasks improved from “severely disturbed” and “couldn’t move” to “no problem” or “slightly disturbed” after 2 h.ConclusionsLasmiditan was associated with a manageable safety profile and effectiveness in patients with acute migraine in routine clinical practice in Japan, with no new safety concerns identified.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00966-4.Keywords: Dizziness, Effectiveness, Lasmiditan, Migraine, Pain freedom, Pain relief, Post-marketing surveillance, Real-world study, Safety, Somnolence

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Migraine is a prevalent and disabling neurological disorder in Japan, with significant unmet needs for safe and effective acute treatments. Limitations of existing therapies such as triptans highlight the need for alternative acute migraine treatments.As part of post-marketing pharmacovigilance requirements in Japan, this study aimed to evaluate the real-world safety and effectiveness of lasmiditan in Japanese patients with acute migraine. What was learned from the study? Lasmiditan had a manageable safety profile, with dizziness and somnolence as the most frequent adverse events (AEs). No serious AEs or deaths were reported. The most commonly prescribed initial dose of lasmiditan was 50 mg (n = 224, 56.0%), followed by 100 mg (n = 171, 42.8%).In all attacks treated with lasmiditan, pain freedom was achieved in 21.5% and pain relief in 53.9% at 2 h post-administration.These findings provide real-world evidence on lasmiditan use in routine clinical practice across diverse patient populations, highlighting the value of ongoing pharmacovigilance to guide future research and clinical practice.

Introduction

Migraine is a chronic, disabling neurological disorder marked by recurrent moderate-to-severe headaches, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia, significantly impairing patients’ quality of life and daily activities [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, migraine is the third leading cause of living with disability worldwide [3], reflecting its substantial societal burden, particularly due to its high prevalence among working-age individuals [2].

In Japan, migraine affects approximately 6–9% of the population annually [1, 4] with prevalence nearly four times higher in female individuals (12.9%) than male individuals (3.6%), and peaking among those aged 30–40 years [5]. Although several acute treatments, including prescription drugs (triptans) and over-the-counter medications (such as acetaminophen and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]), are available for migraine, up to 42% patients show an inadequate response to these therapies [6, 7]. Moreover, triptans are contraindicated in individuals with, or at risk for, cardiovascular disease, and adverse events (AEs) such as chest discomfort and cardiovascular warnings further limit their use and adherence [6, 8, 9].

Lasmiditan, a highly selective 5-HT1F receptor agonist [9] of the novel ditan class [8, 10], targets the trigeminal system to inhibit hyperactive trigeminal neurons and suppress the release of neurotransmitters such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), without inducing vasoconstriction [11]. Lasmiditan has been approved in Japan and several other countries for the acute treatment of migraine, with or without aura, in adults [12]. Its efficacy and safety have been well established in global phase 3 trials (SAMURAI, SPARTAN, GLADIATOR, and CENTURION), across both single and multiple migraine attacks [13–16]. These studies demonstrated its efficacy across multiple endpoints, including pain freedom and pain relief, freedom from the most bothersome symptom, along with a favorable safety profile, even in patients with cardiovascular risk factors [13–16].

In Japan, the phase 2 MONONOFU study confirmed the efficacy of lasmiditan in patients with migraine. At the 100-mg dose, 32.4% patients achieved pain freedom at 2 h post-dose, compared with 16.6% patients receiving placebo [17]. Prespecified analyses also demonstrated rapid and sustained relief from moderate-to-severe migraine attacks [18]. Pain freedom with lasmiditan was achieved even when administered more than 1 h after migraine onset, offering an advantage over triptans, which are less effective with delayed dosing [19].

Central nervous system (CNS)-related AEs, such as dizziness and somnolence, were important identified risks in Japan. In the MONONOFU study, dizziness was reported in 39.4% patients and somnolence in 19.3%; these events were mild to moderate in severity and not reported as serious AEs (SAEs) [17]. Although these AEs did not lead to accidents or injuries, their potential clinical impact, especially in real-world settings, may warrant close monitoring.

To gain further insight into lasmiditan’s safety profile and guide risk mitigation strategies, this post-marketing safety study, conducted as a pharmacovigilance requirement in Japan, utilized data from electronic medical records and an electronic diary (eDiary) to capture patient-reported data under routine clinical care. The use of eDiary ensured that study results reflected real-world experiences of patients with acute migraine and enabled assessments from the patient’s perspective.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Overall, 550 patients from 53 medical sites across Japan were enrolled in this single-arm, multicenter, non-interventional, prospective observational study. The sites were selected from hospitals that treat patients with migraine, primarily those with internal medicine, neurology, and neurosurgery departments. Patients who were newly prescribed lasmiditan for acute treatment between June 2022 to September 2024 were included. An additional criterion was applied for treated attacks: lasmiditan had to be administered within 4 h of headache onset for the analysis of pain freedom and pain relief. Patients participating in any clinical trial or those with contraindications to lasmiditan use, as specified in the product label, were excluded. Patient assessment and treatment were based on the physician’s judgment.

The index date was defined as the date of the first lasmiditan prescription. The baseline period was defined as 3 months prior to the index date. The exposure period was defined as the period spanning from each lasmiditan administration to the end of the following day (e.g., for administration on 16 October 16:00 hours, the exposure period spanned to 17 October 23:59 hours). This exposure window was prespecified in the study protocol and selected to capture clinically relevant treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) within the expected post-dose elimination period of lasmiditan (up to 32 h) [20], while facilitating calendar-day-based patient reporting via the eDiary [17]. Patients were observed for up to 12 weeks from the index date. All patients were followed until the earliest occurrence of treatment discontinuation, completion of the 12-week observation period, or withdrawal/loss to follow-up.

The study was conducted per ethical principles originating from the Declaration of Helsinki and was compliant with the Japanese “Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects”. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Research Institute of Healthcare Data Science (Tokyo, Japan; Approval No. RI2022002). Before enrollment, patients received a detailed study explanation and provided informed consent, including agreement for information release. Only those who consented to participate and to this use of their personal devices were enrolled.

Data Collection

Patients were registered on the day of initial lasmiditan prescription or within 14 days thereafter. Patient data were obtained using an electronic case report form (eCRF), eDiary, and paper questionnaire, and entered in the electronic data capture (eDC) system. Patient-reported migraine characteristics and treatment details, including lasmiditan use and concomitant medications, were recorded using a study-specific eDiary app on their personal devices. For certain data entered in the eDiary, including lasmiditan administration, concomitant medication use, and safety-related information, physicians verified the data through patient interviews and resolved any discrepancies based on clinical judgment. Data from eDiary were subsequently transcribed into the eCRF.

At baseline, demographic and lifestyle-related information, including lactation status, smoking habits, and frequency of operating machinery, along with risk factors for cerebrocardiovascular disease, any drug hypersensitivity, medical history, and current comorbidities, was recorded. Pregnancy status was documented on the index date and monitored throughout the observation period. Additionally, migraine history prior to the index date, vital signs on the index date, menstruation information, concomitant medications, and details of migraine attacks during the observation period were collected.

Outcomes

For safety assessments, all AEs reported during the patient interviews, including CNS-related AEs such as dizziness and somnolence, were collected throughout the exposure period. Additionally, information on serious injuries and/or accidents, including falls and motor vehicle incidents, was collected. During the exposure period, patients used the eDiary to record any adverse signs or symptoms that were unfavorable and unintended following lasmiditan administration.

For effectiveness assessments, all treated attacks were evaluated for improvement in baseline headache severity 2 h after administration using the International Headache Society four-point headache severity rating scale (0 = no pain; 1 = mild pain; 2 = moderate pain; and 3 = severe pain). Patients recorded the time to meaningful relief and the time to becoming headache-free. Impairment in daily activity, including basic daily activities, household chores, and work or school tasks, during lasmiditan dosing was measured at baseline and 2 h post-dose by determining the level of interference with normal activities, using three response options: “activity was not impaired”, “activity was impaired to some degree”, and “activity was severely impaired”. Patients also recorded the time to return to their daily activities. Satisfaction with returning to daily life was evaluated at 24 h post-administration using a seven-point Likert scale (very satisfied, satisfied, slightly satisfied, neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, slightly dissatisfied, dissatisfied, and very dissatisfied).

Statistical Analyses

This study aimed to enroll approximately 500 patients in order to secure at least 384 with sufficient follow-up. This was determined using a one-group χ2 test, with assumptions based on previous clinical trial results. Patients who received at least one lasmiditan dose were included in the safety and effectiveness analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), or as median, minimum and maximum. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For AEs, SAEs, treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs), adverse drug reactions (ADRs), serious ADRs, and treatment-emergent ADRs (TEADRs), the number of cases, incidence proportion (%), and total number of events were calculated. Kaplan–Meier plots were used to present the survival time analysis. Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test was used for categorical analysis. Data were analyzed using SAS (SAS Institute Corporation) version 9.4. and were presented as observed; no imputation was made for variables.

Results

Patient Demographics

Of the 550 patients enrolled in the study, 400 and 328 were included in the safety and effectiveness analyses, respectively (Fig. 1). Patients were excluded from the safety analysis if they had no data available after the first prescription date (n = 24) or had not taken lasmiditan within 12 weeks (n = 126). For the effectiveness analysis, exclusions were due to protocol-defined exclusions for labeling/use (n = 34) or missing dose information (n = 24). Protocol-defined exclusions for labeling/use included administration to patients aged < 15 years, use of non-approved doses (other than 50, 100, or 200 mg), multiple doses for the resolution of a single migraine attack, or administration without any migraine symptoms (e.g., aura, premonition, or associated symptoms including cutaneous allodynia symptoms) and occurring outside of all headache start/end durations (Fig. 1). Additionally, patients were excluded due to protocol defined off-label use or missing dose-day information for all administrations (n = 14). This combined category comprised patients who received treatment while asymptomatic (no aura, premonitory, or associated symptoms, including cutaneous allodynia), with dosing occurring outside the start and end of all headache episodes.

Among 400 patients in the safety analysis population, the majority were female (n = 318, 79.5%) (Table 1). The mean (SD) age of patients was 40.5 (12.5) years, and the mean (SD) body mass index (BMI) was 22.67 (4.74) kg/m2. No pregnancies were reported (Table 1), and only 1 patient (0.3%) reported breastfeeding at baseline (Table S1). Most patients (n = 268, 67.0%) had never smoked, and 25 (6.3%) were current smokers. Eleven patients (2.8%) reported a family history of coronary artery disease (involving parents, grandparents, or siblings). Nearly half (n = 195, 48.8%) of the patients had no cerebrocardiovascular risk factors, while the remaining (n = 205, 51.3%) had up to five risk factors, with the number of patients decreasing progressively as the number of risk factors increased. Overall, 120 patients (30.0%) reported not operating machines, including cars, in daily life. The mean (SD) onset age of migraine was 24.0 (11.5) years, with migraine without aura as the most common diagnosis (n = 281, 70.3%), followed by chronic migraine (n = 98; 24.5%) and migraine with typical aura (n = 90; 22.5%). Migraine with brainstem aura was reported in 3 patients (0.8%), while hemiplegic migraine was observed in 1 patient (0.3%) (Table 1). At baseline, patients reported an average of 10.2 (SD 7.5) migraine attacks per month. Commonly associated symptoms included dizziness (n = 134, 33.5%) and somnolence (n = 99, 24.8%). The median number of migraine attacks per month during the 3 months preceding the index date was 8 (Table S1). Overall, 43 patients (10.8%) had at least one past medical history, and 189 patients (47.3%) had at least one comorbidity (Tables S2 and S3). Prior to enrollment, triptans were the most commonly used acute migraine treatment (n = 288, 72.0%) (Table 1). During the observation period, half of the patients (n = 204, 51.0%) used triptans concomitantly. Other frequently prescribed drugs included heart rate-lowering agents (n = 134; 33.5%), acetaminophen or NSAIDs (n = 111; 27.8%), and antiepileptics (n = 87; 21.8%) (Table 2).

Lasmiditan Treatment

Among patients in the safety analyses, the most commonly prescribed initial lasmiditan dose was 50 mg (n = 224, 56.0%), followed by 100 mg (n = 171, 42.8%) (Fig. 2). No patients received 200 mg as their starting dose. Of those who started with 50 mg, 49 (21.9%) patients continued the same dose, 26 (11.6%) had their dose increased to 100 mg, and 149 (66.5%) did not receive a second prescription. Among those initially prescribed 100 mg, 60 (35.1%) maintained the same dose, 3 (1.8%) had their dose reduced to 50 mg, and 1 (0.6%) had their dose increased to 200 mg. Most patients (n = 107; 62.6%) did not receive a second prescription (Fig. 2). During the first treated attack with lasmiditan, 64 (16.0%) patients took an additional lasmiditan dose, and 171 (42.8%) received other treatments, with triptans (n = 85, 21.3%) and NSAIDs (n = 45, 11.3%) being the most commonly used (Table S4). Overall, 59 (14.8%) patients discontinued lasmiditan treatment during the observation period, with most discontinuations occurring within the first 8 weeks (Fig. 3a). Of those who discontinued, the most common reasons were AEs (n = 29, 49.2%) and lack of effectiveness (n = 21, 35.6%). Dizziness was the most frequently reported AE leading to discontinuation (n = 12, 20.3%), followed by malaise (n = 3, 5.1%), somnolence (n = 3, 5.1%), and nausea (n = 2, 3.4%) (Fig. 3b).

Safety Analyses

The safety analysis set included 400 patients who received 1759 lasmiditan doses. Lasmiditan-associated AEs, according to physicians’ judgment, were referred to as ADRs. AEs and ADRs occurring from the day of lasmiditan administration to 23:59 hours on the next day were referred to as TEAEs and TEADRs, respectively.

Overall, 194 (48.5%) patients experienced at least one AE, while 192 (48.0%) patients reported at least one ADR. No SAEs were observed. Additionally, 167 (41.8%) patients experienced at least one TEAE, and 165 (41.3%) reported at least one TEADR, with no serious TEAEs reported. A total of 29 (7.3%) patients discontinued lasmiditan treatment due to AEs. No deaths occurred during the study period (Table 3). Among patients with “migraine with brainstem aura” (n = 3), 1 patient experienced a TEAE. In the patient with “hemiplegic migraine” (n = 1), no TEAEs were reported. Among patients with “chronic migraine” (n = 98), TEAEs occurred in 39 (39.8%) patients (Fig. S1).

Out of 1662 administered lasmiditan doses, 272 (16.4%) were associated with at least one TEAE (Table 4). Dizziness was the most frequently reported TEAE, occurring in 139 (8.4%) doses, followed by somnolence (65 [3.9%] doses). The overall incidence of dizziness tended to decrease with subsequent lasmiditan administrations across all dose levels (50, 100, and 200 mg) (Table S5). The median time to onset for dizziness or somnolence based on 1662 total treated attacks could not be estimated because the cumulative incidence proportions of 25%, 50%, or 75% were not reached within the observation period (Figs. S2 and S3, and Table S6). At 0 h post-administration, the cumulative incidence of dizziness was 2.0% (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.4–2.8%) and increased to 8.1% (95% CI 6.9–9.6%) at 12 h; for somnolence, it was 0.4% (95% CI 0.2–0.8%) at 0 h and 3.9% (95% CI 3.1–4.9%) at 12 h (Table S6). Regarding time to resolution, 25% dizziness events resolved by 1 h (95% CI 0.8–1.5 h) and 25% somnolence events by 2.2 h (95% CI 1.5–2.6 h) post-onset. The median time to resolution (50% resolution) was 3 h (95% CI 2.0–3.0 h) for dizziness and 4 h (95% CI 2.6–5.5 h) for somnolence. Furthermore, 75% dizziness events resolved by 6 h (95% CI 4.7–11.0 h) and 75% somnolence events by 9 h (95% CI 5.5–15.0 h) post-onset (Table S6). When calculated based on the number of patients who experienced dizziness and somnolence, the median time to onset was 0.6 h and 1.0 h, respectively; the median time to resolution was 2.2 h and 3.5 h, respectively.

Effectiveness Analyses

The effectiveness analyses set comprised 328 patients, with 1345 migraine attacks treated. Across all treated attacks (lasmiditan administered within 4 h post-headache), among 386 lasmiditan doses administered for moderate-to-severe baseline headache severity, pain freedom (defined as no headache) was achieved in 83 (21.5%) doses at 2 h post-administration. Pain relief (i.e., reduction to mild headache severity) was observed in 208 (53.9%) doses (Fig. 4). In the 50-mg dose group, among 197 initial administrations for moderate-to-severe headaches, 50 (25.4%) doses resulted in pain freedom, and 111 (56.3%) doses provided pain relief at 2 h post-dose. For the 100-mg dose group, 184 initial administrations were analyzed. Of these, 31 (16.8%) doses provided pain freedom, and 95 (51.6%) doses provided pain relief at 2 h (Fig. 4). In the overall analysis of 99 lasmiditan doses administered for first moderate-to-severe migraine attacks (first treated attack), 21 (21.2%) doses resulted in pain freedom, while 50 (50.5%) doses provided pain relief at 2 h post-administration (Fig. S4).

Analysis of 1345 lasmiditan doses, with time-to-improvement data available for 557 (41.4%) doses, indicated the likelihood of achieving meaningful headache improvement (Fig. 5). These included 296 (n = 603, 49.1%), 257 (n = 735; 35.0%), and 4 (n = 7; 57.1%) doses of 50, 100, 200 mg, respectively (Table S7) and were consistent across all dose levels. Meaningful improvement was observed in 5.3% (71 doses) within 0.5 h, whereas the cumulative proportion rose to 20.6% (277 doses) by 2 h, 29.6% (398 doses) by 4 h, and 39.2% (527 doses) within 24 h post-dose (Fig. 5). As a result of limited use of the 200-mg dose, the analysis primarily focused on the overall population and the 50-mg and 100-mg dose groups. Of the 1345 lasmiditan doses included, time-to-headache resolution data were available for 41.7% (561 doses) (Fig. 6) and demonstrated that headache resolution occurred progressively over time. This pattern was generally consistent across all dose levels (50, 100, and 200 mg; as a result of the small sample size (n = 7) in the 200-mg dose group, data are not shown). Specifically, headache resolution was reported in 42 (3.1%) doses within 0.5 h, rising cumulatively to 242 (18.0%) doses within 2 h, 371 (27.6%) doses within 4 h, and 537 (39.9%) doses within 24 h post-administration. In the effectiveness analysis set, six patients took an additional dose within 2 h of the first treated attacks. All six patients initially took 50 mg; among them, one patient took an additional 100-mg dose, while the remaining five took an additional 50-mg dose.

Discussion

This post-marketing observational study provides real-world evidence on the safety and effectiveness of lasmiditan as an acute treatment for migraine in routine clinical practice in Japan. No new safety concerns were identified beyond those described in clinical trials and the product label.

The mean age of patients and the proportion of female patients in the current study (40.5 years; 79.5%) were comparable to those reported in the phase 2 Japanese clinical trial (45.2 years; 83.1%) [17]. This real-world cohort included a diverse and higher-risk population in Japan, often excluded from controlled clinical trials, such as those with chronic migraine and rare migraine subtypes, including migraine with brainstem aura and hemiplegic migraine [13, 14]. Although hypertension and ischemic cardiac disease were less frequently documented in our cohort (7.5% and 1.0%, respectively), the global SAMURAI/SPARTAN trials enrolled far higher cardiovascular risk populations (78–80% with ≥ 1 risk; 40% with ≥ 2 risks), supporting a comparable overall risk profile when accounting for differing definitions and data capture [13, 14]. In this study, concomitant medication refers to any drug administered during the 12-week observation period following the index date (initial lasmiditan prescription). This includes medications used for migraine prevention, acute migraine relief, and treatment of comorbid conditions. As per the study protocol, only CGRP-related monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were categorized as preventive migraine medications. Other medications (e.g., heart rate-lowering agents, antiepileptics) may have been used for prevention, but their purpose (preventive vs. other indications) was not systematically captured. In this study, triptans were commonly used as prior acute medication to lasmiditan or concomitant acute medication, reflecting real-world prescribing practices. Although co-use with lasmiditan was allowed and observed, the influence of triptan use on common AEs, such as dizziness or somnolence, could not be assessed in this study.

Among the 1759 total doses administered, physicians frequently initiated treatment with 50 mg (56.0%), despite the package insert recommending 100 mg as the standard starting dose, likely reflecting a cautious approach. Additionally, the dose was escalated from 50 to 100 mg in 11.6% patients, whereas a reduction from 100 to 50 mg was rare (1.8%). These findings suggest that while 50 mg may be appropriate for some patients, it may be insufficient for others, reinforcing the recommendation of 100 mg as the standard starting dose. Notably, no patients received 200 mg as an initial prescription. Dose escalation to 200 mg was observed in only one patient at the second prescription, primarily as a result of an insufficient response to lower doses. This limited use of the highest approved dose suggests a cautious prescribing approach in routine clinical practice, likely reflecting concerns about dose-dependent CNS-related AEs such as dizziness and somnolence. These findings indicate general adherence to established dosing recommendations, with 200 mg reserved for select cases where lower doses are ineffective. Lasmiditan discontinuations occurred throughout the study, with most happening early in the treatment course, suggesting initial concerns about tolerability or effectiveness. This is consistent with findings from a real-world study in Korean patients, which also points to tolerability or effectiveness concerns as common reasons for discontinuation [8]. Differences in baseline patient characteristics, such as fewer prior triptan use in the present study (72% vs. 95.7% in the Japanese phase 2 trial) and a lower proportion of patients receiving preventive migraine medication (7.0% used CGRP-related mAbs vs. 37.5% used preventive medications, including a few CGRP-related mAbs, in the Japanese phase 2 trial) [17], may have influenced the observed outcomes, potentially reflecting differences in migraine severity or treatment patterns in the real-world setting. Additionally, the possibility that AEs related to concomitant medications were more thoroughly captured in the clinical trial setting cannot be ruled out. In contrast, in this real-world study, safety data were self-reported by patients via an eDiary, which may have introduced information bias and affected the completeness of AE reporting.

Lasmiditan’s safety profile observed in this study was generally consistent with that reported in the Japanese phase 2 trial [17], with no new safety concerns identified that would necessitate additional safety measures. The overall incidence of TEAEs at the first treated attack was notably lower (41.8%) than that observed in the phase 2 study (50.6%, 70.7%, and 80.8% for the 50, 100, and 200 mg doses, respectively) [17]. In global phase 3 trials (SAMURAI and SPARTAN), dizziness was dose-related and generally mild to moderate [9% (50 mg), 13–18% (100 mg), and 16–18% (200 mg)]. Dizziness onset occurred at 30–50 min, with a median duration of 2 h. Somnolence was also common but short-lived [13, 14, 21]. These findings were replicated across multiple attacks in the CENTURION study, where CNS-related TEAEs decreased with repeated use across four attacks and remained of short duration [22]. In our post‑marketing observational cohort, when analyzed per dose, dizziness occurred in 8.4% of doses and somnolence in 3.9%, with the frequency decreasing with subsequent administrations, consistent with the CENTURION study. In contrast, the MONONOFU study reported a higher proportion of patients experiencing dizziness (39.4%) and somnolence (19.3%) [17]. Emerging real‑world data on lasmiditan use in Korea also identify dizziness as the most frequent AE and report higher AE rates than in trials [8], suggesting that clinical context, dose selection (more 50–100-mg dose starts), and reporting practices may influence apparent AE burden as much as ethnicity. Importantly, the tendency for these effects to diminish with repeated use suggests potential adaptation over time. Given the rapid onset of such AEs and their potential to impair alertness, the recommendation to avoid driving or operating machinery for at least 8 h after taking lasmiditan remains clinically relevant and aligns with existing prescribing guidance [20].

The effectiveness analysis set in this real-world post-marketing study comprised 328 patients who collectively treated 1345 migraine attacks. In contrast, the Japanese phase 2 trial included 682 treated attacks, corresponding to the modified intent-to-treat population, as each participant treated only a single attack [19]. When evaluating headache severity, the proportion of patients achieving pain freedom and pain relief at 2 h post-administration of 100 mg dose in this real-world study was lower (16.8% and 51.6%) than that reported in the Japanese phase 2 trial (32.4% and 80.2%) [17] and the SAMURAI/SPARTAN study (28–31% and 59–65%) [21]. The generally lower effectiveness observed in this study, particularly for pain relief, may be due to the inclusion of patients with more severe or refractory migraine, less stringent dosing timing, and use of concomitant acute medications, all of which are common in routine clinical practice but controlled for in clinical trials. Despite these differences, consistent pain-free rates, particularly with the prevalent 100-mg dose use, suggest meaningful therapeutic benefit in real-world use. Additionally, pain-free and pain-relief rates were similar between the 50- and 100-mg doses and remained stable across initial and subsequent attacks, supporting a consistent effectiveness profile over repeated use. In contrast to the Japanese phase 2 clinical trial, where the 200-mg dose of lasmiditan demonstrated efficacy in achieving 2-h headache pain freedom and most TEAEs were mild in severity (84.4%) [17], the 200-mg dose was used infrequently in the current real-world study. Lasmiditan demonstrated a meaningful impact on daily functioning in patients with severe migraine-related impairment. At 2 h post-dose, a notable proportion of doses initially categorized as “severely disturbed” or “couldn’t move at all” demonstrated improvement to “no problem” or “slightly disturbed”. These findings highlight lasmiditan’s potential not only for pain relief but also for restoring functional capacity and improving quality of life in real-world settings. The study demonstrated improved patient satisfaction with lasmiditan, increasing from 43.5% after the first treated attack to 57.6% across all treated attacks, suggesting positive reinforcement with repeated use. This favorable trend indicates that, beyond pain relief, repeated lasmiditan use contributed to improved daily functioning and overall quality of life for patients.

Limitations

The present study outcomes indicate that real-world prescription of lasmiditan largely aligns with the dosage recommendations outlined in the product label. The study’s new-user design and broad enrollment, spanning approximately 400 patients across 53 medical sites nationwide, enhance the generalizability of its findings to Japan’s diverse migraine population, even during the early phase of lasmiditan’s market adoption. However, important limitations inherent to post-marketing observational studies should be considered while interpreting the results. First, as a single-arm, non-randomized study, the results are primarily descriptive and cannot be used to draw causal conclusions regarding treatment effects. As a result of the descriptive nature of this study and the relatively small sample sizes within many subgroups, comparative interpretations were approached cautiously. While some numerical differences across strata were observed, potential confounding factors and random variation cannot be excluded. Potential confounders include (but not limited to) migraine severity, prior treatment history, concomitant acute and preventive medications, comorbidities, and physician dosing decisions. However, these observations remain informative for identifying patterns that may warrant further investigation. Patient-reported information is inherently subject to bias, including potential misclassification bias, if data entry is delayed. Yet, eDiaries are widely accepted in migraine research for capturing real-time patient experiences, and their use likely reduces bias compared to retrospective reporting. Missing data across key variables, including patient demographics, migraine history, and effectiveness measures, further limits the robustness of the analysis. All results were analyzed and presented largely as observed (no imputation), and effectiveness data were only available for a subset of treated attacks, which may introduce selection/reporting bias. Nonetheless, the available data provides meaningful insights into safety trends and treatment experiences, which are valuable for guiding clinical practice and future research. Larger, long-term real-world studies incorporating robust patient retention strategies and comprehensive follow-up may be needed to better define lasmiditan’s impact on optimizing acute migraine management.

Conclusions

This study found no new safety concerns with lasmiditan in patients with migraine in routine clinical practice in Japan. The overall AE profile, characterized predominantly by dizziness and somnolence, was consistent with that reported in previous clinical trial findings in Japan. Although these CNS-related AEs occasionally led to discontinuation, they were transient and non-serious. Real-world data showed a preference for the 50- and 100-mg doses, and effectiveness analyses summarized observed patient-reported outcomes. Overall, these findings provide real-world evidence of lasmiditan use in Japan.
