Non-invasive biomarkers in pediatric MASLD: utility of gamma-glutamyltransferase for steatohepatitis and the FIB-4 index for fibrosis
Authors: Shun Watanabe, Shinji Suzuki, Norito Tsutsumi, Aoi Sukeda, Takashi Yorozu, Shigeo Nishimata, Hisashi Kawashima, Gaku Yamanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is a growing pediatric health concern. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for grading steatohepatitis and staging fibrosis, but its invasive nature necessitates reliable non-invasive alternatives, particularly for children. While adult-derived scoring systems like the FIB-4 index are widely used, their validity in pediatric populations is not well established. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of the FIB-4 index for detecting advanced fibrosis and to assess the performance of other biomarkers, including gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), for differentiating metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) from simple steatosis in children with biopsy-proven MASLD.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective diagnostic accuracy study included 27 children (aged 5–18) with liver biopsy-confirmed MASLD. Histological staging was performed using the FLIP/SAF scoring system, with advanced fibrosis defined as stage ≥ 2. The diagnostic performance of non-invasive markers, including the FIB-4 index, AST to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI), and GGT, was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to determine the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity for predicting advanced fibrosis and identifying MASH.ResultsThe cohort had a mean age of 11.7 years and was predominantly male (96.2%). A high prevalence of advanced fibrosis was observed, with 14 of 27 patients (51.9%) having fibrosis stage ≥ 2. The FIB-4 index demonstrated fair discriminatory ability for detecting advanced fibrosis (AUC = 0.725; 95% CI: 0.530–0.921), with an optimal cut-off of 0.215 yielding a sensitivity of 57.1% and specificity of 84.6%. In contrast, GGT emerged as an excellent marker for differentiating MASH from MASLD (AUC = 0.914; 95% CI: 0.804–0.980). A GGT cut-off value of 20 IU/L provided a sensitivity of 77.3% and a specificity of 100% for identifying MASH.ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that the FIB-4 index may have some utility in identifying advanced fibrosis in pediatric MASLD, although its performance might be limited compared to adult cohorts. Furthermore, GGT shows promise as a simple and non-invasive biomarker for distinguishing MASH from simple steatosis in children. Integrating GGT into future screening protocols could contribute to improved risk stratification, potentially aiding in the identification of high-risk patients who may benefit from earlier, more intensive clinical interventions or prioritized consideration for liver biopsy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06455-1.Keywords: FIB-4 index, Gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), Liver biopsy, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), Non-invasive biomarkers

Background

NAFLD, recently redefined as MASLD, is characterized by hepatic steatosis in association with metabolic dysfunction [1]. Obesity is a major risk factor, and its prevalence in children increased by 1.5-fold between the decades after 2000 and after 2012, becoming a major global health issue [2]. In Japan, the prevalence of childhood obesity rose from 6.72% to 11.83% over the past 40 years, paralleling an increase in pediatric MASLD cases [3, 4]. Liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing MASH; however, abdominal ultrasonography, while effective for detecting steatosis, cannot distinguish MASH from MASLD. Fibrosis is a key prognostic factor for hepatocellular carcinoma, and its progression is associated with poor outcomes [5, 6].

Although no globally adopted, evidence-based guidelines exist for the timing of liver biopsy in pediatric MASLD, criteria have been proposed to identify high-risk patients. For instance, in a study of 347 obese children aged 10 years and older with elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT), patients meeting criteria such as an ALT level > 80 IU/L or a persistent elevation > 2 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) for over six months are considered to be at high risk for MASH [7]. Furthermore, that study reported that an ALT level > 80 IU/L had a sensitivity of 76% and a specificity of 59% for predicting advanced fibrosis (bridging fibrosis or cirrhosis) [8]. Accordingly, liver biopsy is often considered for patients meeting these high-risk criteria to definitively stage fibrosis.

Serum type IV collagen 7 S (4COL7S), Mac-2 binding protein (M2BP), and Mac-2 binding protein glycosylation isomer (M2BPGi) have been reported as useful non-invasive fibrosis markers in adults with MASLD [9]. However, these laboratory assays are costly and not widely available. In contrast, the FIB-4 index—based on age, AST, ALT, and platelet count—offers a simple approach. Sumida et al. reported that, in Japanese adults with suspected MASLD, using a low cut-off of 1.30 and high cut-off of 3.25 for the FIB-4 index allowed 58% of patients to avoid biopsy [10].

Applying non-invasive fibrosis assessment tools to children could reduce the need for invasive biopsy. While promising in adults, the performance of these scores has not been sufficiently validated in children. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of the FIB-4 index and other adult-derived fibrosis scores in a pediatric MASLD cohort, using liver biopsy as the reference standard.

Methods

We conducted a single-center, retrospective diagnostic accuracy study at the Department of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine, Tokyo Medical University Hospital, between 2012 and 2022. Eligible participants were children diagnosed with MASLD or MASH confirmed by liver biopsy. Indications for biopsy included ALT levels > 80 IU/L or increased hepatic echogenicity suggestive of MASLD on ultrasonography. Patients with other liver diseases—such as viral hepatitis or Wilson’s disease—or with incomplete clinical data were excluded.

All liver biopsy specimens were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to clinical and laboratory data. Histological assessment included the Matteoni classification, the NAFLD Activity Score (NAS), and the Fatty Liver Inhibition of Progression/Steatosis-Activity-Fibrosis (FLIP/SAF) scoring system. In the FLIP/SAF fibrosis grading, stages 0–1 were defined as the low-fibrosis group and stages 2–3 as the high-fibrosis group [11–13].

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of established non-invasive fibrosis scores for detecting significant fibrosis in children with MASLD. As a secondary objective, we explored the utility of other individual biochemical markers, including gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), for their potential to differentiate MASH from MASLD.

The following scores were calculated using clinical, demographic, and laboratory data obtained at the time of liver biopsy:

・FIB-4 index = [Age (years) × AST (U/L)]/[Platelet count (×10⁹/L) × √ALT (U/L)]

・APRI = [(AST (U/L)/ULN of AST (U/L))/Platelet count (×10⁹/L)] × 100.

・NAFLD Fibrosis Score (NFS) = − 1.675 + (0.037 × Age in years) + (0.094 × BMI in kg/m²) + (1.13 × [Impaired Fasting Glucose or Diabetes: 1 if yes, 0 if no]) + (0.99 × AST/ALT ratio) − (0.013 × Platelet count in ×10⁹/L) − (0.66 × Albumin in g/dL).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics version 27. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (%). Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the AUC calculated for each fibrosis prediction model.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Medical University (approval number: T2019-0259). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the board because this was a retrospective study using fully anonymized data. A clinical trial number was not applicable for this study.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Percutaneous liver biopsies were performed under intravenous sedation in 33 patients aged 5–18 years; complete datasets were available for 27 patients. No procedural complications were observed. The cohort was predominantly male (26/27; 96.2%), as female patients more often declined invasive examinations.

Pathological Findings

Based on the Matteoni classification, 22 patients (81.5%) were categorized as type 3 or 4 (MASH). Using the NAS system, 6 patients (22.2%) were diagnosed with MASLD, 15 (55.6%) with borderline disease, and 6 (22.2%) with MASH. According to the FLIP/SAF fibrosis score, 13 patients (48.1%) had stage 2 fibrosis and 1 (3.7%) had stage 3 fibrosis, indicating that over half of the cohort exhibited advanced fibrosis [14, 15]. No significant group differences were noted in steatosis or activity scores. Among the 22 patients with MASH, one was classified as having co-existing type 1, 11 with type 2, and 10 with overlap type, based on histopathological findings. [see Additional file 1]

Clinical and Laboratory Data

The mean patient age was 11.7 ± 2.9 years. The median AST was 31.0 IU/L (IQR: 20.0–67.0), median ALT 48.0 IU/L (IQR: 25.0–156.0), and GGT 25.0 IU/L, within the normal range. The median homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was 5.4 (normal < 2.5). No significant differences in age, height, weight, BMI, or laboratory parameters were observed between the low- and high-fibrosis groups defined by FLIP/SAF. [see Additional file 2]

Fibrosis Prediction Models

Fibrosis Prediction Models

The FIB-4 index was significantly higher in the high-fibrosis group compared to the low-fibrosis group (p = 0.048), although no cases exceeded the adult low cut-off value of 1.3. No significant differences were found for the AAR, APRI, or NFS. The ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.725 (95% CI: 0.530–0.921) for the FIB-4 index, with 57.1% sensitivity and 84.6% specificity at a cut-off of 0.215. The AUCs for NFS, AAR, and APRI were 0.516 [95% CI 0.290–0.743], 0.429 [95% CI 0.203–0.655], and 0.665 [95% CI 0.458–0.871], respectively (Fig. 1).

Differentiation of MASH from MASLD

When comparing MASH and MASLD groups (Matteoni classification), significant differences were observed for FIB-4 and GGT. The FIB-4 index had an AUC of 0.718 (95% CI: 0.521–0.915), while GGT showed excellent discriminative power, with an AUC of 0.914 (95% CI: 0.804–0.980) (Fig. 2). A GGT cut-off of 20 IU/L yielded 77.3% sensitivity and 100% specificity for MASH. The overall mean GGT was 36.3 IU/L, while the mean for patients with type 1 MASH was 53.3 IU/L.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that while the FIB-4 index shows some utility in assessing liver fibrosis in pediatric MASLD, it may underestimate the degree of fibrosis compared to its established performance in adults [10]. This limitation likely reflects age-related physiological differences; for instance, platelet counts in children often remain within the normal range despite the presence of significant fibrosis [16]. Furthermore, because the FIB-4 index calculation incorporates age, adults inherently yield significantly higher scores than children even with identical laboratory values. This age-related effect was not adjusted for in our analysis. The low FIB-4 cut-off value of 0.215 identified in our study underscores the necessity of establishing pediatric-specific cut-offs for its clinical application.

In our cohort, 13 children (48.1%) had stage 2 fibrosis and one child (3.7%) had stage 3 fibrosis, a prevalence that exceeds the 22.9% for stage 3 and 0% for stage 4 reported by Mansoor et al. [17]. When combined with histological evidence of MASH, these cases accounted for 81.5% of our study population. This indicates that advanced fibrosis can develop in pediatric patients even when biochemical abnormalities are mild. Given the risk of fibrosis progression in MASH, there is a critical need for reliable diagnostic tools capable of detecting early-stage fibrosis. Such tools should ideally incorporate additional clinical or biochemical markers beyond conventional fibrosis indicators, thereby enabling earlier intervention to prevent complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

Because of the limited sensitivity of FIB-4 for detecting MASH with mild fibrosis, we evaluated other factors that might distinguish MASH from MASLD. GGT (AUC 0.914, 95% CI: 0.804–0.980) emerged as a promising non-invasive marker; in our study, GGT levels < 20 IU/L were observed exclusively in the MASLD group. However, the GGT cut-off value of 20 IU/L for differentiating MASH presents an interpretative challenge, as it falls within the normal range for children. This suggests that even GGT levels near the upper limit of the normal range may indicate underlying hepatocellular inflammation (steatohepatitis) in children with MASLD. This finding is not intended to propose a new diagnostic threshold for MASH. Rather, it indicates that in children with suspected MASLD, GGT may serve as a crucial warning sign for risk stratification of MASH, potentially warranting closer monitoring or further evaluation.

MASH is histologically classified into type 1, characterized by fatty accumulation and ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, and type 2, characterized by portal vein inflammation and periportal fibrosis. Since type 2 is more common in children (51%) and type 1 is less common (17%), GGT is expected to be elevated in the early stages of pediatric MASH as a result of localized inflammatory changes in the periportal area [18, 19]. In our cohort, type 2 MASH was also predominant: of the 22 patients with MASH, 11 (50%) were classified as type 2 and 10 (45.5%) as overlap type. In addition, GGT maintains intracellular homeostasis against oxidative stress by catalyzing the breakdown of glutathione and promoting the formation of cysteine, which exerts antioxidant effects [7, 20]. In pediatric MASLD, elevated GGT may therefore represent activation of protective mechanisms against oxidative stress that, over time, are associated with fibrogenesis, suggesting that timely therapeutic intervention could be beneficial [21].

The mean GGT in the type 2 group (mean GGT 53.3 IU/L) showed an upward trend compared to the overall MASH cohort (mean GGT 36.3 IU/L). Our finding that GGT is associated with MASH is consistent with previous research [22]. For example, a study by Xanthakos et al. reported a link between GGT and the progression of MASH and fibrosis, with a mean GGT of 47.2 U/L in their cohort [23]. Similarly, Sood et al. found that GGT levels were significantly higher in children with liver stiffness measurements (LSM) > 7 kPa, further linking elevated GGT to liver fibrosis [24]. A key contribution of our study is its focus on a Japanese cohort, as much of the existing data is derived from large, predominantly Hispanic American cohorts [25]. Furthermore, our cohort exhibited a higher prevalence of advanced disease. Whereas Sood et al. reported advanced fibrosis (stage ≥ 2) in only 14% of their biopsied patients, 14 of our 27 patients (51.9%) had advanced fibrosis [24]. This suggests our findings may be particularly reflective of the pathophysiology in patients with more advanced fibrotic MASH. Although our study has a small sample size, demonstrating GGT’s utility in this Japanese population supports its potential as a universally applicable marker and encourages further research across diverse ethnic backgrounds.

In addition to serum biomarkers, non-invasive imaging techniques such as Transient Elastography (TE) and MR Elastography (MRE) are also useful for monitoring pediatric MASLD [26]. While these modalities assess liver stiffness and serve as surrogate markers for fibrosis, their limited availability and cost can be barriers to widespread use. Our study focused on widely available and low-cost serum biomarkers, which could serve as an initial screening tool to identify patients who may require more advanced imaging assessments.

This study has several limitations. The small sample size may have reduced the statistical power, and validation in larger, multi-center, and diverse pediatric cohorts is necessary before these findings can be applied to clinical practice. The predominance of male participants reflects both the higher prevalence of MASH in boys and the tendency for female patients to decline liver biopsy [27, 28]. Most participants were ≥ 10 years old, as liver biopsies in younger children are generally avoided, and therefore caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to females or younger children. In addition, our cohort lacked cases of stage 4 fibrosis, which may have influenced the analysis of FIB-4 performance.

Conclusion

In pediatric MASLD/MASH, the FIB-4 index may have some utility for detecting advanced fibrosis; however, because of the risk of underestimating disease severity compared to adults, the validation of pediatric-specific cut-off values is essential. GGT has emerged as a promising non-invasive marker that could enhance risk stratification and guide early interventions to prevent disease progression. Further validation through larger prospective studies is required to establish the clinical utility of these markers.
