OS-R1: Agentic Operating System Kernel Tuning with Reinforcement Learning
Hongyu Lin, Yuchen Li, Haoran Luo, Kaichun Yao, Libo Zhang, Mingjie Xing, Yanjun Wu

Abstract
Linux kernel tuning is essential for optimizing operating system (OS) performance. However, existing methods often face challenges in terms of efficiency, scalability, and generalization. This paper introduces OS-R1 , an agentic Linux kernel tuning framework powered by rule-based reinforcement learning (RL). By abstracting the kernel configuration space as an RL environment, OS-R1 facilitates efficient exploration by large language models (LLMs) and ensures accurate configuration modifications. Additionally, custom reward functions are designed to enhance reasoning standardization, configuration modification accuracy, and system performance awareness of the LLMs. Furthermore, we propose a two-phase training process that accelerates convergence and minimizes retraining across diverse tuning scenarios. Experimental results show that OS-R1 significantly outperforms existing baseline methods, achieving up to 5.6% performance improvement over heuristic tuning and maintaining high data efficiency. Notably, OS-R1 is adaptable across various real-world applications, demonstrating its potential for practical deployment in diverse environments. Our dataset and code are publicly available at https://github.com/LHY-24/OS-R1 .

Introduction

The Linux kernel, as the core of modern operating systems (OSs)
(
Torvalds 1991
)
, plays a pivotal role in determining system performance. As computing workloads become more diverse and complex,
kernel tuning
(illustrated in Figure
1
), which refers to the process of systematically adjusting kernel configurations to optimize system performance for specific workloads, has become an essential task. This process demands a deep understanding of both the kernel’s internal mechanisms and the demands of the target workload.

Despite extensive research in kernel tuning, it remains a challenging and labor-intensive task. As shown in Figure
2
,
Heuristic tuning
methods rely on expert knowledge to manually adjust kernel configurations
(
Braswell, Ciliendo, and Kunimasa 2008
;
Gembala et al. 2018
)
. However, these approaches are time-consuming, error-prone, and challenging to scale, particularly for dynamic workloads.
Machine learning-based
approaches have shown potential in automating kernel tuning
(
Lee, Jung, and Jo 2022
;
Sokolov, Bavshin, and Arlazarov 2023
)
, yet these methods often struggle with scalability, data requirements, and generalizability across diverse hardware and workloads. Additionally,
LLM-assisted tuning
frameworks, which leverage large language models (LLMs) to interpret workload demands and suggest kernel configurations
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
, still encounter significant limitations in effectively navigating the vast configuration space and ensuring the validity of generated configurations.

Comparison of different kernel tuning methods, including heuristic-based tuning through variable control, machine learning-based tuning using neural networks, LLM-assisted tuning with tree-based search, and our proposed OS-R1 method, which incorporates RL and agent-based optimization.

Recent advancements in
reinforcement learning
(RL) have significantly enhanced the capabilities of LLMs, enabling them to decompose tasks, invoke tools, and refine solutions
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
. However, applying RL to Linux kernel tuning presents several unique challenges:
(1) Complex Environment Exploration
: The kernel configuration space includes over 18,000 configurations with complex dependencies, requiring efficient exploration mechanisms.
(2) Reasonable Reward Design
: Designing appropriate feedback signals is critical to balance exploration and exploitation in kernel tuning.
(3) Cross-scenario Generalization
: The model must generalize across diverse workloads with minimal retraining, ensuring robustness and adaptability when applied to new tuning scenarios.

To address these challenges, we present
OS-R1
, an agentic Linux kernel tuning framework powered by rule-based RL. The innovative design of OS-R1 introduces several novel strategies that enhance the overall tuning process.
First, we abstract the kernel configuration space as a RL environment
to facilitate multi-turn autonomous exploration.
Furthermore, we design several rule-based reward functions
to enhance the reasoning standardization, configuration modification accuracy, and system performance awareness of the agent.
Additionally, we propose a two-phase, data-efficient training pipeline
leveraging our high-quality kernel tuning dataset to accelerate convergence. Through these innovations, the agent learns the underlying mechanism of the kernel space and achieves effective, reliable, and automated kernel tuning.

OS-R1 undergoes extensive experimental validation across diverse representative tuning targets. Experimental results demonstrate that OS-R1 significantly outperforms existing tuning methods in terms of performance improvements, while maintaining high data efficiency with limited training. Notably, OS-R1 exhibits strong scalability across diverse real-world applications, highlighting its potential for practical deployment. By effectively bridging the gap between abstract tuning targets and concrete kernel configurations, OS-R1 lays the foundation for the development of next-generation RL-based OS optimization agents, paving the way for more efficient and adaptable operating systems.

Related Work

2.1
Linux Kernel Tuning

Linux kernel tuning has been explored through three main approaches.
Heuristic Tuning
relies on expert experience for manual configuration adjustments
(
Braswell, Ciliendo, and Kunimasa 2008
;
Bovet and Cesati 2006
;
Yi and Connan 2014
;
Franz et al. 2020
)
. Although effective for specific tasks, it is labor-intensive, error-prone, and not scalable, especially for dynamic workloads.
Machine Learning-Based Tuning
leverages data-driven methods to automate kernel tuning
(
Lee, Jung, and Jo 2022
;
Sokolov, Bavshin, and Arlazarov 2023
;
Fingler et al. 2023
)
, but is limited by the need for hand-crafted features and extensive labeled datasets, restricting adaptability to new or unseen workloads.
LLM-Assisted Tuning
aims to automate kernel tuning by utilizing LLMs to understand workload requirements
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
, but faces challenges in efficiently navigating the configuration space and avoiding invalid configurations due to model hallucinations.
OS-R1 addresses these limitations by introducing a rule-based RL framework, enabling LLMs to autonomously optimize kernel configurations with minimal reliance on labeled datasets, offering a more scalable and efficient solution.

Dataset Construction

The application of RL models in kernel tuning requires the development of a high-quality dataset to ensure the model’s effectiveness and generalizability across diverse workloads. The subsequent section provides a detailed description of the dataset construction pipeline, as illustrated in Figure
3
.

Tuning Target Determination.

The primary objective of kernel tuning is to enhance the performance of core kernel modules, such as CPU, memory, and file system, in order to meet the demands of diverse workloads. We have identified a set of typical tuning tasks and corresponding performance metrics for these core modules, which serve as the foundation for dataset construction. A detailed list of these tasks and metrics is provided in the appendix.

Raw Dataset Collection.

To build a comprehensive dataset, we collect baseline kernel config files from several sources:
(1) Official Kernel Configs,
extracted from official kernel documentation and various Linux distributions;
(2) Historical Tuning Data,
encompassing performance metrics such as CPU utilization, I/O throughput, and memory consumption, collected using profiling tools like
perf
;
(3) Expert Tuning Knowledge,
incorporating manual tuning logs and optimization strategies, providing essential insights for model learning;
(4) Real-world Workloads Benchmarking,
simulating realistic load scenarios with benchmarking tests on a diverse set of applications.

Dataset Preprocessing.

Once the data is collected, it is preprocessed to create a structured dataset suitable for RL model training. The Linux kernel config file contains over 18,000 configurations, posing a significant challenge for RL models due to the impracticality of processing all configurations simultaneously. To address this complexity, we employ a hierarchical, dependency-based batching strategy that aggregates functionally related configurations into smaller, manageable configuration groups. Each group is treated as a distinct training sample, structured as follows:

Automatic Configuration Generation.

For each tuning target, we invoke the LLMs using specialized prompts to: (1) Enumerate only the kernel configurations relevant to the tuning task; (2) Determine the assignments for these configurations step-by-step, ensuring that the output follows a structured schema. Since rules are enforced at prompt time, the collected data are format-correct by construction and immediately usable for the following training process.

Resulting Dataset.

The dataset consists of over 3,000 kernel configuration samples, spanning various tuning targets. Importantly, all configurations are verified to compile and boot successfully. This carefully structured dataset supports data-efficient RL, enabling models to learn kernel tuning strategies that generalize well across different scenarios. It facilitates rapid convergence, even with limited training data, ensuring OS-R1’s effective deployment in diverse real-world environments with minimal retraining.

4
OS-R1

In this section, we provide a detailed design of OS-R1. Figure
4
illustrates the overall training pipeline of OS-R1.

4.1
Problem Formulation

Given the intricate hierarchical dependencies among kernel configurations and their impact on the kernel’s state, we model the kernel tuning task as a dynamic programming problem under the Markov Decision Process (MDP)
(
Puterman 1994
)
paradigm:

\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})\right]

(1)

where each components at time
t
are defined as follows:

•

Kernel State

\mathbf{s_{t}}
includes the current kernel configuration, workload status, and system performance metrics.

•

Action

\mathbf{a_{t}}
corresponds to a specific configuration modification (e.g., the assignment of a Value configuration).

•

Reward Function

\mathbf{R(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})}
evaluates the performance and validity of the new configuration after
a_{t}
.

•

Discount Factor

\gamma
controls the trade-off between immediate and long-term rewards.

•

Policy

\pi_{\theta}
determines the action selection base on
\mathbf{s_{t}}
.

•

Trajectory

\tau
is a sequence of state-action pairs taken by the agent under the policy
\pi_{\theta}
.

4.2
RL in Kernel Tuning

The Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
training process for OS-R1, specifically designed for efficient kernel tuning, follows the steps below:

Policy Initialization and Action Grouping.

The process starts with a randomly initialized policy
\pi_{\theta_{0}}
to explore the kernel configuration space. At each time step
t
, the policy selects an action from the configuration distribution:

a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{i}|s_{t}),\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,G\}

(2)

For each state
s_{t}
, a group of
G
actions
\{a_{t}^{1},a_{t}^{2},\dots,a_{t}^{G}\}
is sampled, where each action represents a potential configuration modification. A multi-turn interaction trajectory
\tau
is then generated by executing
\pi_{\theta}
in the kernel space:

\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})\}_{t=1}^{T}

(3)

Reward Computation and Normalization.

The reward for each action
a_{t}^{i}
in the group is
r_{i}=R(s_{t},a_{t}^{i})
, reflecting its impact on the kernel. To ensure stability, rewards are normalized by calculating the mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
as follows:

\mu=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}r_{i},\quad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(r_{i}-\mu)^{2}}

(4)

This normalization ensures that the rewards are on a comparable scale, facilitating stable policy updates during training.

Advantage Computation and Policy Update.

The advantage
A_{i}
for each action
a_{t}^{i}
is normalized as:

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\mu}{\sigma}

(5)

The policy parameters
\theta
are then optimized using the objective function:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s_{t},a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\min\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{i}|s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}^{i}|s_{t})},1+\epsilon\right)A_{i}\right]

(6)

where
\epsilon
is a clipping parameter to ensure stable updates. This process continues by collecting new trajectories and updating the policy until convergence.

4.3
Reward Design

The reward function
R
in OS‑R1 combines format standardization, answer validity, and performance improvement. Specifically, we propose an outcome-based reward function consisted of the following three components.

Format Reward (
R_{format}
)

: To promote structured decision-making, the model is encouraged to follow a predefined response format: (1) reasoning within
<think>
and
</think>
tags, (2) tool invocations within
<tool_call>
and
</tool_call>
tags, and (3) the final answer within
<answer>
and
</answer>
tags. The format reward is defined as:

R_{format}=\begin{cases}1&\textit{if the format matches constraints}\\
0&\textit{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

A key innovation of OS-R1 is the integration of tool-augmented reasoning, where the agent can query a domain-specific knowledge base (KB) for configuration auxiliary information. This architecture ensures format compliance and dependency satisfaction, thus reducing hallucinations.

Answer Reward (
R_{answer}
)

: Following format validation, the correctness of configuration modifications is assessed based on the configuration type:

•

Bool
: reward for a valid ”yes” or ”no” assignment.

•

Menu
: reward for selecting at least one valid option from the candidate set without selecting any invalid options.

•

Choice
: reward for selecting exactly one valid option from the candidate set.

•

Value
: reward for assigning a value within the domain.

A score of 1 point is awarded for valid modifications and 0 otherwise, ensuring that configuration are both valid and correctly modified.

Performance Reward (
R_{perf}
)

: To measure performance improvement after tuning, we utilize LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
to evaluate kernel configurations.
R_{perf}
captures both performance improvement and configuration modification complexity:

R_{\text{perf}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{P_{\text{new},i}-P_{\text{base},i}}{P_{\text{base},i}}\right)\cdot\left(1+\lambda_{i}\cdot\frac{C_{\text{config},i}}{C_{\text{max},i}}\right)

(8)

where
N
is the number of configuration modifications,
P_{\text{new},i}
and
P_{\text{base},i}
are the new and baseline scores for modification
i
,
\lambda_{i}
,
C_{\text{config},i}
and
C_{\text{max},i}
are related with modification complexities. This approach automates performance evaluation, reducing time, effort and the need for manual kernel testing, while accelerating the training process.

4.4
Training Process

To align with real-world deployment constraints, OS-R1 follows a two-phase training process, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

Warm-up Phase.

In this phase, the model is trained on configuration group samples to enhance configuration-level understanding using
R_{answer}
and
R_{format}
. Specifically, the model learns to: (1) follow a standardized reasoning process to facilitate structured thinking; (2) invoke tools to support knowledge-assisted decision-making; (3) identify different configuration types to perform appropriate modifications. The warm-up training loop is formalized as:

R_{\text{warm-up}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\alpha_{i}\cdot R_{\text{answer},i}+\beta_{i}\cdot R_{\text{format},i}\right)

(9)

where
R_{\text{answer},i}
and
R_{\text{format},i}
represent the rewards for configuration
i
. The tunable parameters
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
enable dynamic adjustment of the agent’s learning priorities.

Exploration Phase.

In the second phase,
R_{perf}
is introduced to enhance the model’s performance awareness. The model explores the kernel space, performs various configuration modifications, gathers feedback from the LLM, and refines its policy using a GRPO-based approach. The exploration phase is formalized as:

\Delta\theta=\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{s_{t},a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}R_{\text{perf},t}\right]

(10)

where
\theta
represents the policy parameters. This formulation updates the agent’s policy by maximizing expected rewards, aligning decision-making with real-world performance, and minimizing invalid configurations.

5
Experiment

We evaluate the effectiveness, efficiency, and generalizability of OS-R1 through a series of empirical studies aimed at addressing the following research questions (RQs): RQ1: How does the performance of OS-R1 compare with existing baselines? RQ2: What is the impact of each reward component in OS-R1? RQ3: How does OS-R1 perform in real-world applications scenarios? RQ4-5: What are the training efficiency and comprehensive quality of OS-R1?

5.1
Experimental Setup

Models.

We utilize Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
as the base models.

Baselines.

We compare OS-R1 with: (1)
Heuristic Tuning
: expert-tuned kernel configurations. (2)
Vanilla LLMs
: few-shot prompting with chain-of-thought using models like GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, and Qwen2.5. (3)
LLM-Assisted Tuning
: A state machine-based kernel tuning framework, AutoOS
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
. Notably, we exclude other ML-based methods due to non-public datasets.

Benchmark.

UnixBench
(
Byte UnixBench Developers 1983
)
is used to evaluate kernel performance, executing sub-tests such as integer operation, context switching, and file copy, with results aggregated into a comprehensive system performance score.

Implementation.

All tuning methods are under identical runtime conditions, with independent runs per tuning target. Experiments use GPT-4o-mini for knowledge base construction and LLM-as-a-Judge. LLM-assisted tuning methods are executed on 4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs (80GB).

5.2
Main Results (RQ1)

From the experimental results shown in Table
1
, we observe the following key findings:

OS-R1 can effectively enhance the model’s tuning capabilities.

Both OS-R1-3B and OS-R1-7B demonstrate significant performance improvements at both the kernel module level and the system level compared to Qwen-3B and Qwen-7B, validating the effectiveness of our approach.

RL unlocks the potential of kernel tuning.

OS-R1-7B achieves the best performance in overall system performance. DeepSeek-R1, also trained with RL, demonstrates the second-best performance. Notably, OS-R1-3B yields results comparable to large models like GPT-4o, underscoring the potential of RL for kernel tuning.

The dataset and model parameters are of vital importance.

The suboptimal performance of OS-R1-7B on certain modules (e.g., Memory, Pipe, Shell) is due to limited data in the training set. Additionally, larger models, such as DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-4o, exhibit stronger reasoning capabilities, which are beneficial for establishing associations between tuning targets and kernel configurations.

6
Conclusion

We introduced OS-R1, an RL-based framework for automated Linux kernel tuning that integrates rule-based rewards and tool-augmented reasoning. OS-R1 utilizes a two-phase training process to accelerate convergence. Experimental results show that OS-R1 significantly outperforms existing methods, achieving notable performance improvements while maintaining high data-efficiency. Additionally, OS-R1 demonstrates strong generalization across real-world applications, making it a scalable and adaptable solution for kernel tuning. By combining advanced RL techniques with LLMs, OS-R1 paves the way for more efficient, flexible, and automated OS optimizations in the future.

Appendix C

UnixBench Benchmark

Overview.

UnixBench is a widely-used benchmark suite designed to measure the performance of Unix-based systems, including Linux. It evaluates the system’s performance across a variety of workloads, including integer and floating point calculations, context switching, and file system operations. The results are then aggregated into a single score that reflects the system’s overall performance. UnixBench is designed to be a comprehensive and consistent tool for comparing the performance of different systems, configurations, or hardware.

Test Suites.

UnixBench includes several test suites, each measuring different aspects of system performance. The key components of the benchmark are as follows:

•

Dhrystone Benchmark
: This test evaluates the system’s integer performance, specifically its ability to perform a series of basic arithmetic and logical operations. It measures the system’s ability to handle computational tasks.

•

Whetstone Benchmark
: This test evaluates floating point performance by running a series of complex mathematical operations, including trigonometric and exponential functions. It assesses the system’s efficiency in handling high precision floating point operations.

•

File Copying Benchmark
: This test measures the system’s file input/output (I/O) performance, simulating file copying operations, which are critical for system tasks that require disk access.

•

Context Switching Benchmark
: This test evaluates the efficiency of the system’s process scheduling and context switching capabilities. It measures how well the system can handle multiple processes that require switching between different tasks.

•

Pipe Throughput Benchmark
: This test evaluates the system’s ability to efficiently transfer data between processes using pipes. It simulates real-world usage where processes need to communicate and share data efficiently.

•

System Call Performance Benchmark
: This test measures the performance of system calls by simulating various system-level operations like file system access, memory allocation, and process management. It gauges how quickly the system can handle these fundamental tasks.

•

Memory and Cache Performance Benchmark
: These tests focus on measuring the speed and efficiency of the system’s memory and cache subsystems. It evaluates how effectively the system can manage memory and execute memory-intensive tasks.

Scoring and Results.

UnixBench produces scores for each of the tests mentioned above. These individual scores are then aggregated into a final performance score that reflects the overall system performance. The final score is normalized, making it easier to compare systems with different hardware and configurations.

\text{UnixBench Score}=\frac{\text{Total Test Score}}{\text{Reference Score}}\times 100

The higher the score, the better the system performs. This benchmark is often used to compare the impact of different kernel configurations, hardware upgrades, or tuning methods, making it a reliable tool for evaluating the efficacy of system optimization techniques like OS-R1.

The effectiveness of the OS-R1 kernel tuning framework is evaluated across various real-world applications to assess its generalization capability and performance improvements in different environments. The key applications used in the evaluation are as follows:

•

Nginx (HTTP Proxy)
: Nginx is an open-source web server and reverse proxy server, widely used for serving static content and load balancing in high-traffic environments. It is known for its high performance and low resource consumption. In OS-R1 evaluation, Nginx is used to test how well OS-R1 optimizes the kernel for web-serving tasks and network-heavy applications.

•

Redis (Dictionary Server)
: Redis is an in-memory key-value store used for caching, messaging, and real-time data analytics. Redis is highly sensitive to system performance, particularly memory bandwidth and I/O performance. Evaluating Redis on OS-R1 tests how well the kernel tuning framework can optimize for memory-intensive operations and fast I/O throughput.

•

PostgreSQL (Database)
: PostgreSQL is a powerful, open-source relational database system known for its robustness and extensibility. It is commonly used for data-driven applications and complex queries. In OS-R1 testing, PostgreSQL is used to evaluate kernel tuning in database environments, which require efficient memory management, I/O operations, and system call handling.

Comprehensive Analysis: Metrics and Calculation Methods

In the comprehensive analysis section, various metrics are used to evaluate the performance of OS-R1 in comparison to other kernel tuning approaches. These metrics measure the system’s efficiency, scalability, and generalization across different workloads. The following section describes the key metrics, their sources, and how they are calculated.

Performance Efficiency.

This metric evaluates the overall effectiveness of the kernel tuning method in optimizing system performance across various kernel modules (CPU, memory, file I/O, etc.). Performance efficiency is typically calculated as:

\text{Performance Efficiency}=\frac{\text{Performance Improvement}}{\text{System Resource Utilization}}

Where
Performance Improvement
is the difference in performance score (e.g., throughput, latency) before and after kernel tuning and
System Resource Utilization
refers to the CPU and memory usage required to achieve the performance improvement.

Adaptation Speed.

Adaptation speed measures how quickly the kernel tuning method can achieve optimal performance with respect to a new workload or system configuration. It is computed as the time taken for the system to reach a specified performance threshold after a tuning action is applied. This can be expressed as:

\text{Adaptation Speed}=\frac{\text{Time to Reach Target Performance}}{\text{Number of Iterations}}

Where
Time to Reach Target Performance
is the time required for the system to reach a defined performance threshold (e.g., 95% of the maximum performance achieved by the tuning) and
Number of Iterations
refers to the number of training steps or optimization iterations required to reach that target.

Scalability.

Scalability measures how well the kernel tuning framework can adapt to different system configurations or workloads. It is typically evaluated by comparing the performance across various system configurations (e.g., different numbers of CPU cores, memory sizes, or system types). A common metric for scalability is the scaling factor, calculated as:

\text{Scaling Factor}=\frac{\text{Performance on Larger Workload}}{\text{Performance on Smaller Workload}}

Where
Performance on Larger Workload
refers to the performance on a system with increased resources (e.g., more CPU cores, memory) and
Performance on Smaller Workload
refers to the performance on a system with fewer resources.

Configuration Accuracy.

Configuration accuracy measures how well the kernel tuning method can select valid and optimal configurations. This is calculated as the percentage of valid configurations out of all proposed configurations, using the formula:

\text{Configuration Accuracy}=\frac{\text{Valid Configurations}}{\text{Total Proposed Configurations}}

Where
Number of Valid Configurations
is the count of configurations that do not cause errors or system instability and
Total Number of Proposed Configurations
is the total number of configurations suggested by the tuning framework.

Learning Efficiency.

Learning efficiency evaluates how quickly the system can converge to an optimal kernel configuration with minimal training data. It is often measured as:

\text{Learning Efficiency}=\frac{\text{Performance Gain}}{\text{Training Data Usage}}

Where
Performance Gain
is the improvement in system performance after training and
Training Data Usage
refers to the amount of training data (e.g., number of kernel configuration samples) used to achieve the performance gain.

Resource Utilization.

Resource utilization measures how effectively the kernel tuning framework uses available system resources (e.g., CPU, memory, and I/O). This is calculated as:

\text{Resource Utilization}=\frac{\text{Total System Resources Used}}{\text{Maximum Available Resources}}

Where
Total System Resources Used
refers to the CPU, memory, and I/O resources consumed during the tuning process and
Maximum Available Resources
refers to the total available resources on the system.

Limitation

We chose to optimize the Linux kernel because it is open source and has a mature configuration framework that is easy to operate. Other kernels, such as Windows and MacOS, are closed source, and we cannot obtain their kernel source code and configuration items.

Future Work

Despite the promising results and contributions of OS-R1, there are several avenues for future research and improvement in the field of kernel tuning:

•

Cross-Architecture Tuning
: OS-R1’s current framework is primarily designed for specific architectures. Future work could extend its applicability to multiple hardware architectures, such as ARM and RISC-V, ensuring that OS-R1 can generalize kernel tuning to various system types and configurations .

•

Real-Time Tuning and Adaptation
: While OS-R1 shows strong generalization across static workloads, further work is needed to enable real-time kernel tuning that can adapt to dynamic workloads without requiring retraining or excessive computational overhead . This would be particularly useful in cloud or edge computing environments where workloads are highly variable.

•

Improved Exploration Techniques
: Although OS-R1 effectively explores the kernel configuration space, further research into more sophisticated exploration strategies (e.g., meta-learning, multi-agent systems) could improve the framework’s efficiency in discovering optimal configurations faster .

•

Deeper Integration with Hardware Performance Counters
: OS-R1 could benefit from deeper integration with hardware performance counters and other low-level system diagnostics tools. This would allow for more granular performance feedback, enhancing the model’s understanding of kernel configurations in relation to hardware-level performance .

•

Multi-Objective Optimization
: Current kernel tuning primarily optimizes for a single performance metric. Future work could incorporate multi-objective optimization, where OS-R1 can simultaneously optimize for multiple conflicting objectives (e.g., throughput vs. latency, energy consumption vs. speed) .

•

Generalization Across Kernel Versions
: OS-R1’s training process can be expanded to ensure better generalization across different kernel versions. As new Linux kernel versions are released, ensuring that OS-R1 can adapt to changes in configuration parameters would increase its robustness and long-term applicability .

•

Human-in-the-Loop Systems
: OS-R1 can be augmented with human-in-the-loop mechanisms, where domain experts can intervene and refine the model’s decisions when needed. This would make OS-R1 more transparent and adaptable to specific user needs .

•

Scalability in Large-Scale Systems
: Future research could focus on scaling OS-R1 for large-scale systems such as cloud clusters and supercomputers. Ensuring that OS-R1 can efficiently tune kernels across thousands of nodes while maintaining high performance would be a significant challenge and a promising research direction .
