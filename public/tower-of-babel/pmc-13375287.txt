Yellow fever in older adults: an overlooked high-risk population in South American outbreaks
Authors: Alfonso J Rodriguez-Morales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYellow fever (YF) remains a re-emerging public health threat in South America, with recent outbreaks reporting high case-fatality rates. Although transmission has predominantly affected rural and sylvatic populations, mounting evidence indicates that older adults experience disproportionately severe outcomes and mortality. This commentary highlights the elderly as an overlooked high-risk group in the current epidemiological landscape. Age-related immunosenescence, comorbidities, endothelial dysfunction, and reduced hepatic reserve likely amplify the pathophysiological impact of YF virus infection. At the same time, concerns about vaccine-associated adverse events in individuals ≥60 years complicate risk–benefit decision-making, contributing to under-vaccination in this vulnerable population. Delayed diagnosis and shifting eco-epidemiological patterns further exacerbate risk. As South America undergoes demographic aging alongside environmental change, targeted, age-specific strategies, including prioritized vaccination, tailored risk communication, and enhanced surveillance for older adults, are urgently needed to reduce preventable fatalities.

Sustainability Statement

This commentary aligns with UN SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by highlighting preventable mortality among older adults during yellow fever outbreaks in South America. As demographic aging intersects with environmental change, urban expansion, and shifting vector ecology, integrating age-specific risk assessment into vaccination strategies and surveillance systems is essential for sustainable outbreak preparedness. Protecting vulnerable elderly populations strengthens health system resilience, reduces inequities in infectious disease outcomes, and supports long-term public health sustainability in endemic regions facing recurrent arboviral threats.

Introduction: a silent demographic at risk

Yellow fever (YF) has re-emerged as a major public health concern in South America over the past decade, with recurrent outbreaks affecting Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, Colombia, and Venezuela (Ortiz-Martínez et al. 2017). Much of the epidemiological focus has centered on unvaccinated rural populations, sylvatic transmission cycles, and international travelers. However, a critical and underexamined demographic has emerged: older adults. Up to 27 April 2026, 465 cases with 200 deaths (43.01%) have been reported in South America, based on aggregated data from the Pan American Health Organization regional surveillance platform and national public health reporting systems (https://shiny.paho-phe.org/yellowfever/). These observations are derived from routinely reported surveillance data, which, while subject to reporting delays and heterogeneity across countries, provide consistent epidemiological signals across multiple outbreak settings.

The elderly have consistently shown higher case-fatality rates during recent outbreaks. Yet public health messaging, vaccination strategies, and risk assessments have largely framed YF as a disease of younger, mobile, or occupationally exposed populations (Callender 2019). Despite these observations, elderly populations remain insufficiently characterized in current YF frameworks, with limited integration of age-specific risk into surveillance reporting, outbreak analytics, and vaccination prioritization strategies. In practice, most surveillance systems and public health responses continue to prioritize geographic exposure and transmission dynamics, while systematically underrepresenting age as an independent determinant of severity and mortality. This commentary aims to highlight older adults as an overlooked high-risk population in YF outbreaks by synthesizing emerging epidemiological evidence, underlying biological mechanisms, and gaps in current surveillance and vaccination strategies. Taken together, these elements suggest that age is not merely a demographic variable but a composite marker of biological vulnerability, limited healthcare access, and policy neglect. This shifts the interpretation of YF risk from a predominantly exposure-driven framework toward one that must incorporate host susceptibility and structural determinants. By doing so, it seeks to reframe current outbreak narratives and inform more targeted, age-inclusive public health strategies. While previous commentaries have largely focused on transmission patterns and vaccination strategies, the current analysis advances the field by synthesizing emerging age-stratified mortality data with pathophysiological and policy perspectives, thereby reframing older adults as a central, yet systematically overlooked, high-risk population in YF outbreaks.

Epidemiological signals: age and mortality

Data from recent outbreaks in Brazil have shown that advanced age is strongly associated with severe disease and death. Case-fatality rates increase significantly in individuals over 60 years, particularly among those with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or chronic liver disorders. In several outbreak waves, a disproportionate number of fatalities occurred in individuals above 60 years of age, even when they represented a minority of total reported cases (Avelino-Silva et al. 2023). Recent regional data indicate that older adults (≥60 years), despite representing a smaller proportion of cases, experience a markedly amplified mortality burden, with case-fatality rates exceeding 65% and more than doubling those observed in younger populations (Rodríguez-Morales et al. 2026a). Similar age-related gradients in mortality have been documented across multiple countries in South America and in previous outbreak analyses, supporting the consistency of advanced age as a key determinant of fatal outcomes despite variations in transmission intensity and healthcare access. Importantly, this pattern indicates that case counts alone may underestimate the true burden of disease, as relatively fewer infections in older adults translate into disproportionately higher mortality.

This age gradient in mortality is not entirely unexpected (de Sousa et al. 2023). YF’s pathogenesis, characterized by high viremia, systemic inflammatory response, hepatic failure, coagulopathy, and multiorgan dysfunction, places extraordinary physiological demands on the host (Srivastava et al. 2024). In older adults, reduced immunological reserve and impaired organ resilience amplify disease severity (Debbag et al. 2026).

However, the public health narrative has rarely emphasized this demographic reality. Risk communication has focused on geographic exposure rather than biological vulnerability, thereby underestimating age as an independent determinant of fatal outcomes. This disconnect reflects a limitation in current outbreak analytics, where age-stratified mortality patterns are insufficiently integrated into real-time risk assessment, potentially delaying targeted interventions for the most vulnerable populations.

Immunosenescence and pathophysiology

Immunosenescence, the gradual deterioration of immune function with age, likely plays a central role in the severe clinical course observed in elderly patients (Debbag et al. 2026). Aging is associated with diminished naïve T-cell production, altered cytokine responses, impaired interferon signaling, and dysregulated innate immunity. These changes can delay viral clearance while simultaneously predisposing to exaggerated inflammatory responses (Gomez Rial et al. 2026).

YF virus induces hepatotropic and viscerotropic infection, with high viral replication in the liver, spleen, and lymphoid tissues (Charó et al. 2025). In older adults, reduced hepatocyte regenerative capacity and baseline subclinical hepatic dysfunction may accelerate progression to fulminant hepatic failure (Lemos et al. 2020). Furthermore, age-related endothelial dysfunction and prothrombotic states may exacerbate the hemorrhagic manifestations characteristic of severe YF (de Sousa et al. 2023).

Comorbidities compound this vulnerability (Chen and Wilson 2020). Hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome (Rodriguez-Morales and Arteaga-Livias 2026), chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and other common conditions in elderly populations across South America limit physiological reserve (Grahn et al. 2024). The intersection of viral cytopathic injury and preexisting organ compromise likely explains the steep increase in mortality observed beyond the sixth decade of life (Lopes et al. 2019). These mechanisms act synergistically rather than independently. The convergence of impaired antiviral immunity, dysregulated inflammatory responses, and reduced organ reserve creates a multilayered vulnerability that helps explain the increased severity and consistently elevated case-fatality rates observed in older adults across outbreak settings. However, these biological factors alone are unlikely to fully account for the magnitude of mortality differences, which are also shaped by delayed diagnosis, atypical clinical presentation, comorbidities, and structural factors affecting access to timely care.

Vaccination paradox: risk–benefit dilemmas

A central tension in addressing YF among the elderly lies in vaccination policy (Srivastava et al. 2026). The live-attenuated 17D vaccine remains one of the most effective viral vaccines ever developed, conferring long-lasting immunity after a single dose in most recipients. However, rare but severe adverse events, particularly YF vaccine-associated viscerotropic disease and YF vaccine-associated neurotropic disease, occur more frequently in individuals aged ≥ 60 years than in those <60 years (Reno et al. 2020).

These safety concerns have contributed to cautious vaccination recommendations for older adults, often requiring individualized risk–benefit assessment. Current international and national guidelines generally classify age ≥60 years as a precaution rather than a contraindication for YF vaccination, recommending individualized risk–benefit assessment based on exposure risk, comorbidities, and prior vaccination history. However, guidance is often broad and lacks detailed operational criteria for decision-making during outbreaks, leaving substantial room for clinical interpretation (Rodríguez-Morales et al. 2026a). In non-outbreak settings, clinicians may defer vaccination in elderly patients with comorbidities. Yet during active transmission, the risk of wild-type infection may far exceed the risk of vaccine-associated adverse events (Shearer et al. 2017).

This paradox creates a dangerous gap that extends beyond individual clinical decision-making. Structurally, it reflects the absence of age-specific vaccination frameworks during outbreaks, in which older adults are neither systematically prioritized nor adequately supported in risk–benefit assessments, which vary considerably across countries in the region. For example, some settings have adopted more proactive vaccination approaches for older adults during active outbreaks (e.g., Colombia) (Rodríguez-Morales et al. 2026a), particularly in high-risk areas, whereas others maintain more restrictive practices due to safety concerns, especially in the absence of robust pharmacovigilance systems (e.g., Venezuela) (Rodriguez-Morales et al. 2026b). These differences highlight the absence of harmonized, age-specific vaccination strategies across the region. As a result, they occupy a high-risk intersection of biological susceptibility and policy ambiguity, which may contribute to preventable mortality at the population level (de Oliveira Figueiredo et al. 2020).

A nuanced approach is required—one that incorporates local transmission intensity, individual health status, and updated safety data. Enhanced pharmacovigilance systems and real-time risk communication are essential for informed decision-making (Nwachukwu William et al.). In this context, optimizing vaccination strategies for older adults is not only a matter of individual safety but also a key determinant of overall reductions in outbreak mortality.

From a practical perspective, vaccination decisions in individuals aged ≥60 years should be guided by a structured assessment that prioritizes exposure risk (e.g., residence in or travel to active transmission areas), evaluates comorbidities and immunological status, and incorporates shared decision-making. For policymakers, developing clearer, outbreak-specific frameworks that standardize these criteria could reduce variability in practice and improve protection of this high-risk population.

Clinical recognition and delayed diagnosis

Another contributor to fatal outcomes in elderly patients is delayed diagnosis. YF’s early clinical presentation—fever, myalgia, headache, and nausea—is nonspecific and may be misattributed to influenza, dengue, leptospirosis, or bacterial sepsis (Rodriguez-Morales et al. 2025). In older adults, atypical presentations are common, and fever responses may be blunted (Gehrke et al. 2021).

Moreover, clinicians in urban settings may underestimate the risk of YF in elderly patients without clear occupational or forest exposure (Cuéllar-Sáenz et al. 2025). However, recent outbreaks have demonstrated peri-urban transmission and spillover into areas previously considered low risk. Delays in recognition can delay supportive care, including careful fluid management, monitoring of hepatic function, and early detection of coagulopathy (Uchenna Emeribe et al.). This highlights a broader clinical gap, where atypical presentations in older adults intersect with low diagnostic suspicion, reinforcing under-recognition in the population at highest risk of deterioration.

Given the lack of specific antiviral therapy, early supportive management remains the cornerstone of care (Forero-Delgadillo et al. 2025). Heightened clinical suspicion in older adults residing in or traveling to endemic areas is therefore critical.

Urbanization, mobility, and changing risk landscapes

South America’s demographic transition toward aging populations intersects with rapid urban expansion into forested areas (Rodriguez-Morales 2026). Older adults increasingly reside in peri-urban zones adjacent to sylvatic transmission cycles (Bonilla-Aldana et al. 2025). Environmental changes such as deforestation, land-use transformation, and urban expansion increase human–vector–reservoir contact, thereby elevating exposure risk for populations residing at these ecological interfaces, including older adults who may not traditionally be considered at occupational risk. This convergence of demographic aging and environmental change suggests that YF risk is being redefined not only geographically but also demographically, with older adults emerging as a newly exposed and highly vulnerable group. Retirement migration to rural or semi-rural areas, as observed in parts of Brazil and Colombia, further increases exposure risk (Mantilla-Granados et al. 2022).

Simultaneously, vaccine coverage gaps, exacerbated by health system disruptions, economic instability, and misinformation, have reduced herd immunity in several countries (Rodríguez-Morales and Navarro 2025). In this context, elderly individuals without prior vaccination constitute a growing pool of susceptibles.

Climate variability and deforestation also influence vector ecology, potentially altering transmission patterns (Rodriguez-Morales 2026). As outbreaks extend into previously unaffected regions, populations with historically low vaccination coverage, including older cohorts, face unexpected exposure (Rodríguez-Morales and Navarro 2025).

These dynamics suggest that future outbreak patterns may increasingly involve older populations in peri-urban and transitional environments, where demographic aging intersects with ecological disruption, potentially amplifying both the scale and severity of YF outbreaks.

Policy implications and research priorities

From a public health perspective, priority should be given to strengthening age-stratified surveillance and targeted vaccination strategies in outbreak settings, as these interventions offer the most immediate potential to reduce mortality, while research and long-term preparedness efforts remain essential but secondary. Addressing YF among the elderly requires a shift from generalized outbreak responses toward precision public health approaches that explicitly incorporate age as a determinant of risk (Baba and Ikusemoran 2017). First, surveillance systems should systematically incorporate age-stratified reporting of cases, severity, and outcomes as a standard requirement, enabling real-time identification of high-risk groups and more targeted public health responses (Jácome et al. 2019). Second, vaccination campaigns in outbreak settings should incorporate targeted communication for older adults and their healthcare providers, emphasizing updated risk–benefit analyses (Farnsworth et al. 2025). Third, research should explore immune responses to 17D vaccination in elderly populations, including the durability of immunity and its correlates (Amanna and Slifka 2016). Alternative vaccine platforms or dose-optimization strategies may merit investigation in high-risk groups. Fourth, integration of geriatric considerations into arboviral preparedness plans is essential. As South America continues to age demographically, infectious disease frameworks must adapt accordingly (Callender 2019). Collectively, these priorities indicate that failure to integrate age-specific risk into surveillance and response systems represents a modifiable gap in current outbreak preparedness.

In resource-limited settings, these strategies can be implemented through integration into existing surveillance platforms, prioritizing vaccination in high-risk geographic and demographic groups rather than universal campaigns, and using simplified clinical risk assessment tools to support decision-making. Leveraging existing primary healthcare networks and community-based programs will be essential to ensure feasibility and sustainability.

Conclusion: reframing the narrative

YF is often portrayed as a re-emerging tropical disease threatening travelers and forest workers. Yet within recent South American outbreaks lies a quieter tragedy: disproportionately high mortality among older adults. Recognizing the elderly as a high-risk group does not merely refine epidemiological understanding—it reshapes prevention strategies, vaccination policy, and clinical vigilance. In the context of expanding transmission zones and aging populations, overlooking this demographic risk perpetuates preventable fatalities. Importantly, this commentary contributes to the field by explicitly linking age-specific mortality patterns with biological vulnerability and structural gaps in vaccination policy. This represents not only an epidemiological oversight but also a structural limitation in current public health strategies, as this area has received limited attention in prior discussions of YF. A more age-conscious approach to YF preparedness and response is urgently needed. The intersection of viral resurgence and demographic transition demands nothing less. Failure to explicitly incorporate older adults into surveillance, vaccination strategies, and outbreak preparedness will likely result in persistently high and preventable mortality, particularly as demographic aging and environmental change continue to reshape YF transmission dynamics in the region.

Older adults face disproportionately high mortality from yellow fever and must be prioritized in surveillance and vaccination strategies.
