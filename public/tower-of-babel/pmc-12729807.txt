Pyoderma gangrenosum associated with granulomatosis with polyangiitis: a case report
Authors: Marwa Ben Brahim, Sirine Abdellatif, Asma Ben Mabrouk, Olfa Jomaa, Azer Chebil, Mahdi Hasnaoui, Sondess Arfa, Olfa Berriche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPyoderma gangrenosum is an ulcerative disorder belonging to the neutrophilic dermatoses group. The pathogenesis of pyoderma gangrenosum is not fully understood and its association with granulomatosis with polyangiitis has been scarcely reported. Herein, we report a case of pyoderma gangrenosum in a young patient treated for granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Case presentationA 26-year-old Tunisian man presented with a 2-month history of bilateral nasal obstruction and bloody nasal discharge. Nasal endoscopic assessment revealed the presence of mucosal hyperemia, crusting, and granulation. Nasal biopsy showed chronic inflammation with necrosis. Clinical, radiological, and histological findings supported the diagnosis of localized granulomatosis with polyangiitis. The patient underwent therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone and intravenous cyclophosphamide, and was subsequently lost to follow-up. Three years later, the patient was readmitted with chief complaints of bloody crusting rhinorrhea, anosmia, persistent nasal obstruction, and a 4-month history of a large ulcer in the right lower limb. A computed tomography scan revealed complete lysis of the nasal septum and nasal conchae, along with a perforation of the hard palate. On physical examination, he had a 20 cm lesion with necrosis and pus. The skin biopsy showed a dense inflammatory infiltrate formed by polymorphonuclear neutrophils with fragmented nuclei forming leukocytoclastic lesions associated with lymphocytes and histiocytes without signs of malignity. The diagnosis of pyoderma gangrenosum associated with granulomatosis with polyangiitis was made. He was also started on three methylprednisolone pulses relayed by oral corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide, with prompt recovery. ConclusionsSkin involvement in granulomatosis with polyangiitis is common and is characterized by a wide spectrum of manifestations. However, the association between granulomatosis with polyangiitis and classic pyoderma gangrenosum is rare. The differential diagnosis between classic pyoderma gangrenosum and pyoderma gangrenosum-like ulceration is challenging. Only histopathological features can help practitioners differentiate the two conditions.

Background

Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a chronic inflammatory neutrophilic dermatosis with an estimated worldwide incidence of 3–10 cases per million people per year [1]. It is typically characterized by erythematosus nodules or pustules that evolve into painful ulcerations with violaceous undermined borders in the lower limbs. PG can be either primary or associated with systemic diseases. Associations with systemic diseases such as chronic inflammatory bowel diseases, inflammatory arthritis, and hematological disorders have been reported in up to 86% of patients [2]. The physiopathological mechanisms of this dermatosis are still very hypothetical. However, an immune-mediated mechanism seems to be plausible regarding the overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and good responses to immune-modulatory drugs in patients with PG [2]. However, its association with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) has rarely been reported. Herein, we report a rare case of GPA associated with PG.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old Tunisian man presented with a 2-month history of bilateral nasal obstruction and bloody nasal discharge. Nasal endoscopic assessment revealed the presence of mucosal hyperemia, crusting, and granulation. A craniofacial computed tomography (CT) scan revealed mucosal thickening in the right maxillary sinus. Nasal biopsy was positive for chronic inflammation with necrosis. Serum anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) against PR3 was negative. Clinical, radiological, and histological findings supported the diagnosis of localized GPA. The patient underwent therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days) that was slowly tapered accompanied by intravenous cyclophosphamide pulses at days 1 and 14, and was subsequently lost to follow-up. Three years later, he was readmitted with chief complaints of bloody crusting rhinorrhea, anosmia, persistent nasal obstruction and a 4-month history of a large ulcer in the right lower limb. A CT scan revealed complete lysis of the nasal septum and nasal conchae, along with a perforation of the hard palate. On physical examination, he had a 20 cm lesion with necrosis and pus (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, cell blood count, creatinine, electrolytes, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase were normal. Pus culture was negative. The skin biopsy revealed a dense inflammatory infiltrate formed by polymorphonuclear neutrophils with fragmented nuclei forming leukocytoclastic lesions associated with lymphocytes and histiocytes without signs of malignancy (Figs. 2 and 3). The diagnosis of PG associated with localized GPA was made. Discussion and conclusion

GPA is a systemic necrotizing vasculitis affecting small vessels involving mainly the upper and lower respiratory tract and kidney [3]. Skin involvement is less common, with an estimated prevalence of 10–15% of cases and is more common in systemic forms [4]. On the basis of the underlying histopathological changes, skin lesions in GPA can be classified as specific and nonspecific [5]. Specific skin lesions present histopathological features of vasculitis or granulomas [4, 5]. However, nonspecific skin manifestations do not present any of the characteristic features [4, 5]. The most common clinical presentation of skin involvement in GPA involves palpable purpura or petechia, livedo reticularis or racemosa, papules and subcutaneous nodules [4]. Several cases of pyoderma gangrenosum-like ulcers characterized by large, painful ulcers with undetermined edges have been reported [4]. However, cases with genuine PG in patients with GPA have rarely been reported [3]. The differential diagnosis between authentic PG ulcers and PG-like ulcers is quite difficult because of the lack of specific diagnostic tools [6]. In fact, both conditions share common features, including the clinical presentation of single or multiple painful ulcers with raised, undermined erythematous edges mainly located in the lower limbs [6]. However, this typical presentation is less evident in GPA ulcerations than in PG [6]. Granulomatosis inflammation, with or without vasculitis, as well as a positive serum antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (C-ANCA) test and systemic involvement are common in patients with GPA and are useful for differentiating between the two conditions [6]. Our patient had a typical presentation of a localized GPA with severe and necrotizing involvement of the nose and sinuses even though C-ANCAs and nasal biopsies were negative. However the patient course was complicated by large ulceration with histopathological findings, demonstrating mixed inflammatory infiltrates with predominant neutrophils consistent with the diagnosis of PG. To the best of our knowledge, very few cases reporting associations between GPA and PG have been described in the literature [6, 7].

Treatment of PG remains empirical and depends on the severity and extent of the lesion, as well as the associated disease [8]. Systemic corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment for both PG and GPA [8, 9]. Immunosuppressive agents, including methotrexate, cyclophospamide azathioprine, mofetil mycophenolate, and rituximab are recommended depending on the extent and pattern of organ involvement in patients with GPA [9]. These immunosuppressive drugs have been shown to be effective in inducing a rapid response when combined with intravenous corticosteroids in patients with PG [8]. Our patient presented with active destructive nasal involvement requiring the combination of intravenous methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide pulses to control the disease course. Prompt recovery and healing of the skin lesion was achieved.

In conclusion, PG could be one of the cutaneous manifestations of GPA. Its diagnosis remains difficult in the absence of specific biological and histological diagnostic tools. Current standard treatment is based on corticosteroid therapy. Biological treatments are promising and require more randomized trials.
