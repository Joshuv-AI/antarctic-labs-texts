Dorsal CA1 projections to nucleus accumbens and lateral septum differentially regulate social and locomotor behavior
Authors: Ye Han, Gai-Linn Besing, Rachelle Larivee, Daniel Y Guo, Snigdha Mukerjee, Kirsty R Erickson, Alan S Lewis, William P Nobis, Kyle A Lyman, Cody A Siciliano, Dane M Chetkovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeyond its established role in spatial and episodic memory, the dorsal hippocampus also shapes motivated behavior by routing contextual information to downstream targets. Dorsal CA1 (dCA1) projects to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and lateral septum (LS); here, we show that these pathways regulate distinct behaviors. We mapped and selectively manipulated these circuits in mice using projection-specific viral tracing, intersectional labeling, and terminal optogenetics. Activating dCA1-to-NAc terminals selectively increased social interaction without altering locomotion or novel-object exploration, consistent with a role in social motivation rather than generalized exploration. In contrast, the dCA1-to-LS pathway bidirectionally regulated locomotion, with activation increasing movement and inhibition decreasing locomotion without specifically influencing social behavior. Intersectional labeling also identified a small population of dCA1 neurons that projects to both targets. Together, these findings suggest that distinct dCA1 output pathways can differentially regulate social interaction and locomotor state, providing a circuit-level view of how hippocampal contextual signals are routed to subcortical regions.Keywords: behavior, hippocampal circuitry, motivation, optogenetics, projection-specific tracing

Introduction

The hippocampus plays an important role in spatial navigation and memory by allowing past experience to guide current and future actions. It is organized along a dorsal–ventral axis, with the dorsal (dHPC) and ventral (vHPC) subregions contributing to cognitive and emotional processes, respectively (Moser and Moser 1998; Bannerman et al. 2004; Van et al. 2009; Fanselow and Dong 2010; Strange et al. 2014). While the ventral hippocampus is primarily associated with affective and motivational regulation, the dorsal hippocampus has traditionally been studied for its role in spatial learning and memory formation (Kjelstrup et al. 2002; Fanselow and Dong 2010; Strange et al. 2014; Gergues et al. 2020). However, recent findings with more advanced circuit mapping and functional studies suggest that the dHPC (particularly dorsal CA1, a key region for memory) also contributes to spatial reward processing, expanding its functions beyond its classical roles (Soltesz and Losonczy 2018; Trouche et al. 2019; Ibrahim et al. 2024). A key pathway supporting this integration is the projection from dorsal CA1 (dCA1) pyramidal neurons to the nucleus accumbens (NAc). This circuit has been shown to guide reward-directed behavior and promote drug-associated place preference by linking spatial context with motivation (Sjulson et al. 2018; Trouche et al. 2019; Ibrahim et al. 2024). However, despite the central role of the NAc in social reward behavior, it remains unclear whether its role in this key domain can be meaningfully influenced by direct input from dCA1.

In contrast to the unexplored role of dCA1–NAc projections, there is a large body of work implicating the ventral hippocampal (vHPC) projections to the NAc and lateral septum (LS) in regulating social behavior, motivation, and locomotor state. Ventral CA1 (vCA1) neurons and their projections to the NAc shell are necessary and sufficient for social recognition memory (Okuyama et al. 2016) and are required for encoding the rewarding context of social interaction (LeGates et al. 2018). In addition, ventral hippocampal inputs to the NAc have also been shown to modulate social interaction under conditions of stress and motivational conflict (Bagot et al. 2015a; Patterson et al. 2025). Similarly, vHPC projections to the LS have been shown to regulate social novelty preferences (Rashid et al. 2024). Because both dorsal and ventral hippocampus project to accumbal and septal circuits, an important open question is whether dCA1 outputs play distinct roles in behavioral regulation or primarily complement ventral circuits by providing spatial information to shared downstream regions. Although we did not directly compare dorsal and ventral pathways here, we tested the behavioral impact of projection-defined dCA1 outputs to NAc and LS.

Both the NAc and LS are each well positioned to integrate dorsal and ventral hippocampal inputs to shape motivated and state-dependent behaviors. The NAc serves as a central hub for motivated action, integrating information from the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and amygdala to guide responses to rewarding stimuli, including social reward (Kelley 2004; Bartal et al. 2011; Dölen et al. 2013; Gunaydin et al. 2014; Xia et al. 2017; Sjulson et al. 2018; Trouche et al. 2019; Sosa et al. 2019). The LS receives dense input from the hippocampus, and has been implicated in social behaviors (social interaction, aggression, and social memory), while also contributing to state-dependent locomotor activity (Risold and Swanson 1996, 1997a; Sheehan et al. 2004; Tingley and Buzsáki 2018; Opalka and Wang 2020; Wirtshafter and Wilson 2020; Menon et al. 2022).

Recent transcriptomic studies have revealed substantial molecular and functional diversity among dCA1 pyramidal cells (Cembrowski et al. 2016, 2018; Cembrowski and Spruston 2019; Yao et al. 2021). This heterogeneity suggests the existence of projection-specific dCA1 neuronal populations with distinct functional roles. In the present study, we combined projection-specific viral tracing and optogenetic manipulations to examine how dCA1 neurons projecting to the NAc and LS contribute to behavior. We found that only a small subset of pyramidal neurons projects to both targets. Functionally, increased activity in the dCA1-to-NAc pathway, but not the dCA1-to-LS pathway, selectively enhances social interaction. In contrast, the dCA1-to-LS pathway bidirectionally regulates locomotor activity. Together, these findings demonstrate that dCA1 engages distinct downstream pathways to differentially regulate social and locomotor behaviors.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6J male mice (6–8 weeks old; JAX # 000664) were housed 5 per ventilated cage. Mice aged 10–14 weeks were used for all behavioral experiments unless otherwise specified. All mice were group housed under standard conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 AM), with food and water available ad libitum. All animal protocols were approved by the IACUC committees of Vanderbilt University Medical Center. Animals were randomly assigned to groups, investigators were blinded during scoring/analysis, and a priori exclusion criteria were prespecified (cannula misplacement, off-target viral spread, illness).

Viral injections

We obtained the following viruses from commercially available sources: AAV8-hSyn-DIO-mCherry (Addgene #50459), AAV8-EF1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry-WPRE-HGH-pA (Addgene #20297), AAV9-Ef1a-DIO-eNpHR3.0-EYFP (Addgene #26966), AAV9-Ef1a-DIO-eYFP (Addgene #27056), AAV (retrograde)-EF1a-Flpo (Addgene # 55637), AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre (Addgene #24593), and AAV5-hSyn-Con/Fon EYFP-WPRE (UNC vector core).

Viral injections were performed with minimal modifications from our previous work (Han et al. 2017). In brief, a small incision was made to visualize bregma and lambda and a craniotomy was made with a dental drill. A 5 μl Hamilton syringe was then lowered to the coordinates, for CA1, −2.3 mm A/P, 1.3 mm M/L, −1.7 mm D/V; for NAc, 1.75 mm A/P, 0.5 mm M/L, −4.4 mm D/V; for LS, 0.6 mm A/P, 0.3 mm M/L, −3.3 mm D/V. Typical injection volume was 500 nl/site delivered at 100 nl/min; the syringe was then held in place for an additional 5 min before the syringe was slowly withdrawn. Titers ranged from 5 × 1012 – 3 × 1013 vg/ml. All mice were allowed to recover for 3–5 weeks prior to experimentation.

Optogenetic behavioral experiments

Optogenetic experiments were performed on male mice implanted with optical cannulas (Doric Lenses, Quebec, Canada) and controlled by Doric Neuroscience Studio. Males were used to reduce variability arising from social hierarchy and aggression, consistent with prior hippocampal optogenetic studies (Bender et al. 2015; Trouche et al. 2019). Optical fibers had the following specifications: 200 μm core, 230 μm cladding, numerical aperture 0.48, flat tip; 4.5 mm GS1.0 cannulas for NAc and 3.4 mm GS 0.6 cannulas for LS (Doric Lenses, Quebec, Canada). Cannulas were implanted bilaterally over NAc or LS and secured with dental bond (Patterson Veterinary). Light output was measured at the patch-cord tip with a PM100D (Thorlabs); reported powers are estimated at the implant tip after adjusting for coupling efficiency measured prior to surgery. After 2 h of acclimation in the test room, cage mates were removed and separated to a new cage, and the test mouse remained in the home cage and connected to the fiber-optic patch cord (Doric Lenses Quebec, Canada), allowing the mouse to explore the cage for a few minutes and to ensure the patch cord was connected with cannula properly. The patch cord was connected to an FC/PC adaptor and then to the Doric Lenses system. For the blue light (473 nm) stimulation paradigm, we delivered 8 pulses at 20 Hz, followed by a 5 s pause, for a total of 5 min with 10 mW power output at the fiber tip. For the yellow light (591 nm), we delivered continuous light (1 mW at the implant tip after coupling loss correction; about 5–8 mW/mm2 estimated irradiance at terminals) for 5 min (Gunaydin et al. 2014). eYFP/mCherry only mice (AAV9-Ef1a-DIO-eYFP and AAV8-hSyn-DIO-mCherry) served as controls. Viral expression and fiber placements were verified histologically; mice with off-target expression or misplaced fibers were excluded. Each experiment was replicated in three independent cohorts to validate the findings.

Social interaction

Each test mouse performed two social interactions, one without stimulation (Light OFF) and the other with stimulation (Light ON); the two tests were 1 h apart. Mice were tethered to patch cords in the home cage and recorded on video. During the stimulation test (total 5 min), light was turned on for 3 min prior to introducing a novel mouse (same sex, 6–8 weeks old), and a novel mouse was introduced in the home cage; the light then continued to be delivered for another 2 min. During the 2 min period, videos were scored by a blinded rater for nose to body or nose to anogenital contact or close following (<1 cm), excluding climbing (Gunaydin et al. 2014).

Novel object interaction

The novel object test was conducted in the home cage using the same timing as the social interaction test. During the light ON condition, a novel object (eg glass bottle) was introduced to the animal’s home cage after 3 min of stimulation, and exploration time was recorded during the final 2 min of the stimulation period. During the light OFF condition, a different novel object (eg toy truck) was introduced for 2 min. Object identity, order, and locations were counterbalanced across animals and experiments. Exploration was defined as the animal directing its nose toward the object within ~ 1 cm or contacting it. Objects were wiped with 70% ethanol between trials.

Locomotor test

Using the same setup and timing as the social and novel object assays, we quantified total distance traveled during the 5-min light period to assess whether manipulating the dCA1-to-NAc or dCA1-to-LS terminals (light ON) regulates locomotion relative to the light OFF condition.

Tail suspension test

TST was performed as described previously (Lewis et al. 2011; Han et al. 2017). Each mouse was individually assayed by being suspended by their tail for 6 min over the period of stimulation, and the immobile time (no gross body movement) was assessed by a blinded rater during the stimulation. Mice were excluded from analysis if they climbed their tails at any point in the assay.

Immunohistochemistry

For cFos staining and qualification, mice were subject to blue light (473 nm) optical stimulation in their home cage for 5 min, utilizing the identical stimulation setup used for behavioral test, 90 min prior to sacrifice. Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were then removed and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight. 30 μm coronal sections were made on a vibratome (Leica, Buffalo Grove, IL). Sections were blocked in 5% normal goat serum +0.3% Triton X-100 (PBS) for 1 h. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution (1:1000 for all antibodies) and applied overnight at 4°C. Sections were washed 3 times prior to a 1 h incubation at room temperature in secondary antibody, followed by 3 additional washes in PBS. Primary antibodies include cFos antibodies (Abcam, ab190289), GFP antibodies (Millipore AB3080P), and RFP antibodies (Synaptic Systems, 390004). Imaging was performed at the Vanderbilt Cell Imaging Shared Resource (CISR) core on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope and a STORM microscope.

Image acquisition and cell counts

Coronal sections containing dorsal CA1 were imaged on a Nikon confocal microscope using a 20× objective. Single optical sections were acquired through the pyramidal cell layer. Images were analyzed in Aivia (Leica Microsystems). Supervised pixel classifiers were trained independently for the mCherry and eYFP channels using user-annotated training regions drawn from representative images across multiple animals. A random forest classifier generated per-channel confidence maps (0–255 scale), and the training dataset was iteratively refined to ensure accurate classification across the range of fluorescence intensities and cellular morphologies present in the dataset. The finalized classifiers were applied uniformly to all images. Soma detection was then performed independently in each channel using the Cell Count–Cellpose recipe in Aivia, a deep learning-based 2D detection workflow. The Cellpose model type, typical cell diameter, probability threshold, and size-filtering range were optimized empirically and held constant across all sections and animals. Colocalization between mCherry-positive and eYFP-positive populations was assessed using the Spatial Relations Tool in Aivia, with mCherry-positive objects designated as the primary set and eYFP-positive objects as the associated set. Spatial relationships were determined using the Overlap (All) rule, in which objects from the associated set were classified as overlapping when their segmented masks intersected with the mask of a primary object. An overlap tolerance value of 1 pixel was applied to define overlapping objects. Candidate overlapping objects were subsequently reviewed to confirm true colocalization and exclude spurious overlapping arising from segmentation boundaries. Cells were classified as dual projecting when overlapping objects were detected in both channels according to this criterion. mCherry-positive, eYFP-positive, and double-labeled cell counts were quantified per section and aggregated by animal prior to statistical analysis. All image acquisition and analysis were performed with the experimenter blinded to experimental condition.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad software). Normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk. Statistical tests are indicated throughout the article with * indicating a statistically significant result. Unless noted otherwise, data are represented as mean ± s.e.m., and paired t tests were used for comparisons. All tests were two tailed with α = 0.05, and test statistics (t, df) are reported in the figure legends. Sample sizes were determined based on pilot effect sizes and prior publications using similar behavioral paradigms (Gunaydin et al. 2014; Trouche et al. 2019).

Results

dCA1 pyramidal neurons project to both the NAc and the LS

Based on the known projections from the dCA1 to NAc and LS, both of which are implicated in social behavior, we hypothesized that dCA1 projections to the NAc or LS contribute to the regulation of social interaction. To test this, we first sought to confirm prior observations that the dCA1 projects to both regions using a viral recombination strategy (Trouche et al. 2019). A Cre-dependent anterograde viral vector encoding mCherry (AAV8-hSyn-cDIO-mCherry) and a Flpo-dependent anterograde viral vector encoding eYFP (AAV9-EF1a-fDIO-EYFP) were unilaterally injected into the dCA1 region. Simultaneously, retrograde viruses carrying either Cre (AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre) or Flpo (AAV (retrograde)-EF1a-Flpo) were injected into the ipsilateral LS and NAc, respectively (Fig. 1A). After several weeks to allow for expression, we examined the density of mCherry positive (LS-projecting) and EYFP positive (NAc-projecting) cells in the dCA1. Both markers were expressed at high levels, as shown in Fig. 1C–F for the CA1, Fig. 2A–D for the LS, and Fig. 2E–H for the NAc. There was no clear anatomical pattern distinguishing deep/superficial or medial/lateral expression of the cells projecting to either region (Soltesz and Losonczy 2018). To validate our observations, we performed a virus swap experiment. Specifically, AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre was injected into the NAc and AAV (retrograde)-EF1a-Flpo into the LS on the same side, and we observed comparable outcomes (n = 5, data not shown). As a control, no reporter expression was detected in animals that received only AAV8-hSyn-cDIO-mCherry and AAV9-EF1a-fDIO-EYFP in dCA1, in the absence of Cre or Flpo (n = 3, data not shown).

A small subset of dCA1 pyramidal neurons project to both the NAc and LS

Although dCA1 neurons projecting to NAc and LS were largely non-overlapping, a small population of neurons appeared to be double labeled in the initial dual color labeling approach (eg yellow cells in merged images, Fig. 1C and F). Quantification of labeled dCA1 neurons revealed that, among singly labeled projection neurons, 37.5 ± 6.8% were NAc-only and 57 ± 7.1% were LS only, whereas dual projecting neurons were rare, comprising 2.72 ± 0.4% of all labeled neurons (n = 6 mice, 3 per counterbalanced scheme; mean ± SEM) (Fig. S1). To validate this finding, we next sought to verify the overlap using an independent approach. Specifically, we employed an eYFP reporter construct that requires co-expression of both Cre and Flippase for activation (Fenno et al. 2014). We injected AAV5-Fon-Con-EYFP unilaterally into the dCA1 along with ipsilateral injection of AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre into the LS and ipsilateral injection of AAV (retrograde)-EF1a-Flpo into the NAc (Fig. 3A). This approach confirmed there is a small subset of dCA1 pyramidal neurons projecting to both LS and NAc (Fig. 3). The presence of dual projecting cells indicates that dCA1 signals can access NAc and LS in parallel, providing a plausible substrate for coordinated output across these targets. We did not assess causality here but note this organizational feature as relevant context for pathway-specific findings.

Activating dCA1 projections to the NAc increases social interaction

We next employed an optogenetics approach to study the function of the projections from the dCA1 to NAc. A retrograde cre virus (AAV pgk-Cre) was injected bilaterally into the NAc and a cre dependent ChR2 (AAV8-EF1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry-WPRE-HGH-pA) was injected bilaterally into the dCA1 (Figs 4A and 5A1). Next, a customized dual cannula (Doric, Canada) was implanted into the NAc to stimulate the dCA1 terminal fibers synapsing onto NAc cells. Based on prior work, we chose a paradigm of stimulating for 8 pulses at 20 Hz with 5 s in between pulses (Fig. 4B) (Gunaydin et al. 2014). To confirm the efficacy of this paradigm in activating NAc cells, we initially assessed cFos induction. Animals underwent a 5-min stimulation session in their home cage, and were sacrificed 90 min later, and brains were processed for cFos immunohistochemistry, an immediate early neuronal marker of recent neural activity. As illustrated in Fig. 4, cFos immunostaining revealed an increase in the light ON condition compared to the light OFF condition, indicating successful activation of NAc neurons.

We then evaluated the role of the dCA1-to-NAc pathway in regulating social interaction using a novel mouse assay. In this paradigm, the dCA1-to-NAc pathway was labeled with ChR2 (as in Fig. 4A), and a novel mouse was introduced into the subject’s home cage. The dCA1-to-NAc pathway was stimulated with blue light (light ON condition), and 1 h later the same test mouse was subjected to the same task (with another novel mouse) without blue light stimulation (light OFF). Stimulation of the dCA1-to-NAc terminals led to a significant increase in time spent interacting with the novel mouse (Fig. 5B1). To confirm that the effect was not dependent on test order, the experiment was repeated a month later with the order of light conditions reversed (light OFF > light ON), and similar results were observed. Three independent cohorts were tested, with some cohorts starting with the light ON condition and others with the light OFF condition; the results shown represent data combined from all cohorts (Fig. 5B1). These findings demonstrate that activation of the dCA1-to-NAc pathway robustly enhances social interaction, independent of testing order.

We subsequently asked if the increased social interaction seen with stimulating the dCA1-to-NAc pathway was the result of a generalized increase in exploratory behavior. To probe this possibility, we performed a related experiment in which the novel mouse was replaced with a novel object (Hammond et al. 2004), reasoning that there would still be a novel stimulus but without the valence of social interaction. All mice that completed the social interaction assay also performed the following behavioral testing. Unlike the novel mouse, stimulating the dCA1-to NAc pathway did not lead to a significant change in novel object exploration (Fig. 5C1). Similarly, there was no difference in general mobility in the open field test in the home cage (Fig. 5D1). Previous studies have linked increases in dCA1 excitability to less time spent immobile on the tail suspension test (TST) (Han et al. 2017; Lyman et al. 2021), which has been described as a test of motivated behavior (Tye et al. 2013 Jan 15; Lyman et al. 2021). However, there was no difference in the time spent immobile during TST in the light ON condition (Fig. 5E1).

To ensure that our results with ChR2 were not the result of some unforeseen effect of the light stimulation paradigm, we next expressed a control construct in the same dCA1-to-NAc pathway using a control virus (AAV8-EF1a-DIO-mCherry, Fig. 5A2). Unlike the ChR2 construct, there was no influence of the light ON condition on social interaction when the mCherry control construct was expressed (Fig. 5B2). There was also no effect of the light ON condition on novel object exploration (Fig. 5C2), distance traveled in open field testing (Fig. 5D2), or time spent immobile on TST (Fig. 5E2).

Combined, these results indicate that activating the dCA1-to-NAc pathway specifically leads to greater social interaction without affecting general exploratory behaviors.

Inhibiting the dCA1-to-NAc pathway does not impair social interaction

Having established that increasing activity in the dCA1-to-NAc pathway promotes social interaction, we next investigated whether inhibiting the activity in the same pathway would reduce social interaction. Toward this end, we bilaterally injected cre-dependent halorhodopsin (AAV9-Ef1a DIO eNpHR 3.0-EYFP) into the dCA1 region, and AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre into the NAc. We then conducted the same behavioral assays while utilizing 591 nm continuous light (1 mW) for the 5-min test to activate NpHR (Gunaydin et al. 2014). Inhibition of dCA1-to-NAc pathway had no detectable changes in social interaction, novel object interaction, open field, and TST tests (n = 4, all P > 0.5; data not shown). Because there was no apparent trend, we did not repeat this experiment in additional cohorts. These findings suggest that, although activation of the dCA1-to-NAc pathway promotes social interaction, its inhibition does not appear to limit social behavior.

dCA1-to-LS pathway bidirectionally controls locomotion

Having found that activating the dCA1-to-NAc pathway promotes social interaction, we next investigated the specificity of this finding by examining the projection from the dCA1 to LS, focusing on social interaction and locomotion. We employed the same dual virus strategy and administered bilateral injections of cre-dependent ChR2 into the dCA1 region and AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre into the LS region. After several weeks to allow for viral expression, we conducted the same behavioral tests utilizing the identical stimulation protocol (8 pulses at 20 Hz with 5 s intervals between pulses). In the initial set, the light OFF condition was performed first, followed by repeating the experiment an hour later using the light ON paradigm.

Activating the dCA1-to-LS pathway did not influence social interaction in either sequence (Fig. 6B1). In contrast, stimulating the dCA1-to-LS projections led to a significant increase in locomotor activity (Fig. 6C1). To validate these findings and control for potential light-related artifacts, the assays were repeated one month later in the reverse order, with the light ON first followed by light OFF. Three independent cohorts were tested with mixed starting orders, and results are pooled across cohorts (Fig. 6B1/C1).

As a control, we injected AAV8-EF1a-DIO-mCherry instead of ChR2 into the LS region. Unlike the ChR2 construct, there was no influence of the light ON condition on social interaction or distance traveled in open field testing in both sets of experiments (Fig. 6A2–C2).

We next asked whether silencing the dCA1-to-LS pathway would affect locomotion. Using a similar viral methodology, we administered bilateral injections of a cre-dependent halorhodopsin (AAV9-Ef1a-DIO eNpHR 3.0-EYFP) into the CA1 region, and AAV (retrograde)-pgk-Cre into the LS (Fig. 7A1). Subsequently, we conducted identical behavioral assays while activating NpHR. Inhibiting the dCA1-to-LS pathway significantly reduced locomotor activities under both conditions of initial light ON and initial light OFF without affecting social interaction (Fig. 7B1–C1). Animals administered with control viruses did not exhibit any effects on the tested behavioral assays (Fig. 7A2–C2). The data suggest that inhibition of dCA1-to-LS pathway limits locomotor activities.

Discussion

Our results demonstrated that the dCA1 projections to NAc and LS pathways play distinct roles in regulating behaviors. Stimulating the dCA1-to-NAc pathway significantly enhanced social interaction without altering novel object exploration and locomotor activity, suggesting a specific influence on social interaction. In contrast, dCA1 projections to the LS provide robust and bidirectional control over locomotor activity without specifically influencing social interaction. Together, these findings highlight a functional divergence in the behavioral regulation of dCA1 outputs and provide insights into how the dorsal hippocampus shapes complex behaviors through specific circuits.

The dorsal hippocampus has long been associated with spatial navigation and contextual memory, but accumulating evidence using modern circuit-level approaches suggests that dCA1 is also involved in encoding motivated behaviors (Soltesz and Losonczy 2018; Trouche et al. 2019; Barnstedt et al. 2024; Ibrahim et al. 2024). However, prior studies of dCA1-to-NAc projections have focused on reward and place related learning (Trouche et al. 2019; Barnstedt et al. 2024; Ibrahim et al. 2024), leaving their potential contribution to social motivation unexplored. Our findings extend this idea by showing that activation of this pathway contributes to social interaction. Notably, this social interaction increase was highly specific, as activation of dCA1 to the NAc did not affect novel object exploration (another hippocampus-dependent behavior) or locomotor activity, indicating that the increased social interaction was not the result of a generalized increase in novelty-seeking activity. We cannot exclude contributions of arousal or attention; however, the lack of effects on novel object exploration and open field behavior argues against a generalized increase in exploratory drive. In line with our dCA1-to-NAc findings, previous studies indicated that this circuit encodes reward-related spatial information and regulates location-specific reward behavior during cocaine seeking (Sjulson et al. 2018; Trouche et al. 2019). In addition, activation of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to NAc pathway has also been reported to selectively increase social interaction, without influencing novel object exploration (Gunaydin et al. 2014), consistent with the idea that NAc integrates motivational signals with contextual inputs to regulate social behavior (Sjulson et al. 2018; Trouche et al. 2019).

In contrast to the limited characterization of dCA1 projections to the NAc, there is a large body of work establishing an important role for vCA1-to-NAc projections in regulating social behavior, motivation, and affective processes. This vCA1 pathway appears to play a key role in contextualizing social interactions, as inhibition of vCA1-to-NAc function abolished social discrimination between novel and familiar conspecifics, with overall social interaction unaltered (Okuyama et al. 2016). More recent work suggests that this pathway can also influence social approach. Chemogenetic inhibition of the vCA1-to-NAc shell pathway reduces social interaction with novel conspecifics, indicating that this circuit promotes social approach under conditions of social novelty (Patterson et al. 2025). In addition, the vHPC to NAc pathway is also important for affective regulation. Activation of VHPC to NAc shell is sufficient to promote conditioned place preference and is required for encoding contextual reward associated with social interaction (LeGates et al. 2018). Consistent with this, stress-induced plasticity in this circuit has been shown to causally drive maladaptive behaviors such as anhedonia and passive coping, underscoring the role in motivation and emotional context (Pignatelli et al. 2021). In addition, vHPC to NAc signaling has been implicated in promoting positively motivated social behaviors in stress models (Bagot et al. 2015b; Muir et al. 2020).

Collectively, these findings support a functional dissociation in which dCA1 inputs to NAc directly promote social interaction, whereas the vCA1-to-NAc pathway primarily modulates social behavior in memory and context-dependent processes, such as social discrimination, reward-context encoding, and stress-dependent regulation. This dissociation highlights complementary roles for dorsal and ventral hippocampal projections to the NAc in shaping adaptive behavior.

Interestingly, inhibition of the dCA1-to-NAc pathway did not reduce social interaction in our study, unlike prior work showing that similar terminal inhibition disrupts conditioned place preference, which was interpreted as limiting appetitive memory (Trouche et al. 2019). The same NpHR optogenetic protocol produced a clear behavioral effect when applied to the dCA1-to-LS pathway (Fig. 7); we are reluctant to conclude that the lack of a change in behavior with inhibition of the dCA1-to-NAc pathway is a technical issue. It may be the result of a floor effect of circuit-level manipulation or reflect task- or state-dependent recruitment of dCA1-to-NAc circuitry. Notably, this type of asymmetry (activation produces a robust behavioral effect, but inhibition has minimal impact) has been described in the limbic system previously. In particular, increases and decreases in VTA dopamine neuron population activities are controlled by separate limbic routes, where increased dopaminergic activity is contingent on ventral hippocampal output while decreases in dopaminergic activity depend on the basolateral amygdala (Floresco et al. 2001; Patton et al. 2013; Chang and Grace 2014). Similarly, our findings suggest that dCA1-to-NAc signaling may be sufficient to promote social interaction when activated, but reducing social interaction may require the recruitment of additional circuitry. Future experiments that combine dCA to NAc inhibition with manipulations of candidate down-regulatory nodes (such as the amygdala) could directly test this possibility.

Our initial hypothesis was that both dCA1-to-NAc and -to-LS outputs would regulate social interaction, given extensive evidence linking these downstream structures in social interaction. Unexpectedly, optogenetic stimulation of dCA1-to-LS projections did not affect social investigation in our home cage assay, but robustly and bidirectionally influenced locomotor behavior (Figs 6 and 7), indicating that the dCA1-to-LS pathway plays an important role in regulating locomotor drive. Importantly, despite strong locomotor modulation, social investigation itself was unchanged, suggesting locomotor drive and social investigation can be dissociated under our conditions. This finding is consistent with prior work showing that theta oscillation regulates running speed via the hippocampus and septal pathway (Risold and Swanson 1997b; Sheehan et al. 2004; Bender et al. 2015; Wirtshafter and Wilson 2019). Our study extends this network-level observation by directly activating dCA1 terminals in the LS with optogenetics and shows that dCA1 to LS constitutes a functionally distinct pathway for locomotor activity, without affecting social interaction. In contrast, ventral hippocampal projections to LS have been implicated in social novelty preference and affective state regulation. For example, Rashid et al. identified that the vHPC to LS pathway is necessary for preferential investigation of a novel conspecific over a familiar mouse (Rashid et al. 2024). This circuit is necessary for appetitive goal-directed spatial memory (Décarie-Spain et al. 2022) and has also been shown to bidirectionally regulate anxiety-like behavior (Parfitt et al. 2017), supporting the idea that vHPC inputs to LS regulate affective state and motivationally relevant behavior. Together with our findings, these results point to functional diversity across the hippocampal longitudinal axis, with dHPC to LS outputs preferentially influencing locomotor state while vHPC to LS outputs more strongly engage social novelty and affective state computations.

Our optogenetic approach to interrogating the function of dCA1 terminal fibers carries some intrinsic limitations. Viral strategies may introduce potential bias, such as virus spread and retrograde efficacy, and our intersectional reporter may underestimate the abundance of dual-projecting neurons. Behaviorally, we focused on spontaneous social interaction in the home cage, novel object exploration, open field locomotion, and TST, and we used male mice only so sex as a biological variable was not assessed. Although there are many tests of social behavior and cognition, we evaluated a limited number with which we have extensive experience. A comprehensive assessment of circuit function would ideally include simultaneous neuronal recordings during behavior (not performed here). Future studies using projection-specific fiber photometry would clarify how dCA1-to-NAc and dCA1-to-LS populations encode social versus locomotor context in vivo, refining our understanding of how hippocampal outputs organize complex behavior.

In summary, dCA1 projections to the NAc and LS regulate distinct behaviors. Activating the dCA1-to-NAc pathway promotes social interaction, whereas inhibiting it does not reduce social interaction. On the other hand, the dCA1-to-LS pathway bidirectionally regulates locomotion, with activation enhancing and inhibition suppressing locomotor activity.
