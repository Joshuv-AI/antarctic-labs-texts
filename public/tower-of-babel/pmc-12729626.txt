Genome-based classification of pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma reveals similarities to KRAS wild-type PDAC
Authors: Mengyuan Liu, Kenneth Seier, Mithat Gonen, Carlie Sigel, Joslyn Jung, Alex B Blair, Thomas C Howell, Diane Reidy-Lagunes, Michael E Lidsky, Peter J Allen, Dan G Blazer III, Sabino Zani, Alice C Wei, Jeffrey A Drebin, Vinod Balachandran, Michael D’Angelica, Kevin C Soares, T Peter Kingham, William R Jarnagin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (ACC) is a rare primary neoplasm of acinar cell origin. Histologically, it can present as pure ACC or mixed with neuroendocrine or ductal differentiation. We studied whether ACC shared genomic similarities with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) or well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNET) using the hidden genome classifier methodology.MethodsThe hidden genome classifier was trained using next-generation sequencing of KRAS wild-type (KRAS-WT) PDAC (n = 251), KRAS-mutated (KRAS-mut) PDAC (n = 1872) and PNET (n = 127). We applied the classifier to 62 primary and metastatic ACC samples from two institutions to identify the genomic class and its relationship to histologic differentiation and clinical outcomes.ResultsAccording to the classifier, KRAS mutations and tumor mutation burden contributed to the KRAS-mut PDAC class, while ATRX, VHL and SETD2 mutations contributed to the PNET class. Copy number alterations (CNA) in chromosomes 1q and 11q were key factors in the genomic classification of KRAS-WT PDAC. Most ACC (49/62) aligned genomically with KRAS-WT PDAC. Fifteen ACCs had mixed histology, and the majority of those (11/15) also classified as KRAS-WT PDAC. CNA were present in most ACC tumors, even those without mutations in DNA damage repair or chromatin modification pathways. The predicted genomic class was not associated with survival outcomes.ConclusionsThe hidden genome classifier found most ACC shares genomic similarities with KRAS-WT PDAC, independent of ductal or neuroendocrine differentiation. This may reflect a common cell of origin in ACC tumorigenesis and explain the improved clinical outcome compared to KRAS-mut PDAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07381-7.Keywords: Pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma, Hidden genome classifier, KRAS wild-type pancreatic adenocarcinoma, Chromosomal instability

Background

Pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (ACC) is a rare primary neoplasm of the exocrine pancreas with unclear oncogenic driver [1]. ACC can exhibit substantial ductal or neuroendocrine differentiation on histology [2, 3], yet this does not appear to contribute to clinical outcomes or chemotherapy sensitivity [4]. Similar to pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC), almost half of ACC (40%) presents with metastatic disease. Current treatment also mirrors that of PDAC with predominantly fluoropyridine and platinum-based chemotherapy [5], though biologically, ACC exhibits extended survival compared to PDAC in general. A recent ACC cohort was reported to have a median overall survival (OS) of 73 months after surgical resection and it included patients with oligometastatic disease [6].

Prior studies have identified high tumor mutation burden (TMB) and chromosome alterations in 11p as hallmarks of ACC [7–9]. A recent study showed that genes implicated in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) and DNA damage repair (DDR), such as BRCA1/2, PALB2, ATM, and CHECK2, are altered in up to 37% of ACC [6, 10]. ACC is not known to share mutational profiles with PDAC, 90% of which contain mutations in KRAS [11], nor pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNET), which commonly carry alterations in MEN1, ATRX and DAXX [12]. Instead, the most common alterations in ACC are SMAD4 mutations, occurring in 25% of tumors [7, 9, 13], followed by alterations in Wnt signaling (i.e. APC/CTNBB1, 9–24%) and Ras/Raf signaling (i.e. BRAF, 13–25%). These pathways overlap with those found in KRAS wild-type (KRAS-WT) PDAC [11]. Clinically, KRAS-WT PDAC has better outcomes than KRAS-mutated (KRAS-mut) PDAC. After resection, the median OS of KRAS-WT PDAC is 91 months compared to 39 months for KRAS-mut PDAC [14].

Previously, we developed and reported on a hidden genomic classifier methodology [15, 16] to understand the heterogeneity of ampullary cancers and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [17–19]. This statistical tool analyzes genomic variations through multilevel meta-feature regression to determine similarities among cancer types that may not be obvious solely based on histology or common mutations. The hidden genome classifier uses the assumption that the somatic mutations acquired in a tumor depend on its developmental origin, so tumors that share genomic context also share lineage. The classifier determines genomic context from sequencing data of known tumor subtypes (KRAS-mut PDAC, KRAS-WT PDAC, PNET) and then applies that statistical model to unknown tumor subtypes (ACC). The chromosomal landscape of ACC, containing multiple amplifications and deletions [1, 20], is suitable for this type of analysis as it takes into consideration large volumes of genomic alterations. The primary aim of this study was to assess a potential genomic basis for the histologic heterogeneity observed in ACC and whether genomic or histologic subtypes influence clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients and tissue

Patients with histologically confirmed ACC treated at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC, n = 53) and Duke University (n = 7) from 1999–2022 were identified from institutional databases. Targeted next generation sequencing (NGS) was routinely performed as part of clinical practice using MSK-Integrated Molecular Profiling of Actional Cancer Target (IMPACT) [21], a validated platform for detection of selected mutations, copy number alterations (CNA) and some rearrangements. In cases where no IMPACT was available, we obtained micro-dissected tumor tissue (by a pathologist) from sectioned formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks (FFPE) and ran IMPACT on the extracted DNA (MagMAX FFPE DNA/RNA Ultra Kit, ThermoFisher). Whole exome sequencing (WES) on one matched primary and metastatic sample was also performed with extracted DNA from FFPE blocks and ran on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) through the MSKCC Integrated Genomics Operations. The samples were sequenced to an average depth of 132X. The MSKCC Bioinformatic core performed alignment using FACETS (Fraction and Allele-Specific Copy Number Estimate from Tumor Sequencing) analysis [22].

A senior pancreas pathologist (CS) reviewed all slides to determine the histologic classification of pure acinar, mixed acinar-ductal or mixed acinar-neuroendocrine (NEC) differentiation based on WHO guidelines where the tumor must contain > 30% mixed differentiation [23]. Clinical and outcomes data were reviewed from medical records with patient consent and IRB-approval.

Classifier

We trained two hidden-genome classifier models: 1) a two-class model based on 2,123 PDAC and 127 PNET (well-differentiated G1 and G2) specimens; 2) a three-class model that separated PDAC into KRAS-mut PDAC (n = 1,872) or KRAS-WT PDAC (n = 251), using IMPACT data collected at our institution. The statistical methodology for prediction model was previously described [17]. The model predicts a probability of a genomic class based on a group-lasso regularized multinominal logistic regression that considers gene variants, copy number ratios and total tumor mutational burden as predicators. Features were screened using mutual information. The hidden genome classifier was then fitted on this cohort of ACC patients by calculating a predicted class probability and assigning a genomic class if the highest probability for that class was 0.5. If no class reached the threshold of 0.5, the sample was classified as “indeterminate.”

Statistics

Summary statistics, counts and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. Median and interquartile range IQR were used to describe continuous variables. Overall survival (OS) of primary tumors was determined from the date of surgery while OS of metastatic tumors was determined from the date of biopsy until death or patients were censored at last follow up. For matched tumors, only results from the primary tumor was considered for survival analysis. OS was estimated with Kaplan-Meier methods and compared with log-rank test. All tests were two sided with p < 0.05 deemed significant. SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute) or R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for all analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics and outcomes

The study cohort consisted of 59 patients and 62 ACC tumors, including 47 pure ACC (76%), 5 ACC-ductal (8%), and 10 ACC-NEC (16%) tumors. There were 33 primary and 29 metastatic tumors, 3 of which were matched pairs (Table 1). No differences were observed between the histologic distributions of primary and metastatic tumors. With a median follow up of 46.8 (IQR 25.6–63.7) months, 70% (CI 47%-85%) of the primary and 55% (CI 32%-74%) of the metastatic ACC patients were alive at three years (Fig. 1).

Subtyping of pancreatic tumors using a genomic classifier

The first genomic classifier was built using PDAC and PNET data (Fig. S1A). The PDAC classification was associated with mutations in KRAS, TP53, CNA and TMB. The PNET classification was associated with mutations in ATRX, VHL and SETD2. Most ACC tumors (56/62) classified as PDAC based on genomic features (Fig. S1B), regardless of their histologic classification. Only one KRAS mutation was identified in the ACC cohort, suggesting that the classifier found additional genomic similarities between PDAC and ACC. To understand the contribution of the KRAS mutation, we retrained the classifier by dividing the PDAC class into KRAS-WT PDAC and KRAS-mut PDAC (Fig. 2). The KRAS-mut PDAC class continued to be influenced by TMB and KRAS mutations, while the KRAS-WT PDAC class was influenced by CNA, particularly on chromosomes 1q and 11q.

Genomic classifier aligns ACC with KRAS WT PDAC

Using the three-class model (KRAS-WT PDAC, KRAS-mut PDAC, and PNET), most ACC tumors (49/62) classified as KRAS-WT PDAC, including 7/10 ACC-NEC and 4/5 ACC-ductal mixed histology tumors (Fig. 3). Among the remaining 13 ACC tumors, 5 classified as PNET, 3 as KRAS-mut PDAC, and 5 as indeterminate. One ACC-NEC was grouped with the PNET genomic class (Fig. 4A) and one ACC-ductal fell into the KRAS-mut PDAC genomic class, which was the tumor that harbored the sole KRAS mutation identified in this cohort. Despite these genomic classes, the predicted classification did not correlate with specific oncogenic mutations or altered signaling pathways (Fig. 4B). For example, Wnt pathway alterations were observed in KRAS-WT PDAC, KRAS-mut PDAC, and PNET groups.

In primary tumors, neither the genomic nor the histologic classification significantly impacted overall survival (Fig. S2). The 3-year OS rates by genomic class were 71% (42%–87%) for KRAS-WT PDAC, 100% (NE–NE) for KRAS-mut PDAC, 67% (5%–95%) for PNET, and 50% (1%–91%) for the indeterminate group (p = 0.902). When examined by histology, the 3-year OS was 65% (37%–83%) for ACC, 50% (1–91%) for ACC-ductal, and 100% (NE–NE) for ACC-NEC (p = 0.723).

Copy number alterations (CNA) played a major role in defining the KRAS-WT PDAC genomic class (Fig. 5A). Specifically, changes at chromosomes 11q21, 11q22.1, and 1q23.1 contributed to the classification of ACC as KRAS-WT PDAC. Notably, one patient with a fusion involving NTRK1 (located on chromosome 1q23.1) received an NTRK1 inhibitor [24, 25]. Two patients with mutations in ATRX were still classified as KRAS-WT PDAC rather than PNET, likely because the contribution of CNA outweighed the presence of these mutations in the model. Ten tumors had high tumor mutation burden (TMB; > 10 mutations/mbp), but only 3 classified as KRAS-mut PDAC and the other 7 high TMB tumors were assigned to KRAS-WT PDAC, further underscoring the importance of CNA in the multi-level regression that predicted the KRAS-WT PDAC genomic class. In three of these high-TMB cases, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were evident (Fig. 5B).

Chromosome instability in ACC

There were three matched pairs of primary and metastatic ACC in our cohort. In two pairs, both the primary tumor and metastasis were predicted to be in the KRAS-WT PDAC genomic class (Fig. 3). In the third pair, however, the primary fell into the KRAS-WT PDAC class but the metastasis from 5 years later was classified as KRAS-mut PDAC (Fig. S3A). The metastasis had an increase in TMB, which may have accounted for this reclassification, as well as the presence of TILs. Only one mutation was shared by the primary and metastatic samples (KMT5A) and the metastasis gained a BRCA2 mutation. To examine the genomic evolution in detail, we performed WES on this matched pair. FACETS analysis of the primary and metastatic tumor samples revealed extensive copy number gains and losses across all chromosomes (Fig. S3B). The metastasis had an overall ploidy of 4.1, revealing a hyper-diploid genome driven by copy number gains.

In the matched case, the metastasis acquired a BRCA2 mutation, a known driver of HRD [10], so we evaluated its potential contribution to chromosomal instability. The fraction of the genome altered (FGA) was compared to the presence of HRD or chromatin-modifying mutations e.g., ARID1A [1] (Fig. S4). Patients without these mutations still exhibited substantial copy number changes, suggesting that DNA repair and chromatin accessibility pathways are not the sole drivers of chromosomal instability in ACC.

Discussion

In this study, we applied the hidden genome classifier to an ACC cohort and uncovered molecular alignments between ACC and KRAS-WT PDAC. Despite the various histologic differentiations of ACC—pure acinar, acinar-ductal, or acinar-neuroendocrine—most ACC shared genomic features such as copy number alterations on chromosomes 1q and 11q. This similarity to KRAS-WT PDAC rather than KRAS-mut PDAC may account for the improved survival of ACC [6], with most patients alive at 3 years, even with metastatic disease.

In the current study, only one ACC-neuroendocrine and one ACC-ductal tumor were classified genomically as PNET and KRAS-mut PDAC, respectively. In a mouse model investigating the cell of origin in pancreatic cancer, the KRAS mutation was shown to drive ductal differentiation - a pattern also observed in the one KRAS-mutated ACC in our cohort [26]. Most mixed phenotypes in this study (11/15) still classified as KRAS-WT PDAC, which may reflect lineage plasticity arising from a common cell of origin during tumorigenesis. In the one paired primary and metastatic sample, the tumor evolved from genomically KRAS-WT PDAC to KRAS-mut PDAC without the addition of a KRAS mutation, while exhibiting hyperploidy and increased TMB. These genomic characteristics may be more informative than the histologic subtypes in predicting outcomes, but we currently do not have a sufficient sample size for a definitive conclusion in this regard.

Copy number alterations defined these ACC samples but were not associated with a particular mutation or amplification. While HRD genes, specifically BRCA2, are prevalent in ACC [10, 20], they are not the sole driving force behind CNA in these tumors. Mutations in chromatin modifiers such as KMT2D or ARID1A [1] are also found in ACC, but do not account for all the CNA either. Instead, most ACC classified as KRAS-WT PDAC due to CNA on chromosomes 1q and 11q. Amplification of chromosome 1q was previously found to be characteristic of KRAS-WT PDAC in an independent cohort and was also a feature that was shared between KRAS-WT PDAC and cholangiocarcinoma [27]. These genomic similarities may support the use of targeted therapies in ACC since inhibiting tyrosine kinase activity and the Ras/Raf pathways in KRAS-WT PDAC has had some success [28] and these pathways are also activated in ACC [9, 29]. There is one recent case report of response of BRAFV600E- mutated ACC treated successfully with a MEK inhibitor [30].

We identified only one mismatch deficient ACC tumor, though 10/62 (16%) tumors had high TMB ( > 10 mutations/mbp), which is the threshold for consideration of immune checkpoint blockade [31]. Altogether, six patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors in our cohort, of which only one had high TMB and stable disease for over two years on pembrolizumab. The rest received immunotherapy late in the disease course without high TMB and had no response. There is one case report of mismatch-deficient ACC treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy in the literature that had a clinical complete response [32]. Previously, tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were reported in a subset of mismatch deficient ACC [33] as well as early-onset KRAS-WT PDAC [34], in contrast to the microenvironment of pancreatic cancer, which is generally considered to be lymphocyte-depleted [35]. We also found TILs in our cohort, even those without mismatch deficiency, suggesting that immunotherapy may have a role for this subtype of pancreatic cancer. Based on the presence of TILs and high TMB, we had subsequently treated one patient with metastatic ACC using single agent pembrolizumab and had observed radiologic response (Fig. S5).

Our study has some limitations, predominately the small sample size that is reflective of a rare cancer. We had limited samples with mixed histologies and in the non-KRAS-WT PDAC genomic classes, which underpower our ability to make conclusions about their relationship to survival outcomes. We only had 3 matched primary and metastatic ACC tumors, so it is not possible to draw conclusions on tumor evolution and future studies are needed. Future multi-institution collaborations may overcome this sample power limitation. In the mixed histologic differentiation samples, we derived DNA from whole tissue sections, which does not deconvolute the genomic heterogeneity of cells displaying different histologic phenotypes. Single cell technology may be able to detail the relationship between histology and clonal genomic heterogeneity [36]. Aside from HRD and chromatin modification genes, mutations that are not currently captured by the IMPACT platform may also be contributing to the CNA that we observed in ACC. As it stands currently, this ACC genomic classifier was built from IMPACT data and used IMPACT data as input, so the depth of sequencing is limited and not as robust as WES. Future studies may explore building an ACC genomic classifier with alternative sequencing platforms.

In summary, our results indicate that ACC, despite histologic heterogeneity, shares key genomic features with KRAS-WT PDAC, particularly due to copy number alterations on chromosomes 1q and 11q. Actionable mutations and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes highlight potential for targeted and immunotherapeutic approaches.
