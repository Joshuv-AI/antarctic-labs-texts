Extreme value statistics in a continuous time branching process: a pedagogical primer
Satya N. Majumdar and Alberto Rosso

Abstract
We study a continuous time branching process where an individual splits into two daughters with rate b b and dies with rate a a , starting from a single individual at t = 0 t=0 . We show that the model can be mapped exactly to a random walk problem where the population size N ⁡ ( t ) N(t) performs a random walk on a positive semi-infinite lattice. The hopping rate of this random walker out of a site labelled n n is proportional to n n , i.e., the walker gets more and more ‘active’ or ‘agitated’ as it moves further and further away from the origin–we call this an ‘agitated random walk’ (ARW). We demonstrate that this random walk problem is particularly suitable to obtain exact explicit results on the extreme value statistics, namely, on the distribution of the maximal population size M ⁡ ( t ) = max 0 ​ τ ≤ t ⁡ [ N ⁡ ( τ ) ] M(t)=\max_{0\tau\leq t}[N(\tau)] up to time t t . This extreme value distribution displays markedly different behaviors in the three phases: (i) subcritical ( b < a b<a ) (ii) critical ( b = a b=a ) and (iii) supercritical ( b > a ) (b>a) . In the subcritical phase, Q ⁡ ( L , t ) Q(L,t) becomes independent of time t t for large t t and the stationary distribution Q ⁡ ( L , ∞ ) Q(L,\infty) decays exponentially with increasing L L . At the critical point b = a b=a , the distribution again becomes independent of t t for large t t , but the stationary distribution Q ⁡ ( L , ∞ ) ∼ 1 / L 2 Q(L,\infty)\sim 1/L^{2} has a power law tail for large L L . For finite but large t t , the distribution at the critical point exhibits a scaling form Q ⁡ ( L , t ) ∼ f c ​ ( L / a ​ t ) / L 2 Q(L,t)\sim f_{c}(L/{at})/L^{2} where the scaling function f c ​ ( z ) f_{c}(z) has a nontrivial shape that we compute analytically. In the supercritical phase, the distribution Q ⁡ ( L , t ) Q(L,t) has a ‘fluid’ part that becomes independent of t t for large t t and a ‘condensate’ part (a delta peak centered at e ( b − a ) ​ t e^{(b-a)t} ) which gets disconnected from the ‘fluid’ part and moves rapidly to ∞ \infty as time increases. We also verify our analytical predictions via numerical simulations finding excellent agreement. Our results can be applied to characterize the time dependence of the size of the maximally infected population up to a fixed time during an epidemic spread.

Consider a bacterial colony that starts with a single bacteria at

The bacteria can diffuse and also reproduce through binary fission, i.e., it undergoes

diffuse and reproduce independently via binary fissions. This leads to an exponential growth
of the pupulation size in the colony. However, a bacterial cell can also die due

stochastic process where both the population size

Characterizing the behaviors of these random variables provides important information

via the following simple rules. In a small time interval
\Delta t

With probability

With probability

With the complementary probability

Thus this simple model has three parameters

It is useful to summarize our main results for the distribution of maximum

subcritical phase (

II
Population size distribution of a branching process with death

The process starts with a single bacteria at
t=0
. Let
N(t)

denote the population size of the bacterial colony at time
t
. Clearly,
N(t)
is
a random variable that fluctuates from one realization of the process to another. We are interested
in computing the size distribution
P(n,t)={\rm Prob.}[N(t)=n]
, i.e.,
the probability that the colony has size
n
at time
t
. As emphasised earlier, the spatial diffusion
plays no role on the population size distribution and hence, the dynamics of
P(n,t)
is controlled
by only two parameters: the branching rate
b
and the death rate
a
.
There are two alternative methods to derive
P(n,t)
exactly: the first by employing a backward Fokker-Planck
approach in Section
II.1
and the second by mapping the process to an auxiliary lattice
random walk problem that can be subsequently solved exactly as illustrated in Section
II.2
.
Finally in Section
II.3
we discuss the continuous space limit of the lattice random walk problem and show that for
b<a
it reduces
to a special case of the Feller process.

II.1
Size distribution via a backward Fokker-Planck approach

This derivation was presented in Ref.
[
16
]
. Here we reproduce
it for the sake of completeness. In the “backward” method, one considers
the time interval
[0,t+\Delta t]
and splits it into two subintervals:

[0,\Delta t]
and
[\Delta t,t+\Delta t]
. There are three possibilities
in the first time interval
[0,\Delta t]
: (i) with probability
b\Delta t
,
the initial particle at
t=0
splits into two daughter particles, (ii) with
probability
a\Delta t
the initial particle dies and (iii) with probability

1-(1+b)\Delta t
, the initial particle does nothing. Let
P(n,t+\Delta t)

denote the probability of having
n
particles at time
t+\Delta t
, starting
from a single particle at
t=0
.
Then considering the three events that happen in
[0,\Delta t]
and
the subsequent evolution over the second interval
[\Delta t,t+\Delta t]
,
we can write the evolution equation for
P(n,t)

P(n,t+\Delta t)=\left[1-(a+b)\,\Delta t\right]\,P(n,t)+b\,\Delta t\,\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}P(n_{1},t)\,P(n_{2},t)\,\delta_{n+1+n_{2},n}+a\,\Delta t\,\delta_{n,0}\,.

(5)

Here
\delta_{m,n}
is the Kronecker delta function which is
1
if
m=n

and is zero otherwise.
The first term in Eq. (
5
) corresponds to the event (iii) when nothing happens in

[0,\Delta t]
and hence the subsequent evolution from
\Delta t
to

t+\Delta t
of time interval
t
must be
P(n,t)
. The second term
corresponds to the branching event (ii) where two independent copies of
the process starts at
\Delta t
each with a single particle. The total
number of particles in the colony at time
t+\Delta t
is the sum of the
population sizes from each of these daughters. Similarly, the third term
corresponds to the event (iii) when the initial particle dies, and hence
contribute only to the event of zero population size
n=0
at time

t+\Delta t
. This method is called ‘backward’, since we write down
the recursion equation by considering events in the first interval
[0,\Delta t]
at the begining of the history of evolution.
Taking the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, gives the
nonlinea
r evolution equation

\partial_{t}P(n,t)=-(a+b)\,P(n,t)+b\,\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}P(n_{1},t)P(n_{2},t)\,\delta_{n_{1}+n_{2},n}+a\,\delta_{n,0}\,,\quad n\geq 0

(6)

This equation starts from the initial condition

P(n,0)=\delta_{n,1}\,.

(7)

To solve Eqs. (
6
), we introduce the generating function

\hat{P}(z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t)\,z^{n}\,.

(8)

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
6
) by
z^{n}
and summing
over all
n\geq 0
, we get a first order differential equation

\partial_{t}\hat{P}(z,t)=[1-\hat{P}(z,t)]\,[a-b\,\hat{P}(z,t)]\,,

(9)

starting from the initial condition

\hat{P}(z,0)=z\,,

(10)

which simply follows from Eq. (
7
). Eq. (
9
)
can be trivially solved, giving

\hat{P}(z,t)=\frac{(a-bz)\,e^{(a-b)t}-a\,(1-z)}{(a-bz)e^{(a-b)t}-b(1-z)}

(11)

Clearly, for
z=1
, we get
\hat{P}(1,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t)=1
, ensuring
the normalization.
Expanding in powers of
z
, one gets the exact distribution of the
population size at any time
t

\displaystyle P(n,t)=\begin{cases}&\frac{(b-a)^{2}e^{(a-b)t}}{\left[b-a\,e^{(a-b)t}\right]^{2}}\,\left[\frac{b(1-e^{(a-b)t}}{b-a\,e^{(a-b)t}}\right]^{n-1}\,\quad{\rm for}\quad n\geq 1\\
&\\
&\frac{a\,\left(1-e^{(a-b)t}\right)}{b-a\,e^{(a-b)\,t}}\,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,{\rm for}\quad n=0\,.\end{cases}

(12)

For the special case
b=a
, by taking the limit
b\to a
in
Eq. (
12
) one gets

\displaystyle P(n,t)\Big|_{b=a}=\begin{cases}&\frac{1}{(1+a\,t)^{2}}\,\left[\frac{at}{1+at}\right]^{n-1}\quad{\rm for}\quad n\geq 1\\
&\\
&\frac{at}{1+at}\,,\quad\quad\quad\quad{\rm for}\quad n=0\,.\end{cases}

(13)

The exact results in Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) are shown in Fig.
2
and are in excellent agreement with numerical simulations.

decays
exponentially at late times

P(n,t)\approx\left(1-\frac{b}{a}\right)^{2}\,e^{-(a-b)t}\,\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{n-1}\,

(15)

while

P(0,t\to\infty)\approx 1\,.

(16)

Thus, in this subcritical phase, the colony
becomes extinct exponentially fast
with time.

•

Critical
phase
b=a
. In this case, from Eq. (
13
),
one gets for large
n

and large
t

P(n,t)\approx\frac{1}{a\,t}\,\frac{1}{n_{\rm c}(t)}\,e^{-n/n_{\rm c}(t)}\,,

(17)

where

n_{\rm c}(t)\approx a\,t\,.

(18)

Thus in this case, the size distribution again has an exponential tail
over a characteristic size
n_{\rm c}(t)\sim at
that grows much slower,
as linearly, with increasing
t
, in contrast to the exponential growth
in the supercritical phase. In this case, it follows from
Eq. (
13
) that at late times

P(0,t\to\infty)=1\,.

(19)

Thus, in the critical case, with probability
1
the pupulation
eventually becomes extinct.

•

Supercritical
phase
b>a
. In this case, one can express

P(n,t)
in Eq. (
12
), for large
n
and large
t
, in the form

P(n,t)\approx\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)\,\frac{1}{n_{\rm sc}(t)}\,e^{-n/n_{\rm sc}(t)}\,,

(20)

where

n_{\rm sc}(t)=\frac{b}{(b-a)}\,e^{(b-a)t}\,.

(21)

Thus the size distribution has an exponential tail with a characteristic
size
n_{\rm sc}(t)
in Eq. (
21
) that grows exponentially with time.
In contrast, for
n=0
one gets at late times

P(0,t\to\infty)=\frac{a}{b}

(22)

In fact, integrating Eq. (
20
) over all
n\geq 1
, one
gets
\sum_{n\geq 1}P(n,t)\approx 1-a/b
, which exactly
complements
P(0,t\to\infty)=a/b
.
Thus, in the supercritical phase
b>a
,
the population becomes extinct
with a finite probability
a/b<1
, while with the complementary probability

(1-a/b)
the size grows exponentially.

Remark:
Let us make a side remark which will be useful later.
In the supercritical phase (
b>a
), the exact size distribution

P(n,t)
in Eq. (
12
), as a function of
t
for fixed large
n
,
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P(n\geq 1,t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}\,e^{-(b-a)t}\,\left[1-\frac{b-a}{b}\,e^{-(b-a)t}\right]^{n-1}

(23)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}\,\exp\left[-(b-a)t-n\frac{(b-a)}{b}\,e^{-(b-a)t}\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}\,\exp\left[-(b-a)\,t-e^{-(b-a)\,\left(t-\frac{\ln\left(n\,(b-a)/b\right)}{(b-a)}\right)}\right]\,.

Thus, as a function of
t
for fixed but large
n
, the distribution

P(n,t)
has a Gumbel form with a peak at
t=t^{*}\approx\ln(n)/(b-a)
.

II.2
An alternative random walk representation

The model of the branching with death, described and solved above, can
also be solved using an alternative random walk representation. This
random walk representation turns out to be useful to compute some other
observables in the model, such as the maximum population size up to time

t
, as we will demonstrate later. To derive this alternative representation,
suppose we have
n
bacteria at time
t
, i.e.,
N(t)=n
. In a small time
\Delta t
, each
of these
n
bacteria attempts to split into two daughters independently.
The probability that
m
out of
n
will split is
clearly given by
{n\choose m}(b\Delta t)^{m}(1-b\,\Delta t)^{n-m}
.
Hence to leading order in
\Delta t
as
\Delta t\to 0
, only one out of
n

will split with probability
n\,b\,\Delta t
. Thus with this probability
the population size will increase by
1
, i.e.,
N(t)\to N(t)+1
.
Similarly, to leading order
in
\Delta t
, only

1
out of
n
will die with probability
n\,a\Delta t
, i.e.,

N(t)\to N(t)-1
. Consequently,
the random variable
N(t)
has the following continuous time dynamics

\displaystyle N(t+\Delta t)=\begin{cases}&N(t)+1\quad{\rm with\,\,prob.}\quad b\,N(t)\,\Delta t\\
&\\
&N(t)-1\quad{\rm with\,\,prob.}\quad a\,N(t)\,\Delta t\\
&\\
&N(t)\quad{\rm with\,\,prob.}\quad 1-(a+b)\,N(t)\,\Delta t\end{cases}

(24)

These transition rates hold for all
n\geq 1
. Note, however, that the
state
n=0
is special. It is an ‘absorbing’ state. Once the system
reaches
n=0
, it just stays there with probability
1
. Thus, one
can represent this population size
N(t)
as the position of a random walker on a semi-infinite lattice
whose sites are labelled by
n=0,1,2,\ldots
. A random walker, representing
the population size, hops on this lattice starting from the site
n=1

at
t=0
. From a site labelled
n
, the walker hops to the site
n+1

with probability
b\,n\,\Delta t
(corresponding to branching),
hops to the site
n-1
with probability
a\,n\,\Delta t
(corresponding to natural
death) and
stays at site
n
with the complementary probability

1-(a+b)\,n\,\Delta t
. The origin
n=0
is an ‘absorbing’ site
(see Fig. (
3
)). The dynamics stops once the walker reaches the site
n=0
for the first time.

Figure 3:
A random walker (green) moving on a semi-infinite lattice
whose sites are labelled as
n=0,1,2,3\ldots
. The position of the walker
n

represents the population size. The walker moves in continuous time with
a site dependent transition rates. In a small time
\Delta t
,
the walker at site
n
hops to the right neighbour
n+1
with probability

b\,n\,\Delta t
,
to the left neighbour
n-1
with probability
a\,n\,\Delta t
and
stays at site
n

with probability
1-(a+b)\,n\,\Delta t
. The site
n=0
(shown in red)
is an absorber, i.e., the dynamics stops once the walker reaches the
site
n=0
.

One can then write a forward Fokker-Planck equation for the population size
distribution
P(n,t)
which just represents the probability that the random walker is
at site
n
at time
t
, starting from site
1
at
t=0
.
Imagine evolving the time from
t
to
t+\Delta t
. Then
P(n,t+\Delta t)

can be written for all
n\geq 1

P(n,t+\Delta t)=a\,(n+1)\,\Delta tP(n+1,t)+b\,(n-1)\,\Delta t\,P(n-1,t)+\left[1-(a+b)\,\Delta t\right]\,P(n,t)\,.

(25)

In order to arrive at
n
at time
t+\Delta t
,
the walker at time
t
must be either at site
n+1
(from which it hops
to site
n
with prob.
a\,(n+1)\,\Delta t
), or at site
n-1
(from which
it hops to site
n
with probability
b\,(n-1)\,\Delta t
, or
at site
n
where it stays with probability
1-(a+b)\,n\,\Delta t
–
explaining the three terms on the right hand side (rhs) of Eq. (
25
).
This is called ‘forward’ since we consider the evolution from time
t
to time
t+\Delta t
. Taking the limit
\Delta t\to 0
in Eq. (
25
),
we arrive at a
linear
equation for
P(n,t)

\partial_{t}P(n,t)=-(a+b)\,n\,P(n,t)+b\,(n-1)\,P(n-1,t)+a\,(n+1)\,P(n+1,t)\,.

(26)

Note that this equation also holds for
n=0
, provided we interpret

P(-1,t)=0
. There is no outgoing current from the site
n=0
, but
there is an ingoing current from the
site
1
. Eq. (
26
) starts from the initial condition

P(n,0)=\delta_{n,1}\,.

(27)

Note that Eq. (
26
) is linear in
P(n,t)
, while
Eq. (
6
) is
nonlinear
in
P(n,t)
. We will see shortly
that both equations yield the same solution for
P(n,t)
as it should.
The linear Eq. (
27
) has some advantages as we will see later,
however the price we pay is that the transition rates depend on the
site index
n
.

To solve Eq. (
26
), we again define the generating function
as in Eq. (
8
), namely

\hat{P}(z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t)\,z^{n}\,.

(28)

Let us note the identity

z\,\partial_{z}\hat{P}(z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,P(n,t)\,z^{n}\,.

(29)

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
26
) by
z^{n}
and summing over all

n\geq 0
(using
P(-1,t)=0
and the identity in Eq. (
29
)),
it is easy to show that
\hat{P}(z,t)
satisfies the first order
equation

\partial_{t}\hat{P}(z,t)=(1-z)\,(a-b\,z)\,\partial_{z}\hat{P}(z,t)\,,

(30)

starting from the initial condition

\hat{P}(z,t=0)=z\,.

(31)

The general solution of a first-order equation of the type (
30
)
can be expressed as a travelling front (using the method of characteristics)

\hat{P}(z,t)=F\left(t+f(z)\right)\,,

(32)

where both
f(z)
and
F(u)
are yet to be fixed. Substituting the
anticipated solution (
32
) in Eq. (
30
), one
immediately gets a first-order differential equation for
f(z)
(while
the function
F(u)
is still arbitrary)

\frac{df}{dz}=\frac{1}{(1-z)(a-b\,z)}\,.

(33)

The general solution of Eq. (
33
) is given by

f(z)=\frac{1}{a-b}\,\ln\left(\frac{(a-b\,z)}{1-z}\right)+C_{0}\,,

(34)

where
C_{0}
is an arbitrary constant. Substituting this solution for
f(z)

in Eq. (
32
), we then get the general solution in the form

\hat{P}(z,t)=F\left(t+C_{0}+\frac{1}{a-b}\,\ln\left(\frac{(a-b\,z)}{1-z}\right)\right)\,.

(35)

The important point to note is that this solution is valid for any time
t
,
including
t=0
. Thus the function
F(u)
can then be fixed from the initial
condition in Eq. (
31
). Indeed, setting
t=0

in Eq. (
35
) and using Eq. (
31
) gives

F\left(C_{0}+\frac{1}{a-b}\,\ln\left(\frac{(a-b\,z)}{1-z}\right)\right)=z\,.

(36)

Setting

u=C_{0}+\ln\left(\frac{(a-b\,z)}{1-z}\right)\,,

(37)

or equivalently

z=1-\frac{a-b}{e^{(a-b)(u-C_{0})}-b}\,,

(38)

we obtain from Eq. (
36
)

F(u)=z=1-\frac{a-b}{e^{(a-b)(u-C_{0})}-b}\,.

(39)

Finally, substituting this on the rhs of Eq. (
35
) gives
us the desired solution explicitly, which upon simplification, reads

\hat{P}(z,t)=\frac{(a-bz)\,e^{(a-b)t}-a\,(1-z)}{(a-bz)e^{(a-b)t}-b(1-z)}

(40)

which, of course, coincides with the solution in Eq. (
11
)
obtained via the backward nonlinear equation. Consequently, one obtains
explicitly
P(n,t)
as in Eq. (
12
) for all
n
and all
t
.
Thus, quite remarkably, the size distribution
P(n,t)
satisfies
simultaneously two very different evolution equations,
one nonlinear in Eq. (
6
)
and the other linear as in Eq. (
26
), but with the same final solutions.
We will see in the next section that for computing the distribution
of the maximal population size up to a fixed time
t
, the second approach
using the random walk representation is much more convenient.

II.3
The continuum limit and the Feller process

In our branching process with death in continuous time, the population size

n
is a discrete non-negative integer. For large
n
, one can approximate this
process by an auxiliary process that takes place in continuous space. To
arrive at this continuum limit, we first introduce a lattice spacing
\delta

and write

x=n\,\delta\,.

(41)

Under this rescaling the probability distribution
P(n,t)
of the population
size
n
must scale as

P(n,t)=\delta\,\tilde{P}(n\delta,t)\,

(42)

where
\tilde{P}(x,t)
can be interpreted as the
probability density of the population in the continuous space
variable
x
.
Note that the scaling in Eq. (
42
) also ensures the normalization

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(x,t)\,dx=1\,,

(43)

upon identifying
dx=\delta
. The continuum limit corresponds to taking the
limit
\delta\to 0
,

n\to\infty
, keeping the product
x=n\,\delta
fixed.
We then substitute this scaling form (
42
) in the evolution
equation (
26
), replace
n=x/\delta
and expand for small
\delta
.
Keeping terms upto order
\delta
, we get

\partial_{t}\tilde{P}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}(b+a)\,\delta\,x\,\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{P}(x,t)+(a-b)\,\partial_{x}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)\,+(a+b)\,\delta\,\partial_{x}\tilde{P}=\frac{1}{2}(b+a)\,\delta\,\partial_{x}^{2}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)-(b-a)\partial_{x}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)\,.

(44)

Next we divide both sides of Eq. (
44
) by
2\,b\,\delta
and
define a rescaled time,

T=2\,\delta\,b\,t\,.

(45)

In this rescaled time,
Eq. (
44
) reduces to

\partial_{T}\tilde{P}(x,T)=\frac{(b+a)}{4b}\,\partial_{x}^{2}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)-\frac{(b-a)}{2\,\delta\,b}\,\partial_{x}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)\,.

(46)

We then see that in order to have a well defined continuum limit
when
\delta\to 0
, we must also scale

b-a=2\,b\,B\,\delta\,,

(47)

where
B
is a constant.
Thus one must take the branching rate
b
and annihilation rate
a
very
close to each other, i.e.,

b-a\to 0
,
\delta\to 0
while keeping the ratio

B=(b-a)/(2b\delta)
fixed. In this limit,
we then have a well defined Fokker-Planck equation in the continuous
semi-infinite line
x>0
that reads

\partial_{T}\tilde{P}(x,T)=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{x}^{2}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)-B\,\partial_{x}\left(x\,\tilde{P}\right)\,,\quad{\rm where}\quad B=\frac{(b-a)}{2\,b\,\delta}\,.

(48)

Note that
x=0
is a special sink site (as in the lattice model). Once the particle reaches
x=0
, the
dynamics is over and the process stops.

The Fokker-Planck equation (
48
) describes a process on the
continuous semi-infinite line
x\geq 0
with a space dependent drift and
diffusion. Indeed, the Langevin equation that gives rise to this Fokker-Planck
equation (
48
) can be written explicitly as

\frac{dx}{dT}=B\,x+\sqrt{x}\,\eta(T)\,

(49)

where
\eta(T)
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean and a
delta correlator,

\langle\eta(T)\eta(T^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(T-T^{\prime})
. This Langevin Eq. (
49
) has a multiplicative noise
term
\sqrt{2D(x)}\,\eta(t)
with
D(x)=x/2
and it has to be interpreted in the It
\hat{\rm o}
sense.
Thus the effective diffusion constant
D(x)=x/2

vanishes as
x\to 0
.
Identifying the drift term
B\,x=-\partial_{x}V(x)
,
the Langevin equation (
49
) corresponds to the motion of an
overdamped particle in an external harmonic potential

V(x)=-\frac{B}{2}x^{2}\,,

(50)

Note that in our problem, the parameter
B=(b-a)/(2b\delta)
can be both
positive or negative. In fact,
B<0
(
b<a
) corresponds to the
‘subcritical’ phase while
B>0
(
b>a
) corresponds to the ‘supercritical’ phase. The
case
B=0

corresponds to the ‘critical’ case
b=a
. One important point about this process is
that both the drift and the diffusion term vanishes as
x\to 0
, making the origin a sink.
The process terminates when the particle reaches
x=0
, starting from some initial position
x_{0}>0
.

To summarize, the continuum limit makes sense when
n\to\infty
,
t\to\infty

and
(b-a)\to 0
such that

b-a=2\,b\,B\,\delta\,,\quad\,t=\frac{T}{2b\delta}\,,\quad\,n=\frac{x}{\delta}\,

(51)

with
B
,
T
and
x
held fixed.
One can now check that the exact solution
P(n,t)
, obtained for discrete
n

in Eq. (
12
), does produce an exact solution of
the Fokker-Planck equation (
48
)
in this continuum limit given in Eq. (
51
)
For example, consider
the subritical case
b<a
. Here the exact solution for
P(n,t)

from Eq. (
12
) for
n>0
is given by (with
b<a
)

P(n,t)=\frac{(b-a)^{2}e^{(a-b)t}}{\left[b-a\,e^{(a-b)t}\right]^{2}}\,\left[\frac{b(1-e^{(a-b)t}}{b-a\,e^{(a-b)t}}\right]^{n-1}\,\quad{\rm for}\quad n\geq 1\,.

(52)

By taking the scaling limit as in Eq. (
51
) and using
the correspondence in Eq. (
42
), one gets

\tilde{P}(x,T)=C\,\frac{e^{|B|T}}{\left(e^{|B|T}-1\right)^{2}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{2|B|e^{|B|T}}{\left(e^{|B|T}-1\right)}\,x\right]\,,

(53)

where
|B|=-B=(a-b)/(2b\delta)>0
and
C=4|B|^{2}\,\delta
.
One can now check by direct substitution, that the solution in Eq. (
53
)
indeed satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation (
48
).
The total mass contained in positive semi-infinite axis is then given by (to leading order in
\delta
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{P}(x,T)\,dx=\frac{2\,|B|\,\delta}{e^{|B|T}-1}\,.

(54)

This indicates that the total density of particles in
x>0
decreases monotonically with increasing
T
, demonstrating
a nonzero leakage current to the sink site
x=0
. To check this, we note that exactly at
n=0

the discrete solution (
12
) reads

P(0,t)=\frac{a\,\left(1-e^{(a-b)t}\right)}{b-a\,e^{(a-b)\,t}}\,.

(55)

Taking the scaling limit (
51
) of this solution, one gets, to
O(\delta)

P(0,T)\approx 1-\frac{2\,|B|\,\delta}{e^{|B|T}-1}\,.

(56)

From (
54
) and (
56
), it follows that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{P}(x,T)\,dx+P(0,T)=1\,,

(57)

ensuring that the total probability is conserved at all time
T
if we take into acount the sink site at
x=0
.

The Langevin process in Eq. (
49
) is a particular case of the
celebrated Feller process that describes an overdamped particle
in the presence of a linear drift and a space dependent diffusion
constant
[
43
]

\frac{dX}{dT}=-\alpha\,X(T)+\beta+\sqrt{X(T)}\,\eta(T)\,,

(58)

where
X(T)\geq 0
,
\alpha>0
and
\beta
is a constant. The noise

\eta(T)
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean and a correlator

\langle\eta(T)\eta(T^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(T-T^{\prime})
. As before, Eq. (
58
)
is interpreted in the It
\hat{\rm o}
sense. The Feller process (
58
)
has been studied extensively and has found numerous applications
across disciplines: from neurobiology
[
44
,
45
]
to
finance
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
and
its first-passage properties have been investigated more
recently
[
50
,
51
]
. The continuum limit
of our branching process in Eq. (
49
) in the
subcritical case
(b<a)
corresponds to Feller process
in Eq. (
58
) with
\alpha=-B>0
and
\beta=0
.
However, the supercritical phase
b>a
, i.e.,
\alpha<0

is usually not studied in the context of the Feller process.
Thus, the Feller process may provide some asymptotic information
about the population size distribution in the branching process for large
n
in the subcritical
phase, but the discrete time
random walk problem is much richer. In particular, it allows us to study the supercritical and the critical
regime, thus going beyond the Feller process.

III
Maximal population size up to a fixed time
t

In this section, we study the main object of our interest in the branching process,
namely the maximum population size up to a fixed time
t
, starting from a
single bacteria. More precisely, if
N(\tau)
represents the
population size at time
\tau
starting from
N(0)=1
,
we define the maximal size up to time
t
as

M(t)=\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\left[N(\tau)\right]\,.

(59)

Clearly,
M(t)
is a random variable and in this section we compute exactly its
distribution
{Q(L,t)=\rm Prob.}[M(t)=L]
. We first tried to compute this distribution
using the traditional backward Fokker-Planck approach used for the derivation of the
population size distribution detailed in Section
II.1
. But very quickly, we realized
that this method is not suitable to compute the distribution of the maximum
M(t)
up to time
t
.
This is because of the following technical reason.
At any given time
t
, the total population size
N(t)=N_{1}(t)+N_{2}(t)
, where
N_{1}(t)
and
N_{2}(t)

denote the population sizes of the two daughter branches after the first branching event.
Thus one needs to calculate the distribution of the maximum of the sum

M(t)=\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\,[N_{1}(\tau)+N_{2}(\tau)]
. It is not obvious how to write
a recursion relation that keeps track of the maximum of the sum of two independent
populations. In contrast, it is much easier to compute the distribution
of
M(t)
using the second random walk approach depicted in Fig. (
3
) and discussed in detail
in Section
II.2
. The main idea is to study this random walk problem by placing
an absorbing barrier at some value
L>0
. Then, the survival probability of the walker up to time
t
starting
initially at
N(0)=1

(i.e., the probability that the walker stays strictly in the regime
0<n<L
up to time
t
) is
precisely the probability that the maximum
M(t)
of the walker up to time
t
is less than
L
. Thus, this
survival probability gives access to the cumulative distribution of the maximum
M(t)
and from this,
one can compute the PDF of
M(t)
. The survival probability up to time
t
can be computed exactly, leading
eventually to an exact derivation of the PDF
Q(L,t)={\rm Prob.}[M(t)=L]
. This is the strategy we follow
in detail below.

To proceed, we first define for this random walk problem

q(n,t|L)={\rm Prob.}\left[{\rm{the\,\,walker\,\,does\,\,not\,\,reach\,\,the\,\,site\,\,L\,\,up\,\,to\,\,time\,\,t,\,\,starting\,\,at\,\,the\,\,initial\,\,position\,\,n}}\right]\,.

(60)

Then,
q(n,t|L)
is also the probability that the maximum
M(t)
of the
walk up to time
t
, starting at the site
n
, stays below
L
. Hence,
this is just the cumulative distribution of the maximum up to
t
for the process
N(t)
starting at

N(0)=n
. In our case, the walker starts at
n=1
, and hence

q(1,t|L)={\rm Prob.}\left[M(t)\leq L\Big|{\rm{starting\,\,at\,\,n=1\,\,at\,\,t=0}}\right]

(61)

Once we know this cumulative distribution, the probability distribution
function (PDF) of
M(t)
, starting from
N(0)=1
, can be obtained from it simply by

Q(L,t)={\rm Prob.}\left[M(t)=L\right]=p(1,t|L)=q(1,t|L)-q(1,t|L-1)\approx\partial_{L}q(1,t|L)\,,

(62)

where the last step holds for large
L
where one can replace the discrete
L

by its continuum analogue. Our next goal is to write down the evolution equation
for
q(n,t|L)
for general
n
. From the solution for
q(n,t|L)
, we
will then be able to compute the cumulative distribution of the maximum

q(1,t|L)
and then derive the PDF of the maximum
M(t)
using
Eq. (
62
).

To write the evolution equation for
q(n,t|L)
, we first make a shorthand
notation
q(n,t|L)\equiv q(n,t)
for simplicity. The dependence on
L
is
implicit and will be restored later. It is easy to write down the
backward evolution equation for
q(n,t)
as

\partial_{t}q(n,t)=b\,n\,q(n+1,t)+a\,n\,q(n-1,t)-(a+b)\,n\,q(n,t)\,,

(63)

valid for all
1\leq n\leq L-1
, and satisfying the boundary conditions

q(0,t)=1\,,\quad{\rm and}\quad q(L,t)=0\,,

(64)

and the initial condition

q(n,0)=1\,,\quad{\rm for}\,\,0\leq n\leq L-1\,.

(65)

Eq. (
63
) is easy to understand. Note that
q(n,t)
is the ‘survival’
probability that the walker, starting at
n
at
t=0
, does not reach
L

up to time
t
. In the first time interval
\Delta t
, the walker
moves from
n
to
n+1
with probability
b\,n\,\Delta t
, to site

n-1
with probability
a\,n\,\Delta t
and with the rest of the
probability
1-(a+b)\,n\,\Delta t
it stays at site
n
. Writing
the three events explicitly, and taking the limit
\Delta t\to 0

gives Eq. (
63
). The boundary conditions in Eq. (
64
)
follow from the fact that if the walker starts at
n=0
it stays there
forever and hence never reaches the site
L
, explaining
q(0,t)=1
.
Similarly, if the walker starts at
L
, clearly its survival probability
(i.e., the probability of not reaching
L
) is zero. The initial
condition in Eq. (
65
) also follows immediately, since
if the particle starts at any site
0\leq n<L
, it can not reach the site
L

at time
t=0
, hence its survival probability is
1
.

To solve Eq. (
63
), it is convenient to define the
Laplace transform with respect to
t

\tilde{q}(n,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(n,t)\,e^{-s\,t}\,dt\,.

(66)

Taking Laplace transform of Eq. (
63
) with respect to
t

and using the initial condition
q(n,0)=1
for all

0\leq n\leq L-1
we get

b\,n\,\tilde{q}(n+1,s)+a\,n\,\tilde{q}(n-1,s)-(a+b)\,n\,\tilde{q}(n,s)=-1+s\,\tilde{q}(n,s)\,.

(67)

with the boundary conditions (from Eqs. (
64
))

\tilde{q}(0,s)=\frac{1}{s}\,,\quad{\rm and}\quad\tilde{q}(L,s)=0\,.

(68)

It is further convenient to make the shift

\tilde{q}(n,s)=\frac{1}{s}+\tilde{r}(n,s)

(69)

in Eq. (
67
) that makes the equation homogeneous, i.e.,

b\,n\,\tilde{r}(n+1,s)+a\,n\,\tilde{r}(n-1,s)-\left[(a+b)\,n+s\right]\,\tilde{r}(n,s)=0\,,\quad{\rm for}\quad 1\leq n\leq L-1\,,

(70)

with the associated boundary conditions

\tilde{r}(0,s)=0\,\quad{\rm and}\quad\tilde{r}(L,s)=-\frac{1}{s}\,.

(71)

To solve Eq. (
70
) over the finite interval
1\leq n\leq L-1
,
with the boundary conditions (
71
), it is actually convenient
to consider an auxiliary problem in which
Eq. (
70
) is assumed to hold in the full semi-infinite
line
n\geq 0
, with the two conditions in Eq. (
71
). The
solution for
n>L
in this auxiliary semi-infinite problem
is of no importance to us, but its solution for
n\leq L

will coincide with our finite interval problem. It turns out
that the solution of the auxiliary semi-infinite problem is
much simpler, compared to the finite interval problem. Thus,
the extension to this full semi-infinite lattice is purely
for mathematical convenience.

To proceed, we define the generating function

W(z,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tilde{r}(n,s)\,z^{n}\,.

(72)

Multiplying Eq. (
70
) by
z^{n}
, summing over all
n\geq 1
and
after some simplifying algebra (and using
\tilde{r}(0,s)=0
), we get a first-order differential equation

\left[a\,z^{2}-(a+b)\,z+b\right]\,\partial_{z}W(z,s)=\left(\frac{b}{z}+s-a\,z\right)\,W(z,s)\,.

(73)

This equation can be trivially solved to give

W(z,s)=A\,\frac{z}{(1-z)^{1+s/(b-a)}}\,\frac{1}{(b-a\,z)^{1-s/(b-a)}}\,,

(74)

where
A
is an arbitrary constant, yet to be fixed. Inverting
Eq. (
72
) formally using Cauchy’s inversion formula and
using Eq. (
74
) we get

\tilde{r}(n,s)=\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{n+1}}\,W(z,s)=A\,\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{n}}\,\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+s/(b-a)}\,(b-a\,z)^{1-s/(b-a)}}\,.

(75)

We can now fix the unknown constant
A
, using the condition

\tilde{r}(L,s)=-1/s
in Eq. (
71
). This gives

A=-\frac{1}{s}\,\frac{1}{\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{L}}\,\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+s/(b-a)}\,(b-a\,z)^{1-s/(b-a)}}}\,.

(76)

Once we have
\tilde{r}(n,s)
from Eq. (
75
), we can finally get
our desired
\tilde{q}(n,s)=1/s+\tilde{r}(n,s)
explicitly as

\tilde{q}(n,s)=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{\tilde{I}_{n}(s)}{\tilde{I}_{L}(s)}\right]\,,

(77)

where

\tilde{I}_{n}(s)=\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{n}}\,\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+s/(b-a)}\,(b-a\,z)^{1-s/(b-a)}}\,.

(78)

In particular, one finds that
for
n=1
(i.e., when the walker starts at site
n=1
), the
expression for
\tilde{I}_{1}(s)
simplifies

\tilde{I}_{1}(s)=\frac{1}{b^{1-s/(b-a)}}\,.

(79)

This follows from the fact that
\tilde{I}_{1}(s)
is just the coefficient of
z^{0}

in the power series expansion of the factor

(1-z)^{-1-s/(b-a)}(b-az)^{-1+s/(b-a)}
. Hence, from Eq. (
77
),
we get, for
n=1

\tilde{q}(1,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(1,t|L)\,e^{-s\,t}\,dt=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{1}{\tilde{J}_{L}(s)}\right]\,,

(80)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{L}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{L}}\,\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+s/(b-a)}\,\left(1-\frac{a\,z}{b}\right)^{1-s/(b-a)}}

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{L-1}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(L-\frac{s}{b-a}\right)}{\Gamma(L)\,\Gamma\left(1-\frac{s}{b-a}\right)}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-L+1,1+\frac{s}{b-a},-L+1+\frac{s}{b-a},\frac{b}{a}\right)\,,

(82)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,z)
is the Gauss hypergeometric funnction.
The last identity follows from Mathematica. In principle,
the results in Eqs. (
80
) and (
82
) are exact
for all
a
and
b
. However, one has to be a bit careful
for the critical case
a=b
(the limit
a\to b
has to be taken
carefully). In fact, in Appendix
A
, we show that in the critical case
a=b
,
Eq. (
80
) reduces to

\tilde{q}(1,s)\Big|_{b=a}=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(1,t|L)\,e^{-s\,t}\,dt=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{1}{L\,\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)}\right]\,

(83)

From the exact Laplace transform
\tilde{q}(1,s)
in Eq. (
80
),
one can extract the late time behavior of the cumulative distribution

q(1,t|L)
of the maximum
M(t)
. Below we summarize this asymptotic behavior
of the distribution of
M(t)
in the three phases. For convenience, we
define the parameter

c=\frac{a}{b}\,.

(84)

We will consider the subcritical
(c>1)
, critical
(c=1)
and supercritical

(c<1)
phases separately.

•

Subcritical
phase
c=a/b>1
. To extract the late time behavior
of
q(1,t|L)
, we need to analyse the small
s
behavior of its Laplace
transform in Eq. (
80
). We first note that for
c>1
, the
intgral
\tilde{J}_{L}(s)
has a finite limit as
s\to 0
. In fact, by setting
s=0
in
Eq. (
82
), we get

\tilde{J}_{L}(0)=\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{L}}\,\frac{1}{(1-z)\,(1-cz)}=\frac{c^{L}-1}{c-1}\,.

(85)

The last identity follows simply by expanding
1/[(1-z)(1-cz)]
in
powers of
z
and computing the coefficient of
z^{L-1}
in this
expansion. Hence, from Eq. (
80
), we get

\tilde{q}(1,s)\xrightarrow[s\to 0]{}\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{c-1}{c^{L}-1}\right]\,.

(86)

Inverting the Laplace transform trivially, it follows that

q(1,t|L)
approaches a time-independent value as
t\to\infty

q(1,t|L)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}1-\frac{c-1}{c^{L}-1}\,.

(87)

First note that since
c>1
, we have
q(1,t\to\infty|L)\to 1
as
L\to\infty
.
Since
q(1,t|L)
is the probability that
M(t)\leq L
, it follows
that the PDF of
M(t)
is normalized to unity.
Indeed, for large
L
, it follows from Eq. (
62
), that
the PDF of the maximum
M(t)
decays exponentially

Q(L,t\to\infty)={\rm Prob.}[M(t)=L]=p(1,t\to\infty|L)\approx\frac{(c-1)\,\ln c}{c^{L}}\,.

(88)

This result in Eq. (
88
) is shown in Fig.

and is in good agreement with numerical simulations for large

.

Critical

. In this case, we analyse

) in the limit

(89)

Consequently, inverting the Laplace transform, we see that

approaches a time-independent distribution as

Hence the PDF of the maximum, from Eq. (

), decays

(91)

Once again, this PDF is normalized to unity,
\int_{1}^{L}dL/L^{2}=1
.
Actually, for finite but large

, one can show that the PDF

as a function of

, gets cut-off at a scale
L_{c}(t)\sim a\,t
.
More precisely, for large but finite

, the PDF

approaches

where the scaling function

reads (see Appendix B)

f_{c}(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{}_{0}F_{1}(1,-p_{n}^{2}/4)}\left(1-\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{4z}\right)e^{-p_{n}^{2}/(4z)}\,,

where

is the confluent hypergeometric function and

(with

).

are derived in Appendix B and read

\displaystyle f_{c}(z)\to\begin{cases}&1+\frac{1}{{}_{0}F_{1}(1,-p_{1}^{2}/4)}\left(1-\frac{p_{1}^{2}}{4z}\right)e^{-p_{1}^{2}/(4z)}\,\quad{\rm as}\quad z\to 0\\
&\\
&\sqrt{2}\,z^{2}\,e^{-z}\,\hskip 116.65646pt{\rm as}\quad z\to\infty\,,\end{cases}

where we recall that

is the confluent hypergeometric function and

Thus, for

The front

Supercritical

c=a/b<1
. In this case, as in the

is finite and is given by
Eq. (

). Substituting this in Eq. (

and inverting the Laplace transform, we find that

approaches a time-independent form

q(1,t|L)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}1-\frac{1-c}{1-c^{L}}\,.

(95)

However, unlike in the subcritical phase (
c>1
),
in the supercritical phase where

, we find from Eq. (

) that as
L\to\infty

q(1,t\to\infty|L)\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}c<1\,.

(96)

Thus, the corresponding time-independent PDF

for large

has an exponentially decaying tail

and is not normalized to unity. In fact, the total probability weight
c<1
carried
by this part of the PDF is precisely
the extinction probability
P(0,t\to\infty)
in Eq. (

).
Thus this part of the PDF of the maximum that becomes time-independent
at late times is associated to
samples or realizations of the process where the population becomes eventually extinct in the
supercritical phase. The rest of the PDF with mass
1-c
is associated
with realizations in the supercritical phase where the population
explodes. We will call this part of the PDF as ‘condensate’.

To compute this condensate part, we need to analyse

q(1,t|L)
for finite but large
t
, which is done
in detail in Appendix C. We just summarize our main result here.
For finite but large

and large

, keeping
t-\ln(L)/(b-a)
fixed,
we simply obtain

q(1,t|L)\approx 1-\frac{1-c}{1-c^{L}}\,\theta\left(t-\frac{\ln L}{b-a}\right)\,,

where

is the Heaviside theta function:
\theta(z)=1
for
z>0

and
\theta(z)=0
for
z<0
.
From Eq. (
98
), it follows that

\displaystyle q(1,t|L)\to\begin{cases}&1-\frac{1-c}{1-c^{L}}\quad{\rm as}\quad t\to\infty\quad{\rm for}\,\,{\rm fixed}\,\,L\\
&\\
&1\hskip 45.52458pt{\rm as}\quad L\to\infty\quad{\rm for}\,\,{\rm fixed}\,\,t\,.\end{cases}

(99)

Thus, there is a exponentially growing
time-dependent scale

such that

, as a function of
L
,
undergoes a discontinuous jump to
1
from the value
1-(1-c)/(1-c^{L})

as
L
crosses

(see Fig.
(

of the maximum for large

and

\displaystyle Q(L,t)=p(1,t|L)\approx\partial_{L}q(1,t|L)\approx(-\ln c)\,(1-c)\,c^{L}\theta\left(e^{(b-a)t}-L\right)+(1-c)\,\delta\left(L-e^{(b-a)t}\right)\,.

(100)

The ‘delta’ peak at the end of the regular (the second term) part
of the PDF corresponds precisely to the ‘condensate’ that carries
an additional probability weight
(1-c)
and corresponds to
realizations where the population explosion occurs.

and is in good agreement with numerical simulations for large

.

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the dynamics of the population size
N(t)
of a branching process with death, where an
individual splits into two daughter with rate
b
and dies with rate
a
. Each individual may also diffuse as in branching
Brownian motion with death, but the spatial diffusion does not affect the statistics of
N(t)
. While the instantaneous
distribution
P(n,t)=Prob.[N(t)=n|N(0)=1]
was well known, in this paper our main interest was on the history of the process

N(t)
up to a fixed time
t
. In particular, we were interested in the extreme value statistics, i.e., the distribution of
the maximal population size
M(t)=\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\,[N(\tau)]
up to time
t
. We showed that this extreme value
distribution can be computed exactly by mapping the nonlinear branching process into an auxiliary random walk problem on the
positive semi-infinite axis, where the walker hops from site
n
to one of its neighbours: to site
n+1
with rate
b\,n
and
to site
n-1
with rate
a\,n
. The process terminates when the walker hits the origin for the first time. This random walk
representation with a state dependent hopping rate that increases linearly with distance from the origin is particular useful
to obtain an exact solution of the problem for the distribution
Q(L,t)={\rm Prob.}[M(t)=L]
(starting at
N(0)=1
). The
distribution
Q(L,t)
exhibits markedly different bbehaviors in the subcritical (
b<a)
, critical (
b=a
) and the
supercritical
(b>a)
phases. In the subcritical phase,
Q(L,t)
becomes stationary as
t\to\infty
and this
stationary distribution
Q(L,\infty)
decays exponentially with increasing
L
. At the critical point
b=a
also, the distribution
again becomes independent of
t
for large
t
, but
Q(L,\infty)\sim 1/L^{2}
has a power law tail
for large
L
. For finite but large
t
, the distribution at the critical point exhibits a scaling form
Q(L,t)\sim f_{c}(L/{at})/L^{2}
with a nontrivial scaling function
f_{c}(z)
that we compute analytically. In the supercritical
phase, the distribution
Q(L,t)
has a ‘fluid’ part that becomes independent of
t
for large
t
and a ‘condensate’ part (a
delta peak centered at
e^{(b-a)t}
) which gets disconnected from the fluid part and moves exponentially fast to
\infty
as time
increases. Since the underlying process
N(t)
is strongly correlated in time
t
, our results thus provide an exactly
solvable case for the extreme value statistics for strongly correlated variables, a subject that has seen a lot of interests
off late
[
22
,
23
]
. Besides, our results can be applied to characterize the
statistics of the maximally infected population up to a certain fixed time during the spread of an epidemic.

The population size
N(t)
in our model at the critical point
b=a
performs an unbiased random walk on the semi-infinite
axis (with the origin as a sink) where the hopping rate out of a site at
n
is proportional to
n
. Thus the random walker
gets more and more ‘active’ or ‘agitated’ as it goes further and further away from the origin. This is very different from
the ‘sluggish’ random walk models studied recently in the literature in the context of slow subdifusive dynamics in
inhomogeneous systems
[
52
,
53
]
and the stochastic porous medium equation
[
54
]
, where the hopping
rate out of a site at
n
scales as
|n|^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
. In those models, the walker gets more and more
sluggish as it deviates futher and further from the origin, leading to a very slow subdiffusive dynamics at late times. The
behavior in our model at the critical point is thus quite different from this sluggish random walk model. A natural
generalisation of this ‘agitated’ random walk (ARW) model would be when the hopping rate out of a site at
n
scales as

n^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
. It would be nice to see if there is an underlying ‘branching’ type process that can be
represented by this generalised ARW model with index
\gamma>0
. In this paper, we have computed exactly the distribution
of the maximum up to time
t
in this ARW model with index
\gamma=1
. It would be interesting to compute the distribution
of the maximum for the ARW model with arbitrary
\gamma>0
. Finally, an interesting extension of this problem would be to
study this branching process in the presence of stochastic resetting
[
55
,
56
]
whereby the population size

N(t)
gets reset to its initial value
1
with a constant rate
r
. For example, if a bacterial population is subject to
antibiotics at random times, a large fraction dies thus effectively resetting the process. In this case, it would be
interesting to study the fate of the population size of this branching-resetting process at long times.

for the critical case
b=a

\tilde{q}(1,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(1,t|L)\,e^{-s\,t}\,dt=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{1}{\tilde{J}_{L}(s)}\right]\,,

(103)

with

\tilde{J}_{L}(s)=\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{z^{L}\,(1-z)^{2}}\,e^{\frac{s}{a(1-z)}}\,.

(104)

To perform this integral, we make the change of variable

\frac{s\,z}{a(1-z)}=v\,,

(105)

which leads to

\tilde{J}_{L}(s)=\frac{a}{s}\,\oint\frac{dv}{2\,\pi\,i}\,\frac{1}{v^{L}}\,\left(\frac{s}{a}+v\right)^{L}\,e^{v}\,.

(106)

Using Cauchy’s theorem, the integral in Eq. (
106
) is precisely the coefficient of
v^{L-1}

in the power series expansion of
(s/a+v)^{L}\,e^{v}
. Expanding this product in a power series in
v
we get

\left(\frac{s}{a}+v\right)^{L}\,e^{v}=\sum_{n=0}^{L}{L\choose n}\,v^{n}\,\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)^{L-n}\,\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{m}}{m!}\,,

(107)

from wich one can read off the coefficient
C_{L-1}
of
v^{L-1}
as

C_{L-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}{L\choose k}\,\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)^{k}=\frac{s}{a}\,L\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)\,.

(108)

Substituting this result in Eq. (
106
) and then in (
103
) gives our desired
result (
83
), i.e.,

\tilde{q}(1,s)\Big|_{b=a}=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(1,t|L)\,e^{-s\,t}\,dt=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{1}{L\,\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)}\right]\,

(109)

Appendix B
Derivation of the scaling function
f_{c}(z)
in Eq. (
92
)

Let us first anticipate the scaling form in Eq. (
92
) and then show that
it is true (aposteriori) by computing the scaling function
f_{c}(z)
. Assuming the
validity of Eq. (
92
), we get the assocaited scaling form for the
cumulative distribution

q(1,t|L)=\int_{0}^{L}p(1,t|L^{\prime})\,dL^{\prime}=1-\int_{L}^{\infty}p(1,t|L^{\prime})dL^{\prime}\approx 1-\int_{L}^{\infty}\frac{1}{L^{\prime 2}}\,f_{c}\left(\frac{L^{\prime}}{a\,t}\right)\,dL^{\prime}=1-\frac{1}{a\,t}\,F_{c}\left(\frac{L}{a\,t}\right)\,

(110)

where

F_{c}(z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{f_{c}(x)}{x^{2}}\,dx\,\quad{\rm or\,\,\,equivalently}\quad f_{c}(z)=-z^{2}\,\frac{dF_{c}(z)}{dz}\,.

(111)

Hence if we can determine
F_{c}(z)
, we can extract the desired scaling function
f_{c}(z)
from Eq. (
111
)
Substituting the scaling form (
110
) for
q(1,t|L)
in is exact Laplace transform in Eq. (
83
),
we get

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{a\,t}\,F_{c}\left(\frac{L}{a\,t}\right)\,e^{-s\,t}\approx\frac{1}{s\,L}\,\frac{1}{{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)}\,,

(112)

where we recall the definition of the hypergeometric function

{}_{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,z)=1+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,x+\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta(\beta+1)}\,\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\ldots\,.

(113)

Hence

{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)=1+\frac{(L-1)}{2}\,\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)+\frac{(L-1)(L-2)}{3!\,2!}\,\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)^{2}+\ldots

(114)

We now take the scaling limit
s\to 0
,
L\to\infty
, keeping
the product
s\,L
fixed. Taking this limit term by term in the series in Eq. (
114
), we get

{}_{1}F_{1}\left(1-L,2,-\frac{s}{a}\right)\to\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!(m+1)!}\,\left(\frac{sL}{a}\right)^{m}=\sqrt{\frac{a}{sL}}\,I_{1}\left(2\,\sqrt{\frac{sL}{s}}\right)\,,

(115)

where
I_{1}(z)
is the modified Bessel function. Finally, substituting Eq. (
115
) on the rhs
of Eq. (
112
), and setting
p=sL/a
, we get in the scaling limit (with
p
fixed) the exact
relation satisfied by the scaling function

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{y}\,F_{c}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\,e^{-p\,y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}\,I_{1}\left(2\,\sqrt{p}\right)}\,.

(116)

Defining

\frac{1}{y}F_{c}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)=G_{c}(y)\,,\quad{\rm or\,\,\,equivalently}\quad F_{c}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\,G_{c}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\,

(117)

one then has the exact Laplace transform for the function
G_{c}(z)
from Eq. (
116

\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{c}(y)\,e^{-p\,y}\,dy=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}\,I_{1}\left(2\,\sqrt{p}\right)}\,.

(118)

To proceed, we first formally invert Eq. (
118
) using
the Bromwich inversion formula

G_{c}(y)=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{dp}{2\,\pi\,i}\,\frac{e^{p\,y}}{\sqrt{p}\,I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{p}\right)}\,,

(119)

where
\Gamma
denotes the vertical Bromwich contour in the complex
p
-plane whose real coordinate
lies to the right of all singularities of the integrand. To evaluate this complex integral, we first identify the poles of the integrand, which has a pole at
p_{0}=0
and infinitely many poles on the negative real line at
p_{n}
with
n\geq 1
. The pole
p_{n}
coincides with the
n

(we will use the convention
p_{n}>p_{n+1}
). Evaluating the residues using Mathematica, we find that the function

can be written as

G_{c}(y)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{}_{0}F_{1}(1,-p_{n}^{2}/4)}e^{-p_{n}^{2}y/4}\,,

(120)

where
{}_{0}F_{1}(1,z)

f_{c}(z)=-z^{2}\,\frac{dF_{c}(z)}{dz}=G_{c}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)+\frac{1}{z}\,G_{c}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\,.

(121)

Plugging the exact expression in Eq. (
120

121

(122)
