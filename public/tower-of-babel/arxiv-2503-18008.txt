Personalized Language Models via Privacy-Preserving Evolutionary Model Merging
Kyuyoung Kim, Jinwoo Shin, Jaehyung Kim

Abstract
Personalization in large language models (LLMs) seeks to tailor models to individual user or user group preferences. Prompt-based methods augment queries with user preference information, whereas training-based methods directly encode preferences into model parameters for more effective personalization. Despite achieving some success in personalizing LLMs, prior methods often fail to directly optimize task-specific metrics and lack explicit privacy-preservation mechanisms. To address these limitations, we propose Privacy-Preserving Model Merging via Evolutionary Algorithms (PriME), a novel approach to personalization that employs gradient-free methods to directly optimize task-specific metrics while preserving user privacy. By incorporating privacy preservation into optimization, PriME produces a personalized module that effectively captures the target user’s preferences while minimizing the privacy risks for the users sharing their private information. Experiments on the LaMP benchmark show that PriME outperforms both prompt-based and training-based methods, achieving up to a 45% performance improvement over the prior art. Further analysis shows that PriME achieves a significantly better privacy-utility trade-off, highlighting the potential of evolutionary approaches for privacy-preserving LLM personalization.



1
Introduction

Pre-trained on internet-scale data, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools
(
Radford et al. 2018
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
, capable of performing a wide range of natural language processing (NLP) tasks, from conversational agents, translation, and question-answering to code generation
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Team et al. 2024
)
.
Notably, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable effectiveness as generalist models, able to perform complex tasks with little to no task-specific data
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
.
To extend their utility beyond general-purpose applications, LLM personalization seeks to tailor model responses to the preferences of individual users or user groups
(
Salemi et al. 2023
)
.
Such LLM personalization is particularly important in domains such as education
(
Kasneci et al. 2023
)
, healthcare
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, and content recommendation
(
Baek et al. 2024
)
to enhance performance and user experience.

Previous approaches for LLM personalization can be categorized into two main types:
prompt-based
and
training-based
methods.
Prompt-based methods incorporate users’ historical data or preferences relevant to the queries into input prompts, enabling models to better understand and respond to user-specific queries
(
Mysore et al. 2023
;
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Kim & Yang 2024
)
.
While these methods are relatively simple to implement and avoid the need for additional training, the performance is inherently constrained by the model’s context length and may result in significantly higher inference costs due to enlarged input prompts.

In contrast, training-based methods capture user behavior patterns and preferences directly in model parameters.
One straightforward training-based approach is to fine-tune a single parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
module per user on the user’s own personal data
(
Tan et al. 2024b
)
.
However, when user data is limited, training-based approach may not be sufficient for effective personalization.
Furthermore, individually owned modules limit the ability to leverage collaborative benefits through shared modules.
To partly address this, several approaches assume access to preference data from a range of users or groups to adapt models to the preferences of individual groups
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
.
However, direct access to raw data from multiple users or groups is often unavailable in practice, and training on combined data to serve such groups poses significant privacy risks, especially in the absence of explicit mechanisms for preserving user privacy.

To address these challenges, we propose
Pri
vacy-Preserving
M
odel Merging via
E
volutionary Algorithms (PriME), a novel approach to LLM personalization that leverages knowledge from a community of data-sharing users while preserving their privacy.
Given a set of users who have consented to share their PEFT modules, PriME employs evolutionary algorithms to find the optimal weights for linearly interpolating the shared modules (i.e., model merging
(
Wortsman et al. 2022
)
) to create a personalized module for a target user.
Here, to explicitly measure the risk of privacy leakage, we define privacy preservation in terms of the similarity between the predictions made with the merged module and those made with each individual module corresponding to a sharing user.
Intuitively, this similarity estimates the extent to which information about each sharing user can be inferred from the merged module.
Then, the gradient-free nature of evolutionary algorithms allows for the direct optimization of non-differentiable target metrics, such as F1 and ROUGE, with the privacy preservation measure also incorporated into the optimization.
Consequently, it enables us to make a trade-off between fitting the target user’s data and preserving the privacy of users sharing their PEFT modules, ensuring that the resulting module effectively captures the target user’s preferences while minimizing the risk of privacy leakage for the sharing users.
Note that this is achieved without introducing additional complexities to the model architecture or training, unlike existing methods
(
Tan et al. 2024a
)
.

Experimental results on LaMP
(
Salemi et al. 2023
)
, a comprehensive benchmark comprising personalized text classification and generation tasks, demonstrate that PriME consistently outperforms both prompt-based and training-based baselines, achieving up to a 45% improvement in performance over the best-performing training-based baseline.
Notably, this performance is achieved by merging as few as three shared PEFT modules, reducing the risk of privacy leakage by limiting the use of modules trained on private data.
Moreover, by directly optimizing for privacy preservation, our approach produces a personalized module that is more distant from the sharing users’ modules than that of a competing merging-based method, while achieving substantially better performance in capturing the target user’s preferences.
We also observe that optimizing for privacy preservation can serve as a regularizer to often enhance performance compared to when it is excluded.
These findings underscore the potential of evolutionary methods for achieving privacy-preserving LLM personalization.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce PriME, an evolutionary approach that automates the discovery of optimal model merging recipes for privacy-preserving LLM personalization.

•

We propose a measure of privacy preservation that is intuitive and well-suited for optimization, and show that PriME effectively balances capturing user preferences with preserving the privacy of sharing users.

•

We demonstrate that PriME, leveraging evolutionary methods, consistently outperforms both prompt-based and training-based baselines on the LaMP benchmark, achieving a significantly better utility-privacy trade-off through explicit optimization for privacy preservation.

Preliminaries

LLM Personalization.

Personalizing LLMs involves tailoring model responses to individual users or groups based on their historical behavior data.
Specifically, given an LLM
\mathcal{M}
and history data
\mathcal{H}_{u}=\{h_{u}\}
for user
u
, LLM personalization aims to generate an output
y
that is aligned with the user’s preferences conditioned on both the input
x
and
\mathcal{H}_{u}
:

\max_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(\cdot\mid x,\mathcal{H}_{u})}f(y,y^{*}),

(1)

where
f
is a target metric, such as ROUGE, that measures the similarity between the generated response
y
and the user-preferred ground-truth response
y^{*}
to
x
.
A user history item
h_{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}
may consist of a
(x_{u},y_{u})
pair in the task-specific query-response format, capturing how the user has historically responded to similar queries, or text sequences that provide contextual information about behavior patterns.

Model Merging for Personalization.

Given a group of sharing users
\mathcal{S}
, each with a personalized PEFT
\Delta W_{s}
trained on their private data, our goal is to create a module
\Delta W_{u}
for a target user
u
that aligns with the history
\mathcal{H}_{u}
using the shared modules.
To achieve this, we (1) identify a subset of users
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathcal{S}
that are most relevant to the target user and the corresponding set of modules
\{\Delta W_{s}\mid s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}\}
.
Then, we (2) determine the optimal weights
\{w_{s}\mid s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}\}
to linearly interpolate the selected modules.
Based on the weights, we construct
\Delta W_{u}
as

\Delta W_{u}=\sum_{s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}}w_{s}\Delta W_{s}.

(2)

If the weights are scalars, merging is done at the PEFT module level, whereas if they are vectors, merging occurs at a more granular level, such as LoRA weights for each layer.
Once we obtain a module
\Delta W_{u}
for user
u
, we condition the LLM
\mathcal{M}
on
\Delta W_{u}
to generate personalized responses:

\mathcal{M}_{u}(\cdot)=\mathcal{M}(\cdot\mid\Delta W_{u}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{M}_{u}
represents the LLM conditioned on
\Delta W_{u}
, e.g., integrating the personalized LoRA module
\Delta W_{u}
into
\mathcal{M}
.

While our method is applicable to arbitrary PEFT types, such as Adapter
(
Houlsby et al. 2019
)
and
(\text{IA})^{3}

(
Liu et al. 2022
)
, we present it in terms of LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
for the remainder due to its widespread use.

3
PriME: Evolutionary Privacy-Preserving LLM Personalization

This section introduces PriME, an evolutionary approach to LLM personalization via model merging, with an explicit privacy-preserving mechanism for users sharing their data.

3.1
Personalization via Evolutionary Model Merging

User Embedding and Selection.

Selecting a subset of LoRA modules relevant to the target user is important from a privacy perspective, as it reduces the number of modules trained on private data used in merging.
To measure the similarity between users, we adopt the approach from
Tan et al. 2024a
, where an encoder-only language model
\mathcal{E}
encodes each history item
h_{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}
, and the user embedding is computed as the average of these encoded items:

\mathbf{e}_{u}=\sum_{h_{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}}\mathcal{E}(h_{u})/|\mathcal{H}_{u}|.

(4)

Given the user embedding
\mathbf{e}_{u}
for the target user
u
and
\mathbf{e}_{s}
for each sharing user
s\in\mathcal{S}
computed as in Eq.
4
, we calculate the cosine similarity between them to select the top-
k
most relevant sharing users:

\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{s\in\mathcal{S}\mid\text{cos}(\mathbf{e}_{u},\mathbf{e}_{s})\geq\text{top-$k$}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}(\text{cos}(\mathbf{e}_{u},\mathbf{e}_{s}))\right\}.

(5)

Our empirical results demonstrate that far fewer LoRAs selected via this approach are often sufficient to achieve effective personalization (see Section
4.2
).

Evolutionary Merging for Personalization.

Inspired by the success of evolutionary approaches in developing strong foundation models through model merging
(
Akiba et al. 2024
)
, we extend the idea to automatically discover merging recipes for creating personalized LoRA modules.
Specifically, we use an evolutionary algorithm, such as CMA-ES
(
Hansen 2006
)
, to find the optimal weights for linearly interpolating a subset of LoRAs
\{\Delta W_{s}\mid s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}\}
, selected according to Eq.
5
.
Then, the weights are evolved to maximize alignment with the preferences of the target user
u
:

\argmax_{\{w_{s}\mid s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}\}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}_{u}}\left[f(y,y^{*})\right],

(6)

where
\mathcal{M}
is an LLM and
f
is a target metric that measures the similarity between the response
y
generated by LLM conditioned on
\Delta W_{u}
and the user-preferred response
y^{*}
.

As evolutionary algorithms are gradient-free, one can directly optimize the often non-differentiable
f
, without introducing additional complexities to model implementation or training, as seen in other methods such as Per-Pcs
(
Tan et al. 2024a
)
.
Note that
f
can also be some suitable combination of multiple metrics that are relevant to the task.

3.2
Optimization of Privacy Preservation

Measuring Privacy Preservation.

Direct access to the sharing LoRAs raises privacy concerns due to the potential leakage of sensitive information captured within model parameters
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
.
To address this, we propose to measure privacy preservation using the similarity between predictions made using each shared LoRA and those made using the merged LoRA via PriME.
Specifically, for a sharing user
s
, we define the degree of privacy preservation for
s
using the task-specific metric
f
, where the predictions made with the sharing user’s LoRA
\Delta W_{s}
serve as reference labels and those made with the merged LoRA
\Delta W_{u}
as regular predictions.
Assuming access only to the target user’s data, we compute this measure based on predictions made on the target user’s history,
\mathcal{H}_{u}
, as follows:

P_{u}(s)=\sum_{h_{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}}f(\mathcal{M}_{u}(h_{u}),\mathcal{M}_{s}(h_{u}))/|\mathcal{H}_{u}|.

(7)

Intuitively, it captures the extent to which information about the sharing user can be inferred through the merged LoRA.

Optimizing Privacy Preservation.

Personalization with privacy preservation can be viewed as a multi-objective optimization, where we maximize alignment with the target user’s preferences while minimizing privacy risks associated with the shared components used in the process.
To this end, we optimize the following objective leveraging the privacy-preservation measure defined in Eq.
7
:

\displaystyle\argmax_{\{w_{s}\mid s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}\}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}_{u}}\left[f(y,y^{*})\right]-\alpha\cdot\sum_{s\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{P_{u}(s)}{|\bar{\mathcal{S}}|},

(8)

where
\alpha
controls the trade-off between aligning with the target user’s preferences and minimizing the average prediction similarity with the sharing users.
The use of
f
in defining privacy preservation in Eq.
7
allows us to account for task-specific considerations of prediction similarity.
It also leads to natural integration of privacy preservation into the optimization objective, which includes how well the user preferences are captured, also defined in terms of
f
.

We empirically demonstrate that, for an appropriate value of
\alpha
, the resulting target user’s LoRA exhibits significantly less similarity to the sharing users compared to that produced by a competing model-merging method, while substantially better capturing the target user’s preferences.

4
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate PriME in terms of (a) its effectiveness in optimizing various task-specific target metrics and (b) its ability to create a personalized LoRA module that effectively balances capturing the target user’s preferences and preserving the privacy of the sharing users.
See Appendix
B
for more experimental details.

4.1
Setup

Benchmark and Tasks.

For our evaluation, we use the language model personalization benchmark LaMP
(
Salemi et al. 2023
)
, a widely adopted benchmark comprising a diverse set of personalized text classification and generation tasks.
Specifically, we focus on three classification tasks - (1) citation identification (LaMP-1), (2) movie tagging (LaMP-2), and (3) product rating (LaMP-3) - and three generation tasks - (4) news headline generation (LaMP-4), (5) scholarly title generation (LaMP-5), and (6) tweet paraphrasing (LaMP-7).
For data splitting, we follow the approach from
Tan et al. 2024a
to ensure fair comparison, randomly selecting 25% of users for task adaptation of the base model, 100 users for testing, and the remaining users as candidates for sharing their LoRA modules.
See Section
A
for more details on the benchmark and tasks.

Sharing users are selected in a similar manner as in
Tan et al. 2024a
by first computing user embeddings using the DeBERTa V3 large model
(
He et al. 2021
)
, as described in Section
3.1
.
We then apply the
k
-means clustering algorithm to group the embeddings into up to
k=50
clusters and select the user with the largest history data from each cluster as the sharing user.
For each sharing user, we train a LoRA module using the task-adapted model as the base model.

Baselines.

We compare PriME with state-of-the-art personalization baselines, including both prompt-based and training-based methods.
For prompt-based baselines, we use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
Salemi et al. 2023
)
, which augments the query with the most relevant
k
items retrieved from the user’s behavior history, and profile-augmented generation (PAG)
(
Richardson et al. 2023
)
, which incorporates a textual summary of the user’s profile.
Additionally, we evaluate against Per-Pcs
(
Tan et al. 2024a
)
, which leverages what are called
gating
vectors to select sharing LoRAs and performs autoregressive merging to create a personalized LoRA module.

For all baselines and PriME, we use Llama-2-7b
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
as the base LLM and BM25
(
Robertson et al. 2009
)
to retrieve history items.
User profiles are generated by summarizing a random sample of the user’s history using Mistral-7B-Instruct v0.2
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
.
These summaries capture user preferences or behavior patterns, e.g., the user’s most frequently explored research topics.

Evaluation.

Following
Salemi et al. 2023
, we use accuracy and F1 score for text classification tasks (LaMP-1 and LaMP-2), mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) for ordinal multi-class classification tasks (LaMP-3), and ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L for generation tasks (LaMP-4, LaMP-5, and LaMP-7).

Implementation Details.

As the details of task adaptation are not reported in
Tan et al. 2024a
, we perform additional hyperparameter tuning to train task-adapted models that are comparable (on LaMP-2, LaMP-4, LaMP-5, and LaMP-7), or superior (on LaMP-1 and LaMP-3).
For Per-Pcs, we experiment with the reported settings and explore additional hyperparameters, reporting the best reproducible results.
For PriME, we optimize the sum of two task-specific metrics on the training data.
Note that for LaMP-1, the training data consists of textual citations paired with titles and abstracts that differ from the evaluation task, which is a binary classification.
Hence, for this task, we optimize for the sum of ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L between the target and generated citations.
See Section
B.1
for more details on the hyperparameters and other implementation details.

Overall Performance.

Table
1
summarizes the performance across six public personalization tasks in the LaMP benchmark.
Overall, PriME consistently and, in many cases, significantly outperforms Per-Pcs, as well as the prompt-based baselines RAG and PAG.

In classification tasks, PriME achieves relative improvements of 4.11% in accuracy and 4.49% in F1 score on LaMP-1, and 45.18% and 34.46% on LaMP-2, respectively, over Per-Pcs.
On LaMP-3, despite our best efforts to reproduce results comparable to those reported in
Tan et al. 2024a
, we observe significantly suboptimal performance, with results much worse than the non-personalized baseline.
This suggests that Per-Pcs may be particularly sensitive to hyperparameter choices, or that its autoregressive approach in selecting relevant LoRA modules and merging weights may have limitations in certain cases.
In contrast, by directly optimizing the target metric, PriME achieves considerably better results, with relative improvements of 45.92% in MAE and 25.31% in RMSE.
Notably, PriME also outperforms the original Per-Pcs results.
Moreover, in generation tasks, PriME demonstrates superior performance, achieving relative improvements in ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L of 7.26% and 6.79% on LaMP-4, and 1.97% and 0.65% on LaMP-7, compared to Per-Pcs.

OPPU
(
Tan et al. 2024b
)
is a straightforward approach that directly trains a LoRA module on target user data.
Due to limited reproducibility and missing hyperparameter details, we include the results from
Tan et al. 2024a
verbatim in Table
1
as an additional comparison.
While OPPU generally outperforms Per-Pcs, PriME delivers competitive results on LaMP-1, LaMP-4, and LaMP-7, often matching or exceeding OPPU’s performance.
On LaMP-3, PriME achieves a 10.29% reduction in MAE and 12.07% in RMSE with RAG, and 3.76% and 6.95% reductions with PAG.
More substantial improvements are observed on LaMP-2, with relative gains of 13.82% in accuracy and 34.38% in F1 score without prompt augmentation, 14.99% and 21.49% with RAG, and 8.68% and 14.81% with PAG.
These findings indicate that effective personalization can be achieved even without directly training on target user data, instead utilizing model merging of shared LoRAs.

Lastly, we observe that PAG, particularly when combined with RAG, performs competitively on several tasks, including LaMP-1 and LaMP-4, even surpassing Per-Pcs in some cases.
We hypothesize that this effectiveness stems from the test users in the benchmark having consistent behavior patterns, which can be succinctly captured in text.
However, for users with more complex or evolving behavior patterns, relying solely on text-based encoding may be less effective.
In such scenarios, combining prompt-based and training-based methods may yield the best results, especially when there is minimal conflict between parametric and non-parametric knowledge, though evidence of such conflicts is observed not infrequently in our experiments.

To assess how well PriME balances capturing user preferences and preserving the privacy of sharing users, we evaluate its performance alongside the average prediction similarity between the merged LoRA and the LoRAs of the top three most relevant sharing users on LaMP-2, as we vary the value of
\alpha
in Eq.

.
For each
\alpha
, we report the mean of the average similarity with sharing users across all 100 test users.
As illustrated in Figure

, the average prediction similarity on test queries, measured in terms of both accuracy and F1 score, consistently decreases as
\alpha
increases, while the performance in capturing user preferences remains well maintained up to a value of 2.0.
Both accuracy and F1 score even slightly improve with moderate
\alpha
values, likely because it acts as a regularizer to mitigate overfitting on the training data.
At
\alpha=2.0

5
Related Work

LLM Personalization.

Personalization seeks to adapt general-purpose models to better serve individual users or groups according to their preferences. Prompt-based methods integrate historical user data or preferences directly into input prompts, allowing models to tailor the responses without requiring additional training
(
Mysore et al. 2023
;
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Kim & Yang 2024
)
.
Vanilla approaches leverage the in-context learning capabilities of LLMs by providing few-shot examples of historical user behavior
(
Kang et al. 2023
;
Christakopoulou et al. 2023
)
.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
Salemi et al. 2023
)
retrieves relevant records from user history to augment prompts, and profile-augmented generation (PAG)
(
Richardson et al. 2023
)
enhances queries with abstract summaries of user history to generate personalized content.
Although relatively simple to implement, these methods are limited by the model’s context length and can significantly increase inference costs due to enlarged prompts.

Training-based methods, in contrast, capture user preferences within model parameters.
OPPU
(
Tan et al. 2024b
)
fine-tunes a single parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) module, such as LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
, per user on directly their personal data.
However, in case user data is limited, training exclusively on it may not suffice for effective personalization.
Several methods, including Group Preference Optimization
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
and soft-prompting models
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
, have explored leveraging data from multiple users or groups to improve personalization.
While adapting models to collective preferences can enhance individual personalization, accessing raw data of multiple groups of users poses privacy risks, especially without explicit privacy-preserving mechanisms.

Per-Pcs
(
Tan et al. 2024a
)
partly addresses this challenge by merging shared PEFT modules, each trained separately on individual users’ data, to create a personalized module for a target user.
However, it introduces additional model complexities, lacks direct optimization for task-specific metrics, and does not explicitly address the privacy risks associated with using shared PEFT modules.
To this end, PriME leverages evolutionary methods to solve the multi-objective optimization of capturing user preferences while preserving the privacy of the users sharing their PEFT modules.

Model Merging.

Model merging is a surprisingly effective approach to developing models by integrating multiple models with diverse capabilities into a unified architecture in parameter space.
A simple approach of averaging the weights of multiple models fine-tuned from the same base model has demonstrated significant performance in image processing and classification tasks
(
Wortsman et al. 2022
)
.
To extend the technique to the language domain, methods such as Task Arithmetic
(
Ilharco et al. 2022
)
construct task vectors that represent models weights encoding task-specific abilities and combine them to create a merged model with the desired capabilities.
Despite the surprising effectiveness of merged models, the process is often considered an art, requiring human intuition and expertise in model selection and crafting merging recipes, such as interpolation weights.

Recently,
Akiba et al. 2024
have demonstrated the use of evolutionary algorithms to automatically discover effective model merging recipes, such as interpolation weights for mixing parameters and layer permutations, to further enhance the technique.
In this work, we extend the evolutionary approach to model merging for personalizing language models, with an explicit component in optimization to safeguard the privacy of shared user information.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce PriME, a novel approach to LLM personalization that leverages evolutionary algorithms to directly optimize task-specific metrics while preserving the privacy of users sharing their information.
Experiments on the LaMP benchmark demonstrate that PriME outperforms state-of-the-art methods, with significant improvements in capturing user preferences while achieving a better utility-privacy trade-off.
These findings highlight the potential of evolutionary model merging as an effective framework for achieving privacy-conscious personalization in LLMs.

Future Work.

Further exploration of more advanced model merging techniques, such as random weight dropping
(
Yu et al. 2024
)
and the removal of conflicting weights
(
Yadav et al. 2024
)
, and understanding their impact on the resulting personalized module and the privacy preservation of the sharing users would be an interesting direction to pursue.
Additionally, addressing conflicts between parametric and non-parametric knowledge when combining prompt-based and training-based methods, or between two sources of non-parametric knowledge (e.g., RAG and PAG), as observed in our empirical results, could offer valuable insights into further enhancing LLM personalization.

Impact Statement

This work introduces a novel framework for implementing personalized machine learning systems using language models while minimizing the risk of information leakage.
By leveraging gradient-free optimization, our approach achieves effective personalization, with significantly better privacy-utility trade-offs compared to existing methods.
As with any optimization process, it is important to carefully consider the objectives being optimized, as the resulting models may not align with expectations, especially when the objective is merely a proxy for human intent.
Furthermore, as this work highlights, explicitly incorporating privacy preservation into personalization methods should be a priority as such techniques continue to evolve.

Appendix A
Benchmark and Task Details

In this section, we provide additional details on the individual tasks in the benchmark.

LaMP-1: Personalized Citation Identification.

This is a binary classification task in which a language model identifies which of two candidate papers a user would cite in a paper.
User’s profile contains a set of titles, abstracts, and citations of previous publications.
During task adaptation, the model is trained to generate a citation based on the title of a paper.

LaMP-2: Personalized Movie Tagging.

In this multi-class classification task, a language model needs to classify a given movie description into one of 15 possible categories, e.g., ‘comedy’ or ‘action’.
Given a user’s historical tagging assignments, the model is adapted to generate appropriate tags for movie descriptions.

LaMP-3: Personalized Product Rating.

In this ordinal multi-class classification task, a language model predicts product ratings based on a user’s review history.
Given pairs of a review and the corresponding historical rating, the model needs to generate an integer rating from 1 to 5.

LaMP-4: Personalized News Headline Generation.

In this task, a language model generates a news headline for an article based on a user’s stylistic patterns as an author.
Based on the user’s article history and titles, the model is adapted to generate headlines that align with the identified style.

LaMP-5: Personalized Scholarly Title Generation.

Similar to LaMP-4, in this task, a language model generates research article titles based on a user’s profile of historical article-title pairs.
In LaMP-5, only the abstracts of articles are provided.

LaMP-7: Personalized Tweet Paraphrasing.

This is another personalized generation task in which a language model needs to paraphrase a tweet in the style of a user based on the user’s historical tweets.
