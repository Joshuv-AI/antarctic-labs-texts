Transcriptome based WGCNA analysis reveals the mechanisms underlying corolla abscission in blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.)
Authors: Liangqin Liu, Yanqin Jiang, Xiaomin Wang, Zhenghai Mo, Mengxi Liu, Hong Yu, Yujun Pei, Xingru Wei, Liangliang Tian, Qilong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorolla abscission is an important agronomic trait that affects fruit quality and disease susceptibility in blueberry. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the molecular and physiological factors associated with corolla abscission by comparing two blueberry cultivars with contrasting corolla abscission characteristics: the caducous ‘Magica’ (‘F6’) and the persistent ‘Eureka-Sunrise’ (‘L25’).ResultsCytological analysis revealed distinct structural differences in the abscission zone (AZ) between the two cultivars. In ‘F6’, cell separation occurred after full bloom, whereas AZ cells in ‘L25’ remained intact and gradually resembled adjacent tissues. To explore the molecular basis of these differences, transcriptome analysis was performed at three developmental stages. Differential expression and enrichment analyses indicated that genes related to plant hormone signaling, cell wall organization, and oxidative stress response were consistently involved. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis further identified modules associated with cultivar-specific traits, with genes related to ethylene and abscisic acid pathways showing higher expression in ‘F6’, while those associated with auxin metabolism and stress-related processes were more active in ‘L25’. To validate these findings, the activities of cell wall–related enzymes were measured. Polygalacturonase activity increased at the stage corresponding to abscission initiation in ‘F6’, while remaining relatively low in ‘L25’. In addition, cellulase activity was consistently higher in ‘F6’ than in ‘L25’.ConclusionsThese results indicate that corolla abscission in blueberry is associated with coordinated changes in hormone-related pathways and cell wall modification. Differences in the regulation of these processes between cultivars may contribute to variation in abscission behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09029-0.

Introduction

Plant organ abscission is a developmentally programmed process governed by genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors. It occurs in specialized tissues termed abscission zones (AZs), which typically consist of 1–50 layers of small cells with dense cytoplasm and form at the junction of organs destined for shedding. The abscission process initiates with the differentiation of AZs, which subsequently develop competence to perceive and respond to developmental or environmental signals. This is followed by the establishment of an abscission layer through localized degradation of the cell wall, while a protective scar layer develops on the distal side of the detaching organ [1, 2].

Changes in endogenous hormone levels play a crucial role in regulating organ abscission [3]. Phytohormones involved in this process are generally classified as either promoters or inhibitors. Ethylene, abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), and cytokinins (CK) typically promote abscission, whereas auxin, gibberellin (GA), and brassinosteroid (BR) suppress the process [4, 5]. For instance, the ethylene-insensitive mutants such as etr1-1 and ein2 in Arabidopsis inhibited floral organ abscission by disrupting ethylene signaling [6]. Similarly, increased expression of SAMs, ACS, ACO, and ETR genes within the AZ was associated with early fruit abscission in melon [7]. Conversely, auxin generally acts as a negative regulator of abscission by inhibiting cell separation [8]. Specific auxin transporters like PbPIN11, PbPIN02, and PbPIN09, played critical roles in calyx tube abscission in Korla fragrant pear [9].

The abscission signal induces AZ cells to secrete cell wall-degrading enzymes that collectively dismantle the middle lamella (pectin matrix) and primary cell wall. Specifically, pectinases (PGs, PLs, PMEs) depolymerize pectin within the middle lamella, whereas cellulases (CELs) hydrolyze cellulose microfibrils. In addition, xyloglucan endotransglucosylases/hydrolases (XTHs) and expansins (EXPs) act coordinately to remodel the cellulose-xyloglucan network [10–12]. This enzymatic synergy loosens the cell wall structure, generating mechanical force for organ detachment. Subsequently, enzymes such as peroxidase, phenylalanine ammonialyase, and chitinase participate in the post-abscission phase by contributing to the formation of a protective scar layer [3]. Together, these processes suggest that organ abscission is regulated by complex interactions between hormone signaling and cell wall remodeling pathways. These dynamics can now be systematically unraveled through high-throughput transcriptome sequencing to identify key regulatory networks.

In recent years, transcriptome sequencing has become a pivotal tool for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying abscission. For example, transcriptome analysis of soybean leaf AZs identified 188 transcription factors that were significantly enriched during early abscission (0–12 h), suggesting their roles in regulating organ polarity, hormone balance, and cell differentiation, thereby influencing abscission-layer cell fate and the initiation of cell separation [13]. Fei et al. [14] used girdling plus defoliation (GPD) treatment to induce massive macadamia fruit abscission within a few days and identified several pathways and candidate genes related to abscission through weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Furthermore, integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis of the rose AZ revealed that STS suppressed abscission by reducing cell wall hydrolase activity and altering key metabolite levels. It also modulated the expression of associated genes involved in the pentose phosphate and amino acid pathways, as well as phytohormone (auxin and ethylene) and transcription factor networks [15]. In contrast, transcriptome analysis of the petal AZ in the ethylene-sensitive rose (Rosa bourboniana) demonstrated that ethylene treatment induced 3,700 differentially expressed genes within 8 h, during which the ethylene signaling pathway was systematically activated, whereas the JA, auxin, and light signaling pathway was significantly inhibited [16]. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying corolla abscission in blueberry remain unclear.

Blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.), often referred to as the “king of berries”, has recently attracted considerable scientific attention due to its high content of phenolic compounds, including anthocyanins, flavonols, and procyanidins [17]. This has led to growing consumer demand and a rapid expansion of blueberry cultivation worldwide. However, the rapid expansion of blueberry production has been accompanied by an increased incidence of Botrytis cinerea infection following pollination, which threatens the sustainable development of the blueberry industry [18].

In this study, RNA-seq analysis was performed on the corolla abscission zones of two blueberry cultivars with contrasting abscission phenotypes, ‘Eureka-Sunrise’ (‘L25’, persistent corolla) and ‘Magica’ (‘F6’, caducous corolla). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses to elucidate the biological processes and pathways associated with corolla abscission. In addition, WGCNA was conducted to further explore the coordinated gene expression patterns underlying this process. In the ‘L25’ cultivar, the persistent corollas remains attached after pollination, increasing the risk of gray mold infection caused by Botrytis cinerea. As a result, manual removal of withered corollas is often necessary, leading to increased labor costs during production. Therefore, this study provides new insights into the molecular regulatory pathways underlying corolla abscission and establishes a foundation for improving blueberry production efficiency.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sample preparation

Two blueberry cultivars exhibiting contrasting corolla abscission phenotypes, ‘L25’ and ‘F6’ were used as experimental materials. The plants were obtained from the greenhouse of Shenglan Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd, Gaoyou, Jiangsu Province, China. The ‘F6’ cultivar exhibited caducous corolla after anthesis, whereas ‘L25’ exhibited persistent corollas that remained attached throughout fruit development. Samples of the corolla AZ were collected by dissecting a segment less than 1 mm in length that included the proximal petal base and the adjacent receptacle tissue, following the method previously described for rose petals [19]. Each flower was labeled with a small tag indicating its bloom date. AZ tissues were harvested at 0, 3, and 6 days after the peak flowering. The collected samples were recorded as L25_1, L25_2, L25_3, F6_1, F6_2, and F6_3 for the respective time points of each cultivar. After collection, the AZ tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at -80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction and enzyme activity assays. Three biological replicates were collected for each cultivar at each time point.

Cytological observation of cells in the AZ of blueberry corolla

Samples of the corolla AZs from different flower stages of two blueberry cultivars, ‘L25’ and ‘F6’, were collected during the same flowering period. These samples were designated as L25_1, L25_2, L25_3, F6_1, F6_2, and F6_3, and fixed in FAA’s solution (formalin: acetic acid:70% ethanol = 5:5:90, v/v/v) for approximately 24 h. Following fixation, specimens were submitted to Servicebio Technology for sequential processing including dehydration, paraffin embedding, microtome sectioning, and safranin-fast green dual staining. Histomorphological characteristics of the AZ tissues were examined under an Olympus microscope, and representative photomicrographs were captured for documentation.

RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 18 samples (two cultivars × three time points × three biological replicates) using an RNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and DNA contamination were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, while RNA quality was detected using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (A260/A230 ratios > 1.8, and A260/A280 values between 1.8 and 2.2). The construction of the cDNA library and Illumina sequencing were performed by Bioyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Qualified RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq platform.

Detection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using fastp (v0.21.0) to obtain clean reads for further analysis. The clean reads were aligned to the blueberry reference genome (https://www.vaccinium.org/analysis/49) using HISAT2 software (v2.1.0). Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie (v2.1.5), which generated both raw read count matrices and normalized expression values in fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Based on the raw read counts, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software (v1.30.1) with screened conditions as follows: expression difference multiple |log2FoldChange| > 1, significant padj ≤ 0.05.

Gene enrichment analysis of DEGs in blueberry

DEGs identified between the two blueberry cultivars at different developmental stages were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the R package clusterProfiler. Only GO terms belonging to the biological process (BP) category were considered. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were determined based on a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Enrichment analyses were performed independently for each developmental stage.

WGCNA analysis

A gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package (Version 4.0.2) in R software [20] to identify key pathways involved in corolla abscission. After filtering low-expression genes (FPKM ≤ 2.0), a total of 14,501 genes were retained to construct co-expression network. To build the network, the appropriate soft threshold power (β) was selected by applying the scale-free topology fit index (R²) as the criterion. The value of β at which R² first exceeded 0.80 was selected. Then, the parameter settings were as follows: power value = 26, minModuleSize = 100, and mergeCutHeight = 0.25. The Cytoscape software (Version 3.8.2) [21] was used to visualize the network.

Using sample trait data (variety type and time point), we correlated module eigengenes to identify modules associated with cultivar differences (‘L25’ vs. ‘F6’) and developmental progression. The trait variables included: abscission-related variety (1 for ‘F6’, 0 for ‘L25’), developmental stage (1 for peak flowering, 0 for other stages), and a time-variety interaction trait. For the interaction trait, samples of ‘L25’ collected at successive developmental stages were represented as 1, 3, and 5, whereas the corresponding stages of ‘F6’ were represented as 2, 4, and 6. This sequential coding scheme was designed to simultaneously capture temporal expression dynamics and cultivar-associated differences, thereby facilitating the identification of gene modules exhibiting coordinated developmental trends as well as differential expression between cultivars at equivalent developmental stages. Module eigengenes (MEs) were computed for each co-expression module, followed by hierarchical clustering of MEs. Modules exhibiting high eigengene similarity were subsequently merged to generate consolidated modules. Sample-specific modules were identified based on significant correlations between module eigengenes and sample traits (|r| > 0.7, p < 0.05). Genes from the four significantly correlated modules were then subsequently extracted for enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler, with significantly enriched terms defined as those with an adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

To assess the reliability of the transcriptome data, a total of 18 RNA samples used for RNA-seq were also subjected to qRT-PCR validation. The first-strand cDNA was synthesized using a Prime-Script™II First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Dalian, China) in a final volume of 20 µL, following the manufacturer’sprotocol. qRT-PCR was carried out using SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CWBIO, Taizhou, China) under the following cycling conditions: 30 s of predenaturation at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 90 s. The GAPDH was chosen as the internal reference gene, and the primers were designed using Beacon Designer software v8. All the primers in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 1. The relative expression levels were calculated with the formula of 2−ΔΔCt [22]. All procedures were performed in triplicate, both biological and technical.

Verification of cell wall degradation-related enzyme activities during corolla abscission

Polygalacturonase (PG) and cellulase (CEL) are key enzymes involved in cell wall degradation during organ abscission. Their activities were measured in the corolla AZs of ‘F6’ and ‘L25’ at different developmental stages using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates. Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Differences in PG and CEL activities between the two cultivars at each developmental stage were evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed t-test at P < 0.05.

PG activity was measured according to the following steps: Crude enzyme extracts were prepared from corolla AZ tissues. The samples were homogenized on ice with 5–10 times volume of extraction buffer (mL) and then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and kept on ice for subsequent assays. For the enzyme activity assay, the supernatant was mixed with reagent 1 and incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding reagent 2, followed by heating in boiling water for 5 min and rapid cooling on ice. A 200 µL aliquot of the reaction solution was taken, and its absorbance at 540 nm was measured. △A was calculated as A (measuring tube) – A (control tube). PG activity (expressed as mg/h/g fresh weight) was calculated using the formula: 5.045 × (ΔA + 0.008) / W.

CEL activity was measured according to the following procedure: Crude enzyme extracts were prepared from corolla AZ tissues. The samples were homogenized on ice with 5–10 times their volume of extraction buffer (mL) and centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and kept on ice for subsequent assays. The supernatant was then mixed with reagent 1, reagent 2, and water, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking for 1 h. After that, the mixture was heated at 90 °C for 15 min, rapidly cooled on ice, and centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 25 °C to obtain the saccharified supernatant. Finally, the saccharified supernatant was mixed with reagent 3 in proportion, heated at 90 °C for 10 min, and rapidly cooled on ice. A 200 µL aliquot of the reaction solution was taken, and its absorbance was measured at 620 nm. △A was calculated as A (measuring tube) – A (control tube). CEL activity (expressed as µg/min/g fresh weight) was calculated using the formula: 79.6 × (ΔA + 0.0462) / W.

Results

Cytological characteristics of the corolla AZs in two blueberry cultivars

To investigate the cytological basis of corolla shedding in blueberry, the cultivars ‘L25’ and ‘F6’ were selected for comparative analysis. Figure 1A showed the histological structure of the corolla AZs in the two cultivars at different developmental stages. During peak flowering, both cultivars formed 3–5 layers of densely arranged small cells at the base of the corolla. In ‘F6’, cell separation initiated three days after full bloom, and abscission was completed within six days. By contrast, AZ cells in ‘L25’ gradually enlarged by the third to sixth day, ultimately resembling the surrounding non-abscission cells (Fig. 1B). These cytological differences between ‘F6’ and ‘L25’ showed that variations in cell wall degradation and tissue integrity may contribute to their distinct corolla abscission phenotypes.

Analysis of transcriptome sequencing data and identification of DEGs

To identify the possible genes that led to the variance in the corolla abscission among different blueberry genotypes, ‘L25’ and ‘F6’ were subjected to RNA sequencing. A total of 18 samples, assigned to six groups were sequenced. After removing the low-quality data, the clean reads ranged from 39,905,446 to 64,251,206 were retained. The Q30 value of all libraries exceeded 97.59%, with an average GC content of 46.4%. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome, with overall mapping rates exceeding 90% across all samples (Table S2). Among these, approximately 53–56% of reads were uniquely mapped.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 36.08% and 16.04% of the total variance, respectively. The results indicated that the 18 samples were distinctly separated into six groups. Biological replicates (three per stage) clustered within the same region, while samples from different cultivars were clearly distinguished (Fig. 2A). To identify DEGs associated with corolla abscission after peak flowering between the two cultivars, three pairwise comparison groups (F6_1_vs_L25_1, F6_2_vs_L25_2, and F6_3_vs_L25_3) were performed using raw read count data. The numbers of DEGs identified in the three comparisons mentioned above were 24,432, 28,145, and 26,192, respectively (Fig. 2B). The relatively large number of DEGs may be associated with the extensive gene repertoire of the blueberry genome (128,559 annotated genes), together with its polyploid nature and high level of gene duplication, which can result in multiple homologous genes exhibiting differential expression under the same developmental stage. In addition, MA plots were generated for each comparison to visualize the distribution of gene expression changes (Fig. S1). The results showed that DEGs were widely distributed across different expression levels, with both upregulated and downregulated genes clearly distinguished in each stage.

To further investigate the biological functions of DEGs identified between the two blueberry cultivars at different developmental stages, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for each comparison group. At 3 days after full bloom, GO terms were predominantly enriched in processes including plant-type cell wall organization, DNA duplex unwinding, fatty acid biosynthetic process, systemic acquired resistance, and cellular response to oxidative stress (Fig. 3C). Correspondingly, pathways associated with DNA replication, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, were significantly enriched (Fig. 3D).

At 6 days after full bloom, photosynthesis, sulfate transport, cellular response to oxidative stress, plant-type cell wall organization, and glycine decarboxylation via glycine cleavage system were significantly enriched (Fig. 3E). Moreover, enrichment of amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and energy metabolism suggested differences in metabolic adjustment between the two cultivars (Fig. 3B, D, F). Overall, these findings suggested that blueberry corolla abscission was associated with coordinated changes in oxidative stress response, metabolic activity, and cell wall remodeling.

Construction of co-expression network

After excluding lowly expressed genes (FPKM ≤ 2), 14,501 genes were retained for WGCNA analysis. The overall relationships among the 18 samples were shown in the cluster dendrogram (Fig. 4A). To construct a scale-free network, a suitable soft threshold power (β) was selected. When the β value reached 26, R2 was greater than 0.8 for the first time (Fig. 4B, C). Therefore, this β value fulfilled the scale-free network requirement and was selected as optimal for constructing the co-expression network. A total of 7 distinct modules (labelled with different colours) were identified based on similarities in gene expression patterns, including blue (5522 DEGs), brown (1321 DEGs), green (839 DEGs), yellow (860 DEGs), red (285 DEGs), turquoise (5639 DEGs), and grey (35 DEGs) (Fig. 5A).

Functional enrichment analysis of genes in significant modules

GO enrichment analysis was performed on genes from biologically significant modules to define their functional properties (Fig. 6). The turquoise module, in which gene expression levels were higher in ‘F6’ than in ‘L25’, was significantly enriched in processes such as superoxide metabolic process, nucleoside diphosphate phosphorylation, glucose metabolic process and so on (Fig. 6). The enriched processes in the blue module were mainly related to metabolic regulation, stress response and transmembrane transport, including auxin metabolic process, fatty acid biosynthetic process, cell wall macromolecule catabolic process, cellular response to oxidative stress, copper ion transmembrane transport and many more (Fig. 6). The processes in the green module were mainly associated with hormone regulation and flower organ development, such as the ethylene-activated signaling pathway, regulation of abscisic acid biosynthetic process, flower development and so on (Fig. 6). In the brown module, enriched processes were mainly related to energy metabolism and hormone regulation, including strigolactone biosynthetic process, carotenoid biosynthetic process, cell redox homeostasis and others (Fig. 6).

Dynamic changes in PG and CEL activities during corolla abscission

PG and CEL activities showed distinct dynamic patterns between the two blueberry cultivars during corolla abscission (Fig. 8). PG activity in ‘F6’ increased markedly from approximately 31 U/g fresh weight at 0 days after full bloom to about 65 U/g fresh weight at 3 days, followed by a decline at 6 days. In contrast, PG activity in ‘L25’ remained relatively stable throughout the examined stages. Notably, PG activity in ‘F6’ was significantly higher than that in ‘L25’ at 3 days after full bloom, coinciding with the active corolla abscission stage (Fig. 8A). CEL activity in ‘L25’ decreased progressively during flower development, whereas relatively stable activity levels were maintained in ‘F6’ (Fig. 8B). At 6 days after full bloom, CEL activity in ‘F6’ was significantly higher than that in ‘L25’.

Discussion

Corolla abscission is a complex developmental process that directly affects fruit quality and disease susceptibility in blueberry. In this study, we combined cytological observation, enzyme activity assays, and transcriptome-based WGCNA to systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying corolla abscission in two blueberry cultivars with contrasting abscission phenotypes. Our results suggested that corolla abscission was associated with coordinated changes in hormone signaling, cell wall remodeling, and oxidative stress-related processes.

Hormone signaling pathways are closely associated with corolla abscission

Plant organ abscission is closely linked to the biosynthesis, response, signal transduction, and metabolism of hormones, in which different hormones play distinct regulatory roles. In particular, ethylene plays a key role in regulating organ senescence and abscission. In roses, ethylene-induced petal abscission was mediated by altered gene expression within the AZ [16]. Conversely, treatment with the ethylene perception inhibitor Silver Thiosulfate (STS) delayed rose petal abscission by suppressing ethylene action and reducing the activities of cell wall-degrading enzymes [15]. Additionally, ABA has also been reported to participate in abscission regulation. Previous studies showed that ABA promoted citrus fruitlet abscission by enhancing ACC accumulation, a key precursor of ethylene [23]. Similarly, studies in Lupinus luteus have demonstrated that ABA promotes flower abscission by up-regulating ethylene biosynthesis genes and increasing ACC accumulation in the AZ [24]. These findings indicate that ABA and ethylene may act synergistically during the regulation of abscission. In our study, the ethylene-activated signaling pathway (GO:0009873) and regulation of abscisic acid biosynthesis (GO:0010115) were significantly enriched in the green module (Fig. 6). Expression levels in this module were generally higher in ‘F6’ than in ‘L25’. These results suggest that the coordinated activation of ethylene and ABA signaling pathways may contribute to the rapid corolla abscission in blueberry.

Auxin is another central regulator of plant growth and development, influencing processes ranging from cell division and embryogenesis to tissue patterning, as well as later events such as organ senescence and abscission [4, 25]. In sweet cherry, fruit abscission coincided with reduced expression of the auxin biosynthesis gene (ɑ-Trp) [26]. Similarly, in tomato, three IAA-amino acid hydrolases (SlILL1, SlILL5, and SlILL6) delayed flower pedicel abscission by hydrolyzing IAA-Ile to release active free auxin, thereby regulating auxin concentration in the AZ [27]. In the present study, the abscission-resistant cultivar ‘L25’ exhibited higher expression of genes in the blue module (Fig. 6), which was enriched not only in auxin metabolic process (GO:0009850), but also in multiple biological processes, including cellular response to oxidative stress (GO:0034599), protein dephosphorylation (GO:0006470), and cell wall macromolecule catabolic process (GO:0016998). This pattern suggested that delayed corolla abscission in ‘L25’ might involve coordinated regulation of multiple physiological processes rather than auxin signaling alone. Notably, genes related to cell wall modification, including chitin catabolic process (GO:0006032), were also highly expressed in ‘L25’. Plant chitinases are known to participate in both cell wall remodeling and defense responses, which may help maintain cell wall integrity [28]. Taken together, these findings suggested that coordinated regulation of auxin metabolism, stress responses, and cell wall-associated pathways might help maintain AZ stability in ‘L25’, thereby delaying corolla abscission.

The blueberry corolla abscission might be associated with cell wall degrading enzyme activity

The activity of cell wall-degrading enzymes, such as pectinases and cellulases, is a key driver of plant organ abscission, primarily through the breakdown of the primary cell wall [29]. Our study comparing the easy-abscission cultivar ‘F6’ and the abscission-resistant cultivar ‘L25’ revealed clear differences in both enzyme activity and transcriptional regulation of cell wall-related processes. WGCNA combined with GO enrichment analysis consistently identified cell wall-related pathways, including “plant-type cell wall organization” (Fig. 3) and “cell wall macromolecule catabolic process” (Fig. 6), indicating that dynamic remodeling of the cell wall is closely associated with corolla abscission in blueberry. This was further supported by the dynamics of PG and CEL activities. In ‘F6’, PG activity increased markedly at 3 days after full bloom, coinciding with the onset of abscission, and declined after completion of shedding by day 6 (Fig. 8). Meanwhile, CEL activity remained consistently higher in ‘F6’ than in ‘L25’. Supporting evidence from other species further confirms the conserved role of these enzymes in abscission. For example, PG and PME accumulation was associated with drought-induced flower shedding in yellow lupin [30]; ethylene promoted tomato pedicel abscission via upregulation of SlTAPG1 and SlTAPG4 [31, 32]; and MdPG2 expression was correlated with fruitlet abscission in apple [33]. Moreover, in rose, ethylene treatment significantly increased the activities of PG and CEL in the AZ, further supporting the role of these enzymes in promoting cell wall degradation during abscission [15]. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that ethylene treatment markedly affected the expression of numerous cell wall-related genes, highlighting the central role of ethylene in regulating cell wall remodeling during abscission.

Cellulose breakdown is achieved through the collaborative action of a multi-enzyme extracellular system known as cellulase. This system primarily comprises three key enzymes: endoglucanase (EG), exoglucanase or cellobiohydrolase (CBH), and β-glucosidase (BGL) [34]. CEL is generally understood to facilitate cell separation by hydrolyzing cellulose [3], and has been reported to be upregulated in abscising tissues such as in Morus laevigata [35]. In litchi, the HD-Zip transcription factor LcHB2 directly activated the expression of two cellulase genes LcCEL2 and LcCEL8, which acted as key regulators of fruitlet abscission by promoting cell wall degradation and precocious organ shedding [36]. In Arabidopsis, AtCEL6 (At4g39010) played a key role in silique dehiscence by driving the breakdown of cells in the separation layer [37]. These observations were consistent with our results and suggested that coordinated degradation of PG and CEL may play a conserved role in abscission across species. Notably, the distinct enzyme activity profiles observed between ‘F6’ and ‘L25’ suggest that variation in the timing and extent of cell wall degradation is associated with differences in corolla abscission capacity. This is consistent with the view that hormonal regulation coordinates the activation of cell wall-modifying enzymes, ultimately facilitating cell separation during corolla abscission.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Although this study provides insights into the regulatory processes underlying corolla abscission in blueberry, several limitations remain. The sampling strategy included only three developmental stages selected to represent major transitions during the abscission process. However, a finer temporal resolution would enable a more detailed characterization of the dynamic molecular and physiological changes. In addition, the involvement of hormone signaling pathways was inferred primarily from transcriptomic data. Although genes related to ethylene, ABA, and auxin pathways showed differential expression between cultivars, endogenous hormone levels were not directly quantified. Future studies incorporating hormone profiling approaches, such as gas chromatography for ethylene and LC–MS/MS analysis for ABA and IAA, would help clarify the relationship between transcriptional regulation and hormone dynamics. Furthermore, the regulatory modules and candidate pathways identified through WGCNA and enrichment analyses still require experimental validation to confirm their biological functions in corolla abscission. Approaches including expanded expression analysis, reverse genetics, and heterologous functional assays would provide further evidence for their regulatory roles. Additional physiological and biochemical investigations, such as reactive oxygen species detection and detailed characterization of cell wall composition, would also provide valuable support for the proposed mechanisms. Overall, the integration molecular, physiological, and biochemical evidence will be valuable for building a more complete understanding of the mechanisms governing corolla abscission in blueberry.

Conclusions

In this study, corolla abscission in blueberry was investigated through an integrative approach combining cytological observation, enzyme activity assays, and transcriptome analysis with WGCNA. The results showed that the two cultivars exhibited distinct regulatory patterns associated with abscission. Hormone-related pathways, particularly ethylene and ABA signaling, were mainly enriched in the easy-abscission cultivar ‘F6’, suggesting that these pathways may be associated with the regulation of corolla abscission. In contrast, the abscission-resistant cultivar ‘L25’ showed enrichment of processes related to auxin metabolism and oxidative stress responses, which may contribute to delayed abscission. In addition, cell wall-related processes were consistently identified in both transcriptomic and physiological analyses. The increased activities of PG and CEL observed in ‘F6’ were associated with the progression of abscission, indicating that cell wall remodeling plays an important role in this process. Overall, these findings suggested that corolla abscission in blueberry was regulated through coordinated changes in hormone signaling pathways and cell wall dynamics. However, further studies incorporating hormone quantification and functional validation of candidate genes will be required to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms more comprehensively.
