MERIT Feedback Elicits Better Bargaining in LLM Negotiators
Jihwan Oh Murad Aghazada Yooju Shin Se-Young Yun Taehyeon Kim

Abstract
Bargaining is often regarded as a logical arena rather than an art or a matter of intuition, yet Large Language Models (LLMs) still struggle to navigate it due to limited strategic depth and difficulty adapting to complex human factors. Current benchmarks rarely capture this limitation. To bridge this gap, we present an utility feedback centric framework. Our contributions are: (i) AgoraBench , a new benchmark spanning nine challenging settings (e.g., deception, monopoly) that supports diverse strategy modeling; (ii) human-aligned, economically grounded metrics derived from utility theory. This is operationalized via agent utility, negotiation power, and acquisition ratio that implicitly measure how well the negotiation aligns with human preference and (iii) a human preference grounded dataset with learning pipeline that strengthens LLMs’ bargaining ability through both prompting and finetuning. Empirical results indicate that baseline LLM strategies often diverge from human preferences, while our mechanism substantially improves negotiation performance, yielding deeper strategic behavior and stronger opponent awareness.

AgoraBench
mirrors real-world economic bargaining across nine distinct market types, curated to surface specific commercial negotiation challenges. It provides both an online simulator (§

3.1
Simulator environment

Two LLM agents engage in negotiations within the simulator using a structured
Thoughts–Talk–Action
schema
(
42
;
41
)
, also referred to as ReAct (
Figure 1
(a)). Each agent operates within a constrained action space (BUY, SELL, REJECT, DEAL, and QUIT) following the framework established by
41
. Each dialogue is grounded in a specific buyer–seller scenario defined by key hyperparameters: the market setting, agent profiles, and product attributes such as cost, willingness to pay (WTP), and initial ask. The
AgoraBench
simulator encompasses nine distinct market regimes, constructed by instantiating five market settings under the
Single
condition and four additional settings under the
Multi
condition (
Figure 1
(c)). A full specification of the dataset format and fields is provided in Appendix
A
.

•

Vanilla market:
A baseline, product negotiation with no additional complicating factors.

•

Deceptive market:
Settings in which either party may misrepresent information (e.g., product quality), testing agents’ ability to identify reliable signals. Except for the vanilla case, all other markets are built on this deception layer, with further conditions added atop it.

•

Monopoly market:
A single-seller environment that limits buyer choice, enabling analysis of bargaining under asymmetric power.

•

Installment possible market:
Introduces deferred or staggered payments, bringing time-sensitive financial trade-offs and flexible terms into the dialogue.

•

Negative perception market:
The seller carries a prior reputational disadvantage (e.g., scandals), biasing buyers toward lower offers and creating distinct strategic dynamics as the seller attempts to counteract this effect.

•

Single product market:
Buyers negotiate over a single item with no substitution across alternatives. This isolates bargaining behavior in a focused setting where outcomes depend solely on the agreed price and the buyer’s willingness-to-pay.

•

Multi product market:
Buyers consider multiple items and can substitute away from a preferred option due to constraints (e.g., budget). This highlights the trade-off between product preference and cost savings. We layer this condition atop each non-vanilla market.

4.1
Merit
: Formulation

The performance of a language model as a buyer is quantified by
Merit
through a score reflecting how effectively the agent achieves its objectives within a given negotiation scenario. In microeconomic terms, this score corresponds to the
overall satisfaction
or
total benefit
an agent derives from a transaction, paralleling the notion of
cardinal utility
where a measurable value is assigned to outcomes. Specifically, the
Merit
score for a buyer is a weighted combination of three key components:
Consumer Surplus (CS)
,
Negotiation Power (NP)
, and
Acquisition Ratio (AR)
. Each component captures a distinct economic concept related to consumer behavior, bargaining dynamics, and preference fulfillment, as defined in
Equation 1
:

\vskip-5.0pt\textsc{Merit}_{\text{buyer}}=\alpha\times\text{CS}+\beta\times\text{NP}+\gamma\times\text{AR}

(1)

Consumer surplus

In economics,
consumer surplus
refers to the net benefit (or satisfaction) a consumer derives from purchasing a product or service
(
29
)
. It traditionally measures the difference between the consumer’s
willingness to pay
(
P_{\text{wtp}})
and the actual price paid (
P_{\text{deal}}
). Within
Merit
, we normalize this surplus relative to the potential surplus range determined by the seller’s production cost (
P_{\text{cost}}
):

\text{Consumer Surplus (CS)}=\frac{P_{\text{wtp}}-P_{\text{deal}}}{P_{\text{wtp}}-P_{\text{cost}}}

(2)

Bounding CS between 0 and 1 translates consumer surplus into a scaled,
cardinal measure
of how effectively the buyer maximized net benefits. A value approaching 1 indicates a highly favorable deal, while 0 suggests an unfavorable outcome or an infeasible price.

Negotiation power

Bargaining outcomes also hinge on the buyer’s ability to
shift
the final price in their favor—an aspect tied to the broader economic concept of
bargaining power

9
;
30
.
Merit
incorporates this through:

\text{Negotiation Power (NP)}=\frac{P_{\text{initial}}-P_{\text{deal}}}{P_{\text{initial}}-P_{\text{cost}}}

(3)

where
P_{\text{initial}}
is the seller’s initial asking price. A higher NP indicates that the buyer successfully negotiated the deal price downward from the initial offer. In classic bargaining theory (e.g., Nash bargaining
7
), each party’s utility share depends on their relative power. Here, NP complements the
cardinal utility
of consumer surplus by capturing how effectively the buyer reclaims surplus that might otherwise go to the seller. Values near 1 imply maximal movement from the seller’s initial price, reflecting strong buyer influence, whereas values near 0 imply that the buyer gained little ground in negotiations.

Acquisition ratio of desired goods

Finally, a consumer’s satisfaction also depends on obtaining the
right
product, reflecting the ordinal aspect of utility—that is, how closely the outcome aligns with the consumer’s preference ranking. We quantify this via an
Acquisition Ratio
, which measures how semantically similar the buyer’s acquired items are to their desired items. By embedding items in a high-dimensional space using
text-embedding-ada-002
, we compute:

\text{Acquisition Ratio (AR)}=\frac{\mathbf{v_{\text{acquired}}}\cdot\mathbf{v_{\text{desired}}}}{\|\mathbf{v_{\text{acquired}}}\|\|\mathbf{v_{\text{desired}}}\|}

(4)

where
\mathbf{v_{\text{acquired}}}
and
\mathbf{v_{\text{desired}}}
represent embedding vectors of the items actually purchased and initially sought, respectively. A higher AR indicates that the buyer’s outcome closely matches their
top preferences
, embodying the idea that utility is also determined by which specific goods are consumed, not merely how profitable the purchase is.

4.2
Human preference alignment

Figure 3:
Demonstration that a human-aligned metric outperforms a purely profit-based one.

To ensure
Merit
reflects human judgments, we ran an Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) study. Participants were shown pairs of negotiation dialogues (20 comparisons per product; 180 participants total) and asked to choose the better one. The survey included both same-item price comparisons and trade-offs between similar items; full details are in Appendix
C
.

The coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in
Equation 1
were optimized globally using merged data from all product categories based on Bradley-Terry model
8
. Since only relative magnitudes matter in the Bradley-Terry model, we scale them for interpretability so that their sum is 3, as follows:

\scriptstyle\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3,\hskip 8.19447pt\beta^{\prime}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3,\hskip 8.19447pt\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3.

After optimization, the final scaled coefficients are:
\alpha^{\prime}=1.0139
,
\beta^{\prime}=0.8812
,
\gamma^{\prime}=1.1049
.

As shown in
Figure 3
, this process confirmed that
Merit
aligns more closely with human preferences than traditional profit-based ones (e.g., achieving an ROC AUC of 0.80 vs. 0.68 for the latter). This human-aligned metric is subsequently used for model evaluation and as feedback in our system. Details on the optimization of the metric model are provided in Appendix
C
.

In this section, we evaluate the negotiation capabilities of contemporary LLMs, specifically the
GPT-series
and

Anchoring effect and first-mover advantage (
Table 3
)

We investigated the
anchoring effect

(
32
)
, a cognitive bias where initial information heavily influences subsequent decisions. Experiments in the vanilla market, using two distinct initial camera prices ($550 and $520, denoted Market 1
∗
and Market 2
∗∗
respectively) set by the seller, demonstrated this bias: higher initial anchor prices generally led to higher final deal prices. Furthermore, when the buyer initiated the first offer (Market 3
∗∗∗
), the average deal price was even lower compared to scenarios where the seller proposed first, highlighting a potential first-mover advantage for the buyer in price setting.

Irrational concessions: Unstable anchoring (
Table 19
)

We observed unnatural negotiation behavior in LLMs, especially smaller models such as
gpt-4o-mini
. In
Table 19
, the buyer shows unstable anchoring: after proposing a price, it reverses direction with strictly lower counteroffers, effectively backtracking on its stated stance. Concretely, the buyer drops from $480 to $475 and $450, then later returns to $480 before agreement. Such reversed concessions are atypical in human bargaining, where offers are generally monotone toward settlement, and they weaken the buyer’s anchor by signaling inconsistent
willingness to pay
reflecting anchor instability rather than strategic bargaining.

Impact of market conditions (
Table 2
)

•

Deceptive.
Allowing buyers to use deception generally
improves buyer outcomes
:
Merit
increases vs. vanilla (avg. +0.10) and deal rates rise substantially (+10.62%p). Some GPT models trade slightly lower deal rates for higher scores (e.g.,

•

Monopoly.
Monopoly consistently harms buyers across models: deal rates drop by 3.34% (single-item) and 8.80% (multi item), while

•

Installment.
Installment options have mixed effects: in single-item settings they often increase deal rates but at the cost of worse buyer prices (lower
Merit
); in multi-item settings, added bargaining complexity tends to reduce deal rates overall.

•

Negative perception.
A negative prior about the seller consistently reduces deal rates, with a sharper effect in single-item negotiations (few outside options). In multi-item settings, substitutes provide limited mitigation but do not eliminate the decline.

•

Multi product.

Across conditions, multi-item settings generally exhibit higher deal rates than single-item ones, consistent with the presence of substitutes that facilitate agreement. However, this benefit is not uniform: under
installment
, GPT models show a reversal where deal rates drop despite substitutes. Such sign reversals serve as a useful diagnostic of market compatibility, indicating failures to leverage multi product option when bargaining becomes multi-dimensional.

By comparing each market condition against the benchmark’s baseline (
deceptive
or vanilla) in terms of both deal rate and buyer
Merit
, we can assess whether LLM’s
market compatibility
they align with patterns typical in human bargaining or deviates from them. In particular, directional shifts across conditions (e.g., reduced agreement and utility under
monopoly
, or trust-driven drops under

Merit

We compared ICL-MF with two baselines; ReAct

Analysis of results.
OG-Narrator often underperforms ReAct due to its rigid price schedule, which fails to adapt to opponent temperament. Against patient sellers like
gemini-1.5-pro
, it excessively prolongs dialogue (
Figure 4
), leading to bargaining impasses
3
;
20
;
4
, without reaching a deal. Conversely, with impatient sellers like
deepseek-v3.2
, negotiations terminate abruptly as opponents exit prematurely. While ReAct settles too quickly due to premature assumptions
26
, ICL-MF balances efficiency and effectiveness through appropriate negotiation lengths. Crucially, ICL-MF demonstrates a clear positive correlation between model capability and performance, better leveraging the inherent strengths of stronger LLMs.

Merit
elicits opponent-aware reasoning.
Adapting
Merit

Conclusion

We introduce a dataset and benchmark that mirror economically grounded market complexity, a human-aligned multi-facet metric, and an in-context learning and training method that uses this metric as feedback to enhance negotiation skills. By capturing both profit and acquisition priorities, our framework fills gaps left by profit-only metrics and, we hope, helps the community build more effective, human-aligned LLM bargaining agents.

Limitations

While our work advances LLM bargaining, several limitations suggest avenues for future exploration. First, our study is primarily buyer-centric, overlooking seller-specific objectives such as inventory risk or tiered profit targets; future work should incorporate a dedicated seller perspective. Second, while
AgoraBench
covers diverse scenarios, it does not yet capture global market subtleties like regulatory contexts or cultural norms. Third, we do not cover tool-augmented agentic environments (e.g., LLM-agent tool use for local retrieval or search), which could materially affect bargaining behavior by enabling grounded, real-time access to external information (e.g., comparable prices or product specifications). While building such environments would be valuable, it is beyond the scope of this study; we leave tool-integrated settings (including local search) to future work.

Potential risks

A key potential risk of our work concerns negotiation ethics, which are primarily implicated by the benchmark’s deception setting. With the exception of the vanilla market, most scenarios (Deceptive, Monopoly, Installment, Negative Perception) are constructed atop a deception layer that permits agents to misrepresent product attributes or bargaining signals. While this design choice is intended to stress-test strategic depth and robustness under realistic informational asymmetries, it may inadvertently incentivize or normalize deceptive tactics. Evaluating (and potentially tuning) LLM negotiators for effectiveness under such rules can increase the likelihood that deployed agents learn to use lying or strategic misrepresentation as an instrument for goal achievement, which could erode user trust and cause concrete harms in real-world contexts such as pricing, contracting, and customer support.

Appendix C
Human preference dataset collection

Survey for human preference

To collect human preference data and align our metrics with human judgment, we conducted an online survey on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Each worker completed one survey containing 20 market-related binary-choice questions, which required no expert knowledge. We initially estimated that the survey would take approximately 10 minutes to complete and set the payment to $1.45 per survey, corresponding to an expected effective hourly wage of $8.70 above the U.S. federal minimum wage of $7.25
18
.

In practice, the average completion time was 26.1 minutes, which corresponds to an effective hourly wage of $3.33. To improve data quality, we incorporated an internal consistency check using the first 10 dialogue sets: for Question 1 in each of these sets, we included an unambiguous attention-check item for which an attentive annotator would be expected to select the same option. We retained responses only from annotators who selected this expected option in at least 7 out of the 10 sets; all other annotators’ responses were excluded from our analysis. As noted in the survey instructions (
Figure 8
), we informed participants that the collected responses would be used for research analyses of negotiation strategies.

We evaluated a total of nine products and recruited 20 participants per product, yielding 180 survey responses in total. We used only responses from participants who passed the attention-check described above. Each participant was presented with 20 pairs of negotiation dialogues drawn from bargaining experiments for the corresponding product, where the product context and price information were explicitly provided. The 20 dialogue pairs were divided into two distinct categories:

1.

Same Product Negotiation (Pairs 1–10):

For the first ten pairs, both dialogues within each pair resulted in negotiations for the same desired product for the buyer.
Participants evaluated dialogues based solely on the quality and outcomes of the negotiation, given identical product contexts.

2.

Different Product Negotiation (Pairs 11–20):

The remaining ten pairs presented dialogues where the negotiated products differed, introducing complexity into participants’ judgments, as they had to consider not only the negotiation quality but also the suitability of the alternative products.

Each dialogue set involved varying negotiated prices, ensuring participants’ preferences were influenced by realistic price differences. Each participant assessed dialogues from the buyer’s perspective based on the following evaluation criteria:

1.

Preferred Negotiation Scenario:

The participants chose the negotiation they found to be the most favorable, considering multiple attributes, including negotiation outcome, budget adherence, and product features.

2.

Realistic Purchasing Scenario:

The participants identified which negotiation scenario was closer to realistic purchasing decisions.

3.

Negotiation Strategy Assessment:

The participants evaluated the negotiation strategies, considering factors such as the price of the product and the availability of cheaper alternative products.

Optimization of the Metric Model

To align our metric with these human judgments, we employ the Bradley-Terry model
(
8
)
. The probability that entity
i
(parameter
\theta_{i}
) is preferred to entity
j
(parameter
\theta_{j}
) is:

P(\text{$i$ is preferred to $j$})=\frac{\exp(\theta_{i})}{\exp(\theta_{i})+\exp(\theta_{j})}.

(7)

Taking the log-odds yields:

\log\biggl(\frac{P(\text{$i$ preferred})}{1-P(\text{$i$ preferred})}\biggr)=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}.

(8)

For two negotiation conversations (1 and 2) and a human choice, we first compute differences in our three core metrics:

\scriptstyle dCS=CS_{1}-CS_{2},\hskip 8.19447ptdNP=NP_{1}-NP_{2},\hskip 8.19447ptdAR=AR_{1}-AR_{2}.

A linear score is then defined as:

\text{score}=\alpha\cdot dCS+\beta\cdot dNP+\gamma\cdot dAR,

which plays the role of
(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})
in
Equation 7
. This score is passed through a logistic function to yield the probability
p
that conversation 1 is preferred:

p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\text{score}}}.

The coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are optimized by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observed human preferences.

Global Optimization Across Products

The coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
were optimized globally using merged data from all product categories (e.g., wine, shoes). Since only relative magnitudes matter in the Bradley-Terry model, we scale them for interpretability so that their sum is 3, as follows:

\scriptstyle\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3,\hskip 8.19447pt\beta^{\prime}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3,\hskip 8.19447pt\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\times 3.

After optimization, the final scaled coefficients are:
\alpha^{\prime}=1.0139
,
\beta^{\prime}=0.8812
,
\gamma^{\prime}=1.1049
.

Intra-series negotiation dynamics (
Figure 11
)

An interesting phenomenon emerged regarding deal rates: excluding
gpt-4o-mini
, models generally achieved higher deal rates when negotiating with opponents from the same model series
12
. This tendency was particularly pronounced for the Gemini models. For instance,
gpt-4o
secured higher deal rates in four out of five markets when negotiating within the GPT series compared to cross-series negotiations. Similarly, both
gemini-1.5-pro
and
gemini-1.5-flash
consistently demonstrated improved deal rates in all markets during intra-series negotiations. These observations suggest that shared architectural or training paradigms might foster more compatible negotiation styles.

Model size and performance (
Table 2
)

Contrary to common expectations that LLMs inherently perform better across tasks, our benchmark did not consistently show a strong correlation between model size and negotiation performance as measured by
Merit
. While a size-performance trend was observable within the Gemini series, such a pattern was not broadly evident across all models tested, suggesting that factors beyond model size significantly influence negotiation efficacy in these settings.

Impact of market conditions (
Table 2
)

The specific market conditions within
AgoraBench
significantly influenced negotiation dynamics and buyer agent outcomes. When buyers could employ a
deceptive strategy
, their
Merit
scores generally improved compared to the vanilla market (average increase of 0.10), accompanied by a substantial rise in deal rates (10.62% points). Notably, while some GPT series achieved higher scores at the cost of marginally lower deal rates (e.g.,
gpt-4o
), Gemini often exhibited concurrent boosts in both scores and deal rates, indicating a distinct buyer advantage under these conditions.

Conversely,
monopoly
conditions consistently led to poorer outcomes for buyer agents across all models. Average deal rates decreased by 3.34% in single-item scenarios and by 8.80% in multi-item (starred*) settings. Correspondingly, buyer
Merit
scores fell by approximately 0.10 and 0.13 points, respectively. This demonstrates the seller’s enhanced leverage in monopolistic markets, resulting in higher prices and reduced negotiation convergence, a trend robustly observed in both single and multi-item LLM negotiations and reflective of real-world monopoly effects.

Other market structures yielded more varied impacts. The availability of
installment
plans in single-item scenarios tended to increase deal rates but often at the expense of higher final prices (lower buyer
Merit
). However, in multi-item scenarios, the added complexity of negotiating multiple factors alongside installment terms led to an overall decrease in deal rates. Similarly, a pre-existing
negative perception
of the seller consistently lowered deal rates. This effect was particularly acute in single-item negotiations where buyers lacked alternatives, although the presence of substitutes in multi-item scenarios still permitted some degree of negotiation. These findings illustrate how diverse market dynamics shape LLM negotiation strategies and effectiveness, mirroring key economic principles.

LLMs often reach good deals, but their tactics can stray from human norms—especially in smaller models like
gpt-4o-mini
. They may secure low prices through unusual moves that would likely stall if the counterpart were human. This gap shows LLMs are not yet full stand-ins for human negotiators and calls for further study of human-AI bargaining.
