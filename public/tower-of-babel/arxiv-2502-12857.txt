A point-line approach to the nonexistence of buildings of types 𝖧 3 \Hdrei and 𝖧 4 \Hvier


Abstract
. We present a novel proof for the fact that thick spherical buildings of types 𝖧 𝟥 \mathsf{H_{3}} and 𝖧 𝟦 \mathsf{H_{4}} do not exist. For that, we first provide an elementary axiom system for Lie incidence geometries associated with buildings of type 𝖧 𝟥 \mathsf{H_{3}} . This way, we can write our arguments purely in the language of point-line geometries, not needing the theory of buildings. Assuming the existence of thick spherical buildings of type 𝖧 𝟥 \mathsf{H_{3}} , we construct nontrivial root elations of generalized pentagons contained within them. This leads to a contradiction with Tits’s result on the nonexistence of Moufang generalized pentagons. Consequently, we obtain a new, direct, and geometric proof for the nonexistence of thick spherical buildings of types 𝖧 𝟥 \mathsf{H_{3}} and 𝖧 𝟦 \mathsf{H_{4}} , without invoking Tits’s extension theorem. Together with similar geometric constructions of the author for root elations of buildings of types 𝖡 𝗇 \mathsf{B_{n}} and 𝖢 𝗇 \mathsf{C_{n}} , and known constructions for buildings of type 𝖠 𝗇 \mathsf{A_{n}} , this yields an alternative, elementary proof for the fact that all thick irreducible spherical buildings of rank 3 3 have the Moufang property, not using Tits’s extension theorem.

1.
Introduction

In 1976, an article by Jacques Tits was published, showing that Moufang
m
-gons only exist for
m\in\{2,3,4,6,8\}
(cf.
[
6
, Théorème 1]
).
Another result by Tits — proved in
[
7
]
and already mentioned in
[
5
, Addenda, p. 275]
— states that thick spherical buildings of types
\mathsf{H_{3}}
and
\mathsf{H_{4}}
do not exist.
This latter result relies on the former and uses Tits’s extension theorem (
[
5
, Theorem 4.1.2]
), which is regarded as rather technical (cf.
[
5
, p. 7]
).
Consequently, there have already been attempts to find different proofs avoiding the use of this extension theorem.

A new proof, that does not invoke the extension theorem, was given by Hendrik Van Maldeghem in 1995 (cf.
[
10
, § 5]
).
Van Maldeghem’s argument assumes the existence of thick spherical buildings of type
\mathsf{H_{3}}
,
shows that the rank-
2
residues that are generalized pentagons are regular, and that regular generalized pentagons do not exist.

In this article, we present another alternative proof that is more direct and less intricate, as it does not require the notion of regularity.
The entire article will be written in the language of point-line geometries.
Only in Section
6
do we briefly comment on buildings; this section is not essential for our proof but provides additional context for interested readers.

For our main endeavor, we first recall some basic definitions in the context of point-line geometries and then introduce
icosahedral geometries
as point-line geometries satisfying certain axioms (cf. Definition
2.8
).
We show that the maximal singular subspaces of icosahedral geometries are projective planes, that their apartments are icosahedra,
and prove that any two singular subspaces of an icosahedral geometry lie in a common apartment
(it will then be clear that Lie incidence geometries of type
\mathsf{H_{3}}
coincide with icosahedral geometries, as we will explain in Section
6
).
Subsequently, we define root elations and the Moufang property for the point residues of an icosahedral geometry, which are generalized pentagons.

Assuming the existence of thick icosahedral geometries (or thick spherical buildings of type
\mathsf{H_{3}}
),
we construct nontrivial root elations for the generalized pentagons they contain (cf. Section
5
)
and thereby obtain a contradiction to the nonexistence of Moufang generalized pentagons (cf. Corollary
5.2
).

Together with the geometric constructions for root elations of buildings of types
\mathsf{B_{n}}
and
\mathsf{C_{n}}
in
[
3
]
, and the known constructions for buildings of type
\mathsf{A_{n}}
,
this yields novel, geometric proofs for Tits’s results on thick irreducible spherical buildings of rank
3
without the use of
Tits’s extension theorem (or Tits’s classification of all thick irreducible spherical buildings of rank at least three, cf. Section
6
).

Although the main motivation for this work lies in the theory of buildings, we have written it entirely in the language of point-line geometries to make it more accessible and potentially more broadly applicable.

2.
Icosahedral geometries

We start with the most basic and essential definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

A

\Delta=(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{L})

lies on

the point

goes through

. If two points

and

lie on a common line, they are called
collinear

p\perp q
. For any point

and any subset

, we define

p^{\perp}:=\{q\in\mathscr{P}\mid q\perp p\}\text{ and }P^{\perp}:=\bigcap_{p\in P}p^{\perp}.

Note that, if there exists a line

through a point

in
\Delta
, then

is on a common line with itself, and therefore

.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Delta=(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{L})
be a point-line geometry.

(i)

A subset

of
\mathscr{P}
is called a

\Delta
if every line

that contains at least two points of

We usually regard subspaces of

as subgeometries of
\Delta

(ii)

A subspace

.
If, moreover,

is not contained in any other singular subspace, it is called a

.
A singular subspace is called

if, as a subgeometry, it is a projective space.

(iii)

For a subset

of
\mathscr{P}
, the
subspace generated by

is denoted

and is defined to be the intersection of all subspaces containing

A subspace generated by three mutually collinear points not on a common line is called a

. (Note that, in general, this is not necessarily a singular subspace.)

Example & Definition 2.3
.

A

is a point-line geometry

such that (cf.

)

(i)

such that

(ii)

such that

(iii)

there exist at least
4
points of which any
3
points are non-collinear. (There exists a quadrangle.)

Example & Definition 2.4

A point-line geometry

if we are in one of the following scenarios:

(1)

(3)

(5)

The characterization of (5) is due to Veblen and Young. Axiom (ii) of (5) is often called the

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Delta=(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{L})

(i)

(ii)

Example & Definition 2.7
.

A

is a point-line geometry

such that the following axioms are satisfied (cf.

(i)

(iii)

An equivalent definition is the following (cf.

A point-line geometry

4.2.
Root elations and the Moufang property

Definition 4.2
.

An

of a point-line geometry is a permutation of the point set inducing a
permutation of the line set; that is, the set of points on any line is mapped onto the set of points of another line, and each line occurs as such an image. In particular, collineations preserve collinearity. The set of all collineations forms a group called the

(cf.

8

Definition 4.3
.

Let
