Developmental pathways to autism in tuberous sclerosis complex: Evidence from a longitudinal cohort
Authors: Fiona S McEwen, Abigail K Runicles, Holan Liang, Emma Woodhouse, Lisa Underwood, Elizabeth Shephard, Edward D Barker, Fintan Sheerin, Juul W Steenbruggen, Tuberous Sclerosis 2000 Study Group, John R W Yates, Charlotte Tye, Patrick F Bolton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association between autism spectrum disorder (hereafter referred to as autism) and tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is well established, yet the developmental pathways linking genetic mutation, cortical pathology, and epilepsy with autism remain unclear. The Tuberous Sclerosis 2000 Study recruited children newly diagnosed with TSC (N = 125). Data on mutation status, cortical tuber burden (magnetic resonance imaging/computed tomography), seizure history, and cognitive ability were collected. Approximately 10 years later, follow‐up assessments of autism, cognitive ability, and epilepsy were completed (n = 86). Almost 40% of participants met diagnostic criteria for autism, with an additional 42% showing elevated autistic traits. Structural equation modeling identified two indirect pathways linking TSC1/TSC2 mutation with the autism factor score: one via higher cortical tuber burden and infantile spasms and one via spasms alone. Concurrent seizure severity and lower intelligence quotient scores were also associated with higher autism factor scores. These findings provide preliminary longitudinal evidence supporting developmental associations between genetic vulnerability, cortical pathology, and early severe epilepsy with later autism. This highlights the importance of further investigation of early epileptic activity and associated neurodevelopmental outcomes in TSC and may inform understanding of epilepsy‐related pathways associated with autism.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, epilepsy, infantile spasms, longitudinal, tuberous sclerosis complex

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (hereafter referred to as autism) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition affecting 1% of the general population. 1 It is characterized by difficulties in social communication, together with restricted, repetitive behaviors. 1 Although autism has a high heritability (64%–91%), 2 its diverse genetic and neurobiological underpinnings complicate the identification of underlying developmental mechanisms. Single‐gene conditions such as tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) provide a valuable model for identifying developmental pathways linking genes and neurodevelopmental outcomes. In TSC, autism is markedly more prevalent, with 30%–60% of individuals meeting diagnostic criteria and many others exhibiting elevated autistic traits. 3 These features form part of broader TSC‐associated neuropsychiatric disorders, affecting up to 90% of individuals. 4

These neurodevelopmental differences are underpinned by the biological mechanisms of TSC. 5 TSC is a multisystem genetic disorder caused by pathogenic mutations in the TSC1 or TSC2 genes, resulting in dysregulated mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling and widespread effects on brain development. 6 Dysregulated mTOR contributes to the formation of cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, and white matter abnormalities. 5 , 6 Epilepsy affects 80%–90% of individuals with TSC and typically begins in infancy; approximately half develop infantile spasms, a severe epileptic encephalopathy associated with poor developmental outcomes. 6

Although structural brain abnormalities, 5 , 6 early epileptiform activity, 3 , 5 and mutation type have each been linked to autism in TSC, 3 most studies have examined these factors separately, leaving the interplay among these mechanisms unclear. Prior analyses from this cohort identified indirect pathways linking mutation, tuber burden, and early life seizures to intellectual ability 7 and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms. 8 However, analogous pathways to autism have not been evaluated. We therefore used structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine whether cortical tuber burden and early life epilepsy contribute to associations between TSC1/TSC2 genotype and autism.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Tuberous Sclerosis 2000 Study (TS2000) is a UK population‐based, prospective study of the natural history of TSC. Ethical approval and written informed consent were obtained at each phase (REC ref: 00/7/061, Supplementary Section 1 in Appendix S1 and Yates et al. 9 ). In phase 1 (2001–2005), 125 participants met diagnostic criteria for TSC and completed study assessments (49.6% male, median age at recruitment = 2.7 years, range = 4 weeks–18 years). Clinicians assessed participants across UK clinics, obtaining a full medical history and a physical examination. 9 During phase 2 (2012–2015), participants completed assessments of autism (n = 86) and cognitive ability (n = 88; 45% male, median age = 12.5 years, range = 7.8–26.9 years, 91.9% of the sample aged <18 years).

The causal mutation was determined for 96 children (TSC1, n = 19; TSC2, n = 77; Supplementary Section 1 in Appendix S1, Table S1). Cranial computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging was available for most cases and reviewed in phase 1 (n = 86). In phase 2, more recent (n = 41) and new (n = 23) scans were obtained (total n = 109). Scans were coded by trained clinicians, blinded to outcomes (Supplementary Section 2 in Appendix S1, Table S2). Interrater reliability was acceptable. Epilepsy history was obtained from interviews, seizure diaries, and medical records, and summarized using the Early Childhood Epilepsy Severity Scale. 10 Factor analysis indicated a single underlying severity dimension comprising of number of seizure types, duration, frequency at most severe, number of antiepileptic drugs, and treatment response. Severity factor scores were calculated for year 1 (n = 120), year 2 (n = 120), and 3 months prior to phase 2 assessment (age = 7+ years, n = 94). Additional scores were derived for spasm severity (all features excluding number of seizure types) and other seizure severity (all features including seizure types other than spasms) in the first 2 years, plus a lifetime “other seizure severity” score used to dichotomize cases into low versus high severity (mean difference d = 2.46; Supplementary Section 3 in Appendix S1, Table S2, Figure S4–S8).

Autism was assessed in 86 participants during phase 2 using the Autism Diagnostic Interview‐Revised (ADI‐R) and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS‐2), administered according to age and language level, with appropriate module and algorithm selection for minimally verbal participants (Supplementary Sections 4 and 6 in Appendix S1, Table S6). Categorical ADI‐R/ADOS‐2 scores were used to provide a best estimate clinical diagnosis by experienced child and adolescent psychiatrists, and a latent autism factor score (from ADI‐R diagnostic and current behavior algorithms and ADOS‐2 comparison scores; Supplementary Section 4 in Appendix S1, Figure S9) was generated via confirmatory factor analysis as a continuous measure of autistic traits. Further details are provided in Supplementary Section 4, Figures S9–12, and Supplementary Section 6 in Appendix S1, Table S6.

Intellectual ability was assessed at both study phases. In phase 1, children completed the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL) or the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS). In phase 2, cognitive ability was assessed using the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence‐II (WASI‐II), the British Picture Vocabulary Scale, and/or VABS‐II. Following previous work, 7 a best estimate intelligence quotient (IQ) was derived for 124 participants using the highest quality available measure in the following order of priority: WASI‐II, MSEL, VABS‐II, then VABS (Supplementary Section 5 in Appendix S1).

Statistical analysis

Aim 1: Neurobiological and epilepsy correlates of autism

Preliminary analyses examined associations between the autism factor score, cortical tuber burden, epilepsy characteristics (including infantile spasms and nonspasm seizure severity), and IQ (Supplementary Sections 4 and 6 in Appendix S1, Tables S5 and S6).

Aim 2: Pathways from TSC genotype to autism

SEM was performed in Mplus (version 7.31) using full‐information maximum likelihood and 1000‐sample bootstrapping to estimate indirect effects and construct 95% confidence intervals (CIs), thereby providing robustness to missing data and nonnormality. Additional details and variable definitions are in Supplementary Sections 6 and 7 in Appendix S1, Table S7.

RESULTS

Of the 125 children enrolled in phase 1, 86 completed autism assessments at follow‐up and were broadly representative of the baseline cohort across demographic and clinical characteristics (Supplementary Section 1 in Appendix S1). Cortical tuber data were available for 109 participants; the mean total count was 19.3 (SD = 14.4), highest in frontal regions and lower in temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes (Supplementary Section 2 in Appendix S1, Table S2). Epilepsy was highly prevalent (91%), and 52% of participants experienced infantile spasms, which had an earlier onset and frequently co‐occurred with other seizure types. Exploratory factor analyses supported modeling spasms and nonspasm seizure types as distinct severity dimensions across the first 2 years of life (Supplementary Section 3 in Appendix S1, Figures S4–S6).

Autism was common; 39.5% of participants met full ADI‐R and ADOS‐2 criteria for autism, and an additional 41.9% met criteria on one instrument or had subthreshold levels of autistic traits. Total tuber count was positively associated with the autism factor score (rho = .27, p = .017). However, the presence of tubers in specific lobes was not associated with autism when controlling for total tuber count (Section 4.6 in Appendix S1, Table S5). Earlier seizure onset was associated with greater epilepsy severity and lower IQ but was not significantly associated with autism factor score (rho = −.20, p > .05; Supplementary Section 6 in Appendix S1, Table S6). Several seizure features, including spasms, status epilepticus, and other seizure types, were individually associated with elevated autism factor scores (Supplementary Section 4.7 in Appendix S1, Figures S13–S16). However, after accounting for estimated IQ, only infantile spasms remained significantly associated with the autism factor score.

Structural equation models

Bivariate associations between variables were used to guide specification of the SEM (Supplementary Section 6 in Appendix S1). The SEM (Figure 1) showed no direct effect of mutation on autism factor score. Instead, TSC2 (vs. TSC1) mutation was associated with autism factor score through association with cortical tuber burden and early epilepsy. TSC2 predicted higher cortical tuber burden, which in turn predicted greater infantile spasm severity and, more weakly, other seizure types. IQ was modeled as a correlated factor because autism symptoms and cognitive ability are strongly associated in TSC and may reflect shared genetic and developmental pathways. Modeling autism and IQ as correlated outcomes allowed for both overlapping and distinct predictors and enabled us to examine whether associations between epilepsy severity and autism remained after accounting for cognitive ability (Supplementary Sections 5 and 6 in Appendix S1, Figure S18, and Table S6). After accounting for this overlap, only infantile spasms were associated with autism factor scores, whereas both spasm and nonspasm seizures were associated with IQ scores. Two significant indirect pathways linked mutation to autism factor scores: via increased tuber count and spasms (β = 2.08, 95% CI = .15–8.02) and via spasms alone (β = 2.98, 95% CI = .04–8.89). These pathways were similar in magnitude and converged on spasms, indicating that infantile spasms may represent the primary epilepsy‐related pathway associated with autism variation in TSC. For bivariate associations and full SEM specifications, including alternative models and fit indices, see Supplementary Sections 6–7 in Appendix S1, Tables S6 and S7.

DISCUSSION

This study identified developmental associations between genotype, early neurological features, and autism in TSC. These findings extend previous TS2000 analyses demonstrating indirect effects of genotype on intellectual ability 7 and ADHD symptoms, 8 thereby supporting a developmental model in which genetic vulnerability, cortical organization and early epileptogenesis are associated with later behavioral outcomes. Earlier TS2000 analyses linked infantile spasms and epilepsy severity to later intellectual ability, 7 suggesting shared developmental pathways between intellectual disability and autism. The present findings further implicate infantile spasms as an important epilepsy‐related pathway linking genotype to autism symptomatology.

Notably, the association between genotype and infantile spasms persisted after accounting for cortical tuber burden, suggesting contributions from both widespread cortical dysregulation and focal tuber pathology. 11 Occurring during a critical period for the development of social and language networks, 12 spasms may disrupt emerging connectivity patterns and contribute to both autism and intellectual impairment. 7 , 13 This finding aligns with previous studies identifying early epileptiform activity 3 , 14 and cortical abnormalities related to autism in TSC, 3 as well as reviews emphasizing interactions among cortical pathology, network disruption, and mTOR pathway dysregulation. 5

Clinically, the findings support further investigation of early epileptic activity and developmental outcomes in young children with TSC. Although vigabatrin is effective for seizure control 15 and early treatment may improve short‐term outcomes, 16 recent evidence suggests that presymptomatic therapy delays spasms but does not prevent autism or cognitive difficulties, 17 consistent with intrinsic developmental vulnerabilities related to mTOR dysregulation. Emerging mTOR‐targeted therapies show promise; however, evidence for effects on behavioral outcomes remains limited. 18

Limitations of this study include a modest sample size, which constrained model complexity and statistical power, and challenges in assessing autism in children with severe cognitive impairments, where overlap between autism‐related behaviors and cognitive difficulties may have influenced assessment scores. Future longitudinal studies incorporating neuroimaging, electrophysiology, and molecular biomarkers from infancy will be essential for identifying early markers of atypical development and informing targeted interventions.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, autism factor scores in TSC were associated with developmental pathways linking TSC2 mutation to cortical tuber burden and infantile spasms, with preliminary evidence supporting spasms as an important epilepsy‐related correlate of later autistic traits. Although early treatment may improve short‐term outcomes, 15 , 16 its relationship with long‐term autism risk requires further investigation. 18 TSC therefore provides a useful model for understanding shared mechanisms underlying autism, epilepsy, and cognitive impairment.
