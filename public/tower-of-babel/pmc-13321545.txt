Facilitators’ perspectives on a pre-matriculation program in basic biomedical sciences for medical students: a qualitative study
Authors: Yani Istadi, Rinawati Rinawati, Endang Lestari, Dina Fatmawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-matriculation programs are commonly used to help new medical students transition into medical school, especially when students come from varied educational background. Little is known about facilitators’ perspectives, even though they play a key role in designing and delivering these programs. This study aimed to explore facilitators’ views on the effectiveness, challenges, and future improvement of a basic biomedical science-matriculation program.MethodsA qualitative exploratory study was conducted using a focus group discussion involving ten facilitators from various department at a private medical faculty in Indonesia. Participants were purposively sampled to represent diverse disciplines and teaching experience. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s framework.ResultsFour main themes were identified. First, facilitators had different views on the program’s effectiveness. Second, students showed weak understanding of key biomedical subjects, often relying on memorization rather than understanding concepts. Third, challenges in implementation included low student motivation, weak literacy and learning culture, generational and cultural gaps, curriculum overload, and limited pedagogical support. Fourth, facilitators proposed recommendations for improvement, emphasizing the need to incorporating learning skills, stronger integration of basic and clinical sciences, structured evaluation, faculty development, and institutional support.ConclusionsFacilitators generally valued the program but noted key challenges. Targeted redesign may improve effectiveness, emphasizing the need for learner-centered, integrated, and systematically evaluated approaches to support meaningful learning and transition to medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09363-9.Keywords: Pre-matriculation, Basic biomedical sciences, Medical education, Faculty perspectives, Qualitative study

Background

A strong foundation in basic biomedical sciences is critical for preparing medical students to meet the academic and professional challenges of their future careers. These disciplines such as anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, histology, and chemistry, provide the conceptual basis upon which clinical reasoning and medical practice are built [1–4]. However, new medical students often enter with diverse educational backgrounds, resulting in variable readiness including to understand these fundamental concepts [5, 6]. The transition from secondary to higher education is therefore a critical period that can influence academic confidence, performance, and even mental health [7–10]. Students with weaker preparation may face greater difficulties adapting which in turn can lead to lower academic outcomes and increased stress [11, 12].

To address such challenges, many institutions have implemented pre-matriculation or bridging programs designed to prepare students for the demands of medical school aiming to equalize knowledge gaps, strengthen study skills, enhance confidence and readiness for medical training [13–18]. While such programs are widely implemented internationally, there is little consensus on standardized design or evaluation. A survey of 170 U.S. medical schools found that most pre-matriculation programs were introduced more as a tradition than as an evidence-based practice, with limited rigorous evaluation of their effectiveness [19]. Similarly, in the Indonesian context, pre-matriculation programs in basic biomedical sciences have been implemented as an institutional strategy to support new students. A previous study at a Public Faculty of Medicine in Indonesia explored students’ perspectives on the effectiveness of a biomedical pre-matriculation program, reporting positive impacts on confidence and early adaptation [20]. Nevertheless, evidence from the medical educators’ perspective remains limited, despite their central role in shaping program design, content delivery, and student engagement [21–24]. This study addressed that gap by exploring facilitators’ perspectives on the effectiveness, challenges, strategies and opportunities in delivering such program.

To comprehensively explore facilitators’ experiences, this study is guided by constructivist learning theory. Constructivism conceptualizes knowledge as actively constructed through individuals’ interactions with their experiences and social contexts, emphasizing the subjective meanings they attribute to those experiences [25]. In line with this perspective, a qualitative approach was adopted, with focus group discussions used to explore how facilitators collectively evaluate the effectiveness of the pre-matriculation program and identify its associated challenges. This approach facilitates interaction among participants, enabling them to share perspectives and build on one another’s ideas, thereby supporting the co-construction of meaning [26].

Methods

Study design

Guided by a constructivist framework, we conducted a qualitative exploratory study using focus group discussions (FGD) to capture basic biomedical facilitators’ perspectives on the pre-matriculation program. A qualitative design was chosen to allow in-depth exploration of perspective, experiences, challenges, and suggestions in a naturalistic and dialogic setting, which aligns with the study’s aim to generate insights rather than measure outcomes [27].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Islam Sultan Agung (No. 514/XI/2025/Komisi Bioetik). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Setting

The study was conducted in September 2025 at the Faculty of Medicine, Sultan Agung University, Indonesia. Since 2023, this institution has implemented a pre-matriculation program in basic biomedical sciences to prepare new medical students for transition from secondary school to university-level learning. The program runs over a two- week period prior to the start of the early modules in the first semester. The curriculum for the pre-matriculation program was determined by the Vice Dean of Academic Affairs and developed in consultation with faculty members responsible for the biomedical sciences curriculum.

Participants and sampling

Participants were biomedical science facilitators directly involved in designing and/or teaching the pre-matriculation modules. Disciplines represented included anatomy, biochemistry, physiology, histology, chemistry. Inclusion criteria were being lecturer from Department of represented discipline and serving as the facilitator of pre-matriculation program during the last two academic years.

A purposive sampling strategy was applied to ensure diversity across biomedical disciplines while also including both junior and senior faculty members [28]. A total of 10 facilitators from various biomedical departments including anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, histology, chemistry participated, representing both junior and senior faculty, with teaching experience ranging from 5 to 20 years.

Data collection

An FGD was conducted in September, 2025 following completion of the pre-matriculation program. The FGD guide was developed by the research team specifically for this study and has not been published elsewhere (Supplementary File 1). It was developed to explore facilitators’ perspective on pre-matriculation, perceived challenges and recommendations for improvement following qualitative research principles in which data collection is guided by the research questions [27].The FGD were conducted in Indonesian by a trained moderator with a note-taker. the session lasted approximately 100 min. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and anonymized.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework [29]. Transcriptor software was used to facilitate data transcription. Coding was conducted independently by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The example of the coding process is presented in Table 1.

Results

Ten facilitators from 5 biomedical disciplines (anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, histology, chemistry) participated in the focus group discussions. Their teaching experience ranged from fewer than five years to more than two decades, and most had been involved in the pre-matriculation program since its inception (Table 2). Thematic analysis produced four overarching themes: (1) facilitators’ perspective on the effectiveness of the program; (2) students’ weak mastery of biomedical content; (3) the challenges of delivering biomedical sciences and (4) recommendation for program refinement and sustainability. Different perceptions of program

Facilitators had different opinions about the value and impact of the pre-matriculation program. Some saw it as a support to help new medical students adjust to academic and personal challenges. Others, however, questioned how effective or sustainable the program really was. These differences showed that there was still uncertainty about whether the program’s design and results matched its main purpose.

Several facilitators believed the program was essential, especially for students from non-science or weaker academic backgrounds. They felt it helped students to be more prepared before entering the main curriculum.

“compared to previous years, current student input quality appears lower, making matriculation even more necessary.” (Facilitator 2).

For those (the students) who are enrolled despite weak foundations, we still have to find ways to support them so they can progress”. (Facilitator 4)

These facilitators viewed the program as a way to reduce differences among students with varied educational backgrounds. They believed it helped students build confidence, understand learning expectations, and start forming effective study habits before facing regular coursework. Facilitator 2 and 3 expressed their concerned about students’ different educational background that may affect the students‘ readiness.

“Differences in high school curricula also play a role.

Not all students receive the same foundational material before entering university. (Facilitator 2)

This disparity becomes a major issue, as students enter higher education with unequal preparation. (Facilitator 3)

In contrast, other facilitators were less positive. They felt that the results did not match the effort and time invested by faculty.

“Sometimes it feels disheartening when instructors invest so much effort but student engagement remains minimal.” (Facilitator 4)

These facilitators also pointed out that the program lacked systematic evaluation.

“When exam results are perceived as unfair, students become disappointed. They expect certain outcomes from their learning process. There is still a lack of consistency and clarity from the beginning.” (Facilitator 2)

Despite their different opinions, all facilitators agreed that the pre-matriculation program served an important purpose: helping students adjust to the demands of medical school. They believed that even if the program was not perfect, it should be improved not removed.

“We often discuss among ourselves how matriculation should be strengthened. (Facilitator 4)

Students’ weak mastery of biomedical contents

Facilitators consistently pointed out that students had the greatest difficulty mastering three core biomedical disciplines (biochemistry, physiology, and anatomy). These subjects were described as essential for understanding medicine but also the most conceptually demanding. As a result, they revealed clear gaps in students’ ability to reason scientifically and integrate basic concepts.

“Students struggle the most in biochemistry…. In cell biology and tissue-related modules, performance varies widely, but many students lack strong foundational understanding.” (Facilitator 2).

“….physiology, If students do not grasp basic processes such as respiration and metabolism, they cannot understand higher-level concepts.” (Facilitator 2).

“Yes, anatomy is the most prominent problem overall…, they often fail to identify anatomical landmarks correctly“. (Facilitator 3)

Facilitators agreed that these difficulties stemmed mainly from students’ long-standing learning habits. Many were accustomed to rote learning focusing on remembering facts for exams rather than understanding the underlying principles or mechanisms.

“Students rely heavily on memorization rather than investigation and conceptual understanding.” (Fasilitator 3).

This surface-learning approach made it hard for students to grasp how different biomedical concepts were connected. Facilitators often found that students could recall isolated details but struggled to apply or explain them logically.

“They may answer questions correctly, but often their understanding is superficial.

When asked to explain mechanisms or apply concepts, they struggle significantly” (Facilitator 3).

In addition to learning habits, one facilitator believed that the differences in academic background contributed to uneven readiness among students.

“There are also challenges related to student backgrounds. Some students have already entered the program with limited academic preparation.” (Facilitator 4)

Facilitators also observed that their knowledge was fragmented across disciplines. They exemplified that students could often recall anatomical structures but failed to relate them to physiological functions or biochemical mechanisms.

“They know the organs in anatomy but not their functions in physiology or reactions in biochemistry.” (Facilitator 8)

“When asked to explain the link between anatomy and pathophysiology, they go silent. Their concepts are not connected.” (Facilitator 7)

The short duration and heavy content of the pre-matriculation program further limited students’ ability to build these connections.

“Students today are forced to absorb large amounts of information, and the burden is often overwhelming”. (Facilitator 6)

The rapid pace encouraged surface learning, with students rushing to complete topics rather than engage deeply with them. Facilitator 8 mentioned that “Many problems stem from habits formed earlier, including copying and pasting previous work without true understanding”.

Challenges

Student motivation and engagement

Facilitators overwhelmingly described low intrinsic motivation among students. Many joined medical faculty because of family influence, particularly when parents were physicians or owned clinics, rather than out of personal interest. This resulted in superficial participation, with students attending sessions to comply rather than to learn. One facilitator explained:

“Their desire to learn was very minimal … many only joined the program because their parents already have a clinic.” (facilitator 2).

Low motivation often manifested as lack of accountability where students for example are arriving late, skipping sessions, or disengaging during class. Without consequences for absenteeism, facilitators felt it was difficult to foster a sense of seriousness.

“During matriculation sessions, attendance itself became an issue, some arrived late others missed sessions altogether . This reflects a lack of commitment and accountability ( facilitator 2).

These patterns of disengagement suggested to facilitators that unless the program incorporated mechanisms to build intrinsic motivation—through relevance, mentorship, or assessment—students would continue to treat pre-matriculation as optional or low-stakes.

Literacy and learning culture

Facilitators reported that another challenge was students’ limited engagement with sustained academic reading. The facilitators observed that many had never finished reading a textbook in their academic lives, reflecting a tendency toward surface learning strategies, in which students rely on summaries or condensed materials rather than engaging deeply with primary learning resources.

“Reading literature is often perceived as burdensome.” (Facilitator 4)

“I asked them, have you ever in your life finished reading a whole book? They said: never.” ( Facilitator 5).

Instead of developing habits of critical reading, students relied on lecture slides, seniors’ notes, or condensed summaries.

“….such as relying only on PowerPoint slides or shared materials in …students feel that learning should be limited to what is explicitly provided by lecturers …” (Facilitator 6)

With the rise of digital tools, they increasingly turned to AI-generated content. While this provided quick answers, facilitators worried it discouraged critical evaluation and fostered a “surface learning” mindset.

“Unfortunately, with the widespread availability of AI, they assume everything can be obtained instantly. They trust the AI output blindly and do not validate the information.” (Facilitator 2)

“Many students do not even question whether the information they read or obtain from AI is correct.” (Facilitator 2)

This reliance on surface learning, coupled with low reading stamina, undermined the very purpose of pre-matriculation: to cultivate readiness for the heavy reading, comprehension, and synthesis required in medical school. Facilitators felt that without explicit literacy support—such as structured reading tasks or guided study skills—the program risked reinforcing poor habits rather than correcting them.

Conceptual understanding and clinical linkage

Facilitators viewed a lack of integration of conceptual understanding and clinical relevance as one of the biggest barriers to higher-level reasoning in medical studies. Without understanding how systems connect, students struggled to see the relevance of basic sciences to clinical problem-solving.

Facilitators expressed concern that students approached biomedical sciences primarily through memorization rather than conceptual understanding. This was particularly evident in anatomy and physiology, where students memorized terms or processes without grasping their functional relationships.

“The problem is that students fail to connect memorized knowledge with broader concepts and clinical relevance” (Facilitator 4)

“., many students assume that the material is the same as what they learned in high school. For example, they think epithelium is just epithelium—nothing new.

This becomes a challenge for us as educators, particularly in physiology, to help them realize why this knowledge is essential and how it relates to clinical practice.” (Facilitator 2)

“This becomes apparent when they fail to connect theoretical anatomy knowledge with clinical correlations.” (Facilitator 4)

Similarly, in biochemistry, students often failed to see the value of learning abstract pathways, asking why such content mattered for their future careers. Facilitator 8 added that the students struggled to link basic concepts, such as biochemical reactions, with clinical applications. Facilitators believed this disconnect reflected both students’ immature epistemological beliefs or knowledge as facts to memorize and curricular design that emphasized content coverage over clinical contextualization. They argued that unless the program explicitly demonstrated how biomedical sciences underpin clinical reasoning, students would continue to see them as irrelevant hurdles rather than as foundations of medical practice.

Generational and cultural gaps

A striking theme was the generational gap between facilitators and students. Many facilitators described today’s learners as “Generation Z” often referred to as “ digital natives” who display short attention spans, become easily distracted, and expect rapid, visually engaging content delivery.

“Unfortunately, their attention span is short, and prolonged explanations often fail to engage them.” (Facilitator 2)

“Another challenge is maintaining student enthusiasm. The most engaging part tends to be visual explanations.” (Facilitator 3)

This digital fluency did not translate into intellectual curiosity. Instead, facilitators felt that students demonstrated low curiosity and critical thinking,

“They rarely ask follow-up questions or seek clarification. When asked whether they truly understand, they often respond affirmatively without being able to explain further.” (Facilitator 2)

Facilitator 2 added that the students often give shallow, AI-like responses to probing questions.

“Their answers to questions often resemble AI-generated responses—brief, direct, but lacking depth. (Facilitator 4)

Beyond generational learning styles, facilitators also pointed to a cultural gap in academic discipline. Students were perceived as permissive, reluctant to commit to self-directed study, and unaccustomed to rigorous academic norms leading to difficulty to engage in building an independent learning culture.

“From my perspective, teaching is also affected by generational and cultural differences between lecturers and students. Students today are more resistant to authority and less receptive to traditional teaching approaches. This makes it harder to instil discipline and academic culture” (Facilitator 10)

“They are task-oriented and require constant guidance. If supervision is reduced, tasks are often left incomplete.” (Facilitator 4)

These generational and cultural gaps required facilitators to rethink pedagogy. However, many admitted they felt ill-equipped to design learning experiences tailored to students with such different expectations and habits.

“I sometimes feel overwhelmed by this generation. However, we must find ways to bridge the gap between us and them, so that we can design learning modifications that suit their needs”

Pedagogical and curricular challenges

Facilitators identified content overload as a significant challenge. The pre-matriculation course attempted to cover a broad range of biomedical content in a condensed timeframe, which overwhelmed students and discouraged deep engagement.

“Students are forced to absorb large amounts of information, and the burden is often overwhelming.” (Facilitator 6)

In addition to content overload, Facilitators reported difficulties in students’ to integrate basic biomedical sciences with clinical context. One facilitator exemplified a specific case when the students fail to integrate the basic concept and the clinical concept.

In histology, students can identify structures such as nerves or blood vessels.

But when asked to relate these structures to mechanisms or clinical relevance, they struggle. (Facilitator 5)

These observations suggest that, within the current structure of the program, students may develop fragmented knowledge that is not easily applied in clinically meaningful ways.

Facilitators also highlighted limitations in current teaching approaches. The traditional lectures, even if well-prepared, were poorly suited for students with limited attention spans.

“Current lectures are considered too heavy”. (Facilitator 3)

“If we only rely on interaction-based teaching without proper structure, learning outcomes remain unclear. (Facilitator 6)

Recommendations for a more effective program

Despite these challenges, facilitators agreed that the pre-matriculation program should be retained but redesigned to better achieve its goals. They advocated incorporating the content transmission and cultivating self-directed learning skills. As one facilitator explained:

“Perhaps the focus of matriculation should shift—not only toward content knowledge, but toward teaching students how to learn: how to read, how to manage time, how to study effectively, and how to engage in discussion” (Facilitator 2)

Facilitators also recommended a pedagogical innovation incorporating technologies. This was linked to their observation that many students, often described as Generation Z, are accustomed to accessing information quickly and using digital tools as part of their learning. As such, technology integration may help align teaching approaches with students’ learning habits. As reflected by another facilitator:

“However, we must reconsider the teaching model. Students today are often labeled as Generation Z…This requires us, as educators, to adapt our teaching strategies accordingly “ (Facilitator 6)

“I sometimes feel overwhelmed by this generation. However, we must find ways to bridge the gap between us and them, so that we can design learning modifications that suit their needs”

Facilitators also recommended strengthening the integration of basic sciences with clinical cases to enhance relevance and engagement. By linking foundational concepts to clinical contexts, they believed students would better appreciate the application of biomedical knowledge in medical practice and engage more meaningfully with the material. Facilitator 2 emphasized the importance of connecting foundational concepts to clinical context.

“Many students also fail to see why material from earlier semesters is still relevant in later modules.” (Facilitator 5)

“Students perform better once material is connected to clinical contexts” (Facilitator 2)

In addition,, facilitators highlighted the importance of faculty development and institutional support to ensure quality and consistency.

“Another issue is inconsistency among tutors. Different tutors apply different standards, which confuses students”. (Facilitator 8)

Collectively, these recommendations reflected a shared aspiration to transform the pre-matriculation program into an integrated, evidence-informed, and learner-cantered foundation for medical study.

Discussion

This study explored facilitators’ experiences in implementing a pre-matriculation program in basic biomedical sciences for medical students. Four key themes emerged: (1) facilitators perceptions of program effectiveness; (2), student’s weak mastery of biomedical content; (3) challenges of delivering biomedical sciences within a short preparatory course and (4) recommendation for program refinement and sustainability Overall, the findings suggest that the challenges encountered extend beyond gaps in prior knowledge and reflect complex interaction between educational background, motivation and evolving learning practices.

From a constructivist perspective, students’ prior knowledge plays a crucial role in shaping their ability to engage with new learning. Facilitators reported that students entered the program with heterogeneous academic backgrounds, resulting in varying levels of preparedness in basic sciences. This findings reinforce concerns raised in previous research highlighting disparities in foundational knowledge among incoming medical students. Such differences may hinder students ‘ability to construct new understanding.

In addition to knowledge gabs, facilitators highlighted issue related to low motivation and external pressure influencing students ‘decision to pursue medicine. These observation align with studies showing that students’ intrinsic motivation is critical for academic performance and professional identity formation [30–32].

Weak academic literacy and limited reading habits were recurring concern. Facilitators noted that a growing reliance for AI-assisted resources over traditional textbook reading,. This may contribute to surface learning approach which has been shown to weaken critical thinking and learning outcomes [33–36] The generational shift towards digital learning may exacerbate this issue, requiring innovative approaches to foster deep learning [37–40].

The challenge of rote memorization without integration into clinical relevance echoes long-standing debates about the basic sciences’ place in medical curricula. with increasing emphasis on linking foundational knowledge to clinical application [41–46].

Facilitators also identified generational gaps and cultural mismatches between faculty and students were also evident. The characteristics commonly associated with “Generation Z” learners including short attention spans, reliance on gadgets, and limited curiosity mirror findings in studies of Generation Z learners, These observations are consistent with descriptions of Generation Z learners, who are often characterized as digitally oriented and accustomed to concise, easily accessible content [47–51]. While these traits may work well in technology-rich environments, they may also encourage surface- learning, particularly if not supported by appropriate pedagogical strategies [36]. This highlights the importance of aligning teaching approaches with learners’ characteristics. Faculty development is thus essential to bridge expectations and adapt pedagogy [52–54].

While prior research has primarily examined students’ outcomes or perspectives [55–57], our study contributes novel insights from facilitators, highlighting the need to incorporating learning skills, stronger integration of basic and clinical sciences, structured evaluation, faculty development, and institutional support.

Practical implications

The insights from facilitators highlight several strategies to enhance the effectiveness of pre-matriculation programs (Table 3). First, structured efforts to foster intrinsic motivation and accountability are needed to ensure active participation. Second, targeted support in academic literacy, particularly reading comprehension and critical appraisal of digital and AI-generated information—may reduce reliance on surface learning. Third, embedding case-based and problem-based learning, with early clinical integration, can improve conceptual understanding and relevance. Fourth, faculty development initiatives are essential to adapt pedagogical approaches to the preferences of Generation Z learners, including the use of technology-enhanced and interactive methods. Finally, curricular redesign to streamline content and improve integration with the first-year medical curriculum may help reduce overload and increase perceived value. Together, these recommendations provide a roadmap for institutions seeking to strengthen pre-matriculation as a meaningful bridge into medical education.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers novel insights into how biomedical sciences facilitators perceive the value and challenges of pre-matriculation programs designed to support students’ transition into medical school. Its strength lies in addressing an under-explored area within medical education and drawing on perspectives from facilitators representing multiple biomedical disciplines who have been involved since the program’s initiation. The use of focus group discussions provided a rich platform for interactive reflection and for capturing shared experiences across departments.

One important limitation is the potential influence of group dynamics inherent in focus group discussions, where dominant participants may shape the direction of the conversation and less assertive facilitators may have contributed less fully. In addition, as a qualitative inquiry, the findings are context-specific and not intended for generalization.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into facilitators’ perspectives on a basic biomedical sciences pre-matriculation program designed to support students’ transition into medical school. Facilitators generally perceived the program as a useful bridging mechanism. However, its perceived impact was limited by ongoing challenges, including low student motivation, weak academic literacy, fragmented conceptual understanding across biomedical disciplines, and inconsistencies in curriculum structure and delivery. Despite its limitations, facilitators agreed that the program fulfills an important transitional role and should be improved rather than discontinued. The findings indicate that pre-matriculation programs are most likely to be effective when they are learner-centered, conceptually integrated, and supported by systematic evaluation and faculty development. Future research should explore how pre-matriculation programs can be designed to more effectively integrate content support with the development of academic literacy, motivation, and responsible use of digital and AI-assisted learning tools, as well as examine their longer-term impact on students’ academic progression and professional development.
