Fundamentals of Theory of Continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance.
Semeon A. Bogaty, Alexey A. Tuzhilin

Abstract
The Gromov–Hausdorff distance (hereinafter referred to as the GH-distance) is a measure of non-isometricity of metric spaces. In this paper, we study a modification of this distance that also takes topological differences into account. The resulting function of pairs of metric spaces is called the continuous GH-distance. We show that many basic properties of the classical GH-distance also hold in the continuous case. However, the continuous GH-distance, distinguishing between topologies, can differ significantly from the classical one. We will provide numerous examples of this distinction and demonstrate the role of topological dimension here. In particular, we will prove that the continuous GH-distance, like the classical one, is intrinsic, but, unlike the classical one, it is incomplete. Since we are dealing with all metric spaces, we will show, within the framework of the von Neumann-Bernays-Gödel set theory, how topological concepts can be transferred to proper classes. Keywords : metric space, Hausdorff distance, Gromov–Hausdorff distance, topological dimension, small and large inductive dimensions, hyperspace, continuum.

Introduction.

This paper develops the theory of a variation on Gromov–Hausdorff distance geometry. Classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance measures the difference between two metric spaces: if the spaces are isometric, then they are at zero distance from each other, and the greater the distance, the greater their metric differences. The classical definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance does not take into account additional structures that metric spaces may possess. Even the topology generated by the metric is ignored by this distance. This feature of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance was modified, for example, by Rieffel
[
1
]
, who proposed a more subtle comparison of the so-called quantum metric spaces. In the paper citeLimMemoliSmith, another modification of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance was proposed that takes continuity into account. It was noted that comparing spheres in Euclidean space, endowed with the standard intrinsic metric, the classical Gromov-Hausdorff distance differs from those obtained with its continuous analog. Another version of the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance was proposed by
[
3
]
for comparing dynamical systems and solutions of partial differential equations. However, these authors’ approach significantly complicates the technique, since their version of the distance does not satisfy the triangle inequality. We decided to use the definition from
[
2
]
and begin a more fundamental study of the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance. We do not restrict ourselves to compact metric spaces, which constitute the traditional domain of the classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance, but consider all spaces, which also leads us to some difficulties associated with the paradoxes of set theory. For the sake of completeness, we present in the supplementary Section

Preliminaries

The proper class of all metric spaces considered up to isometry is denoted by
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, and the subset of
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
consisting of all compact metric spaces is denoted by
\mathcal{M}
.

A more technical definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is often used. For non-empty metric spaces
X
and
Y
, the set of non-empty relations between
X
and
Y
, i.e., non-empty subsets of the Cartesian product
X\times Y
, is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
. For
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
, we define the
distortion
by setting

(1)

\operatorname{dis}\sigma=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|:(x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\sigma\Bigr\}.

In particular, if
f\colon X\to Y
is an arbitrary mapping, then we define the
distortion
\operatorname{dis}f
for it as the distortion of its graph

(2)

\operatorname{dis}f=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|f(x)f(x^{\prime})|\bigr|:x,x^{\prime}\in X\Bigr\}.

Let us note a well-known property of distortion of the relations composition.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
,
\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(Y\times Z)
and
\tau\circ\sigma\neq\emptyset
then
\operatorname{dis}(\tau\circ\sigma)\leq\operatorname{dis}\sigma+\operatorname{dis}\tau
.

A multivalued surjective mapping
R
from
X
to
Y
is called a
correspondence between
X
and
Y
. The set of all correspondences between
X
and
Y
is denoted by
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
. The Gromov–Hausdorff distance is calculated by the formula
[
8
, Theorem 7.3.25]

(3)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

A special case of a correspondence can be constructed for any pair of mappings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
by setting
R_{f,g}=f\cup g^{-1}
, where
g^{-1}
denotes the inverse relation to the relation
g
. On the other hand, in each correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
, a subcorrespondence
R_{f,g}
can be extracted if
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
are chosen such that
f\subset R
and
g\subset R^{-1}
. It is easy to see that the distortion of each subcorrespondence does not exceed the distortion of the correspondence, in particular,
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}\leq\operatorname{dis}R
. To calculate the distortion of the correspondence
R_{f,g}
, we need the
codistortion
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)
, defined as follows:

(4)

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup\Bigl\{\bigr||x\,g(y)|-|f(x)\,y|\bigr|:x\in X,\,y\in Y\,\Bigr\}.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, a correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
and any two mappings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
such that
f\subset R
and
g\subset R^{-1}
, the following holds
:

(5)

\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}=\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}f,\,\operatorname{dis}g,\,\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\bigr\}\leq\operatorname{dis}R.

From Equality (
3
) and Proposition
2.2
the following result is immediately obtained.

Corollary 2.3

(see
[
2
, p. 3]
)
.

For any
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, the following holds
:

(6)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\colon X\to Y\\
g\colon Y\to X\end{subarray}}\,\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}f,\,\operatorname{dis}g,\,\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\bigr\}.

It follows from Proposition
2.1
that the distortion of the composition of mappings does not exceed the sum of the distortions of these mappings.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let three metric spaces
X
,
Y
and
Z
be given, as well as mappings
X\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}Y\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{h}_{k}Z
.
Then

\operatorname{codis}(h\circ f,g\circ k)\leq\operatorname{codis}(f,g)+\operatorname{codis}(h,k).

Proof.

Let’s choose arbitrary
x\in X
,
z\in Z
and let’s put
y_{1}=f(x)
,
y_{2}=k(z)
. Then

\Bigl|\bigl|x\,(g\circ k)(z)\bigr|-\bigl|(h\circ f)(x)\,z\bigr|\Bigr|=\Bigl|\bigl|x\,g(y_{2})\bigr|-\bigl|h(y_{1})\,z\bigr|\Bigr|=\\
=\Bigl|\bigl|x\,g(y_{2})\bigr|-\bigl|y_{1}y_{2}\bigr|+\bigl|y_{1}y_{2}\bigr|-\bigl|h(y_{1})\,z\bigr|\Bigr|\leq\\
\leq\Bigl|\bigl|x\,g(y_{2})\bigr|-\bigl|f(x)y_{2}\bigr|\Bigr|+\Bigl|\bigl|y_{1}k(z)\bigr|-\bigl|h(y_{1})\,z\bigr|\Bigr|\leq\operatorname{codis}(f,g)+\operatorname{codis}(h,k).

The result follows from the arbitrariness of
x
and
z
.
∎

If in Corollary
2.3
we restrict ourselves to continuous mappings
f
and
g
, then we obtain the definition of the
continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance
:

(7)

d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C(X,Y)\\
g\in C(Y,X)\end{subarray}}\,\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}=\frac{1}{2}\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C(X,Y)\\
g\in C(Y,X)\end{subarray}}\,\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}f,\,\operatorname{dis}g,\,\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\bigr\},

where

denotes the set of all continuous mappings from the metric space

to the metric space

.
For brevity, we will also call this distance the

.

For a (non-single-point) metric space
X
and a point
x\in X
, we define the circumscribed and inscribed radii (centered at

) as
R_{x}=\sup\bigl\{|xx^{\prime}|:x^{\prime}\in X\bigr\}
and
r_{x}=\inf\bigl\{|xx^{\prime}|:x^{\prime}\in X,\ x^{\prime}\neq x\bigr\}
, respectively. We introduce four values:

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigl\{R_{x}:x\in X\bigr\}=\sup\bigl\{|xx^{\prime}|:x,x^{\prime}\in X\bigr\}\ \text{(\emph{diameter\/})},

\displaystyle R(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{R_{x}:x\in X\bigr\}\ \text{(\emph{Chebyshev radius\/})},

\displaystyle d(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigl\{r_{x}:x\in X\bigr\},

\displaystyle s(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{r_{x}:x\in X\bigr\}=\inf\bigl\{|xx^{\prime}|:x,x^{\prime}\in X,\ x^{\prime}\neq x\bigr\}.

For a one-point metric space we reserve the notation
{\Delta}_{1}
. It is natural to assume
\operatorname{diam}({\Delta}_{1})=R({\Delta}_{1})=d({\Delta}_{1})=s({\Delta}_{1})=0
. It is known
[
8
, p. 255]
that

. Note that for an unbounded space, it holds
\operatorname{diam}(X)=R(X)=\infty
.

It is clear that for a non-single-point metric space,
d(X)=0
if and only if
X
has no isolated points. For a non-single-point finite metric space,
s(X)>0
. When
s(X)>0
we say that the metric is bounded away from zero. For the standard sphere
S^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
, equipped with both the metric induced from
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
and the intrinsic metric, we have
0=s(S^{m})=d(S^{m})<R(S^{m})=\operatorname{diam}(S^{m})
; for a ball

, we have
0=s(B^{m})=d(B^{m})<2R(B^{m})=\operatorname{diam}(B^{m})
.

The next result is obvious.

Proposition 2.5
.

For an arbitrary metric space
X
, the following chains of inequalities are valid

0\leq s(X)\leq d(X)\leq\operatorname{diam}(X)\leq 2R(X)\ \ \text{and}\ \ s(X)\leq R(X)\leq\operatorname{diam}(X).

For a metric space
(X,\rho)
and a number
\lambda\geq 0
, we will denote by

the set

with (pseudo)metric

.

2.1
Dimensions of Topological Spaces

We will present three different notions of dimension, which coincide in the case of separable metric spaces and may differ for more general metric and topological spaces. We will begin with dimension, which in different sources is called either the Lebesgue dimension, or the covering dimension, or the topological dimension. This is the one we will primarily use. We will give the classical definition from
[
17
]
. Recall that for a covering
\mathcal{U}
of a topological space
X
, its
multiplicity
\operatorname{ord}\,\mathcal{U}
is defined to be either the smallest natural number
n
such that every point of
X
is contained in at most
n
elements of
\mathcal{U}
, or, if such
n
does not exist, then the infinity symbol
\infty
. A family
\mathcal{A}
of subsets of a topological space
X
is called
locally finite
if every point of
X
has a neighborhood that intersects only finitely many elements of
\mathcal{A}
. An important special case of such families are
locally finite coverings of
\mathcal{U}
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
17
, 1.1.12]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a locally finite family of closed sets of a topological space
X
. Then the union of elements of this family is a closed subset of
X
.

Furthermore, a covering
\mathcal{V}
of
X
is said to be
inscribed
in a covering
\mathcal{U}
of this space (we denote this property by
\mathcal{V}\succ\mathcal{U}
) if for every
V\in\mathcal{V}
there is
U\in\mathcal{U}
such that
V\subset U
. Of most interest to us are open coverings of a topological space
X
, so we denote the set of such coverings by
\operatorname{cov}(X)
. The subfamily of
\operatorname{cov}(X)
consisting of finite coverings is denoted by
\operatorname{cov}_{f}(X)
.

Theorem 2.7

(Stone, see also
[
17
, 4.4.1]
)
.

Every open covering of a metric space can be inscribed with a locally finite open covering.

A topological space is called
paracompact
if every open covering of it can be inscribed with a locally finite covering. In these terms, Theorem
2.7
states:
every metric space is paracompact
.

Note that each of the three dimensions is defined for its own class of metric spaces (regular, normal, Tychonoff). In what follows, we will apply the corresponding results exclusively to metric spaces, so we will assume from the outset that the space
X
for which we will define dimensions is Hausdorff and normal, like every metric space: such an
X
is always automatically regular and Tychonoff.

We first define the
Lebesgue dimension
\dim\,X
, also called the
covering dimension
or the
topological dimension
:

•

if
X=\emptyset
, then
\dim\,X=-1
;

•

if
X\neq\emptyset
, then we set

\dim\,X=-1+\sup_{\mathcal{U}\in\operatorname{cov}_{f}(X)}\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{cov}(X)\\
\mathcal{V}\succ\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{ord}\,\mathcal{V}.

In other words, for a non-empty space
X
, we consider
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that every finite open covering
\mathcal{U}
of

can be inscribed with an open covering
\mathcal{V}
of multiplicity at most

, and we take the smallest of these

.

Note that
\dim\,X=0
is equivalent to the possibility of inscribing an open partition into every finite open covering. Spaces

for which

are called

.

Example 2.8
.

Let
X
be a nonempty discrete topological space. Then every finite open covering of

can be inscribed with a covering of singleton open sets, hence
\dim\,X=0
.

Remark 2.9
.

In a number of monographs, for example in
[
18
]
, when defining

are considered. We will follow a more traditional definition.

Theorem 2.10

(Dowker
[

]
, see also
[

A normal Hausdorff space

satisfies
\dim\,X\leq n
if and only if each of its locally finite open coverings can be inscribed with a covering of multiplicity at most

We now define two
inductive dimensions
of a normal Hausdorff space

:

\operatorname{ind}\,X
and

\operatorname{Ind}\,X
. Recall that every subspace of a Hausdorff space is also Hausdorff, and for normal spaces, a more subtle assertion holds: every closed subspace of a normal space is itself normal. In particular, since the boundary of an arbitrary subset of a topological space is closed, the boundaries of normal Hausdorff topological spaces are also closed. We will use this idea in defining the inductive dimensions.

So, let

•

\operatorname{ind}\,X=-1
precisely when
X=\emptyset
;

•

if
X\neq\emptyset
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have already defined what
\operatorname{ind}\,Y\leq n-1
means for Hausdorff normal topological spaces, then we put
\operatorname{ind}\,X\leq n
if and only if for every point

•

, then we obtain the Nagami–Roberts theorem
[
20
]
:
for a metric space
X
, the condition
\operatorname{Ind}\,X>0
is equivalent to the existence of closed sets
F,H\subset X
such that
|FH|>0
and every clopen set
U\supset F
intersects
H
.

Remark 2.14
.

Since all singleton subsets of
X
are closed by virtue of the Hausdorff property,
\operatorname{ind}\,X\leq\operatorname{Ind}\,X
.

Theorem 2.15

(
[
17
]
)
.

For any separable metric space
X
, it holds
\dim\,X=\operatorname{ind}\,X=\operatorname{Ind}\,X
.

Recall that a topological space is called
Lindelöf
if any of its open covering contains an at most countable subcovering. Examples of Lindelöf spaces include both compacta and spaces with a countable base.

Theorem 2.16

(
[
17
]
)
.

For any Lindelöf
X
, the conditions
\dim\,X=0
,
\operatorname{ind}\,X=0
, and
\operatorname{Ind}\,X=0
are equivalent.

3
General Properties of Continuous Gromov–Hausdorff Distance

Proposition 3.1
.

The distance
d^{c}_{GH}
satisfies the triangle inequality.

Proof.

Let
X
,
Y
, and
Z
be arbitrary metric spaces. We will show that

d^{c}_{GH}(X,Z)\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z).

If one of the distances
d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)
or
d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)
is infinite, then the inequality holds. Now let both of these distances be finite. For any
\varepsilon>0
, choose
f\in C(X,Y)
and
g\in C(Y,X)
, as well as
h\in C(Y,Z)
and
k\in C(Z,Y)
, for which

d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}f,\,\operatorname{dis}g,\,\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\bigr\}-\varepsilon
and

d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)\geq\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}h,\,\operatorname{dis}k,\,\operatorname{codis}(h,k)\bigr\}-\varepsilon
.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dis}(h\circ f)\leq\operatorname{dis}f+\operatorname{dis}h\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)+2\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dis}(g\circ k)\leq\operatorname{dis}g+\operatorname{dis}k\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)+2\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\operatorname{codis}(h\circ f,g\circ k)\leq\operatorname{codis}(f,g)+\operatorname{codis}(h,k)\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)+2\varepsilon,

where

d^{c}_{GH}(X,Z)\leq\max\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}(h\circ f),\,\operatorname{dis}(g\circ k),\,\operatorname{codis}(h\circ f,g\circ k)\bigr\}\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+d^{c}_{GH}(Y,Z)+2\varepsilon.

It remains to use the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
∎

The proper class of all non-empty metric spaces, considered up to isometry, endowed with the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance is denoted by
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
. It follows from the above that
d^{c}_{GH}
is a generalized pseudometric on
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
. The subset of
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
consisting of all compact metric spaces is denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{c}
. As in the case of the ordinary Gromov–Hausdorff distance, the restriction of
d^{c}_{GH}
to
\mathcal{M}^{c}
is a metric (Theorem
5.1
).

The basic properties of the ordinary Gromov–Hausdorff distance also hold to its continuous analogue.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
, the following holds
:

(1)

if metric spaces
X
and
Y
are isometric, then
d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)=0
;

(2)

d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)
;

(3)

2d^{c}_{GH}({\Delta}_{1},X)=\operatorname{diam}X
;

(4)

2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\}
;

(5)

if the diameter of
X
or
Y
is finite, then
2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\bigl|\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y\bigr|
;

(6)

if the diameter of
X
is finite, then for any
\lambda\geq 0
,
\mu\geq 0
, we have
2d^{c}_{GH}(\lambda X,\mu X)=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X
, whence it follows immediately that the curve
\gamma(t):=t\,X
is shortest between any of its points, and the length of such a segment equals the distance between its ends
;

(7)

for any
\lambda>0
, we have
d^{c}_{GH}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)=\lambda\,d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)
, and if the spaces
X
and
Y
are bounded, then the equality also holds for
\lambda=0
;

(8)

if
X_{1},\,X_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of metric spaces converging in the metric
d^{c}_{GH}
, then it also converges in the metric
d_{GH}
.

Proof.

(
1
) The isometries
h\colon X\to Y
and
h^{-1}
are continuous and
\operatorname{dis}h=\operatorname{dis}h^{-1}=\operatorname{codis}(h,h^{-1})=0
.

(
2
) The inequality holds because the distance
d_{GH}(X,Y)
is the infimum over a larger family of mappings than that needed to calculate
d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)
.

(
3
) For any continuous
{\Delta}_{1}\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}X
, we have
\operatorname{dis}f=0
,
\operatorname{dis}g=\operatorname{diam}X
, and if
{\Delta}_{1}=\{p\}
, then

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X}\Bigl|\bigl|p\,g(x)\bigr|-\bigl|f(p)\,x\bigr|\Bigr|=\sup_{x\in X}\bigl|f(p)\,x\bigr|\leq\operatorname{diam}X,

which is what was required.

(
4
) For any continuous
X\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}Y
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{dis}f\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\},\ \ \operatorname{dis}g\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}\Bigl|\bigl|x\,g(y)\bigr|-\bigl|f(x)\,y\bigr|\Bigr|\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\},

which is what was required.

(
5
) This follows from the corresponding inequality for
d_{GH}
and the fact that
d_{GH}\leq d^{c}_{GH}
.

(
6
) By property (
5
) we have

2d^{c}_{GH}(\lambda X,\mu X)\geq\bigl|\operatorname{diam}(\lambda X)-\operatorname{diam}(\mu X)\bigr|=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X.

To prove the converse inequality, we choose the identity mapping as continuous
X\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}X
, then if we denote by
\operatorname{dis}_{\lambda,\mu}
and
\operatorname{codis}_{\lambda,\mu}
the distortion and codistortion of the mappings between the spaces
\lambda X
and
\mu X
, respectively, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dis}_{\lambda,\mu}f=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl|\lambda|xx^{\prime}|-\mu\bigl|xx^{\prime}\bigr|\Bigr|=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X,

\displaystyle\operatorname{codis}_{\lambda,\mu}(f,g)=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl|\lambda|xx^{\prime}|-\mu\bigl|xx^{\prime}\bigr|\Bigr|=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X,

from which the required result follows.

(
7
) We choose arbitrary continuous
X\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}Y
and denote by
\operatorname{dis}_{\lambda}
and
\operatorname{codis}_{\lambda}
the distortion and co-distortion of these mappings, but between
\lambda X
and
\lambda Y
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dis}_{\lambda}f=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl|\lambda|xx^{\prime}|-\lambda\bigl|f(x)f(x^{\prime})\bigr|\Bigr|=\lambda\,\operatorname{dis}_{\lambda}f,

\displaystyle\operatorname{codis}_{\lambda}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}\Bigl|\lambda|x\,g(y)|-\lambda\bigl|f(x)y\bigr|\Bigr|=\lambda\,\operatorname{codis}(f,g),

from which the declared statement follows.

(
8
) This follows from the inequality
d_{GH}\leq d^{c}_{GH}
.
∎

4
Comparison of Metrics (on Zero-Dimensional Spaces)

Proposition 4.1
.

If
X
and
Y
are discrete metric spaces, then
d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that all mappings between
X
and
Y
are continuous.
∎

To prove the following proposition, we need a sufficient condition for the continuity of the mapping of metric spaces.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be an arbitrary mapping of metric spaces, and there exists a positive number
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any distinct
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
with non-empty preimages and any
x_{1}\in f^{-1}(y_{1})
and
x_{2}\in f^{-1}(y_{2})
, it holds
|x_{1}x_{2}|>\varepsilon
. Then the mapping
f
is continuous.

Proof.

Indeed, let
y\in Y
have a non-empty preimage, then for any point
x\in f^{-1}(y)
, we have
U_{\varepsilon}(x)\subset f^{-1}(y)
, therefore
f^{-1}(y)
is open and, hence, for any open
V\subset Y
, the set
f^{-1}(V)=\cup_{y\in V}f^{-1}(y)
is also open in
X
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be arbitrary metric spaces such that
2d_{GH}(X,Y)<s(X)
, then
d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Let
s:=s(X)
and let
\varepsilon>0
such that
2d_{GH}(X,Y)<s-\varepsilon
. Then there exists a correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
such that
\operatorname{dis}R<s-\varepsilon
. The set of all such correspondences will be denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)
.

According to formula (
3
), the equality
d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)\bigr\}
holds. We choose an arbitrary
R\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)
. We show that
\bigl|R(x_{1})R(x_{2})\bigr|>\varepsilon
for
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Indeed, for any
(x_{1},y_{1}),\,(x_{2},y_{2})\in R
,
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, the inequality
|y_{1}y_{2}|\geq|x_{1}x_{2}|-\operatorname{dis}R>s-(s-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon>0
holds. Therefore, the sets
\{R(x):x\in X\}
form an open partition of the space
Y
.

We construct mappings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
as follows: for each
x\in X
, we choose an arbitrarily point
f(x)
in
R(x)
, and for each
y\in R(x)
, we set
g(y)=x
. Since
s(X)>0
,
X
is discrete, so
f
is continuous. The continuity of
g
follows from Lemma
4.2
. By Proposition
2.2
, we have
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}\leq\operatorname{dis}R
. Thus, we have constructed a mapping
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)\to\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)
,
R\mapsto R_{f,g}
, therefore, by the definition of
d_{GH}^{c}
,

d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}:R\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)\bigr\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(X,Y)\bigr\}=d_{GH}(X,Y).

It remains to use Inequality (
2
) of Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
\dim X=0
. Then for every non-empty
A\subset X
, we have
d_{GH}^{c}(A,X)\leq d_{H}(A,X)
.

Proof.

For an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, we put
r=d_{H}(A,X)+\varepsilon
, then, by the definition of the Hausdorff distance,
\lambda=\bigl\{U_{r}(a):a\in A\bigr\}
is an open covering of
X
. Since
\dim X=0
, by the theorems of Stone
[
16
]
(see also
[
17
, Theorem 4.4.1]
) and Dowker
[
19
]
(see also
[
17
, Theorem 7.2.4]
), there exists an open covering
\mu=\{U_{\beta}\}
of multiplicity
1
inscribed in
\lambda
, i.e.,
\mu
is a partition of
X
by open sets. Therefore, for every
U_{\beta}
, there exists a point
a_{\beta}\in A
such that
U_{\beta}\subset U_{r}(a_{\beta})
.

Let
f\colon A\to X
be the inclusion, and let the mapping
g\colon X\to A
be defined by the formula
g(U_{\beta})=a_{\beta}
. Since the sets
U_{\beta}
are open and pairwise disjoint, the mapping
g
is well-defined and continuous, and for every point
x\in X
, the inequality
\bigl|x\,g(x)\bigr|<r
holds. Clearly,
\operatorname{dis}f=0
,

\operatorname{dis}g=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl||xx^{\prime}|-\bigl|g(x)g(x^{\prime})\bigr|\Bigr|\leq\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl(\bigl|x\,g(x)\bigr|+\bigl|x^{\prime}\,g(x^{\prime})\bigr|\Bigr)\leq 2r,

and finally

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X,\,a\in A}\Bigl|\bigl|x\,f(a)\bigr|-\bigl|g(x)a\bigr|\Bigr|=\sup_{x\in X,\,a\in A}\Bigl|\bigl|xa\bigr|-\bigl|g(x)a\bigr|\Bigr|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|x\,g(x)|\leq r,

therefore
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}\leq 2d_{H}(A,X)+2\varepsilon
and, because of arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, we have
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}\leq 2d_{H}(A,X)
, whence
d_{GH}^{c}(A,X)\leq d_{H}(A,X)
, which is what was stated.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

For metric spaces
X
,
Y
, and any of their everywhere dense subsets
A\subset\overline{A}=X
,
B\subset\overline{B}=Y
such that
\dim A=\dim B=0
, we have

d_{GH}^{c}(A,B)=d_{GH}(X,Y).

Proof.

For a given
\varepsilon>0
, we choose discrete
\varepsilon
-nets
A_{\varepsilon}\subset A
and
B_{\varepsilon}\subset B
in the sets
A
and
B
, respectively. According to the triangle inequalities for distances
d_{GH}^{c}
and
d_{GH}
, as well as by Propositions
4.4
and
4.1
, the following chain of inequalities holds:

d_{GH}^{c}(A,B)\leq d_{GH}^{c}(A,A_{\varepsilon})+d_{GH}^{c}(A_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon})+d_{GH}^{c}(B_{\varepsilon},B)\leq\\
\leq 2\varepsilon+d_{GH}^{c}(A_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon})=2\varepsilon+d_{GH}(A_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon})\leq\\
\leq 2\varepsilon+d_{GH}(A_{\varepsilon},A)+d_{GH}(A,X)+d_{GH}(X,Y)+d_{GH}(Y,B)+d_{GH}(B,B_{\varepsilon})\leq\\
\leq 2\varepsilon+\varepsilon+0+d_{GH}(X,Y)+0+\varepsilon=4\varepsilon+d_{GH}(X,Y).

From arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
and Inequality (
2
) of Proposition
3.2
, we get

d_{GH}^{c}(A,B)\leq d_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(A,B)\leq d_{GH}^{c}(A,B),

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

For any zero-dimensional metric spaces
X
and
Y
, it holds

d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y).

Let us emphasize that Corollary
4.6
is a generalization of Proposition
4.1
, which, however, we used in the proof of this more general statement.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
\operatorname{ind}X\neq 0
. Then there exists
r>0
such that for every continuous mapping
f\colon X\to Y
into a metric space
Y
with
\operatorname{ind}Y=0
, the inequality
\operatorname{dis}f\geq r
holds. In particular, we have
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq r
.

Proof.

By the condition
\operatorname{ind}X\neq 0
, there exist a closed subset
F\subset X
and a point
x\in X\setminus F
such that every clopen set containing the point
x
, intersects the set
F
. Put
r=|xF|>0
. For every mapping
f\colon X\to Y
, the alternative holds: either
\bigl|f(x)f(F)\bigr|=0
, or
\bigl|f(x)f(F)\bigr|>0
.

In the first case, the estimate
\operatorname{dis}f\geq|xF|=r
is valid.

In the second case, the condition
\operatorname{ind}Y=0
implies the existence of a clopen neighborhood
U^{f(x)}
of the point
f(x)
that lies outside the closure
\overline{f(F)}
of the set
f(F)
. The preimage
U^{x}:=f^{-1}\bigl(U^{f(x)}\bigr)
is a clopen neighborhood of the point
x
, and
U^{x}\cap F=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction with the choice of the point
x
and the closed set
F
.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
\operatorname{Ind}X\neq 0
. Then there exists
r>0
such that for every continuous mapping
f\colon X\to Y
into a metric space
Y
with
\operatorname{Ind}Y=0
, the inequality
\operatorname{dis}f\geq r
holds. In particular, we have
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq r
.

Proof.

By the Nagami–Roberts Theorem
[
20
, Theorem on p. 601]
there exist closed subsets
F,H\subset X
such that
r=|FH|>0
and every clopen set containing
F
intersects
H
. For every mapping
f\colon X\to Y
, the alternative holds: either
\bigl|f(F)f(H)\bigr|=0
, or
\bigl|f(F)f(H)\bigr|>0
.

In the first case, the estimate
\operatorname{dis}f\geq|FH|=r
takes place.

In the second case, the condition
\operatorname{Ind}Y=0
implies the existence of a clopen neighborhood
U^{f(F)}
of the closed set
\overline{f(F)}
that lies outside the closure
\overline{f(H)}
of the set
f(H)
. The preimage
U^{F}=f^{-1}\bigl(U^{f(F)}\bigr)
is a clopen neighborhood of the set
F
and
U^{F}\cap H=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction with the choice of the closed sets
F
and
H
.
∎

Recall that a topological space is called
totally disconnected
if all its connected components are singletons.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
K\subset X
be a connected subset of a metric space
X
, and
Y
be a totally disconnected metric space. Then for every continuous mapping
f\colon X\to Y
, we have
\operatorname{dis}f\geq\operatorname{diam}K
and therefore
2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{diam}K
. In particular, if
X
is a connected metric space, and
Y
is a totally disconnected metric space such that
\operatorname{diam}X\geq\operatorname{diam}Y
, then
2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)=\operatorname{diam}X
. For example, this holds for any discrete
\varepsilon
-net
Y\subset X
.

Proof.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a continuous mapping, then, by virtue of the connectedness of
K
, the restriction of
f
to
K
is constant,
so that
\operatorname{dis}f\geq\operatorname{diam}K
, therefore
2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{diam}X
by the definition of continuous Hausdorff distance.
The second assertion follows from Item (
2
) of Proposition
3.2
.
∎

5
Zero Distance

We have already pointed out that the (continuous) Gromov–Hausdorff distance is a pseudometric, i.e., it can be zero between non-isometric spaces. Therefore, it is important to describe certain classes of metric spaces that are at zero distance from each other.

Theorem 5.1
.

For compact metric spaces
X
and
Y
, the following conditions are equivalent
:

(1)

d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)=0
;

(2)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=0
;

(3)

spaces
X
and
Y
are isometric.

Proof.

The implications (
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
) follow from Properties (
1
) and (
2
) of Proposition
3.2
, respectively.

Implication (
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
) is the famous deep Theorem
[
8
, theorem 7.3.30]
.
∎

For applications, we will need the following version of the previous theorem, which follows immediately from
[
8
, Exercise 7.3.31]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

If a metric space
X
is compact, then the following conditions on a metric space
Y
are equivalent
:

(1)

d_{GH}(X,Y)=0
;

(2)

the space
Y
is isometric to a dense subset of the space
X

(
e.g., in the case of
X=S^{n}
there are at least continuum number of pairwise non-isometric
Y
, see Introduction
)
.

The situation with the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance is significantly different.

We fix on the sphere
S^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, some metric
\rho
and some continuous free involution
\sigma
(for example, an antipodal mapping). Since each continuous function on a compact set attains its minimum, then

d(\rho,\sigma):=\inf\Bigl\{\rho\bigl(x,\sigma(x)\bigr):x\in S^{n}\Bigr\}=\min\Bigl\{\rho\bigl(x,\sigma(x)\bigr):x\in S^{n}\Bigr\}>0.

We put

d(\rho):=\sup\bigl\{d(\rho,\sigma):\text{$\sigma$ --- continuous free involution on $S^{n}$}\bigr\}>0.

Proposition 5.3
.

For an arbitrary metric
\rho
on the sphere
S^{n}
and an arbitrary metric space
Y
that is topologically embeddable in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following inequalities hold
:

d(\rho)\leq 2d^{c}_{GH}(S^{n},Y)\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}S^{n},\operatorname{diam}Y\}.

Proof.

Let
\nu\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a topological embedding,
f\colon S^{n}\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to S^{n}
be continuous mappings, and
h=\nu\circ f
. Consider an arbitrary continuous free involution
\sigma
on the sphere
S^{n}
. According to generalized theorem of Borsuk–Ulam type
[
13
, Theorem 5]
, there exists a point
x\in S^{n}
such that
h(x)=h\bigl(\sigma(x)\bigr)
, therefore, since
\nu
is injective, we have
f(x)=f\bigl(\sigma(x)\bigr)
. This means that
\operatorname{dis}f\geq d(\rho,\sigma)
. Consequently,
d(\rho,\sigma)\leq 2d^{c}_{GH}(S^{n},Y)
. The left inequality follows from the arbitrariness of the involution under consideration.

The right inequality is Property (
4
) of Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

From Proposition
5.3
it follows that for the standard sphere of unit radius
S^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, endowed with a geodesic or induced Euclidean distance, for every
m>n
, the equality
2d^{c}_{GH}(S^{n},S^{m})=\operatorname{diam}S^{n}
holds. Note that according to
[
2
, Theorem A, p. 5]
, we get
2d_{GH}(S^{n},S^{m})<\operatorname{diam}S^{n}
.

Corollary 5.5
.

For an arbitrary metric
\rho
on the sphere
S^{n}
and an arbitrary metric space
Y
, the following conditions are equivalent
:

(1)

d_{GH}^{c}(S^{n},Y)=0
, and

(2)

the space
Y
is isometric sphere
(S^{n},\rho)
.

Proof.

(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
).
Since
d_{GH}(S^{n},Y)\leq d_{GH}^{c}(S^{n},Y)=0
, then according to Theorem
5.2
, one can assume that the space
Y
is contained in
(S^{n},\rho)
. If
Y
is a proper subset of the sphere, then it is topologically embedded in the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
2d^{c}_{GH}(S^{n},Y)\geq d(\rho)>0
according to Proposition
5.3
.

The implication (
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
) is obvious.
∎

We say that a metric space
X

has the uniqueness property in the class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,
\mathcal{M}
,
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
, or
\mathcal{M}^{c}
if for every metric space

6
Comparison of Topologies

Let
p_{c}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
be the identity mapping. By Property (
1
) of Proposition
3.2
,
p_{c}
is non-expanding and hence continuous. Recall also that a continuous bijective mapping is called a
condensation
. This concept also carries over to topological classes, so
p_{c}
is a condensation. Recall (Proposition
4.9
) that in this case, two metric spaces at positive distance
d_{GH}^{c}
can be sometimes mapped to spaces with zero distance
d_{GH}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
is discontinuous at every point
X\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
such that
\operatorname{Ind}X\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let us choose an arbitrary non-empty metric space
X
such that
\operatorname{Ind}X\neq 0
. By Proposition
4.8
, there exists
r>0
such that for every non-empty metric space
Y
with
\operatorname{Ind}Y=0
, we have
d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq r
. For every discrete space
Z
, it holds
\operatorname{Ind}Z=0
. On the other hand, it is easy to show that for every
\delta>0
, there exists a discrete
Y\subset X
such that
d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq d_{H}(X,Y)<\delta
. The latter means that there is no a neighborhood
U_{\delta}(X)
with
\delta<r
and
p_{c}^{-1}\bigl(U_{\delta}(X)\bigr)\subset U_{r}(X)
. The latter means the discontinuity of
p_{c}^{-1}
at the point
X
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

In the proof of Proposition
6.1
we actually showed more, namely that
p_{c}^{-1}
is discontinuous at
X
,
\operatorname{Ind}X\neq 0
on various smaller sets,
for example,

•

on the union of
\{X\}
with the class of all spaces with zero large inductive dimension;

•

on the union of
\{X\}
with the class of all discrete spaces;

•

for compact
X
, on the union of
\{X\}
with the set of all finite metric spaces.

Recall that the mapping
p_{c}
is an isometry on the subclass of all zero-dimensional spaces in the sense of Lebesgue (Corollary
4.6
). However, the results obtained above show that adding even a single point
X
can lead to discontinuity in
p_{c}^{-1}
and, hence, to violation of isometry.

Below we will show that the mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
can be discontinuous at points corresponding to discrete spaces, and the discontinuity manifests itself on a sufficiently thin subset of
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
. Nevertheless, on completely discrete spaces
X
, i.e., when
s(X)>0
, the mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
is continuous. The following result follows immediately from Proposition
4.3
.

Corollary 6.3
.

If
s(X)>0
, then the mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
is continuous at the point
X\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
.

For compact metric spaces, there is a natural criterion for the continuity of the mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

For a compact metric space
X\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, the following conditions are equivalent

(1)

\operatorname{Ind}X=0
;

(2)

the mapping
p_{c}^{-1}
is continuous at
X
.

Proof.

(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
). Assume the contrary, i.e., that
\operatorname{Ind}X\neq 0
. By Proposition
4.8
, there exists
r>0
, depending only on
X
, such that
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq r
. But the latter contradicts the continuity of
p_{c}^{-1}
at
X
.

(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
). We need to prove that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
d_{GH}(X,Y)<\delta
implies
d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)<\varepsilon
. For
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon/4
, consider the covering
\lambda=\bigl\{U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)\bigr\}_{x\in X}
. Since
\dim X=0
, there exists a clopen partition
\mu
inscribed in
\lambda
. Since
X
is compact, the partition
\mu
is finite. Let
\mu=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\}
, then
r:=\min\bigl\{|U_{i}U_{j}|:i\neq j\bigr\}>0
and
\operatorname{diam}U_{i}\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon/2
. We set
\delta=\min\{r/2,\varepsilon^{\prime}\}<\varepsilon/4
and show that
\delta
is what we are looking for.

Let
d_{GH}(X,Y)<\delta
, then there exists
\alpha>0
such that
d_{GH}(X,Y)<\delta-\alpha
and, therefore, we can choose a correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
for which
\operatorname{dis}R<2\delta-2\alpha\leq r-2\alpha
. Let
V_{k}=R(U_{k})
, then for
i\neq j
, we have
V_{i}\cap V_{j}=\emptyset
, since for any
y_{k}\in V_{k}
and
x_{k}\in U_{k}
such that
(x_{k},y_{k})\in R
, we have

|y_{i}y_{j}|\geq|x_{i}x_{j}|-\operatorname{dis}R>r-(r-2\alpha)=2\alpha.

Thus,
V_{k}=\cup_{y\in V_{k}}U_{\alpha}(y)
is an open set for every
k
, so
\{V_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
is a partition of
Y
by clopen sets. Note that
\operatorname{diam}V_{k}\leq\operatorname{diam}U_{k}+\operatorname{dis}R\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}<3\varepsilon/4
.

Let us now choose arbitrary
x_{k}\in U_{k}
,
y_{k}\in V_{k}
,
(x_{k},y_{k})\in R
and define the mappings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
as follows:
f(U_{k})=y_{k}
and
g(V_{k})=x_{k}
, then
f
and
g
are continuous. Let us show that
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}<2\varepsilon
, whence
d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}<\varepsilon
, which is what we want.

Let us choose arbitrary
x\in U_{i}
and
x^{\prime}\in U_{j}
, then

\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|y_{i}y_{j}|\bigr|=\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime}x_{i}|+|x^{\prime}x_{i}|-|x_{i}x_{j}|+|x_{i}x_{j}|-|y_{i}y_{j}|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime}x_{i}|\bigr|+\bigl||x^{\prime}x_{i}|-|x_{i}x_{j}|\bigr|+\bigl||x_{i}x_{j}|-|y_{i}y_{j}|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq|xx_{i}|+|x^{\prime}x_{j}|+\operatorname{dis}R\leq\operatorname{diam}U_{i}+\operatorname{diam}U_{j}+\operatorname{dis}R<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/4<2\varepsilon,

therefore
\operatorname{dis}f<2\varepsilon
.

Let us now choose arbitrary
y\in V_{i}
and
y^{\prime}\in V_{j}
, then

\bigl||x_{i}x_{j}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|=\bigl||x_{i}x_{j}|-|y_{i}y_{j}|+|y_{i}y_{j}|-|y_{j}y|+|y_{j}y|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq\bigl||x_{i}x_{j}|-|y_{i}y_{j}|\bigr|+\bigl||y_{i}y_{j}|-|y_{j}y|\bigr|+\bigl||y_{j}y|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq\operatorname{dis}R+|y_{i}y|+|y_{j}y^{\prime}|\leq\operatorname{dis}R+\operatorname{diam}V_{i}+\operatorname{diam}V_{j}<\varepsilon/4+3\varepsilon/4+3\varepsilon/4<2\varepsilon,

therefore
\operatorname{dis}g<2\varepsilon
.

Finally, let us estimate the codistortion
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)
. To do this, we choose arbitrary
x\in U_{i}
and
y\in V_{j}
, then

\bigl||x\,x_{j}|-|y_{i}y|\bigr|=\bigl||x\,x_{j}|-|x_{j}x_{i}|+|x_{j}x_{i}|-|y_{j}y_{i}|+|y_{j}y_{i}|-|y_{i}y|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq\bigl||x\,x_{j}|-|x_{j}x_{i}|\bigr|+\bigl||x_{j}x_{i}|-|y_{j}y_{i}|\bigr|+\bigl||y_{j}y_{i}|-|y_{i}y|\bigr|\leq\\
\leq|xx_{i}|+\operatorname{dis}R+|y_{j}y|\leq\operatorname{diam}U_{i}+\operatorname{dis}R+\operatorname{diam}U_{j}<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/4+\varepsilon/2<2\varepsilon,

from which
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)<2\varepsilon
and, therefore,
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}<2\varepsilon
, which completes the proof.
∎

Example 6.5
.

Theorem

such that the mapping

is discontinuous at the point

. By Corollary

Let us take a countable number

. In the interval

. We put

X_{\infty}=\sqcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}S_{k}\subset Z\ \ \text{i}\ \ X_{n}=\bigl(\sqcup_{k<n}S_{k}\bigr)\sqcup\bigl(\sqcup_{k\geq n}I_{k}\bigr)\subset Z.

7
Incomparable Spaces

We say that a topological space
X
is
incomparable
with
Y
if every continuous mapping
f\colon X\to Y
is trivial, i.e., there is a point
y_{f}\in Y
such that
f(X)=y_{f}
. For example, every connected space is incomparable with every totally disconnected space. Note that the incomparability relation is not symmetric; for example, the interval
X=[0,1]
is incomparable with the set
Y
of its rational points, but
Y
is comparable with
X
(the inclusion of
Y
in
X
is a nontrivial continuous mapping).

Proposition 7.1
.

Let the metric space
X
be incomparable with the metric space
Y
. Then

(1)

for any mapping
f\colon X\to Y
, it holds
\operatorname{dis}f=\operatorname{diam}X
;

(2)

for any mappings
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
, it holds

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\geq\max\bigl\{R(X),R(Y)\bigr\}.

Proof.

(
1
) We have

\operatorname{dis}f=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|f(x)f(x^{\prime})|\bigr|=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}|xx^{\prime}|=\operatorname{diam}(X).

(
2
) We have

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}\bigl||x\,g(y)|-|f(x)y|\bigr|\geq\sup_{x\in X}\bigl||x\,g(y_{f})|-|f(x)y_{f}|\bigr|=\\
=\sup_{x\in X}|x\,g(y_{f})|=R_{g(y_{f})}(X)\geq R(X).

Similarly,

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X,y\in Y}\Bigl|\bigl|x\,g(y)\bigr|-\bigl|f(x)y\bigr|\Bigr|\geq\sup_{y\in Y}\Bigl|\bigl|g(y)g(y)\bigr|-\bigl|f\bigl(g(y)\bigr)y\bigr|\Bigr|=\\
\sup_{y\in Y}\Bigl|\bigl|g(y)g(y)\bigr|-|y_{f}y|\Bigr|=\sup_{y\in Y}|y_{f}y|=R_{y_{f}}(Y)\geq R(Y).

To complete the proof, it remains to gather together the two resulting inequalities.
∎

Corollary 7.2
.

Let a metric space
X
be incomparable with a metric space
Y
. Then
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{diam}(X)
.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be mutually incomparable metric spaces. Then

2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)=\max\bigl\{\operatorname{diam}(X),\operatorname{diam}(Y)\bigr\}.

Proof.

The inequality
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq\max\bigl\{\operatorname{diam}(X),\operatorname{diam}(Y)\bigr\}
follows from Corollary

. The inverse inequality is Item (
4
) of Proposition

∎

Hyperspace of Cook’s Continuum

Recall that a
continuum
is every connected compact Hausdorff topological space. We will restrict ourselves to continua that are metric spaces. Thus, in what follows, by
continuum
we mean a metrizable continuum.

We will also be interested in various
hyperspaces
. If
X
is a metric space, then by
\mathcal{H}(X)
we denote the family of all non-empty closed bounded subsets of

, endowed with the Hausdorff distance

[

that

is a metric on
\mathcal{H}(X)
. This

will be in our case the most general example of hyperspaces (a richer list of hyperspaces can be found in
[

]
). An important subspace of

is the family
\mathcal{K}(X)
of all non-empty compact subsets of

is compact, then

is denoted by

. Since every point in

is a degenerate subcontinuum and the Hausdorff distance

between one-point subspaces of

coincides with the distance in

between the corresponding points, there is an isometric embedding

from

into

. In what follows, we will informally write

to refer to this embedding.

Theorem 8.1
.

The spaces
X
and
\mathcal{CK}(X)
are closed subsets of
\mathcal{K}(X)
.

Theorem 8.2

(
[
8
]
)
.

A space
\mathcal{H}(X)
is complete
(
totally bounded, compact, boundedly compact
)
if and only if

is of the same type.

If

is a continuum, then, by Theorem

is a closed subset of the compact set

is also inherited. And even more interesting:

turns out to be path-connected, and for a non-degenerate

.

For any continuum

is linearly connected continuum
(
[

, has trivial shape

For a hereditarily indecomposable or locally connected continuum

If the continuum

A continuum
X
is called

if each of its subcontinuum

cannot be represented as the union of two proper (non-empty and distinct from

Recall that we call a topological space

Proposition 8.5
.

Let

be a path-connected space, and
Y
a hereditarily indecomposable continuum. Then

is incomparable with
Y
. In particular,
2d_{GH}^{c}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{diam}X

Proof.

Let

be a continuous mapping such that
f(X)
contains at least two distinct points
y_{0}=f(x_{0})\neq f(x_{1})=y_{1}
. Since
X
is path-connected, there exists a continuous mapping
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to X
such that
\varphi(0)=x_{0}
and
\varphi(1)=x_{1}

\varphi(t_{0})=x_{0}

A

9
Continuous GH-Distance is Intrinsic

Let
X
and
Y
be arbitrary metric spaces such that
2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)<\infty
. Choose an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, then there exist continuous
X\operatornamewithlimits{\rightleftarrows}\limits^{f}_{g}Y
such that
\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}<2d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+2\varepsilon
. For each
t\in(0,1)
, define a metric
d_{t}
on
R:=R_{f,g}
as follows:

d_{t}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)=(1-t)|xx^{\prime}|+t\,|yy^{\prime}|,

and we denote the resulting metric space by
R_{t}
. We extend
R_{t}
by setting
R_{0}=X
and
R_{1}=Y
.

Theorem 9.1
.

In the notation introduced above, we have
d^{c}_{GH}(R_{t},R_{s})\leq|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R
. In particular, the mapping
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}^{c}
is a continuous curve whose length
|\gamma|
satisfies the inequality
|\gamma|<d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+\varepsilon
, and the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance is intrinsic.

Proof.

Let
0<t,\,s<1
, and
R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(R_{t},R_{s})
be the correspondence generated by the identity mapping. Then

\operatorname{dis}R^{\prime}=\sup_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}\Bigl|d_{t}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)-d_{s}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\Bigr|=\\
=\sup_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}|ts|\,\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|=|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R.

Further, for
t=0
,
X=R_{t}
and
R_{s}
,
0<s<1
, we choose the correspondence
R_{f^{\prime},g^{\prime}}
as
R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(R_{t},R_{s})
, where
f^{\prime}(x)=\bigl(x,f(x)\bigr)
for all
x\in X
, and
g^{\prime}\bigl((x,y)\bigr)=x
. The mapping
f^{\prime}
is continuous, since it is the restriction to
X\times f(X)
of the mapping
x\mapsto\bigl(x,f(x)\bigr)
from
X
to
X\times Y
with continuous coordinate mappings. The mapping
g^{\prime}
is also continuous as the restriction of the projection
X\times Y\to X
. Further,

\operatorname{dis}f^{\prime}=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Bigl||xx^{\prime}|-(1-s)|xx^{\prime}|-s\,\bigl|f(x)f(x^{\prime})\bigr|\Bigr|=\\
=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}s\,\Bigl||xx^{\prime}|-\bigl|f(x)f(x^{\prime})\bigr|\Bigr|=|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}f\leq|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R;

\operatorname{dis}g^{\prime}=\sup_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-(1-s)|xx^{\prime}|-s\,|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|=\sup_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}s\,\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|=|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R;

\operatorname{codis}(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})=\sup_{x\in X,\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}\biggl|\Bigl|x\,g^{\prime}\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\Bigr|-\bigl|(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,f^{\prime}(x)\bigr|\biggr|=\\
=\sup_{x\in X,\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}\Bigl||xx^{\prime}|-(1-s)|xx^{\prime}|-s\,\bigl|y^{\prime}\,f(x)\bigr|\Bigr|=\\
=\sup_{(x,f(x)),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R}s\,\Bigl||xx^{\prime}|-\bigl|y^{\prime}\,f(x)\bigr|\Bigr|\leq|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R.

Thus, we have shown that
\operatorname{dis}R^{\prime}\leq|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R
also in the case
t=0
and
0<s<1
. The case
0<t<1
and
s=1
is analyzed similarly. Finally, for
t=0
and
s=1
, we set
R^{\prime}=R
, so that here
\operatorname{dis}R^{\prime}=|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R
. Thus, for all
0\leq t,\,s\leq 1
, we have

d^{c}_{GH}(R_{t},R_{s})\leq\frac{1}{2}|ts|\,\operatorname{dis}R,

from which it immediately follows that the mapping
\gamma\colon t\mapsto R_{t}
is continuous with respect to
d^{c}_{GH}
, and the length
|\gamma|
of the curve
\gamma
does not exceed
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dis}R<d^{c}_{GH}(X,Y)+\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we conclude that the distance
d^{c}_{GH}
is intrinsic.
∎

As in the standard theory, the correspondence
R_{f,g}\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
is called
optimal
if
2d^{c}(X,Y)=\operatorname{dis}R_{f,g}
.

Corollary 9.2
.

If the correspondence
R=R_{f,g}
is optimal, then the curve
R_{t}
constructed above is the shortest geodesic, the length of which is equal to the distance between its ends.

Incompleteness

Proposition 10.1
.

The continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance restricted to the space
\mathcal{M}^{c}
of metric compacts is not complete.

Proof.

Consider the sequence
X_{n}=\{i/n\}_{i=0}^{n}
of subsets of the interval
[0,1]
with induced distance. Since the continuous and ordinary GH-distances coincide on finite metric spaces, this sequence is fundamental in
\mathcal{M}^{c}
. Suppose that
X_{n}\operatornamewithlimits{\longrightarrow}\limits^{d^{c}_{GH}}X
, then
X_{n}\operatornamewithlimits{\longrightarrow}\limits^{d_{GH}}X
, and since
\mathcal{M}
is a metric space, the limit is uniquely defined, hence
X=[0,1]
. However, by Proposition
4.9
we have
d^{c}(X_{n},X)=1/2
, so the sequence
X_{n}
does not converge to
X
in
\mathcal{M}^{c}
.
∎

The validity of Proposition
10.1
is due to the fact that we have restricted the class of metric spaces (compacta) in which we seek the limit of the given fundamental sequence. However, in the class of all metric spaces, this sequence has a limit, namely, it is each zero-dimensional dense subset of the interval. Let us construct a fundamental sequence for which no metric space is its limit w.r.t. the continuous Gromov-Hausdorff metric.

We will need additional technical result.

Lemma 10.2
.

If for a subset
A\subset X
and a mapping
f\colon X\to Y
at least one of the sets
A
or
f(A)
has a finite diameter, then it holds
\operatorname{dis}f\geq\bigl|\operatorname{diam}f(A)-\operatorname{diam}A\bigr|
.

Proof.

Suppose that only one of the sets
A
and
f(A)
has a finite diameter. Then, in the set of finite diameter, each pair of points is at a bounded distance, while in the second, the corresponding points (from the image or preimage of
f
) can be chosen arbitrarily far apart, which proves
\operatorname{dis}f=\infty
, and the inequality holds.

Now let both
A
and
f(A)
have finite diameters. We fix
\varepsilon>0
.

Let us take points
a,a^{\prime}\in A
such that
|aa^{\prime}|>\operatorname{diam}A-\varepsilon
. Then

\operatorname{dis}f\geq|aa^{\prime}|-\bigl|f(a)f(a^{\prime})\bigr|>\operatorname{diam}A-\varepsilon-\bigl|f(a)f(a^{\prime})\bigr|\geq\operatorname{diam}A-\varepsilon-\operatorname{diam}f(A).

Due to the arbitrariness of the number
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain
\operatorname{dis}f\geq\operatorname{diam}A-\operatorname{diam}f(A)
.

Next, we take points
a,a^{\prime}\in A
such that
\bigl|f(a)f(a^{\prime})\bigr|>\operatorname{diam}f(A)-\varepsilon
. Then

\operatorname{dis}f\geq\bigl|f(a)f(a^{\prime})\bigr|-|aa^{\prime}|>\operatorname{diam}f(A)-\varepsilon-|aa^{\prime}|\geq\operatorname{diam}f(A)-\varepsilon-\operatorname{diam}A.

Since the number
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain
\operatorname{dis}f\geq\operatorname{diam}f(A)-\operatorname{diam}A
, which completes the proof.
∎

The resulting inequality is a strengthening of Proposition
3.2
Property (
5
).

Lemma 10.3
.

For any mappings
f\colon X\to Y
,
g\colon Y\to X
, and any point
y_{0}\in Y
, we have
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\geq\bigl|y_{0}\,f(X)\bigr|
.

Proof.

Take a point
x=g(y_{0})\in X
. Then

\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\geq\bigl|f(x)\,y_{0}\bigr|-\bigl|x\,g(y_{0})\bigr|=\bigl|f(x)\,y_{0}\bigr|\geq\bigl|y_{0}\,f(X)\bigr|,

which is what was required.
∎

Example 10.4
.

In the plane with coordinates
(x,y)
we choose the following intervals:
J=\{(0,y):-1\leq y\leq 1\}
,
I=\{(x,0):0\leq x\leq 1\}
, and
I_{n}=\{(x,0):\frac{1}{n}\leq x\leq 1\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the triode
X=J\cup I
and the sequence of spaces
X_{n}=J\cup I_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. On the triode
X=J\cup I
we take the intrinsic metric, and on its subsets
X_{n}
the metric induced by this intrinsic metric.

Theorem 10.5
.

The sequence of spaces
X_{n}=J\cup I_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is fundamental w.r.t. the metric
d^{c}_{GH}
, but no metric space is its limit in this metric.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
d_{GH}^{c}(X_{m},X_{n})\leq\frac{m-n}{mn}<\frac{1}{n}
for
m\geq n
. Therefore, the sequence of compact metric spaces
X_{n}
is fundamental in the metric
d_{GH}^{c}
.

One can easily verify that
d_{GH}(X,X_{n})\leq d_{H}(X,X_{n})=\frac{1}{2n}
. Therefore, the triode
X
is the limit of the sequence
X_{n}
in the metric
d_{GH}
.

Let this sequence have a limit
X_{\infty}
in the metric
d^{c}_{GH}
.

From the inequality
d_{GH}\leq d_{GH}^{c}
it follows that the space
X_{\infty}
is also the limit of the sequence
X_{n}
in the metric
d_{GH}
. Therefore,
d_{GH}(X,X_{\infty})=0
.

According to Theorem
5.2
, we can assume that the space
X_{\infty}
belongs to the triode
X
and is dense there.

Let us show that
\bigl\{(0,-1)\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{(0,1)\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{(1,0)\bigr\}\cup X_{\infty}=X
. The inclusion of the left set into the right set is obvious.

Take an arbitrary
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X\setminus\Bigl(\bigl\{(0,-1)\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{(0,1)\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{(1,0)\bigr\}\cup X_{\infty}\Bigr)
.

a. Let
(x_{0},y_{0})\neq(0,0)
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
(x_{0},y_{0})=(r,0)
,
r>0
. Then one of the connected components
K
of the space
X\setminus\bigl\{(x_{0},y_{0})\bigr\}
has diameter
\operatorname{diam}K=1-r<1
. We fix an index
n
such that
\operatorname{diam}I_{n}=1-1/n>\operatorname{diam}K
.

Let
m\geq n
and the mapping
f\colon X_{m}\to X_{\infty}
be continuous. The point
(r,0)
partitions the space
X_{\infty}
, so any connected set intersecting
K
lies entirely in
K
.

If
f(J)\cap K\neq\emptyset
, then
f(J)\subset K
and
\operatorname{dis}f\geq 2-(1-r)=1+r
according to Lemma
10.2
.

If
f(I_{m})\cap K\neq\emptyset
, then
f(I_{m})\subset K
and
\operatorname{dis}f\geq 1-\frac{1}{m}-(1-r)=r-\frac{1}{m}>r-\frac{1}{n}
according to Lemma
10.2
.

If

, then
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\geq 1-r
according to Lemma
10.3
for any mapping

.

b. Let
(x,y)=(0,0)
. Then the space
X\setminus\bigl\{(x,y)\bigr\}=K_{1}\sqcup K_{2}\sqcup K_{3}
consists of three connected components of diameter

. Hence, for every continuous mapping

, there is a connected component

such that

. Therefore,
\operatorname{dis}(f)\geq\operatorname{diam}J-\operatorname{diam}K_{i}=1
by Lemma

.

In account, we proved that the space
X_{\infty}
is connected. This means that for every continuous mapping

of a connected space into a space with two connected components, the latter has a connected component
K
such that

. By Lemma

,
\operatorname{codis}(f,g)\geq 1-\frac{1}{m}
for any mapping

.

Therefore, for any metric space
X_{\infty}
, the sequence of distances
d_{GH}^{c}(X_{m},X_{\infty})
cannot tend to zero.
∎

Addition: An Analog of Topology on Proper Classes

The theory we develop in this article deals with all nonempty metric spaces, considered up to isometry. Since a metric can be introduced on every set, for example, by setting all distances between distinct points equal to
1
, the family of all metric spaces is not a set. To work with such families, we will use von Neumann-Bernays-Gödel set theory
[
4
,
5
]
, and we will always assume that the axiom of choice holds. This theory is usually abbreviated NBGC. Recall that all objects in this theory are called
classes
and they come in two types:
sets
are the classes that are elements of other classes, and
proper classes
, which are not elements of any other classes. Here are some examples of proper classes that are important to us:

•

the class
\mathcal{V}
of all sets;

•

the class
\operatorname{ORD}
of all ordinals;

•

the class
\operatorname{CARD}
of all cardinals;

•

the class
\operatorname{TOP}
of all topologies on all sets in
\mathcal{V}
;

•

the class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
of all metric spaces, considered up to isometry.

Many standard operations are defined for classes, such as intersection, complement, product, mapping, and others.

We will use the following terminology: a
generalized pseudometric on a set
X
is any mapping
\rho\colon X\times X\to[0,\infty]
satisfying the conditions
\rho(x,y)=\rho(y,x)
for all
x,y\in X
(symmetry),
\rho(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
, and
\rho(x,z)\leq\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
(triangle inequality). If
\rho(x,y)=0
exactly when
x=y
, then the word ‘‘pseudometric’’ is replaced by the word
metric
. If
\rho(x,y)<\infty
for all
x,y\in X
, then the word ‘‘generalized’’ is dropped. Since the product and mapping are defined for all classes, the concepts just described carry over word for word to arbitrary classes. Below, we recall the definitions of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance and the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance, which, as will be noted, are generalized pseudometrics on the proper class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
.

An interesting feature of proper classes is the impossibility of literally transferring the concept of topology to them: indeed, the entire set on which the topology is defined is an element of the topology, so if we consider a proper class instead of the set, it cannot be an element of the topology. To circumvent this problem and introduce an analogue of topology, it was proposed in
[
26
]
to consider
classes
\mathfrak{C}
filtered by sets
. The latter means that for every cardinal number
n
, the subclass
\mathfrak{C}_{n}=\{x\in\mathfrak{C}:\#x\leq n\}
, where
\#x
denotes the cardinality of the set
x
, is a set. Examples of such classes include any sets, as well as the proper classes
\operatorname{ORD}
,
\operatorname{CARD}
, and
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
. The classes
\mathcal{V}
and
\operatorname{TOP}
are not such.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a set-filtered class. Consider a mapping
\tau\colon\operatorname{CARD}\to\operatorname{TOP}
such that

•

\tau_{n}:=\tau(n)
is a topology on
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
for every
n\in\operatorname{CARD}
,

•

for every
m,n\in\operatorname{CARD}
,
m\leq n
, the topology
\tau_{m}
is induced from
\tau_{n}
.

Note that if
\mathfrak{C}
is a set of cardinality
n
, then
\tau_{n}
is the usual topology on
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, and for every
m\geq n
we have
\mathfrak{C}_{m}=\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, and also
\tau_{m}=\tau_{n}
. For
m\leq n
, the topology
\tau_{m}
on
\mathfrak{C}_{m}
is induced from
\tau_{n}
in the usual way. This allows us to call the mapping
\tau
a
topology
even in the case of proper classes. A class
\mathfrak{C}
, filtered by sets, for which the topology
\tau
is defined, will be called a
topological class
.

Remark 11.1
.

A topology in the above sense can also be defined for any proper class. To do this, it is sufficient to modify the concept of a filtration by sets, without tying it to the cardinality of the elements in the class. It is well known that in NBGC there is a bijection between any proper classes. Therefore, if
\mathfrak{C}
is a topological class and
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}
is an arbitrary class, say
\mathcal{V}
, then using the bijection
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}
, we can transfer the filtration by sets
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
to
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}
, setting
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}_{n}=\varphi(\mathfrak{C}_{n})
and performing all the topological constructions described above. However, for our purposes, the filtration defined by cardinality is sufficient.

An important special case is the metric topology defined on the class
\mathfrak{C}
, filtered by sets. Let
\rho\colon\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C}\to[0,\infty]
be a generalized pseudometric. Then, for each
n\in\operatorname{CARD}
, there is a corresponding metric topology
\tau_{n}
on
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
. The corresponding mapping
\tau\colon\operatorname{CARD}\to\operatorname{TOP}
,
\tau\colon n\mapsto\tau_{n}
, is called the
metric topology
, and the space
\mathfrak{C}
endowed with this topology is called the
metric class
.

Note that for every
x\in\mathfrak{C}
and
r\in(0,\infty]
, there is an
open ball

U_{r}(x)=\{y\in\mathfrak{C}:\rho(x,y)<r\}
, which is, generally speaking, a proper subclass of
\mathfrak{C}
. It is easy to see that if
m\in\operatorname{CARD}
is not less than
\#x
, then the set
U_{r}(x)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}
is an open ball of radius
r
in
\mathfrak{C}_{m}
.

Proposition 11.2
.

For any
x\in\mathfrak{C}
,
r>0
, and
m\in\operatorname{CARD}
, the set
U_{r}(x)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}
is open in the
\tau_{m}
topology.

Proof.

Choose an arbitrary
y\in U_{r}(x)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}
, then
|xy|<r
. There exists
\delta>0
such that
|xy|<r-\delta
, so
U_{\delta}(y)\subset U_{r}(x)
, but
U_{\delta}(y)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}
is an open ball of radius
\delta
in
\mathfrak{C}_{m}
, and
U_{\delta}(y)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}\subset U_{r}(x)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}
, therefore
U_{r}(x)\cap\mathfrak{C}_{m}\in\tau_{m}
.
∎

If
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
is a mapping between two topological classes, then its continuity is defined naturally. Namely, it is known that the image of every set is also a set, so for every cardinal
n
there exists a cardinal
m
such that
f(\mathfrak{A}_{n})\subset\mathfrak{B}_{m}
. The smallest of such cardinals
m
will be denoted by
n_{f}
. Restricting
f
to the mapping from
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
to
\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
, we obtain the usual mapping of topological spaces. It is easy to see that if we take a cardinal number
m\geq n_{f}
instead of
n_{f}
, then the restriction of
f
to
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{m}
is continuous (at a point or in the whole) if and only if its restriction to
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
is continuous. Thus, the
continuity
of a mapping
f
at a point or in the whole is defined as the continuity of all its restrictions
f\colon\mathfrak{A}_{n}\to\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
(we will continue to denote the restriction by the same letter as the original mapping).

Theorem 11.3
.

Let
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
be a mapping of metric classes. Then
f
is continuous at a point
x\in\mathfrak{A}
if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that
f\bigl(U_{\delta}(x)\bigr)\subset U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
. The mapping
f
is continuous in the whole if and only if it is continuous at every point.

Proof.

First, let for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
f\bigl(U_{\delta}(x)\bigr)\subset U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
. We show that
f
is continuous at
x
. Choose an arbitrary
n
, an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, and a corresponding
\delta>0
satisfying the above property. Since
U_{\delta}(x)\cap\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
are open balls in
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
, respectively, and by assumption the image of the first is contained in the second, the restriction
f\colon\mathfrak{A}_{n}\to\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
is continuous at
x
.

We now prove the converse by contradiction. Namely, suppose that for some
f
there exist
x\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
f\bigl(U_{\delta}(x)\bigr)\subset U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
does not hold for any
\delta>0
. The latter means that there exists a sequence
y_{k}\in\mathfrak{A}
for which
|x\,y_{k}|<1/k
, but
\bigl|f(x)f(y_{k})\bigr|\geq\varepsilon
. Let
n
be the cardinality of
x
, and
n_{k}
the cardinality of
y_{k}
. Since the restriction
f\colon\mathfrak{A}_{n}\to\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}
is continuous, there exists
s>0
such that
f
maps
U_{s}(x)\cap\mathfrak{A}_{n}
into
U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{n_{f}}\subset U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
. Thus, we can immediately assume that all
n_{k}
are at least
n
.

Since a countable union of sets is still a set, there exists
p\in\operatorname{CARD}
such that
p\geq n_{k}
for all
k
. Note that
x\in\mathfrak{A}_{p}
,
f(x)\in\mathfrak{B}_{p_{f}}
, and the restriction
f\colon\mathfrak{A}_{p}\to\mathfrak{B}_{p_{f}}
are continuous, so there exists
s>0
such that the
f
-image of the ball
U_{s}(x)\cap\mathfrak{A}_{p}
of radius
s
, open in
\mathfrak{A}_{p}
, is contained in the ball
U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{p_{f}}\subset U_{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
of radius
\varepsilon
, open in
\mathfrak{B}_{p_{f}}
. But then for
1/k<s
, we have
y_{k}\in U_{s}(x)\cap\mathfrak{A}_{p}
, and hence
\bigl|f(x)f(y_{k})\bigr|<\varepsilon
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 11.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
be a Lipschitz mapping of metric classes, in particular, non-expanding. Then
f
is continuous.

A mapping
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
of metric classes is called
open at
x\in\mathfrak{A}
if for every
r>0
, there exists
s>0
such that
f\bigl(U_{r}(x)\bigr)\supset U_{s}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
. The following result is immediately implied by the theorem.

Corollary 11.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
be a bijective mapping of metric classes. Then
f^{-1}
is continuous at
z=f(x)\in\mathfrak{B}
if and only if
f
is open at
x
.
