Evasion of CARD8 activation during HIV-1 assembly
Authors: Ivy K. Hughes, James B. Hood, Andrés A. Quiñones-Molina, Hisashi Akiyama, Suryaram Gummuluru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As intracellular parasites, viruses must devise sophisticated mechanisms to produce and assemble viral components while suppressing activation of innate immune effectors. Here, we report that coordination of HIV-1 assembly by the viral polyprotein Gag suppresses inappropriately timed protease (PR) activity to evade the PR activity sensor, caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 8 (CARD8). Using mutants of Gag, we show that disruption of domains controlling viral assembly site [matrix (MA)] or virus particle release (nucleocapsid and p6) leads to premature activation of PR and the CARD8 inflammasome, resulting in interleukin-1β (IL-1β) secretion and pyroptotic cell death. Further, we demonstrate that previously observed host-adaptive mutations in HIV-1 MA (M30K) and p6 (PTAP duplication) associated with greater fitness in humans improve infected CD4+ T cell survival in a PR-dependent manner, which may be regulated by CARD8. Together, this work reveals virus-encoded mechanistic control over PR activation and CARD8 sensing by HIV-1 Gag.

INTRODUCTION

HIV-1 encounters multiple innate immune sensors during viral replication, recognition by which can lead to the activation of type I interferon (IFN) and proinflammatory signaling cascades and cytokine secretion. Activation of innate immune pathways is most often deleterious to viral replication, particularly during early events of viral transmission at mucosal surfaces (1). Multiple cell-intrinsic antiviral IFN-stimulated genes have been shown to restrict productive HIV-1 infection, including antagonism of viral budding by tetherin [counteracted by HIV-1 viral protein U (Vpu)] and targeted hypermutation of viral genomes by apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic subunit 3G (APOBEC3G) [counteracted by HIV-1 viral infectivity factor (Vif)], highlighting the critical importance of IFN resistance displayed by successful transmitter/founder strains of HIV-1 (2). In addition to IFN-inducing sensors, cell-intrinsic inflammasome sensors also restrict virus infection via induction of pyroptosis of productively infected cells, which eliminates virus-producing cells and results in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18 (3).

Among these cell-intrinsic inflammasome sensors is CARD8, which mimics HIV-1 protease (PR) cleavage site specificity and initiates pyroptosis in response to intracellular viral proteolytic activity (4). CARD8 patrols the cytoplasm as a noncovalent complex of an N-terminal fragment (CARD8-N) and a C-terminal fragment (CARD8-C). Upon proteolysis of the N-terminal fragment by viral proteases, including HIV-1 PR, the C-terminal fragment is liberated and forms the CARD8 inflammasome, which initiates pyroptosis (5). The detection of HIV-1 PR activity by CARD8 was first reported in the context of the treatment of productively infected cells with non-nucleoside reverse transcription inhibitors (NNRTIs) (4). NNRTIs artificially enforce Gag–Pol dimerization and inappropriate activation of cytoplasmic PR activity (6). Activation of CARD8 by incoming virion-associated HIV-1 PR has also been reported and is initiated upon virus particle fusion with target cells, particularly at high multiplicities of infection (MOIs) or during cell-to-cell transmission (7, 8). However, the activation of CARD8 or lack thereof during the process of viral particle assembly has until this point not been investigated.

HIV-1 assembly begins with recognition of the phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P2]-rich plasma membrane (PM) by the matrix (MA) domain of Gag (9). Additional Gag and Gag-Pol monomers, as well as unspliced genomic RNA, are recruited to the assembly site, and capsid (CA)– and nucleocapsid (NC)–mediated interactions drive Gag oligomerization and particle assembly (10). Recruitment of ESCRT factors Tsg101 and/or ALIX by p6 results in timely budding, completing the assembly process (11–15). PR is required for final maturation of the viral particle into an infectious state (16). The timing of HIV-1 PR activity is tightly controlled, and the dimeric PR does not activate until the moment of or slightly after viral particle release (17, 18). This tight control over the timing of PR activity, combined with the apparent lack of CARD8 activation in the absence of NNRTI treatment, together strongly suggests the existence of virus-encoded mechanisms to restrain PR activity to avoid spurious CARD8 activation during viral assembly. To investigate these mechanisms of virus-encoded control of PR activity, we performed in vitro studies using cell lines and primary cells infected with viruses encoding mutants of Gag. We found that multiple regions of Gag, namely, MA, NC, and p6, regulate viral proteolytic activity and CARD8 activation. We also find that previously documented mutations in the MA and p6 regions of Gag associated with greater replicative fitness in humans may improve evasion of pyroptotic cell death in HIV-infected cells. In summary, this work reveals a previously undescribed interaction between HIV-1 and the cell-intrinsic innate immune system and highlights domains of Gag potentially amenable to pharmaceutical intervention to kill infected cells.

RESULTS

HIV-1 viral MA protein suppresses inflammasome activation

HIV-1 MA is composed of a lipidated N-terminal myristoyl moiety, followed by a series of basic residues known as the highly basic region (HBR). HBR displays a specific recognition capacity for the PI(4,5)P2-rich inner leaflet of the PM (19). In addition, HBR coordinates temporal binding of cellular tRNAs to suppress promiscuous binding of Gag to internal membranes and spontaneous myristoyl exposure, thus allowing for specific PM recognition (20, 21). As the driver of membrane recognition and initial steps of membrane-associated virus assembly, we chose to first analyze the role of MA in CARD8 activation. We began our investigation by overexpressing caspase-1, pro–IL-1β, and single-round HIV-1 proviral constructs in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells by transient transfection. We analyzed inflammasome activation by measuring pro–IL-1β cleavage and IL-1β secretion by Western blot and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), respectively. Overexpression of wild-type (WT) HIV-1 in HEK293T cells, which leads to spontaneous dimerization of Gag-Pol, resulted in intracellular pro–IL-1β processing and IL-1β secretion (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S1A). Overexpression of a Rev-inactive virus (M10), which attenuates cytoplasmic Gag/Gag-Pol expression [Fig. 1A and (22)], or a myristoylation-deficient MA (G2A) virus mutant, which attenuates membrane-association of Gag (22), abrogated IL-1β secretion (Fig. 1A), consistent with a model where CARD8 is activated by viral PR generated during the process of de novo membrane-associated virus assembly under overexpression conditions. To analyze MA’s role in the regulation of viral PR activity during assembly, we used an MA mutant where all amino acids except the myristoylation signal and MA-CA PR cleavage site have been removed (ΔMA6–125) (22, 23). Notably, overexpression of ΔMA6–125 resulted in enhanced pro–IL-1β processing (Fig. 1C) and significant increase in IL-1β secretion (Fig. 1D and fig. S1B) compared to WT. This effect was dependent on viral PR, as a catalytically inactive PR mutation combined with the ΔMA6–125 mutation (ΔMA6–125pro-) showed a lack of Gag processing, pro–IL-1β cleavage, and IL-1β secretion (Fig. 1, C and D).

We then sought to confirm this effect in a model of HIV-1 infection. To this end, we differentiated monocytic THP-1 cells into macrophage-like cells by treatment with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (PMA/THP-1). We then coinfected PMA/THP-1 cells with single round [envelope (Env)-deficient], green fluorescent protein (GFP)–expressing, vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSVG)–pseudotyped HIV-1 and simian immunodeficiency virus viral protein X (Vpx)-containing virus-like particles [SIV3+ VLPs; to overcome SAM domain and HD domain-containing protein 1 (SAMHD1)-mediated restriction of HIV-1 in myeloid cells] (24). To normalize early postentry behavior between WT and mutant viruses, we produced all mutant viruses with WT HIV-1 Gag/Pol packaging plasmid. Three days postinfection (dpi), we observed a notable failure in survival of infected GFP+ cells in ΔMA6–125–infected cultures, which was rescued upon treatment with HIV-1 PR inhibitor saquinavir (SQV) or PR inactivation (Fig. 1E). Further, cells infected with ΔMA6–125 virus secreted high levels of IL-1β, which was not observed in WT-infected PMA/THP-1 cells (Fig. 1F). IL-1β secretion in ΔMA6–125 virus–infected PMA/THP-1 macrophages required HIV-1 PR activity, as it was completely abolished by SQV treatment or catalytic inactivation of PR (ΔMA6–125pro-). Inflammasome activation was also not dependent on the incoming ΔMA6–125 virus particle–associated PR, as IL-1β secretion was completely abrogated by treatment with HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT) inhibitor efavirenz (EFV) before the initiation of infection (Fig. 1F), which does not block entry of virus particles or the deposition of viral PR into the cytoplasm. Further, compared to WT-infected cells, ΔMA6–125–infected cells secreted higher levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Fig. 1G), indicating pyroptosis of infected cells and extracellular release of cytoplasmic contents. These experiments indicate that MA plays an important role in restraining PR activity and evasion from inflammasome activation during HIV-1 infection of macrophages.

Assembly-driven inflammasome activation is orchestrated by CARD8

The dependence on PR for inflammasome activation in ΔMA6–125–infected cells suggested that PR-sensing CARD8 was the inflammasome sensor responsible for the phenotype. To confirm this, we repeated overexpression experiments in HEK293T–CARD8 knockout (CARD8KO) cells (with the CARD8 open reading frame disrupted via CRISPR) and infection experiments with PMA/THP-1–short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting CARD8 (shCARD8) cells (where CARD8 expression was knocked down via transduction with shCARD8-expressing lentivector) (Fig. 2A). Notably, in comparison to parental HEK293T cells, pro–IL-1β cleavage and IL-1β secretion were abrogated in ΔMA6–125–transfected HEK293T-CARD8KO cells (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S2A). Similarly, in PMA/THP-1-shCARD8 cells, IL-1β (Fig. 2E) and LDH secretion (Fig. 2F) were abolished, and survival of GFP+ cells (Fig. 2D) was similar between the WT and ΔMA6–125 virus–infected cultures. These results confirm that CARD8 is the sensor activated during MA-deficient virus assembly in HEK293T cells and PMA/THP-1 macrophages.

Viral assembly is required for maximal CARD8 activation in MA-deficient virus infection

PR activation occurs during the process of viral assembly and maturation, when Gag-Pol monomers are recruited to the membrane-associated virus assembly site and PR domains of adjacent Gag-Pol molecules come in close proximity to dimerize and become enzymatically active. Hence, we sought to determine whether initiation of virus assembly was needed for CARD8 activation. To this end, we added additional mutations in the context of ΔMA6–125 that disrupt early steps of virus assembly: the myristoylation-deficient mutant (G2A) that prevents membrane association of Gag and a C-terminal CA (CA-CTD) mutation (VK181/2AA) that inactivates CA-CA dimerization needed for the formation of higher-order CA oligomers (22). We found that these mutations attenuated PR activity (evidenced by p24/p55Gag ratio) and significantly reduced pro–IL-1β cleavage and IL-1β secretion in ΔMA6–125–overexpressing HEK293T cells (Fig. 3, A to D, and fig. S3A) and IL-1β and LDH secretion in ΔMA6–125–infected PMA/THP-1 macrophages (Fig. 3, E to G), suggesting that the source of the majority of CARD8-activating viral PR in infected cells was the membrane-associated virus assembly site. To determine whether CARD8 activation required membrane-associated PR activity, we induced CARD8 activation via NNRTI [rilpivirine (RPV)] treatment in HIV-1/WT or G2A-infected PMA/THP-1 cells. Unexpectedly, we observed similar levels of IL-1β secretion and LDH release in both virus infections and a concomitant induction of Gag processing in G2A infection upon RPV treatment (fig. S3, B to D), suggesting that enzymatically active membrane-associated PR or cytosolic PR (upon NNRTI-induced activation) can be sensed by CARD8.

p6 modulates CARD8 sensing by preventing PR assembly site escape

Besides MA, NC contributes to immature virus CA assembly by promoting viral genomic RNA (gRNA) binding–dependent Gag multimerization (25). In addition, basic residues of NC can bind to acidic phospholipids in host membranes and, along with p6, can facilitate virus particle release by recruiting cellular ESCRT complexes for membrane scission (26, 27). We reasoned that gRNA recruitment by NC or timely membrane scission driven by NC and p6 could have roles in the initiation or shielding of PR activity, thus preventing CARD8 activation. To inactivate NC, we generated ΔNC15–49 by deleting the entirety of the two zinc finger domains (28), and to inactivate p6, we performed mutagenesis where both Tsg101/ESCRT-I–recruiting PTAP motifs in Lai-p6 were changed to the inert sequence LIRL (named ΔPTAP; Fig. 4A) (29). We also probed NC function with a mutant in which the entirety of NC sequence was replaced with a leucine zipper domain, which artificially enforces Gag-Gag binding (GagZip) (30). In PMA/THP-1 macrophages, infection with ΔPTAP mutant virus resulted in markedly enhanced IL-1β and LDH release, similar to that observed with ΔMA6–125 mutant virus infection (Fig. 4, B to D). In contrast, inflammasome activation was not observed in ΔNC15–49 or GagZip mutant virus infections. Further, knockdown of CARD8 or prevention of infection establishment abrogated IL-1β and LDH secretion in ΔPTAP virus–infected cells (Fig. 4, E to G). Last, we observed notable increases in pro–IL-1β cleavage in HEK293T cells overexpressing ΔPTAP mutant (Fig. 4H), which was abrogated by PR inhibitor lopinavir (LOP) treatment (Fig. 4I). In contrast, pro–IL-1β cleavage in HEK293T cells was nearly absent in both ΔNC15–49 and GagZip mutants (Fig. 4H). We additionally performed infection experiments with primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) and observed death of infected GFP+ cells (fig. S4A), IL-1β and LDH secretion (fig. S4, B and C) and cleavage of gasdermin D (fig. S4D) in ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP virus infections compared to WT. Further, upon infection of PMA/THP-1 macrophages with replication-competent HIV-1 (with all viral open reading frames intact), we observed similar increases in pyroptosis upon disruption of MA (ΔMA6–125) or p6 (ΔPTAP) (fig. S5A), which was readily observable even in the absence of Vpx-containing VLP addition (fig. S5B).

Because p6 is responsible for ESCRT recruitment and virus particle budding is roughly 10-fold slower in the absence of p6 (31), we hypothesized that CARD8 was being activated by autoprocessed PR that escaped the viral assembly site due to defects in timely budding in ΔPTAP virus–infected cells. Previous findings have suggested that ΔPTAP virus particles contain suboptimal levels of Pol if PR is intact (32), implying viral enzymatic activities escape from membrane-associated assembly sites to the host cell cytoplasm if budding kinetics are impaired. We additionally hypothesized that a similar defect in particle release or aberrant initiation of Gag-Pol autoprocessing could explain the role of MA in evading CARD8 activation. To address these hypotheses, we analyzed Gag/Gag-Pol processing in transfected HEK293T cells and their virus progeny via Western blot analysis. We first reasoned that analyzing the successful complete processing of p25Gag to p24Gag in virus particles, as one of the final PR cleavage events occurring in virus particle maturation after budding, could be indicative of the quantity of PR being retained in the assembling virus particle as opposed to escaping to the host cell cytoplasm. WT, ΔMA6–125, and ΔPTAP virus–transfected cell lysates all contained a mixture of unprocessed p25Gag and p24Gag, as expected in a bulk population under overexpression conditions (Fig. 4J). In virus particles, however, ΔPTAP was the only mutant in which a significant portion of p25Gag remained unprocessed, consistent with the hypothesis that active PR (after autoprocessing of Gag-Pol) escapes from the assembly site due to delays in virus particle budding during ΔPTAP virus assembly. While we observed a third p24Gag-like band in ΔMA6–125–transfected virus pellets, that band was also present in ΔMA6–125–transfected cell lysates (Fig. 4J), which we hypothesize likely reflects a failure to process the ΔMA6–125/CA cleavage site, as HIV-1 PR is notably dependent on sequence context for cleavage site recognition (33, 34). When we probed virus pellets with anti–HIV–immunoglobulin (Ig), however, we noticed a loss of p66RT in both ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP virus particles (Fig. 4, J and K), corroborating our hypothesis that there was poor retention of active viral enzymes in both ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP virus assembly. In both ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP transfections, we observed a concomitant increase in processed p66RT in cell lysates, in contrast to virus pellets (Fig. 4, L and M). Last, in ΔMA6–125–transfected cell lysates, we observed a decrease of full-length unprocessed p160Gag-pol, most likely indicating aberrant initiation of cytoplasmic processing of Gag-Pol (Fig. 4N). Together, these results indicate that both MA and p6 regulate CARD8 sensing of intracellular PR activity during viral assembly in HEK293T cells and macrophages.

Gag regulates PR-induced CARD8 activation in primary CD4+ T cells

After determining that MA and p6 orchestrated viral assembly to conceal PR activity in infected myeloid and transfected HEK293T cells, we next determined the effects of Gag assembly mutants on inflammasome activation in primary CD4+ T cells. To this end, we infected activated primary CD4+ T cells with VSVG-pseudotyped, GFP-expressing, single-round viruses and probed CARD8 activity by monitoring survival of infected CD4+ T cells in the presence or absence of the PR inhibitor LOP. Control experiments showed that with WT infection, LOP treatment did not affect survival of infected GFP+ cells. In contrast, death of infected cells (measured by comparative loss of GFP positivity) could be robustly induced by treating WT-infected cells with RPV, and RPV-mediated cytotoxicity could be prevented by pretreatment with LOP (Fig. 5A). To further confirm the activity of CARD8 in activated primary CD4+ T cells, we also treated activated, uninfected CD4+ T cells with the CARD8-activating ligand Val-boroPro (VbP) and observed LDH release (fig. To quantify PR-mediated cytotoxicity of Gag-mutant viruses, we calculated a percentage of killing value comparing GFP positivity in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)–treated cultures to LOP-treated cultures (Fig. 5B and fig. S6, B and C). As expected, infection of activated CD4+ T cells with ΔMA6–125 or ΔPTAP viruses resulted in a significant increase in death of infected GFP+ cells relative to the LOP-treated control (Fig. 5C and fig. S6C), similar in magnitude to that observed upon RPV treatment of WT-infected CD4+ T cells. No death of GFP+ cells was observed whether the ΔMA6–125 virus also carried the inactivating PR mutation, confirming that the cytotoxicity observed was not due to PR inhibitor treatment. Unexpectedly, in contrast to myeloid cells, we now observed modest PR-mediated cell death upon infection with the ΔNC15–49 mutant virus. This NC-modulated cell death was prevented in GagZip mutant virus infection, highlighting the important interplay between successful virus assembly and release and evasion of CARD8 sensing in CD4+ T cells. We additionally confirmed that the GagZip virus was competent for PR activity, since addition of RPV postinfection establishment promoted efficient IL-1β and LDH release in GagZip-infected PMA/THP-1 macrophages (fig. S6D).

To confirm that CARD8-initiated pyroptosis was the modality of GFP+ CD4+ T cell death observed in LOP survival experiments, we stained infected cell cultures with caspase-1–specific FLICA dye and observed significantly increased FLICA staining in GFP+ ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP virus–infected CD4+ T cells relative to WT virus–infected CD4+ T cells, which was prevented by LOP treatment (Fig. 5D). We also generated edited primary CD4+ T cells via CRISPR-Cas9 nucleofection using CARD8-targeting single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) or scramble sgRNA (Fig. 5E) and, in LOP survival experiments, observed a decrease in PR-driven loss of GFP+ cells in CARD8KO CD4+ T cells compared to scramble CD4+ T cells upon infection with ΔMA6–125 or ΔPTAP virus (Fig. 5F and fig. S6E). In total, these results indicate that infected CD4+ T cells are susceptible to CARD8-mediated pyroptotic cell death if virus assembly is dysregulated.

Previously documented host-adaptive mutations in MA and p6 may prevent pyroptosis of infected cells

Since structural mutants of Gag activate CARD8 via dysregulation of PR activation at the viral assembly site, it is likely that HIV-1 assembly is subject to innate immune pressure in human cells. Hence, we hypothesized that polymorphisms in Gag sequences might affect viral assembly and therefore modulate the ability of HIV-1 to evade CARD8 activation. For instance, near-universal adaptations in MA occurred during zoonotic transformation of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIVcpz) to non–clade C HIV-1 isolates, which presumably occurred concurrently with the encounter of CARD8 immune pressure in human cells (35). While chimpanzees encode a functional CARD8 sensor, chimpanzee CARD8-N does not contain a cleavage site efficiently targeted by HIV-1 or SIVcpz PR (36), and, therefore, Gag-mediated regulation of PR during viral assembly in chimpanzee cells may have less restrictive innate immune pressures compared to assembly in human cells. In contrast, human CARD8-N is efficiently cleaved by both HIV-1 and SIVcpz PR. We therefore hypothesized that adaptations in HIV-1 MA compared to SIVcpz may have arisen to combat innate immune pressure targeting virus assembly in human cells. To test this hypothesis, we used a chimeric HIV-1 where only the HIV-1 MA sequence was replaced with SIVcpz MA (CPZ-MA) (Fig. 6A) (22). When we tested PR-driven cytotoxicity of this chimera in activated primary CD4+ T cells, we observed significantly greater PR-driven cytotoxicity (Fig. 6B and fig. S7A). To probe this effect more closely at the single residue level, we mutated the K30 residue of Lai-MA to the chimpanzee-ancestral M (K30M), as this site is among the most universally conserved human-adaptive mutations that occurred during zoonosis, and M is the most common ancestral residue in SIVcpz (37). We observed a significant increase in PR-induced cytotoxicity with K30M virus infection (Fig. 6C and fig. S7B), although we did not have sufficient statistical power to establish a definitive link with CARD8 (fig. S7D). Together, these results suggest that at least part of the function of this near-universal adaptation in HIV-1 MA could be to prevent CARD8 activation during viral assembly in human cells, although more definitive evidence is still needed.

We next turned our attention to human-adapted variants of HIV-1 p6. Multiple studies have shown that duplication of the Tsg101-recruiting PTAP motif in p6 confers faster virus budding and a replicative advantage in primary isolates of HIV-1 (38–40). The laboratory-adapted Lai clone used in these studies contains two PTAP motifs, possibly attained after serial passage in human primary cell cultures vulnerable to CARD8 activation. Since disruption of both Lai PTAP motifs resulted in a marked enhancement of CARD8 activation during viral assembly (Fig. 4), we hypothesized that PTAP motif duplication could have possibly arisen in part to modulate CARD8 sensing during viral assembly. To address this question, we generated a single-PTAP mutant by mutating only one of the PTAP motifs in HIV-1/Lai to inert LIRL sequence (1×PTAP) and hypothesized that it would have a greater CARD8 activation phenotype compared to the WT virus containing two PTAP motifs (2×PTAP) (Fig. 6A). When we infected PMA/THP-1 macrophages with 1×PTAP virus, we observed IL-1β and LDH secretion levels greater than WT (2×PTAP) virus infection (Fig. 6, D to F). Further, when we tested PR-induced cell death in primary activated CD4+ T cells, we found significantly increased PR-driven cell killing in 1×PTAP infection compared to WT (2×PTAP) virus infection (Fig. 6G and fig. S7C). In addition, we analyzed the extent of PR-driven killing between the common HIV-1 laboratory isolates Lai (here called WT) and NL4-3, as one of the few notable differences between the Gag sequences of these two closely related isolates is the duplication of the PTAP motif in Lai p6 (fig. S8A). Consistent with our result with the 1×PTAP Lai mutant, we noted a significant enhancement in PR-driven killing in single-PTAP NL4-3 infection compared to WT Lai (2×PTAP) (fig. S8B). These results suggest that in addition to MA, the p6 region of Gag may also be subject to selective pressure to improve survival of infected cells.

DISCUSSION

In this report, we demonstrate that Gag-mediated virus assembly provides spatial and temporal control of PR activation until the final membrane scission step to prevent CARD8 sensing during productive HIV-1 infection. In the case of MA, we hypothesize a model that MA selects not only the PM but also selects specific lipid microdomains within the PM for proper initiation of Gag-Pol autoprocessing and viral assembly. Notably, we observed enhanced autoprocessing of Gag-Pol and a concomitant increase in processed p66RT in the cytoplasm of ΔMA6–125–transfected HEK293T cells, indicating abnormal initiation of PR activity. This model is supported by findings demonstrating that HIV-1 buds selectively from sphingolipid-enriched lipid microdomains (indicating that MA localizes viral assembly to specific lipid environments) (41) and also by findings indicating that inactivation of PM lipid–modifying enzymes or mutations in the membrane-interacting HBR have marked effects on Gag processing (42–44), implying a cross-talk between MA-engaged lipids and PR activity. We speculate that membrane fluidity determined by local lipid microenvironment is altered during virus assembly in the absence of MA HBR or in the presence of HBR mutations, which might increase the probability of Gag-Pol monomer interactions resulting in enhanced propensity for dimerization and initiation of PR activity and maturation.

In contrast, PTAP-deficient viral assembly proceeds past the point where active PR is generated but is halted before the final budding step, which evidently must complete within a short time frame to avoid escape of catalytically active PR from the assembly site and CARD8 activation. This finding is consistent with previous reports relating mutations in p6 to a reduced retention of viral enzymes during assembly (31, 32). Separately, it is intriguing that NC has a divergent phenotype in macrophages and CD4+ T cells in terms of CARD8 evasion. However, HIV-1 NC has been previously reported to contain an ALIX-binding motif (29), and Gag processing has been shown to be dysregulated in the absence of NC, implicating NC as a regulator of PR activity and budding (45). NC’s role in CARD8 evasion in T cells specifically might reflect differences in budding mechanisms between myeloid and lymphoid cells infected with HIV-1, as virus assembly is known to occur at different membrane sites between these cell types (46).

To become pandemic in the human population, HIV-1 had to simultaneously antagonize multiple mechanisms of restriction between great apes and humans. Perhaps expectedly, then, most zoonotic transmission events of SIVcpz or SIVgor from great apes to humans did not result in the establishment of widely successful HIV-1 lineages. This work reveals that, in addition to the previously described species-specific restrictions, CARD8 activation by assembly site–generated PR could theoretically be an additional layer of restriction between chimpanzees and humans, although additional investigation and validation are needed. Effect sizes reported here for human-adapted motifs are modest and would likely only significantly affect viral replication over multiple rounds of viral infection. This could separate CARD8-mediated restriction from other previously established restriction factors [such as tetherin, SAMHD1, tripartite motif-containing protein 5 (TRIM5α), and APOBECs], which require direct viral antagonism for completion of even one round of lentiviral replication. Recent research has shown that chimpanzee CARD8 is not sensitive to lentiviral PR cleavage (36), implying that CARD8 evasion may have been an innate immune barrier encountered by zoonotic SIVcpz immediately at the cross-species transmission event to humans. Our CPZ-MA chimera is based on the SIVcpz isolate TAN2, which was sampled from Pan troglodytes schweinfurthii (P.t.s.) in Tanzania, not from Pan troglodytes troglodytes (P.t.t.) in central Africa, which are believed to be the hosts of the direct ancestors of HIV-1 (47). The presence of innate immune–activating motifs in P.t.s. SIVcpz viruses (namely, the C terminus of SIVcpz P.t.s. MA, which is about 20 amino acids longer than the C terminus of HIV-1 and SIVcpz P.t.t. MA) could partially explain why only viruses descended from central African P.t.t. achieved pandemicity in the human population.

There are some limitations to the model presented here that must be acknowledged. Most of the results presented here were obtained using artificial systems (HEK293T overexpression, VSVG-pseudotyped viruses for single-cycle infection analysis, and coinfection with SIV3+ VLPs in myeloid cells). We were able to recapitulate many, but not all, effects in primary MDMs and CD4+ T cells. For instance, we were unable to establish a definitive link between relatively minor changes in GFP positivity of chimpanzee motif (MA/M30)–infected CD4+ T cells and human motif (MA/K30)–infected CD4+ T cells and CARD8 functionality. Further, we were unable to ascertain a difference in the activation of pyroptotic markers, such as caspase-1 activity or gasdermin D cleavage in cells infected with chimpanzee motif viruses. Therefore, additional investigation is required to definitively link differences in cell survival upon infection with chimpanzee motif viruses in the presence or absence of PR inhibition to CARD8 activation.

This work also has important implications for drug discovery and therapeutic approaches to taming the HIV-1 reservoir in antiretroviral therapy (ART)-suppressed people with HIV. NNRTIs have recently garnered interest for drug-induced killing of HIV-infected cells via CARD8 activation. Our results show that MA and p6 could be attractive targets to either enhance NNRTI-driven cell killing or induce CARD8 activation themselves. Therefore, drug development strategies should consider modulation of virus assembly to achieve maximum CARD8-mediated elimination of infected cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Viruses, plasmids, and cloning

To make a pro–IL-1β expression vector, the pro–IL-1β coding sequence was excised from pLV–mTurqoise2–IL-1β–mNeonGreen plasmid (Addgene, catalog no. 166783) via Xho I/Bam HI restriction sites and introduced into pcDNA3.1. The pCI–caspase-1 expression plasmid was also received from Addgene (catalog no. 41552). For transfection experiments, the “empty” vector transfected was a pCS2 plasmid encoding enhanced yellow fluorescent protein. The single-round proviral molecular clones LaiΔenvGFP (here called “WT”) and the MA mutant LaiΔMA6–125ΔenvGFP with inactivating deletions in env and GFP in place of nef have been described previously (22). NL4-3ΔenvGFP has been described previously [received from National Institutes of Health (NIH)/National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) HIV Reagent Program, catalog no. ARP-11100]. The M10 and G2A mutations on the Lai backbone were previously reported (22). The Lai pro- (in which residues 25, 49, and 50 are mutated from D/G/I to K/W/W) and Lai VK181/2AA (CA-CTD dimer interface mutant) clones were generous gifts from J. Lingappa at the University of Washington (22). The pro- mutation was swapped into the LaiΔMA6–125ΔenvGFP plasmid via Apa I/Sal I. The VK181/2AA CA mutation was swapped into the LaiΔMA6–125ΔenvGFP plasmid via Age I/Apa I. To make a CARD8-targeting shRNA lentivector, CARD8 shRNA was excised from a construct purchased from MilliporeSigma (catalog no. TRCN0000118329, target sequence GCACAAACAGATACAGCGTTT) and introduced into a hygromycin-resistant pLKO.1 vector via Spe I/Mfe I. To generate G2A-ΔMA6–125, ΔNC15–49, and ΔPTAP, a Gibson-based strategy was used. First, two fragments were generated from LaiΔenvGFP using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, New England Biolabs, catalog no. M0530) with primers 1 to 12 included in table S1. N- and C-terminal fragments of the correct size were gel purified. Then, Gibson assembly was performed using the two amplified fragments (Gibson Assembly Cloning Kit, New England Biolabs, catalog no. E5510S). One microliter of the Gibson assembly reaction was then used as template in a final PCR reaction using the furthest 5′ and 3′ primers, with final fragments of the correct size gel purified, restriction digested, and ligated into LaiΔenvGFP (G2A/ΔMA6–125, BssH II/Age I; ΔNC15–49, Age I/Pas I; ΔPTAP, Apa I/Pas I). To inactivate PTAP motifs, PTAP (CCAACAGCCCCA) was mutated to the inert sequence LIRL (CTAATACGACTA). ΔNC15–49 was generated by deleting residues 15 to 49 of NC (inclusive), from the beginning of the first zinc finger to the end of the second. Lai-GagZip was a generous gift from J. Lingappa, which was ligated into LaiΔenvGFP via Age I/Sal I (30). To produce macrophage-tropic, replication-competent HIV-1 proviral constructs, Apa I/Sal I fragments containing the ΔMA6–125 and ΔPTAP mutations were introduced into the Lai/YU-2env(vpu+) plasmid described previously (48, 49). To make the K30M mutation in LaiΔenvGFP, the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, catalog no. 200523) was used using primers in table S1. To make LaiΔenvGFP/1×PTAP, a DNA fragment was purchased from Genewiz-Azenta with the second PTAP in Lai-p6 mutated to LIRL, which was digested and ligated into LaiΔenvGFP via Apa I/Bcl I. The CPZ-MA plasmid was described previously (22). All plasmid sequences were confirmed via Sanger sequencing (Genewiz-Azenta) and/or restriction digestion before use.

All viruses were produced via calcium phosphate–mediated double/triple transfection of HEK293T cells in dishes, followed by ultracentrifugation over a 20% sucrose cushion, extensively described elsewhere (22). To generate single-round or replication-competent VSVG-pseudotyped viruses, all mutant viral plasmids [except LaiΔenvGFP or Lai/YU-2env(vpu+)] were cotransfected with an HIV-1 packaging plasmid (psPAX2) and a VSVG-encoding expression vector. Lentivectors for cell line generation were produced via triple transfection. Vpx-containing SIV3+ particles were obtained by double transfection of SIV3+ plasmid and VSVG, which were titered for CA content using p27 ELISA (XpressBio, catalog no. SK845) before use. The VSVG, SIV3+, and psPAX2 plasmids have been described before (22, 24).

Isolation and differentiation of primary cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from deidentified leukopaks obtained from New York Biologics, as described previously (22). Primary CD4+ T cells were obtained either from whole PBMCs or CD14− flowthrough and positively isolated using anti-CD4 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotech, catalog no. 130-045-101). CD4+ T cells were stimulated in R10 medium with 2% phytohemagglutinin (PHA; Gibco, catalog no. 10576015) and IL-2 (50 U/ml; NIH/NIAID HIV Reagent Program, catalog no. 136) for 2 days before washing in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and resuspension in R10 medium with IL-2 (50 U/ml). Primary CD14+ cells were obtained from PBMCs by positive isolation using anti-CD14 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotech, catalog no. 130-050-201) for differentiation into MDMs. CD14+ cells were seeded in ultralow attachment six-well plates and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. H4522) and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (20 ng/ml; PeproTech, catalog no. 300-25) before lifting and counting for infection experiments.

Infections

THP-1 cells in suspension were differentiated into PMA/THP-1 macrophage-like cells by treatment with PMA (100 nM; Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. P8139) for 2 days before infection. PMA/THP-1 cells were seeded at 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates (or MDMs were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well) for infection and coinfected with single-round or replication-competent VSVG-pseudotyped HIV-1 with or without 5 ng of p27 Vpx-containing SIV3+ VLPs in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/ml; MilliporeSigma, catalog no. TR1003G) by spinoculation at 2300 rpm for 1 hour at room temperature before incubation for 2 to 3 hours at 37°C. After the incubation, cells were washed with DPBS to remove unbound viral particles, and 500 μl of fresh R10 medium was added supplemented with tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα) (10 ng/ml; PeproTech, catalog no. 300-01A). For MDMs, R10 without TNFα was replenished. Cells were harvested at 3 dpi. Supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation (1200 rpm for 5 min) before analysis, and cells were harvested for radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis or flow cytometry.

Activated primary CD4+ T cells (including CRISPR-edited CD4+ T cells) were infected in the presence of polybrene in 0.5 ml of R10 medium in 24-well plates at 2 × 106 cells per well. As with macrophages, spinoculation was performed for 1 hour at room temperature before incubation for 2 to 3 hours at 37°C. Infected cells were then thoroughly washed with DPBS, before cultures were split in two, and 1 × 106 cells were cultured in 300 μl of DMSO- or LOP-containing R10/IL-2 medium in flat-bottom 96-well plates. T cells were harvested for flow cytometry analysis at 3 dpi or FLICA analysis at 2 dpi. The percentage of killing in primary CD4+ T cell experiments was calculated using the formula%killing=1−%GFP(DMSO)%GFP(LOP)All infections were performed at an MOI of 1 based on TZM-bl titer.

CRISPR editing of primary CD4+ T cells

Edited primary CD4+ T cells were generated by CRISPR-Cas9 nucleofection similarly to previous descriptions (50, 51). Freshly isolated CD4+ T cells were cultured overnight in R10 medium supplemented with IL-7 (2 ng/ml; PeproTech, catalog no. AF-200-07) and IL-15 (2 ng/ml; PeproTech, catalog no. AF-200-15) before nucleofection with 100 pmol each of two CARD8-targeting sgRNAs (UGACGAUUGCGUUUGGUUCC and AGCGUUUGGUUCCCCACUGC, Synthego) or 200 pmol of scramble sgRNA (Synthego, Negative Control SpCas9 gRNA #1) incubated for 10 min with 80 pmol of Cas9 (IDT, catalog no. 1081059), using program EH100 on a 4D-Nucleofector System (Lonza). Cells were rescued in 100 μl of warm RPMI 1640 and rested at 37°C for 1 hour before stimulation with anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Gibco, catalog no. 11131D) and culture in R10 medium supplemented with IL-7, IL-15, IL-2 (50 U/ml), and 2.5% human AB serum. After 2 to 3 days, beads were removed, and cells were cultured in medium with only IL-2 until use in infection experiments. Alternatively, CD4+ T cells were periodically restimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads or 2% PHA as needed for cell expansion.

HEK293T transfection

To analyze IL-1β cleavage and secretion in situ, HEK293T cells were transfected using TransIT-293 transfection reagent (Mirus Bio, catalog no. MIR 2704). A total of 2.5 × 105 HEK293T cells per well were seeded in 1 ml of D10 medium in 12-well plates. The next day, cells were transfected according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each well was transfected with 5 ng of plasmid encoding caspase-1, 200 ng of plasmid encoding pro–IL-1β, and 200 ng of control plasmid or HIV-1 construct. Cells were harvested roughly 24 hours posttransfection. Supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation. Cell lysates were harvested for Western blot analysis by washing with DPBS before lysis in RIPA buffer. To transfect HEK293T cells for virus assembly analysis, one 10-cm dish was transfected with 10 μg of Δenv proviral plasmid alone. Cells were lysed 48 hours posttransfection, and viruses were pelleted in an ultracentrifuge using sucrose as described above before lysis in RIPA buffer (150 μl).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Human IL-1β in clarified culture supernatants was quantified using the IL-1β DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems, catalog no. DY201) according to the manufacturer’s protocol after inactivation in DPBS buffer with 10% normal calf serum (Invitrogen, catalog no. 26170043) and 0.5% Triton X-100 detergent. HIV-1 p24Gag levels were quantified using in-house ELISA as previously described after a similar inactivation step (22).

FLICA staining

To measure active caspase-1 in virus-infected primary CD4+ T cells, FLICA staining (FLICA 660 caspase-1 kit, ImmunoChemistry Technologies, catalog no. NC0885715) was used. CD4+ T cells were infected as described above. At 2 dpi, 5 × 105 cells were stained with FLICA following the manufacturer’s instructions (using 1:60 FLICA dilution) in 300 μl of R10 medium at 37°C for 90 min with resuspension every 20 to 30 min. Stained cells were washed three times with the included wash buffer and fixed with the included fixative, followed immediately by flow cytometry.

LDH release assay

LDH release into culture supernatants was quantified using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, catalog no. PR-G1780) according to the manufacturer’s instructions after inactivation in DPBS with 0.5% Triton X-100. LDH assays were stopped with Stop Solution after 15 to 30 min of development and read at 490 nm.

Western blot analysis

To perform quantitative Western blot analysis, 30 μg of protein (or 10 μl of virus lysate) was normalized to the same volume in DPBS/loading buffer before boiling and loading into Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad, 10%: catalog no. 4561034 or 4 to 20% gradient: catalog no. 4561094) along with a protein ladder (PageRuler Prestained NIR Protein Ladder, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 26635). To resolve small proteins for analysis of p25/p24Gag levels, a 12.5% acrylamide hand-poured gel was used. Blots were blocked in LI-COR Intercept blocking buffer (LI-COR, catalog no. NC1660556) before membrane staining and imaging on a LI-COR Odyssey CLx imager.

Flow cytometry

Cells to be analyzed by flow cytometry were first washed with DPBS before fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 to 30 min. To analyze cells for intracellular p24Gag, cells were fixed with Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences, catalog no. 554722) before 30 min of antibody staining and washing in 1× Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences, catalog no. 552723). Cells were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora spectral analyzer. Data were analyzed and plots were created in FlowJo 10.8.1 software.

Alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Amino acid alignments were generated using Clustal Omega (European Bioinformatics Institute), and alignment was visualized using Jalview (52).
