Shared decision-making process and its influencing factors among patients undergoing kidney replacement therapy: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Youying Zhang, Hongmei Peng, Yang Li, Shi Pu, Xia Huang, Yu Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with end‑stage kidney disease (ESKD) often struggle to choose among kidney replacement therapy options due to information ambiguity and the complexity of treatment pathways. In nursing, comprehensive, person-centered care has become more popular, placing a strong emphasis on advocacy and the defense of patients’ rights. Discussions about the value of patient-physician collaboration in decision-making, patient involvement strategies, and the nuances of decision-making processes have resulted from this. Currently, there is a steady increase in research on dialysis modality decision-making for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). The qualitative data from these interviews underwent an inductive thematic analysis to identify underlying themes and patterns. Introduction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) refers to the final phase of irreversible deterioration in kidney function resulting from several kidney disorders [1]. Patients with ESKD face a series of sequential treatment decisions. Once kidney replacement therapy is initiated, patients must choose a dialysis modality (e.g., hemodialysis vs. peritoneal dialysis) and may later evaluate eligibility for kidney transplantation. Treatment selection significantly affects the patient’s quality of life, ability to work, and social interactions, and various treatment methods could impact and shape the patient’s future lifestyle and work habits [2]. As a result, the subject of dialysis modality decision-making in individuals suffering from ESKD has garnered growing interest.

Previous studies have shown that most patients with ESKD struggle with making decisions when faced with many choices for kidney replacement therapy, particularly due to complex therapy alternatives, individualized patient factors, and uncertainty about outcomes [3]. A qualitative study showed that patients experienced a process from avoidance to forced acceptance and then active participation in action decision-making during dialysis treatment [4]. The study summarized the facilitating factors for decision-making, including family accompaniment and the listening of healthcare providers, and demonstrated the importance of shared decision-making in the treatment process of dialysis patients. An Australian study analyzed the clinical interactions between elderly patients and nephrology healthcare providers, confirmed that effective interpersonal communication was crucial for shared decision-making, proposed initiatives to advance shared decision-making, and emphasized that healthcare providers, patients, and their family members should jointly participate in decision-making discussions and formulate treatment plans [5]. Another qualitative study summarized the challenges faced by advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) treatment in shared decision-making [6], including discrepancies between patients and clinicians regarding dialysis regimens, differences in focus between patients and doctors on dialysis prognosis, disparities in professional cognition of dialysis treatment, and inequality between patients and doctors in the therapeutic relationship.

Qualitative studies in this field, such as dialysis modality decision-making [7, 8] and its cultural influences [9, 10], highlight patients’ unmet informational needs and the tension between clinician recommendations and personal values. However, these studies predominantly focus on Western populations and static decision points rather than the dynamic, iterative nature of decision-making in real-world clinical pathways. Comprehensive, person-centered care has gained prominence in nursing, emphasizing the protection of patient rights and advocacy. These have sparked debates on the importance of collaborative decision-making between medical professionals and patients, methods for patient involvement, and the intricacies of decision-making procedures [11]. At present, there has been a progressive rise in research dedicated to kidney replacement therapy decision-making for individuals suffering from ESKD. Nevertheless, this research still needs more thorough context-specific conceptual frameworks to assist. The kidney replacement therapy decision-making for ESKD is a an iterative, context-adapted process that is constantly adjusted and influenced by the patient’s disease progression, family relationships, material resources, and limitations in doctor-patient communication. This underscores the need for a perspective aligned with shared decision-making principles, which emphasize dynamic collaboration between patients and clinicians [12].

To our knowledge, limited research has focused on patients’ treatment experiences regarding kidney replacement therapy. Most studies have focused on the treatment compliance experience of patients with hemodialysis [13] or the psychological experience of patients with kidney transplant [10]. A qualitative descriptive study can explore clinical phenomena when little is known about a field, allowing participants to interpret their lived experiences. Our aim is to observe and understand participants’ life experiences and explore influencing factors relevant to real-life situations among patients with ESKD. In conclusion, this study used a descriptive qualitative approach to examine the kidney replacement therapy decision-making process among patients with ESKD in the nephrology department of a tertiary hospital in China, contributing to a broader shared decision-making paradigm.

Methods

Study design

The study was designed as a qualitative descriptive study [14] with individual interviews, which aimed to investigate the elements and mechanisms that impact kidney replacement therapy decision-making in these patients and suggest strategies to improve decision-making in clinical settings. This study was prepared in line with the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist provided by the EQUATOR network [15].

Setting

This study was conducted in the nephrology outpatient clinic of a tertiary hospital in Chongqing, China. The clinic provides comprehensive kidney replacement therapy options, including hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD). A multidisciplinary team (nephrologists, dialysis nurses, social workers) oversees patient care, with a structured pre-dialysis education program that includes modality selection counseling, nutritional guidance, and psychosocial support.

Participants

The study employed a purposive sample strategy to choose patients with end-stage kidney disease who were admitted to the nephrology department from October to December 2020. The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) be diagnosed with end-stage kidney disease and with a glomerular filtration rate of less than 15 ml/min/1.73 m2; (3) no kidney replacement therapy history; (4) Proficient in Mandarin to ensure effective communication and comprehension of interview questions. Exclusion criteria include individuals with significant cognitive or communicative impairments and those with other severe medical conditions.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted face-to-face, guided by a framework adapted from prior qualitative studies on ESKD decision-making [9, 10, 12]. To ensure participants reflected solely on pre-treatment decision-making, interviews occurred before dialysis or transplantation initiation. Pre-interviews were conducted to gain an understanding of the circumstances of the two patients with ESKD. Final interview outlines were then established after seeking advice from relevant specialists [16]. The interview questions predominantly focused on four aspects: treatment experience, modality preferences, information sources, decision influences (see details in Appendix 1: Interview Guide).

The interviews took place in an office in the CKD Management Centre of the Department of Nephrology. Before each interview, participants provided written consent and demographic information. Interviews were conducted in Mandarin and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Two researchers with extensive coursework and training acted as interviewers. Before the interview, the patient’s identity was anonymous by assigning a numerical identifier. Consent was routinely sought and recorded throughout the interview process. During the interview, one interviewer conversed with the patient while the other attentively observed and recorded the patient’s nonverbal clues and gestures. The interviewers actively and attentively interacted with the respondents, carefully attending to their thoughts and emotions. They utilized acknowledgment, repetition, encouragement, and relevant follow-up questions to facilitate comprehensive and in-depth interviews. Concurrently, notes were swiftly generated to ensure the comprehensiveness and fairness of the material. Each interview had an average duration of around 30 min. The interview was developed for this study and has not been published elsewhere. See Appendix 2 for Patient Interview Record.

Data analysis

The audio recordings and transcribed non-verbal behavior were converted into text and meticulously reviewed for semantic and conceptual accuracy within 24 h after the interviews. The interview data were organized and analyzed using NVIVO 12.0 software. Data analysis was conducted by using inductive thematic analysis [17]. Data were compiled, disassembled, and reassembled, following a multistage recursive coding process. These coding levels will be utilized to systematically extract concepts and process mechanisms.

First, the first and second authors independently coded all the transcripts, naming and categorizing phenomena, which formed the basis for further coding. This process was repeated with additional transcripts, refining and reducing categories by grouping codes, and aimed to uncover relationships among initial categories. The remaining transcripts remained open to new additions if needed. Two transcripts and some primary extracts exemplifying the sub-themes were translated into English by the first author and verified by the second author to facilitate theme and sub-theme formation. All authors repeatedly read and analyzed these translated interviews to deepen their understanding. If consensus could not be reached, the team sought advice from relevant specialists until agreement on the codes and themes was achieved. The research team conducted iterative coding sessions using constant comparative analysis. Weekly meetings over three months ensured rigorous theme development, with disagreements resolved through consensus-building discussions. For example, initial debates about whether ‘familial pressure’ constituted a standalone theme or a subtheme of ‘social factors’ were resolved by revisiting raw data and refining definitions. This process aligns with Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework [17], which emphasizes collaborative refinement to enhance analytic credibility. Authors maintained reflexivity through bracketing pre-existing assumptions and documenting biases in a reflexivity journal [17].

Ethical considerations

The research process followed the principles of voluntariness, secrecy, and ethical criteria, including adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. The initiative was approved by the Ethics Review Committee (No. 2019-067-01). The study followed ethical guidelines, including obtaining informed consent, maintaining confidentiality, and demonstrating respect for the autonomy and privacy of participants. Participants were allowed to refuse to answer questions or discontinue their interview participation. The data was securely maintained in an anonymized and encrypted format and was finally deleted once the study was completed.

Shared decision-making process and its influencing factors for patients undergoing kidney replacement

Based on the coding results (Table 2), we propose a preliminary conceptual framework for shared decision-making in patients undergoing kidney replacement (Fig. 1). The narrative of this framework can be briefly summarized as follows: (1) The precondition: symptom perception (physical and psychological perceptions); (2) The dilemma: information and communication (information dilemma and communication dilemma); (3) The outcome: decision-making behavior (personal, family, social, and therapeutic factors). Patients should thoroughly evaluate these matters and make the optimal choices. By examining the primary categories and narrative threads discussed earlier, we can comprehend how end-stage kidney disease patients choose their treatment. Furthermore, we can explore the influence and interrelationships among many factors pertinent to this decision-making process.

The precondition: symptom perception

Symptom perception is a prerequisite for end-stage kidney disease patients’ decision-making, including physical and psychological perception.

Physical perception

Patients reported that the physical symptoms were elusive and showed no overt signs of discomfort. They affirmed their excellent health, with no medical symptoms, and expressed a sense of physical well-being and the ability to consume food and beverages without abnormalities: “I did not experience any noticeable physical discomfort, as the symptoms were quite concealed. I was uncertain about the origin of my urinary tract infection” (P2). According to some patients, the experience was similar to having a cold. However, some patients reported that they suffered from pain, specifically nocturia, and edema: “I noticed that my ankle joint was slightly swollen from my socks, and it was tough to climb up and down the stairs, feeling a bit breathless. I also urinate frequently and frequently at night” (P7). Patients might feel easily tired and nauseous: “But now I don’t feel like working anymore; I feel exhausted. When I ride in the car, I feel nauseous from the beginning, lack strength, and it’s not as normal as before” (P11). Younger patients (< 50 years) initially minimized symptoms, delaying decisions until acute crises (e.g., hyperkalemia) forced action: “In just one year, the pace of progress was too fast, and I didn’t expect it to be so fast myself” (P8).

Psychological perception

Patients exhibited profound psychological distress, shaped by the irreversible trajectory of their illness. Three distinct emotional patterns emerged. Some feels ambivalence and fear. The diagnosis triggered existential uncertainty, with patients oscillating between resistance and dread: “I feel perplexed, and my heart is slightly resistive. I have already had stomach and hemodialysis, and my heart still resists; hence, I am incredibly terrified, not because of any physical discomfort (P9).” Disease progression exacerbated psychological fragility, with patients reporting abrupt mood shifts and emotional volatility: “About half a month ago, my feelings were particularly volatile, and I couldn’t control them. Sometimes I felt sad and inexplicable” (P12). A subset of patients adopted pragmatic coping strategies, prioritizing immediate action over emotional reflection: “We must address the immediate issue, provide treatment to the fullest extent possible, and strive for a good quality of life” (P7). “Since you’ve been sick, there’s no way you can say anything more. You have to face it correctly, without considering how to manage the follow-up (P8).”

The dilemma: information and communication

Patients often faced difficulties in decision-making due to insufficient information and communication barriers, which can lead to decision-making difficulties.

Information dilemma

Patients faced significant uncertainty in navigating treatment options due to fragmented or conflicting information. “I only know a rough idea now, not very familiar, not very detailed” (P12) underscored widespread gaps in understanding dialysis modalities and outcomes. While some sought information proactively, their sources were often unreliable: “I occasionally learned some things from my patients’ mouths, but I didn’t go professional to learn about those kidney-related things” (P9). Patients also obtained information related to disease treatment through various channels, including the Internet, medical personnel, patients, and family members: “At that time, there was an online forum called ‘Kidney Disease Forum’” (P11), “I asked different people, such as nurses and doctors in your area, and their opinions may differ” (P6). This patchwork approach amplified confusion, as patients struggled to discern credible guidance: “Everything you have is good or bad…because of limited knowledge, right? After all, you’re not in this significant, right?” (P8). Despite efforts to engage clinicians, hierarchical communication styles left critical questions unresolved, exacerbating the dilemma.

Communication dilemma

Patients with ESKD faced significant challenges in engaging meaningful discussions with clinicians, rooted in time constraints and health-related communication barriers. The first barrier is clinician time pressures. Participants perceived physicians as overburdened, leading to fragmented exchanges that prioritized logistical instructions over collaborative deliberation: “Every time I go, people are queuing up for … I feel like I have been talking to the doctor for a long time, which is not good for taking up others’ time” (P11), “The doctor will only tell you what you will do next, but he won’t tell you. I think the doctor is also quite busy “(P7). Physical and emotional exhaustion from advanced kidney disease further stifled patients’ ability to articulate concerns or absorb complex information: “I may lack the energy to communicate effectively. Additionally, I refrained from eating during the initial two days” (P1).

The outcome: decision-making behavior

Decision-making behavior in individuals with end-stage kidney disease was the outcome of the decision-making process, including personal, family, social, and therapeutic factors.

Personal factors

The primary factor to consider while selecting a treatment is its effect on one’s personal and professional life. “Now you feel that starting with abdominal dialysis may be better for yourself, mainly because it is more convenient for the first job. If you can do it at home, then the impact on life should be smaller, mainly due to these two considerations” (P10). Performing peritoneal dialysis four times a day is excessively burdensome. The patient also preferred peritoneal dialysis as the initial treatment option due to its greater convenience and the potential for receiving it at home, which would have a relatively more minor impact on their daily lives: “Due to my familial responsibilities and having children, it is impractical for me to visit the hospital three times a week. It has disrupted my regular life” (P11). Furthermore, there are apprehensions regarding how the treatment method may affect their social reputation and ability to interact: “The attending physician recommended that I undergo a tube insertion procedure, but I am hesitant as I believe it may not be aesthetically favorable for a female to have a tube” (P7). “If I am required to engage in social interactions, I would prefer to receive a different type of response while still maintaining the ability to interact with people and go out in a typical manner” (P2). Adolescent patients also contemplated matrimony and procreation. “Given my current unmarried status, am I eligible to marry someone of my kind while contemplating the future?” (P6).

Family factors

Family considerations primarily encompass financial circumstances and familial obligations. “At present, both of my parents have reached old age; I am the sole offspring, and I also have school-going children for whom I must assume the role of caretaker for the family“(P8). Assess whether the family has the necessary conditions for peritoneal dialysis procedures. “Peritoneal dialysis necessitates the preparation of a small vacant space at home, which, unfortunately, I do not possess “(P11). The patients also took into account the emotions of their family members. “I will discuss with my family because, in the face of such a serious illness, we cannot act hastily and should consider our family’s feelings” (P12). They emphasized the need to avoid impulsive actions and consider their loved one’s emotions and perspectives.

Social factors

Social factors critically shaped patients’ decisions, primarily through healthcare accessibility and financial constraints. Participants emphasized how systemic barriers influenced their choices. Out-of-pocket costs and reimbursement policies directly impacted modality preferences: “If we opt for a kidney transplant, our specific health insurance policy provides partial reimbursement for the expenses” (P9). While hemodialysis (HD) was perceived as financially manageable for some (“It costs over 10,000 RMB a year, which is not a big problem” [P8]), limited insurance support for peritoneal dialysis or transplantation deterred equitable access. Rural patients faced logistical challenges due to centralized dialysis facilities: “Our home is in a town with no blood dialysis available. We can only go to county-level hospitals or travel to Chongqing” (P4). This inequity forced many to prioritize HD—despite personal preferences—due to proximity. The lack of kidney donors and systemic delays dominated transplantation discussions: “Kidney replacement isn’t feasible—it’s too hard to find a donor. Even with funding, the main problem is scarcity” (P11). For many, this scarcity rendered transplantation a theoretical rather than practical option. These systemic constraints often overrode personal preferences, highlighting how social determinants disproportionately dictated choices in resource-limited settings.

Therapeutic factors

During the treatment process, patients have varying degrees of concern about the prognosis and complications of the disease. “During hemodialysis, there are many infection risks, such as hepatitis B infection and other viruses “(P9). Some patients may be very concerned about the success rate and survival time of treatment. “To be honest, if there is an 80% or 90% chance of a successful kidney transplant, I would be willing to undergo it” (P4). “For kidney transplantation, I have received information from friends and family that the survival rate of the kidney after transplantation is not high, so I have a certain degree of rejection towards kidney transplantation” (P10). The patient is looking forward to the development of medicine and hopes for better treatment options. “I hope technology can develop rapidly and solve this problem. I heard that the United States has created an extracorporeal artificial kidney that can be carried on the body, equivalent to a small dialysis machine, making it more convenient” (P10).

Discussion

Person-focused attention to symptoms and subjective experiences is critical in managing CKD as it progresses towards end-stage, where patients often face increasing discomfort such as fatigue, bone/joint pain, and severe vomiting, consistent with previous studies [18, 19]. These symptoms, including fatigue, loss of appetite, and muscle weakness, can serve as indicators of renal impairment severity [20], affecting physical strength, sleep quality, emotional well-being, and overall quality of life [21]. Understanding these subjective symptoms helps healthcare providers evaluate and improve patient well-being effectively. Patient engagement in recognizing and communicating symptoms supports self-care and therapeutic decision-making [22, 23]. While patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are not the core of our study, they provide relevant insights into how patients perceive their health status, which can inform the design of decision aids [24]. By selectively incorporating PROMs—particularly those related to emotional burden and quality of life—our conceptual framework aims to better reflect patients’ experiences in the decision-making process. This provides theoretical support for implementing shared decision-making that is easy to understand, of high quality, unbiased, with timely information updates, and that meets cultural and language requirements [23].

In designing decision aids for kidney replacement therapy in end-stage kidney disease, it is crucial to consider patients’ self-reported symptoms and emotional states. Our proposed conceptual framework integrates not only clinical indicators but also psychosocial elements such as patient confidence, family influence, and perceived quality of life to support shared decision-making. This approach differs from pre-structured decision aid frameworks and instead emphasizes the dynamic, value-laden, and context-dependent nature of decision-making [25, 26]. A qualitative study revealed that shared decision-making aimed at enhancing the treatment of CKD requires a clear identification of the disparities in professional perceptions between patients and healthcare providers regarding the decision-making approaches for advanced CKD treatment, as well as an alleviation of the imbalance in decision-making authority between the two parties [6]. Equal and effective communication between healthcare providers and patients, along with the implementation of appropriate health education and professional training, helped improve patients’ professional cognition and enhanced their information access and capabilities to participate in shared decision-making [5].

Shared decision-making, involving collaboration between physicians and patients to align treatment options with patient values and preferences, has shown to enhance decision quality and patient outcomes across various conditions [27]. Developing decision aids specifically for kidney replacement therapy in end-stage kidney disease can bridge information gaps and facilitate informed patient-provider discussions [28, 29]. Patients with end-stage kidney disease must consider personal, familial, societal, and treatment-related factors when making decisions. Recognizing the complexity of decision-making, optimal outcomes require support from multiple sources. However, limited healthcare resources can constrain treatment options and hinder shared decision-making processes. Our findings align with these challenges, emphasizing collaborative decision-making involving patients, families, healthcare professionals, and society. Our conceptual framework adds to the field by introducing a customizable and interactive decision support system that adapts to individual patient priorities, enabling not only informed but also value-congruent decisions.

Innovative approaches include visualization technologies, which offer intriguing possibilities. These tools help convey complex medical information clearly and facilitate exploration of treatment options. Research demonstrates that visualization enhances doctor-patient communication and decision-making [30]. Implementing visualization techniques is a promising strategy to improve decision support for patients with end-stage kidney disease. Customizable visualizations based on patient preferences help them understand treatment choices, potential risks, and benefits [31]. Virtual reality can simulate kidney replacement therapy experiences, aiding patients in experiencing different modalities firsthand. This immersive approach supports confident decision-making among healthcare professionals and patients alike. Visualization technologies have the potential to provide comprehensive support, enhancing patient satisfaction with critical decisions.

Some limitations in our study should be considered. First, the research object was limited to patients in the nephrology department of a tertiary hospital in Chongqing, and further studies should verify this shared decision-making conceptual framework in other hospitals. Secondly, the shared decision-making conceptual framework constructed was only a preliminary exploration, which still needed long-term testing and improvement.

Conclusions

This study explored the formation process and influencing factors of kidney replacement therapy decision-making in patients with ESKD, and identified three themes: symptom perception as the precondition, dilemmas in information and communication, and multifaceted decision-making behavior involving personal, familial, social, and therapeutic factors. This result provide a foundational understanding of how patients perceive, process, and act upon complex information in kidney replacement therapy decision-making.
