Melatonin attenuates Mycoplasma pneumoniae-Induced Inflammation and barrier dysfunction by CXCL10/TLR4/STAT1 axis
Authors: Chun-Yi Lee, Tsung-Hua Wu, Meei-Ling Sheu, Wen-Hsuan Chiang, Hung-Chuan Pan, Yu-Ping Fang, Yi-Ching Tsai, Chia-Yun Tsai, De-Wei Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) is a major pathogen of community-acquired pneumonia increasingly resistant to macrolides. Mp infection provokes oxidative stress, disrupts epithelial integrity, and skews T helper (Th) cell balance through CARDS toxin–mediated immune dysregulation. Here, we investigated the protective effects of melatonin, a pleiotropic hormone with antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions, in patients and a murine model of Mp pneumonia. Mp infection perturbed the local Th1/Th2/Th17 balance, accompanied by ROS accumulation, CXCL10-induced TLR4/STAT1 activation, and loss of ZO1-dependent tight junctions. Melatonin administration suppressed ROS production, restored epithelial barrier integrity, and rebalanced Th responses within the airway microenvironment. Mechanistically, melatonin inhibited CXCL10–TLR4/STAT1 signaling, with TLR4 residue A366 identified as a key regulatory site. These findings reveal that melatonin alleviates Mp-induced airway injury by maintaining immune and epithelial homeostasis, highlighting its potential as an adjunctive therapy for macrolide-resistant Mp pneumonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02954-5.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Melatonin, T helper cells, TLR4, STAT1, Epithelial barrier, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) is a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia, especially in children and young adults. Clinical outcomes range from mild tracheobronchitis to severe pneumonia and extrapulmonary complications [1]. Diagnosis mainly depends on PCR or serological assays, and treatment commonly relies on macrolides, although the emergence of macrolide-resistant Mp strains is a growing global concern [2]. Melatonin, has recently been recognized as an immunomodulatory molecule with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities in the context of infectious diseases [3]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms by which Mp infection and melatonin interact within the pulmonary microenvironment remain poorly defined.

The airway epithelium represents the first line of defense against Mp, but infection disrupts tight junction proteins such as occludin and ZO-1, thereby weakening barrier integrity [4, 5]. In the context of M. pneumoniae, infection triggers immune activation, including expansion of Th17 cells and neutrophilic infiltration, particularly in severe forms of the disease [6, 7]. A key virulence factor, the community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome (CARDS) toxin, induces vacuolization and amplifies oxidative stress, resulting in reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and epithelial injury [8, 9]. However, how epithelial barrier breakdown, immune cell imbalance, and ROS signaling interact to drive disease progression remains unclear. CXCL10 is a chemokine strongly induced after Mp and viral infections, orchestrating the recruitment of activated T lymphocytes through interaction with CXCR3 [10, 11]. Its transcription is largely regulated by IFN-γ and STAT1 activation, which also influence epithelial cell junction dynamics [12–14]. Additional evidence indicates that CXCL10 signaling engages TLR4 and MyD88, amplifying downstream pro-inflammatory cascades [15]. Despite these insights, the contribution of CXCL10–STAT1 signaling to epithelial barrier disruption and immune dysregulation in Mp infection has not been fully defined.

Melatonin plays a critical role in respiratory immune homeostasis, where it mitigates cytokine storms and protects against oxidative damage in models of influenza and coronavirus infection [16–18]. Mechanistically, melatonin can regulate NF-κB and STAT signaling pathways, thereby limiting pro-inflammatory cytokine release and enhancing antioxidant defenses [19, 20]. In bacterial settings, melatonin has been reported to inhibit TLR4/MyD88-dependent inflammatory responses and decrease the ROS burden, suggesting broad host-protective effects [21]. Nevertheless, whether melatonin exerts similar immunomodulatory functions during Mp infection has not been established.

Together, current knowledge highlights Mp as an important respiratory pathogen, melatonin as a candidate immunomodulatory agent, and CXCL10–STAT1 as a critical inflammatory pathway, yet their interconnections remain poorly understood. The relationships between epithelial junction integrity, ROS accumulation, and CXCL10-driven immune responses during Mp infection are particularly understudied. Here, we investigate how melatonin modulates the pulmonary microenvironment during Mp infection, focusing on ROS regulation, epithelial barrier function, and CXCL10–STAT1 signaling. This approach aims to uncover novel molecular mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic strategies for Mp-associated airway inflammation.

Methods

Study population

Patients with M. pneumoniae (Mp) infection (n = 24) and non-Mp infection (n = 7) were recruited via Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved No. 1,111,106 for melatonin and CXCL10 quantification (combined n = 31 for correlation analysis). These participants were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Department of Pediatrics at Chang Bing Show Chwan Memorial Hospital (Changhua, Taiwan). In a separate cohort (IRB No. 1051007, CF17158A, and CS18084), 112 confirmed Mp-infected patients were stratified by CARDS toxin cycle threshold (Ct) values for cytokine array analysis (n = 16, 8, 68, and 20), with a subset (n = 9) utilized for melatonin testing. Additionally, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected for ex vivo assays under the framework of IRB approved No. 1,101,107. All blood samples were collected between 08:00 and 10:00 AM. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians prior to participation. Mycoplasma pneumoniae culture

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (ATCC® 29085) was cultured in SP-4 medium (ATCC medium 0988) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Colony development of M. pneumoniaewas monitored using the phenol red pH indicator (ATCC PCS-999-001); a color shift from red–orange to yellow was considered indicative of logarithmic-phase growth. For quantification, a color-changing unit (CCU) assay was performed by preparing serial 10-fold dilutions of the culture in SP-4 broth and incubating at 37 °C for 7–14 days, as previously described [22, 23]. The CCU titer was defined as the highest dilution that exhibited a distinct color change from red to yellow. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was calculated as the ratio of infectious MP units (expressed as CCU) to the number of host cells used in each assay. For in vitro experiments, host cells were infected at designated MOIs of 1, 10, or 100, corresponding to 1, 10, or 100 CCU per target cell, respectively. Prior to infection, Mp cultures were harvested, washed, and resuspended in serum-free medium to remove residual metabolites that could influence host cell responses.

Animal experiments

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (National Laboratory Animal Center, Taiwan) were used. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and intranasally inoculated with 1 × 10⁷ CCU of Mp in 50 µL PBS or PBS alone as control. Melatonin (1 or 10 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich) was administered intranasally once daily for six consecutive days, with Mp infection performed on day 4 of treatment. Mp infection was performed on day 4 of melatonin treatment. For CXCL10 intervention, recombinant CXCL10 (200 ng/mouse; R&D Systems) was administered intranasally beginning on the day of Mp inoculation and continuing for three consecutive days. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chang Bing Show Chwan Memorial Hospital (approval no. 113044).

Blood oxygen saturation and pulmonary function measurements

Arterial O₂ saturation and respiratory rate were monitored non-invasively using MouseOx Plus (Starr Life Sciences). Pulmonary function, including airway resistance, elastance, compliance, inspiratory capacity, and forced expiratory volume at 0.1 s (FEV₀.₁), was assessed using a flexiVent FX system (SCIREQ) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Histological and tissue analyses

Lung tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, following procedures similar to those described in our previous study. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general morphology, periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) for goblet cell hyperplasia, and Masson’s trichrome for collagen deposition. Histopathological scores were evaluated by two independent blinded pathologists. Histological scoring was performed in a blinded manner by two independent pathologists. The severity of lung injury was graded using a 0–3 scale for four parameters: inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar wall thickening, peribronchiolar damage, and congestion/edema, yielding a maximum score of 12.

Cell culture

Human bronchial epithelial cells (Beas-2B; ATCC), mouse endothelial cells (SVEC; ATCC), and human lung fibroblasts (HLF; ScienCell) were cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were seeded at 70–80% confluence and treated with Mp (MOI as indicated), melatonin (1 mM; Sigma-Aldrich), and/or recombinant CXCL10 (1 ng/mL; R&D Systems) for the indicated time periods. In selected experiments, cells were pretreated with the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 (100 µM; InvivoGen) or N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) (1 µM; Sigma A9165) 1 h before infection.

Ex vivo assay and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements

Human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells (ATCC) were cultured and co-cultured with freshly isolated PBMCs at a 1:5 ratio using transwell systems. Mp infection was established at multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. TEER was measured at 24 h and 72 h using an EVOM2 voltohmmeter (World Precision Instruments).

ROS detection

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using 10 µM DCFH-DA (Beyotime) after 30 min of incubation at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using confocal microscopy.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from BEAS-2B cells (RNeasy Mini Kit, Qiagen). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeqRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differentially expressed genes were defined using DESeq2 (P < 0.05, log₂FC > 2). Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) enrichment was performed using DAVID. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using MSigDB hallmark gene sets. Protein–protein interaction networks were analyzed using STRING v12.

Protein–ligand binding prediction

Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina. Predicted interactions of melatonin with TLR4 and CCR3 were analyzed, with binding energy and root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) serving as metrics. Visualization was performed with PyMOL.

Cell transfection and site-directed mutagenesis

BEAS-2B cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1 constructs encoding wild-type or mutant TLR4 (A366G, N365A, G343E) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and verified by Sanger sequencing. For gene knockdown experiments, BEAS-2B cells were transfected with specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting TLR4 (siTLR4) or CXCR3 (siCXCR3), while a non-targeting siRNA (siCon) served as the negative control. All siRNA transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 100 nM. Knockdown efficiency and protein expression were subsequently validated by quantitative real-time PCR analysis 24 h post-transfection.

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was analyzed by SDS–PAGE followed by immunoblotting, as described in our previous study. Briefly, cells or lung tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Equal amounts of protein (60 µg) were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; GE Healthcare). Confocal microscopy and immunofluorescence

Cells and tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated with primary antibodies, and then with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (see Table 2). Confocal imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 microscope.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD4, T-bet, GATA3, RORγt, FOXP3, and CD25. Data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa and analyzed with FlowJo v10.

Recombinant protein synthesis and thermal stability analysis

The extracellular domain (ectodomain) of human TLR4 (residues 1–628) was cloned into a pET-28a vector and expressed in E. coli (BL21). Recombinant proteins were purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. Protein purity and molecular weight were verified by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. The thermal stability of the purified proteins was assessed using a Tycho NT.6 instrument (NanoDSF). Samples were subjected to a temperature gradient from 35 °C to 95 °C, and the melting temperature (Ti) was determined by monitoring the ratio of intrinsic fluorescence at 350 nm and 330 nm.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Clinical samples (serum and BALF) and mouse tissue homogenates were analyzed for melatonin, human CXCL10, and murine cytokines (CXCL10, IFN-γ, CXCL15, IL-17, IL-5) using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems and Cayman Chemical) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

For protein-protein interaction assays, 96-well microplates were coated with 2 µg/mL of recombinant WT or mutant TLR4. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), plates were incubated with serial dilutions of recombinant human CXCL10. Bound CXCL10 was detected using a biotinylated anti-CXCL10 antibody followed by streptavidin-HRP. To assess the competitive inhibitory effect of melatonin (MT), TLR4-CXCL10 pre-complexes were incubated with 1 mM MT for 2 h. Residual binding was determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Binding curves were fitted using non-linear regression models (GraphPad Prism).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9. Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests were used for comparisons between two groups. For multiple comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Correlations were assessed by Pearson’s test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Melatonin levels are altered in M. pneumoniae-infected patients and associate with CARDS toxin load and cytokine responses

To investigate the relationship between Mycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) infection and melatonin, we measured serum melatonin concentrations, CARDS toxin load, and cytokine profiles in clinical subjects (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figure S1). Compared with uninfected individuals, melatonin concentrations were significantly higher in Mp-positive patients (Fig. 1A); however, among infected individuals, those with moderate disease exhibited lower melatonin levels than those with mild disease (Fig. 1B). Stratification by CARDS toxin cycle threshold (Ct) values revealed that IL-12 concentrations were significantly lower in the < 20 Ct group compared with the 30–40 and > 40 Ct groups (Fig. 1C), whereas IL-13 showed no significant differences across Ct categories (Fig. 1D). IL-33 levels were significantly higher in the < 20 Ct group compared with the 30–40 and > 40 Ct groups (Fig. 1E), while IL-10 levels showed no significant changes (Fig. 1F). Correlation analysis indicated no significant associations between melatonin and IL-12 or IL-13 (Fig. 1G, H), but revealed a modest negative correlation with IL-33 (Fig. 1I) and a modest positive correlation with IL-10 (Fig. 1J). Expanded analysis including IL-6, IL-8, MMP1a, and MMP9 revealed no significant associations with CARDS toxin load (Supplementary Figure S1 A–D), but each displayed a weak negative correlation with melatonin concentrations (Supplementary Figure S1 E–H). Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated a distinct separation in the overall biomarker profiles between Mp-negative individuals and Mp-positive patients with moderate CARDS toxin loads (Ct 20–30), with PCA1 and PCA2 accounting for 35.61% and 21.5% of the total variance, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1I). Taken together, these findings suggest that Mp disease severity and CARDS toxin production are associated with altered melatonin levels and specific cytokine changes; however, the mechanisms by which melatonin modulates epithelial responses to Mp and subsequent T cell interactions remain to be determined.

Melatonin reduces ROS accumulation, preserves epithelial barrier integrity, and modulates T helper cell differentiation after 24 h M. pneumoniae infection

To examine the effect of melatonin on the airway epithelial cell–immune response axis during Mp infection, we established an ex vivo platform to model epithelial–immune cell interactions within a confined microenvironment (Supplementary Figure S2 A). Previous studies have shown that Mp-induced CARDS toxin can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and alter local tissue conditions. In our 24 h infection model, Mp markedly increased intracellular ROS levels in BEAS-2B cells, whereas melatonin effectively suppressed ROS accumulation (Supplementary Figure S3 A). In a BEAS-2B–PBMC co-culture system, Mp infection significantly reduced transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) by approximately 30% compared with controls, indicating barrier disruption, whereas melatonin treatment significantly restored barrier integrity (Supplementary Figure S2 B). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that Mp infection significantly decreased the frequency of CD4⁺T-bet⁺ Th1 cells (Supplementary Figure S2 C, G) while increasing CD4⁺GATA3⁺ Th2 and CD4⁺RORγt⁺ Th17 subsets (Supplementary Figure S2 D–E, H–I). CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺ Treg frequencies remained unchanged (Supplementary Figure S2 F, J). Melatonin treatment reversed Th2 and Th17 expansion and partially restored Th1 cell proportions to near-control levels. Together, these results demonstrate that 24 h Mp infection promotes ROS accumulation, impairs epithelial barrier function, and skews CD4⁺ T cell differentiation toward Th2/Th17 lineages, effects that are partially reversed by melatonin. However, whether these changes persist or evolve with prolonged infection remains unclear.

Melatonin preserves epithelial barrier integrity and modulates helper T cell differentiation after 72 h of M. pneumoniae infection

To assess the long-term effects of M. pneumoniae (Mp) infection, we extended the in vitro co-culture period to 72 h (Fig. 2A). Mp markedly increased intracellular ROS levels in BEAS-2B cells, and melatonin effectively attenuated ROS accumulation (Supplementary Figure S3 B). TEER measurements revealed more pronounced barrier disruption at 72 h compared with 24 h, and melatonin continued to exert significant restorative effects (Fig. 2B). Flow cytometric analysis showed a further decrease in CD4⁺T-bet⁺ Th1 cell frequency (Fig. 2C, G) and sustained expansion of CD4⁺GATA3⁺ Th2 and CD4⁺RORγt⁺ Th17 subsets (Fig. 2D–E and H–I), whereas CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺ Treg frequencies remained unchanged (Fig. 2J). Prolonged melatonin treatment reversed Th2 and Th17 expansion and restored Th1 frequencies toward control levels. Notably, although immune cell infiltration and barrier damage were exacerbated, ROS levels at 72 h were not significantly higher than at 24 h (Supplementary Figure S3 B), suggesting that the exacerbated immune imbalance and barrier injury during prolonged Mp infection may involve mechanisms beyond ROS accumulation.

Transcriptomic analysis identifies chemokine signaling, particularly CXCL10, as a key target of melatonin during prolonged M. pneumoniae infection

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying epithelial alterations after 72 h of M. pneumoniae infection, we performed transcriptomic profiling of BEAS-2B cells (Supplementary Figure S4 A). Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) analysis revealed significant enrichment of multiple pathways, notably IL-17 signaling, chemokine signaling, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed robust activation of chemokine signaling (NES = 2.75, P < 0.001) and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (NES = 2.08, P < 0.001) in Mp-infected cells. Among up-regulated genes, CXCL10 emerged as a central hub within the interaction network, clustering alongside STAT family members and TSLP (Supplementary Figure S4 B–E). Under the thresholds of P < 0.05 and log₂ fold change > 2, Mp infection induced 108 significantly up-regulated and 59 down-regulated genes relative to controls. We next examined transcriptional changes in Mp-infected cells treated with melatonin (Fig. 3A). GOBP analysis indicated that melatonin significantly impacted IL-17 signaling and chemokine signaling (Fig. 3B), with GSEA showing negative enrichment for chemokine signaling (NES = − 2.74, P < 0.001) and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (NES = − 2.32, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). Under the same thresholds, melatonin treatment resulted in 199 significantly up-regulated and 326 down-regulated genes compared with Mp infection alone (Fig. 3D). In the down-regulated gene network, CXCL10 clustered with other CXCL family members, as well as MMP3 and CCL2 (Fig. 3E). Consistently, heatmap analyses further demonstrated that melatonin reduced Mp-induced up-regulation of chemokines and interleukin family members, while preserving epithelial marker expression and attenuating mesenchymal marker expression. (Supplementary Figure S5 A-F).These results identify chemokine signaling—particularly CXCL10—as a potential driver of epithelial dysfunction during prolonged Mp infection and suggest that melatonin may restore barrier integrity by modulating this pathway.

CXCL10 is upregulated in M. pneumoniae infection and inversely correlated with melatonin

To investigate the role of CXCL10 in M. pneumoniae infection and the potential effect of melatonin, we first measured serum CXCL10 levels in Mp-infected individuals. CXCL10 expression was markedly higher in CXCL10-positive individuals compared with CXCL10-negative individuals (Fig. 4A). Moreover, CXCL10 levels were inversely correlated with serum melatonin concentrations (R2 = 0.1670, p = 0.0203; Fig. 4B). In vitro, Mp infection significantly increased CXCL10 secretion in Beas2b, SVEC, and HLF cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4C). Time-course analysis further revealed that Mp (1 × 10^6 CCU) induced the greatest elevation of CXCL10 at 72 h post-infection (Fig. 4D). Given previous reports suggesting CXCL10 signaling via TLR4, we examined whether melatonin or TLR4 inhibition could modulate this response. Melatonin, TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 and NAC significantly reduced CXCL10 secretion across cell types (Fig. 4E). Molecular docking analysis further predicted that melatonin could bind the TLR4 dimer, with residues G343, N365, and A366 contributing to the interaction (binding affinity − 6.1 kcal/mol, RMSD lower bound 2.02 Å; Fig. 4F and Supplementary Figure S6 A). By contrast, melatonin showed weaker predicted binding to CCR3 (binding affinity − 5.2 kcal/mol), suggesting a preferential interaction with TLR4 (Supplementary Figure S6 B). Collectively, these findings indicate that CXCL10 is robustly induced by Mp infection, inversely associated with melatonin, and regulated via a melatonin–TLR4 interaction.

CXCL10 does not contribute to ROS accumulation but mediates barrier dysfunction through TLR4 signaling

To further explore the molecular mechanism of CXCL10 during Mp infection, we assessed its contribution to oxidative stress. Fluorescence imaging showed that CXCL10 alone did not induce ROS production at 72 h, nor did it interfere with the ability of melatonin to suppress Mp-induced ROS accumulation (Supplementary Figure S7A). These results suggest that ROS generation is primarily an early event (within 24 h) in Mp infection and occurs independently of CXCL10 signaling. To clarify the role of CXCL10 dosage and timing, we further examined ROS levels across a range of CXCL10 concentrations (1–1000 ng/mL) at 6 h and 24 h. We found that while high concentrations of CXCL10 (100 and 1000 ng/mL) could induce detectable ROS levels, low-dose treatment (1 ng/mL) did not trigger significant ROS generation at either time point (Supplementary Figure S7B). These findings suggest that ROS generation is primarily associated with high-level CXCL10 exposure or early infection stages, whereas the epithelial barrier dysfunction observed at lower CXCL10 levels (1 ng/mL) occurs independently of substantial ROS production. Instead, CXCL10 may contribute to later pathological changes through distinct pathways. Supporting this notion, recombinant CXCL10 mimicked Mp-induced epithelial barrier disruption, as shown by reduced TEER, whereas melatonin treatment effectively restored barrier integrity (Fig. 4G). To validate the predicted melatonin–TLR4 interaction, we first assessed the protein expression and structural integrity of wild-type (WT) and mutant TLR4 constructs (A366G, N365A, and G343E). Western blot analysis of transfected BEAS-2B cells demonstrated that all TLR4 variants were expressed at comparable levels without significant signs of protein degradation, ensuring that the mutations did not affect intracellular protein stability (Supplementary Figure S8A). To further investigate the direct molecular interaction at the protein level, we purified the recombinant extracellular domains (residues 1–628) of these variants using an E. coli expression system. Thermal stability analysis via Tycho NT.6 confirmed that these point mutations did not alter the melting temperature (Ti) of the TLR4 ectodomain compared to the WT protein, suggesting that the observed functional impairments were not due to gross protein misfolding (Supplementary Figure S8B). In BEAS-2B cells, N365A and G343E mutations significantly reduced CXCL10 secretion and attenuated CXCL10-induced barrier permeability, whereas A366G had no such effect (Supplementary Figure S8C). Confocal imaging further demonstrated that N365A and G343E preserved ZO-1 localization at the cell membrane under CXCL10 stimulation, while A366G failed to do so (Supplementary Figure S8C). Finally, competitive ELISA assays confirmed that the A366G mutation significantly impairs the ability of MT to compete with CXCL10 for TLR4 binding (Supplementary Figure S8D). These findings suggest that the A366 residue represents a critical regulatory site for the melatonin-mediated inhibition of CXCL10/TLR4 signaling. To validate the predicted melatonin–TLR4 interaction, we generated site-directed mutants (A366G, N365A, and G343E). In Beas2b cells, N365A and G343E mutations significantly reduced CXCL10 secretion and attenuated CXCL10-induced permeability, whereas A366G had no effect on basal CXCL10 expression or permeability (Supplementary Figure S9 A and B). Confocal imaging further demonstrated that N365A and G343E preserved ZO1 localization at the cell membrane, while A366G failed to do so (Supplementary Figure S9 C). Strikingly, the A366G mutation markedly diminished the protective effect of melatonin on barrier function, suggesting that A366 represents a critical regulatory site for melatonin-mediated inhibition of CXCL10/TLR4 signaling.

Melatonin attenuates M. pneumoniae-induced epithelial barrier disruption via CXCL10/TLR4/STAT1 signaling

To define the downstream pathway of CXCL10 in Mp infection, we examined TLR4–STAT1 signaling cascades. Dose-dependent Mp infection increased TLR4 expression, MyD88 recruitment, STAT1 phosphorylation, and induction of the transcription factor SNAIL, which in turn suppressed ZO-1 expression. Treatment with melatonin reduced MyD88 and p-STAT1 levels, downregulated SNAIL, and restored ZO-1 expression (Fig. 4H). To further elucidate whether CXCL10 disrupts epithelial barrier integrity through the TLR4/ROS axis, we employed the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242, the ROS scavenger NAC, and siRNA-mediated knockdown of TLR4 or CXCR3. We observed that CXCL10-induced reduction in TEER was significantly attenuated by TAK-242, NAC, and TLR4 knockdown (Supplementary Figure S10A). Consistent with these functional findings, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that treatment with TAK-242 or NAC prevented the CXCL10-mediated disorganization of the tight junction protein ZO-1 (Supplementary Figure S10B). Furthermore, TLR4 knockdown exerted a more pronounced protective effect on ZO-1 distribution compared to CXCR3 knockdown, confirming that CXCL10 primarily mediates epithelial barrier disruption through a TLR4-dependent and ROS-associated mechanism (Supplementary Figure S10C). Finally, the protective effect of melatonin was substantially impaired in the presence of the A366G mutant (Fig. 4I). Consistently, immunofluorescence revealed that melatonin restored ZO-1 localization at cell–cell junctions, whereas recombinant CXCL10 and A366G mutation disrupted this protective effect (Fig. 4J). Together, these findings demonstrate that melatonin protects against Mp-induced epithelial barrier dysfunction by targeting the CXCL10/TLR4/STAT1 axis, and that A366 of TLR4 is a key residue mediating this regulatory effect.

Melatonin improves pulmonary function and attenuates airway inflammation in M. pneumoniae-infected mice

To investigate whether melatonin confers protective effects against M. pneumoniae infection in vivo, we established a mouse model with intranasal Mp challenge and intranasal administration of melatonin with or without recombinant CXCL10 (Fig. 5A). Continuous monitoring revealed that Mp infection led to a rapid decline in arterial O₂ saturation and an increased breathing rate, accompanied by significant hemodynamic instability characterized by elevated heart rate (Supplementary Figure S11A and Fig. 5B, C). Consistently, forced expiratory volume in 0.1 s (FEV₀.₁) was markedly reduced following Mp infection but was restored by melatonin treatment (Fig. 5D). Histological analysis confirmed severe airway and parenchymal inflammation with epithelial damage in Mp-infected lungs. These pathologies were significantly attenuated by melatonin, but the protective effect was reversed with CXCL10 supplementation (Fig. 5E). Moreover, melatonin decreased Mp-induced CXCL10 levels in both serum and BALF (Fig. 5F and G). Extended lung mechanics analysis showed that melatonin improved resistance, elastance, compliance, and inspiratory capacity in Mp-infected mice, effects that were again negated by recombinant CXCL10 (Supplementary Figure S11B-G). Histopathological staining further demonstrated that melatonin reduced goblet cell hyperplasia and collagen deposition, quantified by PAS and Masson’s trichrome staining, respectively. These improvements were diminished with recombinant CXCL10 administration (Supplementary Fig. 11H and S12 A and B). Collectively, these results indicate that melatonin improves pulmonary function and reduces airway inflammation in Mp-infected mice primarily through suppression of CXCL10 signaling.

Melatonin restores epithelial barrier integrity and suppresses Th17-associated inflammation in M. pneumoniae-infected lungs

To further clarify whether melatonin protects epithelial integrity during Mp infection, we examined the expression and localization of key cell-cell junction proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, and P-cadherin (p-Ecad), in lung tissues. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that Mp infection markedly disrupted the distribution of ZO-1, Occludin, and p-Ecad at airway epithelial borders (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figure S13). Notably, melatonin treatment preserved the junctional integrity of these proteins in a dose-dependent manner; however, this protective effect was significantly reversed by the co-administration of recombinant CXCL10 (Supplementary Figure S13A and B). In parallel, Mp infection induced a strong increase in RORγt⁺ cells, consistent with activation of Th17 responses, which was significantly reduced by melatonin, but re-established with recombinant CXCL10 supplementation (Fig. 6B and D). Quantification of Mp burden further revealed reduced bacterial load in melatonin-treated mice, suggesting that melatonin not only alleviates host inflammatory injury but also enhances pathogen clearance (Fig. 6E). Together, these results suggest that melatonin restores epithelial barrier integrity and suppresses Mp-induced Th17-associated airway inflammation via inhibition of CXCL10.

Melatonin attenuates TLR4/STAT1 activation and modulates cytokine responses in M. pneumoniae-infected lungs

To dissect the molecular mechanism underlying the protective role of melatonin, we examined TLR4/STAT1 signaling in Mp-infected lungs. Immunofluorescence staining showed strong induction of TLR4 and phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1) in airway tissues of Mp-infected mice, with robust co-localization signals, whereas melatonin significantly suppressed both TLR4 expression and STAT1 activation. Co-administration of recombinant CXCL10 largely reversed these inhibitory effects (Fig. 7A). Consistent with these findings, melatonin reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, CXCL15, IL-17 and the type 2 cytokine IL-5 in BALF; these regulatory effects were counteracted by CXCL10 supplementation, except IL-5 (Fig. 7B). Supporting this, immunofluorescence analyses further confirmed that melatonin promoted immune homeostasis by reducing T-bet⁺ and GATA3⁺ cells in lung tissue, an effect dependent on CXCL10 suppression (Supplementary Figure S14). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that melatonin attenuates Mp-induced airway inflammation by inhibiting CXCL10-driven TLR4/STAT1 activation and restoring immune balance. Finally, to define the therapeutic window of melatonin, we evaluated its direct effect on M. pneumoniae (Mp) and host cell viability. Phenol red colorimetric assays and qPCR analysis revealed that melatonin exerts a robust dose-dependent inhibitory effect on Mp metabolic activity and bacterial load (Supplementary Figure S15A–E). Crucially, cytotoxicity assays (CCK-8) showed that melatonin exhibits a favorable safety profile, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for host cells determined at 16.8 mM for BEAS-2B cells and 17.5 mM for HLF cells, while PBMC remained unaffected up to 100 mM (Supplementary Figure S15F–H). Given that melatonin effectively inhibits Mp growth at concentrations significantly lower than its IC50 for primary airway cells, these findings highlight a clear therapeutic window, supporting the conclusion that melatonin exerts protective effects by simultaneously suppressing bacterial growth and mitigating inflammatory signaling, without inducing significant host cytotoxicity.

Discussion

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) remains a major cause of community-acquired pneumonia, particularly in children and young adults, with complications ranging from mild respiratory illness to severe extrapulmonary manifestations [1]. Current treatment relies heavily on macrolides; however, the global rise of macrolide-resistant Mp strains severely limits therapeutic options [24]. In addition, refractory Mp pneumonia (RMPP), characterized by persistent fever, progressive radiological findings, and excessive immune responses despite appropriate macrolide therapy has emerged as a growing clinical concern, especially in pediatric populations⁴. These limitations highlight the urgent need for novel adjunctive strategies targeting host immune dysregulation rather than pathogen elimination alone [25]. Given its pleiotropic roles in immunoregulation, antioxidant defense, and tissue protection, melatonin has recently gained attention as a potential therapeutic candidate in infectious and inflammatory diseases. Investigating the role of melatonin in modulating host immune responses during Mp infection may provide novel insights into managing refractory disease.

Mp infection–induced immune responses have been shown to display distinct patterns of T helper (Th) cell dysregulation. In this study, we observed that Mp infection induced a substantial expansion of T-bet⁺ CD4⁻ cells, such as cytotoxic T cells or NK-like populations, which contribute to early immune defense but may also drive excessive inflammation. Following melatonin treatment, these innate-like populations were reduced, accompanied by an increase in T-bet⁺ CD4⁺ Th1 cells, indicating a shift from atypical innate-driven inflammation toward a more balanced adaptive Th1 response that facilitates symptom resolution and pathogen clearance [26–28]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that M. pneumoniae infection induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in host cells, leading to oxidative stress [29]. In parallel, the Fas/FasL pathway has been shown to mediate T-cell apoptosis in various bacterial infections, although its specific involvement in M. pneumoniae infection has not yet been directly confirmed [30]. Therefore, we hypothesize that oxidative stress and Fas/FasL signaling may play potential roles in T-cell apoptosis following M. pneumoniae infection, warranting further investigation.Although previous studies have reported variable effects of M. pneumoniae on Th1 cell populations, we propose that the timing of infection represents a key factor influencing the dynamics of the immune response [26, 31].In contrast, we observed expansion of Th2 and Th17 subsets, consistent with clinical reports linking Mp infection to post-infectious asthma and allergic phenotypes, as well as hyperinflammatory responses during severe pneumonia [4, 32]. Specifically, Th2 skewing has been implicated in airway remodeling and allergic sensitization, whereas exaggerated Th17 responses drive neutrophilic inflammation and cytokine storms [33]. Interestingly, we did not detect significant changes in Treg frequencies, in line with previous findings suggesting that Mp-induced immune dysregulation is primarily mediated through Th1/Th2/Th17 imbalance. Together, these results integrate with prior evidence and suggest that Mp infection may tip the immune axis away from protective Th1 responses toward pathogenic Th2/Th17 expansion, thereby amplifying airway inflammation and tissue injury. Notably, our clinical observations revealed a non-linear relationship between serum melatonin levels and the severity of Mp infection. While melatonin levels were generally elevated in infected patients compared with healthy controls, patients with moderate disease exhibited lower levels than those with mild disease. We hypothesize that this biphasic pattern reflects a transition from an initial adaptive, compensatory protective response to a state of ‘disease-associated consumption.’ In the early or mild stages of infection, the host likely upregulates melatonin production to mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation. However, as the disease progresses to moderate severity, this endogenous compensation may be overwhelmed by sustained systemic oxidative stress or a potential breakdown in circadian signaling pathways. Furthermore, the integrity of these clinical data was ensured by stringent control of potential confounding factors. All samples were collected during the morning hours (08:00–10:00 AM) to standardize circadian variance, and patients with recent international travel or shift-work history were excluded to mitigate disruptions to the melatonin-secreting rhythm. These findings suggest that while endogenous melatonin plays a protective role in M. pneumoniae pneumonia, its exhaustion or regulatory failure in more severe cases highlights the clinical rationale for exogenous supplementation as a therapeutic strategy to restore immune homeostasis and prevent further disease progression. In interpreting our clinical stratification, we acknowledge a potential limitation in utilizing CARDS toxin PCR Ct values to represent inflammatory responses. While Ct values provide a standardized measure of transcriptional activity, they do not necessarily reflect absolute CARDS toxin protein abundance, as translation efficiency and protein stability can be modulated by host factors. To mitigate this uncertainty, we utilized multiple complementary measures, including total bacterial burden quantification, to ensure the robustness of our inflammatory stratification. Future studies incorporating mass spectrometry or novel ELISA platforms will be essential to provide a comprehensive view of CARDS toxin dynamics during infection.

Melatonin has been recognized as a broad-spectrum immunomodulator with the capacity to regulate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)–dependent signaling [34]. Prior reports demonstrated that melatonin suppresses TLR4/MyD88 activation, thereby limiting downstream NF-κB–mediated cytokine release and oxidative stress [35, 36]. Our study extends these findings by showing that melatonin directly attenuates CXCL10-induced TLR4 activation, and through mutational analysis, we identify A366 of TLR4 as a key regulatory site mediating this effect. In parallel, STAT1 signaling, a central pathway in Mp-driven epithelial barrier dysfunction, was also modulated by melatonin, leading to reduced phosphorylation of STAT1 and downregulation of the transcription factor SNAIL, ultimately preserving ZO1-dependent tight junctions. These results corroborate earlier studies linking melatonin to STAT1-dependent immune regulation and highlight a novel mechanism through which melatonin stabilizes epithelial integrity during infection. Notably, the A366G mutation did not compromise the TLR4-mediated cellular response to rCXCL10, confirming that this residue is dispensable for CXCL10-driven signaling. In contrast, the A366G mutation completely abrogated the suppressive effect of melatonin on CXCL10-induced signaling. These findings lead us to conclude that A366 serves as a critical regulatory hotspot specifically for melatonin–TLR4 interaction. We propose that melatonin binding at the A366 site allosterically modulates TLR4 conformation or inhibits the recruitment of downstream signaling adaptors, thereby preventing the exacerbation of inflammation by CXCL10, without interfering with the basal ligand-receptor interaction required for physiological immune responses. Finally, our study demonstrates that pharmacological suppression of CXCL10 levels (via melatonin) restores epithelial barrier integrity and immune homeostasis in the context of M. pneumoniae infection. While these findings establish a clear therapeutic link, future investigations utilizing CXCL10-neutralizing antibodies or CRISPR-Cas9 mediated depletion will be instrumental in confirming the precise temporal contribution of endogenous CXCL10 to the full spectrum of M. pneumoniae-induced pathogenesis. Collectively, these findings position melatonin as a highly promising adjunctive strategy for managing refractory Mp pneumonia by simultaneously targeting both pathogen proliferation and host-mediated inflammatory injury. The use of intranasal administration in our study is strategically aligned with current pharmacological trends in respiratory medicine. Unlike systemic delivery, which is often hampered by rapid metabolic clearance and systemic side effects, intranasal delivery enables the direct targeting of the pulmonary compartment. Pharmacokinetic studies have highlighted that IN delivery allows for significant accumulation of therapeutic agents within the airway lining fluid, maintaining localized concentrations that are often difficult to achieve via oral or intravenous routes [37]. Furthermore, clinical trials investigating the delivery of immunomodulators have shown that IN administration is well-tolerated and promotes rapid symptom resolution in acute respiratory infections [38]. Given that RMPP often involves localized lung injury and refractory inflammation, our findings provide a robust basis for evaluating intranasal melatonin as a practical, high-efficacy adjunctive therapy in future clinical settings. Future clinical studies are warranted to further delineate the optimal dosing regimen and therapeutic potential of melatonin in addressing severe Mp infection, particularly in contexts where macrolide resistance is prevalent.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the beneficial effects of melatonin in Mp infection are mediated through suppression of CXCL10-driven TLR4/STAT1 signaling, leading to reduced ROS accumulation, restoration of epithelial barrier integrity, and rebalancing of Th cell responses (Fig. 8). These findings provide mechanistic insight into how melatonin protects against Mp-induced airway injury and support its potential as an adjunctive therapy for macrolide-resistant Mp pneumonia. Future clinical studies are warranted to validate these translational implications.
