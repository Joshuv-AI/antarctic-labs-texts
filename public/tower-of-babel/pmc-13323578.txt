Predictors and Consequences of Serious Adverse Events in Patients Treated With Thrombolysis: A Prespecified Secondary Analysis of the AcT Trial
Authors: Aria Appoo, Katrina Ignacio, Cody Doolan, Fouzi Bala, Mohammed Almekhlafi, Brian H. Buck, Aleksander Tkach, Luciana Catanese, Richard H. Swartz, Tolulope T. Sajobi, Aravind Ganesh, Bijoy K. Menon, Nishita Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing use of tenecteplase, it is important to understand its safety compared with alteplase. We aimed to determine the incidence, predictors, and functional impact of serious adverse events (SAEs) in patients treated with alteplase versus tenecteplase.MethodsThis is a post hoc analysis of the AcT (Alteplase Compared to Tenecteplase) trial, a phase 3 multicenter randomized controlled trial that randomized 1577 ischemic patients with stroke (2019–2022) into tenecteplase versus alteplase presenting <4.5 hours of onset. SAEs were recorded within 24 hours of treatment and classified by organ system using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities. Mixed‐effects logistic regression evaluated predictors of SAEs and their impact on 90‐day modified Rankin Scale.ResultsOf the 1577 enrolled, 219 (13.9%) patients had SAEs. Patients with SAEs had higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (median, 11 versus 9; P=0.002) and higher endovascular treatment rates (50.2% versus 29.2%; P<0.001) than those without SAEs. Nervous system disorders were the most common SAE (58.2%), including stroke worsening (26.7%) and intracranial hemorrhage (25%). No significant differences were observed in SAE distribution by thrombolytic. Patients with SAEs had higher 90‐day modified Rankin Scale scores (median, 4 versus 2; odds ratio [OR], 3.93 [95% CI, 2.78–5.56]). Independent predictors of SAEs included baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (per SD increase: OR, 1.2 [95% CI, 1.0–1.5]), large‐vessel occlusion (OR, 1.6 [95% CI, 1.1–2.5]), Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (per point increase: OR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–1.0]), and cerebral atrophy (per point increase: OR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.1–1.9]).ConclusionsOur study found no difference between rate or type of SAEs by thrombolytic type, supporting the safety of tenecteplase.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT03889249.

Since the approval of alteplase in 1996 by the Food and Drug Administration, intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) has been the standard of care for treating acute ischemic stroke presenting within 3 hours of symptom onset. 1 , 2 In 2000, tenecteplase, a second‐generation thrombolytic, was approved for use in myocardial infarction. 3 It offers several advantages over alteplase, including a longer half‐life, higher fibrin specificity, and administration via bolus rather than infusion. 2 The AcT (Alteplase Compared to Tenecteplase) trial for acute ischemic stroke was a phase 3 randomized controlled trial that showed the noninferiority of tenecteplase (0.25 mg/kg) to alteplase (0.9 mg/kg) for ischemic stroke presenting within 4.5 hours of symptom onset in terms of both safety and efficacy. These findings were corroborated by the TRACE‐2 (Tenecteplase Versus Alteplase in Acute Ischemic Cerebrovascular Events 2) and ATTEST‐2 (Alteplase Versus Tenecteplase for Thrombolysis After Ischemic Stroke 2) trials. 2

While IVT is effective, it also carries a risk of complications, especially hemorrhagic events, which can impact patient outcomes. Serious adverse events (SAEs) are defined by the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use as any untoward medical occurrence that at any dose results in death, is life threatening, requires inpatient hospitalization or prolongs existing hospitalization, results in persistent or significant disability, or a congenital anomaly or birth defect. 4 There is limited evidence on detailed comparisons of SAEs occurring as a result of tenecteplase versus alteplase in acute ischemic stroke. 5 , 6 The AcT trial analyzed the following SAEs 24 hours after treatment with IVT: symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, extracranial bleeding requiring blood transfusions, orolingual angioedema, and any other event qualifying the definition of SAE as described above. 7

In this prespecified secondary analysis, our primary objective was to assess the association between SAEs and functional outcomes in the AcT trial. Additionally, we aimed to (1) summarize and compare all SAEs that occurred in patients who received tenecteplase versus alteplase, (2) compare SAEs by organ system using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities coding system between tenecteplase versus alteplase, (3) assess expectedness and relatedness of SAEs, and (4) identify predictors for the occurrence of specific SAEs.

METHODS

The data for the study are available upon reasonable request from author B.K.M. (bkmmenon@ucalgary.ca). Author N.S. had full access to all the data in the study and takes responsibility for their integrity and the data analysis.

Data were derived from the AcT trial (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; NCT03889249), which was a pragmatic, multicenter, parallel group, open label with blinded end point assessment, randomized trial. The trial was approved by the local institutional review boards of all participating centers. Deferred consent procedures were used wherever approved by local research ethics boards. Two centers, in the province of Quebec, Canada, used only prospective consent (written or verbal) from patients or their representatives. At the remaining centers where consent was deferred, patients or their legal representatives were asked to provide written or electronic informed consent as soon as possible after treatment, within 7 days of randomization, or before discharge, whichever was earlier. The trial evaluated the noninferiority of intravenous tenecteplase versus intravenous alteplase in patients presenting with ischemic stroke. Comprehensive inclusion and exclusion criteria were published in the trial protocol. 7 , 8 In brief, patients aged ≥18 years with acute ischemic stroke presenting within 4.5 hours of symptom onset and who met the Canadian guidelines for thrombolysis were eligible for the study and were enrolled across 22 primary and comprehensive stroke centers in Canada between December 2019 and January 2022. Trial data were linked to the QuiCR (Quality Improvement and Clinical Research) registry (a provincial Alberta registry capturing data on all patients treated for acute ischemic stroke) or the OPTIMIZE (Optimizing Patient Treatment in Major Ischemic Stroke With Endovascular Thrombectomy) registry, which captures comparable data from other provinces, including workflow metrics and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores. Trial data were also linked to the Canadian Institute for Health Information data to capture comorbidities and inpatient procedures. Imaging data were collected in a central imaging core laboratory by experienced assessors who were blinded to all treatment allocations and outcomes. The primary outcome of AcT was the proportion of patients with excellent functional outcome defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0 to 1 at 90 to 120 days. All 90‐day follow‐up assessments were done using centralized telephone interviews by trained personnel who were masked to treatment allocation.

In the AcT trial, SAEs were collected within 24 hours of IVT, consistent with the short half‐lives of thrombolytics and with the known safety profiles of alteplase and tenecteplase. All SAEs were coded using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology by personnel blinded to treatment allocation and reviewed monthly by an independent safety committee. As an additional safeguard, patients identified by the imaging core laboratory with parenchymal hematoma type 1 or type 2 were queried to confirm whether hemorrhage met trial definitions for symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage was defined as any intracerebral hemorrhage that was temporally related to and directly responsible for the worsening of the patient's neurological condition and that, in the investigator's opinion, was the most important factor for neurological worsening. Similarly, all adverse events/SAEs reported in registry data were queried against site charts to ensure that none met SAE criteria or occurred within the initial 24‐hour window.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between patients who developed any SAE versus those who did not. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, as the distributions were nonnormal. Ordinal variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical and binary variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using Fisher's exact test given small cell counts and increased preciseness of the test. Differences in SAE frequencies between patients receiving tenecteplase and alteplase were also assessed using Fisher's exact test for the same reason. SAEs were grouped into organ class affected according to Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities classification. Events occurring in <5 patients were grouped into an “Other” category to aid interpretability and avoid presenting extremely sparse categories that have limited statistical stability and clinical interpretability. Predefined clinically important SAEs, including those related to bleeding or procedural complications, were reported individually regardless of frequency.

Logistic regression was performed to determine the effect of any SAE on functional outcomes. Models were adjusted for age, sex, NIHSS, drug type, presence of SAEs, imaging characteristics (presence of large‐vessel occlusion (LVO), Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score [ASPECTS], global cerebral atrophy [GCA], presence of microbleeds, white matter hyperintensities), and comorbidities (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, and dyslipidemia) as fixed effects, with enrolling center treated as a random effect. We further did a sensitivity analysis for predictors of SAEs excluding magnetic resonance imaging–based brain frailty variables (ASPECTS score, GCA, presence of microbleeds, and white matter hyperintensities) since magnetic resonance imaging was done in only a subset of patients on follow‐up. Adjusted models were optimized using constrained nonlinear minimization (nlminbwrap optimizer on R [R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria]) to improve convergence. Continuous variables, NIHSS, age, and door‐to‐needle time, were all scaled by Z score scaling to improve model convergence. Bootstrapping with 500 simulations was performed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and their CIs from the adjusted logistic regressions were estimated. Descriptive horizontal bar graphs were used to show the effect of different organ class SAEs on mRS outcomes.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to understand predictors of experiencing an SAE. The following baseline clinical variables were included in the model: age, sex, baseline NIHSS, comorbidities (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, dyslipidemia), thrombolytic type, and imaging characteristics (ASPECTS score, LVO, GCA, presence of microbleeds, and white matter hyperintensities) as fixed effects, with enrolling center treated as a random effect. The choice of variables was planned a priori on the basis of clinical relevance and previous data showing that these variables are known to influence outcomes. 9 , 10 , 11

A 2‐tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 2024.04.2+764.

The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies (checklist in supplementary material).

RESULTS

Overall Study Population Characteristics

Of the total 1577 patients enrolled in the AcT trial, SAEs were reported in 219 (13.4%) patients, with a total of 256 SAEs recorded (Figure 1). Patients with any SAE compared with those with no SAEs had a higher prevalence of cerebral microbleeds (8.9% versus 4.4%; P=0.010), worse NIHSS scores (median, 11 versus 9; P=0.002), and LVOs (36.4% versus 23.15; P<0.001). A higher proportion of patients with any SAE versus no SAE also received endovascular treatment (50.2% versus 29.2%; P<0.001). All other baseline demographics were similar between the 2 groups (see Table 1 for comparison of baseline characteristics).

Effect of SAEs on Functional Outcome

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, patients experiencing SAEs consistently demonstrated worse outcomes compared with those without SAEs (Table 2). Rates of excellent outcome (mRS 0–1) were 9.1% in the any‐SAE group versus 18.8% in the no‐SAE group, corresponding to a significantly reduced likelihood of excellent functional outcome among patients with SAEs (adjusted OR [aOR], 0.26 [95% CI, 0.15–0.45]). Functional independence (mRS 0–2) was also significantly lower in patients with any SAEs (16.4% versus 29.0%; aOR, 0.20 [95% CI, 0.12–0.33]). Ordinal analysis of mRS confirmed a strong association between SAEs and worse disability (aOR, 4.43 [95% CI, 3.05–6.43] for any 1‐point increase in mRS). Mortality (mRS 6) was markedly higher in the any‐SAE group versus no‐SAE group (26.5% versus 7.0%; aOR, 6.44 [95% CI, 3.89–10.68]). Similarly, return to prestroke functional status was observed less frequently in patients with any SAEs (5.9% versus 13.4%; aOR, 0.31 [95% CI, 0.16–0.61]; Table 2). Predictors of SAEs

Logistic regression models were performed to identify predictors of developing SAEs (Table 3). In simple logistic regression analyses, the presence of LVO (OR, 1.6 [95% CI, 1.1–2.3]; P=0.014), higher NIHSS score (per SD increase: OR, 1.2 [95% CI, 1.0–1.5]; P=0.044), lower ASPECTS score (per point increase: OR, 0.9 [95% CI, 0.8–1.0]; P=0.015), and greater GCA (per point increase: OR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.1–1.9]; P=0.004) were significantly associated with a higher risk of SAEs.

Mixed‐effects logistic regression showed that among the predictors above, only greater GCA was significantly associated with developing SAEs (aOR, 1.4 [95% CI, 1.0–2.0]; P=0.031). Meanwhile, age, sex, comorbidities, thrombolytic type, and other imaging markers (cerebral microbleeds and white matter hyperintensities burden), were not significantly associated with SAE risk. Mixed effects logistic regression excluding brain frailty showed similar results (Table S2).

A further sensitivity analysis was done for the subgroup of patients where magnetic resonance imaging was not available (Table S3). In this analysis, the mixed‐effects logistic regression showed that tenecteplase was significantly associated with developing SAEs (aOR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.0–2.5]; P=0.037).

SAE‐Level Analysis

When looking at the distribution of SAEs at an SAE level, nervous system disorders were the most common (58.2% of all SAEs), followed by vascular disorders (12.5% of all SAEs) and cardiac disorders (8.2% of all SAEs). There was no significant difference between SAE frequency by organ class or individual SAE between thrombolytic groups (Tables S4 and S5).

In total, 87 SAEs were reported from bleeding events, with 12 requiring transfusion (Table S6). The most common bleeding event requiring transfusion was intracranial hemorrhage (n=57) followed by epistaxis (n=6) and groin hematoma (n=6). There were 7 bleeding events in the alteplase group and 5 bleeding events in the tenecteplase group (Table S7).

DISCUSSION

In our prespecified secondary analysis of patients with acute ischemic stroke presenting within 4.5 hours who received IVT with tenecteplase or alteplase, we found that SAEs occurred in 13.4% of patients. The most common SAE was symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Development of SAEs had a negative impact on functional outcomes and death at 90 days. Presence of LVO, lower ASPECTS, higher NIHSS score, and higher GCA scores were significant predictors of developing SAEs using a simple logistic regression model. Finally, the type and rates of SAEs were similar between those who received tenecteplase and those who received alteplase.

In our secondary analysis of AcT trial data, we found that the rate of SAEs was similar to previously reported thrombolysis trials for acute ischemic stroke. Apart from TRACE‐2, most of these trials provided data for only a small subset of SAEs. TRACE‐2 found a similar rate of SAEs (16.3% and 15.2% for tenecteplase and alteplase, respectively) to our trial (14.6% and 13.1% for tenecteplase and alteplase, respectively). 12 TRACE‐2 also reported nervous system disorders as the most common class of SAEs, with a similar incidence as our study (8.9% and 9.5% for tenecteplase and alteplase, respectively). It is worth noting that they did not specify which SAEs were classified as nervous system disorders. Rates of intracranial hemorrhage and angioedema were universally recorded and found to have similar rates to our study. ECASS‐3 (European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study 3), EXTEND‐IA TNK (Tenecteplase Versus Alteplase Before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke), TRACE‐2 and ATTEST‐2 found the incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage to be between 1% and 2.4% and the incidence of angioedema to be between 0% and 1%. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Furthermore, trials such as EXTEND‐IA TNK, TRACE‐2, and ATTEST‐2 as well as retrospective reviews that compared tenecteplase with alteplase, found no significant difference in rates of SAEs between the thrombolytics. 5 , 6 , 12 , 13 , 15 Altogether, this demonstrates that our results are in line with the existing literature.

Our study also demonstrated that experiencing SAEs significantly increases the risk of a worse functional outcome at 90 days. This is in line with a previous post hoc analysis of the WAKE‐UP (Efficacy and Safety of MRI‐Based Thrombolysis in Wake‐Up Stroke) trial, which was a placebo‐controlled randomized trial of alteplase in patients with unknown time of stroke onset that evaluated the effects of SAEs on mRS scores. 16

The last part of our analysis focused on identifying predictors of SAEs. A retrospective cohort study of patients who had received alteplase found that age and increased NIHSS score increased the likelihood of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. 17 Interestingly, unlike our study, they found that male sex, higher blood glucose, and higher systolic blood pressure increased the risk of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. These results cannot be directly compared with our study, as we only evaluated risk of all SAEs together. The WAKE‐UP trial evaluated predictors for all SAEs. 16 They identified age and male sex as predictors of SAEs. Our study showed higher GCA scores to be a predictor of developing SAEs. A previous post hoc analysis of the AcT trial showed that the associations of imaging markers of brain frailty, cortical atrophy, white matter changes, and total brain frailty were associated with worse outcomes. 18 Brain frailty is thought to reflect the cumulative burden of chronic vascular injury and neurodegeneration, which may reduce structural and functional brain reserve. 19 Reduced reserve could plausibly increase vulnerability to adverse effects from acute illness or pharmacologic therapies; however, the mechanisms underlying these associations remain to be established in future studies. Interestingly, in our sensitivity analysis of patients who did not receive magnetic resonance imaging, tenecteplase was a significant predictor of SAEs. As this was a subgroup analysis of a population, these results should be interpreted with caution. This post hoc analysis of the AcT trial has provided a comprehensive overview of the SAEs affecting patients receiving tenecteplase versus alteplase. In addition, we demonstrated the significant impact of SAEs on functional outcomes and how baseline characteristics influence the risk of developing SAEs and impact outcomes.

One of the key strengths of this study is the use of a large, pragmatic clinical trial design with linkage to administrative data, enabling comprehensive outcome assessment while maintaining real‐world applicability. Standardized SAE definitions and a centralized adjudication process were applied to ensure consistency across sites. Several safeguards were incorporated to minimize missed events: All SAEs were coded by blinded personnel using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology, monthly review was undertaken by an independent safety committee, registry‐reported adverse events were systematically cross‐checked against site charts, and any cases with imaging evidence of parenchymal hematoma type 1 or type 2 were specifically queried to confirm whether they met trial definitions of symptomatic hemorrhage. These measures enhanced the completeness and reliability of SAE capture. Furthermore, the observed associations were internally consistent and aligned with findings from previous studies in the field, supporting the validity of the results. Nevertheless, this study has limitations. It was not powered to detect differences in SAEs, particularly rare events, and the potential for residual underreporting or misclassification cannot be excluded despite the safeguards implemented.

As tenecteplase is increasingly adopted in clinical practice, establishing its safety profile relative to alteplase is essential. In this multicenter phase 3 pragmatic trial, the rate and type of any SAE did not differ by thrombolytic agent, suggesting that tenecteplase does not confer excess systemic safety risk compared with alteplase. Instead, the risk of SAEs appeared to be driven primarily by baseline clinical and imaging characteristics, including stroke severity (NIHSS), LVO, ASPECTS, and markers of brain frailty such as GCA. Emerging studies have suggested that postthrombolysis monitoring intensity may potentially be reduced in selected patients with mild or moderate strokes. 20 , 21 Our findings support a risk‐stratified approach to postthrombolysis care, whereby patients with severe strokes and high‐risk imaging features may instead warrant closer monitoring for recognition of systemic complications or lower thresholds for intensive care unit admission. In addition, ongoing work within the ACT‐WHEN (A Multicenter, Prospective, Randomized, Open Label, Blinded‐Endpoint Trial to Optimize the Use of Intravenous Tenecteplase in Participants With Acute Ischemic Stroke)/ACT‐GLOBAL (A Multi‐Factorial, Multi‐Arm, Multi‐Stage, Randomized, Global Adaptive Platform Trial for Stroke) platform (NCT06352632) is evaluating whether higher‐risk patients may benefit from reduced thrombolytic dosing, specifically comparing two thirds versus standard‐dose tenecteplase to determine whether dose reduction may mitigate bleeding risk. Together, these efforts underscore the imperative to individualize thrombolytic care, tailoring dose and monitoring strategies to patient risk profiles.
