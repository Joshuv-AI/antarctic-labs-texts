Psychological distress among family caregivers of patients with cardiovascular disease
Authors: Koichiro Matsumura, Eijiro Yagi, Yasuhiro Kakiuchi, Takahiro Tabuchi, Nobuhiro Yamada, Yohei Funauchi, Kazuyoshi Kakehi, Kosuke Fujita, Takayuki Kawamura, Hiroki Matsuzoe, Masafumi Ueno, Gaku Nakazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Various studies have reported the clinical importance of psychological distress in patients with cardiovascular diseases (CVD). However, few studies have examined the psychological distress experienced by family caregivers of patients with CVD. This study aimed to examine how having to care for patients with CVD affects psychological distress among family members.Methods:Data on patients with CVD requiring care and family caregivers living with them were extracted from a nationwide population-based study in Japan (2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions), conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare. Psychological distress was assessed using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale-6 item score (0–24 points) from the questionnaire, and a score ≥9 was defined as psychological distress.Results:Family caregivers of patients with CVD who required care in 1990 were analyzed. The proportion of family caregivers experiencing psychological distress was 12.9% (257 in 1990). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the patients themselves had psychological distress, younger family caregivers, female family caregivers, family caregivers and patients residing alone, family caregivers who were not working, and patients with CVD complicated with dementia were independent factors affecting psychological distress in family caregivers.Conclusions:Multidisciplinary healthcare providers need to be aware of the mental health of not only patients with CVD requiring care but also their family caregivers and implement appropriate medical support.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide and a major contributor to excessive healthcare costs.[1] The need for home care following CVD-related hospitalization is common, with more than half the patients planning to receive caregiver support after discharge.[2] In most cases, family caregivers comprise the majority.[34] The family caregiver was mostly a spouse or a middle-aged adult with functional limitations. Caregivers’ responsibilities include medication management, scheduling, and helping patients practice self-care, including adherence to diet and exercise regimens. In addition, they assist with activities of daily living such as housekeeping and psychosocial support.[5] Cost of caring for patients can be a considerable burden.[6] These multifaceted factors lead to psychological distress in family caregivers of patients with CVD.[7]

In contrast, while many studies have been conducted on the assessment of psychological distress and therapeutic interventions for patients with CVD, little research has focused on the psychological distress experienced by family caregivers. However, family caregivers often report high levels of perceived psychological distress, with studies showing that 6–67% of family caregivers experience substantial psychological distress.[8] However, the emotional stress of caregiving can increase the risk of death for family caregivers.[9] As a potential mechanism, greater caregiving burden has been linked to higher intake of saturated fatty acids, higher levels of obesity, and higher rates of physical inactivity.[10] Healthcare providers need to be aware of psychological distress not only in patients with CVD but also in family caregivers. Therefore, this study investigated how the factors of patients with cardiovascular disease affect psychological distress in family caregivers using a nationwide database in Japan.

Methods

Data sources

We analyzed data from the 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC), a nationwide cross-sectional survey of basic living conditions conducted every three years by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare. The survey was designed to examine health, medical insurance, public assistance, pension, and income. All households (approximately 30,000) and household members (approximately 720,000) in 5,530 randomly stratified districts were surveyed using household and health questionnaires.[11] The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution and conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Data on family caregivers living with patients with CVD were extracted from the CSLC 2019 data [Figure 1]. Patients admitted to a hospital or nursing home for whom information on medical history and the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale-6 items (K6) were not available were excluded. CVD was defined as a history of at least one episode of angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke (cerebral hemorrhage or ischemic stroke).

Data collection

Regarding patients and family caregivers, data on socioeconomic and lifestyle background factors such as age, sex, comorbidity, K6 score, living environment, employment status, number of family members living with the patient, and walking ability were extracted from the CSLC2019. Walking ability was categorized according to whether the person needed assistance or supervision in daily living due to a disability or physical impairment.

Assessment of psychological distress

Psychological distress was assessed using the K6 scale, which is widely used to evaluate psychological distress such as depression and anxiety.[12] The K6 score consists of six questions, each assessing the frequency of feeling nervous, hopeless, restless, fidgety, “too depressed to do anything,” “everything is an effort,” and “worthless” in the past 30 days. A 5-point Likert scale was used to answer the questions, with choices ranging from “always agree” (4 points) to “not at all agree” (0 points), for a total score ranging from 0 to 24. A cutoff point of 9 or higher is commonly used to identify moderate psychological distress.[13] Based on this cut-off point, this study determined that psychological distress was present when the K6 scale score was 9 or higher.

Outcome

Psychological distress among family caregivers was defined as feeling stressed about caregiving for a patient on the questionnaire, with a K6 score of 9 or higher. Psychological distress among patients with CVD was defined as a K6 score ≥9.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Differences between the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with cancer screening. Multivariate analyses were conducted for age, sex, comorbidities (history of cancer, depression, dementia, and smoking), employment status, number of family members living with the patient, number of patients, whether the family caregiver was a spouse, and the presence of psychological distress. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. JMP Pro 16.2.0 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

In this analysis, 1,990 family caregivers were analyzed (mean age, 64.5 years; 27% of men), of whom 12.9% (257 of 1,990) family caregivers had psychological distress. Among the 1,990 patients with CVD (mean age, 79.0 years, 52% of men), psychological distress was observed in 27.8% (554 of 1,990) of patients with CVD.

Relationship between family caregivers and patients

Regarding the relationship between family caregivers and patients, the most common pattern was that the patients’ spouses provided care for 50% (990 in 1,990), followed by the patients’ children at 34% (669 in 1,990), the spouse of the patients’ children at 13% (256 in 1,990), and the patients’ parents at 1.8% (35 in 1,990) [Figure 2a]. Other family members accounted for 2.0% (40 in 1,990).

Psychological distress in family caregivers

Among family caregivers, the rate of emotional distress was 12% (116 of 990) for patients’ spouses, 15% (97 of 669) for patients’ children, 12.5% (32 of 256) for spouses of patients’ children, 5.7% (2 of 35) for patients’ parents, and 25% (10 of 40) for other relatives [Figure 2b].

Multivariate logistic regression analysis related to psychological distress in family caregivers

Factors associated with psychological distress of the family caregiver were examined using multivariate logistic regression analysis [Figure 3]. Family caregiver-related factors included middle age, female sex, depression, employment, and living alone with the patient. Patient-related factors were also associated with dementia and psychological distress. The patients’ psychological distress was the most highly associated with the psychological distress in family caregivers (odds ratio of 3.63 and a 95% confidence interval 2.75–4.79).

Discussion

Few studies have evaluated the association between caring for patients with CVD and psychological distress among family caregivers in Japan. This study conducted a cross-sectional survey of psychological distress among family caregivers using epidemiological big data from the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare. The mean age of the family caregivers was 65 years, and that of the patients with CVD was 79 years. The percentage of family caregivers who experienced psychological distress was 12.9%. More than 80% of the family caregivers were spouses or children. Factors associated with psychological distress in family caregivers included middle age, female sex, comorbid depression, employment, and living alone with patients. Factors on the patient side were related to the complications of dementia and patients’ psychological distress. In particular, psychological distress in patients themselves was most highly associated with psychological distress in family caregivers, with an odds ratio of 3.63.

Previous observational studies on caregiving for patients with heart failure have identified various sociodemographic factors, such as physical health of the caregiver, self-care behaviors, health literacy, and relationship with the patient as being associated with the psychological distress of family caregivers.[14] The risk factors for psychological distress in family caregivers include being young, female, unmarried, living with the patient, length of caregiving time, and limited social support.[14] Meta-analyses have shown an association between emotional burden of caregivers and adverse clinical manifestations in patients.[15] A recent study examined caregiving for patients with heart failure from a bilateral perspective and found a bidirectional impact on caregiver and patient health.[16] Healthcare providers should manage the treatment of CVD and mental status of not only patients with CVD but also their family caregivers. Based on the results of our study, focusing on the mental stress of family caregivers when patients have high psychological distress is necessary to reduce the mental burden and improve the long-term prognosis of patients and family caregivers.

To reduce the emotional burden on family caregivers, a simple screening scale for family caregivers can be used to identify emotional stress.[14] However, effective intervention components and methods for assisting family caregivers remain unclear.[17] Education-only interventions appear to be valuable in reducing emotional stress for some family caregivers; however, intensive multifactorial interventions are necessary.[8] In a qualitative study examining the intervention needs of patients and their family caregivers, practical/personal support, peer interaction, and relationship support were found to be desirable.[18] Healthcare providers should consider interventions such as care coordination, navigation of public insurance and public services, and collaboration with visiting physicians for family caregivers experiencing psychological distress.[17]

Burden on family caregivers is a multifaceted problem. The psychological distress and negative health effects of caregivers are often based on prioritizing the health and needs of patients over those of the caregivers themselves. However, the impact of family caregiver strain extends beyond the family caregivers themselves. Family caregiver stress can increase the risk of elder abuse, leading to the premature placement of patients in nursing homes.[1920] Consequently, healthcare systems face the burden of accepting patients who might have been cared for at home if their family caregivers do not receive adequate support. Addressing the burden on family caregivers is not only a matter of personal health and well-being but also an important component of providing efficient and compassionate health care across the cardiovascular care continuum.

Strengths and limitations

Our study, based on a survey of randomly selected families across the country, provides a comprehensive snapshot of psychological distress among family caregivers of patients with CVD. Most previous studies on psychological distress in patients with CVD and their family caregivers have been conducted on a scale of several hundred people, largely from overseas.[14] Our study is valuable because it elucidates the current status of psychological distress among patients with CVD and their family caregivers in Japan using a large database with a total sample size of nearly 4,000 individuals. Furthermore, we conducted an interactive analysis from a more multifaceted perspective that included factors related to patients as well as factors related to family caregivers as risk factors for psychological distress of family caregivers. As the role of family caregivers continues to increase in Japan, owing to its aging population, analysis using a large database is becoming increasingly important.

Our study has several limitations. First, although cross-sectional data can reveal associations, they cannot be used to determine whether caregivers’ psychological distress is a cause or consequence of patients’ depressive state. Further research is required to examine the causal relationships between family caregivers and patients. Second, the response rate for the 2019 National Life Survey instrument was 55%,[11] and family caregivers who responded may have had a better overall health status or fewer barriers in accessing care than the target population. Therefore, the prevalence of mental health conditions may have been underestimated in the present study.

Conclusions

Multiple factors are associated with psychological distress among family caregivers of patients with CVD. A strong association was found between psychological distress among family caregivers and patients with CVD. The role of family caregivers is increasing in Japan’s aging society, and healthcare providers need to consider not only the mental health care of patients with CVD but also their family caregivers.
