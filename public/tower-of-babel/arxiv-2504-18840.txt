Swarming in the Wild: A Distributed Communication-less Lloyd-based Algorithm dealing with Uncertainties
Manuel Boldrer, Vít Krátký, Viktor Walter, Martin Saska

Abstract
In this work, we present a distributed algorithm for swarming in complex environments that operates with no communication, no a priori information about the environment, and using only onboard sensing and computation capabilities. We provide sufficient conditions to guarantee that each robot reaches its goal region in a finite time, avoiding collisions with obstacles and other robots without exceeding a desired maximum distance from a predefined set of neighbors (flocking constraint). In addition, we show how the proposed algorithm can deal with tracking errors and onboard sensing errors without violating safety and proximity constraints, still providing the conditions for having convergence towards the goal region. To validate the approach, we provide experiments in the field. We tested our algorithm in GNSS-denied environments i.e., a dense forest, where fully autonomous aerial robots swarmed safely to the desired destinations, by relying only on onboard sensors, i.e., without a communication network. This work marks the initial deployment of a fully distributed system where there is no communication between the robots, nor reliance on any global localization system, which at the same time it ensures safety and convergence towards the goal within such complex environments.

I
Introduction

Over the past few decades, numerous applications have emerged for
multi-robot systems, exhibiting their undisputed benefits in diverse fields.
These applications span domains such as logistics, surveillance, environment
monitoring, disaster response, search and rescue, and agriculture among others,
demonstrating their versatility. For
many of these applications, having a swarm of robots able to flock together
towards a desired location can be highly beneficial, due to redundancies and
parallelization. The definition of flocking is provided by the seminal work of
Reynolds
[
1
]
, which defines it as a non-colliding
aggregate motion. Depending on the environments where robots operate, specific
factors like the reliability or availability of direct communication between
robots may be compromised.

In this work, we propose a Lloyd-based algorithm that relies only on on-board
sensors. We aim to synthesize a distributed control algorithm, where each robot
safely reaches the desired position, maintaining proximity between robots in
the team. In particular, we provide sufficient conditions to guarantee safety,
proximity maintenance and convergence towards the goal in cluttered
environments by accounting for bounded tracking errors and uncertainties
in the estimation of neighbors and obstacles positions.

Our algorithm takes inspiration from nature; in fact, just like flock of birds
or a school of fish, the robots do not need a leader and each robot makes its
own decisions based on relative information, without relying on explicit
communication. We validate our approach through an extensive simulation
and experimental campaign in the field. In Figure
1
, we
illustrate a snapshot during a mission in the forest. This work represents the
first deployment of a fully distributed system with absence of any communication
between the robots and any global localization system, which also provides
conditions for safety and convergence towards the goal in such a complex
environments.

I-A

Related works

Prior literature provides a considerable amount of algorithms for distributed
motion coordination of multiple robots, but only a few of them can manage to
work in a cluttered environment by also imposing the flocking constraint. Among
them, a smaller subset is devoted to applications in the field without relying
on direct communication between the robots.

To position our algorithm within the state of the art, we considered the
following
features:

1.

Distributed control: each robot makes decisions on the basis of
local information, which is obtained only by relying on
onboard sensors;

2.

Asynchronous control: each robot makes decisions independently,
in asynchronous fashion;

3.

Communication independence: the robots do not need to
communicate, since the algorithm does not rely on unobservable
information, e.g., future trajectories, velocities, and accelerations;

4.

Safety: the system is provably safe, i.e., given the upper
bounds on uncertainties (see item 7), collision and obstacle avoidance is
guaranteed at any time;

5.

Convergence: the sufficient conditions for the robots to
converge
to their goal locations are provided (i.e., absence of deadlock and
live-lock);

6.

Proximity maintenance: the algorithm allows to set hard
constraints on the maximum distance between robots;

7.

Uncertainties: the system is provably safe and respects proximity
constraints even in the presence of bounded tracking errors and/or
bounded measurement uncertainties;

8.

Cluttered and highly dense environments: the
algorithm can safely manage the presence of static obstacles in the
environment. It can also handle highly dense cluttered
environments, such as a dense forest.

9.

Onboard localization: the algorithm is tested by relying
on onboard localization in unknown environments.

10.

Experiments: the proposed algorithm is tested on real robotic platforms
in unstructured environments;

11.

Open source code:
the algorithm is open source on Github.

I-B

Contribution and paper organization

Our contribution is fivefold:

•

We propose a novel algorithm for flocking control in dense cluttered
environments exploiting some of the theoretical findings
in
[
2
]
. With respect to
[
3
]
, where the
authors introduced the flocking behavior in the Lloyd-based framework,
we do not impose the line-of-sight constraint, i.e., two robots are
considered in the proximity if they are close enough. This allows to increase the
robots mobility and also to not rely on communication. At the same time, it
introduces high uncertainties in the position estimation of the neighbors when
robots are not in the line of sight.

•

We provide sufficient conditions for safety,
proximity maintenance, and convergence even in cluttered environments and with
flocking constraints.

•

We propose a novel adaptive method to account for
tracking errors and measurement uncertainties.

•

We tailor the use of Convex
Weighted Voronoi Diagrams (CWVD)
[
13
]
for our problem. By
doing that, we considerably improve the performance of the algorithm in dense
cluttered environments such as a dense forest.

•

We validate the algorithm in the
forest, relying only on on-board sensors i.e., IMU, 2-D Lidar and UVDAR system,
without relying neither on a communication network nor GNSS signal.

provides

In Sec.

II
Problem description

Let us denote the positions of the robots with
p=[p_{1},\dots,p_{N}]^{\top}
, where
p_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i}]^{\top}
and the obstacle positions by
o=[o_{1},\dots,o_{N_{o}}]^{\top}
,

o_{k}=[x_{k},y_{k}]^{\top}
. The mission space is represented by
\mathcal{Q}
, the
weighting function that assess the weights of the points
q\in\mathcal{Q}
is

\varphi_{i}(q):\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, while
\delta_{i}
and

\delta_{k}^{o}
are the radius of encumbrance of the
i
–th robot and the
k
–th
obstacle respectively.

The problem that we aim to solve reads as follow:

Problem 1
.

Let
N
be the number of robots in the swarm, let
\mathcal{N}_{i}
be the
set of neighbors of the robot
i
, and
\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{i}
the set of neighbors that has to remain connected the entire
mission. We want to synthesize a distributed control law, such that the
swarm maintains the desired proximity constraints, which is characterized by a set
of maximum distances
\Gamma_{ij},\,\,\,\forall i,\forall j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
. Each robot in the swarm has to reach its goal region
avoiding collisions with other robots and obstacles.

Assumption 1
.

Each robot
i
can perceive the relative position of robot (obstacle)
j
if
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
(
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
). Where
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
if

\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|\leq R_{s}
(
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
if
\|p_{i}-o_{j}\|\leq R_{s}
),
with
R_{s}
indicating the sensing range, equal for all the robots.

Assumption 2
.

The flocking constraints are symmetric, i.e.,
\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|\leq\Gamma_{ij},\forall j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i},\forall i=1,\dots,N
, where
\Gamma_{ij}=\Gamma_{ji}<R_{s}
.

Assumption 3
.

The size of the neighboring robots and obstacles is known and it can be
approximated with circles/cylinders.

Assumption 4
.

In our theoretical analysis, we assume that the robots are holonomic agents.

Assumption 5
.

From Section
III-C
, we assume more complex dynamical
systems and a more realistic perception system, where the tracking error is
bounded as well as the error on the measured distances from neighbors and
obstacles.

III
Proposed Approach

The presented approach is grounded in the Lloyd
algorithm
[
27
]
. In the literature, its distributed nature was
exploited to develop novel algorithms for several collective
behaviors
[
3
,
2
,
13
,
28
,
29
]
.

Let us consider the following cost function

J_{\operatorname{cov}}({p},\mathcal{V})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\left\|q-p_{i}\right\|^{2}\varphi_{i}(q)dq,

(1)

where

\mathcal{V}_{i}=\{\mathcal{V}_{i}^{p}\cap\mathcal{V}_{i}^{o}\}

with
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{p}
being the
i
–th Convex Weighted Voronoi Diagram (CWVD)
introduced in
[
13
]
, which accounts only for neighboring
robots positions

\small\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{i}^{p}=\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid w_{i}^{x}\cos\alpha_{ij}+w_{i}^{y}\sin\alpha_{ij}<\frac{1}{\epsilon_{p}}&\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|,\\
&\forall j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\}.\end{split}

(2)

While
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{o}
is the
i
–th CWVD accounting only for neighboring obstacles positions

\small\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{i}^{o}=\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid w_{i}^{x}\cos\alpha_{ij}+w_{i}^{y}\sin\alpha_{ij}<\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}&\left\|p_{i}-o_{j}\right\|,\\
&\forall j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\},\end{split}

(3)

where
w=[w^{x},w^{y}]^{\top}=q-p_{i}
and
\alpha_{ij}=\arctan\left(\frac{y_{j}-y_{i}}{x_{j}-x_{i}}\right)
, while
\epsilon_{p(o)}\in[1,2]
, is an arbitrary real number, which regulates the cautiousness in the
robot motion (i.e.,
\epsilon_{p(o)}=1
more aggressive behavior,

\epsilon_{p(o)}=2
more cautelative behavior, notice that the latter
coincides with the classic Voronoi diagram).

:
Representation of the Convex Weighted Voronoi Diagram (CWVD)

\mathcal{V}_{i}^{p}
in (
2
) with
\epsilon_{p}=2
and
\epsilon_{p}=1
.
Where the green circle is the i–th robot and the cyan circles represents the
neighboring robots.

In Figure
2
, we depict an example of the CWVD (
2
) for
the case where
\epsilon_{p}=2
and
\epsilon_{p}=1
.

By exploiting Assumption
4
, we can impose
\dot{p}_{i}=u_{i}
. By
following the gradient descent of the cost function (
1
), we obtain
the following proportional control law

\dot{p}_{i}\left(\mathcal{V}_{i}\right)=-k_{p,i}\left(p_{i}-c_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\right),

(4)

where
k_{p,i}>0
is a positive real number, and

c_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}q\varphi_{i}(q)dq}{\int_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\varphi_{i}(q)dq}

(5)

is the centroid position evaluated over the
\mathcal{V}_{i}
cell. By
imposing (
4
), assuming a time-invariant
\varphi_{i}(q)
, each robot

i
,
\forall i,1\dots,N
, converges asymptotically to the centroid position

c_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}

[
30
]
. In the following, we show how,
by introducing suitable modifications to the geometry of cell
\mathcal{V}_{i}

and crafting an appropriate weighting function
\varphi_{i}(q)
, we can attain a
safe and effective algorithm for multi-robot swarming.

III-A

Cell geometry
\mathcal{F}_{i}

The reshape of the cell geometry is both necessary and sufficient to ensure
safety guarantees. Hence, we define the cell
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{p}\cap\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{o}\cap\mathcal{S}_{i}\}
,
where

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{p}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left\{q\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{p}\right\}\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|\geq\Delta_{ij}\\
\left\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\left\|q-p_{i}\right\|\leq\left\|q-\tilde{p}_{j}\right\|\right\}\text{ otherwise, }\end{array}\right.\

(6)

with
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
,
\Delta_{ij}=\delta_{j}+\delta_{i}
, and

\tilde{p}_{j}=p_{j}+2\left(\Delta_{ij}-\frac{\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|}{2}\right)\frac{p_{i}-p_{j}}{\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|}
, takes into consideration the physical
dimension of the robots. On the other hand,

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{o}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left\{q\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{o}\right\}\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|\geq\Delta^{o}_{ij}\\
\left\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\left\|q-p_{i}\right\|\leq\left\|q-\tilde{o}_{j}\right\|\right\}\text{ otherwise, }\end{array}\right.\

(7)

with
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
,
\Delta^{o}_{ij}=\delta_{i}+\delta^{o}_{j}
and

\tilde{o}_{j}=o_{j}+2\left(\Delta^{o}_{ij}-\frac{\left\|p_{i}-o_{j}\right\|}{2}\right)\frac{p_{i}-o_{j}}{\left\|p_{i}-o_{j}\right\|}
, takes into consideration the
size of the robot and the obstacles, while

\mathcal{S}_{i}=\left\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\left\|q-p_{i}\right\|\leq r_{s,i}\right\},

(8)

with
r_{s,i}

is half of the sensing radius
R_{s}
.

Theorem 1

(Safety
[
2
]
)
.

By imposing the control
\dot{p}_{i}(\mathcal{A}_{i})
in (
4
), collision
avoidance is guaranteed at every instant of time.

Proof.

The proof can be found in
[
2
]
, the addition of static
obstacles and CVWD does not change the result. In particular, by adding
static obstacles and/or introducing CVWD does not alter the convexity of
the set.
∎

We further apply an additional reshaping of the cell geometry in order to
maintain the proximity, i.e., to obtain swarming. By imposing our final cell
geometry
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap\mathcal{M}_{i}\}
, where

\mathcal{M}_{i}=\{q\in\mathcal{Q}\mid\|q-p_{j}\|<\Gamma_{ij},\forall j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i}\},

(9)

we guarantee proximity maintenance while preserving safety. In
Figure
3
, we depict an example of cell geometry
\mathcal{F}_{i}
.

Theorem 2

(Proximity maintenance)
.

By imposing the control
\dot{p}_{i}(\mathcal{F}_{i})
in (
4
), both
safety and proximity maintenance are guaranteed at every instant of time.

Proof.

Given that
\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{i}
, safety comes from
Theorem
1
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is convex by definition, then the
centroid
c_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
. It implies that by imposing

\dot{p}_{i}(\mathcal{F}_{i})
in (
4
),
p_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
at any
time. Hence, by the definition of the
\mathcal{F}_{i}
set, proximity
maintenance between
i
and
j
is always preserved.
∎

III-B

The weighting function
\varphi_{i}(q)

Although ensuring safety and proximity maintenance only involves properly
shaping the cell geometry, achieving convergence and enhancing navigation
performance largely hinges on how the function
\varphi_{i}(q)
is defined. We
adopt a Laplacian-shaped function centered at
\bar{p}_{i}
, which represents a
point in the mission space that ultimately needs to converge to the goal
location. We design the
\varphi_{i}(q)
function as follows:

\varphi_{i}(q)=\text{exp}{\left(-\frac{\|q-\bar{p}_{i}\|}{\beta_{i}}\right)},

(10)

where the spreading factor
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is time-variant, it follows
the dynamics

\dot{\beta}_{i}(\mathcal{A}_{i})=\begin{cases}-\beta_{i}\,\,\,&\text{if}\,\,\|c_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}-p_{i}\|<d_{1}\,\land\\
&\|c_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}-c_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\|>d_{2}\\
-(\beta_{i}-\beta^{D}_{i})&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(11)

and

\begin{split}\dot{\bar{p}}_{i}&=\begin{cases}-(\bar{p}_{i}-R(\pi/2-\varepsilon)e_{i})\,\,\,&\text{if}\,\,\|c_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}-p_{i}\|<d_{3}\,\land\\
&\|c_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}-c_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\|>d_{4}\\
-(\bar{p}_{i}-e_{i})&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\\
\bar{p}_{i}&=e_{i}\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,\|p_{i}-\bar{c}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\|>\|p_{i}-c_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\|\,\land\,\bar{p}_{i}=R(\pi/2-\varepsilon)e_{i}.\\
\end{split}

(12)

The parameters
\beta_{i}^{D},d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}
are positive scalars,
R(\theta)

is the rotation matrix,
e_{i}
is the goal position for the
i
–th robot,

\varepsilon
is a small number, which allows to avoid rotations equal to

\pi/2
, while
\bar{{c}}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}
is the centroid position computed
over the cell
\mathcal{A}_{i}
with
\bar{p}_{i}\equiv e_{i}
.

Corollary 1

(Convergence in cluttered environment and with flocking
constraints)
.

Let us assume we have an unbounded space
cluttered with circular obstacles of radius
\delta^{o}<\Gamma_{ij}/2-2\delta
and at a minimum distance of
\|o_{i}-o_{j}\|>4\delta+2\delta^{o}
one
to the other. The initial and goal positions are adequately apart from each
other, i.e.,
\Gamma_{ij}>\|p_{i}(0)-p_{j}(0)\|>\Delta_{ij}
,
\Gamma_{ij}>\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|>\Delta_{ij}
,
\forall i,j=1\dots N
with
i\neq j
. By
imposing the control law
\dot{p}_{i}(\mathcal{F}_{i})
in (
4
),
(
10
), (
11
), and (
12
) to compute the centroid
position in (
5
), by properly selecting
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}
, and
\beta^{D}_{i}
, we have that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|p_{i}(t)-e_{i}\|<r_{s,i},\forall i=1,\dots,N.

:
We depict in cyan the robots and in grey the static obstacles. The

Proof.

With respect to the proof of convergence with no obstacles and no proximity
maintenance constraints, which are provided in
[
2
]
, we
recognize, beside the trivial initial and final conditions requirements, the
addition of two potential deadlock scenarios, which are depicted in
Figure
4
.

Notice that the addition of static obstacles is equivalent to non-cooperative
static robots. In order to not affect the proof of convergence obtained
in
[
2
]
, two requirements need to be considered. 1. We
require a minimum distance between the obstacles of
\|o_{i}-o_{j}\|>4\delta+2\delta^{o},\,\forall i\neq j
, in order to avoid the presence of
narrow hallways that can generate deadlock issues (left hand side of
Figure
4
). This is a consequence of the results
in
[
2
]
. In fact, static obstacles can be thought as robots
that reached their goal and have a value of
\beta_{i}\rightarrow 0
. However,
from
[
2
]
, by design this is not possible if the robots are
at a distance from their goal smaller than
d_{2}
. Since
d_{2}>2\delta
and

\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|>\Delta_{ij}
, then the condition
\|o_{i}-o_{j}\|>4\delta+2\delta^{o},\,\forall i\neq j
is equivalent. 2. We require that the
maximum radius of the obstacles is
\delta^{o}<\Gamma_{ij}/2-2\delta
, in
order to avoid strict proximity maintenance constraints (right hand side of
Figure
4
). By imposing these additional conditions, the
guarantees of convergence in
[
2
]
holds.

III-C

Dealing with dynamics and uncertainties

The real robot deployment requires to consider a set of additional issues, such
as 1. More complex dynamics of the robots must be applied, i.e.,
\dot{p}_{i}=u_{i}
(Assumption
4
) cannot be directly applied. 2. Tracking error,
which can be due to external disturbances or model mismatches, generating a
difference between the desired and the actual velocity, 3. Measurement
uncertainties, due to the limitations of the perception system providing an
estimate of the positions of other robots and obstacles with a certain error.

To account for the dynamic constraints of the robots, we synthesize the control
inputs through an MPC formulation (similarly
to
[
10
,
2
]
), which steers each
i
–th
robot towards the centroid position
c_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}
, by constraining the
trajectory to not exceed its
\mathcal{F}_{i}
cell. By doing that, we can
account for the robot dynamics without violating the safety and proximity
maintenance constraints. Hence, each robot
i
solves the following optimization
problem (for the sake of clarity we discard the
i
indexing),

\displaystyle\minimize_{{x}_{k},{u}_{k}}

\displaystyle\sum^{N_{t}}_{k=0}J({x}_{k},{u}_{k},c_{\mathcal{F}}),

(13a)

s.t.:

\displaystyle{x}_{k}=f({x}_{k-1},{u}_{k-1}),\,\,\,\forall k\in(1,N_{t}),

(13b)

\displaystyle\,{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{X},\,{u}_{k}\in\mathcal{U},\,\,\,\forall k\in(0,N_{t})

(13c)

\displaystyle p_{k}\in\mathcal{F},\,\,\,\forall k\in(0,N_{t})

(13d)

\displaystyle{x}_{0}={x}_{\textrm{init}},

(13e)

where
k
indicates the time index,
N_{t}

indicates the horizon length,
J
represents the cost function,
{x}_{k}
and

{u}_{k}
are the state and control inputs respectively,

f({x}_{k-1},{u}_{k-1})
indicates the dynamics of the robot,
{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{X},\,{u}_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
are the state and input constraints
respectively,
p_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
constrains the future positions of the
robot in its cell, and
{x}_{0}={x}_{\textrm{init}}
is the initial
condition.

Both the tracking errors and measurement uncertainties can be
accounted for by means of an adaptive strategy. In fact, it can be
achieved by adapting the value of
\beta_{i}
in Equation (
11
) in an
online fashion. An alternative approach would be reshaping the

\mathcal{F}_{i}
cell, similarly to
[
10
]
. However, this
method cannot be applied in our case, since, by following this approach, the
robot may fall out of its cell due to tracking error, and once it happens, both
the cell and the centroid position are no longer defined. Conversely, the value
of
\beta_{i}
defines the aggressiveness of the
i
–th robot. A smaller value
corresponds to a centroid position closer to the goal (and hence closer to the
boundaries of the
\mathcal{F}_{i}
cell, namely
\partial\mathcal{F}_{i}
), while
larger values of
\beta_{i}
push the centroid position as far as possible from
the boundaries of the cell
\partial\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Thus, managing errors and
uncertainties can be achieved by selecting a value of
\beta_{i}
such that the
centroid position stays at a controlled distance from the boundary of
the cell. This distance must be chosen in accordance with the maximum
positional tracking error and the maximum error in the neighbors’ position
estimates. More formally, it can be done by imposing a lower bound to
\beta_{i}

in (
11
) equal to

\beta^{\min}_{i}=\argmin_{\beta_{i}}\left(\|c_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}-p_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{i}}\|-d_{u}\right)^{2},

where
p_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{i}}
is the position of the closest point on the
contour of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, while
d_{u}
is a threshold value that has to be
tuned on the basis of the tracking errors and measurement uncertainties. For
the sake of clarity, we depicted three scenarios in Figure
5
.
Figure
5
a considers the tracking error; in this case the
value of
c_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}
is controlled to stay at least at a distance

d^{t}_{u}
from the boundary
\partial\mathcal{F}_{i}
. We indicate with

\mathcal{Z}_{i}
the region set where the
i
–th robot is allowed to go in the
next time step. Notice that the dimension of the region
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
can be
controlled by
d_{u}^{t}
. To be conservative, the value of
d_{u}^{t}
should be
determined experimentally as the maximum tracking error, and it can be set as a
function of the velocity. In Figure
5
b, we show the case
where we have measurement uncertainties. We illustrate both the
i
–th and the

j
–th robots’ positions and the ellipse of uncertainty
\mathcal{E}_{i,j}
.
In this case,
\mathcal{K}_{i}
indicates the region where the centroid

c_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}
has to be constrained to preserve safety. By relying on
geometric reasoning, depending on the measurement uncertainty, when

\left(1-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{p}}\right)\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|<k\sqrt{\lambda^{\max}}+\delta
, we can tune the parameter
d_{u}^{m}
as

d_{u}^{m}\geq\left|\left(1-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{p}}\right)\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|-\left({k\sqrt{\lambda^{\max}}}+\delta\right)\right|+\delta,

(14)

where
\lambda^{\max}
is the maximum eigenvalue of the covariance matrix
associated with the measurement and
k
is a scale factor that defines the
confidence ellipse (e.g.,
k\approx 2.795
for a confidence level of
95\%
).

From a practical point of view, the value of
d_{u}^{m}
may be set empirically to a
constant that satisfies inequality (
14
) in the worst-case
expected scenario.

In real-world equipments the uncertainties associated with the measurements are
far from being constant values. Hence, the user may instead set
d_{u}^{m}

dynamically, based on the current measurement uncertainties, which are reported
by the equipment together with the measurement means. One method to do this, is
to use the
restraining
method from
[
31
]
. This
technique offsets the set-points of the robots from an ideal target location
according to the probability distributions of relative pose observations.

On the other hand, to preserve proximity maintenance, if
\Gamma_{ij}-\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|\approx k\sqrt{\lambda^{\max}}
, we need to set
d_{u}^{m}\geq k\sqrt{\lambda^{\max}}
. Since this value may be quite high in practice, it is
sufficient to impose that if the mean value of the neighbor position is out of
the proximity area, then we can re-project its position to the closest point
inside the proximity area. In this way, we can effectively maintain the
proximity with the neighbors.

where
d_{u}=d_{u}^{m}+d_{u}^{t}
in Figure

Remark 1

(Tracking error and measurement uncertainties invariance
property)
.

Since we are adopting the same strategy to account for the tracking errors and
measurements uncertainty, we get the property of invariance. In practice, it
does not matter how
d_{u}^{m}
and
d_{u}^{t}
are chosen, what matters is the sum

d_{u}
, hence in the case of underestimation of one of the two terms, it is
compensated by the other.

Remark 2

(Convergence for the adaptive solution)
.

By relying on the adaptive
solution, while safety and proximity maintenance have been taken into account,
we lost the convergence guarantees. In fact, by relying on the adaptive
strategy that accounts for tracking error and measurement uncertainties, a new
sufficient condition is introduced, which strictly depends on
d_{u}
and

\epsilon_{o}
. In particular, given the minimum distance between the obstacles
in the environment being
d^{\min}_{o}
, in order to ensure the passage of the
robot between two obstacles, it can be shown through geometrical reasoning,
that we need to have
d_{u}<d^{\min}_{o}-\sqrt{2}(\delta_{i}+\delta^{o})
.
Notice that, if
d^{\min}_{o}<2\sqrt{2}(\delta_{i}+\delta^{o})
, the value
of
\epsilon_{o}
also plays a role. Hence, in this case, either the value of
d_{u}

should be selected in a less conservative way, or we need to select

\epsilon_{o}=1
. A situation where the cell is too small to allow the centroid
to be at the required distance from the boundary of the cell can also occur.
However, we claim that this is not an issue for safety nor for proximity
maintenance, since in these cases the robot velocity approaches zero. Such
situation is transitory and may occur in very crowded conditions or when the
robot has to maintain proximity with two or more robots which are close to the
maximum allowed distance
\Gamma_{ij}
.

V
Experimental Results

To validate our algorithm, we implemented it on real robotic platforms. We
tested the proposed approach in two different scenarios: in the field with
artificial obstacles, and in the forest. While in the former case we designed
the distances between the obstacles and we relied on GNSS signal, in the forest
we faced more challenging conditions both in terms of obstacle density, and in
terms of localization, since we did not rely on GNSS signal. We started in a
more controlled environment in order to have easier access to a ground truth
measurement of the positions of the robots to evaluate the algorithm, and also
for a higher repeatability of the experiment, because of the reduced complexity
of the obstacle distribution.

Obstacle detection

To detect the surrounding obstacles we relied on
the RPLiDAR A3. In order to have a robust and safer perception system, we
introduced a double layer of obstacles. The first layer of obstacles is given by
the occupancy map built online by Hector
[
33
]
, while the second
layer is given by the raw pointcloud provided by the RPLiDAR A3. Given this
information, we cluster the points on the basis of their spatial distribution.
Then we compute the closest point to the robot position for each cluster, in
order to estimate the position of the obstacles in the scene. By doing that, we
exploit both the reactiveness of the raw data coming from the LiDAR pointcloud,
and at the same time, the scan-matching based obstacle representation, which is
provided by the Hector mapping algorithm.

Relative localization

The relative positions of the neighboring robots are obtained by relying on the

UVDAR
(
UVDAR
) technology
[
34
]
. The sensor system operates by
transmission of modulated ultraviolet light from active on-board markers. These
markers consist of blinking ultraviolet LEDs and their signal is retrieved by
on-board cameras with tailored filters. Each of these markers is individually
identifiable according to its blinking signal, and retrieving a set of them
corresponding to a specific
UAV
(
UAV
) allows the system to estimate the

UAV
’s relative pose.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we presented a novel distributed algorithm to swarm reliably and
effectively in dense cluttered environments such as a forest, accounting for
tracking errors and measurements uncertainties. We provide sufficient conditions
for the theoretical guarantees of safety, proximity maintenance and convergence,
compare it with state of the art algorithms, and finally we validate our
