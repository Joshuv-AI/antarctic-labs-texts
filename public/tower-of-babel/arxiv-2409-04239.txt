Hidden chiral symmetry for kagome lattice and its analogs
Tomonari Mizoguchi, Yasuhiro Hatsugai

Abstract
Chiral symmetry plays an essential role in condensed matter physics. In tight-binding models, it is often attributed to bipartite lattice structures, and its typical consequence is the “particle-hole symmetric” band structures, that is, the positive and negative eigenenergies appear in a pairwise manner. In this work, we address the chiral symmetry for non-bipartite lattice models with flat bands. Our argument relies on what we call the molecular-orbital representation, by which we can guarantee the existence of the flat band. We show that the chiral symmetry is preserved for the non-flat bands when the molecular orbitals are normalized and divided into two sets within which they are non-overlapping. The chiral operator is constructed by the same molecular orbitals as the Hamiltonian. This results in the characteristic relation between the chiral operator and the Hamiltonian, which we call the Pythagoras relation. We present the examples of such models, i.e., the kagome lattice and its one-dimensional analog, and address the characteristics originating from the chiral symmetry, such as the emergence of the topological edge modes. We also show an example where the numbers of molecular orbitals in each set are different from each other, resulting in multiple flat bands with different energies.

Characterization of the band structures

Among various symmetries, chiral symmetry plays important roles in various condensed matter systems.
It becomes important especially

In such systems,

III
Hidden Chiral symmetry

We now turn to the main claim of this paper, that is, the chiral symmetry of
{H}
.
Clearly,
{H}
is a positive semi-definite matrix, hence it does not preserve the chiral symmetry in a standard manner.
However, it can preserve the hidden chiral symmetry when

\Psi
satisfies certain conditions.

Suppose that the MOs are classified into two classes,
namely, we call the MO of class A (B) when

I=1,\cdots,M_{A}

(
I=M_{A}+1,\cdots,M
);
for future use, we also define

M_{B}:=M-M_{A}
.
Then,
\Psi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=(\Psi_{\rm A},\Psi_{\rm B}).

(16)

Now, let us further suppose that the MOs are normalized,
and the MOs in the same class are non-overlapping.
See also appendix
A.1
.
In other words,
\Psi_{\rm A}

and
\Psi_{\rm B}

\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}_{\rm A}\Psi_{\rm A}={I}_{M_{A}},\hskip 1.0pt\Psi^{\dagger}_{\rm B}\Psi_{\rm B}={I}_{M_{B}},

(17)

Then, the overlap matrix

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi^{\dagger}_{\rm A}\\
\Psi^{\dagger}_{\rm B}\\
\end{pmatrix}(\Psi_{\rm A},\Psi_{\rm B})=\begin{pmatrix}{I}_{M_{A}}&D^{\dagger}\\
D&{I}_{M_{B}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(18)

\displaystyle\{h_{C},\gamma\}=0,

(19)

\displaystyle\gamma={\rm diag}\,(I_{M_{\rm A}},-I_{M_{\rm B}})=\begin{pmatrix}{I}_{M_{A}}&{O}_{M_{\rm{A}},M_{\rm{B}}}\\
{O}_{M_{\rm{B}},M_{\rm{A}}}&-{I}_{M_{B}},\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle h_{C}={\mathcal{O}}-{I}_{M}.

Here,

stands for the

and

.

Now, defining

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger},

(22)

\displaystyle H_{C}=H-{I}_{N}=\Psi\Psi^{\dagger}-{I}_{N},

(23)

\displaystyle\{H_{C},\Gamma\}=0.

(24)

One can explicitly check Eq. (

\displaystyle\{H_{C},\Gamma\}=

\displaystyle(\Psi\Psi^{\dagger}-{I}_{N})\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger}+\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger}(\Psi\Psi^{\dagger}-{I}_{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\left(\{\gamma,{\mathcal{O}}\}-2\gamma\right)\Psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\{\gamma,h_{C}\}\Psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

We further describe some of the important properties of the chiral operator

First,

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(\Psi_{A},\Psi_{B})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{M_{A}}&O\\

\Psi_{B}^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{A}\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}-\Psi_{B}\Psi_{B}^{\dagger}.

(30)

This leads to

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\,\Gamma

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\,(\Psi_{A}\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}-\Psi_{B}\Psi_{B}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\,(\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}\Psi_{A}-\Psi_{B}^{\dagger}\Psi_{B})

(31)

Second, the square of

\displaystyle=\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\gamma\Psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle{[}\Gamma,H_{C}^{2}{]}

\displaystyle=0,

(34)

and

\displaystyle{[}\Gamma^{2},H_{C}{]}

\displaystyle=0.

Also the Hermiticity of

\displaystyle-1\leq\

(36)

\displaystyle-1\leq\

where

VIII
Summary and discussions

We have presented the method of constructing the flat-band models that preserve the hidden chiral symmetry of the finite-energy modes.
Our construction relies on the MO representation, by which we can guarantee the existence of the zero-energy flat bands.
The main claim of this work is that
the MO models can preserve the chiral symmetry,
despite being defined on the non-bipartite lattice,
when the MOs satisfy the “separatable” and “non-overlapping” conditions.
Importantly, the chiral operator
\Gamma

for the present model has the distinctive features from the conventional ones, as the chiral operator itself is constructed by the same MOs as the Hamiltonian.
One of its manifestations is
the Pythagoras relation of Eq. (
33
),
which ties the eigenvalues of
H_{C}
with those of the
\Gamma
.
As a consequence of the chiral symmetry,
the band structures of the finite-energy bands
are particle-hole symmetric around
\varepsilon=1
.
We have presented two concrete examples, namely,
the modified saw-tooth lattice model and the kagome lattice model,
and have discussed their characteristic band structures
as well as the topological boundary modes.
We additionally show the example of the square-octagon lattice as an example of
M_{A}\neq M_{B}
.
The characteristics of this type of model is that there exist two flat bands with different energies,
one of which originates from the hidden chiral symmetry under the condisition of
M_{A}\neq M_{B}
.

Our construction applies to higher-dimensional systems, such as the pyrochlore lattice.
It also applies
to the random systems, too.
Actually, the random U(1) MO model which we have introduced in the previous work
[
24
]
is one of the examples of this class of model.
Further studies on the spatially non-uniform models,
such as revealing the characteristics of the localization phenomena, will be worth considering
in the future work.

As for the experimental realization,
the three examples we present in this paper consist of
finite-ranged hopping only,
but the hopping amplitude
and the on-site potentials are not independent.
Considering the fact that
the fine-tuning is needed,
we think that the electric circuit
[
43
,
44
]
will be a good platform of realizing the topological boundary modes shown in this paper.
