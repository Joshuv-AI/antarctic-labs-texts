Unveiling the Enigma: The Rare Non-Calcifying Variant of Calcifying Epithelial Odontogenic Tumor
Authors: L. Malathi, Radhika Sridharan, N Aravindha Babu, K. M. K. Masthan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCalcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT), first identified by Jens Jorgen Pindborg in 1955, is a rare developmental odontogenic tumor, accounting for approximately 0.4% of all such tumors and most commonly occurring in the third and fourth decades of life. A 63-year-old man presented with a painful swelling in the right lower face, which had progressively enlarged over four months. Clinical and radiological findings revealed a dentoalveolar abscess with an osteolytic lesion mimicking involving the mandibular ramus and an impacted tooth. Histopathological examination confirmed CEOT. This case is notable due to its unusual presentation, mimicking dental osteomyelitis and occurring in an older individual, emphasizing the need for thorough diagnostic evaluation in atypical cases.

INTRODUCTION

Calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT), also known as Pindborg tumor, is one of the rarest odontogenic tumors of benign epithelial origin, with incidence typically low accounting for about 1–2% of all odontogenic tumors. The characteristic features of CEOT include amyloid globules and Liesegang ring calcifications in the tumor tissue.[1] This report presents a non-calcifying variant of intraosseous CEOT in a 63-year-old man, initially referred for the treatment of a painful swelling with a unilocular radiolucent lesion in the right posterior mandible, which was initially diagnosed as osteomyelitis of the mandible. Non-calcifying odontogenic epithelial tumors are quite uncommon and exhibit a low rate of malignant transformation, with recurrence being significantly lower.[2] The tumor in this case consisted of loose fibrous connective tissue stroma, amorphous eosinophilic globules resembling amyloid, and small nests or strands of odontogenic epithelial cells. Pan-cytokeratins (AE1 and AE3) were detected positively in the tumor epithelial cells.

CASE REPORT

A 63-year-old man presented with a four-month history of painful swelling on the right lower side of his face, progressively increasing in size without secondary changes. The swelling involved the right posterior mandible with buccal obliteration, an impacted tooth (48), a periapical lesion at tooth 47, and infected root stumps at teeth 44, 45, and 46. Extraoral swelling was also noted. Clinically, a differential diagnosis included a dentoalveolar abscess associated with tooth 47, root stumps at 45 and 46, and osteomyelitis of the right mandible. An orthopantomogram (OPG) revealed unilocular radiolucency at tooth 48, periapical radiolucency at tooth 47, and infection at root stumps 44, 45, and 46 [Figure 1a]. A CT scan showed a diffuse osteolytic lesion involving the posterior ramus of the mandible, with buccal cortical plate breach and mild involvement of the lingual cortex [Figure 1b]. Radiographically, differential diagnoses included osteomyelitis, dentigerous cyst, odontogenic keratocyst (OKC), and unicystic ameloblastoma. Following routine hematological investigations, an incisional biopsy was performed for further evaluation.

Histopathological examination revealed polyhedral epithelial cells scattered within a fibrous stroma, arranged in sheets [Figure 1c]. These sheets of polyhedral cells were interspersed with eosinophilic material and areas of irregular calcification [Figure 1e]. High-power magnification showed nuclear and cellular pleomorphism within the tumor cells [Figure 1d]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated diffuse strong positivity for Pan CK in the tumor cells, while markers, such as CK7, S100, vimentin, CK19, and desmin, were negative, supporting the diagnosis. Based on these findings, an initial diagnosis of calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT)—non-calcifying type was made [Figure 1f].

Hemimandibulectomy measuring 6 cm × 8 cm × 4.5 cm was performed, along with resection of the submandibular lymph nodes. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed the initial diagnosis from the incisional biopsy, with the lymph nodes showing reactive changes without evidence of malignancy.

DISCUSSION

As per the WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumors (2022), calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT) is classified into three subtypes: clear cell CEOT, cystic/microcystic CEOT, and non-calcifying/Langerhans cell-rich CEOT. The non-calcifying variant of calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT) is an exceedingly rare subtype of this already uncommon tumor.[3] Unlike the classical form of CEOT, which is characterized by calcifications and amyloid-like deposits, the non-calcifying variant lacks these diagnostic features, making it more difficult to identify both radiographically and histologically.[4] As a result, it can be misdiagnosed as other odontogenic lesions.

Clinically, the presentation of non-calcifying CEOT is similar to the classic variant, typically manifesting as a slow-growing, painless swelling of the jaw.[5] However, radiographically, the absence of calcifications can lead to a purely radiolucent appearance, making the lesion more easily confused with entities, such as ameloblastoma, odontogenic keratocyst (OKC), or even fibro-osseous lesions.[6] In the present case, this diagnostic ambiguity was compounded by the patient’s age and the lack of the classical calcifications seen in conventional CEOT. The absence of calcifications in non-calcifying CEOT may suggest different biological behavior compared to the classic variant, which is more calcified. This lack of calcification can complicate diagnosis and lead to misinterpretation as other lesions, such as ameloblastomas or odontogenic keratocysts. Consequently, purely radiolucent lesions might be more aggressive or diagnosed later, resulting in potential delays in treatment. While the absence of calcifications does not directly indicate a worse prognosis, it can influence management and outcomes due to the timing of diagnosis.

The lack of calcification in the non-calcifying variant of calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor may be due to genetic mutations affecting mineralization, local microenvironmental factors inhibiting calcification, and the presence of inhibitory proteins that prevent calcium deposition. Additionally, altered cellular differentiation and chronic inflammation could further impede the mineralization process.

Histologically, the non-calcifying variant retains the presence of polyhedral epithelial cells arranged in sheets, with eosinophilic amyloid-like material, but it lacks the characteristic calcifications, such as Liesegang rings.[7] Importantly, the absence of Langerhans cells in this case is notable, as these cells are sometimes observed in CEOT but are not consistently present. Their absence here further narrows the differential diagnosis, especially in combination with other histological findings.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis. In this case, Pan CK positivity confirmed the epithelial origin of the lesion, while negativity for CK7, S100, vimentin, CK19, and desmin excluded other potential diagnoses, such as ameloblastoma and Langerhans cell histiocytosis.[8] The absence of S100 staining, in particular, helped rule out lesions involving Langerhans cells, reinforcing the diagnosis of non-calcifying CEOT.

Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for non-calcifying CEOT, as with the classical variant. However, the lack of calcifications can make complete excision harder to assess intraoperatively.[8] Despite this challenge, the recurrence rate for non-calcifying CEOT remains low when excision is adequate. Long-term follow-up is important to watch for recurrence, though the prognosis is typically positive after complete resection. In our case, the follow-up duration was under one year.[9]

CONCLUSION

Pathology is the art of seeing what others miss; training the eye transforms microscopic mysteries into diagnostic clarity. This case highlights the importance of considering non-calcifying CEOT in the differential diagnosis of purely radiolucent mandibular lesions. A careful combination of histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and clinical correlation is critical to arrive at an accurate diagnosis, particularly when the classic features of CEOT are absent.[10]
