A direct approach to soft and hard edge universality for random normal matrices
Joakim Cronvall and Aron Wennman

Abstract
. We develop a unified approach to universality of local scaling limits for eigenvalues of random normal matrices, or equivalently for planar Coulomb gases at inverse temperature β = 2 \beta=2 . The approach is direct in that it does not rely on expressing the kernels in terms of orthogonal polynomials. There are three main results. The first is a proof of universality at hard edges with no symmetry assumptions on either the potential or the hard edge. We also prove local universality at regular soft edges for droplets with several components, and lastly for soft/hard edges where a hard edge perfectly aligns with the droplet boundary. The main ingredients are Paley-Wiener type spectral embeddings for the Hilbert space associated with a limiting kernel, and the construction of weighted polynomials peaking near a given boundary point.

1.
Introduction

A central theme in random matrix theory, going back to the work of Wigner

[
53
]
, is the universality of local eigenvalue statistics.
For unitary Hermitian ensembles this phenomenon is well understood: local
scaling limits in the bulk, at soft edges and at hard edges are governed
respectively by the
sine
,
Airy
and
Bessel kernels
, see

[
19
,
18
,
52
,
51
]
. This holds independently of the
choice of potential, provided that some mild regularity and non-criticality
assumptions are met. The sine and Airy kernels appear also for Hermitian
Wigner matrices with i.i.d. entries, see e.g.
[
47
,
46
,
24
,
26
]
. We refer to the surveys
[
36
,
20
,
25
]
for additional background and perspectives.

This paper concerns the universality of local eigenvalue statistics for a class of
non-Hermitian random matrices, specifically for the
normal matrix ensembles
.
These ensembles are natural non-Hermitian analogues of the classical unitary
matrix ensembles, and just like in the Hermitian case the eigenvalues form
Coulomb gases of charged particles. Universality of local scaling limits
has been established in the bulk
[
13
,
5
]

and at regular soft edges
[
33
]
. The roles of the sine
and Airy kernels are played by the
Ginibre kernel

(1.1)

G(\zeta,\eta)=e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}-\frac{1}{2}(|\zeta|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}

and the
Faddeeva kernel
, defined in terms of the complementary
error function
\operatorname{erfc}(z)
by

(1.2)

H(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{\zeta+\bar{\eta}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\,e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}-\frac{1}{2}(|\zeta|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}.

To the best of our knowledge, these kernels first appeared as random matrix
scaling limits in
[
29
]
and
[
27
]
, respectively.
We note in passing that the very same kernels (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
govern the local eigenvalue statistics of non-Hermitian Wigner-type random
matrices as well
[
48
,
17
,
15
]
.

The geometry of the support of the associated equilibrium measure (the
“droplet”
)
is richer in two dimensions than in one, making the edge behavior rather more
involved. Apart from a few case studies, the general theory is so far limited
to connected droplets with analytic boundary, and only soft edges have been explored
in generality. Edge universality has therefore remained open at hard edges, and also at
soft edges for disconnected (
“multi-cut”
) droplets. The goal of this paper is
to develop a unified approach that resolves these problems. A key feature of our
approach is that it is
direct
: it altogether avoids orthogonal
polynomials, which have proven difficult to analyze in these regimes. In the case
of the real line, several powerful direct approaches have appeared, see, e.g.,

[
9
,
23
,
22
,
41
,
39
]
and the references therein.
However, these rely essentially on the self-adjoint nature of the problem or
apply only to fixed measures. In particular, the limiting kernels that arise in these
settings are reproducing kernels for de Branges spaces, see e.g.
[
40
]
.

Our main results (Theorems
A
,
B
and

C
below) settle the question of universality of local
eigenvalue statistics at regular interfaces in the random normal matrix model,
including the previously inaccessible hard edges. The proofs moreover give a
conceptual description of the limiting kernels in terms of Hilbert spaces of
entire functions. We assume throughout that the weights and the interfaces are
real-analytic, but impose no symmetry conditions on either the potential
or the interface.

1.1.
Interface transitions in the random normal matrix model

We proceed to introduce the normal matrix model and explain how the various edge
regimes arise. Fix a real-analytic function
Q:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}

(the
“potential”
) subject to the growth bound

(1.3)

\liminf_{|z|\to+\infty}\frac{Q(z)}{\log|z|^{2}}>1,

and consider the random
n\times n
-matrix
M
picked from the probability
measure

(1.4)

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n}}e^{-n\mathrm{Tr}(Q(M))}dM,

where
dM
is the Riemannian volume form
on the (non-singular part of the) manifold of
normal

n\times n
-matrices
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is a normalizing constant. A remarkable but well-known fact
is that the eigenvalues of
M
follow a Coulomb gas law on the complex plane
with external field
Q(z)
and inverse temperature
\beta=2
. That is, the
joint eigenvalue density with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by

(1.5)

\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-nQ(z_{j})}

where
Z_{n}
is a (different) normalizing constant. As
n
tends to infinity,
the internal repulsive forces between the eigenvalues (or
“particles”
) are
counterbalanced by the external confinement and as a result the gas condensates
to a compact set called the
droplet
. To see what this set is, consider
the weighted logarithmic energy functional

(1.6)

I_{Q}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-w|}d\mu(z)d\mu(w)+\int_{\mathbb{C}}Q(z)d\mu(z).

The functional
I_{Q}(\mu)
admits a unique minimizer
\sigma=\sigma_{Q}
among
all probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
, which we refer to as the
(weighted)
equilibrium measure
. By classical potential theory, the equilibrium measure
is absolutely continuous with respect to normalized area measure

dA(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}dxdy
and its density is given by

\Delta Q(z)1_{{S}}
for some compact set
{S}
, see

[
43
,
31
]
(our Laplacian

\Delta=\partial\bar{\partial}
is a quarter of the usual one).
We then have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(z_{j})=\int_{{S}}f(z)\Delta Q(z)dA(z)

in probability for any bounded continuous function
f
. In fact, much stronger
estimates of large deviation type are known, see e.g.

[
31
,
28
,
37
,
11
]
.

A key feature of the eigenvalue process (
1.5
) is that it forms
a determinantal point process. Its
correlation kernel
, denoted
K_{n}(z,w)
,
is the reproducing kernel for the space of weighted polynomials

(1.7)

\mathcal{W}_{n}=\Big\{p(z)e^{-nQ(z)/2}:p\in\mathbb{C}[z],\,\deg p\leq n-1\Big\}

endowed with the Hilbert space structure of
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},dA)
. Denoting by

P_{0,n},P_{1,n},\ldots,P_{n-1,n}
the first
n
normalized orthogonal
polynomials in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},e^{-nQ}dA)
the kernel is given by

(1.8)

K_{n}(z,w)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}P_{j,n}(z)\overline{P_{j,n}(w)}\hskip 1.0pte^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z)+Q(w))}.

The condensation of the eigenvalues to the droplet is reflected by the weak convergence
of the one-point function

\frac{1}{n}K_{n}(z,z)\,dA\to d\sigma.

The interface
\partial{S}
is not
prescribed, but emerges naturally as a free boundary in the associated weighted potential
theory problem. When
Q
is strictly subharmonic near the droplet boundary,
there is a sharp density transition across the interface
\partial{S}
which is smooth if
we blow up at scale
n^{-1/2}
. Such interfaces are referred to as
soft edges
. By now
it is well understood that this soft-edge transition is governed by a universal scaling limit
involving the complementary Gaussian error function. More precisely, if
z_{0}\in\partial{S}

is a regular boundary point with outward unit normal
e^{i\theta}
, then for some
cocycles

c_{n}
it should hold that

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\right)c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)\to H(\zeta,\eta),

where
H
is the kernel in (
1.2
).
This was initially observed for the Ginibre ensemble
[
27
]
,
then for other special potentials for which the
orthogonal polynomials can be analyzed using e.g. Riemann-Hilbert methods

[
38
,
10
]
and under an priori translation-invariance
assumption on the limiting kernel
[
7
]
. A first general universality result
for soft edges was obtained in
[
33
]
assuming that

\partial{S}
is a single, regular Jordan curve. This made use of a detailed
asymptotic expansion of the associated orthogonal polynomials
obtained in the same paper.

Hard edges
are interfaces that arise when the eigenvalues are
forbidden from occupying a given region
\Omega
, see Figure
1

for an illustration. The hard-edge constraint corresponds to orthogonal polynomials
and reproducing kernels with respect to the truncated area measure

1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}e^{-nQ}dA
. This type of interface transition is less understood,
and approaching it via orthogonal polynomials seems considerably more difficult than
in the soft-edge case. Indeed, the local hard-edge scaling limits are known only
under radial symmetry constraints
[
44
,
3
,
45
,
54
]

or for Coulomb gases associated with classical Bergman spaces with fixed weights

[
42
,
50
]
. The reason for the difficulties
appears to be that the orthogonal polynomials can have a very complicated behavior in
the presence of hard edges. In particular this is so for varying exponential weights
and degrees
k=\tau n
such that
\partial\Omega
intersects the boundary of the
associated
\tau
-droplet
S_{\tau}
(cf.
[
33
]
*p. 316).
Contrary to the bulk and soft-edge cases the natural scaling near a hard edge is
n^{-1}
.
The kernel appearing in
[
42
]
is

(1.9)

B(\zeta,\eta)=\int_{0}^{1}te^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}dt,

and this should be a natural candidate for a universal
hard-edge kernel.

1.2.
Main results

Figure 1.
Illustration of the Ginibre ensemble (
n=2000
) with
a hard edge along an interior
“hole”

\Omega
. The outer edge
is a regular soft edge.

We make the following standing assumptions. Let
Q
be a real-analytic potential
subject to the logarithmic growth bound (
1.3
) and denote by

S=\operatorname{supp}\sigma_{Q}
the associated droplet. We assume throughout
that
Q
is strictly subharmonic in a neighborhood of
S
, and that

S
consists of a finite number of finitely connected domains with analytic boundaries.
Standard potential theoretic considerations imply that there exists a
constant
F_{\sigma}
for which we have
Q+2U^{\sigma}\geq F_{\sigma}

with equality on
S=\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)
. We assume that
strict

inequality holds away from the droplet.

Denote by
\Omega
a Jordan domain with real-analytic boundary, and condition the
Coulomb gas of eigenvalues (
1.5
) on the event that no particle
lies in
\Omega
. In this way, we obtain a new determinantal point process whose
correlation kernel
K_{n}^{\Omega}(z,w)
is the reproducing kernel for the space

\mathcal{W}_{n}
of weighted polynomials with the inner product of

L^{2}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega,dA)
. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

Macroscopically, the gas is governed by a constrained equilibrium measure which minimizes the
weighted logarithmic energy
I_{Q}(\mu)
from (
1.6
) over all probability measures
with
\mu(\Omega)=0
. The constrained equilibrium measure is known to take the form

\sigma_{\Omega}=\sigma|_{{S}\setminus\Omega}+\mathrm{Bal}(\sigma|_{\Omega},\partial\Omega)

where
\sigma=\sigma_{Q}
is the unconstrained equilibrium measure and

\mathrm{Bal}(\nu,\partial\Omega)
denotes the
balayage
of a measure

\nu
on
\Omega
to
\partial\Omega
, see Section
2

below and
[
8
,
1
,
16
]
for details.
The balayage measure is absolutely continuous with respect to
arc-length on
\partial\Omega
, and we denote its
density by
\rho=\rho_{\Omega}
. For a point
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
we denote by
e^{i\theta}
the outer unit normal to
\partial\Omega

at
z_{0}
, pointing into
{S}
.

Theorem A

(Hard edges)
.

Assume that
\partial\Omega
is a real-analytic Jordan curve,
compactly contained in
\operatorname{Int}(S)
and let
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
Then there exists a cocycle
c(\zeta,\eta)
such that, locally uniformly for

\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{C}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(z_{0})^{2}n^{2}}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}^{\Omega}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\rho(z_{0})n},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\rho(z_{0})n}\right)c(\zeta,\eta)=B(\zeta,\eta),

where
B
is the Hermitian-entire kernel

B(\zeta,\eta)=\int_{0}^{1}te^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}dt.

In other words, the hard-edge kernels that appeared in

[
42
]
for classical Bergman spaces are universal
also for hard-edge scaling limits in the normal matrix model, i.e.,
for varying exponential weights. The locally uniform convergence of
the rescaled kernels shows that the corresponding
rescaled determinantal point processes

converge in law to the unique determinantal point field governed by the kernel

B(\zeta,\eta)
.
The fact that the kernel depends
only on
\zeta+\bar{\eta}
shows that the limiting
point process at the edge is translation-invariant in
the vertical (tangential) direction.

The kernel
B
is the reproducing kernel for a rather curious space

\mathcal{A}
of entire functions. It is the closed subspace of the Bergman
space on the right half-plane consisting of entire functions subject to the
growth constraint

|f(\zeta)|\leq Ce^{|\re\zeta|},\qquad\re(\zeta)\leq 0.

The Bergman space on the right half-plane coincides with the image of

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})
under the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
.
In fact,
\mathcal{L}
is an isometric isomorphism between the two spaces.
The subspace
\mathcal{A}
instead consists of all functions

\mathcal{L}[F]
with
F\in L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})
.

The direct framework allows us to analyze a wide range of boundary
confinements in a unified way. We also prove the following strengthening
of the universality result in
[
33
]

which allows for droplets with several components.

Theorem B

(Soft edges)
.

Assume that
\partial S
consists of finitely many real-analytic Jordan
curves, and let
z_{0}\in\partial S
with outer unit normal
e^{i\theta}
.
Then there exist cocycles
c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
such that, locally uniformly
for
\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\right)c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=H(\zeta,\eta)

where
H
is the Faddeeva kernel
\Phi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})\,G(\zeta,\eta)
, where

(1.10)

\Phi(z)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erfc}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt.

The holomorphic Faddeeva kernel
{\bf H}(\zeta,\eta)=\Phi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})\,e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}

is the reproducing kernel for a subspace
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
of the Fock space
consisting of functions
f(\zeta)
subject to the growth bound

|f(\zeta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C,\qquad\re(\zeta)\geq 0,

see
[
30
,
7
]
. The Fock space is the isometric image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

under the Bargmann transform
\mathcal{B}
and, in analogy with the hard-edge space, the subspace

\mathcal{H}
consists of functions with restricted Fourier support. More
precisely we have that

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},dt))
.

We return to the Coulomb gas conditioned on the event that
no particle lies in
\Omega
.
The case when
\Omega
is precisely aligned with the
droplet, i.e.,
\Omega=S^{c}
, is a critical case which sits
between the soft and the hard-edge cases. We refer
to this regime as the
soft/hard edge regime
.
This appears to have been introduced by Jancovici
[
35
]
,
and several results on this model have
appeared recently, see
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
44
]
.
However, all results to date have either relied on radial symmetry
or an a priori assumption of tangential translation-invariance of the limiting kernel.

Theorem C

(Soft/hard edges)
.

Assume that
\partial S
consists of finitely many real-analytic Jordan curves,
let
\Omega=S^{c}
, and fix
z_{0}\in\partial S
. Then there exist cocycles

c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
such that, locally uniformly for
\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}^{\Omega}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\right)c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=H_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\eta),

where
H_{\mathbb{L}}
is the kernel
\Psi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})\,G(\zeta,\eta)
, where

(1.11)

\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{e^{-(t-z)^{2}/2}}{\Phi(t)}dt,

and
\Phi(t)
is the free boundary function (
1.10
).

The limiting kernel is reproducing for a Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}
of entire functions,
which coincides with the Faddeeva kernel space

\mathcal{H}\subset F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
as sets but with the norm restricted to
the left half-plane
\mathbb{L}
.

1.3.
Outline of the main idea

We illustrate the main new ideas in the soft-edge case.
The overall approach remains the same in the three cases,
but the exact arguments differ between them.
Fix a point
z_{0}\in\partial S
and denote by
e^{i\theta}

the outward unit normal to
S
. Put

k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\right).

It was shown in
[
7
]
that there
exist cocycles
\{c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)\}_{n}
such that

\{c_{n}\cdot k_{n}\}_{n}
is a normal family. Any
(subsequential) limit point has the structure

{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\zeta|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}

where
{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)
is a Hermitian-entire kernel
function belonging to the Fock space
F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
of square-integrable entire
functions with respect to the standard Gaussian
measure on
\mathbb{C}
.
What remains is to establish that
{\bf K}
is the Faddeeva kernel

{\bf H}(\zeta,\eta)=\Phi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})\,e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}.

Since Hermitian-entire kernels are determined by their diagonal restriction
by polarization, it is enough to verify that
{\bf K}(\zeta,\zeta)={\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)
,
which will be done by verifying the complementary inequalities

{\bf K}(\zeta,\zeta)\geq{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)
and

{\bf K}(\zeta,\zeta)\leq{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)
.

Step 1.
(Spectral embedding)  A version of the
Bernstein-Walsh inequality asserts that

K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cne^{-n(Q-\check{Q})(z)},

where
\check{Q}(z)=-2U^{\sigma}(z)+F_{\sigma}
for some constant
F_{\sigma}
,
chosen such that
\check{Q}=Q
on the droplet
S
. This shows
in particular that
K_{n}(z,z)
decays rapidly outside the droplet.
By considering the local behavior of
Q-\check{Q}
near the
boundary of
S
, one gathers that

(1.12)

|{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C,\qquad\re(\zeta)\geq 0.

The key observation here is that (
1.12
)
together with the Paley-Wiener-Schwartz theorem
(Theorem
2.6
) implies that the entire function

{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)\,e^{\zeta^{2}/2}
is the Fourier-Laplace
transform of a distribution supported on the negative half-line.

The Fock space is known to be the isometric image of

L^{2}(\mathbb{R},dt)
under the Bargmann transform, defined by

\mathcal{B}[F](\zeta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\zeta t}e^{-\zeta^{2}/2}e^{-t^{2}/4}F(t)dt.

Moreover, the Faddeeva kernel
{\bf H}(\zeta,\eta)

is the reproducing kernel for the subspace

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},dt))

of
F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, see
[
30
]
*Proposition 5.4.
An inspection reveals that the Bargmann transform is a Fourier-Laplace transform
followed by multiplication by
e^{-\zeta^{2}/2}
.
We thus have two representations of
{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)\,e^{\zeta^{2}/2}
as
Fourier transforms, and by Fourier inversion they have to agree. We may thus
conclude that
F
is in fact supported on the negative half-line,
and hence
{\bf K}(\cdot,\eta)\in\mathcal{H}
. This is what we refer to as
the
spectral embedding
.

An argument combining the reproducing property of
K_{n}(z,w)
with
Fatou’s lemma shows that the limiting kernel
{\bf K}(\cdot,\eta)
satisfies the
norm-bound

(1.13)

\int_{\mathbb{C}}|{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)\leq{\bf K}(\eta,\eta).

Now, the reproducing kernel
{\bf H}
of
\mathcal{H}
is
extremal

in the sense that

{\bf H}(\eta,\eta)\geq\frac{|f(\eta)|^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)},\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}.

Applying this with
f={\bf K}(\cdot,\eta)\in\mathcal{H}

together with the norm-bound (
1.13
),
we obtain the desired inequality

{\bf H}(\eta,\eta)\geq\frac{{\bf K}(\eta,\eta)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}|{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)}\geq{\bf K}(\eta,\eta).

Step 1 shares some similarities with the
approach of Levin and Lubinsky in
[
39
]
.
However, the function theory is quite different without the
symmetries of the real line. The compactness argument and the bound
(
1.13
) are taken from
[
7
]

in the soft-edge case.

Step 2.
(Construction of boundary peak functions)  The complementary
lower bound will again be based on the extremal property of the reproducing kernel,
but this time applied for finite
n
. That is, we will use that

(1.14)

K_{n}(z,z)\geq\frac{|p(z)|^{2}e^{-nQ(z)}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}|p(w)|^{2}e^{-nQ(w)}dA(w)}

whenever
p
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
. We will show how to construct good

trial kernels

K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,w)
lying exponentially close to
\mathcal{W}_{n}
,
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

The trial kernel approximates the desired error
function kernel
{\bf H}
: for
\zeta
fixed,

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}^{\sharp}\left(z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\right)={\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}+o(1).

(ii)

With
w=z_{0}+e^{i\theta}\eta/\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}
and
\eta
fixed,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}dA(w)\leq{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}.

Together with the extremal property of
K_{n}
, these properties imply that

(1.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}(z,z)

\displaystyle\geq(1+o(1))\frac{{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)^{2}e^{-2|\zeta|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}dA(w)}

(1.16)

\displaystyle\geq(1+o(1)){\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}.

Combining this with the convergence of
\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}(z,z)

to
{\bf K}(\zeta,\zeta)e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}

the inequality

{\bf K}(\zeta,\zeta)\geq{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)

follows.

We comment briefly on a problem that arises in the construction of
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
,
cf. Section
2.2
and Section
3.3

below. Property (ii) requires the kernels to be
concentrated close to the point
w
, making them
boundary analogues

of Tian’s classical peak sections, cf.
[
49
]
. The construction of peak sections typically
uses localization with
\bar{\partial}
-estimates, which break down at boundary points.
We instead construct the trial kernels as sums

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)=\sum_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}q_{j,n}(z)\overline{q_{j,n}(w)}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z)+Q(w))},

where the individual wave functions
q_{j,n}e^{-nQ(z)/2}
peak along
certain curves close to the interface and have rapidly oscillating arguments.
The oscillations cause cancellations in the sum for
z\neq w
, resulting in the off-diagonal
decay of
|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}
needed for Property (ii). This type of cancellation was
observed in
[
4
]
for the actual kernel
K_{n}(z,w)
, where it was used to derive
off-diagonal asymptotics for
z,w
near
\partial S
.

One recurring problem in our construction of the polynomials
q_{k,n}
is the
following. We are given a domain
\Omega
, either bounded or unbounded, whose
boundary is a real-analytic Jordan curve in
S
. For definiteness, let’s consider
the unbounded case. To carry out our construction, one would naively like to find a
function
u
of prescribed logarithmic growth such that

(1.17)

\Delta u=0\;\text{ on }\;\Omega\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(u-Q)=\nabla(u-H)=0\;\text{ on }\;\partial\Omega,

where
H
is a given real-analytic function. The function
u

would play a role similar to the function
\breve{Q}_{\tau}
in the construction of
the
“quasipolynomials”
in
[
33
]
*Section 4.1.
However, (
1.17
) is over-determined and can
only be solved for very particular choices of

\Omega
and
H
. (For instance, if
H=Q

and
\Omega
is the complement of a
\tau
-droplet
then we get that
u=\breve{Q}_{\tau}
.)
To overcome this, we can give ourselves some additional freedom by instead considering

\Delta u=\nu\quad\text{on }\Omega,\qquad(u-Q)=\nabla(u-H)=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\nu
is an absolutely continuous measure with smooth positive density on
a Jordan curve
\Lambda\subset\Omega
. Choosing
\Lambda
and
\nu
appropriately,
the problem admits a solution
u
.
This subharmonic function is no longer the real part of
a holomorphic function in
\Omega
, but by discretizing
its Riesz mass we can still find a holomorphic
function
f
, having a
“
necklace
”
of tightly packed
zeros along
\Lambda
,
such that
|f|^{2}e^{-nQ}\sim e^{-n(Q-u)}
with
acceptable error.

In the hard-edge case the construction is used to find weighted polynomials
which peak along
\partial\Omega
with a
prescribed decay rate in the normal direction into
S
.
In the soft and soft/hard-edge cases, we use the same construction to find
weighted polynomials which peak only along one of the components of
\partial S
.

We stress that the polynomials used in the construction of
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)

have nothing to do with the orthogonal polynomials

1

A somewhat similar
flexibility was used in
[
14
,
32
]
.
,
and in fact do not satisfy any orthogonality conditions.

2.
Hard edges

Let
Q
be a real-analytic potential with droplet
S
,
subject to the standing assumptions of Section
1.2
.
We take a simply connected domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
(bounded or unbounded)
with real-analytic boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
compactly
contained in
\operatorname{Int}(S)
. We consider the restricted
Coulomb gas where the particles are forbidden from being in the domain
\Omega
.
There are two cases: either
\Omega
is bounded and compactly contained in
S
,
or
\Omega
is unbounded and the complement
S^{c}
is contained
in
\Omega
. We will give the proof of Theorem
A

in the case when
\Omega
is bounded with
\Omega\subset S
. The modifications
needed for the unbounded case will hopefully be clear from the soft-edge discussion
which deals with that setting explicitly.

The correlation kernel
K_{n}^{\Omega}(z,w)
is given by

K_{n}^{\Omega}(z,w)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}P_{j,n}^{\Omega}(z)\overline{P_{j,n}^{\Omega}(w)}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z)+Q(w))},

where
\{P_{k,n}^{\Omega}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
are the orthonormal
polynomials with respect to the weighted area
measure
1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(z)e^{-nQ(z)}dA(z)
.
In other words
K_{n}^{\Omega}
is the reproducing kernel
of the space
\mathcal{W}_{n}
of weighted polynomials as
a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega,dA)
.
When no confusion should arise, we sometimes omit the
superscript
\Omega
and write
K_{n}(z,w)
for the kernel.

The following result about the restricted equilibrium potential
is well known from potential theory.

Theorem 2.1
.

The equilibrium measure
\sigma_{\Omega}
in potential
Q
restricted to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega

is given by

(2.1)

d\sigma_{\Omega}(z)=\Delta Q(z)1_{S\setminus\Omega}(z)dA(z)+d\mu(z),

where
\mu
is the Balayage measure of
\Delta Q(z)1_{\Omega}(z)dA(z)
onto
\Gamma
.
Moreover,
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
arc-length measure on
\Gamma
with strictly positive density
\rho
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma
is the equilibrium measure on
\mathbb{C}
in the potential
Q
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle 2U^{\sigma}(z)+Q(z)=F_{\sigma},\quad z\in\text{supp}\,\sigma,

(2.3)

\displaystyle 2U^{\sigma}(z)+Q(z)\geq F_{\sigma},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

for a constant
F_{\sigma}
. From the definition of the Balayage measure we have

U^{\sigma}(z)=U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega,

and

U^{\sigma}(z)\geq U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

We get that

\displaystyle 2U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}(z)+Q(z)

\displaystyle=F_{\sigma},\quad z\in\text{supp}\,\sigma\setminus\Omega,

\displaystyle 2U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}(z)+Q(z)

\displaystyle\geq F_{\sigma},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

which by
[
43
]
*Theorem 3.3 implies that
\sigma_{\Omega}
is the restricted
equilibrium measure in the potential
Q
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
.

To prove that the density
\rho
is strictly positive we use a representation of the
Balayage measure in terms of the Green’s function
g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)
on
\Omega
.
The Green’s function on
\Omega
is the harmonic function in
\Omega\setminus\{\zeta\}

such that
g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)=0
for
z\in\partial\Omega
and
g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)=\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
z\to\zeta
.

For a measure
\nu
on
\Omega
we can represent the Balayage measure
\widehat{\nu}

of
\nu
onto
\Gamma
as

\frac{d\widehat{\nu}}{ds}(z)=-\int\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)\,d\nu(\zeta),

where
\bf{n}
is the outward unit normal of
\Omega
and
ds
denotes the
arc-length measure on
\Gamma

normalized by
\frac{1}{2\pi}
.

The curve
\Gamma
is real-analytic so by the Hopf lemma the normal derivative of the
Green’s function is strictly negative on
\Gamma
. In our case
d\nu=1_{\Omega}\Delta QdA
.
By assumption
Q
is strictly subharmonic on
\Omega
and we get that the density
\rho
is also
strictly positive.
∎

There is a constant
F_{\sigma_{\Omega}}
such that the function

\check{Q}(z)=F_{\sigma_{\Omega}}-2U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}(z),

is the largest subharmonic minorant of
Q
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
with growth at most

\log|z|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
|z|\to\infty
. The function
\check{Q}
is harmonic in
\Omega

and we write
V
for the harmonic extension of
\check{Q}
across
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{V}

for the analytic function with real part
V
which can also be defined in a neighborhood of
\Omega
.
The imaginary part is uniquely defined up to a constant, which will not play any role for us.

We can recover the density
\rho
from the function
V
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\partial_{\bf{n}}
denote the normal
derivative in the outward direction of
\Omega
. We have that

\partial_{\bf{n}}(V(z)-Q(z))=-2\rho(z),\quad z\in\Gamma.

Proof.

From basic potential theory it follows that

\rho(z)=-\Big(\frac{\partial U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}}{\partial\bf{n}_{e}}(z)+\frac{\partial U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}}{\partial\bf{n}_{i}}(z)\Big),

where
\bf{n}_{e}
and
\bf{n}_{i}
are the exterior and interior unit normals of
\Omega
,
respectively. The lemma now follows from the identities

\frac{\partial U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}}{\partial\bf{n}_{i}}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\bf{n}_{i}}(z),\quad\quad\frac{\partial U^{\sigma_{\Omega}}}{\partial\bf{n}_{e}}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\bf{n}_{e}}(z).\qed

2.1.
Limiting kernels and the upper bound

We need some properties of the correlation kernel
K_{n}(z,w)=K_{n}^{\Omega}(z,w)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(2.4)

K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cn^{2}e^{n(\check{Q}(z)-Q(z))}.

In particular, the kernel
K_{n}(z,z)
satisfies

K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cn^{2},

for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
.

Remark 2.4
.

The lemma only gives a good estimate close to the domain
\Omega
.
In fact for any interior point of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega

with
{\rm d}(z,\Omega)\geq\delta n^{-1/2}\log n
,
\delta>0
, we have the stronger estimate

K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cn,

with possibly a different constant.

Proof.

Take a curve
\widetilde{\Gamma}
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
at distance
\frac{1}{n}

from
\Gamma
. Around any point on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
we can then take a disc of
radius proportional to
\frac{1}{n}
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
.
A standard subharmonicity estimate (cf.
[
7
]
*Lemma 3.1) then
gives that
K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cn^{2}
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
.

Replace
Q
by
Q_{h}
, the harmonic function on the domain
\operatorname{Int}(\widetilde{\Gamma})

interior to
\widetilde{\Gamma}
which equals
Q

on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. We then have that

\log K_{n}(z,z)+nQ(z)-nQ_{h}(z),

is a subharmonic function bounded by
\log(Cn^{2})
on the curve
\widetilde{\Gamma}
.
From the maximum principle we get that the same bound holds on
\textrm{Int}(\widetilde{\Gamma})

and in particular on
\Gamma
. It only remains to estimate
n(Q-Q_{h})
. Since the distance between
the two curves is only
\frac{1}{n}
and since
Q-Q_{h}
is smooth, this will only contribute with a constant.

The second part of the theorem is a direct consequence of the definition of
\check{Q}
.
∎

We consider the rescaled kernel

k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{n^{2}\rho(z_{0})^{2}}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}(z_{n},w_{n}),

where the points
z_{n}
and
w_{n}
are defined by

(2.5)

z_{n}=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{n\rho(z_{0})},\quad w_{n}=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{n\rho(z_{0})}.

As in the statement of Theorem
A
we let
e^{i\theta}

denote the outer unit normal to
\Omega
at
z_{0}
.

Lemma
2.3
contains all the information we need about
K_{n}(z,w)
to
prove the following compactness result for the sequence of rescaled kernels. It gives
direct hard-edge analogues of the
compactness
,

mass-one inequality
and
a priori estimates

from
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

There exists a cocycle
c
such that
(c\cdot k_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}

is a normal family. Furthermore, any limit point
K(\zeta,\eta)
is a Hermitian-entire function
satisfying the
“mass-one inequality”

\int\limits_{\mathbb{H}}|K(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)\leq K(\zeta,\zeta)

and

(2.6)

|K(\zeta,\eta)|\leq C_{\eta}\,e^{|\re\,\zeta|},\quad\re\zeta\leq 0.

Proof.

Define the function
A_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
by

A_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w_{n})})}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z_{n})+Q(w_{n}))},

where
\mathcal{V}
is the holomorphic function defined above whose real
part equals
V
on a neighborhood of
\Omega
.
Recall from Lemma
2.2
that we have

\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}(V-Q)(z_{0})=-2\rho(z_{0}),

and hence

\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w_{n})}-Q(z_{n})-Q(w_{n}))=-(\zeta+\bar{\eta})+ig(\zeta,\eta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big),

where
g
is the cocycle given by

g(\zeta,\eta)=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\im(\mathcal{V}(z_{0}))\frac{\re(\zeta-\eta)}{2\rho(z_{0})}+\partial_{\mathbf{n}}Q(z_{0})\frac{\im(\zeta-\eta)}{2\rho(z_{0})}.

It follows that, with
c(\zeta,\eta)=e^{-ig(\zeta,\eta)}
, we have

c(\zeta,\eta)A_{n}(\zeta,\eta)\to e^{-(\zeta+\bar{\eta})},

locally uniformly as
n\to\infty
. Define the Hermitian-analytic function

\Psi_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}{A_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}.

We want to prove that
\{\Psi_{n}\}
is a normal family from which the first part of the theorem
would follow. From the reproducing property of
K_{n}
we have

|k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}\leq k_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)k_{n}(\eta,\eta)
. We obtain

|\Psi_{n}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}\leq C\frac{k_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)k_{n}(\eta,\eta)}{|e^{-(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}|^{2}},

for
n
big enough. Using the bound (
2.4
) from Lemma
2.3
we
get that
\Psi_{n}
is locally uniformly bounded and hence a normal family. This proves the first
part of the theorem and we see that the cocycle is given by
c(\zeta,\eta)=e^{-ig(\zeta,\eta)}
.

In order to prove the properties of the limiting kernel we consider a subsequence
n_{k}
along
which
\Psi_{n_{k}}\to\Psi
for some Hermitian-entire function
\Psi
. It follows that along
the same subsequence
c(\zeta,\eta)\cdot k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
converges locally uniformly to
K(\zeta,\eta)
.

The reproducing property of
K_{n}(z,w)
gives that for any
z\in\mathbb{C}

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}|K_{n_{k}}(z,w)|^{2}dA(w)=K_{n_{k}}(z,z).

We choose
z=z_{n_{k}}
and make the change of variables

w=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{n_{k}\rho(z_{0})}
. We get

\int\limits_{H_{n_{k}}}|k_{n_{k}}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)\leq\frac{1}{(n_{k}\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}K_{n_{k}}(z_{n_{k}},z_{n_{k}}),

where
H_{n_{k}}
is the image of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
under the rescaling.
The indicator function of
H_{n_{k}}
converges to the indicator function
of
\mathbb{H}
, hence from Fatou’s lemma we have

\int\limits_{\mathbb{H}}|K(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)\leq K(\zeta,\zeta).

This proves the mass-one inequality. In order to prove the exponential growth
bound on the limiting kernel we make a Taylor expansion for
\re\zeta<0
,

n(\check{Q}-Q)(z_{n})=-2\re\zeta+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big).

From (
2.4
) we then get

\frac{1}{\rho(z_{0})^{2}n^{2}}K_{n}(z_{n},z_{n})\leq Ce^{-2\re\zeta}\big(1+o(1)\big),\quad\re\zeta\leq 0,

as
n\to\infty
.
From Cauchy-Schwarz we see that any limiting kernel
K
must satisfy

|K(\zeta,\eta)|\leq Ce^{-\re\zeta},\quad\re\zeta\leq 0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
2.5
leads us to consider the subspace
\mathcal{A}

of the Bergman space on the right half-plane consisting of entire functions
with the growth restriction

|f(\zeta)|\leq Ce^{|\re\zeta|},\quad\re(\zeta)\leq 0.

This space has a clear interpretation in terms of the Fourier transform, and
for this we will need a version of the classical Paley-Wiener-Schwartz theorem.
For a distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
such that
e^{t\im(\zeta)}u(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
we define the Fourier-Laplace transform at
\zeta
to be

\widehat{u}(\zeta)=\mathcal{F}[u](\zeta)=u_{x}(e^{-ix\zeta}).

The following result is a special case of Theorems 7.4.2 and 7.4.3 in

[
34
]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
U
is an entire function that for some
c\geq 0
satisfies

|U(t+i\eta)|\leq Ce^{c\eta},\quad\eta\geq 0,

then there exists a distribution
u
such that
e^{t\eta}u(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

for every
\eta\geq 0
and such that
u
has Fourier-Laplace transform
U(t+i\eta)

for every
\eta\geq 0
. In addition, the distribution
u
is supported on
(-\infty,c]
.

We now have the following result. Recall the definition of the kernel
B
from (
1.9
).

Theorem 2.7
.

The function
B_{\eta}(\zeta)=B(\zeta,\eta)
is the reproducing kernel of the the space
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Denote by
A^{2}(\mathbb{H})
the Bergman space on the right half-plane, that is, the space of
holomorphic functions in
\mathbb{H}
with finite norm

\|f\|^{2}_{A^{2}(\mathbb{H})}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{H}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}dA(\zeta)<\infty.

The Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{L}[F](\zeta)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F(t)e^{-t\zeta}dt,

is an isometric isomorphism
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})\to A^{2}(\mathbb{H})
,
cf.
[
21
]
. With the aid of Theorem
2.6
applied in both the right and
left half-planes, the growth restriction on the functions in
\mathcal{A}
implies that
any
f\in\mathcal{A}
can be written as a Fourier-Laplace transform

f(\zeta)=u_{x}\left(e^{-x\zeta}\right)

for some distribution
u

supported on
[0,1]
with
e^{-\re(\zeta)t}u(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for

\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, we have the representation
f=\mathcal{L}[F]

for some
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})
, and we claim that
F=u
so that

F
is in fact supported on
[0,1]
. Indeed, since
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})

it holds that
e^{-t\re(\zeta)}F(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
for
\re(\zeta)>0

and hence the claim follows by Fourier inversion in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Since it is moreover clear that
\mathcal{L}[F]\in\mathcal{A}
for any

F\in L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})
, it follows that the Laplace transform is
an isometric isomorphism from
L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})
onto the space
\mathcal{A}
.

It remains to show that
B
is the reproducing kernel
of
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}[L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})]
.
Let
L_{\eta}(\zeta)
be the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{A}
and consider

F\in L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})
. From the reproducing property of
L
we get

\big\langle\mathcal{L}[F],B_{\eta}\big\rangle_{A^{2}(\mathbb{H})}=\big\langle F,\mathcal{L}^{-1}[B_{\eta}]\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\frac{dt}{t})},

from which we see that

\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t)e^{-t\eta}dt=\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t)\overline{\mathcal{L}^{-1}[L_{\eta}](t)}\,\frac{dt}{t},

for all
F\in L^{2}([0,1],\frac{dt}{t})
. We can conclude that

\mathcal{L}^{-1}[L_{\eta}](t)=1_{[0,1]}(t)te^{-t\bar{\eta}}
.
Applying the transform
\mathcal{L}
to both sides gives

L(\zeta,\eta)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}te^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}dt=B(\zeta,\eta).\qed

From Theorem
2.5
we get the following corollary.

Corollary 2.8
.

Any limiting kernel
K_{\eta}(\zeta)
as in
Theorem
2.5
belongs to the space
\mathcal{A}
and satisfies the inequality

K(\zeta,\zeta)\leq B(\zeta,\zeta).

Proof.

Since
B
is the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{A}
we have

(2.7)

B(\zeta,\zeta)=\sup\limits_{f\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|f(\zeta)|^{2}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}

and since from Theorem
2.5
we have

\|K_{\zeta}\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\leq K(\zeta,\zeta)
we get the inequality
by applying the extremal property (
2.7
) with
f=K(\cdot,\zeta)
.
∎

2.2.
Approximate kernel and the lower bound

The goal of this section is to construct polynomial trial kernels
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)

with good off-diagonal decay, and whose rescalings approximate
B(\zeta,\eta)
locally.
In order to do so we first need to define some functions which will be used in the construction.

Recall that the constrained equilibrium measure
\sigma_{\Omega}
takes the form

\sigma_{\Omega}=\sigma|_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}+\mu,

where
\mu=\mathrm{Bal}(\sigma|_{\Omega},\partial\Omega)
is absolutely
continuous with respect to arc-length measure on
\partial\Omega
.
Its density, denoted by
\rho
, is real-analytic and strictly positive.
We begin with an inverse Balayage construction which moves the measure
\mu
into
\Omega
.
More precisely, we want to construct a measure
\nu
on a real-analytic Jordan curve
\Lambda

inside
\Omega
such that
\widehat{\nu}=\mu
. We have the following theorem.

Lemma 2.9
.

There exists a real-analytic Jordan curve
\Lambda
inside
\Omega
and a measure

\nu
with a smooth density on
\Gamma
such that
\widehat{\nu}=\mu
.

Proof.

The potential
U^{\mu}
is harmonic in the exterior of
\Gamma
and continuous up to the boundary.
We can extend it to a harmonic function
H
in a neighborhood of
\textrm{Ext}(\Gamma)
. Now take a
real-analytic Jordan curve
\Lambda
close enough to
\Gamma
so that
H
is defined there.
Define a new function

\widetilde{H}(z)=\begin{cases}H(z),&\quad z\in\textrm{Ext }\Lambda,\\
P_{\Lambda}[H](z),&\quad z\in\textrm{Int }\Lambda,\end{cases}

where
P_{\Lambda}[H]
denotes the harmonic function with boundary values
H
on
\Lambda
.

Define a measure
\nu
on
\Lambda
by

d\nu(z)=-\Big[\frac{\partial\widetilde{H}}{\partial\bf{n}_{e}}(z)+\frac{\partial\widetilde{H}}{\partial\bf{n}_{i}}(z)\Big]ds(z).

By taking
\Lambda
close enough to
\Gamma
we can ensure that
\nu
is a positive measure.
Indeed, if we were to take
\Lambda=\Gamma
then we would have
\widetilde{H}=U^{\mu}
, which
has a strictly positive Neumann jump
-(\partial_{\bf{n}_{e}}+\partial_{\bf{n}_{i}})U^{\mu}

across
\Gamma
. If we move
\Lambda
slightly
inside
\Omega
, the Neumann jump of
\widetilde{H}
clearly remains positive.
The potential
U^{\nu}=\widetilde{H}
will be equal to
H
on the exterior of
\Lambda
and
hence equal to
U^{\mu}
on the exterior of
\Gamma
. We get that
\widehat{\nu}=\mu
.
∎

The function

U_{\Omega}^{\nu}(z)=\int\limits_{\Lambda}g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)d\nu(\zeta),

is the so called Green’s potential in
\Omega
of the measure
\nu
.

Lemma 2.10
.

The function
U^{\nu}_{\Omega}
is zero on
\Gamma
and

\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U^{\nu}_{\Omega}(z)=\rho(z)
, for
z\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

That
U^{\nu}_{\Omega}
is zero on
\Gamma
follows directly since

g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)
is zero for
z\in\Gamma
. To get the expression for the normal
derivative we first write

U_{\Omega}^{\nu}(z)=U^{\nu}(z)-U^{\widehat{\nu}}(z).

We get that for
z\in\Gamma

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U_{\Omega}^{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U^{\nu}(z)-\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U^{\nu}(z)+\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{e}}U^{\nu}(z)\big)-\big(\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{i}}U^{\mu}(z)+\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}_{e}}U^{\mu}(z)\big)

\displaystyle=\rho(z),

where we have used that
U^{\nu}(z)=U^{\mu}(z)
in the exterior of
\Gamma
.
∎

We define the probability measure
\lambda=\frac{\nu}{\nu(\Lambda)}
. We want to
approximate
j\cdot\lambda
by
j
point masses on
\Lambda
. More precisely,
we want to approximate the function
U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}
by a function of the form

U_{\Omega}^{\lambda_{j}}(z):=\frac{1}{j}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta_{k,j}),

where
\{\zeta_{k,j}\}_{k=1}^{j}
are points on the curve
\Lambda
.

Lemma 2.11
.

There exist points
\{\zeta_{k,j}\}_{k=1}^{j}
on

\Lambda
and a positive constant
c>0
such that

U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(z)-U_{\Omega}^{\lambda_{j}}(z)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj}),

as
j\to\infty
, uniformly for
z
in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

There exists a neighborhood
N_{1}
of
\Gamma
and a neighborhood
N_{2}
of
\Lambda

such that
g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)
is real-analytic on
N_{1}\times N_{2}
.

The curve
\Lambda
is real-analytic and the measure
\lambda
has a real-analytic density

\varpi
with respect to arc-length measure on
\Lambda
. Let
\gamma
denote a real-analytic
(periodic) parametrization
\gamma:\mathbb{T}\to\Lambda
. It follows that we can write

\int\limits_{\Lambda}g_{\Omega}(z,\zeta)d\lambda(\zeta)=\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(e^{i\theta}))\varpi(\gamma(e^{i\theta}))|\gamma^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

We now choose a particular parametrization
\gamma
satisfying
the ODE
\varpi(\gamma(e^{i\theta}))|\gamma^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})|=1
.
This is possible to do with
\gamma
real-analytic, and corresponds
to choosing an arc-length parametrization in the metric
\varpi\,ds
.

We want to find points
\{\xi_{k,j}\}_{k=1}^{j}
on
\mathbb{T}
such that

(2.8)

\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(e^{i\theta}))\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{j}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(\xi_{k,j}))+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj})

as
j\to\infty
for some constant
c>0
uniformly in
z
.
The lemma then follows by putting
\zeta_{k,j}=\gamma(\xi_{k,j})
for
k=1,\ldots,j
. To this end,
we write
g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(e^{i\theta}))
as a Fourier series

g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(e^{i\theta}))=\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}(z)e^{i\theta n},

and we get
that the left-hand side of (
2.8
) is equal to
c_{0}(z)
.
We now pick the points
\{\xi_{k,j}\}
as the
j
th roots of unity. We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(\xi_{k,j}))=\frac{1}{j}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}(z)e^{2\pi i\frac{kn}{j}}.

Changing the order of summation and using that

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{j}e^{2\pi i\frac{kn}{j}}=\begin{cases}1,\quad j|n\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

we get

\frac{1}{j}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(\xi_{k,j}))=c_{0}(z)+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}c_{nj}(z).

The function
g_{\Omega}(z,\gamma(e^{i\theta}))
is real-analytic in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}
,
hence its Fourier coefficient have exponential decay

c_{n}(z)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn})
for some
c>0
. This completes the proof.
∎

We define holomorphic functions
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}
and
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}
on
a neighborhood of
\Gamma
, in such a way that

e^{\re\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}=e^{U_{\Omega}^{\lambda_{j}}(z)}

and

e^{\re\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}=e^{U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(z)}.

These functions are defined only up to an imaginary constant,
and we choose this so that
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}

and
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}
are all real at the
fixed point
z_{0}\in\Gamma
. Since
\lambda
is a probability
measure in
\Omega
, an application of Green’s formula shows that

e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}
and
e^{\mathcal{U}^{\lambda_{j}}}

are single-valued on the neighborhood of
\Gamma
.

Remark 2.12
.

Note that the only singularities of
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}

are branch points of the form
(z-\zeta_{k,j})^{1/j}
at each of the
atoms of
\lambda_{j}
, and hence
e^{j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}
admits a holomorphic
extension to a neighborhood of the entire domain
\Omega
with simple zeros of
\zeta_{k,j}
.

From the error estimate in Lemma
2.11
it follows that in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
we have

(2.9)

e^{-j\,\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}=\big(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj})\big)e^{-j\,\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}.

We can now define the approximate reproducing kernel
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
by

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)=e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w)}-Q(z)-Q(w))}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(w)})},

where
h_{j,n}
are positive constants given by

h_{j,n}=\frac{\rho(z_{0})^{2}}{\nu(\Lambda)}\Big(n-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)}\Big).

We have some freedom in choosing
m_{n}
and
M_{n}
. Let us say that

(2.10)

m_{n}=\lfloor n^{1/2}\rfloor,\qquad M_{n}=n\nu(\Lambda)(1-\epsilon_{n}),

where
\epsilon_{n}
is such that
M_{n}
is an integer and satisfies

\epsilon_{n}\to 0,\qquad n\delta_{n}\epsilon_{n}\to+\infty,

as
n\to\infty
, where
\delta_{n}=\frac{\log n}{n}
.

The approximate kernel is defined and holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\Omega
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Locally
uniformly for
\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{H}
we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{c(\zeta,\eta)}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}\hskip 1.0ptK_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w_{n})=B(\zeta,\eta).

Furthermore, as
n\to\infty
we have

(2.11)

\frac{1}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)dA(w)\leq B(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1).

Proof.

From equation (
2.9
) we see that the approximate kernel can be written as

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)=\big(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cm_{n}})\big)\,e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w)}-Q(z)-Q(w))}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)})}.

We assume that
\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{H}
. It follows from
the proof of Theorem
2.5
that

c(\zeta,\eta)e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w_{n})}-Q(z_{n})-Q(w_{n}))}\ =e^{-(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big).

From Lemma
2.10
we get that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{0})+\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(z_{0})(z_{n}-z_{0})+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{|\zeta|^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\im\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{0})-\frac{\zeta}{\nu(\Lambda)n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{|\zeta|^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big),

and hence

\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w_{n})}=-\frac{\zeta+\bar{\eta}}{\nu(\Lambda)n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big).

From this it follows that

c(\zeta,\eta)e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w_{n})}-Q(z_{n})-Q(w_{n}))}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w_{n})})}=e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}-1)}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}}{n}\Big)\Big),

and hence we may write

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w_{n})c(\zeta,\eta)=\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}-1)}+\mathcal{O}\big(n\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}\big),

where we have used the fact that
|e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}-1)}|\leq 1

together with the crude bound
h_{j,n}=\mathcal{O}(n)
to bound the error term.
From the definition of
h_{j,n}
we get

(2.14)

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w_{n})c(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{n^{2}\rho(z_{0})^{2}}{\nu(\Lambda)}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\Big(1-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}\Big)e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}-1)}+\mathcal{O}\big(n\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}\big).

A Riemann sum approximation gives

(2.15)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\Big(1-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}\Big)e^{(\zeta+\eta)(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)n}-1)}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{n}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{n}}\Big(1-\frac{t}{\nu(\Lambda)}\Big)e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(\frac{t}{\nu(\Lambda)}-1)}dt+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{n\delta_{n}}{n}\Big)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\nu(\Lambda)\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)n}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)n}}(t-1)e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(t-1)}dt+\mathcal{O}\big(\delta_{n}\big)

uniformly for
\zeta,\eta\in\mathbb{H}
. It is now clear that locally uniformly in
\zeta,\eta
we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{c(\zeta,\eta)}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}K_{n}^{\sharp}(\zeta,\eta)=B(\zeta,\eta),

since
\frac{m_{n}}{n\nu(\Lambda)}\to 0
and
\frac{M_{n}}{n\nu(\Lambda)}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

To estimate the norm in (
2.11
) we will begin by showing that the contribution
from
w=w(\eta)=z_{0}+\frac{\eta}{n\rho(z_{0})}
such that
\eta\in\mathbb{L}
is negligible.
First, note that
|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|\leq Cn^{2}
on

\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
. It will suffice to estimate the integral

\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)1_{\mathbb{L}}(\eta)dA(w).

This is the area between the tangent line and the boundary in the disc

\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})
and is of order
\mathcal{O}(\delta_{n}^{3})
. We see that

\frac{1}{n^{2}\rho(z_{0})^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)1_{\mathbb{L}}(\eta)dA(w)=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\delta_{n}^{3})=o(1),

as
n\to\infty
.

It remains to consider
\eta\in\mathbb{H}
for which we have already derived an
asymptotic expression. Let
f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)
be the function

f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)n}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)n}}(1-t)e^{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})(t-1)}dt.

We have that

\frac{1}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)c(\zeta,\eta)=f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\Big),

as
n\to\infty
, uniformly for
\eta=\mathcal{O}(n\delta_{n})
. We obtain

(2.17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)1_{\mathbb{H}}(\eta)dA(w)

(2.18)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{4}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(0,n\rho(z_{0})\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w(\eta))|^{2}1_{\mathbb{H}}(\eta)dA(\eta)+o(1)

(2.19)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{H}}|f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)

(2.20)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{H}}|B(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)=B(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1).

The last inequality follows by Fatou’s lemma since

f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)
converges pointwise to
B(\zeta,\eta)
.
∎

The following lemma proves that the norm of the approximate reproducing kernel

K_{n}^{\sharp}
is small outside of the disc
\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})
.
We define the smooth cut-off function
\chi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
equal to 1 on a
neighborhood of
\Omega
and zero outside a slightly larger neighborhood.
The precise conditions on
\chi
will be clarified below, but here we only
require that the approximate kernel
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
is well-defined
wherever
\chi
does not vanish.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
z_{n}=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\rho(z_{0})n}

with
\zeta
in a compact set. We have

\frac{1}{(n\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)\chi(w)|^{2}1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)dA(w)=o(1),

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Because of the cut-off function
\chi
we only have to consider
a neighborhood of
\Gamma
where we know that

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)=(1+o(1))\hskip 1.0pte^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w)})}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z)+Q(w))}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)})}.

The sum has a simple structure and can be computed explicitly. For convenience we let

\Theta=e^{-(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)})}
.
Using that
|\Theta|\geq 1
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
we get the bound

(2.21)

\Big\lvert\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}\Theta^{j}\Big\rvert\lesssim n\frac{|\Theta|^{M_{n}}}{|\Theta-1|}+\frac{|\Theta|^{M_{n}}}{|\Theta-1|^{2}}.

It remains to estimate the integral

(2.22)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}\chi(w)1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(w)\,dA(w)\\
\lesssim\int\limits_{(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}\Big(n\frac{|\Theta|^{M_{n}}}{|\Theta-1|}+\frac{|\Theta|^{M_{n}}}{|\Theta-1|^{2}}\Big)^{2}e^{n(V-Q)(z)+n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w).

We claim that, as
n\to\infty
,

(2.23)

|\Theta|^{2n\nu(\Lambda)}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(w)}\leq 1+o(1),

for
w
in some one-sided neighborhood of
\Gamma
in
S\setminus\Omega

and
\zeta
confined to a compact set.
To see why, consider

G(w)=-2\nu(\Lambda)U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(w)+V(w)-Q(w).

Since
G(w)=0
on
\Gamma
and the normal derivative of
G
is zero on

\Gamma
we get
\nabla G(w)=0
for
z\in\Gamma
. This implies that,

\partial_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}G(w)=0
and we get

(2.24)

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}G(w)=4\Delta G(w)=-4\Delta Q(w)<0.

This shows that there is a one-sided neighborhood of
\Gamma
in which
e^{G(w)}\leq 1
holds.
An inspection shows that

|\Theta|^{2n\nu(\Lambda)}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(w)}=e^{nG(z)+nG(w)}.

For
\zeta
confined to a compact set, a Taylor
expansion of
G
shows that
nG(z_{n})=\mathcal{O}(1/n)

and hence (
2.23
) follows.

We now get some simple estimates for the integrals by integrating over the level
curves
|\Theta|=v
. We make the change of variables
\Theta(w)=v(\theta)e^{2\theta i}
.
The Jacobian of the change of variable can be bounded by a constant. We also have the estimate

|\Theta-1|=|v(\theta)e^{2\theta i}-1|\gtrsim|e^{2\theta i}-1|\gtrsim|\theta|.

We now get the estimates

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}n^{2}\frac{|\Theta|^{2M_{n}-2n\nu(\Lambda)}}{|\Theta-1|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{2}\int\limits_{\delta_{n}}^{1}\frac{1}{|\theta|^{2}}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{1}v^{2M_{n}-2n\nu(\Lambda)}dv

(2.26)

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{2}\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\frac{1}{\nu(\Lambda)n-M_{n}},

and

(2.27)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}\frac{|\Theta|^{2M_{n}-2n\nu(\Lambda)}}{|\Theta-1|^{4}}dA(w)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{\delta_{n}}^{1}\frac{1}{|\theta|^{4}}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{1}v^{2M_{n}-2n\nu(\Lambda)}dv

(2.28)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{3}}\frac{1}{\nu(\Lambda)n-M_{n}}.

Note that from the definition (
2.10
) of
M_{n}
we have

\delta_{n}(\nu(\Lambda)n-M_{n})=\delta_{n}\epsilon_{n}n\nu(\Lambda)\to\infty,

as
n\to\infty
. This together with Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality
shows that the right-hand side in (
2.22
)
is of order
o(n^{2})
as
n\to\infty
, completing the proof.
∎

2.3.
Correction to polynomial

In order to use the reproducing property of
K_{n}
, we will show that

K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,w)
can be corrected to a weighted polynomial in
\mathcal{W}_{n}

at little cost.

We will consider
w=w_{n}
, and try to find a function
u_{w}
such that

(2.29)

\chi(z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}-u_{w}(z)

is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
. The correction
u_{w}(z)
should be small both in norm and pointwise.

If we can show that

(2.30)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\bar{\partial}_{z}\Big(\chi(z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}\Big)\right|^{2}e^{-nQ(z)}dA(z)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn}),

for some
c>0
, a Hörmander estimate (see
[
33
]
*Proposition 2.6)
will give the existence of
u_{w}
such that

\|u_{w}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-c^{\prime}n}),

for some
c^{\prime}>0
and
u_{w}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n-1})
as
|z|\to\infty
. Since

\chi(z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}
vanishes outside a fixed compact set,
it will follow that (
2.29
) defines a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.

The derivative in (
2.30
) is non-zero only where
\bar{\partial}_{z}\chi(z)\neq 0
.
It follows that it is enough to estimate the integral

\int\limits_{N}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}dA(z),

where
N=\{z:\bar{\partial}_{z}\chi(z)\neq 0\}
. From the definition of

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
we get the upper bound

|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}\leq K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(w,w)

and from local estimates from before
K_{n}^{\sharp}(w_{n},w_{n})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.

We consider the function

F_{j,n}(z)=e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}e^{\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{V}(z)}.

From the definition
F_{j,n}
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\Omega
.
Recall that
\chi
is a smooth cut-off function equal to
1
on a neighborhood
of
\Omega
. We want to show that
F_{j,n}(z)e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}
is small
on the support of
\bar{\partial}\chi
.
On a neighborhood of
\Gamma
we have

\displaystyle|F_{j,n}(z)|^{2}e^{-nQ(z)}=(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj}))e^{-2jU_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(z)}e^{nV(z)}e^{-nQ(z)}.

We want to say that the function

-2jU_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(z)+nV(z)-nQ(z),

is large and negative outside a small neighborhood of
\Omega
.
To begin with note that the function
(\nu(\Lambda)n-j)U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}

is zero on
\Gamma
and has negative normal derivative.
It suffices to study the function

G(z)=-2\nu(\Lambda)U_{\Omega}^{\lambda}(z)+V(z)-Q(z).

We have shown (see (
2.24
)) that

\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{n}}G(z)<0,

and since it vanishes along the boundary together with its first
normal derivative it follows that, on the set
N
,

|F_{j,n}(z)|^{2}e^{-nQ(z)}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn}),

for some constant
c>0
. From the definition of the approximate
kernel it is clear that the same estimate applies for
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
.

The function
u_{w}
can also be estimated pointwise. On the set where
\chi(z)=1

we can use a subharmonicity estimate cf.
[
7
]
*Lemma 3.3 and obtain that

u_{w}(z)e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn}),

with possibly a smaller constant
c>0
.

2.4.
Proof of Theorem
A

Let
n_{k}
be a sequence along which we have convergence of the rescaled
kernel
c(\zeta,\eta)\cdot k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
locally uniformly to a limiting kernel

K(\zeta,\eta)
. From Corollary
2.8
we have the upper bound

K(\zeta,\zeta)\leq B(\zeta,\zeta)
.

We will now prove the opposite inequality, using the approximate reproducing kernel

K_{n}^{\sharp}
. We have since
K_{n}
is the reproducing kernel of the space
\mathcal{W}_{n}
that

K_{n}(z,z)=\sup\limits_{f\in\mathcal{W}_{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|f(z)|^{2}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}}.

We have proven that
\chi\,K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,z_{n})-u_{z_{n}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q}
is
a weighted polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.
Since
u_{z_{n}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q}
is small both pointwise and in norm, we get

(2.31)

K_{n}(z_{n},z_{n})\geq(1+o(1))\frac{K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},z_{n})^{2}}{\|K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,z_{n})\chi(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}}.

From Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
2.13
we have that

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(n_{k}\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}K_{n_{k}}(z_{n_{k}},z_{n_{k}})=K(\zeta,\zeta)

and

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(n_{k}\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}K_{n_{k}}^{\sharp}(z_{n_{k}},z_{n_{k}})=B(\zeta,\zeta).

Lemma
2.13
and Lemma
2.14
gives that

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{(n_{k}\rho(z_{0}))^{2}}\|K_{n_{k}}^{\sharp}(\cdot,z_{n_{k}})\chi(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}\leq B(\zeta,\zeta).

Combining these three results with (
2.31
) we obtain the lower bound

K(\zeta,\zeta)\geq B(\zeta,\zeta).

We have already proved the reversed inequality, which means that
K(\zeta,\zeta)=B(\zeta,\zeta)
.
Both functions are Hermitian-analytic so they are equal not only on the diagonal, but everywhere.
This proves that the limit point in Theorem
2.5
is unique, which completes the proof.

\square

3.
Soft and Soft/hard edges

In this section we will prove Theorems
B
and
Theorem
C
. We recall that
Q
is a real-analytic
potential with droplet
S
subject to the standing assumptions from Section
1.2
.
In particular, the boundary
\partial S
consists of finitely many real-analytic Jordan curves.

The following lemma is well known, and plays the same role as as
Lemma
2.3
did in the hard-edge case.

Lemma 3.1
.

The kernel
K_{n}(z,w)
satisfies

K_{n}(z,z)\leq Cne^{n(\check{Q}(z)-Q(z))},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

3.1.
Limiting kernel at soft edges

We define the rescaled kernel

k_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}(z_{n},w_{n})

where
z_{n}
and
w_{n}
denote the rescaled variables

(3.1)

z_{n}=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\zeta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}}\quad\text{ and }\quad w_{n}=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}},

respectively. Here
e^{i\theta}
is the outer unit normal of
S
at
z_{0}
.

The following theorem is proven in
[
7
]
*Theorem 3.5.

Theorem 3.2
.

There exist cocycles
c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
such that
(c_{n}\cdot k_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a normal family.
Any limit point takes the form
K(\zeta,\eta)={\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\zeta|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}
,
where
{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)
is Hermitian-entire and satisfies the
“mass-one inequality”

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}|\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}e^{-|\eta|^{2}}dA(\eta)\leq\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta)

as well as the exterior decay estimate

|\mathbf{K}_{\eta}(\zeta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C_{\eta},\quad\re\zeta\geq 0.

Theorem
3.2
gives an embedding theorem in a
similar way to what we saw in the Hard edge case.
Let
F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be the Fock space of entire functions with finite norm

\|f\|_{F^{2}(\mathbb{C})}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)<+\infty.

The Fock space
F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

via the Bargmann transform
\mathcal{B}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
given by

\mathcal{B}[F](\zeta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\zeta t}e^{-\zeta^{2}/2}e^{-t^{2}/4}F(t)dt.

We consider the space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
entire functions with finite Fock space norm

\|f\|_{F^{2}(\mathbb{C})}^{2}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)<+\infty

and with the growth restriction

|f(\zeta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C,\quad\re\zeta\geq 0.

We recall the definition of the entire function

\Phi(z)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt.

Theorem 3.3
.

We have that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{B}[L^{2}(-\infty,0)]
and
hence Hermitian-entire function

H(\zeta,\eta)=e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}\Phi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})

is the reproducing kernel of the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

We have already that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a subspace of
F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
Hence any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
is given by
f=\mathcal{B}[F]
with
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})
.
From the Paley-Wiener-Schwartz theorem and the growth constraint on functions in

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
it follows that
f
is the Bargmann transform of a distribution

u
supported in
\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}_{-}
. The same argument as in the proof of
Theorem
2.7
then shows that
u=F
, so that in fact

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{B}[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})]
. It follows from

[
30
]
*Proposition 5.4 that

e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}\Phi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})
is the reproducing kernel
of
\mathcal{B}[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})]
and so also of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

By combining Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
we obtain
the following corollary. The proof is directly parallel with the proof of
Corollary
2.8
in the hard-edge case
and we omit the details.

Corollary 3.4
.

Any limiting kernel
\mathbf{K}_{\zeta}
from Theorem
3.2

belongs to the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
and satisfies

\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta)\leq{\bf H}(\zeta,\zeta)
.

3.2.
Limiting kernel soft/hard edge

In
[
2
]
*Lemma 2.4 it was proven that Lemma
3.1

also holds in the soft/hard-edge setting. With some minor modifications, the proof
of Theorem
3.2
then gives the following result.
We omit the proof.

Theorem 3.5
.

There exist cocycles
c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)

such that
(c_{n}\cdot k_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a normal family. Any limit point
takes the form
K(\zeta,\eta)={\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\zeta|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}
,
where the Hermitian-entire function
{\bf K}(\zeta,\eta)
satisfies the

“mass-one inequality”

\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}e^{-|\eta|^{2}}dA(\eta)\leq\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta)

and the exterior decay estimate

|\mathbf{K}_{\eta}(\zeta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C_{\eta},\quad\re\zeta\geq 0.

We denote by
F^{2}(\mathbb{L})
the space of holomorphic functions on
the left half-plane with finite norm

\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(\zeta)<+\infty,

and define the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}
as the subspace
of
F^{2}(\mathbb{L})
of entire functions satisfying

|f(\zeta)e^{\zeta^{2}/2}|\leq C,\quad\re\zeta\geq 0.

We have the following characterization of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

The space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}

coincides with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
as sets. The reproducing kernel of

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}
is given by

{\bf H}_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\eta)=e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}\Psi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})
,
where
\Psi
is given by (
1.11
).

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}\subset F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
Let
f
be a function in
\mathcal{H}
. From the Paley-Wiener-Schwartz theorem it
follows that
f
is the Bargmann transform of a distribution
u
supported on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
.
It only remains to show that
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
f\in F^{2}(\mathbb{L})
we get from
Fubini’s theorem that
f(x+iy)e^{(x+iy)^{2}/2}
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as a function of
y

for a.e.
x<0
. For such an
x
, we have that

f(x+iy)e^{(x+iy)^{2}/2}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}e^{xt}e^{iyt}e^{-t^{2}/4}u(t)dt,

with
e^{xt}e^{-t^{2}/4}u(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Plancherel’s theorem. From the support
of
u
we see that
e^{xt}
is decreasing in
x
. This gives the inequality

(3.2)

\displaystyle N(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x+iy)|^{2}e^{-x^{2}-y^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=e^{-2x^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x+iy)e^{(x+iy)^{2}/2}|^{2}dy

(3.3)

\displaystyle=2\pi e^{-2x^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2xt}e^{-t^{2}/2}e^{-t^{2}/4}|u(t)|^{2}dt.

It follows
N(x)<\infty
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and that
N(|x|)\leq N(-|x|)
.
In particular we obtain

\|f\|_{F^{2}(\mathbb{C})}^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}N(x)dx\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}N(x)dx=2\|f\|_{F^{2}(\mathbb{L})}^{2}<\infty.

Since
f\in F^{2}(\mathbb{C})
we have
f=\mathcal{B}[F]
for
F
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

and in the same way as above, we get that
u=F
.

Next we show that
\mathcal{B}
is an isometry

\mathcal{B}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\Phi(t)dt)\to{F}^{2}(\mathbb{L}).

We compute the norm of
f=\mathcal{B}[F]
where
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\Phi(t)dt)
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(z)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathbf{B}[F](x+iy)|^{2}e^{-x^{2}-y^{2}}\frac{dydx}{\pi}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big\lvert(2\pi)^{-1/4}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}F(t)e^{zt}e^{-z^{2}/2}e^{-t^{2}/4}dt\Big\rvert^{2}e^{-x^{2}-y^{2}}\frac{dydx}{\pi}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big\lvert\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}F(t)e^{zt}e^{-t^{2}/4}dt\Big\lvert^{2}e^{-2x^{2}}\frac{dydx}{\sqrt{2\pi}\pi}.

Using that
\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}F(t)e^{zt}e^{-t^{2}/4}dt
is the Fourier
transform of the function
1_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(t)F(t)e^{xt}e^{-t^{2}/4}
and Plancherel’s theorem we get

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA(z)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}1_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(y)|F(y)e^{xy}e^{-y^{2}/4}|^{2}e^{-2x^{2}}2\pi\frac{dydx}{\sqrt{2\pi}\pi}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}|F(y)|^{2}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-2x^{2}}e^{2xy}e^{-y^{2}/2}2\pi\frac{dxdy}{\sqrt{2\pi}\pi}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}|F(y)|^{2}\Phi(y)dy,

where the last step follows from a change of variables.

Let
L(\zeta,\eta)=L_{\eta}(\zeta)
be the reproducing
kernel of
\mathcal{B}[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})]
as a subset of
{F}^{2}(\mathbb{L})

with norm and inner product from
L^{2}(\mathbb{L},e^{-|\zeta|^{2}}dA)
.
We get that

\langle\mathcal{B}[F],L_{\eta}\rangle_{{F}^{2}(\mathbb{L})}=\langle F,\mathcal{B}^{-1}[L_{\eta}]\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\Phi(t)dt)},

for any function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\Phi(t)dt)
.
From the definition of the Bargmann transform,

\mathcal{B}^{-1}[L_{\eta}](t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}}1_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(t)e^{t\bar{\eta}-\bar{\eta}^{2}/2-t^{2}/4}\frac{1}{\Phi(t)}.

If we apply the Bargmann transform to both sides we obtain

\displaystyle L_{\eta}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}e^{t(\zeta+\bar{\eta)}}e^{-\zeta^{2}/2-\bar{\eta}^{2}/2-t^{2}/2}\frac{1}{\Phi(t)}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-(t-\zeta-\bar{\eta})^{2}/2}\frac{1}{\Phi(t)}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\bar{\eta}}\Psi(\zeta+\bar{\eta})={\bf H}_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\eta),

which is what we wanted to prove.
∎

Similarly as in the soft-edge case, the following corollary follows by combining
Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Any limiting kernel
\mathbf{K}_{\eta}
from Theorem
3.5

belongs to the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}
and satisfies

\mathbf{K}(\zeta,\zeta)\leq{\bf H}_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\zeta)
.

3.3.
Approximate kernel and the lower bound

The construction of the approximate kernel will be almost the same in the soft
and soft/hard edge cases and we will treat both cases simultaneously. Let
U

be the unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus S
. In the proof we will assume that

z_{0}\in\partial U
. The other cases can be treated in more or less the same way.

Let
\Gamma
denote the boundary component of
U
to which
z_{0}
belongs.
By assumption
\Gamma
is real-analytic. We let
\mathcal{D}
denote the
unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
.

Let
\check{Q}
be the solution to the obstacle problem on
\mathbb{C}
, that is the largest
subharmonic function bounded above by
Q
on
\mathbb{C}
with growth at most

\log|z|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
|z|\to\infty
. It is well known that the function

\check{Q}
is harmonic in
U
. We let
V
be the harmonic continuation of

\check{Q}
across
\partial U
and define the holomorphic function

e^{\mathcal{V}}
whose modulus equals
e^{V}
and which is real-valued
at
z_{0}
. We can think of the function
\mathcal{V}
as a multi-valued
function defined in a neighborhood of
U
with single-valued real part.

There exists however harmonic functions
\omega_{n}(z)
in
U
which vanish along

\Gamma
and are equal to constants
\lambda_{j,n}\in[0,1]
on all the other
boundary components of
U
, such that the function

\Omega_{n}(z)=\omega_{n}(z)+i\widetilde{\omega}_{n}(z)
has the opposite
multi-valuedness of
\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{V}(z)
. That is,

e^{\Omega_{n}(z)+\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{V}(z)}
can be realized as a
single-valued holomorphic function in a neighborhood of
U
. The
construction of such functions is standard and we refer to
[
12
]

for the details.

In preparation for the construction of the approximate kernels, we want to
construct a subharmonic function which is zero on the boundary
\Gamma
and
has normal derivative
\sqrt{\Delta Q}
on
\Gamma
. We mimic the construction
in the hard-edge setting and introduce a measure
\mu=\sqrt{\Delta Q}\,ds
,
where
ds
is arc-length measure on
\Gamma
normalized by
\frac{1}{2\pi}
.

The following two lemmas are proven in the same way as
Lemmas
2.9
and
2.10
,
and we omit the details.

Lemma 3.8
.

There exists a real-analytic Jordan curve
\Lambda
in
\mathcal{D}

and a measure
\nu
with smooth density on
\Gamma
such that
\widehat{\nu}=\mu
,
where
\widehat{\nu}
denotes the balayage of
\nu
onto
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

The Green’s potential
U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\nu}
is zero on
\Gamma
and

\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{i}}U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\nu}=\sqrt{\Delta Q(z_{0})}
.

In the same way as in the hard-edge case we define the probability
measure
\lambda=\frac{\nu}{\nu(\Lambda)}
. We can find atomic probability
measures
\lambda_{j}
with atoms of equal size such that

e^{jU_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda}(z)}=e^{jU_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda_{j}}(z)}(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj})).

As in Section
2.2
, we define holomorphic functions

e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}}
and
e^{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}
on a
neighborhood of
\Gamma
such that

e^{\re\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}=e^{U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda_{j}}(z)},

and

e^{\re\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}=e^{U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda}(z)}.

By Remark
2.12
, the function
e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}

is holomorphic on a full neighborhood of
\mathcal{D}
, and we moreover have that

e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)}=(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj}))e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}

on a neighborhood of
\Gamma
.

We now have to distinguish between the soft and soft/hard edge.
Define the approximate kernel
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
by

(3.4)

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)=e^{\Omega_{n}(z)+\overline{\Omega_{n}(w)}}e^{\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w)})}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(Q(z)+Q(w))}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(w)})}.

with
h_{j,n}
given by

(3.5)

h_{j,n}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}u(j/\sqrt{n})\frac{\Delta Q(z_{0})}{\nu(\Lambda)}e^{-\frac{j^{2}}{2n\nu(\Lambda)^{2}}},

where

\begin{cases}u(t)=1&\text{(soft-edge case)},\\
u(t)=\Phi(-t/\nu(\Lambda))^{-1}&\text{(soft/hard edge case)}.\end{cases}

As in the hard-edge case we have some freedom when choosing

m_{n}
and
M_{n}
. Let
m_{n}=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor
.
We let
\delta_{n}=n^{-1/2}\log n
and choose
M_{n}
as
sequence of integers such that

(3.6)

\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to\infty,\qquad\frac{M_{n}}{\delta_{n}n}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.10
.

In the soft-edge case, we have locally uniformly that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w_{n})=H(\zeta,\eta),

where
z_{n}
and
w_{n}
are given by (
3.1
).
Furthermore, for
\zeta
fixed we have

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}dA(w)\leq H(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1),

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

First we get that

\Omega_{n}(z_{n})+\overline{\Omega_{n}(w_{n})}=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Next, since
\nabla(V-Q)=0
on
\Gamma
, Taylor’s formula gives the expansion

\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{V}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{V}(w_{n})}-Q(z_{n})-Q(w_{n}))=-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}+ig_{n}(\zeta,\eta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),

where
g_{n}(\zeta,\eta)
is such that
e^{ig_{n}}
is a cocycle. Another
application of Taylor’s formula to
\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}
around the point
z_{0}
gives

(3.7)

\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w_{n})}=\frac{\zeta+\bar{\eta}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\|(\zeta,\eta)\|^{2}}{n}\Big).

Let
w\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})
and write
w=z_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}\eta}{n\rho(z_{0})}
.
Using that
|\zeta|,|\eta|=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
we get that

\displaystyle K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)

\displaystyle=e^{ig_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{ig_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}+\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n\big).

A Riemann sum approximation using the definition (
3.5
) of
h_{j,n}
gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\nu(\Lambda)}\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-t\frac{(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}{\nu(\Lambda)}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)^{2}}}dt+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{n}),

where we have used that the derivative of

e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}e^{-t^{2}/2}

is uniformly bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
for

t\in I_{n}=[\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}},\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}]
.
Rewriting the last integral gives that

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}(z_{n},w)c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),

where the function
f_{n,\zeta}
be given by

f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)=G(\zeta,\eta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}e^{-(\zeta+\bar{\eta}+t)^{2}/2}dt.

The first claim of the theorem is now obvious since
\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to\infty

and
\frac{m_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. With the notation

w(\eta)=z_{0}+e^{i\theta}\eta/\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}
, the second claim follows from

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n\Delta Q(z_{0}))^{2}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\sqrt{\Delta Q(z_{0})}\log n)}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w(\eta))|^{2}dA(\eta)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\sqrt{\Delta Q(z_{0})}\log n)}|f_{\zeta}(\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}|H(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)=H(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1),

as
n\to\infty
, where the asymptotic inequality follows from Fatou’s lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

In the soft/hard-edge case, we have locally uniformly in
\mathbb{H}
that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w_{n})=H_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\eta)

where
z_{n}
and
w_{n}
are given by (
3.1
).
Furthermore, for
\zeta
fixed we have that

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}1_{S}(w)dA(w)\leq H_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1),

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Repeating the argument from Lemma
3.10
verbatim,
but using the soft/hard-edge definition of
h_{j,n}
in
(
3.5
), we obtain

\frac{e^{-ig_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}\Phi(-t)^{-1}e^{-(\re\zeta)^{2}-(\re\eta)^{2}}e^{-t(\zeta+\bar{\eta})}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

Rewriting the integral gives

\frac{c_{n}(\zeta,\eta)}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n}(z_{n},w)=f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),

where the function
f_{n,\zeta}
be given by

f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)=G(\zeta,\eta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{\frac{m_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}^{\frac{M_{n}}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}\Phi(-t)^{-1}e^{-(\zeta+\bar{\eta}+t)^{2}/2}dt.

The locally uniform convergence now follows since
\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to\infty

and
\frac{m_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We now turn to the norm estimate. Just as in the hard-edge case, the contribution
from
w=w(\eta)\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})
such that
\eta\in\mathbb{H}

is negligible. This follows since we have that
|K_{n}(z,w)|^{2}\leq Cn^{2}
, so we get

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}1_{S}(w)1_{\mathbb{H}}(\eta)dA(w)\lesssim\frac{1}{n}n^{2}\delta_{n}^{3}=o(1),

as
n\to\infty
. The remaining part of the integral can be estimated as in the soft-edge case, and we get

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}1_{S}(w)1_{\mathbb{L}}(\eta)dA(w)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|f_{n,\zeta}(\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{L}}|H_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\eta)|^{2}dA(\eta)+o(1)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=H_{\mathbb{L}}(\zeta,\zeta)+o(1),

as
n\to\infty
. This completes the proof.
∎

It remains to show that the approximate kernel is small outside the
\delta_{n}
-neighborhood
of the boundary point
z_{0}
. We denote by
\chi
a smooth cut-off function equal to
1
on
a neighborhood of the unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus S
.
We require that this neighborhood is sufficiently small that the kernel

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)
is well-defined wherever
\chi
does not vanish.

The following lemma applies to both the soft and soft/hard-edge kernels. The proof is inspired by

[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For fixed
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z_{0})}\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)\chi(w)|^{2}dA(w)=\mathrm{o}(1),

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
z=z_{n}
. Note that we can write

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)\chi(w)|^{2}dA(w)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})}\chi(w)|S_{1}(z,w)|^{2}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w),

where
S_{1}
is given by

S_{1}(z,w)=\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}h_{j,n}e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)})}.

In order to estimate the above integral, we define

a_{j}=h_{j,n}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{j}=b_{j}(z,w)=e^{-j(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})+\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)})},

where
h_{j,n}
is defined in (
3.5
). We now have

S_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}}a_{j}b_{j},

and summation by parts gives

(3.14)

S_{1}=a_{M_{n}}B_{M_{n}}-a_{m_{n}}B_{m_{n}-1}-\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}-1}(a_{j+1}-a_{j})B_{j},

where
B_{j}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}b_{k}
. The sum
B_{j}
can be written as

B_{j}=\frac{1-\Theta^{j+1}}{1-\Theta},

where
\Theta=\Theta(z,w)=e^{-\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z)-\overline{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)}}
.
To estimate the integral in the statement of the lemma we will estimate the integral
of each of the terms in (
3.14
). We will start by considering
integrals of the form

I_{j}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}|B_{j}|^{2}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w),

where
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is the intersection of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(z_{0},\delta_{n})

with a small fixed neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\Gamma
.
To estimate
I_{j}
we integrate over the level curves
\Gamma_{t}
where

(V-Q)(w)=-t^{2}/2
with
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
.
For
w\in\Gamma_{t}
with
t
small, there exists
a unique closest point
w_{0}\in\partial S
to
w
,
and an application of Taylor’s formula shows that

w=w_{0}+\frac{e^{i\theta}t}{2\sqrt{\Delta Q(z_{0})}}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),\quad w\in\Gamma_{t}

as
t\to 0
.
By Lemma
3.9
we thus find that

\re\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(w)=-\frac{t}{2\nu(\Lambda)}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),\quad w\in\Gamma_{t}

as
t\to 0
. From the local asymptotics of
\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}

we have that
|e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(z_{n})}|\leq C_{\zeta}
,
and hence we obtain the bound

|\Theta|^{j}\leq C_{\zeta}e^{-j\frac{t}{2\nu(\Lambda)}+Cjt^{2}},\quad w\in\Gamma_{t}.

In addition, we have the elementary bound

|\Theta-1|^{2}\gtrsim|z-w|^{2},

and combining this with the above we find that

\displaystyle I_{j}\lesssim\int\limits_{\Gamma_{t}}

\displaystyle 1_{\{|z-w|>\delta_{n}\}}(w)|z-w|^{-2}ds_{t}(w)\int\limits_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}e^{-\frac{tj}{\nu(\Lambda)}+2Ct^{2}j}e^{-nt^{2}/2}dt,

where
ds_{t}
denotes the arc-length measure on
\Gamma_{t}
.
The first integral is of order
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\delta_{n}})
. For the second integral we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}e^{-\frac{tj}{\nu(\Lambda)}+2Ct^{2}j}e^{-nt^{2}/2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{-\epsilon\sqrt{n}}^{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}e^{2Cjt^{2}/n}e^{-\frac{tj}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int\limits_{-\epsilon\sqrt{n}}^{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}e^{\frac{j^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)^{2}n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{j}{\nu(\Lambda)\sqrt{n}}+t)^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}e^{\frac{j^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)^{2}n}}.

Combining the two estimates we obtain
I_{j}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}}e^{\frac{j^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)^{2}n}}
. Using that

a_{j}^{2}\lesssim ne^{-\frac{j^{2}}{\nu(\Lambda)^{2}n}}
we find that

\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}a_{j}^{2}|B_{j}|^{2}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w)\lesssim a_{j}^{2}I_{j}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{n}}e^{-\frac{j^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)^{2}n}}=o(n),

which shows that the integral of the absolute value squared of the first two terms
in (
3.14
) are of the right order. It remains to estimate the
integral of the absolute value squared of the sum in (
3.14
).
To begin with, from Cauchy-Schwarz we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle\left|\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}-1}(a_{j+1}-a_{j})B_{j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{n}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}-1}(a_{j+1}-a_{j})^{2}|B_{j}|^{2}.

Using that
|a_{j+1}-a_{j}|\lesssim a_{j}(\frac{j}{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}),
we get

(3.16)

\displaystyle M_{n}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}-1}(a_{j+1}-a_{j})^{2}I_{j}

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{n}\sum\limits_{j=m_{n}}^{M_{n}-1}\Big(\frac{j}{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{2}a_{j}^{2}I_{j}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{n}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(1+s)^{2}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2\nu(\Lambda)}}ds\cdot\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{M_{n}}{\delta_{n}}=o(n),

where the last step uses the definition (
3.6
) of
M_{n}
.

Outside the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
, the contribution to the
norm of
1_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}(0,\delta_{n})}K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},\cdot)
will be small.
This is somewhat non-trivial to show, as the approximate kernel may have some

L^{2}
-mass distributed around distant boundary components of
S
, i.e.,
around
\partial S\setminus\Gamma
. However, this mass will be of a lower
order than
\mathcal{O}(n)
. To see this, we note that

|e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(w)}|=e^{-jU_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda_{j}}(w)}.

For
w\in\mathcal{D}
outside some fixed neighborhood of
\Gamma
and
\zeta\in\Lambda
,
we have the uniform lower bound
g_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\zeta)\geq c>0
. It follows that

|e^{-j\mathcal{U}_{\lambda_{j}}(w)}|=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj}).

This gives that
|S_{1}|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
, and hence

(3.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{N}}|\chi(w)\,K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},w)|^{2}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{N}}|\chi(w)\,S_{1}(z_{n},w)|^{2}e^{n(V-Q)(z_{n})+n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w)

(3.20)

\displaystyle\lesssim n\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{N}}\chi^{2}(w)e^{n(V-Q)(z_{n})+n(V-Q)(w)}dA(w)=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}).

This completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Correction to polynomial

We want to show that the approximate kernel
K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,w)
can
be approximated in a suitable way by weighted polynomials.
We will consider points
w=w_{n}
, and try to find a function
u_{w}
such that

(3.21)

\chi(z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}-u_{w}(z)

is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
. The correction
u_{w}e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q}

should be small both in norm and pointwise near
z_{0}
.

If we can show that

(3.22)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\bar{\partial}_{z}\Big(K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}\chi(z)\Big)\right|^{2}e^{-nQ(z)}dA(z)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn}),

for some
c>0
a Hörmander estimate will give the existence of
u_{w}
such that

\|u_{w}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-c^{\prime}n}),

for some
c^{\prime}>0
and
u_{w}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n-1})
as
|z|\to\infty
. Since

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)e^{\frac{n}{2}Q(z)}=\mathcal{O}(z^{n-1})
as
|z|\to\infty

it will follow that (
3.21
) defines a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.

The derivative in (
3.22
) is non-zero only where the
complex gradient
\bar{\partial}_{z}\chi(z)\neq 0
. It follows that it is enough to estimate the integral

\int\limits_{N}|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}dA(z),

where
N=\{z:\bar{\partial}_{z}\chi(z)\neq 0\}
. From the definition of
K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)

and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality (for sums)
we get

|K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,w)|^{2}\leq K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,z)K_{n}^{\sharp}(w,w)
and
from local estimates from before
K_{n}^{\sharp}(w_{n},w_{n})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.

Let us first consider the part of
N
in the component of
S
to
which
z_{0}
belongs. We use the estimate

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,z)\lesssim M_{n}\max_{m_{n}\leq j\leq M_{n}}e^{-2jU_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda}(z)}e^{n(V-Q)(z)}

First note that

e^{n(V-Q)(z)}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cn}),

uniformly on
N
. Since
j<M_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log n)
we get that

K_{n}^{\sharp}(z,z)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-c^{\prime}n})

for some other positive constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
On the other components of
S
the estimates are even simpler, since we have

e^{-2jU_{\mathcal{D}}^{\lambda_{j}}(z)}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cj}),

uniformly for
z\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\text{dist}(z,\partial\mathcal{D})>d>0
.
This finalizes the proof of the estimate (
3.22
),
and the existence of suitable corrections
u_{w}
follows.

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
B
and
Theorem
C

The proofs will now be almost identical to the proof of
Theorem
A
. We will outline the steps
for Theorem
B
. The corresponding steps for the
soft/hard edge are written in parenthesis. In the soft/hard-edge case,

H
should be replaced by
H_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Let
K(\zeta,\eta)
be a limit point of the rescaled kernel along
the subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k}
. From Corollary
3.4

(Corollary
3.7
)
we get that
K(\zeta,\zeta)\leq H(\zeta,\zeta)
.

Since
K_{n}
is the reproducing kernel of the space
\mathcal{W}_{n}
, we have that

K_{n}(z,z)=\sup\limits_{f\in\mathcal{W}_{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|f(z)|^{2}}{\phantom{a}\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}}.

We have proven that
K_{n}^{\sharp}\chi-u_{z_{n}}
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.
Since
u_{z_{n}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}Q}
is small both pointwise near
z_{n}
and in norm we get

(3.23)

K_{n}(z_{n},z_{n})\geq(1+o(1))\frac{K_{n}^{\sharp}(z_{n},z_{n})^{2}}{\phantom{i}\|K_{n}^{\sharp}(\cdot,z_{n})\chi(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}}.

From Theorem
3.2
(Theorem
3.5
)
and Lemma
3.10
(Lemma
3.11
) we have that

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n_{k}}(z_{n_{k}},z_{n_{k}})=K(\zeta,\zeta)

and

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}\Delta Q(z_{0})}K_{n_{k}}^{\sharp}(z_{n_{k}},z_{n_{k}})=H(\zeta,\zeta).

Lemma
3.10
(Lemma
3.11
)
and Lemma
3.12
gives that

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}\Delta Q(z_{0})}\|K_{n_{k}}^{\sharp}(\cdot,z_{n_{k}})\chi(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{W}_{n}}^{2}\leq H(\zeta,\zeta).

Using these properties together with (
3.23
) gives the lower bound

K(\zeta,\zeta)\geq H(\zeta,\zeta).

We get that any limiting kernel
K(\zeta,\eta)
is equal to
H(\zeta,\eta)
,
which completes the proof.

\square
