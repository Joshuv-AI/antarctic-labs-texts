The first case of Reel Syndrome in a dog after pacemaker implantation: A case report
Authors: Z Wojtczak, M Lewicki, M Płóciennik, J Gach, K Nowak, A Noszczyk‐Nowak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReel Syndrome is a rare complication of permanent pacemaker implantation, characterised by the rotation of the device around its transverse axis, which causes the lead to coil around the generator and displacement, leading to pacing dysfunction. This report presents the first documented case of Reel Syndrome in a dog. The, 6‐year‐old female Labrador Retriever required a pacemaker implantation due to a diagnosed third‐degree atrioventricular block. Forty‐eight hours after pacemaker implantation, the dog developed clinical signs, including diaphragmatic contractions and a decrease in heart rate. Fluoroscopy confirmed the characteristic coiling of the lead around the generator, leading to a definitive diagnosis. Reel Syndrome can occur due to mechanical manipulation of the device by the patient, such as scratching or rubbing against objects as well as due to movement of the pacemaker system if it is not properly secured. Prevention involves securing the pacemaker and lead properly during implantation, ensuring close postoperative monitoring, and implementing protective measures such as an Elizabethan collar and pharmacological sedation to limit activity. Early diagnosis and intervention are crucial to prevent severe clinical consequences. This case highlights the importance of early recognition of Reel Syndrome and the need for proper stabilisation of the pacemaker and lead to prevent complications. Based on experience in human medicine, the described therapeutic approach may serve as a reference point for veterinary practice.Keywords: Canine reel syndrome, heart pacemaker, pacemaker complication, pacemaker lead dislodgement, veterinary medicine

Pacemaker lead dislodgement and failure, related to device manipulation, are rare complications of permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation in both humans and animals. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Lead dislodgement is more common in the postoperative period, before the lead becomes fully embedded in the heart muscle, but it may also occur several months or years after implantation. Reel, Twiddler and Ratchet syndromes are rare complications that cause pacemaker failure and are diagnosed and differentiated based on lead and generator positions observed on chest X‐ray or fluoroscopy imaging. 1 , 5 , 6 Specifically, Twiddler syndrome is defined as a pacemaker malfunction in which the lead is dislodged by physical manipulation, resulting in the coiling of the lead due to the generator's rotation on its longitudinal axis. 7 Conversely, Reel Syndrome is a variant of Twiddler syndrome in which the device rotates on its transverse axis. 8 The electrodes coil near or around the generator and retract away from the heart, resulting in pacemaker malfunction, although there is usually no damage to the leads. Ratchet Syndrome is caused by ‘sliding’ of the generator back and forth without rotation on any axis, resulting in lead displacement with a ratcheting effect. 1 This case describes Reel Syndrome in a female Labrador Retriever 48 h after pacemaker implantation. To the best of our knowledge, this case is the first report of Reel Syndrome in a dog.

Case presentation

A 6‐year‐old, 30 kg female Labrador Retriever was presented to a referring veterinarian with a 3‐week history of exercise intolerance, disinterest in routine activities and weakness. The owners reported no injuries, and the dog showed no signs of lameness. Initial diagnostics did not reveal any abnormalities in clinical tests. The dog did not improve clinically over the following 3 weeks, and the owners later noticed an enlarged abdominal contour. A subsequent clinical examination identified the presence of ascites, and approximately 800 mL of transudate fluid was drained. Laboratory tests revealed elevated liver enzyme levels, while other haematological parameters remained within normal limits. Over the next week, the dog had developed anorexia and ataxia. The neurological examination was unremarkable.

After an episode of ascites, the dog underwent a cardiological consultation. Auscultation revealed a grade 2/6 left‐sided murmur and a grade 1/6 right‐sided murmur; the heart rate was 60 BPM. The clinical examination revealed normally pink mucous membranes, CRT <2 s, a regular and well‐palpable femoral pulse, and slightly enlarged jugular veins. The referring veterinarian made the initial electrocardiographic diagnosis of advanced, high‐grade, second‐degree atrioventricular block with an atrioventricular conduction ratio of 3:1. PQ intervals (0.06 s) and QRS complex duration (0.05 s) were normal. Ventriculophasic sinus arrhythmia associated with AV block was present.

The case was referred to the Department of Internal Medicine with Horse, Dog and Cat Clinic, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Wrocław University of Environmental and Life Sciences for advanced cardiological evaluation to investigate underlying causes and to assess the need for pacemaker implantation. In the days leading up to the visit to our clinic, the dog experienced recurrent ascites, which was alleviated by abdominal puncture performed by veterinarians at a local clinic (most recently on the day before the pacemaker implantation procedure).

On the day of admission for pacemaker implantation at the university clinic, a control electrocardiogram revealed a third degree atrioventricular block with a ventricular rate of 55 BPM (Figure 1). 9 , 10 , 11

The first pacemaker implantation was performed and proceeded without intraoperative complications. The first procedure was carried out via a right external jugular vein cut‐down. Intravenous access was obtained by venesection. As per standard protocol in our team, the dog was secured throughout the procedure with self‐adhesive defibrillator electrodes with an external transthoracic pacing function, although pacing was not required. An active fixation electrode (Medtronic CapSureFix Novus MRI™ SureScan™ 58 cm) was inserted into the right ventricular apex region under fluoroscopic guidance without complications. Only occasional ventricular beats, caused by electrode‐induced myocardial irritation, were observed. After pacing tests such as capture threshold, sensing threshold, impedance, and subjective QRS complex morphology assessment confirmed correct electrode positioning, a pulse generator (Abbott Endurity™) was connected (Figures 2 and 3).

Fluoroscopic image of the active fixation electrode into the area of the right ventricle during first implantation.

Fluoroscopic image of the pulse generator pocket during first operation.

The pacing mode was set to VVI stimulation with a rate of 80 BPM. The pacing threshold was 0.25 V, sensitivity was set to 3.0 mV, pacing parameters were 3.0 V and 0.4 ms. The lead was secured with a single cuff placed inside the jugular vein and a set of three single surgical sutures anchoring the electrode to the vessel and one securing it to the surrounding muscles using non‐absorbable monofilament sutures (Ethicon™ Prolene™ 0). The pulse generator pocket was created in the prescapular region, dorsal and cranial to the site of electrode insertion into the jugular vein via the same incision that was used for venous access. Blunt dissection of tissues in the prescapular region was performed to create a proper pocket under the layer of brachiocephalic muscle. The pulse generator was fixed to the underlying muscles with non‐absorbable monofilament suture material (Ethicon™ Prolene™ 0). The pulse generator pocket and subcutaneous tissue were sutured with absorbable monofilament material (B.Braun Monosyn® 0). A simple continuous suture was used to close both the pulse generator pocket and the subcutaneous tissue. Using single surgical sutures, the skin was sutured with non‐absorbable monofilament material (B.Braun Dafilon® 0).

The postoperative wound was protected with a soft, mildly compressive dressing. The dog recovered well, was fully conscious, and was discharged eight hours after the procedure. No alarming clinical signs were observed during postoperative observation. The owner was advised to maintain the dressing for at least ten days until skin sutures were removed. Forty‐eight hours after the first procedure, the owner reported concerning clinical signs: diaphragmatic muscle contractions synchronised with the pacemaker rhythm and a reduction of the dog's heart rate to pre‐pacemaker values. A fluoroscopic examination performed the same evening confirmed electrode dislocation, with half of the lead coiled around the pacemaker battery. The owners admitted that they had noticed the dog rubbing against the bed but had not considered it dangerous, assuming that the dog was adjusting to the bandage. The dog was discharged and scheduled for re‐operation the following day. On the day of re‐operation, the owner reported no further diaphragmatic contractions. The pre‐operative fluoroscopic examination showed the entire length of the electrode coiled around the pacemaker battery, presumably due to ongoing manipulations by the dog overnight. In addition, the pacemaker battery was reversed from its original position (Figure 4). Re‐operation involved removal of the old electrode and implantation of a new electrode at the target site. We did not find any damage after a thorough manual inspection of the old electrode. Despite the undamaged electrode, we decided to use a new one to maintain higher sterility and minimise the risk of complications. The generator pocket was filled with a small amount of exudative fluid; no signs of bacterial infection were observed. The suture anchoring the generator to the surrounding muscles was intact, and there were no visible twisting of the surgical thread.

Fluoroscopic image of the pulse generator pocket just before re‐implantation showing the retraction of the pacemaker lead characteristic of Reel Syndrome.

During re‐operation, it was possible to preserve the original vascular access to the right external jugular vein, into which a new electrode of the same type and size was inserted. It was secured with a double cuff and additional non‐absorbable sutures (Ethicon™ Prolene™ 0) over a longer than standard section of the vessel. The first cuff was placed inside the vein, while the second was outside overlapping the vein (Figure 5). A total of six single sutures were used to secure the electrode to the vessel and two single sutures to anchor it to the surrounding muscles.

Surgical site showing area of vascular access to external jugular vein with securing the electrode with a double cuff and additional non‐absorbable sutures.

During the operation, the pulse generator pocket was repositioned to a more cranial‐dorsal location compared with the previous site, in order to reduce the risk of lead twisting (Figure 6). To better protect the device from dog manipulation, the pacemaker battery was placed midway down the neck beneath the brachiocephalic muscle. In addition to the standard muscle fixation using non‐absorbable material (Ethicon™ Prolene™ 0), the pacemaker battery was further secured with a set of sutures with prolonged adsorption time (Ethicon™ PDS™ Plus 0). A single cross‐mattress suture was applied to stabilise the pulse generator and press it against the inner muscle layer. The pulse generator pocket was secured with a row of interrupted sutures and a simple continuous suture using material with prolonged adsorption time (Ethicon™ PDS™ Plus 0). The subcutaneous tissue was closed with a simple continuous suture using absorbable material (B.Braun Monosyn® 0). The skin was closed with interrupted sutures using a non‐absorbable monofilament material (B.Braun Dafilon® 0) (Figures 5 and 6).

Fluoroscopic image of the pulse generator pocket after re‐implantation.

After the re‐operation, we intensively monitored the dog for 12 h. During this time, we analysed the parameters such as body temperature, electrocardiography, and pulse oximetry. Capnography was monitored until extubation. Throughout the entire period, the dog was under the constant care of a veterinarian. Pacing parameters were normal and all clinical signs resolved. Final pacing parameters were similar to those of the first implantation (VVI 80 BPM, endogenous R amplitude was >8.0 mV, the impedance 488 Ohm, threshold was 0.25 V, pacing parameters were 3.0 V and 0.4 ms, setting sensing 2.8 mV). The wound was dressed with a pressure dressing and elastic neck wrap. Because after the first operation the dog performed excessive mobility, which could have contributed to lead dislodgement, we decided to make additional recommendations. Owners were advised to avoid situations in which the dog could manipulate the implantation site, sedatives (gabapentin—20 mg/kg every 8 h, trazadone—3 mg/kg every 8–12 h) were introduced for the first 10 days after surgery and a postoperative Elizabethan collar was also applied to further reduce the risk of re‐manipulation by the dog. The dog attended the first follow‐up visit 24 h after the procedure. The dog's clinical condition during the initial follow‐up was good. The control ECG and a brief chest ultrasound confirmed effective pacing. At the follow‐up visit 3 weeks after re‐operation, the dog's clinical condition had improved significantly, echocardiographic parameters were better, no ascites was observed, and normal pacing parameters were maintained. Pacing thresholds and electrode impedance remained constant. There have been no postoperative complications since the operation and 11 months have now passed. The dog continues to attend regular follow‐up appointments, during which no abnormalities have been noted (Figure 7).

Discussion

The dog in this report developed an uncommon pacemaker complication resulting in lead dislodgement and the development of Reel Syndrome. Reel Syndrome occurs when the patient unconsciously or deliberately rotates the subcutaneously implanted pacemaker. In dogs, this can occur when scratching the implantation site with forelimbs or rubbing against objects or surfaces. Such behaviour may be caused by an irritation sensation at the surgical wound site, which highlights the importance of close observation by the owner during the postoperative period and adequate wound protection. 4 , 5 , 17

We believe that this behaviour occurred in this case. The owners admitted that they had observed the dog rubbing against the bed but they did not consider it alarming, assuming that the dog was adjusting to the bandage. After noticing clinical signs and an emergency consultation, a noticeable gradual curling of the electrode around the pacemaker could be seen (fluoroscopic examination was performed the day before and on the day of the re‐operation), which indicated constant, repeated manipulation by the dog. In a case of Twiddler Syndrome in a dog described by Zimmerman and Bright (2004), the pulse generator pocket was initially placed in the caudal cervical region near the scapulohumeral joint. It is likely that movement of the right forelimb caused the rotation of the pacemaker battery, with subsequent twisting and retraction of the lead. An additional factor was insufficient fixation of the pacemaker battery to the underlying tissues, which allowed rotation within the subcutaneous pocket. 5 We cannot rule out that in our dog's case, the displacement of the electrode from the heart muscle may have been related to the initial placement of the pulse generator too close to the foreleg—normal movement and shoulder blade movements may also have contributed to the displacement of the electrode. In combination with our dog's excessive mobility and additional manipulations, the process may have progressed faster than in the case described by Zimmerman and Bright. Considering the fact that the suture anchoring the generator to surrounding muscle was intact, rotation of the generator itself in our opinion was minimal and did not contribute to this complication significantly. Our standard procedure for attaching the generator to the underlying tissues has never failed before, but this could have been influenced by our dog's slight obesity, so we took additional precautions during re‐operation. A sufficiently tight generator pocket is also a significant factor responsible for the risk of perioperative complications—considering the fact that in our case the amount of effusion in the pulse generator pocket was small, the device itself adhered tightly to the tissues, and the sutures closing the pocket were intact, we believe that this factor was not involved in our case.

Intentional or unintentional movement of the electrode leads to withdrawal from the stimulation site and ineffective pacing. In this dog, the first clinical sign noticed by the owners approximately 48 h after the operation was abnormal cyclic muscle tensions of the thoracic region due to stimulation of areas other than the heart muscle by the electrode. The most likely explanation was phrenic nerve stimulation, which runs in the near vicinity of the cranial vena cava. A decrease in heart rate was also observed, which prompted the owners to contact the clinic. Approximately 72 h after the operation, a fluoroscopic examination was performed, which clearly confirmed the characteristic twisting of half the length of the leads around the pacemaker battery, and the decision was made to re‐operate the following day. During the follow‐up fluoroscopy, signs of thoracic stimulation were no longer present, which proved further retraction of the electrode and ultimately complete encirclement of the entire length of the lead around the generator, so that stimulation of the phrenic nerve no longer occurred. Similar clinical progression, including phrenic nerve capture due to lead migration, has been observed in veterinary and human patients with pacemaker complications. 4 , 18 , 19

The terminology of the complications is not harmonised completely in the literature available in veterinary medicine, while in human medicine, the different types of lead twisting have been extensively classified. 2 , 3 , 4 , 6 , 17 , 20 Reel Syndrome, Twiddler Syndrome and Ratchet Syndrome are rather rare complications that may appear in cases of implantable devices, because of which electrode displacement and functional disturbances occur. Each syndrome has a different mechanism and clinical presentation, knowing which is important for its differential diagnosis and management effectively. 1 , 21

The rotation of the generator around its transverse axis, and the coiling of the electrodes around the generator without damaging it, is called Reel Syndrome. It generally occurs within one month of its implantation. Diagnosis is simple from chest radiographs depicting the typical coiling configuration. 1 , 22 , 23 Management usually involves repositioning the electrodes and securing the generator in the implantation pocket.

Twiddler syndrome results from rotation of the generator around the longitudinal axis, usually due to conscious or unconscious patient manipulation of the device. 5 , 7 , 24 The result is braiding of the electrodes and often mechanical damage that requires electrode replacement. This syndrome has been reported in veterinary patients as well, including a dog model and a feline case, and is known to appear later—even up to a year after implantation. 5 , 24 , 25

Ratchet Syndrome is the rarest of the three and arises from a mechanism of unidirectional sliding of the electrodes along a single axis, which results in their displacement. 1 Unlike Reel Syndrome, Ratchet Syndrome usually involves no more than one electrode and does not end up in the coiling of the lead around the generator. 23

In the present case, the final diagnosis of Reel Syndrome was supported by both clinical signs and diagnostic imaging. Fluoroscopic imaging confirmed the typical coiling of the lead around the generator without visible mechanical damage. The onset of clinical signs within a few days of implantation corresponds with the expected timeline for Reel Syndrome, whereas Twiddler Syndrome generally presents much later. If in our case the main pathomechanism was not the gradual coiling of the electrode onto the generator battery (as a result of the patient's manipulation and movement of the shoulder joint), it is possible that further rotation in the longitudinal axis could have led to the development of Twiddler's Syndrome.

Although Reel Syndrome has been sufficiently described in human medicine, 1 , 8 , 21 , 22 this case confirms the possibility of such incidents among veterinary patients. Similarly, mechanisms of operation of the implantable device and the dog's behaviour do suggest that diagnostic and therapeutic approaches from human medicine can be further adapted for veterinary use. Our procedure was similar to those described in humans. Still, we extended it with additional absorbable sutures securing the pacemaker battery and a double cuff securing the electrode to the vein. 23 , 26 Comparable surgical solutions were also recommended by Domenech et al. (2005) and Zimmerman and Bright (2004) in order to reduce the risk of postoperative complications caused by device mobility. 5 , 17 During the re‐operation, the pulse generator pocket was repositioned to a more cranial‐dorsal location compared with the previous site, in order to increase the distance from the shoulder joint and reduce the risk of lead twisting. To better protect the device from dog manipulation, the pacemaker battery was placed midway down the neck beneath the brachiocephalic muscle. An additional recommendation for owners was to administer sedatives (gabapentin—20 mg/kg every 8 h, trazodone—3 mg/kg every 8–12 h) for the next 10 days after surgery to minimise the risk of manipulation by the dog and further complications.

Using a double sleeve with additional sutures to secure the electrode may be standard in obese dogs and in those with a clear tendency to attempt manipulation of the subcutaneously implanted pacemaker. 22 , 26 Munawar et al. (2011) also highlighted the relevance of preventive strategies such as secure fixation and proper postoperative care to minimise risk in human patients susceptible to early‐onset Reel Syndrome. 21

This case, along with others previously described in both veterinary 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 17 , 24 and human 1 , 7 , 8 , 21 , 22 , 23 literature, reinforces the importance of early postoperative monitoring, behavioural observation, and secure surgical technique to minimise the risk of device manipulation syndromes.

Conclusions

Reel Syndrome, although rarely observed in animals, should be considered by veterinarians in cases of sudden dysfunction of implanted cardiac devices. The condition results from unintentional or intentional device rotation, often due to local irritation or patient activity. Rotation may also be caused by local muscle action during normal activities. 25 Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention are crucial to prevent further complications. This case also highlights the importance of appropriate postoperative care and monitoring of device function to ensure the long‐term and proper operation of implanted pacemakers.
