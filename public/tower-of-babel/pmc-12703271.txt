Postsurgical psychogenic non-epileptic seizure: a treatment-related functional neurological disorder
Authors: Shin-ichiro Osawa, Maimi Ogawa, Hirotaka Iwaki, Yuko Akitsuki, Mayu Fujikawa, Kazushi Ukishiro, Kazutaka Jin, Atsushi Sakuma, Hiroaki Tomita, Kyoko Suzuki, Nobukazu Nakasato, Hidenori Endo
Published: 2025

Introduction

Epilepsy surgery is an effective treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy, often improving seizure control and quality of life (QoL). However, the surgical process can involve significant psychological burdens, including time investment and emotional stress.1 In some cases, seizure remission and the resulting shifts in social expectations introduce new psychosocial stressors.2

Functional neurological disorders (FNDs), particularly psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES), sometimes emerge despite seizure freedom.3 Post-surgical PNES (psPNES) remains underrecognised and is often mismanaged, leading to unnecessary treatment escalation and inefficient use of healthcare resources. Prior reports estimate the incidence of psPNES at 3.9%, typically based on episodes captured by inpatient video electroencephalogram (EEG).3 However, outpatient presentations are more commonly encountered in clinical practice and often require diagnosis and management without inpatient monitoring, which increases diagnostic complexity.

This study aimed to investigate the clinical incidence and features of psPNES in a real-world surgical cohort, focusing on preoperative psychological factors and postoperative trajectories to better understand potential mechanisms for symptom emergence.

Methods

We retrospectively analysed 99 patients (aged ≥13 years) who underwent presurgical evaluation and curative craniotomy for drug-resistant epilepsy at the Tohoku University Hospital between April 2018 and March 2024 (online supplemental figure 1). Presurgical assessments included 3T MRI, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, long-term video EEG and magnetoencephalography. Intracranial EEG was used when localisation or mapping was required. A super-selective Wada test was conducted in selected cases with high functional risk. Neuropsychological and psychosocial evaluations included measures of seizure worry, depression, anxiety, QoL and self-stigma. Patients were followed in multidisciplinary clinics for a mean of 51.9 months (range, 12–82 months).

PNES was diagnosed at a level of ‘probable PNES’ or higher based on International League Against Epilepsy criteria,4 via consensus among an epileptologist, psychiatrist and psychologist. Diagnosis emphasised positive clinical features consistent with FND.5 Treatment included psychoeducation, reframing of seizures, psychosocial stressor assessment and caregiver guidance.

We examined clinical features of psPNES including: (1) unplanned care (telephone inquiries, unscheduled visits, ambulance calls), (2) antiseizure medication (ASM) changes (prediagnostic escalation, postdiagnostic reduction) and (3) seizure characteristics (presence of seizure-free period and emergence of new-onset seizures distinguishable from habitual seizures).

Demographic and clinical factors were compared between psPNES and non-psPNES groups using χ2 or t-tests. Logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of psPNES. Further detailed information is provided in the online supplemental materials.

Results

Of the 99 patients, 21 (21.2%) developed psPNES with an onset at 9.7±8.2 months postoperatively. Two had prior PNES. Compared with non-psPNES group, patients with psPNES had significantly higher rates of left-sided epileptogenic foci (71.4% vs 46.2%, p=0.04) and being married (38.1% vs 7.7%, p=0.03). No differences were found in age, sex, age at epilepsy onset, seizure classification, seizure outcome, neurocognitive scores, education or employment (online supplemental table 1).

Self-reported questionnaires showed significantly higher seizure worry (p<0.05) and depression scores (p<0.01) in the psPNES group (online supplemental table 2). Anxiety, cognitive and social functioning did not differ. Logistic regression identified seizure worry (OR 0.90; 95% CI, 0.83 to 0.97; p<0.01) and marital status (OR 16.08; 95% CI, 1.46 to 176.28; p<0.05) as independent predictors of psPNES (table 1).

Unplanned medical resource use was frequent: 71.4% made telephone inquiries, 61.9% had unscheduled visits, 33.3% used emergency transport and 38.1% consulted psychiatry (online supplemental figure 2). ASM escalation occurred in 81.0% prior to diagnosis, but all cases eventually underwent dose reduction. A seizure-free interval was observed in 95.2% before the onset of new episodes, but only 57.1% were clearly recognised as distinct seizure types at presentation. Diagnosis was confirmed retrospectively based on clinical course and treatment response (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights psPNES as a significant postoperative complication that may mimic seizure recurrence. The 21.2% incidence observed is notably higher than prior inpatient-based reports.3 This likely reflects our diagnostic strategy emphasising positive clinical features in line with contemporary FND criteria,5 rather than relying solely on inpatient video-EEG.

Our findings identified preoperative seizure worry and marital status as independent predictors of psPNES. These results indicate that heightened expectations and psychosocial pressures following seizure freedom may paradoxically increase vulnerability to functional symptoms. Despite seizure remission, many patients continued to exhibit excessive concern about recurrence, aligning with models of FND in which persistent threat perception and misinterpretation of bodily signals contribute to symptom generation.5

Importantly, 86% of psPNES patients used unplanned medical resources, yet ASMs were ultimately reduced in all cases without seizure recurrence. This pattern suggests initial misattribution of new-onset symptoms as epileptic, highlighting the diagnostic complexity of psPNES in real-world outpatient practice. Only 57% of cases were recognised as distinct at onset; most were diagnosed retrospectively based on treatment response.

Nonetheless, symptom improvement was achieved in 67% of cases through structured psychoeducation and psychosocial intervention. This supports the benefit of multidisciplinary care models. Integration of psychiatric and psychological expertise appears to optimise outcomes and reduce inappropriate healthcare use.

Our study has limitations. The diagnosis was made at the ‘probable’ level, which could limit generalisability. Also, the single-centre retrospective design and small sample size limit the statistical power. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

psPNES is more common than previously recognised and may develop even after successful seizure control. Psychosocial vulnerability, such as seizure worry and marital stress, is likely to contribute to its development. Early recognition and multidisciplinary evaluation help improve postoperative outcomes and resource use.
