White matter microstructure and motor function in amnestic mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's dementia
Authors: Sarah Cote, Marit FL Ruitenberg, Jos N van der Geest, Nicholas Alonzo Frost, Jace B King, Kevin Duff, Vincent Koppelmans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUp to 30% of amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) subjects exhibit motor dysfunction. However, the relationship between white matter microstructural connectivity (SC) and motor function across the Alzheimer's disease (AD) spectrum remains unclear. This study investigated 1) white matter microstructural differences among cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals, those with aMCI, and those with mild AD; and 2) associations between white matter microstructure and motor performance.MethodsParticipants (CU = 68, aMCI = 44, AD = 34) underwent diffusion- and T1-weighted MRI. Whole-brain SC was assessed using weighted streamline count from probabilistic tractography between regions of the Brainnetome atlas. A subset (n = 103) also completed motor assessments (finger tapping, grip strength, spiral tracing, sequence learning). Group differences and AFD–motor associations were evaluated using threshold-free network-based statistics, corrected for multiple comparisons.ResultsCompared with CU participants, those with AD exhibited reduced SC in 268 connections, predominantly involving the temporal lobe, thalamus, and hippocampus. Compared with aMCI, AD participants showed reduced SC in three connections linking frontal, parietal, and temporal regions. No significant SC differences were observed between CU and aMCI, and SC was not associated with motor performance.ConclusionWhite matter SC is affected in AD, particularly in medial temporal lobe regions that are known to be affected early in the disease. Absence of differences between CU and aMCI, and small differences between aMCI and AD suggests that SC changes appear later in the disease course. The lack of SC-motor associations could reflect lack of variation in motor behavior in the sample.Keywords: Memory impairment, Motor function, Magnetic resonance, White matter, Connectivity, Network-based statistics, Dementia

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide and is characterized pathologically by amyloid-β plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles that lead to neuronal loss (Atri, 2019; Jack et al., 2013). Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is a transition state between normal age-related cognitive decline and dementia (Petersen et al., 1999; Petersen et al., 2010; Manly et al., 2008). Amnestic MCI (aMCI) is a subtype of MCI characterized by prominent memory impairment, particularly in the ability to learn and retain new information and is associated with the highest likelihood of conversion to dementia (Fischer et al., 2007; Perri et al., 2007; Petersen et al., 1999; Petersen et al., 2010).

In addition to cognitive dysfunction, various studies have reported motor dysfunction in both aMCI and AD. Motor dysfunction is relatively common, with more than 30% of individuals with aMCI presenting with gait problems (Verghese et al., 2008). Motor decrements have also been demonstrated in multiple tasks, including finger tapping (Koppelmans et al., 2023, 2024), grip strength (Koppelmans et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2021; Filardi et al., 2022), and drawing (Förstl et al., 1993). Recent work also suggests that motor dysfunction could predict subsequent cognitive and functional decline (Koppelmans et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings underscore the relevance of motor dysfunction as a potential early marker and predictor of disease progression across the AD continuum.

Previous studies have linked motor decrements across the AD spectrum to gray matter volume loss and amyloid accumulation, but the link with white matter microstructure has received less attention (for a review, see Koppelmans et al., 2022a). A recent study in AD subjects found that better performance on a clock drawing task was associated with greater fiber densities of the uncinate fasciculus and left superior longitudinal fasciculus, while weaker grip strength was associated with lower fiber density in numerous white matter tracts (Liu et al., 2024). Another study reported that gait dysfunction in participants with AD was linked to reduced fractional anisotropy in the right corticospinal tract, right cingulum, right splenium, left inferior longitudinal fasciculus, bilateral inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi, left forceps minor, left superior longitudinal fasciculus, and right anterior thalamic radiation (Olazaran et al., 2013). As of now, it remains unclear whether results from these studies generalize to motor functions other than drawing and gait.

To address the gap in knowledge of the neurobiological underpinnings of manual motor dysfunction in dementia, this study investigated a) whole brain network-level white matter structural connectivity differences among cognitively unimpaired (CU), aMCI and mild AD groups; and b) associations between manual motor abilities and structural connectivity (SC) strength throughout the entire brain across the CU to AD spectrum. SC was quantified as the estimated number of streamlines connecting pairs of brain regions, reflecting the strength of connectivity between distant regions. This contrasts with commonly used measures such as fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity, which instead characterize regional white matter microstructure. We hypothesized that structural connectivity strength would be weaker in aMCI than in CU, and less in AD than in aMCI. It was furthermore hypothesized that structural connectivity strength between sensory and motor brain regions would positively correlate with motor performance across the sample.

Materials and methods

Study population

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and T1-weighted MRI scans, sampled from a previously conducted prospective study [R01AG055428; (Duff et al., 2017; Duff et al., 2018; Duff et al., 2019)] were available for 150 subjects. Based on visual inspection and QSIPrep quality control information, data of 4 individuals were excluded because of motion or scan artifacts, leaving a total of 146 scans for analysis (CU n = 68, aMCI n = 44, AD n = 34). While CU subjects and a small number of aMCI subjects were recruited from the community, the majority of aMCI (single or multi-domain) and AD participants were recruited from the University of Utah cognitive disorders clinic. Inclusion criteria were a) age 65 years or older; and b) availability of a knowledgeable collateral source to comment on their cognition and daily functioning. Exclusion criteria comprised a) medical comorbidities likely to affect cognition (i.e., neurological conditions, current severe depression, substance abuse, and major psychiatric conditions); b) inability to complete MRI or positron emission tomography (PET) imaging; c) inability to complete cognitive assessments due to inadequate vision, hearing, or manual dexterity; d) being enrolled in a clinical drug trial related to anti-amyloid agents; e) elevated depression as indicated by a score of greater than 5 on the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale; and f) severe dementia as indicated by a Clinical Dementia Rating score of 2 or greater or a Mini Mental Status Examination score of less than 20.

AD and aMCI diagnoses were based on a neurological visit, neuropsychological evaluation, and brain imaging. Confirmation of group assignment for all three groups was made with the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; Petersen et al., 2010) classification battery, which comprises the Mini Mental Status Examination (Folstein et al., 1975), the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (Morris, 1993), and the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised (Wechsler, 1987) Logical Memory II Paragraph A. The AD group met clinical criteria for probable AD dementia as defined by ADNI, characterized primarily by memory impairment and a Clinical Dementia Rating global score of 0.5 or 1.0. While biomarker data were not used to confirm AD pathology, the clinical profiles of these participants are consistent with memory-predominant dementia in the context of the AD spectrum. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board. CU and aMCI subjects signed informed consent. Individuals with AD signed assent, while a Legally Authorized Representative signed informed consent in their name.

Measures and procedures

MRI image acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 T S Prisma fit scanner with a 64-channel head coil. T1-weighted (T1w) data were acquired using a sagittal MP2RAGE sequence (TR = 5000 ms, TE = 2.93 ms, flip angles = 4° and 5°, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm, slice thickness 1 mm, resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm, acquisition time = ∼7 min). Prior to processing, the UNI and INV2 images were multiplied to remove extra cranial noise inherent to the MP2RAGE sequence. DWI was collected with the following parameters: TR = 6320 ms, TE = 102.8 ms, flip angle = 78°, acquisition matrix = 168, FOV = 250 × 250 mm, number of slices = 93, slice thickness = 1.5 mm, in-plane resolution = 1.49 × 1.49 mm, acquisition time = 7 min 16 s. The b-value was 3000 s/mm2 in 64 non-collinear directions. One volume without diffusion weighting (DW) (b-value = 0 s/mm2) was acquired at the beginning of the acquisition. No reversed phase encoding data was collected and hence, diffusion imaging data were not corrected for geometric distortions.

MRI preprocessing

DICOM images were converted to the brain imaging data structure (BIDS) format using BIDSkit (https://github.com/jmtyszka/bidskit). Preprocessing was then performed using QSIPrep 0.19.0, which is based on Nipype 1.8.6 (Gorgolewski et al. (2011); Gorgolewski et al. (2018); RRID:SCR_002502). The T1w images were corrected for intensity non-uniformity (INU) using N4BiasFieldCorrection (Tustison et al., 2010, ANTs 2.4.3), and used as an anatomical reference throughout the workflow. The anatomical reference image was reoriented into AC-PC alignment via a 6-DOF transform extracted from a full Affine registration to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template. A full nonlinear registration to the template from AC-PC space was estimated via symmetric nonlinear registration (SyN) using antsRegistration. Brain extraction was performed on the T1w image using SynthStrip (Hoopes et al., 2022) and automated segmentation was performed using SynthSeg (Billot et al., 2023) from FreeSurfer version 7.3.1.

MP-PCA denoising as implemented in MRtrix3's DWI denoise (Veraart et al., 2016) was applied with a 5-voxel window. After MP-PCA, the mean intensity of the DWI series was adjusted so all the mean intensity of the b = 0 images matched across each separate DWI scanning sequence. B1 field inhomogeneity was corrected using DWI bias corrected from MRtrix3 with the N4 algorithm (Tustison et al., 2010).

FSL's (version 6.0.5.1:57b01774) eddy was used for head motion correction and Eddy current correction (Andersson and Sotiropoulos, 2016). Eddy was configured with a q-space smoothing factor of 10, a total of 5 iterations, and 1000 voxels used to estimate hyperparameters. A linear first level model and a linear second level model were used to characterize Eddy current-related spatial distortion. Q-space coordinates were forcefully assigned to shells. An attempt was made to separate the field offset from subject movement. Shells were aligned post-eddy. Eddy's outlier replacement was run (Andersson and Sotiropoulos, 2016). Data were grouped by slice, only including values from slices determined to contain at least 250 intracerebral voxels. Groups deviating by more than 4 standard deviations from the prediction had their data replaced with imputed values. Final interpolation was performed using the jac method.

Several confounding time-series were calculated based on the preprocessed DWI: framewise displacement (FD) using the implementation in Nipype (following the definitions by Power et al., 2014). The head-motion estimates calculated in the correction step were also placed within the corresponding confounds file. Slicewise cross-correlation was also calculated. The DWI time-series were resampled to AC-PC, generating a preprocessed DWI run in ACPC space with 1.2 mm isotropic voxels. QSIprep's quality control HTML files were used to evaluate the quality of brain mask and tissue segmentation, T1 to MNI registration, b = 0 masking, b = 0 to T1 registration, and framewise displacement.

Structural connectivity matrix reconstruction

Structural Connectivity Matrix Reconstruction was performed using QSIprep 0.19.0 (based on Nipype 1.8.6; Gorgolewski et al., 2011, 2018; RRID:SCR_002502). Specifically, the built-in 'mrtrix_singleshell_ss3_hsvst' workflow (https://qsirecon.readthedocs.io/en/latest/builtin_workflows.html) was used. This workflow, for which an overview is presented in Fig. 1, runs the following three sub-processes:

1)Reconstruction of Fiber Orientation Distribution (FOD). Single-shell 3-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (SS3T-CSD) was used to estimate FODs (Tournier et al., 2004, 2008; Dhollander et al., 2016, 2019). This process was completed using MRtrix3Tissue (https://3Tissue.github.io), a fork of MRtrix3, with intensity normalization applied using mtnormalize (Raffelt et al., 2017). This node results in voxel-wise white matter fiber orientation distributions (FODs), from which one or more fiber orientations and their associated amplitudes can be estimated. QSIprep's quality control HTML files were inspected to evaluate the accuracy in overlay and directionality of the FOD peaks.

2)Probabilistic Tractography. FastSurfer v2.1.1 (Henschel et al., 2020) outputs were fed into QSIPrep. QSIPrep-preprocessed T1w images and brain masks were used for analysis. FastSurfer outputs were registered to the QSIPrep T1-weighted image and used to generate a hybrid surface/volume segmentation (HSVS) (Smith et al., 2020). Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was generated using iFOD2 (Second-order Integration over Fiber Orientation Distributions; Tournier et al., 2010) where streamline propagation was anatomically constrained (ACT) by the HSVS-derived five-tissue-type (5 TT) image. ACT ensures that streamlines traverse anatomically plausible white matter pathways and terminate at the gray matter–white matter interface. iFOD2 is a probabilistic algorithm that takes the FOD image as input. Candidate streamline paths (based on short curved "arcs") are drawn, and the underlying (trilinear-interpolated) FOD amplitudes along those arcs are sampled. The maximum number of streamlines was set to 10,000,000, while the minimum and maximum streamline lengths were set to 30 mm and 250 mm respectively. The output of this process is a whole-brain tractogram, represented as a set of streamlines. Overlay of the tractogram on the T1-weighted image and Brainnetome atlas in native space (see below) was evaluated.

3)Structural Connectivity Matrix Reconstruction. ROI-to-ROI structural connectivity matrices were constructed using the 246-region Brainnetome atlas as the parcellation scheme (Fan et al., 2016). The Brainnetome atlas was brought into native space using the inverse warp field derived during the QSIPrep preprocessing phase (generated under 2.2.2). Cerebellar ROIs were ignored because the cerebellum was not included entirely in the field of view for the majority of participants. Atlases in native space were visually inspected to ensure correct overlay and coverage. For each pair of Brainnetome atlas regions, the sum of SIFT2 (spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms 2; Smith et al., 2015) streamline weights for all streamlines connecting the regions was calculated. These values provide an estimate proportional to the effective cross-sectional area of the white matter fiber bundle connecting those regions (Smith et al., 2015), and are referred to here as ‘structural connectivity’ (SC). The search radius was set to 2 mm, meaning that if a streamline ended before hitting gray matter, the search for a cortical termination region could be up to 2 mm from the endpoint. SIFT2 weighting adjusts streamline contributions such that streamline density becomes proportional to the underlying FOD-derived fiber density estimates throughout white matter. As a result, higher matrix values indicate stronger estimated white matter structural connectivity between regions.

Motor measures

Out of the total sample (n = 146), 103 (CU n = 52, aMCI n = 31, AD n = 20) participants completed motor measures. Between-group differences in motor function have been reported previously (Koppelmans et al., 2023; Ruitenberg et al., 2023; Koppelmans et al., 2024; Ruitenberg et al., 2026).

Finger tapping

Finger tapping performance was measured using a computerized test that was developed in-house using version 3 of the PsychoPy software suite (Peirce, 2007, 2009, 2019). The task was built to be run independently, without the need for interaction with a study coordinator and has four conditions: left index finger tapping, right index finger tapping, simultaneous index finger tapping, and alternate finger tapping. Each condition consisted of three trials which lasted 10 s. For each condition, two outcome measures were considered: average tapping speed and tapping speed standard deviation (speed variability). Averages of the three trials were calculated per condition. This task and its data pre-processing have been previously described at length (Koppelmans et al., 2023, 2024) and is available via https://github.com/vnckppl/FingerTappingTask.

Grip strength

Grip strength was measured in pounds using a hydraulic Jamar Smart Hand Digital Dynamometer (Bechtol, 1953). The assessment protocol has been described previously (Roberts et al., 2011). Subjects were positioned in a chair with legs, back support, and fixed arms. When the start sign was given, subjects were encouraged to squeeze as long and as tightly as possible for 5 s. Assessments were completed for the left and right hand three times each, in alternating fashion, starting with the left hand. The trial average for the subjects’ dominant and non-dominant hand was calculated, based on their handedness obtained using the Dutch Handedness Questionnaire (Van Strien, 2002).

Motor sequence learning

Motor sequence learning was measured using a computerized test that was programmed in Psychopy (version 3; Peirce, 2007, 2009, 2019). This task and its data pre-processing have been previously described at length (Ruitenberg et al., 2026). Participants were shown a series of arrows on a screen, each pointing in a distinct direction (up/down/left/right), having a distinct color (yellow/green/blue/red) and appearing in a distinct place on the screen (up/down/left/right). These features were always linked such that there were only 4 different arrows (e.g., the arrow pointing upwards always appeared on top of the screen and was always yellow). Subjects were instructed to press the button on a gaming controller that matched the arrow as fast as possible (the buttons on the gaming controller had the same color, direction, and location as the arrows).

Prior to the start of the task, participants completed a brief practice session to familiarize themselves with the stimuli and responses. During the task, stimuli were presented in blocks of 32 trials each (one trial equated to one button press). Subjects were informed that the arrows could be presented in a sequence, but were not told the order or length. Participants first completed a block in which stimuli were presented in a pseudorandom order (i.e., no direct repetitions), which allowed us to evaluate participants’ general motor proficiency. Then, eight alternating random and sequence blocks were presented – each random block included 32 pseudorandom trials, whereas each sequence block included eight repetitions of the same 4-element sequence. The first four and second four sequence blocks encompassed a different sequence to evaluate carry over effects in learning (i.e., learning to learn).

Here, we selected four outcome measures that span the breadth of unique motor aspects in motor sequence learning: 1) average general motor proficiency, defined as the mean reaction time in the first random block; 2) variability in general motor proficiency, defined as the coefficient of variation (CoV) in the first random block; 3) initial motor sequence learning, measured using an exponential decay function to determine the decay constant across trials in the first sequence blocks; and 4) overall motor sequence learning, quantified as the rate of learning (the linear slope coefficient across RT differences between pairs of adjacent random-sequence blocks across the first half of the task).

Archimedes spiral test

Archimedes spiral tracing performance was assessed using an in-house developed computerized test consisting of a spiral template attached to an electronic drawing board (Wacom Intuos Wireless Pen Tablet, model CTL4100) connected to a 2017 13" MacBook laptop running MATLAB R2017b for recording. Subjects were instructed to place the pen in the center of the spiral template before the tracing started. While tracing, they were not allowed to lean on the drawing board with their hand or arm. Subjects were asked to trace the spiral as accurately and as fast as possible using their dominant hand. Automatic quantitative analyses were performed using custom-made software written in MATLAB R2013a. This yielded the following four outcome measures: 1) movement time (seconds), defined as the time it took the subject to trace the spiral; 2) length of drawing (cm), defined as the length of the drawn spiral; 3) error, defined as the mean radial distance between the spiral template and the drawn spiral in pixels; and 4) error variability, defined as the standard deviation of the radial distance between the spiral template and the drawn spiral in pixels. This task has been described previously (Hoogendam et al., 2014, 2015; Koppelmans et al., 2024).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were provided for each of the variables in prior open-access publications (Koppelmans et al., 2023, 2024). All descriptive statistics analyses were conducted in R 4.6.1. Continuous variables were compared using linear regression models and categorical variables using Chi-square tests.

Structural connectivity was analyzed through threshold-free network based statistics (TFNBS) implemented in the QSIPrep toolbox. Network-based statistics is a method for performing statistical analysis to test hypotheses about the human brain connectome (Zalesky et al., 2010). TFNBS is a nonparametric, permutation-based method that enhances test statistics across a continuum of thresholds, allowing edge-wise statistical inference without the need for an arbitrary threshold, and controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER) across the connectome (Baggio et al., 2018). This approach enables the identification of statistically significant edges and topologically coherent subnetworks, sets of edges or connections representing pairwise structural links between brain regions, for which the null hypothesis can be rejected.

A general linear model (GLM) was applied at each edge i, j across the group-level connectivity matrices to compute raw F-statistics. TFNBS enhancement was applied by integrating the raw edge-wise statistics across multiple thresholds. Each edge's TFNBS score reflected both the magnitude of the statistical effect (height) and the extent to which it was embedded in spatially coherent network structure (extension). Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing with 15,000 permutations, to generate a null distribution of maximal TFNBS scores. Each observed TFNBS score was assigned an FWER-corrected p-value based on this empirical null.

Sex, age, and framewise displacement were included in the GLM as covariates (Kaufman et al., 2023). To address the issue of multiple statistical tests performed across the connectome, False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied. This approach aims to limit the expected false-positive rate to 5%. By doing so, the results were adjusted to ensure that no more than 5% of the significant findings were likely to be spurious. The alpha level threshold for all analyses was set to 0.05.

Nodes (specific brain regions of interest (ROIs)) used as seed/target regions were based on the 246-parcel Brainnetome atlas (Fan et al., 2016). TFNBS was used to compare whole-brain structural connectivity through SC between groups (CU, aMCI, and MD). Additionally, TFNBS was used to analyze the linear associations between motor function and whole brain network connectivity across the entire sample. Associations with all motor outcome measures that are described in the motor measures section were conducted.

Group comparisons in SC

Global network analyses through TFNBS assessed SC, measured as the weighted number of streamlines, representing the cross-sectional area of the white matter fiber bundles connecting individual brain regions. These analyses revealed that AD participants demonstrated lower SC across a large neural brain network compared to CU participants (see Fig. 2, Table 2, and Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). This network comprised 268 connections and spanned 144 unique regions of the Brainnetome atlas. The largest number of affected connections were connecting temporal lobe areas (35 unique ROIs with a total of 187 connections) and subcortical areas (28 unique ROIs with 109 connections). The statistical significance of temporal and subcortical connections was also larger than that of affected connections with other brain areas. Within the temporal lobe, connectivity alterations were most pronounced for the bilateral parahippocampal gyri. Subcortical SC was most often affected for connections with the thalamus and hippocampus. SC with frontal (36 unique ROIs with 89 connections) and parietal (18 unique ROIs with 79 connections) was also substantially affected, but SC with occipital, insular, and limbic regions seemed to be spared (<11 unique ROIs with <34 connections). Particularly, SC with the thalamus, parahippocampal gyrus, and cingulate gyrus were affected. Overall, SC with left hemisphere ROIs (56.2% of all affected connections) were slightly more often affected than connections with the right hemisphere (43.8% of all affected connections).

Discussion

The present study examined structural white matter network differences across the AD spectrum. Compared to CU, the AD group showed weaker SC across an expansive network. Compared to aMCI, the AD group showed weaker SC in three connections involving the parietal, frontal, and temporal lobes. No significant differences were found between CU and aMCI and no significant associations between white matter SC and motor behavior were observed.

Group differences in white matter microstructure

As hypothesized, SC of a vast white matter microstructural network was smaller in AD compared to CU individuals. The regions exhibiting significantly reduced SC in AD relative to CU individuals parallel the established progression of AD. The most prominent connectivity disruption involved the medial temporal lobe, particularly the parahippocampal gyri and hippocampi, containing the greatest number of affected connections across the network. This finding is consistent with both neuroimaging studies identifying medial temporal atrophy as a hallmark feature of AD (Jack et al., 2010; Jack Jr et al., 2018), as well as with neuropathological staging models demonstrating that biomarker pathology first accumulates within the entorhinal cortices before spreading into the hippocampus and subsequently neocortical regions (Braak and Braak, 1991; Braak et al., 2011). The weaker SC involving these regions suggests that degeneration extends beyond relevant regional gray matter pathology to disrupt the structural pathways supporting communication among these structures. Beyond the medial temporal lobe, the network connects other brain regions known to be affected by AD. Extensive involvement of the thalamus, basal ganglia, and amygdala is consistent with growing evidence that subcortical nuclei undergo structural degeneration throughout the disease course (Aggleton et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018; Zarei et al., 2010). The distribution of affected connections was largely bilateral with slightly more significant connections observed in the left hemisphere. Our findings are consistent with prior findings of mild left sided disease dominance, particularly within medial temporal and temporoparietal regions (Yang et al., 2017).

A small network of three connections exhibited lower SC in individuals with AD compared to those with aMCI. Given that no differences were observed between CU and aMCI subjects, this indicates that individuals with aMCI in our sample were more similar to CU than to AD, suggesting that although local white matter change occurs as early as the preclinical stage (Benitez et al., 2022), network-level white matter deterioration takes place later in the disease process. Connections that were affected in aMCI spanned the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobe, supporting dispersed impact in early stages and involved the parahippocampal gyrus, an area implicated in early AD-related neurodegeneration and cognitive decline (Bailly et al., 2015; Fan et al., 2016; Minoshima et al., 1997; Nestor et al., 2003; Greicius et al., 2004).

Our results overlap with prior studies that have reported widespread microstructural alterations using traditional diffusion tensor imaging metrics such as fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity (Chen et al., 2023), and reduced apparent fiber density of specific tracts using fixel-based analysis (Mito et al., 2018). in AD Our findings corroborate these studies, together suggesting that white matter microstructural changes may reflect the clinical complexity of AD, which impacts cognitive, emotional, motor, and sensory functioning. This is further supported by negative correlations between white matter connectivity and AD biomarker levels, including tau deposition (Ahmadi et al., 2024) and amyloid burden (Pichet Binette et al., 2021) that highlight the potential neurobiological role of white matter deterioration in functional changes in AD.

Despite prior studies reporting white matter changes across the AD spectrum (Cedres et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2021; Selnes et al., 2012), as early as subjective cognitive decline (Brueggen et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020), we did not observe significant SC differences between CU and aMCI participants and differences between aMCI and AD were minimal. This was contrary to our hypothesis. Prior findings were largely based on traditional diffusion tensor imaging metrics (e.g., fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, radial diffusivity, axial diffusivity), whereas the current study used SC expressed as the sum of weighted streamlines connecting brain regions. The discrepancy between more traditional white matter microstructural metrics and SC when comparing aMCI to controls and AD may indicate that traditional measures are more sensitive to AD white matter pathology. SC reflects a specific aspect of white matter microstructure that is integrated into metrics such as FA. Additional aspects of white matter microstructure include myelination, axon consistency, and diameter (Jones et al., 2013). An alternative explanation for the observed lack of SC differences may be the heterogeneity within the aMCI group in terms of pathology and clinical trajectory. As not all individuals with aMCI will go on to develop AD, and some may even revert to CU status, future retrospective work selecting individuals that went on to develop AD may elicit different findings (Koepsell and Monsell, 2012; Roberts et al., 2014). Taken together, these findings in combination with the literature suggest that while white matter microstructure may begin to deteriorate early in the disease process, SC disruptions measurable by weighted streamline count, SC may only become prominent in later stages of AD, potentially due to the specificity of the metric and/or the heterogeneity within aMCI subjects in our sample.

Motor findings

Absence of associations between SC and motor performance was unexpected, particularly in light of previous work linking white matter microstructure to motor outcomes in aging and AD (Bennett et al., 2011; King et al., 2013) and prior work in this cohort demonstrating small but significant differences in motor performance between groups (Koppelmans et al., 2023, 2024). Although SC tentatively provides greater sensitivity to fiber-specific microstructural changes (Raffelt et al., 2012), its connection-specific nature may make it less sensitive to detecting diffuse, distributed white matter alterations associated with motor function that can be captured by voxel-wise microstructural metrics. Notably, fractional anisotropy has been associated with finger tapping speed in healthy aging (Oschwald et al., 2021), suggesting that a direct comparison of diffusion metrics may be necessary to clarify their relative sensitivity and specificity to motor function. Potentially, the limited variability in motor function in our sample, as reflected in the small effect sizes of the differences in motor performance between groups (Koppelmans et al., 2023, 2024; Ruitenberg et al., 2026) may have limited our ability to detect associations between motor function and SC. Another explanation for the lack of results could be the inclusion of framewise displacement in our analyses. Because group status was both related to more motion and poorer motor function, adjusting for motion in our analyses may have regressed out associations between motor function and SC. Preventing motion with denser padding during MRI data acquisition could help mitigate this issue. Previous studies have reported mixed findings, likely due to differences in imaging modality, analysis approach, disease stage, and behavioral outcomes (Bennett et al., 2011; King et al., 2013; Rieckmann and Bäckman, 2009; Rieckmann et al., 2010). These discrepancies highlight the need for additional work comparing diffusion metrics directly and integrating multimodal approaches to fully characterize motor network integrity across disease stages.

Study limitations and future directions

Several methodological factors may have contributed to some of the null findings. First, as aforementioned, it is possible that SC – while sensitive to microstructural changes – does not fully capture the network-level disruptions most relevant to motor performance in AD. Future studies should therefore compare associations of SC and traditional white matter metrics across the AD spectrum and with motor performance. Second, our diffusion imaging protocol comprised a single image without diffusion and 64 vol with high diffusion weighting (3000 s/mm2). While high diffusion weighting is sensitive to subtle differences in tissue microstructure, it is also sensitive to noise. Low signal-to-noise ratio may have obscured certain associations between white matter microstructure and motor performance. Future studies should ideally include protocols with multiple levels of diffusion weighting, and more diffusion and non-diffusion weighted volumes. Third, because this study utilized a variety of manual motor measures, it remains unclear if these current results translate to other motor functions. Incorporating gait, balance, and dual-task performance (Montero-Odasso et al., 2012), could provide deeper insights into the role of white matter microstructural pathology in motor dysfunction in AD. Fourth, field maps or reversed-phase encoded diffusion maps were not acquired. As such, susceptibility-induced geometric distortions were not corrected for. Some residual spatial distortions, particularly within anterior and posterior cortical regions vulnerable to susceptibility artifacts (e.g., orbitofrontal cortex), may have persisted within the diffusion data. The absence of distortion correction may have reduced the anatomical precision of localization of certain nodes within susceptibility-prone regions. Finally, the present study classified participants using ADNI's clinically defined criteria for CU, aMCI, and probable AD dementia, which rely on neuropsychological testing, clinical examination, and functional assessment rather than biomarker confirmation. It is possible that some individuals in the aMCI and AD groups did not have underlying AD pathology, and conversely, that some participants in the CU group may have had preclinical or prodromal AD (Duff et al., 2024). This possible diagnostic misclassification could have obscured group differences in white matter microstructure. Furthermore, considering the high rate of comorbidity of dementia subtypes, such as AD, Lewy Body disease, and Limbic-predominant Age-related TDP-43 Encephalopathy (LATE; Nichols et al., 2023), it is possible that the present findings are due to multiple etiologies and not solely reflect clinically defined AD. With the growing availability of fluid biomarker tests for AD, a more precise characterization of disease and disease pathology can be established. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal designs and comprehensive biomarker analyses to assess how structural motor network disruptions evolve over time and establish a more precise understanding of their relationship to disease progression and in relation to pathology (amyloid/tau/alpha-synuclein) rather than clinically defined diagnoses.

Conclusion

White matter structural network differences between CU and AD and aMCI and AD were observed in areas known to exhibit amyloid accumulation and atrophy early in AD. In this sample, white matter SC not did not contribute a clear connection to motor performance on measures of grip strength, finger tapping, Archimedes spiral tracing, or motor sequence learning. While SC is suitable for measuring white matter microstructure, the present findings suggest that it may not be sufficiently sensitive to capture the network-level disruptions most relevant to motor performance in AD. Future studies should examine motor behavior in relation to white matter connectivity beyond manual motor function, and continue exploring the role of white matter microstructure using longitudinal designs to track changes over time.
