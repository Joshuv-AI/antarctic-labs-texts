M&A reality check: A longitudinal study of operational cost performance in U.S. hospitals
Authors: Yousef Abdulsalam, Mikaella Polyviou, Eugene Schneller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hospital mergers and acquisitions (M&As) reduce resource dependence on sources of input and purchasers of output, via market power, service diversification, and operational efficiency. While prior research has examined M&A effects on quality and pricing, less is known about their effects on supply chain cost performance—a core input to health care delivery and a frequently cited but not always realized driver of consolidation.Purpose:To determine the impact of hospital M&As on acquired hospital operating costs, in terms of magnitude and timing, and compare possible efficiencies in supply expenses relative to other expense categories.Methodology:We analyzed a longitudinal data set of U.S. acute care hospitals (2016–2021) using propensity score matching and panel regression. This approach isolated the effect of merger events on total operating expenses, salary expenses, supply expense categories (medical-surgical and pharmaceutical), and net income.Findings:Acquired hospitals experienced significant reductions in total operating expenses in the postmerger period. These savings were largely attributable to streamlined payroll costs. We observed a modest reduction in medical-surgical supply costs 1 year post-acquisition, but no significant change in pharmaceutical supply costs.Practice Implications:Supply chain cost savings from hospital acquisitions are easy to imagine but elusive to realize. Hospital leaders should first seek targets whose supply chain operations, strategies, and partners fit their own, and proactively communicate and integrate supply chain operations during post-acquisition planning to realize the anticipated operational synergies.

Mergers and acquisitions (M&As) have become increasingly prevalent in the U.S. health care sector over the past several decades. In the fourth quarter of 2023, for example, over 500 M&As in the health sector were announced, with a sizable portion involving health care organizations and the pharmaceutical sector (Becker, 2024). This continued wave of consolidation can be attributed to strategic ambitions, competitive pressures, and institutional pressures. In this context, many hospital leaders view mergers as a pathway to stabilize finances and enhance competitiveness by integrating into larger health care networks.

One of the central strategic motivations behind hospital M&As is the pursuit of greater operational efficiency and cost reduction. By combining organizations, hospitals aim to achieve economies of scale, eliminate redundant services, and spread high fixed costs over a larger volume of activity​ (Haleblian et al., 2009). In line with the resource dependence theory (Pfeffer & Salancik, 2003), health systems consolidate to better manage their dependencies with their sources of inputs (such as medical equipment suppliers) and the purchasers of their outputs (the payers and covered population). Consolidated health systems leverage their expanded size to streamline operations, standardize processes, and strengthen bargaining with both suppliers and payers. These potential efficiencies are frequently cited to justify hospital acquisitions and make a case that larger “integrated” systems can deliver care at a lower cost per patient​.

A focus area where acquired hospitals seek to reduce dependency and realize efficiencies is in supply chain operations. Supply chain expenses (including medical supplies, pharmaceuticals, devices, and related logistics) account for a significant portion of hospital operating costs—second only to labor (Abdulsalam & Schneller, 2019). As a result, integrating supply chain management across merging hospitals is often seen as a key area for cost containment, especially in domains such as procurement and inventory management​ (Mariani et al., 2022). By consolidating vendors, standardizing product lines, and purchasing products in bulk, merged systems may improve their purchasing power and eliminate waste. Likewise, industry studies suggest that a more coordinated supply chain could save an average hospital up to 9.9 million U.S. dollars annually in operating expenses through efficiencies such as reduced inventory and improved contracting (Lopez & McKevitt, 2017).

Despite the compelling strategic rationale for hospital M&A, empirical evidence on whether M&As deliver on promised operational improvements remains mixed. A growing body of research has raised questions about the realized benefits of hospital mergers for both costs and quality. On one hand, some studies align with the industry perspective and report modest efficiency gains (May et al., 2021; Schmitt, 2017). On the other hand, other studies find little to no improvement—and sometimes deterioration—in key performance outcomes following consolidation. A study of 246 hospital mergers found no significant improvements in clinical quality or patient outcomes after acquisition and even detected a modest decline in patient experience ​(Beaulieu et al., 2020). Earlier literature documented that hospital mergers often lead to higher prices for payers without commensurate quality gains, undermining the argument that consolidation inherently produces more efficient or superior care (Dafny et al., 2016). In short, while merged hospitals may achieve greater market clout, the extent to which they realize operational efficiencies—especially in ways that translate into lower costs or better outcomes—remains unclear.

Contribution

Relatively little scholarly attention has been paid to the post-acquisition changes in operational functions within hospitals. This represents an important gap in the literature. Research on costs within hospitals and markets, particularly in the context of market concentration, has not fully probed the factors influencing supply chain costs (Robinson et al., 2012). While research on hospital supply chains has advanced significantly in recent years, further empirical research on the role of supply chain management in merger activities can provide theoretical and managerial contributions.

Our study addresses the above gap by investigating the longitudinal impact of hospital acquisitions on operational efficiency, with a specific focus on supply chain costs in U.S. acute care hospitals. In contrast to cross-sectional or short-term analyses in prior literature, we track merging hospitals’ performance over an extended period—from pre-acquisition to several years post-acquisition—to observe whether anticipated efficiencies materialize as integration progresses. We assemble panel data on supply-related cost indicators and other operational metrics for a large sample of acute care hospitals that underwent mergers, alongside comparable nonmerging hospitals as a control group. We then evaluate whether hospitals involved in M&As achieve greater improvements in cost efficiency (e.g., reductions in supply expense per patient or total operating cost per admission) compared with their peers. This research offers new evidence on the extent and timing to which hospital consolidation translates into operational efficiencies. The findings contribute to the health care management literature by clarifying the sources of cost efficiencies and informing hospital executives and policymakers about the realistic expectations when estimating supply chain synergies during the planning of an acquisition.

Research Questions

Our inquiry into the vast field of M&As in the health sector focuses on their impact on supply chain performance in terms of cost efficiency. Specifically, we seek to tackle two research questions. First, if there is a reduction in operating expense postmergers, to what extent do supply chain efficiencies contribute to this overall observed cost reduction? Second, how soon after a merger are these operating efficiencies realized? Quantitatively, we address these questions by longitudinally observing expense metrics across multiple years for acquired hospitals both before and after the acquisition to estimate the expected versus actual cost trajectory. Background

The U.S. hospital environment provides health care through more than 5,000 hospitals and thousands of other outpatient facilities, networked into 400 health care systems (American Hospital Association, 2024). The largest of these health systems, also referred to as integrated delivery networks (IDNs), includes over 180 hospitals with over 30,000 beds in total. Today, ~68% are clustered within health systems (up from about 50% in the 1990s), with 76% of total hospital beds being within systems. These large and abundant networks are the culmination of more than 50 years of ongoing mergers, acquisitions, and consolidations.

Resource dependence theory examines how organizations manage their dependencies on their sources of input and channels of output to consolidate power and ensure survival and growth (Hillman et al., 2009). Resource dependence considers multiple drivers that motivate hospital acquisitions: improving supply chain efficiencies (“inputs”), improving quality, increasing market power, and accessing new markets (managing the “output” channels). In measuring the overall financial performance of health systems, Bazzoli et al. (2000) found results that are consistent with resource dependence, where moderately centralized systems overperformed both highly centralized and decentralized systems. However, the question remains as to which resource dependence motivator—managing sources of inputs, purchasers of outputs, or internal dependencies—is the primary driver of financial performance.

Reducing Dependence on Sources of Input—Pursuing Economic Efficiency

Economic efficiency is among the most cited motives for establishing multihospital systems, and much research on M&As has considered the different mechanisms that drive mergers toward efficient operations (Haleblian et al., 2009). In health care, this scale is most often realized in exerting more power and control over the sources of input, enabling bulk purchasing, more favorable contracting, and logistics consolidation. However, as sound as the theory is in predicting value from consolidation, translating the resource consolidation to financial performance has proven challenging.

The results of studies between 1980 and 2003 largely agreed that multihospital arrangements did not appear to provide significant cost savings for the participating hospitals (Bazzoli et al., 2000, 2004). A study scrutinizing M&As on total cost per patient discharge between 2000 and 2010 found a cost reduction of 4%–7% (Schmitt, 2017). The author notes that these cost savings take time to realize, and that savings are not realized for 4 years after the merger date (acquired hospital cost trajectories were no different from control hospitals in the first 2 years). Schmitt (2017) also notes that acquirer hospitals did not show any significant savings. Thus, there is no evidence that the acquirer is gaining efficiencies, innovations, or technologies that directly impact its operating performance from the hospital it absorbs. Changing the operational processes of the acquirer and acquired hospitals involves political and behavioral factors.

Using a more recent data sample, the American Hospital Association observed a 3.3% reduction in operating expenses per admission in the year following the merger between 2009 and 2019 (May et al., 2021). Furthermore, the study observed a reduction in net patient revenue by 3.7%. Both studies considered total operating costs (adjusted for admissions and hospital intensity) without considering the different cost categories.

Reducing Dependence on Purchasers of Output—Enhancing Market Power

Gaining power over the purchasers of an organization’s outputs is another driver explored in resource dependence theory. Health systems, with a wider array of services, decrease their dependence on external players in the health care value chain ecosystem. Resource dependence theory aligns with the market power hypothesis, where firms engage in horizontal mergers to reduce the number of firms in the industry, increasing the firm’s pricing power (Stillman, 1983). In observing the market power hypothesis in the hospital acquisitions context, research noted that “by engaging in mergers, hospitals can take advantage of synergistic impacts; by becoming larger forces in the market”(Harris et al., 2000, p. 801). Studies examining the market power of hospitals in alliance activities have consistently found that alliances can raise prices in the market, ultimately improving revenue and profitability (Bazzoli et al., 2004). Moriya et al. (2010), for example, estimated that market prices for health services increase between 5% and 20% as a result of hospital M&As.

Reducing Dependence on Professional Autonomy—Standardization and Quality Control

It is well-established that professional autonomy, via specialized knowledge and clinical decision-making, provides physicians with substantial power in the health care setting (Burns, 2021). Health care service consolidation via systemization and M&A activity is one mechanism administrators utilize to rebalance power in their favor. Higher degrees of systemization and centralization of systems can consolidate power and increase alignment with physicians, with the ultimate goal to increase standardization and quality (Li et al., 2025). Health care quality is most often operationalized via clinical outcomes such as a hospital’s annual case-mix adjusted mortality rates, patient safety indicators, or adverse events as outlined by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. The American Hospital Association study that scrutinized M&A activities between 2009 and 2019 was largely inconclusive when assessing quality improvements from such activities (May et al., 2021). A similar study by Beaulieu et al. (2020) measured multiple dimensions of quality and found performance improvement on a clinical-process measure, but not on mortality rates or readmissions. Ho and Hamilton (2000) compared the quality of care for hospitals before and after a hospital merger or alliance and found no significant differences resulting from the merger. A study examining the effect of hospital mergers on 15 quality indicators found few significant, positive associations between mergers and quality improvements (Westra et al., 2022). Most recently, Burns et al. (2026) contributed to this branch of literature by synthesizing the previous literature and laying out the multiple theoretical mechanisms that connect hospital consolidation with higher quality. Their work documents “ambivalent” effects of consolidation on quality, noting intermediary conditions that need to be present before quality gains accumulate.

In this study, we focus on the input-driven resource dependencies that health systems wish to achieve with hospital acquisitions. Few studies have examined postmerger outcomes longitudinally, and even fewer have looked at supply-chain-related cost efficiencies.

Methods

Data, Sample, and Variables

The primary unit of analysis for this study is the acquired hospital. The two key data components needed for our analysis are data on hospital M&A events and hospital financial data before and after the M&A event. We focused on M&As that could be linked to at least 2 years of hospital financial data before and 2 years after the M&A, so that long-term financial outcomes could be measured. That is, for each hospital, we collected at least 5 years of data. Since this study required financial data from secondary sources, no ethical approval of the research was required.

To develop our data set, we merged the Change of Hospital Ownership (CHOW) data set, which is publicly available from the Center for Medicare and Medicaid, with Definitive Healthcare’s data set using the CMS provider number. The Definitive Healthcare database is a proprietary hospital-centric database that covers many aspects of hospitals, including financial, operational, and clinical metrics, as well as affiliations with organizations and physicians (Definitive Healthcare, 2024). This database is utilized in both management and clinical research (Beauvais et al., 2023; Wiesenthal et al., 2025).

In total, we matched 255 hospitals that went through an acquisition across the two databases. Some M&A activities were not matched because some hospitals were outside our sample’s scope, such as nonacute hospitals (e.g., rehabilitation or psychiatric hospitals), and hospitals with fewer than 25 beds due to incomplete financial data. Our sample covers the years 2016–2021. We chose this time window because many key financial measures of interest to our study (e.g., supply expenses) were not reported by this database before 2016, while data after 2021 were not consistently available. Our outcome of interest is a hospital’s operating expenses and several subcategories of expenses: payroll expenses, medical-surgical supplies expense, and pharmaceutical supplies. We also measure the effect of a hospital’s acquisition on net income. These metrics are reported by hospitals annually and were available via the Definitive Healthcare data set.

Our primary predictor variable is the acquisition event. We collected information about M&As using the CHOW data set. This data set contains transactions involving changes of ownership (i.e., acquisitions, mergers, or consolidations) in U.S. hospitals since January 2016, indicating the acquirer, the target, and the effective date of the change. The CHOW data was used to identify the date of an M&A event, and a binary variable wascreated to indicate whether an M&A event occurred in a specific hospital in a specific year (i.e., hospital-year). M&As can take months or even years to complete; therefore, we tracked hospital financial performance for multiple years postmerger. Other dummy variables were included to indicate whether an M&A occurred in a previous or subsequent year. For example, a dummy variable labeled “two-year postmerger” equals 1 if a hospital underwent an M&A 2 years ago. The postmerger indicators are important for identifying long-term and gradual changes in hospital financials that result from an M&A activity.

All control variables were collected from the Definitive Healthcare database. The case-mix index (CMI) captures the intensity of the hospital’s patient mix. It is a composite score based on the diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) of patients the hospital admitted over the year. A high case-mix index indicates that more complex patients (requiring more resources) were treated at the hospital. It is commonly used as an adjustment factor for patient admissions when calculating expense ratios, that is, supply expense per case-mix-adjusted patient. The market concentration index is an application of the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), which measures the intensity of competition a hospital faces. To control for hospital size, we collected the number of staffed hospital beds, which may vary from year to year. Annual patient discharges is a proxy for patient volume at the hospital. Case-mix index, market concentration index, number of staffed beds, and patient discharges are common control variables used in similar studies that assess the financial performance of hospitals (Li et al., 2025; Zepeda et al., 2016).

Other data were collected to provide descriptive statistics about the hospitals and to compare acquired versus nonacquired hospitals (Table 2). They have been excluded from the regression analysis to avoid multicollinearity. These other data include rural versus urban, hospital type, academic health center status, and whether the hospital is affiliated with a health system.

Empirical Model

Health systems do not select hospitals to acquire randomly but based on strategic forecasts and characteristics that make them desirable acquisition targets. This fact introduces a sample selection bias that needs to be accounted for. To address this potential risk of self-selection endogeneity, we applied a matching procedure to our full data sample to obtain a subset that approximates a randomized controlled trial. Propensity score matching (PSM) is an important method to reduce self-selection bias in management research that employs difference-in-difference measurement models (Tubik & Herberger, 2024). The drawback of applying PSM is excluding a large portion of the data, thereby reducing statistical power. In the robustness section, we compare the results of running our models with and without applying PSM.

Recently published guidance in the Journal of Operations Management is applied in this context (Yılmaz et al., 2024). First, a logit regression model was estimated with the treatment variable (the merger event) as the dependent variable and covariates that may influence the treatment and the primary outcome. The selected covariates were case mix index, number of staffed beds, market concentration index, and number of patient discharges. In measuring the distance between two observations based on their covariates, a propensity score using nearest neighbor matching was applied (Yılmaz et al., 2024). This was executed in R’s MatchIt package and resulted in a data set with 2,214 hospital-year observations split evenly between treatment and control groups whose covariates did not differ significantly. Our approach follows Zepeda et al.’s (2016) robustness check, which applied PSM on similar empirical hospital data.

The difference-in-differences (DiD) model has become a common standard for quasi-experimental settings, measuring treatment and control trends before and after an event. However, the basic DiD model is limited to only two groups and two time periods. Since our research questions consider the trajectory of costs over multiple postmerger years, we apply a more generalized version of the DiD model, the two-way fixed effects DiD model, also known as a Dynamic DiD model. A Hausman test wasconducted (χ2=45.216, df=13, p < .001), confirming that a fixed effects model is more robust (but less efficient) than the random effects model due to possible correlation between individual-specific effects and the independent variable (Kennedy, 2008). While a fixed effects model’s nature limits our ability to observe differences across hospitals in merger outcomes, it controls for time-invariant confounders, addresses omitted variable bias, and focuses on the main research question at hand: how hospital expenses are impacted by a merger in previous and subsequent years. Time-varying confounders within a hospital setting are accounted for via their inclusion in the fixed effect model as control variables, such as case mix index, patient discharges, number of staffed beds, and the competitive environment (as measured by the market concentration index).

Models 1 through 4 consider various expense categories. In Model 1, where operating expense is the dependent variable, hospitals demonstrated significantly lower expenses in the year of the merger and the 2 years following the merger, as compared with other business-as-usual hospitals. We then ran models with both medical-surgical supply expenses and pharmaceutical expenses to better identify the driver of lower costs. Model 2 (medical-surgical supply expense) was only statistically significant at p < .10, and the reduction in supply expense is only demonstrated 1-year postmerger event. No effect on pharmaceutical expense was detected related to the hospital acquisition event, as presented in Model 3, while the effects of the control variables are consistent with those in the previous models. Model 4 (salary expense) shows more consistent results with Model 1, where merged hospitals demonstrated cost reduction in salary expense for 2 consecutive years postmerger.

Model 5 considered net income as the dependent variable. The results show little evidence that mergers significantly impact an acquired hospital’s net income. It is also worth noting that the overall model fit is significantly lower than that in the other models, as measured by the coefficient of determination (R 2). Only the number of staffed beds was significant among the control variables. Furthermore, unlike the expense-based models, not all year fixed effects were significant. Noteworthy is the coefficient for year 2022 across the expense models (Models 1–4), which shows a significant jump in magnitude compared with smaller and incremental increases across previous years since the 2016 baseline. It is likely that this exceptional increase in 2022’s cost average is associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, although we do not have evidence to confirm this hypothesis.

Robustness Checks

We ran additional analyses to assess the robustness of our results and the sensitivity of our models. First, we considered multiple metrics as proxies for hospital size, including the number of inpatient days and hospital discharges. These metrics were highly correlated with each other and, therefore, interchangeable in the analysis. The models in Table 4 included the two least-correlated measures: staffed beds and patient discharges. To assess the risk of multicollinearity, we estimated the variance inflation factors (VIFs) of our predictor variables. Specifically, we estimated the adjusted generalized variance inflation ( GVIF1/(2×df) ) factor to account for the mix of continuous and categorical variables in our data (Fox & Monette, 1992). All adjusted GVIFs were below the accepted threshold of 5, indicating a low risk of multicollinearity influencing the estimated coefficients.

Second, a basic DiD model, which models two groups (treatment and control) and two periods (before and after treatment), was applied, and the results were consistent with our dynamic model, which measures on a year-by-year basis. We also ran models that included a wider before-and-after event window, including two binary premerger variables and three binary postmerger variables. This reduced the sample size due to data availability and did not change our overall results, nor were any of these variables significant.

Finally, we ran the same model without the PSM process to account for self-selection, which significantly increased our sample size and statistical power. We arrived at the same results, with higher statistical significance on multiple variables, which may be attributed to the larger sample size. For example, in the med-surg supply expense model (Model 2), the 1-year postmerger indicator was significant at p <.05 rather than at p <.10. Thus, our PSM-based models (Table 4) provide more conservative results.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the impact of hospital mergers on supply chain performance, as proxied by supply expenses at the acquired hospital in the years after the merger. As discussed in the background section, much research examines the motivators and impacts of hospital mergers, both planned and unintended. Supply chain performance, however, is among the areas with limited and mixed research outcomes. With a large empirical data set covering thousands of U.S. hospitals and 7 years of financial data, we measured whether planned cost savings materialize, which cost categories were the primary drivers of savings, and how long after the merger it takes to achieve significant cost reductions. Our results show significant cost savings in total operating expenses postmergers. Salary expense accounts for most of the sustained savings in operating expenses. As resource dependence theory may predict, our results may reflect a consolidation of power and a reduction in health care personnel. At the same time, there was modest evidence of a reduction in supply expenses in the first year postmerger, with a lower magnitude. Past research estimates that the cost savings in administrative and operating expenses from hospital mergers are modest, between 2% and 4% (Craig et al., 2021). Economists have deduced that consolidation for market control and expansion of the services portfolio are the most common motivators for hospital mergers (Dafny et al., 2016).

With supply expenses, empirical evidence is difficult to come by, and anecdotal evidence is mixed. Postmerger med-surg supply reduction is also observed in the first year after the merger, but subsequent years show no effect. System-wide integration reduces redundancy, leverages standardization, aggregates procurement, and pools inventories, all of which are common cost-cutting mechanisms that do not compromise service levels or quality. However, two hospitals with different group purchasing organizations, different procurement structures, different services, and different levels of physician power in supply selection (in other words, different supply management philosophies) may not only miss out on cost savings but may also experience a temporary increase in cost until the consolidated supply chain gets smoothed out. In terms of pharmaceutical supply expenses, merger activity appears to have no effect. Pharmaceutical supply management is an area of hospital materials management that is more mature and less nuanced than medical-surgical supply management. We interpret the quantitative results as indicating that there is less room for improvement in pharmaceutical procurement following hospital mergers, given the high levels of supply standardization, traceability, and regulation (Burns, 2021). Unlike medical device manufacturers, pharmaceutical distributors are abundant, pricing is competitive, and product tracking is well-established via the National Drug Code database managed by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.

The year fixed effects confirm that hospital operating expenses have been consistently rising since 2016 (the baseline case). Since the variable coefficients are standardized, we can directly compare their magnitude. In 2022, baseline hospital costs spiked significantly compared with previous years. This could be due to pandemic pressures, but we lack data to directly demonstrate causality.

The final model, which estimated net income as the dependent variable, provided some interesting insights that differ from those observed from the expense models. No effect on net income from the merger activity was detected. The patient discharges control variable, which does not have a significant association with net income, is somewhat surprising and may reflect a wider range in health services pricing (rather than more stable costs) or other nuances related to the revenue cycle. Thus, these findings suggest that improvements in hospital productivity result from more efficient inputs rather than higher output. Furthermore, the net income of acquired hospitals did not show the same significant and consistent incremental yearly increases as operating expenses. The only year with a significant spike in net income (relative to the 2016 baseline) was 2021. This may be attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic, given the spike in hospitalizations during the year, but this cannot be confirmed with the data at hand. Perhaps this finding relates to the transfer of patients with high levels of acuity to other hospitals within the system. The variance in net income for a hospital across years is much larger than that of operating expenses (and the model’s overall statistical significance is lower as a result), which may also partly explain the lack of significant variables in this model.

Practical Implications

Hospital and health system mergers have been much studied, but not from a supply chain impact perspective. A systematic review of the barriers and facilitators of successful hospital mergers, which included 18 quantitative studies and 31 qualitative studies across multiple countries, identified reduced costs, increased efficiency, and reduced duplication among the facilitators of successful mergers (Keane et al., 2018). However, supply chain management did not emerge as a focal area. This is surprising given that supply chain expenses represent the second largest expense category after payroll. While factors associated with the integration of the health care supply chain for medical supplies are considered for individual hospitals, how these factors come together during hospital acquisitions remains unclear.

As resource dependence theory suggests, aligning the resource bases of acquiring and acquired entities is critical for successful merger execution. Beyond evaluating the potential for service, contract, or input consolidation, leaders should assess where power and dependencies reside—specifically, which stakeholder groups within the merging hospitals control which resources. Overlooking these power dynamics can provoke resistance and hinder integration, as resource dependence theorypredicts. Complicating matters further are the inherent power asymmetries within health care delivery systems themselves—between patients, physicians, suppliers, payers, and administrators (Burns, 2021). One effective approach, as noted in conversations with multiple health systems that have executed successful integrations, involves forming interdisciplinary, cross-organizational teams early in the acquisition planning process. These teams persist well beyond deal closure and are anchored by carefully crafted performance metrics—some hospital-specific, others aligned with broader system-level goals.

Effective supply chain integration requires a deliberate, multilevel review of interorganizational relationships: (1) group purchasing memberships and distributor affiliations, (2) supplier contracts and partnerships, and (3) product-level decisions influenced by physician approval and preferences. When alignment exists across these levels, opportunities may emerge to leverage the combined entity’s scale to negotiate cost reductions, consistent with lower transaction costs resulting from internal consolidation. Where discrepancies arise, especially for clinically equivalent products, value analysis teams—traditionally tasked with reviewing new product entries and contract renewals—should evaluate equivalence and collaborate with clinical users to reach consensus. Such coordinated efforts can facilitate supplier rationalization and price improvements, aligning with evidence-based practices in health care supply chain integration (Montgomery & Schneller, 2007).

Case evidence indicates that poorly managed integration efforts can lead to persistent redundancies, cultural misalignment, and stakeholder resistance that ultimately negate potential efficiency gains (Westra et al., 2022). Within supply chain management, acquired hospitals often encounter substantial obstacles in harmonizing formularies, inventory systems, and supplier contracts, particularly when legacy organizations operate under distinct processes and governance structures. The degree of “fit” between merging entities—encompassing corporate culture, operational strategies, and existing strategic suppliers or alliances—represents a critical yet difficult-to-measure determinant of merger success (Chae et al., 2022).

Our study demonstrates that M&As in U.S. acute care hospitals can yield measurable operational benefits, particularly in the form of reduced total operating expenses driven largely by streamlined administrative and salary cost structures. While modest improvements in medical-surgical supply costs are achievable in the first postmerger year, pharmaceutical expenses remain largely unaffected. These findings suggest that the anticipated supply chain synergies often highlighted during merger negotiations may not be fully realized without targeted integration strategies.

Study Limitations

While our study provides valuable insights into the impact of mergers on supply chain cost performance in U.S. acute care hospitals, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, merger events occur in different years across the acquired hospitals. Hence, we acknowledge the risk of treatment effect heterogeneity, which can bias the coefficients of pre- and postmerger indicator variables (Sun & Abraham, 2021). Second, we recognize that annual financial indicators, as proxies for supply chain performance, limit our ability to fully understand supply chain dynamics during a postmerger integration effort: consolidating suppliers, simplifying the supply base, integrating technologies, or integrating inventories and logistics. Third, we did not have access to more granular data (i.e., quarterly rather than annual data) to better gauge merger integration progress, as mergers that occur in Q1 of the year have a “head-start” over those that occur in Q4 of the same year for next year’s financial cycle. Fourth, while we use the official data of the merger based on the CHOW data set, we cannot be sure that integration efforts began at that time, since, in some instances, acquiring systems and acquired hospitals may begin planning the alignment of their systems, technologies, and operations long before the merger officially goes through (or delay integration even after a hospital has been formally acquired). Fifth, the study focuses on M&A targets that are acute care hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other common health care acquisition targets, such as physician practices, specialized hospitals, and surgical centers.

Future Research

Our informal discussions with large systems with a thirst for growth demonstrated significant premerger planning and dedicated postmerger resources. For organizations undergoing infrequent mergers, “merger readiness” need not be a core competency. However, understanding expectations and the ability to craft a playbook that addresses the points above are worth the investment. Analysts suggest that “organizations should consider what they are willing to give up, what the culture of the new board will be, and how decisions will be made after the merger” (Stempniak, 2014). This is consistent with previous research suggesting that organizational culture is a dominant factor in successful mergers (Burns et al., 2026; Dooley & Zimmerman, 2003). Indeed, mergers have frequently been derailed because of a lack of cultural fit, with the failed DaimlerChrysler merger being a commonly-citedexample (Badrtalei & Bates, 2007). Fit—whether cultural, clinical, or supply chain—may prove to be significant in predicting the success of mergers in health care and most other industries (Hertel et al., 2024; Tarba et al., 2019).

Moreover, incorporating additional performance outcome measures—such as service quality, resilience, procurement efficiency, and perhaps even supply network metrics—will fine-tune understanding of merger impacts on the supply chain. Such constructs need high-quality metrics that are more reliable and valid than annual financial metrics. Thus, case study methodology or survey research may be better suited to tackling questions about the mechanisms that drive acquisition-driven success.

Additionally, the year fixed effects show a clear spike in baseline costs, evidenced by the much greater coefficient for year 2022 compared with previous years. The COVID-19 pandemic is the obvious confounding factor in this period, but we lacked data on expenses in subsequent years to comment further on cost trajectories: how long did it take for hospital costs to subside back to their previous trajectory, if they did at all?

We analyzed the acquired hospital’s performance,not the acquirer’s. We notethat research on the efficiencies of acquirer organizations may also be of interest. While research has looked at how mergers increase an acquirer’s service diversification, revenue, and market power, research has much to reveal about the potential logistical benefits from acquisitions for the acquirer. Simply stated: to what extent can the “little guy” (the acquired organization) motivate substantial changes in the acquirer’s supply chains? Often, the acquirer is a system or organization with a portfolio of hospitals, so measuring system-level benefits that are dispersed across multiple hospitals is both a challenge and an opportunity.

Finally, we note that mergers of supply chains are not experienced solely by the acquirer and the acquired. Hospitals engage GPOs and distributors, which, as alluded to in this paper, are important ingredients in managing their resource dependence. How these organizations react and realign with merged entities requires further scrutiny. Scale provides opportunities not afforded to small organizations, such as the choice to craft a system-wide GPO, gain non-price concessions from suppliers, or perhaps develop a consolidated service center. Needed, going forward, is the unpacking of this very complex set of organizations, opportunities, and strategies.
