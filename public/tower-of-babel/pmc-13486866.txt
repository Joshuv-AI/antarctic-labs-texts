Beyond neural oscillations: Stress-related aperiodic activity and aperiodic-oscillatory spectral covariation
Authors: Kar Fye Alvin Lee, Li Liang, Suhail T. Asharaf, Tatia M.C. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryConventional electroencephalography analyses do not distinguish neural oscillations from aperiodic activity, limiting our ability to elucidate specific neural processes underlying acute stress. The present study addressed this gap by disentangling aperiodic and oscillatory activity to examine their associations with acute stress. One hundred and forty-four adults completed a baseline session and the Montreal Imaging Stress Task, during which electroencephalography was recorded. Results revealed a flatter 1/f exponent over the lateral centrotemporoparietal scalp regions during the stress condition. Additionally, there was widespread attenuation of beta activity and enhancement of delta activity during the stress condition. There was also a stress-related decrease in theta activity, but only over the prefrontal, midline frontocentral, and lateral temporoparietal regions. Notably, stress-related shift in aperiodic activity was differentially associated with stress-related changes in these oscillatory bands. By separating aperiodic and oscillatory activity, the study provides a clearer view of the complex neural dynamics underlying psychological stress.

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive method that measures fluctuations in electrical potentials on the scalp generated from the underlying neuronal activity,1 making it a cornerstone technique adopted in neuroscience research.2,3,4,5 One of the central analytical approaches in EEG involves examining its spectral properties, which reflect the strength of neural activity across different frequency bands.6,7 Past EEG research using spectral analysis has predominantly focused on the oscillatory power of canonical frequency bands, such as delta, theta, alpha, and beta.8,9,10 However, there is a growing appreciation for the greater nuance in characterizing the EEG power spectrum.11,12,13 The aperiodic component of the power spectrum, in particular, is increasingly recognized as a distinct spectral feature, and failing to account for it may bias the parameterization of oscillatory activity.12 Hence, disentangling aperiodic activity from oscillatory activity is essential when examining the neural dynamics underlying various psychophysiological phenomena, such as arousal and acute stress.

The aperiodic activity refers to the arrhythmic component of the EEG power spectrum, characterized by a power-law decrease in power as a function of increasing frequency, commonly referred to as 1/f-like decay.14 While initially thought to reflect the large-scale neural population dynamics involved in inhibitory-excitatory balance, whereby a steeper decay reflects greater levels of inhibition,15 more recent accounts suggest that aperiodic activity, among several other EEG metrics, may serve only as a non-specific proxy index of inhibitory-excitatory balance.16,17 Indeed, aperiodic activity has also been thought to reflect neural noise, with a flatter 1/f slope associated with greater neural noise.18 This interpretation is consistent with the neural gain control account, which posits that noradrenergic activity enhances neural signal-to-noise ratio, thereby facilitating task-relevant information processing.19,20 Supporting this interpretation, previous research has demonstrated that the 1/f slope was steeper during high cognitive control demand and varied as a function of noradrenergic activity.21 Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated that the administration of methylphenidate, a dopamine and norepinephrine agonist, also resulted in a steeper 1/f slope.22 While the functional significance of aperiodic activity is yet to be fully understood, current empirical evidence indicates its sensitivity to alterations in cognitive demand and noradrenergic activity, making it a potentially informative marker for examining the neural dynamics of arousal and acute stress.

Recent research has demonstrated that aperiodic activity varied as a function of arousal, indexed through pupil dilation, with the 1/f slope flattening as arousal increased.23 In addition, previous research examining participants under anesthesia as well as during sleep also demonstrated that the 1/f slope flattened as arousal increased.24,25 In contrast to aperiodic activity, oscillatory activity reflects synchronized rhythmic neural activity underlying a diverse range of neural processes, such as cyclical cortical excitability, information encoding, and communication between neuronal populations.26 Not surprisingly, oscillatory activity across different brain regions and frequency bands has been implicated with a wide range of neurocognitive processes, such as attention, inhibition, perception, memory, and sensorimotor processing.27 Alpha and beta oscillations, in particular, have also been implicated in arousal, such that decreased alpha and beta oscillations have been found to vary as a function of greater levels of emotional arousal.28,29,30,31 However, other research has also reported findings in the opposite direction.32,33,34 Given the role of aperiodic activity in arousal, these inconsistencies may partly reflect unaccounted variation in arousal-related aperiodic activity. By isolating oscillatory activity from the aperiodic component, a recent study found that alpha activity decreased as a function of greater emotional arousal.35 Subsequent research examining both aperiodic and oscillatory activity found that emotional arousal was not only linked to a reduction in alpha activity but also an increase in the exponent of the 1/f slope.36 Notably, this shift in aperiodic activity is opposite in direction to that of the shift associated with physiological arousal.23,24,25 Taken together, it appears that disentangling aperiodic from oscillatory activity is crucial when investigating the neural correlates of psychophysiological states.

Building on the aforementioned findings, acute stress, a heightened arousal state, provides another context to further examine whether aperiodic and oscillatory activity mirror or diverge from those observed under emotional or physiological arousal. Our stress response is the physiological, psychological, and behavioral reactivity that enables us to adapt to the demands of a dynamic environment, involving system-wide processes across our brain and body.37,38 The sympatho-adrenal-medullary and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axes are the two primary neurobiological pathways that underlie our physiological response to stressors through the release of catecholamines and glucocorticoids, respectively.38,39 These axes are in turn regulated by various cortical regions, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, which are thought to be involved in the emotional evaluation and cognitive appraisal of stressors.40 Notably, as reported in a recent review, there are multiple empirical studies demonstrating that various canonical neural oscillations varied as a function of stress.41 However, only alpha activity exhibited a robust meta-analytic effect, consistent with previously mentioned empirical evidence linking alpha attenuation to heightened arousal. Given that acute stress is characterized by the release of catecholamines (e.g., norepinephrine), it is therefore imperative to examine its effects on aperiodic activity. In addition, without accounting for the potential confounding effects of aperiodic activity, it remains unclear whether these oscillatory findings reflect true rhythm-specific changes or are driven by alterations in the underlying aperiodic dynamics.

The overarching aim of the present study was to examine alterations in aperiodic and oscillatory activity in the context of stress induction. To our knowledge, prior research investigating acute stress has primarily focused on oscillatory activity without accounting for aperiodic activity, potentially biasing the estimates of oscillatory power. This study extended prior research by separately examining aperiodic and oscillatory activity using a controlled acute stress paradigm, allowing for a more precise characterization of the neural dynamics under stress induction. The present study also investigated the covariation between aperiodic and oscillatory components, providing a more integrated account of their interplay under stress induction. Given the differential associations of emotional and physiological arousal with aperiodic activity, we hypothesized that if stress induction more closely resembles emotional arousal, it would be reflected by a steeper 1/f slope. Conversely, if stress induction more closely resembles physiological arousal, it would be characterized by a flatter 1/f slope. Building on prior research linking oscillatory activity to stress induction, we hypothesized that one or more canonical oscillatory components would remain significantly associated with stress induction even after controlling for aperiodic activity. Following the identification of stress-related aperiodic and oscillatory components, we further examined their associations to uncover more nuanced stress-related neural dynamics.

Manipulation check

There was a significant effect of condition on accuracy, F(3, 510.67) = 3,404.96, p < 0.001. Holm-correct post hoc analyses revealed that accuracy during the training condition was higher than the first stress condition, t(510.67) = 84.31, p < 0.001, the second stress condition, t(510.67) = 81.56, p < 0.001, and the third stress condition, t(510.67) = 81.57, p < 0.001. In addition, the accuracy of the first stress condition was significantly lower than the second stress condition, t(510.67) = −2.75, p = 0.019, and the third stress condition, t(510.67) = −2.75, p = 0.019. By contrast, there was no difference between the second and third stress conditions, t(510.67) = 0.00, p = 0.998. Regarding reaction time, there was also a significant effect of condition, F(3, 448.43) = 230.47, p < 0.001. Post hoc analyses revealed that reaction time during the training condition was longer than the first stress condition, t(448.43) = −17.53, p < 0.001, the second stress condition, t(448.43) = −19.91, p < 0.001, and the third stress condition, t(448.43) = −23.25, p < 0.001. In addition, the reaction time during the first stress condition was longer than the second, t(448.43) = −2.40, p = 0.017, and third stress conditions, t(448.43) = −5.76, p < 0.001. Furthermore, the reaction time of the second stress condition was significantly longer than the third stress condition, t(448.43) = −3.36, p = 0.002. These results indicate that the MIST was successful in manipulating behavioral performance through individualized, adaptive adjustments in task difficulty.

There were also significant differences in perceived stress across conditions, F(4, 587.46) = 328.72, p < 0.001, such that perceived stress levels were higher as the experiment progressed (Figure 1). Holm-corrected post hoc analyses revealed that perceived stress was lower during the baseline condition than the training condition, t(587.46) = −7.19, p < 0.001, lower during the training condition than the first stress condition, t(587.46) = −15.96, p < 0.001, lower during the first stress condition than the second stress condition, t(587.46) = −3.43, p = 0.001, and lower during the second stress condition than the third stress condition, t(587.46) = −2.35, p = 0.019. Overall, the results indicated a successful stress manipulation via the MIST. Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to disentangle the effects of acute stress induction on aperiodic and oscillatory activity as well as examine stress-related aperiodic-oscillatory spectral covariation. Regarding aperiodic activity, we found that acute stress induction elicited a flatter 1/f slope. This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that a flatter 1/f slope varied as a function of increasing physiological arousal.23,24,25 By contrast, a recent study reported the opposite pattern for emotional arousal.36 Given that physiological and emotional arousal involve distinct neurobiological mechanisms,42 it is unsurprising that aperiodic activity may be differentially modulated by varying arousal contexts. Drawing on the various neurophysiological functional interpretations of the 1/f slope, our findings suggest that acute stress induction is associated with a shift in the inhibitory-excitatory balance toward greater excitation15 or with an increase in neural noise.18 Arguably, the alteration in aperiodic activity is potentially due to stress-related increases in noradrenergic activity.21,22 Although the functional significance of aperiodic activity remains to be further investigated, our findings indicate that aperiodic activity varies as a function of stress induction. In addition, our study extends the literature by providing empirical evidence that acute stress induction more closely resembles general physiological arousal than emotional arousal. Furthermore, the alteration in aperiodic activity appears to be most pronounced over the lateral centrotemporoparietal scalp regions. Drawing from the literature, prior research has demonstrated heightened engagement of the salience network, which facilitates multiple stress-related neurocognitive processes, such as threat detection, rapid spontaneous responses, and energy mobilization.43 Notably, several neural regions associated with the salience network, including the temporoparietal junction and inferotemporal cortex, are located in the lateral centrotemporoparietal areas.44 Although our findings were at the scalp level, there appears to be some topographical overlap that is broadly consistent with neural regions associated with the salience network. However, given the spatial limitations of our EEG montage, the specific neuroanatomical origins of these effects cannot be ascertained. Future research employing higher-density EEG with source localization techniques will be required to identify the neural generators underlying these effects.

With regard to neural oscillatory activity, we observed a general enhancement in delta activity and attenuation in beta activity across the entire scalp as a function of stress induction. In addition, theta activity also showed stress-related attenuation, but this effect was more diffusely distributed across the prefrontal, midline frontocentral, and lateral temporoparietal scalp regions. It should be noted that the neural oscillatory pattern observed in our study diverges from a previous meta-analysis, which documented substantial heterogeneity in stress-related effects on delta, theta, and beta power, as well as robust stress-related attenuation of alpha activity.41 One plausible explanation for the heterogeneity in previous findings is that these studies did not explicitly account for underlying aperiodic activity, compromising the accurate quantification of oscillatory activity. After controlling for aperiodic activity, our findings indicate that stress induction differentially modulates delta, theta, and beta activity. These canonical neural oscillations have been implicated in a variety of biological and neurocognitive processes. For instance, delta activity during cognitive processing has been thought to reflect functional cortical deafferentation through the active suppression of sensory input and deactivation of task-irrelevant neural networks.45 Theta activity has been thought to support multimodal sensorimotor integration, attention, and memory.46 Emerging evidence also suggests that theta activity, particularly in the prefrontal cortex, plays a crucial role in cognitive control and emotional regulation.47 By contrast, beta activity has been implicated in higher-order cognitive functions, such as planning, top-down control, working memory, and the maintenance of cognitive states.48 Unsurprisingly, stress has been demonstrated to have widespread effects on some of these aforementioned functions.40,49 Hence, it appears that separating aperiodic from oscillatory activity is crucial for uncovering distinct stress-related patterns of alteration in rhythmic neural activity.

Further investigation of stress-related aperiodic-oscillatory spectral covariations indicates that the relationship between aperiodic and oscillatory activity during stress induction may vary across different frequency bands. In particular, stress-related flattening of aperiodic activity was associated with both stress-related enhancement of delta power and attenuation of beta activity across the scalp, which is broadly consistent with the effects of stress induction on each individual component. By contrast, aperiodic activity showed a more spatially heterogeneous pattern of associations with theta activity, with central midline theta activity increasing and temporoparietooccipital theta activity decreasing as a function of aperiodic activity flattening. These spatially heterogeneous findings suggest that the effects observed over the temporoparietooccipital scalp regions were consistent with stress induction but not those observed over the central midline scalp regions. Taken together, these findings suggest that stress-related effects and their associated spectral covariations exhibit partially dissociable spatial organizations across aperiodic and different oscillatory activity. In addition, the spatial distribution of aperiodic-oscillatory spectral covariations did not fully overlap with the spatial distribution of the stress-related effects on each component, suggesting only partial spatial convergence across these spectral components. Future research is therefore needed to clarify the functional relevance of these spatially specific interactions and to determine the causal pathways through which they mediate stress-related neural dynamics. Importantly, it appears that the relationship between aperiodic and theta dynamics may be region-specific and partially independent of the stress-related effect on either component, reflecting complex neural dynamics beyond the effects of stress induction.

By disentangling aperiodic and oscillatory activity, our findings provide key insights into stress-related neural dynamics. Collectively, these findings extend prior work by jointly characterizing aperiodic and oscillatory dynamics under acute stress induction and revealing how their relationship varies across frequency bands and scalp regions. First, our study highlights the necessity of separating aperiodic and oscillatory components, as neglecting the aperiodic component of the EEG spectrum may conflate the effects of distinct neural processes, potentially leading to inconsistent findings or misinterpretations. Second, acute stress induction broadly influences both aperiodic and oscillatory neural activity throughout the scalp, with each component exhibiting distinct modulation patterns. Third, the interplay between aperiodic and theta activity was not uniform but varied across regions, suggesting a frequency- and region-specific effect beyond that of stress-related effects on either component. In summary, our study highlights the importance of considering both aperiodic and oscillatory components of the EEG power spectrum, as their interplay provides insight into the complex neural dynamics underlying the brain’s flexible response to acute stress. These findings suggest that acute stress modulates aperiodic and oscillatory activity in a dissociable yet interacting manner, such that their relationship is frequency- and region-specific.

Limitations of the study

This study, however, has several limitations that should be noted. First, our sample was predominantly healthy young adults. This choice was made to minimize the risk of potential health complications from inducing acute stress in individuals with undiagnosed cardiovascular disease, which is more prevalent among older adults.50 Therefore, caution is needed when generalizing these findings to other populations. Second, the training condition always preceded the stress condition without counterbalancing. Counterbalancing was challenging because participants’ average response time during training was required for the individualization of the time limit in subsequent stress conditions. Third, as the MIST combines time pressure and social evaluative threat as the primary stressors, the observed effects may partly reflect responses to these factors, which cannot be fully dissociated within the present paradigm. In addition, other potential confounding factors, such as general arousal, mental fatigue, cognitive load, and sustained engagement, may also have contributed to the observed effects. Given the individualized adaptive nature of the MIST, several task-related factors may also act as potential confounders, such as reaction time, accuracy, and the number of completed trials. Future studies should independently manipulate these factors to better isolate their respective contributions to aperiodic and oscillatory dynamics. Lastly, using fixed frequency bands to parameterize canonical oscillatory activity may not fully capture interindividual variability in spectral peaks. It should be noted that this approach was adopted to facilitate comparability with previous stress-related studies. Future work could address this limitation by adopting individualized frequency bands or data-driven spectral parameterization methods to more precisely quantify the oscillatory components.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee, the University of Hong Kong (Reference Number: EA230490). A total of 144 adults with ages ranging from 18 to 44 years old participated in this study (Mage = 24.28; SDage = 4.46; 43.75% Males).

Method details

Procedure

On arrival, participants were required to provide written consent after reading the information sheet. The electroencephalography device was then fitted on the participants. Thereafter, participants completed a 20-min baseline recording while seated on a sofa to establish a naturalistic physiological baseline. During this period, participants were instructed to remain relaxed and were provided with magazines to read at their discretion. Following this, participants completed the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST). Visual Analog Scale (VAS) regarding perceived stress levels was collected after every condition. Baseline salivary samples were collected before the start of the MIST. At the end of the experiment, participants were debriefed regarding the deceptive nature of the study.

Montreal Imaging Stress Task

A modified version of the MIST53 was used to induce an acute stress response in participants through a combination of time pressure and social evaluative threat. The task involved answering arithmetic questions across two conditions, training and stress. Each condition consisted of five levels, with difficulty increasing progressively. The order of the levels was randomized. Each level had a fixed duration of 1 min (for a total of 5 min per condition). Each trial consisted of the presentation of an arithmetic problem. Trial-to-trial visual and auditory feedback was presented after each trial for 500 ms. For the training condition, no time limit was imposed for each trial (i.e., self-paced), allowing for the assessment of individual baseline arithmetic performance. The arithmetic problem remained on screen until a response was provided. During the stress condition, trials were time-limited to increase task difficulty. The initial time limit for each trial was set at 90% of each participant’s mean response time for the corresponding level during the training condition. Thereafter, the time limit was adaptively adjusted on a trial-by-trial basis to either reduce or increase by 10% for every three consecutive correct responses or errors, respectively. The arithmetic problem remained on screen until a response was provided or the time limit was reached. Due to this adaptive procedure, the number of trials completed varied across participants, ensuring that task demands were maintained at a challenging level. In addition, an indicator bar was presented to the participants to indicate that they were performing below the normative performance level. The task consisted of a single training condition and three repeated stress conditions. Prior to the start of each stress condition, participants were reminded that their performance had to meet a normative standard for their data to be useful for the study. After the first and second stress conditions, the participants were informed that they did not meet the required normative performance level. To increase evaluation apprehension, an experimenter was seated behind the participants throughout the task. The entire task lasted for approximately 20 min.

Electroencephalography acquisition, preprocessing, spectral decomposition

Continuous EEG was recorded using a CGX CAMP Wireless EEG System. Nineteen electrodes were positioned on the scalp in accordance with the 10/20 system (i.e., Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8, Cz, C3, C4, T3, T4, Pz, P3, P4, P7, P8, O1, O2). Two additional electrodes were placed at M1 and M2. M1 was set as the online reference. AFz was used for grounding. The signals were amplified and converted from analog to digital at 24-bit resolution and 500 Hz sampling rate. A zero-phase finite impulse response 0.5–45 Hz bandpass filter was applied offline. The signals were re-referenced using a common average reference. Thereafter, the signals were denoised using a generalized eigenvalue decomposition-based spatial filtering approach.51,54,55,56,57 This class of denoising methods projects EEG signals onto neural and non-neural subspaces using a reference covariance matrix. Our study, in particular, employed the Generalized Eigenvalue De-Artifacting Instrument algorithm, which incorporates a biophysical EEG forward model to construct a reference covariance matrix for constraining the decomposition process.51 The EEG signal was first separated into ten frequency bands using the Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform with dyadic scaling. The approximate band was omitted (upper frequency bound ≤ 0.5 Hz). For each wavelet band, generalized eigenvalue decomposition was applied separately. The resulting eigenvalues represented deviation from the reference covariance matrix. The optimal eigenvalue used for separating the neural and non-neural subspaces was identified through an iterative approach by maximizing similarity between the reconstructed clean data and reference covariance matrix, while simultaneously minimizing the similarity with residual noise. Only eigenvectors belonging to the non-neural subspace were projected back to electrode space, which was then subtracted from the wavelet-transformed EEG signal, thereby reducing contamination from non-neural sources (e.g., ocular, myogenic, and environmental artifacts). The denoised wavelet bands were then summed to reconstruct the cleaned EEG signal. Subsequently, power spectral density (PSD) was estimated separately for the baseline (last 5 min), training, and stress conditions using Welch’s method (1000-sample Hann window, 50% overlap, mean averaging, constant detrending, and density scaling) over the entire duration of each condition. To model the aperiodic activity, the 1/f function was fitted on the PSD using the Fitting Oscillations and One Over f algorithm across 2–40 Hz with a fixed mode, a peak width limit of 1–6 Hz, a maximum of 6 peaks, a minimum peak height of 0.05, and a peak detection threshold of 1.5.12 The mean R2 of the fitted 1/f function was 0.98 (SD = 0.05). In addition, there was no significant electrode × condition interaction on R2, χ2(18, 5297.64) = 12.22, p = 0.836 (see Figure 9A for associated scalp topographies). An example of the PSD at electrode T3 across the three experimental conditions, along with the corresponding fitted 1/f function, from a single participant is illustrated in Figure 9B. The fitted function was subtracted from the original PSD in log-space to obtain the residual spectrum. Salivary cortisol assay

Salivary samples were collected using the Salivette Cortisol tubes in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The collected samples were stored at −80°C after the experiment until analysis. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was used for the quantification of salivary cortisol concentration.58

Visual analog scale

The VAS was used to obtain self-reported ratings of perceived stress at a given moment. Participants indicated their current stress levels by selecting a point along a continuous line from 0 (no stress) to 100 (worst imaginable stress), with higher values reflecting greater momentary stress.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Inferential statistical analyses were conducted using the Mixed Generalized Additive Model (GAM) Computation Vehicle with Automatic Smoothness Estimation package (Version: 1.9.1) in R,52 which allows for flexible modeling of fixed and random effects.59 The first set of GAMs was used for manipulation checks on accuracy, reaction time, and perceived stress as outcome variables. Condition was included as a predictor. Gaussian distribution with identity link function was used for accuracy and perceived stress, whereas inverse Gaussian distribution with inverse link function was used for reaction time. The median was used as the measure of central tendency for intraindividual reaction time. The second set of GAMs was used to model the aperiodic (i.e., the exponent and offset of the 1/f slope) and neural oscillatory components (i.e., delta, theta, alpha, and beta) of the EEG power spectrum as separate outcome variables. The outcome variables were baseline-corrected by subtracting each participant’s corresponding baseline estimates from the training and stress conditions to account for interindividual variability in tonic EEG activity. Electrode × condition (i.e., training vs. stress) interaction term was tested on each outcome variable. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for family-wise error rate across these models. Given that there is empirical evidence supporting age, sex, and diurnal cortisol influence on EEG activity,60,61,62,63,64 we included these variables as covariates in the models. Scaled t distribution with identity link function was used. The third set of GAMs was used to examine stress-related aperiodic-oscillatory associations. Specifically, the outcome variables were the changes in oscillatory components that were sensitive to stress induction, calculated as the difference between the stress and training conditions (e.g., Δtheta). Bonferroni correction was also applied across these models. The difference in 1/f slope between the stress and training conditions (i.e., Δexponent) was included as the predictor. Additionally, electrode × Δexponent interaction term was included in these models. Age, sex, and baseline cortisol were also included as covariates. Scaled t distribution with identity link function was also used for these models. It should be noted that in cases where the two-way interactions were non-significant, we systematically reduced the models by removing the interaction terms to only evaluate the main effects. Subject was included as a random effect in all models to account for the non-independence of repeated measures. The DHARMa nonparametric test for dispersion was used to assess homoscedasticity, with cluster wild bootstrapping applied in cases where the assumption was violated. All post hoc GAM linear trends and pairwise contrasts were Holm-corrected (∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05).
