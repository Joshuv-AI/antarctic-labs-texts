Clinical and economic burden of suspected thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura in US hospitals
Authors: Shan Xing, Michael Bullano, Sarah Hale, Tasneem Lokhandwala, Sharanya Murty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSevere ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%) is the diagnostic threshold for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and is associated with various clinical symptoms, abnormal laboratory results, and long-term complications.MethodsThis retrospective, noninterventional cohort study used the Premier Healthcare Database to identify patients with ADAMTS13 activity of <10% in US hospitals from January 1, 2016, through March 31, 2020. The objective was to describe patient characteristics, laboratory results, comorbidities (as measured by the Elixhauser comorbidity index), symptoms, length of stay, treatment patterns, mortality, inpatient costs, and readmission rates (summarized descriptively). Inpatient costs were calculated as total cost to the hospital.ResultsThere were 211 patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency; 89% of patients had a TTP-related diagnosis, of whom 62% had a primary diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy. Over 80% of patients with available data had a decreased platelet count and elevated lactate dehydrogenase; schistocytes were detected in 99%. The most prevalent symptoms/complications were neurological, bleeding, and pain. Most patients (86%) had 2 or more Elixhauser comorbidities. Over 80% of patients received 1 or more TTP-related treatments, mostly plasma exchange. The mean length of stay was 11.5 days; 5% of patients died during their stay. Readmission rates at 30, 60, and 90 days were 20%, 26%, and 28%, respectively. The median (interquartile range) total inpatient cost to the hospital throughout the index admission was $33,221 ($19,431-$64,901).ConclusionPatients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency have substantial clinical burden, have high mortality and readmission rates, and generate high costs for hospitals. There is a high need for a therapy that replaces ADAMTS13, thus addressing the root cause of the symptoms and complications caused by this deficiency.Keywords: clinical burden, hospital costs, readmission rates, severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

ADAMTS13 is a protease that cleaves von Willebrand factor; when its activity is severely reduced, ultralarge von Willebrand factor multimers accumulate in blood, leading to uncontrolled platelet aggregation, adhesion, platelet consumption, and microthrombi.1 Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%) diagnostically defines thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP),2 a rare blood disorder with an annual incidence of 2 to 6 cases per million people.3 Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency can be congenital (cTTP), an autosomal-recessive condition due to mutations in the ADAMTS13 gene, or immune mediated, caused by autoantibodies that neutralize ADAMTS13 activity or bind to the protein, resulting in its accelerated clearance.4 People with this condition may visit the emergency room with vague symptoms and complications, ranging from mild confusion and nausea to stroke and seizures, as well as abnormal laboratory markers, including thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia.5

Multiple long-term complications are associated with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, though manifestation of these complications vary greatly. Studies have reported a substantial association between low ADAMTS13 levels and ischemic strokes, with ADAMTS13 activity improving the accuracy of risk prediction models for ischemic stroke beyond traditional risk factors.6,7 Higher rates of stroke have also been recorded in TTP survivors in remission compared with an age- and sex-matched control population.8 Acute kidney injury has been reported in patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, with some patients requiring renal replacement therapy and long-term dialysis.9 Increased prevalence of hypertension and depression have also been reported in patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.10

There is limited understanding of and research on severe ADAMTS13 deficiency in the hospital setting, including the frequency of ADAMTS13 testing, treatment patterns, and clinical and economic outcomes. This study aimed to describe and evaluate demographic and clinical characteristics, symptoms and treatments, inpatient outcomes, and costs of patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency in a hospital setting.

Methods

Study design.

This retrospective, observational, noninterventional cohort study used data from US patients in the Premier Health Database (PHD). The PHD is a large, payer-agnostic, US hospital–based, patient- and service-level database with information about hospital encounters, mainly from nonprofit, nongovernmental community and teaching hospitals and healthcare systems in rural and urban areas. The PHD provides laboratory results and patient-level billing and financial data, including costs to the hospital. Items billable to the patient or patient’s insurance provider are listed in the PHD charge description master (CDM), which each hospital maintains. Billable items are allocated identifier codes and prices, which are used to calculate patient bills, including full hospital and medical services.11 The study included male and female patients with an ADAMTS13 activity result of <10% (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes: 40824-5, 53622-7, 34589-2, 34590-0, 93371-3) in an inpatient or outpatient hospital setting in the United States. The index event was defined as the inpatient or outpatient hospital discharge record with the lowest ADAMTS13 activity test result during the study period (January 1, 2016-March 31, 2020).

Patient characteristics.

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race, insurance status, and other related conditions, were defined at the index event. The term “TTP-related” is used to describe diagnosis and treatment variables, as severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%) is used to diagnose TTP in clinical practice.2 A diagnosis of 1 or more of the following was deemed TTP related: thrombotic microangiopathy, thrombocytopenia purpura, thrombocytopenia, allergic purpura, qualitative platelet defects, nonthrombocytopenic purpura, and other specified hemorrhagic conditions (eTable 1). Events with a TTP-related diagnosis in the primary position or any position were identified.

Laboratory results at index date were identified and summarized, where data were available, for ADAMTS13 activity levels, platelet count, reticulocyte count, haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, mean corpuscular volume, international normalized ratio, creatinine, blood group, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), schistocyte presence, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and troponin I. For instances with multiple results for a singular laboratory test during the index hospitalization, the first test with valid results was recorded. For all tests except blood group, eGFR, and schistocytes, if the first test of the index hospitalization had more than 1 unique result, the lowest result value was recorded. For eGFR, the following steps were taken: (1) if the test had a race/sex component, the result corresponding to the appropriate race- or sex-specific test was selected; (2) if the test did not have a race/sex component or duplicates existed even after applying step 1, the lowest-value result was recorded. For schistocyte tests, the positive schistocyte test result was recorded. The PLASMIC score (a clinical tool for predicting the likelihood of TTP) was calculated using laboratory data and used to further describe the population by determining the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency/TTP. Scores were calculated using the methodology specified by Bendapudi et al12; patients were categorized as being at low (0-4), intermediate (5), or high (6-7) risk. A score was not calculated for patients who did not have results for platelet count, mean corpuscular volume, international normalized ratio, serum creatinine, or at least 1 of the following: indirect bilirubin, reticulocyte count, and haptoglobin level.

Disease symptoms/complications in patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, such as neurological, cardiovascular, respiratory, bleeding, pain, renal disease, gastrointestinal, and female reproductive symptoms, were identified based on diagnosis codes at the index event. Comorbidity was evaluated using the Elixhauser comorbidity index, which was calculated using diagnosis records at the index event. TTP-related treatments were identified during the index event using a combination of text string searches and diagnosis codes.

Inpatient outcomes and hospital characteristics.

Inpatient outcomes were captured for people whose index event occurred in the inpatient setting. Inpatient mortality was defined as death during the index admission or discharge to the hospice setting. Length of stay was recorded at the index event; readmission rate was calculated as the percentage of patients who were readmitted to the same hospital among patients with an inpatient index event within 30, 60, and 90 days of the initial index event. Readmission rates were only calculated for patients who did not die during the index event and were not discharged to hospice. Hospital characteristics at the index event were summarized and included inpatient/outpatient hospital event, region, teaching status, metropolitan status, and number of beds.

Inpatient costs.

Inpatient costs were calculated as the total cost to the hospital for the index event. Costs were adjusted to 2020 US dollars for inflation using the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index. Costs were further stratified by treatment received during the index event. Total inpatient costs, calculated from the CDM, were obtained from billing records per the cost accounting method used by each hospital and represent costs to the hospital, not charges or amounts reimbursed by the payer.11 Thus, hospital costs are the expense incurred by the hospital to deliver healthcare services to patients, not charges (the amount asked by the hospital that appears on a medical bill) or reimbursed amounts (the payment made by a third party to the hospital). Where available, some cost data were reported and stratified via Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group (MS-DRG) codes.

Results

Patient characteristics.

The initial cohort comprised 2,248 patients with ADAMTS13 test results. This analysis included 211 patients with ADAMTS13 activity of <10% (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency). By age group, the largest percentage of patients (21%) were 41 to 50 years of age; 69% were female, 43% were White, and 39% were Black. The most common payment source was commercial insurance (43%). TTP-related diagnoses.

At the index event, 89% of patients (n = 188) had a TTP-related diagnosis; this was the primary diagnosis for 68% of patients (n = 143). Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was coded as the primary reason for hospital admission in 62% of patients (n = 131), followed by immune thrombocytopenic purpura (3% [n = 7]) and unspecified thrombocytopenia (2% [n = 4]).

The most frequently used MS-DRG code was MS-DRG 546 (connective tissue disorders with complication or comorbidity; 31% of patients [n = 56]), followed by MS-DRG 545 (connective tissue disorders with major complication or comorbidity; 28% [n = 50]) and MS-DRG 547 (connective tissue disorders without complication or comorbidity, or major complication or comorbidity; 9% [n = 16]).

Laboratory parameters.

The median (interquartile range) ADAMTS13 activity level was 4% (2%-5%). The reticulocyte count was elevated in 75% of patients (n = 91/121); 83% of patients (n = 171/206) had platelet counts below the normal range. A normal eGFR was recorded for 10% of patients (n = 18/178); LDH was elevated in 87% of patients (n = 164/189). Troponin I levels were above the normal range in 71% of patients (n = 51/72); 54% of patients (n = 88/164) had undetectable levels of haptoglobin. Schistocytes were present in 99% of patients (n = 108/109); 76% of patients (n = 156/206) had hematocrit levels below the normal range. The median PLASMIC score for patients was 6; 65% (n = 101/156) had scores of ≥6. Laboratory results, including normal ranges, and PLASMIC scores are summarized in Table 2.

Symptoms and comorbidities in patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Symptoms/complications were reported in 82% of patients (n = 172) with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Neurological symptoms were experienced by 49% of patients (n = 103), bleeding by 38% (n = 80), pain by 18% (n = 37), and cardiovascular/respiratory symptoms and complications, primarily myocardial infarction, by 13% (n = 27; Figure 1). Stroke was the most common neurological complication (21% of patients [n = 44]), followed by feelings of anxiety (19% [n = 39]) and headache (15% [n = 31]; Figure 1). Thirteen percent of patients (n = 28) experienced depression. There were no identifiable predominant clusters when symptoms were grouped as neurological symptoms, purpura or other bleeding, and all other symptoms (Figure 1).

Symptoms were reported in 84 women of childbearing potential (those 15-49 years of age) with ADAMTS13 activity of <10%. Complications of pregnancy, including preeclampsia, prenatal hypertension, miscarriage, and intrauterine growth restriction, were reported in 7% of women; 5% reported excessive menstruation.

The median Elixhauser comorbidity score was 4 (Table 1), with 86% of patients (n = 182) having 2 or more Elixhauser comorbidities. The most common Elixhauser comorbidities were rheumatoid arthritis/collagen vascular disease (74% [n = 157]), uncomplicated hypertension (39% [n = 82]), fluid and electrolyte disorders (38% [n = 81]), coagulopathy (34% [n = 72]), and other neurological disorders (27% [n = 56]).

TTP-related treatments.

Eighty-one percent of patients (n = 170) received at least 1 TTP-related treatment (Figure 2). Most patients received plasma exchange (PEX) or plasma (70%); receiving PEX only (55%) was more common than receiving plasma only (15%). Sixty-eight percent of patients (n = 144) received steroids; 28% (n = 59) received rituximab. Only 1% of patients (n = 2) received caplacizumab.

The mean length of hospital stay for patients (n = 182) was 11.5 days; 95% of these patients (n = 172) were discharged to a setting other than hospice (Table 3), and 5% (n = 9) died during admission. Twenty percent of patients (n = 35) were readmitted within 30 days, 26% (n = 45) within 60 days, and 28% (n = 48) within 90 days of discharge from the index event (Table 3).

Inpatient costs.

The median hospital cost for all index admissions (n = 182) was $33,221. Median hospital costs were higher when the analysis was restricted to events with MS-DRG codes that were mapped to TMA diagnosis ($37,924; n = 122). Median hospital costs were lowest ($24,741) for cases with MS-DRG code 547 (indicating no complications) and highest ($49,156) for cases with MS-DRG code 545 (indicating major complications). The median (interquartile range) hospital cost for index inpatient admissions with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and treated with a TTP-related drug was $37,845 ($23,528-$68,882).

Discussion

This was a retrospective, observational, cohort study investigating clinical characteristics, symptoms and treatments, inpatient outcomes, and costs of patients with severe ADAMTS13 activity deficiency in a US hospital setting. Using the PHD, which reports on ADAMTS13 activity test values, uniquely allowed selection of data based on clinical results and coded claims data, like traditional claims databases. The PHD captures hospital costs via the CDM, permitting analysis of the actual costs of care associated with hospitalization of patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency instead of traditional-claims reimbursement data.

Given the presence of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%), the high proportion of cases assigned TTP-related diagnosis codes during hospital admission, and the high median PLASMIC score of this population, it is likely that these patients had TTP.2,12 The most frequent MS-DRG code logged was MS-DRG 546 (connective tissue disorders with complication or comorbidity), reflecting an expected billing pattern for patients with TTP.

Over 80% of patients in our study with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency experienced varied symptoms, demonstrating the high clinical burden of TTP in the hospital setting. The absence of clear symptoms in some patients further contributes to the difficulty recognizing and diagnosing TTP. Neurological symptoms were experienced by the largest number of patients, consistent with a previous registry study that reported neurological issues at presentation in 78% of patients with TTP.13 In our study, stroke was the most common neurological complication. Accordingly, in a case-control study, patients who had experienced acute ischemic strokes had significantly lower ADAMTS13 levels, suggesting severe ADAMTS13 deficiency as a risk factor for ischemic stroke.6 Twenty-one percent of patients with cTTP experience stroke,14 and in the long term patients with TTP in remission are more likely to develop stroke relative to the general population when matched for age and sex.8 Additionally, cost and healthcare resource utilization are increased in patients who experience a stroke independent of TTP (the average per-patient-per-year costs are estimated at $59,90015) and additional medical complications during hospitalization for stroke increase length of stay.16 While only 15% of patients in our study experienced headaches, other research has noted that headaches have a substantial impact on TTP survivors, who have higher headache severity when compared with matched controls.17

Patients also reported experiencing feelings of anxiety and depression. A previous registry study reported that 44% of patients experienced severe or moderate depression when recovering from TTP18 and that the prevalence of flashbacks and fear of relapse was high.19 Additionally, substantial costs are associated with depression, with a previous study estimating the economic burden of major depressive disorder among US adults at $326 billion, 35% of which is attributable to direct medical costs.20 Patients with TTP can experience multiple psychological comorbidities that contribute to total patient costs. Both the psychological comorbidities and increased costs may extend beyond the acute timeframe and into TTP remission.

While only 13% of patients experienced cardiovascular/respiratory complications, a previous prospective, population-based cohort study reported that increased risk of coronary heart disease in elderly patients is associated with low ADAMTS13 activity, even in the absence of established cardiovascular risk factors,21 suggesting that while patients may not present with symptoms initially, they may experience cardiovascular complications in the future. In patients recovering from TTP, research has reported that the mortality rate was increased 2-fold and that leading causes of mortality were cardiovascular disease and relapsed immune-mediated TTP (iTTP).22 In a retrospective study of 15,054 TTP hospitalizations from the National Inpatient Sample, major cardiovascular complications in patients with TTP were associated with a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate (19.7%) than that reported among those without cardiovascular complications (4.1%).23

High clinical burden may be attributed to multiple existing comorbidities in patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency in this study, as indicated by a median Elixhauser comorbidity index score of 4. Other studies have reported additional comorbidities in patients with TTP,8,10,22 including acute kidney injury in 59% of patients with TTP in one study.9 However, in this study, the low number of patients with normal eGFR may also be explained by population age.

In this analysis, laboratory results were indicative of TTP, especially decreased platelet count and hematocrit and elevated LDH and troponin I. Current diagnostic guidelines for TTP highlight that raised reticulocytes and LDH, low haptoglobin and platelet count, and the presence of schistocytes are the result of hemolysis, tissue ischemia, and thrombocytopenia.24 While there was a lack of identifiable symptom clusters in this study, the varied symptoms/manifestations reported, such as stroke, heart injury, renal impairment, and pain, are reflective of widespread multiorgan thromboses resulting from a lack of ADAMTS13 activity, which has been noted in existing literature.24,25 Similar to the data presented here, a recent review described widespread organ damage in patients with TTP whereby the heart and central nervous system were predominantly affected. Cardiac symptoms and complications included elevated troponin and acute coronary syndrome, and central nervous system complications were characterized by mild (eg, headaches, delirium) and severe (eg, strokes, seizures) symptoms.25

In this study, patients stayed 11.5 days in the hospital on average, which is consistent with a previous study that reported an average length of stay for an iTTP episode of 12 to 17 days. For relapse-related hospitalizations within 6 months, this ranged from 7 to 15 days.26 A retrospective analysis reported longer stays in patients with TTP and underlying connective tissue disorders and suggested that this was possibly related to immunosuppression and, therefore, increased infection rates.27

Most patients (95%) were discharged after treatment; however, readmission rates were high, with 28% of patients readmitted within 90 days of discharge. Though data were not collected to understand symptoms around readmission, it is hypothesized that symptom/complication recurrence triggered readmission. The survival rate for patients with TTP treated with PEX is 80% to 90%.1 Patients in this study had a mortality rate of 5% during their index admission or discharge to a hospice setting. However, the experience of living with TTP has been compared to that of a chronic disorder rather than one defined by discrete acute episodes with complete remission.19 Furthermore, TTP survivors may have an increased risk of premature mortality.10 Research has reported various impairments in cognitive function and psychological well-being in adult patients with TTP, regardless of how recent the acute episode was,19 suggesting that patients with TTP need long-term care and management even when in remission. Additionally, iTTP survivors have increased rates of adverse health complications, including hypertension and stroke,10,28 which can result in hospital readmission and contribute to greater costs incurred by the hospital. Incomplete recovery of ADAMTS13 activity when patients are in remission following an acute TTP episode has been associated with increased risk of stroke,8 suggesting that persistently low ADAMTS13 levels result in relapses and future complications that require long-term care and possible hospital readmission.

The availability of cost data in a hospital setting for patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency is limited. In this study, the median total inpatient cost incurred by the hospital was $33,221 throughout an index admission, which increased to $37,924 when the analysis was restricted to TMA-specific MS-DRG codes. A publication describing hospital costs in patients with iTTP reported mean total hospital costs of $9,996 and $29,0244 for patients with commercial and Medicare insurance, respectively.26 These costs were lower than inpatient costs in the current study; the variation may be explained by differences in the study population. In the current study, patients were younger on average and had lower mortality rates; furthermore, patients were selected based on ADAMTS13 levels of <10% and thus were more likely to genuinely have TTP. Strikingly, the hospital charges (Medicare) were reported as $195,131,26 demonstrating significant hospital loss and underscoring the high healthcare resource utilization associated with acute TTP episodes.

Current therapies used to treat TTP have limited ability to replace ADAMTS13 over the long term14,29 and are of limited prophylactic utility.30 The current standard of care for treating severe ADAMTS13 deficiency is plasma infusion or PEX; studies have estimated the cost of a single PEX procedure at $4,000 and that of a prophylactic treatment (3 separate procedures) at $12,000 per patient before a definitive diagnosis can be made.31 Mortality without PEX treatments is high (>90%).24 However, therapeutic challenges associated with PEX and plasma infusion remain, including hypotension, anaphylaxis, and fluid overload.32 In patients with cTTP, current prophylaxis plasma regimens are burdensome, may not be sufficient to prevent acute episodes,30 and only maintain normal ADAMTS13 activity levels for a few days.14 For the treatment of iTTP, caplacizumab has demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials33 and is recommended in iTTP treatment guidelines34; however, replenishment of ADAMTS13 is not addressed.29

The current therapeutic landscape highlights the unmet need for an ADAMTS13 replacement therapy. An international working group for TTP proposed revised clinical outcome definitions of remission and concluded that some patients may achieve clinical remission without remission of ADAMTS13 deficiency, which may result in clinical relapse.35 These revised definitions incorporated ADAMTS13 activity and reflect current iTTP management, which aims to increase ADAMTS13 activity levels and mitigate the risk of exacerbation and relapse,35 potentially resulting in decreased length of hospital stay and readmission rates. Maintaining normal ADAMTS13 activity is imperative for preventing acute episodes and future organ damage,5,36,37 which may potentially decrease the clinical and economic burden of TTP.

Study limitations.

The data were obtained from a billing database and may not include sufficient clinical specificity and detail; thus, identifying patient populations of interest may be limited. Additionally, the PHD is not nationally representative, as the database does not include a random sample of all hospitals across the United States. The results can only be generalized to in-hospital patients with an ADAMTS13 test value of <10%. As this analysis is cross-sectional, and there are no data on serial longitudinal laboratory results relative to treatments during the inpatient stay, conclusions on long-term outcomes of patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency cannot be made. Patients who received care at different hospitals were not assessed longitudinally, as readmissions were only recorded in the PHD for patients who returned to the hospital where the index hospitalization occurred. Additionally, 30- to 90-day readmission rates were not captured for patients with index hospitalizations toward the end of the data period due to insufficient follow-up.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that, in a hospital setting, patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency have a substantial clinical burden, heterogenous symptoms indicative of end-organ damage, long hospital stays, high in-hospital mortality, and high readmission rates. Patients with severe ADAMTS13 deficiency can represent a high cost to hospitals. Heterogeneous symptoms mean that recognition and management of this condition can be complicated. Currently, there is an unmet need for ADAMTS13 replacement therapy to treat the cause of the symptoms and complications associated with the severe deficiency and that may be used for prophylaxis.
