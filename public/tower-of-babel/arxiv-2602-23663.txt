Disentangled Mode-Specific Representations for Tensor Time Series via Contrastive Learning
Kohei Obata, Taichi Murayama, Zheng Chen, Yasuko Matsubara, Yasushi Sakurai

Abstract
Multi-mode tensor time series (TTS) can be found in many domains, such as search engines and environmental monitoring systems. Learning representations of a TTS benefits various applications, but it is also challenging since the complexities inherent in the tensor hinder the realization of rich representations. In this paper, we propose a novel representation learning method designed specifically for TTS, namely MoST . Specifically, MoST uses a tensor slicing approach to reduce the complexity of the TTS structure and learns representations that can be disentangled into individual non-temporal modes. Each representation captures mode-specific features, which are the relationship between variables within the same mode, and mode-invariant features, which are in common in representations of different modes. We employ a contrastive learning framework to learn parameters; the loss function comprises two parts intended to learn representation in a mode-specific way and mode-invariant way, effectively exploiting disentangled representations as augmentations. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets show that MoST consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art methods in terms of classification and forecasting accuracy. Code is available at https://github.com/KoheiObata/MoST .

Introduction

With the rapid growth of digital innovation for the collection of data, a time series sequence is generated from multiple attributes or modes, forming a tensor time series (TTS).

This complex data is increasingly prevalent across divers domains, ranging from environment monitoring

]
to financial analysis
[

]
.
An instance of a TTS is online activity data
[

]
, which records the search volumes of various queries at various locations for each timestamp, in the form
\{

Each temporal slice of the

As depicted in Fig.

Since TTS has a multifaceted structure with intricate interactions, effectively learning meaningful representations from such data is a long-standing challenge.

Modeling TTS requires us to consider the multiple non-temporal modes since tensors arise from the rich information of each mode.
Thus, tensor studies normally assume that different modes are associated with different types of dependencies
[
31
,
2
,
10
,
19
]
,
referred to as
intra-mode dependencies
.
As shown in Fig.
1
(b), location and query dependencies are examples of intra-mode dependencies.
Moreover, while different mode slices in the TTS have different intra-mode dependencies, there are common features invariant in modes as they originate from the same TTS; that is, they have similar
temporal dependencies
.
As shown in Fig.
1
(c) and (d), the search volumes peak before and after every Christmas, meaning they have similar seasonality regardless of locations and queries.
Designing a framework that accommodates these intra-mode dependencies and temporal dependencies inherent in the TTS is expected to facilitate the learning of rich representations.

Despite the number of studies in representation learning, existing methods take little account of the TTS structure
[
34
]
.
Tensor decomposition is a conventional method for handling a tensor that can address intra-mode dependencies, which aims to provide a low-rank representation that minimizes a reconstruction error and additional penalty terms (e.g., Frobenius norm or KL-divergence)
[
8
]
.
Although a compact representation is an essential principle for feature reduction, such objective functions mainly aim for accurate reconstruction and are unsuitable for obtaining rich representation that can be applied to downstream tasks.
Therefore, recent works incorporate deep learning techniques to further facilitate the representation learning from tensor decomposition.
For instance, ATD
[
31
]
, a self-supervised representation learning method for tensors, combines tensor decomposition and contrastive learning (CL) and succeeds in learning representations for the downstream classification task.
However, such tensor decomposition methods usually treat all modes equally, resulting in overlooking temporal dependencies.
Thus, there is a need to develop a new approach to TTS representation learning that effectively addresses the TTS structure.

In this paper,
we present
MoST
,
which learns
disentangled
Mo
de-
S
pecific representations for
T
ensor time series
.
Firstly, to reduce the complexity of the TTS structure,
we utilize a tensor slicing approach that slices a TTS into each non-temporal mode to make sets of mode slices, where each set eventually converts into corresponding mode-specific representations.
After the slicing, slices are independently mapped into latent spaces via a slice feature encoder so that the model can capture intra-mode dependencies,
and then are aggregated to generate mode-specific representations.
The parameters of the model are learned via a CL framework, where we effectively exploit disentangled representations as augmentations to learn mode-specific and mode-invariant features inherent in the TTS.
The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

We propose
MoST
, a new representation learning method specifically designed to leverage the TTS structure.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide representations of TTS via CL.

•

To learn mode-specific representations, we introduce tensor slicing and propose corresponding contrastive losses.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on 11 real-world datasets to verify the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed model in downstream tasks.

Related work

Tensor time series analysis

Analyzing TTS benefits a variety of applications, such as environmental monitoring
[
28
,
5
]
, biomedical analysis
[
15
]
, and online activity data
[
18
]
.
Traditional approaches to tensor analysis typically involve tensor/matrix decomposition: Tucker/CP decomposition and SVD
[
12
,
1
]
.
They obtain a lower-dimensional representation that captures a good summarization of a tensor/matrix, but they do not consider the temporal dependencies
[
31
]
.
To complement the temporal information, tensor/matrix decomposition is further combined with a dynamical system
[
22
,
2
,
9
]
.
For example, SSMF
[
11
]
is an online forecasting method that combines a dynamical system with non-negative matrix factorization to capture seasonal trends in TTS.
Recent work integrates deep learning models with tensor decomposition and is intended to learn invariant tensor features for forecasting
[
10
]
.
However, since the dynamical system conveys the information recurrently from the one previous timestamp, they do not sufficiently capture long-term temporal dependencies.
Some models are designed to analyze a tensor from a specific modality by utilizing their intrinsic characteristics.
These include web data models that utilize the Lotka-Volterra equalitons
[
18
,
16
]

and spatio-temporal models that utilize a pre-defined graph
[
27
]
.
Despite their success, these studies are data and task-specific; how to construct a general-purpose model that can extend to various TTS remains a challenge.

Time series contrastive learning

Self-supervised learning enables transferable representations for diverse downstream tasks
[
34
]
.
Inspired by the success in computer vision and NLP
[
36
,
21
]
, CL has recently emerged as a prominent technique for learning invariant representations in time series data
[
13
,
6
]
.
For example, CPC
[
25
]
, a notable CL method, learns representations by predicting future sequences in latent space,
and TNC
[
23
]
captures temporal smoothness by defining the neighborhood distribution.
However, they generate positive samples from neighboring timestamps and fail to capture global features in time series.
A recent successful method is TS2Vec
[
33
]
, which makes it possible to learn global features for time series by introducing hierarchical and instance losses.
The learned representations are generic to downstream tasks, but some methods focus particularly on learning task-specific representations, such as classification
[
17
,
7
]
, imputation
[
14
]
, and change point detection
[
4
]
.
There is a recent promising trend that learns disentangled representations of time series through CL.
As time series can be decomposed into seasonal and trend or spectral and temporal domains, CoST
[
30
]
learns disentangled seasonal-trend representations for forecasting,
and TF-C
[
35
]
and BTSF
[
32
]
exploit representations from spectral and temporal domains, to obtain global features in time series.

Our work distinguishes itself from these methods in the following aspects:

\mathrm{\hskip 1.79993pti\hskip 1.79993pt)}

MoST
learns representations for TTS that can be applied to any downstream tasks.

\mathrm{\hskip 0.80002ptii\hskip 0.80002pt)}

MoST
learns disentangled representations capturing different
mode-specific features
through a tensor slicing approach and contrasts the disentangled representations to learn
mode-invariant features
.

In this section,
we formally define the TTS representation learning problem and then introduce our proposed approach,
MoST
.

Definition 1
(Tensor Time Series)

A TTS is an
N^{th}
-order tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\dots\times d_{N-1}\times T}
,
where the
N^{th}
mode is the time and its dimension is
T
.

3.1
Problem formulation

We consider a
3^{rd}
-order TTS
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times T}
,
which consists of a set of observations
of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
variables within the two non-temporal modes
at
T
time points for the temporal mode.

2
2

2

In this work, we focus on a
3^{rd}
-order tensor for simplicity;
our method, however, can be generalized to an
N^{th}
-order tensor.

Given a TTS with a
w
length window,
we aim to learn feature representations
V=f(\mathcal{X})
, where

\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times w}
,

V=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w}
,
and
f(\cdot)
denotes the feature embedding function,
which projects
(d_{1}\times d_{2})
-dimensional raw signals into an
h
-dimensional latent space for each time point.

Our
MoST
aims to learn the disentangled mode-specific representations, i.e.,

V=[V^{(d_{1})};V^{(d_{2})}]\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w}
,
where
V^{(d_{1})}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{d}\times w}
is a mode-1-aware representation
and
V^{(d_{2})}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{d}\times w}
is a mode-2-aware representation,
such that
h_{d}=h/2
.

3.2
Model architecture

Our proposed model, illustrated in Fig.
2
(a), consists of three components:

\mathrm{(\hskip 1.79993pti\hskip 1.79993pt)}

Tensor slicing
transforms the tensor for learning disentangled mode-specific representations.

\mathrm{(\hskip 0.80002ptii\hskip 0.80002pt)}

Slice feature encoder
captures intra-mode and temporal dependencies and learns a representation of a slice individually.

\mathrm{(i\hskip-0.80002pti\hskip-0.80002pti)}

Aggregator
summarizes the mode information to form a representation for a particular mode.

Tensor slicing

We describe a method that effectively leverages the tensor structure, which helps learn rich representations from a TTS.
Concretely, the tensor is processed into a set of mode-1,2 tensors, each of which represents a set of mode-1,2 slices.
The tensor slicing is written as follows:

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})},\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}\}=\mathbf{Slice}(\mathcal{X}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}
, is a mode-1 tensor,
and
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}
is a mode-2 tensor.

\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}
is a set of mode-1 slices, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}=\{\mathcal{X}_{:,j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{2}}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{:,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times w}
is a mode-1 slice at the
j
-th mode-2 of length
w
, s.t.,
j=1,\dots,d_{2}
is an index of slices.
Likewise,

\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}
is a set of mode-2 slices, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}=\{\mathcal{X}_{i,:}\}_{i=1}^{d_{1}}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times w}
is a mode-2 slice at the
i
-th mode-1, s.t.,
i=1,\dots,d_{1}
.

Slice feature encoder

After slicing the TTS, each mode-1,2 slice is fed independently into a slice feature encoder, which we call a mode-independence (MI) approach.
Our slice feature encoder maps the observed signals to the latent representations and learns intra-mode and temporal dependencies.
It consists of an embedding layer and a causal convolutional encoder.
Given that the procedure is identical for mode-1,2 tensors,
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}
and
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}
, we only describe the procedure for mode-1 tensor,
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}
.

Each mode-1 slice
\mathcal{X}_{:,j}
of
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}
is mapped to the latent space of dimension
h

via a trainable linear projection
W^{(d_{1})pro}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d_{1}}
.
Then, a determinative additive temporal embedding
[
37
]

W^{tem}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w}
is used to monitor the temporal order of a series:

Z^{(d_{1})emb}_{j}=W^{(d_{1})pro}\mathcal{X}_{:,j}+W^{tem}
,
where
Z^{(d_{1})emb}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w}
denotes the input to be fed into a causal convolutional encoder.

To supply long-range temporal information,
we employ a causal convolutional encoder and capture information from different time scales
[
30
]
.
The causal convolutional encoder consists of a stack of
L+1
one-dimensional causal convolution blocks and an average-pooling layer.
We use
L=7
in this paper.
Each block has
h
input channels and
h_{d}
output channels,
where the kernel size of the
k
-th block is
2^{k}
.
The parameters of the causal convolutional encoder are shared in different modes.
Then, an average-pooling layer is used to summarize the latent representation across different time scales.

\displaystyle Z^{(d_{1})cau}_{j,k}=\mathbf{CausalConvBlock}(Z^{(d_{1})emb}_{j},2^{k}),

(2)

\displaystyle Z^{(d_{1})enc}_{j}=\mathbf{AveragePooling}(Z^{(d_{1})cau}_{j,0},\dots,Z^{(d_{1})cau}_{j,L}).

(3)

Aggregator

We have embedded representations of each slice
Z^{(d_{1})enc}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{d}\times w}
, where
j=1,\dots,d_{2}
and,

Z^{(d_{2})enc}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{d}\times w}
, where
i=1,\dots,d_{1}
,
respectively.
An average-pooling layer or a max-pooling layer is applied depending on the dataset to summarize the mode information.
Through this process,
we acquire mode-1-aware representations
V^{(d_{1})}
and mode-2-aware representations
V^{(d_{2})}
.

\displaystyle V^{(d_{1})}=\mathbf{Pooling}(Z^{(d_{1})enc}_{1},\dots,Z^{(d_{1})enc}_{d_{2}}),

(4)

\displaystyle V^{(d_{2})}=\mathbf{Pooling}(Z^{(d_{2})enc}_{1},\dots,Z^{(d_{2})enc}_{d_{1}}).

(5)

We concatenate them and obtain the final representations

V=[V^{(d_{1})};V^{(d_{2})}]
.

3.3
Contrastive learning

We utilize CL, a self-supervised learning method, to optimize the entire network, resulting in more comprehensive representations of TTS
[
3
]
.
We define a contrastive loss Eq. (
8
) that consists of
instance loss
Eq. (
6
) and
mode loss
Eq. (
7
),
each of which is a variant of InfoNCE
[
25
]
that aims to capture the
mode-specific features
and
mode-invariant features
, respectively.

Instance loss

Instance loss aims to learn the mode-specific features.
As Fig.
2
(b) shows, we apply random cropping to the input tensor before feeding it to the model and create two different augmentations.
Random cropping treats the representations at the same timestamp in two different augmentations as positive samples,
whereas it treats those of other time series samples at the same timestamp as negative samples
[
33
]
.
Concretely, we randomly sample two overlapping sequences from
\mathcal{X}
,
hence
(X_{a_{1}},\ldots,X_{b_{1}})
,

(X_{a_{2}},\ldots,X_{b_{2}})
,
s.t.
0<a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq w
.
The overlapped sequence
(X_{a_{2}},\ldots,X_{b_{1}})
is used as a positive sample to learn position-agnostic representations.
This is only applied in the training.

Let
V_{i,t}
denote the representations for the
i
-th index of the input time series sample at the timestamp
t
.
Then,
V^{\prime}_{i,t}
is a positive sample of
V_{i,t}
,
which denotes the representations for the
i
-th sample at the same timestamp
t
but from a different augmentation.
We calculate instance loss as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{I}=\frac{1}{BN}\sum_{i}^{B}\sum_{t}^{N}-log\frac{exp(V_{i,t}\cdot V^{\prime}_{i,t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}(exp(V_{i,t}\cdot V^{\prime}_{j,t})+\mathbbm{1}_{[i\neq j]}exp(V_{i,t}\cdot V_{j,t}))},

(6)

where
B
is the batch size,
N=b_{1}-a_{2}
is the length of the overlapped sequence.
We use the representations of other samples at timestamp
t
as negative samples.

Mode loss

To learn the mode-invariant features in different mode-specific representations, we treat representations of different modes at the same timestamp as a positive sample, while we treat those of other time series samples at the same timestamp of different modes as negative samples.
The approach attempts to minimize the similarity among different mode-specific representations of the same timestamp.

We describe the mode loss for mode-1
\mathcal{L}^{(d_{1})}_{M}
.
However, the mode loss for mode-2
\mathcal{L}^{(d_{2})}_{M}
is defined as being the same as
\mathcal{L}^{(d_{1})}_{M}
but in a different mode.
Let
V^{(d_{1})}_{i,t}
denote the mode-1-aware representations for the
i
-th index of the input time series sample at the timestamp
t
.

V^{(d_{2})}_{i,t}
is a positive sample of
V^{(d_{1})}_{i,t}
,
which denotes the mode-2-aware representations for the
i
-th sample at the same timestamp
t
.
The mode loss for mode-1 is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(d_{1})}_{M}=\frac{1}{BN}\sum_{i}^{B}\sum_{t}^{N}-log\frac{exp(V^{(d_{1})}_{i,t}\cdot V^{(d_{2})}_{i,t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}exp(V^{(d_{1})}_{i,t}\cdot V^{(d_{2})}_{j,t})}.

(7)

We use the representations of other samples at timestamp
t
from a different mode as negative samples.

Contrastive loss

The overall loss function is the weighted sum of the instance loss and the mode loss of each non-temporal mode.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{I}+\alpha(\mathcal{L}^{(d_{1})}_{M}+\mathcal{L}^{(d_{2})}_{M}),

(8)

where
\alpha
is a hyper-parameter that controls the importance of the mode loss.

4.3
Ablation study

We describe ablation studies concerning the architecture of our method.
Table
3
presents the mean results of the classification datasets and the US datasets on all forecast horizon settings with different architectures.

We first perform an ablation study to understand the effects of the tensor slicing and MI approaches.
We conduct a comparison with the five alternative preprocessing methods.

Mode-1/2 dependency (M1D/M2D)
: Feeds only the mode-1/2 tensor
\mathcal{X}^{(d_{1})}/\mathcal{X}^{(d_{2})}
into the slice feature encoder.

Random
: Creates a randomly organized tensor by flattening, randomizing variables, and restoring the original shape, applied once before learning.

Channel-independence (CI)
: Feeds
\{\mathcal{X}_{1,1},\mathcal{X}_{1,2},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{d_{1},d_{2}}\}
independently into the slice feature encoder.

Channel-dependence (CD)
: Feeds a flattened tensor
X^{flat}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}d_{2}\times w}
into the slice feature encoder.
Table
3
(a) demonstrates the significant impact of our approaches on improving prediction and classification accuracy.
Comparison with M1D and M2D underscores the importance of both mode-1 and mode-2 dependencies for rich representations.
Additionally, comparisons with Random, CI, and CD demonstrate the superiority of our MI approach, which leverages the TTS structure to learn intra-mode dependencies effectively.

We evaluate the impact of our architecture and loss functions.
Table
3
(b) reveals that both temporal embedding and the causal convolutional encoder enhance performance.
Table
3
(c) presents results w/o instance and mode losses and end-to-end training using forecasting loss functions.
Instance loss proves crucial for forecasting, while both losses improve classification accuracy.
Furthermore, our model, which optimizes representations and forecasting separately, outperforms end-to-end training with supervised MSE forecasting loss.

5
Conclusion

In this study,
we proposed
MoST
, a representation learning method that learns disentangled mode-specific representations for TTS.

MoST
consists of the tensor slicing approach and a newly defined contrastive loss to effectively model the TTS.
Thanks to our proposed architecture,
MoST
captures intra-mode and temporal dependencies.
The learned representations can be applied to any downstream tasks, including classification and forecasting,
and extensive experiments show that our method outperforms the state-of-the-art methods.

MoST
has the potential to contribute to enhancing analysis and our understanding of TTS.
