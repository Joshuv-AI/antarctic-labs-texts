Fibroblasts resist Staphylococcus aureus on the skin surface by responding to interleukin-1 and recruiting neutrophils
Authors: Jared S. Simmons, Teruaki Nakatsuji, Fengwu Li, Carlos Aguilera, Tomofumi Numata, Kellen J. Cavagnero, Samia Almoughrabie, Andrea Roso Mares, Consuelo Sauceda, Jacquelyn Castaneda, David J. Gonzalez, Hal M. Hoffman, Richard L. Gallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus aureus can cause serious infections, yet it can also reside on the skin without causing disease. This apparent paradox implies a strong host defense system, which prevents S. aureus from invading the dermis. In this study, we investigate how the skin detects and responds to superficial S. aureus exposure. Using unbiased transcriptomic, biochemical, and phosphoproteomic analyses, followed by targeted validation in human and mouse models, we found that fibroblast recognition of interleukin-1 is essential for immune response. Deletion or blockade of the interleukin-1 receptor type 1 (IL-1R1) in fibroblasts in vitro abolished keratinocyte-driven changes in gene expression and reduced chemokine production. Furthermore, the skin of mice lacking fibroblast IL-1R1 had fewer neutrophils and higher bacterial load after topical S. aureus application. These findings show that fibroblasts actively participate in innate immunity and highlight an IL-1R1–dependent keratinocyte-fibroblast-neutrophil axis of communication. Understanding this pathway provides insights into mechanisms that initiate neutrophil recruitment to the skin and may help develop new approaches to therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most consequential human pathogens, colonizing ∼30% of the global population (1). It is frequently encountered on the skin surface and persists despite routine washing, antimicrobial products, and environmental fluctuations (2). If S. aureus breaches epithelial barriers and enters the bloodstream, then it can disseminate and cause sepsis. Because many strains exhibit resistance to multiple antibiotics, these infections are difficult to treat. As a result, S. aureus is a leading pathogen in morbidity around the world (3).

Although S. aureus has strong virulence factors [e.g., phenol-soluble modulin α (PSMα)] (4), invasive infection of intact skin is relatively rare; S. aureus instead spreads opportunistically when the skin barrier is broken (5), there is genetic susceptibility (6), or inflammation is dysregulated (7). In healthy hosts, S. aureus is usually limited to the skin surface and nasal passages, appearing as an inoffensive colonizer (1). These clinical observations imply the existence of rapid and robust skin defense mechanisms that can restrain early microbial growth at the skin surface.

Multiple cell types contribute to this protective response. Keratinocytes form a physical barrier and produce antimicrobial peptides, inflammatory cytokines, alarmins, and pathogen-sensing receptors (8). Dermal macrophages have been implicated as early responders to S. aureus capable of phagocytosis and cytokine production (9). Neutrophils, however, are considered the most indispensable killers of S. aureus (10). Because neutrophils are scarce in healthy skin (11), they must be recruited to sites of S. aureus proliferation, a process that depends on the induction of chemokine signaling in the dermis (12). This suggests a rapid communication system that translates superficial microbial detection into immune activation of deeper tissue. Several cytokines have been shown to amplify inflammation, including interleukin-1 (IL-1) (13), tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (14), IL-17 (15), and IL-36 (16), but the cellular architecture of this early cross-talk remains poorly understood.

Recent findings have broadened the view of the participation of cells other than classical immunocytes in cutaneous immunity. Dermal fibroblasts (FBs) are a heterogenous collection of cells that include mesenchymal stem cells, preadipocytes, and other FBs in multiple, transcriptionally distinct cell states (17). Preadipocytes produce antimicrobial peptides when differentiating into adipocytes in response to S. aureus penetration into deeper compartments (18, 19). In inflammatory diseases, FBs adopt an activated state, referred to here as immune-acting fibroblasts (IAFs), which are characterized by chemokine production and leukocyte recruitment, highlighting their functional heterogeneity and capacity to shape local immunity (20). These observations indicate that skin immunity may involve coordinated activity between epithelial and stromal compartments rather than a linear keratinocyte-myeloid signaling pathway. Whether such FB-mediated mechanisms contribute to the containment of S. aureus at the skin surface is unknown.

To address this knowledge gap, we combined unbiased transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic analyses with targeted in vitro assays and in vivo infection models to define how early detection of S. aureus coordinates neutrophil recruitment from deeper tissues. Identifying this pathway explains how surface cells quickly communicate with FBs, which serve as a potent source of chemokines, and provides insight into why S. aureus rarely progresses to invasive infection in healthy hosts with an intact barrier.

RESULTS

Fibroblasts are a major immune signaling hub in topical S. aureus response

To better understand the immune recognition system responsible for triggering an immune defense response to S. aureus on the skin surface, we analyzed a published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (21) of mouse skin 48 hours after topical application of S. aureus or vehicle (Fig. 1A). A total of 10,821 cells was studied (fig. S1A), with distinct populations identified as keratinocytes, FBs, myeloid cells, lymphoid cells, endothelial cells, mural cells, and melanocytes based on expression of known markers (Fig. 1B and fig. S1B). Myeloid cells were notably enriched in S. aureus-treated skin compared to control (Fig. 1C). Unexpectedly, despite current models assuming that epidermal keratinocytes are the predominant cell type communicating to immunocytes, ligand-receptor–based network analysis identified dermal FBs as the major cell type communicating with myeloid cells in S. aureus-treated skin (Fig. 1D).

Because the recruitment of myeloid cells is essential to resisting infection and scRNA-seq analysis revealed prominent communication between FBs and myeloid cells in the skin, we next sought to understand how FBs could be activated by S. aureus on the surface. Through pseudobulk differential expression analysis of FB transcripts, 397 genes were identified as highly expressed and increased in skin topically exposed to SA compared with vehicle control. Reactome pathway enrichment analysis revealed a strong inflammatory signature in FBs, including chemokine and cytokine signaling (Fig. 1E). To better characterize the immune response of FBs, we curated a panel of genes, which were differentially expressed across multiple datasets (see Materials and Methods and fig. S1C) and have well-documented involvement in neutrophil recruitment and function, all of which were induced in FBs of skin exposed to topical S. aureus (Fig. 1F). Among this panel are canonical neutrophil chemokines Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 (22, 23) and key neutrophil growth and mobilizing factor Csf3 (24). The panel also includes proinflammatory amplifier Il6, monocyte-recruiting Ccl2, prostaglandin E2–producing enzymes Ptgs2 and Ptges, innate immune effector Ptx3, and hyaluronan synthase Has1, each of which has been strongly linked to acute inflammatory response and neutrophil recruitment in vivo (25–29).

Hierarchical clustering of the FB population defined 14 distinct subsets (Fig. 1G). FB subsets 6 to 14 were enriched in skin exposed to topical S. aureus, suggesting global transcriptional reprogramming in dermal FBs (Fig. 1H). Pseudobulk analysis with principal components analysis (PCA) dimensional reduction (Fig. 1I) and assessment of gene expression across markers for FB population niches and activation states (fig. S1D) confirmed the unique transcriptional profile of each subset. An IAF score was next established on the basis of a composite measure of normalized expression of the inflammatory genes defined in fig. S1C. Skin responding to S. aureus was enriched with high-scoring IAFs compared to control (Fig. 1J). A total of 39.2% of FB in skin exposed to topical S. aureus had an IAF score greater than 0.5 compared to just 1.7% in control skin (Fig. 1K). FB subset 11 had the highest IAF score, followed by 13, 7, and 9 (Fig. 1L), and each subset showed unique transcriptional profiles across our gene panel (fig. S1E).

Keratinocytes activate FBs to recruit neutrophils

As the major cell type of the epidermis, keratinocytes are the first cells to encounter S. aureus. Keratinocytes are also known to be highly active participants in inflammatory signaling and host defense (8). Accordingly, we next sought to test whether keratinocytes could directly trigger the response observed in FBs.

To test this hypothesis, we designed a conditioned medium assay (Fig. 2A). Human keratinocytes were treated with S. aureus-conditioned medium, and then the keratinocyte-conditioned medium (KC-CM) was collected and applied to a cultured human FB cell line [human preadipocyte (HPAd)]. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) after 4 hours revealed a strong induction of CXCL1 in FBs exposed to S. aureus-stimulated KC-CM, which was not seen in the S. aureus-CM control (Fig. 2B), consistent with the increase in FB Cxcl1 in SA-treated skin. FB CXCL1 was notably induced by KC-CM even without S. aureus, albeit to a lesser extent. To reduce variability, therefore, we used unstimulated KC-CM for future assays.

RNA-seq identified 357 genes whose expression in HPAds was significantly increased after 4 hours of exposure to KC-CM, and Reactome pathway enrichment analysis characterized this response as including several inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2C). Among the most significantly up-regulated genes were each of those in our IAF panel from mice exposed to S. aureus (see Fig. 1F), as well as the human-specific but potent neutrophil chemokine CXCL8 (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, qPCR-quantified CXCL1, CXCL8, CSF3, and CCL2 in HPAds confirmed that the expression of these genes is robustly induced in FBs by KC-CM (Fig. 2E). We found that primary mouse dermal FBs also responded to human KC-CM (fig. S2A). Induced expression of CXCL8 in HPAds peaked 4 hours after exposure to KC-CM (fig. S2B) and was dose dependent (fig. S2C). Using normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs), we observed that 20% NHEK medium doubled FB CXCL8 compared to FB media, even without exposure to keratinocytes (fig. S2D). To ameliorate this confounding effect, we dialyzed the KC-CM; dialyzed NHEK medium did not promote CXCL8 induction (fig. S2E), while dialyzed KC-CM retained its FB activity (fig. S2F).

Cultured human FBs also showed increased chemokine protein expression after exposure to KC-CM, including significant increases in C-C Motif Chemokine 2 (CCL2/5/17/20) and CXCL1/5/8/9/10/11 (Fig. 2F). To determine whether the chemokines expressed by FBs after activation by keratinocytes were functionally active and thus capable of recruiting neutrophils or other immune cells, we next tested the conditioned medium from FBs for its capacity to induce migration of cells across a 3-μm transwell membrane (Fig. 2G). A significant increase in neutrophils from either spleen or bone marrow as a cell source occurred when FBs were activated by KC-CM (Fig. 2H). We also saw significant recruitment of other leukocytes, including classical monocytes (fig. S2G), although the predominant cells enriched after recruitment by KC-CM–activated FBs were neutrophils (Fig. 2I).

IL-1α is a candidate keratinocyte activator of FBs

To determine the mechanism(s) by which keratinocytes activate the immune response of FBs, we collected cell lysates at several early time points after exposure to KC-CM and analyzed by mass spectrometry for differential phosphorylation (Fig. 3A). Consistent with other analyses of titanium dioxide (TiO2)–enriched samples, our dataset was biased toward peptides with single phosphorylated serine residues (fig. S3, A and B). Differential phosphopeptide abundance analysis detected a total of 371 phosphopeptides with significant enrichment or depletion in FBs treated with KC-CM compared to control (Fig. 3B). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis of the corresponding genes indicated that KC-CM influenced serine phosphorylation of genes associated with cytoskeletal remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, innate immune activation, and extracellular matrix reorganization. Among these responses, phosphorylation of Activator Protein 1 (AP-1) subunit c-Jun (Ser73) increases, while phospho–Raf-1 (Ser642) is decreased by KC-CM (Fig. 3D). Total protein abundance was correlated over time for c-Jun but inversely correlated for Raf-1. These changes are consistent with increased c-Jun N-terminal kinase, extracellular signal–regulated kinase, and AP-1 activity (30, 31), supporting the hypothesis that these signaling pathways are involved in FB immune activation.

We next assessed the biochemical properties of the factor(s) in KC-CM responsible for activation of FB immune activity. KC-CM was subjected to heat, proteinase K, ammonium sulfate precipitation, and size-based fractionation before addition to FBs and assessment of FB CXCL8 expression by qPCR. These treatments showed the keratinocyte-derived factor is heat and proteinase K sensitive, precipitates in the protein fraction, and behaves as a molecule between 10 and 50 kDa. (Fig. 3E).

On the basis of observations that KC-CM activates AP-1 and has biochemical properties consistent with a cytokine, candidate recombinant proteins were tested by adding them to FBs and measuring FB gene expression by qPCR (Fig. 3F). At the tested doses, IL-1α most closely recapitulated the effects of KC-CM on FB expression in an inflammatory gene panel.

Further analysis of mouse scRNA-seq data showed that Il1r1 is highly expressed in FBs from skin after topical S. aureus exposure (Fig. 3G). The expression of Il1r1 varies between cell subsets (Fig. 3H), with greater expression in those FBs identified with higher IAF scores in Fig. 1L. While many cell types can detect IL-1 (32), within the skin, Il1r1 expression was primarily found within the FB populations (Fig. 3I).

S. aureus induces keratinocyte release of IL-1α (33), and we observed a notable increase in Il1a expression in the keratinocyte population of SA-treated skin (fig. S3C). The primary activation mechanism of IL-1α relies on calpain, and >45% of keratinocytes had detectable coexpression of Capns1 and either Capn1 or Capn2 (calpain subunit genes) (fig. S3D). Furthermore, ∼40% of all cells coexpressing calpain genes in the skin were identified as keratinocytes (fig. S3E), and keratinocytes are known as sources of IL-1α in the skin (34). IL-1α was quantified as ∼200 pg/ml in unstimulated KC-CM, while S. aureus increased output to ∼500 pg/ml (Fig. 3J), consistent with the recombinant IL-1α dose, which recapitulated FB transcriptional effects. Together, these observations suggested that following detection of SA, keratinocytes may communicate to FBs in the dermis through the release of IL-1α.

Keratinocytes trigger FB immune activity through IL-1α–IL-1R1 signaling

To directly test whether IL-1 signaling was required for the response of FBs to KC-CM, we exposed primary cultured dermal FBs from Il1r1−/− mice and wild-type (WT) controls to KC-CM. Lack of Il1r1 in FBs abolished the induction of Cxcl1 by KC-CM (Fig. 4A). Similarly, in human FBs, addition of the specific IL-1R1 antagonist, anakinra, or a neutralizing antibody to IL-1α effectively blocked CXCL8 expression in response to KC-CM, while the IL-1β antibody did not (Fig. 4B). RNA-seq analysis of FBs exposed to KC-CM with and without anakinra showed that blockade of IL-1R1 inhibited all genes defined in the IAF score panel as induced by keratinocytes (Fig. 4C), as well as an extended panel of CCL- and CXCL-family genes (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, global transcriptional analysis of FBs showed that anakinra was broadly effective in blocking the capacity of keratinocytes to activate FBs (Fig. 4, E and F). These results reveal IL-1α as a dominant driver of keratinocyte-mediated FB activation in this system.

Topical S. aureus triggers epidermal release of immunity-required IL-1α

After establishing that keratinocyte IL-1α triggers immune activation of FBs in vitro, we sought to assess the role of IL-1α in defending against S. aureus expansion from the skin surface in vivo. We first sampled the immune response to an occlusive topical S. aureus infection model at multiple time points (Fig. 5A). S. aureus live colony-forming units (CFU) obtained from superficial skin swab samples were similar between 1 and 24 hours after application and then increased at 48 and 120 hours (Fig. 5B). Cxcl1 increased at 2 hours and remained high from 6 hours onward (Fig. 5B). Ly6g, used as a proxy for infiltrating neutrophils, started to increase by 4 hours and was high from 9 hours onward (Fig. 5B). Visual symptoms appeared at 6 hours (Fig. 5A), with erythematous lesions steadily growing larger and worsening in disease severity according to blind scoring (Fig. 5B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining showed basal levels of epidermal IL-1α at baseline, with a strong increase in abundance and expansion into the dermis by 14 hours (Fig. 5, C to E, and fig. S4, A and B). On the basis of this time course analysis, a 14-hour time point was chosen to assess early responses: before bacterial expansion or evidence of severe inflammation and after epidermal IL-1α extended to the dermis and a cutaneous chemokine response was noted.

With this topical infection model, we used a neutralizing antibody for IL-1α 24 hours ahead of S. aureus application. After 14 hours, this systemic blockade of IL-1α impaired neutrophil recruitment (Fig. 5, F and G). We observed Lymphocyte Antigen 6G (Ly6G) signal in the epidermis of immunoglobulin G (IgG)–treated skin (Fig. 5H), indicating that infiltrating neutrophils directly targeted superficial SA. With N = 5 mice, phenotypes were not statistically significant after 48 hours, but lesions trended larger (fig. S4C), redder (fig. S4D), and more severe (Fig. 5I and fig. S4E) in the anti–IL-1α–treated mice. Furthermore, skin swabs recovered more live CFU from antibody-treated mice (Fig. 5J), supporting a functional contribution of IL-1α to immunity.

Fibroblast IL-1R1 signaling drives early immune activation in vivo

Understanding that IL-1α plays a role in resisting S. aureus on the skin surface and that FBs respond to keratinocyte IL-1α in vitro, we next asked whether FB IL-1R1 signaling is a key mechanistic driver of host defense. Accordingly, we generated PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice to selectively delete essential exons 3 and 4 of Il1r1 in all Pdgfra-expressing cells (Fig. 6A) (35, 36). The efficacy of the targeted deletion was confirmed in skin and FB isolated from Il1r1fl/fl control and PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice by qPCR (Fig. 6B). S. aureus or vehicle control was then topically applied to back skin of Il1r1fl/fl control and PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice. scRNA-seq was performed on dorsal skin, recovering 32,095 cells (fig. S5A), from which FBs were identified using known marker genes of skin cell lineages (Fig. 6C and fig. S5, B and C). PCA analysis of pseudobulked FBs demonstrated that the transcriptional profile of FBs in PdgfraΔIl1r1 + S. aureus (PS) was distinct from that of the other experimental groups (Fig. 6D). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis characterized the top 152 down-regulated genes associated with deletion of the Il1r1 in FBs after topical SA, with the most enriched pathway being “chemokine receptors bind chemokines” (Fig. 6E). Among these Il1r1-dependent genes, which were suppressed in PS compared to Il1r1fl/fl + S. aureus (CS), were all IAF panel genes (Fig. 6F).

Reclustering FBs identified 16 distinct subsets with unique transcriptional profiles (Fig. 6, G and H, and fig. S5D). Expression of IAF genes (fig. S5E) and comparison of IAF scores (Fig. 6I) demonstrated that the most immune-active population was subset 15, followed by 1, 11, and 12. Primarily vehicle-associated subsets 4 through 9 scored the lowest. Notably, the highest-scoring subset, 15, was not seen in the PS group (Fig. 6J). Comparing across samples, FBs from PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice had reduced IAF scores; high-scoring FBs represented a lower fraction of all FBs in PdgfraΔIl1r1 skin compared to Il1r1fl/fl skin, both under vehicle conditions and after S. aureus application (Fig. 6, K and L). Quantification by qPCR of Cxcl1 and Ccl2 in whole, homogenized skin also showed a significant decrease in these chemokines in PS mice compared to CS (Fig. 6M). These observations support the conclusion that FB recognition of IL-1 is an essential mechanism of skin immunity during topical exposure to SA.

Loss of FB IL-1R1 signaling alters myeloid cell programming

Given the marked suppression of IAF programs in PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice (Fig. 6, K and L) and prominent FB-myeloid signaling in infected skin (Fig. 1D), we next explored the effects of IL-1R1 signaling on the myeloid population (fig. S6A). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed increased representation of chemokine signaling–associated pathways in PS myeloid cells (fig. S6B). Differential expression analysis demonstrated a shift in transcriptional programs, with suppression of neutrophil-targeting chemokines (Cxcl3 and Ccl3) in favor of interferon-associated chemokines (Cxcl10 and Cxcl11) and the interferon-stimulated gene Ifit2 (fig. S6C).

Reclustering the myeloid population and analyzing marker expression identified 14 subsets (fig. S6D). Compared to CS skin, PS skin contained proportionally fewer cells in subset 2, which was marked by high Il12a, Spp1, and Tnf, instead being richer in subset 1, shifted toward Chil3, Ccr2, and Fcgr1/3 (fig. S6, E and F). This subset analysis revealed prominent differences between myeloid populations of Il1r1fl/fl control and PdgfraΔIl1r1 skin under vehicle conditions as well. Together, these findings demonstrate that FB IL-1R1 signaling affects transcriptional programming of myeloid cells in the skin.

DISCUSSION

Understanding how the skin detects and restrains S. aureus is crucial for preventing the transition of S. aureus from a common colonizer to an invasive pathogen. Until now, direct keratinocyte-to-immune cell communication has been the main explanation for triggering the initial inflammatory response. While IL-1 (13) and IL-36 (37) are known to play important roles in host responses to SA, it was not known whether FBs serve as a link between surface detection and a coordinated systemic response. Here, we describe an epidermal-dermal immune communication axis centered on the capacity of FBs to detect IL-1 and initiate a chemokine response necessary for early neutrophil recruitment and protection against S. aureus invasion. Our findings, therefore, connect superficial microbial sensing to the dermal response, defining an important and previously underappreciated mechanism by which the skin recruits neutrophils and limits S. aureus expansion.

Keratinocytes are well-known sentinels of the skin barrier, yet their communication with stromal cells during acute bacterial challenge remains poorly defined. While prior studies have focused on keratinocyte engagement of leukocytes, our findings present dermal FBs as dominant responders to epidermal alarm signals. As S. aureus expand, quorum sensing drives production of PSMα (4), which is known to trigger keratinocyte release of IL-1α (33). Through single-cell transcriptomics and functional assays, we show that some dermal FBs rapidly transition to an immune-active state, characterized by widespread proinflammatory transcriptional reprogramming. This IAF phenotype is enriched in FB subsets within SA-treated skin and drives FB IL-1R1–dependent recruitment of neutrophils, which are essential to clearing S. aureus from the skin (10). Thus, counter to current models, FBs play a key role in preventing S. aureus proliferation.

Our data reveal IL-1 as the key molecular mediator of this cross-talk. Although both IL-1α and IL-1β can signal through IL-1R1, several observations support a primary role for keratinocyte-derived IL-1α in the early response to S. aureus on the skin surface. Forming the skin barrier, keratinocytes are the first cells to be encountered by proliferating S. aureus and its pathogen-associated molecular patterns, uniquely positioning them for swift response. IL-1α is produced by keratinocytes and stored in the cytosol, allowing rapid release upon cell stress or perturbation (32). While its potency increases following cleavage by calpain (38), the full-length pro–IL-1α is already capable of activating IL-1R1 (39). Calpain is also abundant in keratinocytes (40). In contrast, IL-1β is not constitutively expressed, relying instead on transcriptional induction, and its precursor form is inactive (32). As part of an immune response, the NACHT, LRR and PYD Domains-Containing Protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome can activate IL-1β with caspase-1 and promote secretion through gasdermin D–dependent membrane permeabilization (41). While this process does occur in keratinocytes, it is more prominent in myeloid lineages (42), which are minor populations in healthy skin. Even after 48 hours, despite the development of prominent lesions, IHC staining revealed that S. aureus was still primarily found in the epidermis. Hence, the FB IL-1R1–dependent neutrophil recruitment observed at 14 hours after topical S. aureus application is most likely due to an epidermally sourced signal. Dermal macrophages and infiltrating neutrophils and monocytes may amplify FB IL-1R1 signaling by contributing IL-1β in later stages of S. aureus infection, but in the initial response to S. aureus expansion in intact skin, keratinocyte IL-1α is a more readily available distress signal.

Consistent with this model, IHC staining showed substantial IL-1α in the epidermis of skin topically treated with SA, with clear extension into the dermis by 14 hours. This epidermally derived IL-1α in the dermis coincided temporally with robust chemokine induction and neutrophil recruitment. It bears noting that, to favor detection of extracellular IL-1α, this staining was performed without detergent permeabilization. While this approach does not definitively exclude intracellular antigen, the localization and morphology of staining are consistent with the presence of accessible IL-1α to engage FB IL-1R1. Further supporting this functional role for IL-1α, systemic neutralization of IL-1α in vivo was associated with reduced neutrophil infiltration and a trend toward worse disease outcomes.

Within the framework of controlling S. aureus colonization on the skin, FBs emerge as a major early responder to this keratinocyte alarmin. Keratinocyte IL-1α traverses the basement membrane and can be in a position to affect dermal FB activity (43, 44). Corresponding with Il1r1 expression across cell types in our scRNA-seq data, FB show more sensitivity to IL-1 than other skin cells in vitro (45). Il1r1 is also essential for neutrophilic response to cutaneous S. aureus (13). Together, it follows that early response to superficial S. aureus might rely on FB recognition of keratinocyte IL-1α, and indeed PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice have markedly impaired neutrophil recruitment in early stages of S. aureus growth and develop worse lesions. These findings establish that dermal FBs are central players on the frontline of innate immunity.

The IL-1R1–dependent IAF signature we define here is optimized for neutrophil recruitment and innate immune response. Keratinocyte IL-1α–activated FBs produce potent, neutrophil-targeted chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL2, as well as the critical neutrophil growth factor granulocyte colony-stimulating factor. These FBs also recruit monocytes with CCL2, which amplify the IAF response by contributing IL-1β. IL-6 also promotes further proinflammatory signaling in FBs and nearby cells and is associated with elevated neutrophil chemotaxis (25), while IAF-derived prostaglandin E2 and hyaluronan enhance neutrophil migration (27, 29). IAFs produce the opsonin-like molecule Pentraxin 3 (PTX3), which can aid in pathogen recognition and clearance (46). These functional responses of IAFs collectively support the capacity of neutrophils to manage the threat of SA, which could otherwise lead to a bloodstream infection and sepsis.

Upon arrival, neutrophils can use various mechanisms to target and kill SA, including release of reactive oxygen species (47), recognition and phagocytosis of opsonized bacteria (48), and production of potent antimicrobials such as CAMP (49). The depletion of CAMP in PS mice was likely at least partially due to a lack of neutrophil infiltration. However, comparing with Ly6G localization, the CAMP signal in CS skin appears to be derived from both neutrophils and keratinocytes, another known source of the antimicrobial peptide (50). This observation suggests that FBs may influence keratinocyte antimicrobial production, either directly through an unknown mediator downstream of IL-1R1 or indirectly through other effects of FB IL-1R1 deficiency on the skin. Skin of PS mice also featured distinct transcriptional programming from CS mice, with a bias toward interferon-associated gene programs, suppression of key inflammatory regulators (Il12a, Il12b, and Jun), and induction of extracellular matrix remodeling genes (Mmp3, Tnc, and Tnfaip6). These alterations imply that FB IL-1R1, in addition to promoting neutrophil recruitment, may help coordinate the broader inflammatory state of the skin microenvironment during early infection. While further experimentation is needed to define these mechanisms, our results provide strong indications that epidermal-stromal IL-1 signaling promotes multiple layers of early host defense against superficial SA.

While these findings build on an exciting understanding of FBs in immunity, we recognize some important limitations to this study. Keratinocytes were underrepresented across samples in our scRNA-seq analysis of PdgfraΔIl1r1 and Il1r1fl/fl control mice, limiting our ability to fully capture early keratinocyte transcriptional responses to SA. Complementary studies using Krt14ΔIl1a mice could help to clarify the specific contributions of keratinocyte-derived IL-1α in this context beyond the systemic IL-1α blockade we demonstrate here, but we anticipate logistical challenges, as Il1a and Il1b have been shown to compensate for each other (51, 52). Regarding the lack of statistical significance comparing the response of anti–IL-1α–treated and IgG-treated mice to 48-hour topical SA, reagent cost and feasibility considerations limited our investigation to N = 5. We expect that a higher-powered experiment could better define differences in lesion development in our infection model, but our results were consistent with the more robust data on FB IL-1R1. It bears noting that as neither cultured human cells nor laboratory mice are perfect models of human skin, translating these findings to a clinical setting will require analysis of infected human skin. In addition, the dose, method, growth phase, and duration of topical S. aureus application are all variables that likely influence FB activation, and each merits additional investigation to capture the full kinetics and heterogeneity of FBs in host defense. Despite these logistical constraints, our study provides key insight into epidermal-stromal communication and reinforces the concept that FBs are critical to skin immunity.

Over the past decade, the prevailing view of FBs has expanded from structural cells to dynamic immune effectors. This shift was catalyzed by our group’s finding that dermal adipocytes protect against S. aureus skin infection by rapidly producing CAMP, establishing FB-lineage cells as direct participants in innate host defense (18). Since then, inflammatory FB states have been characterized in rheumatoid arthritis (53), cancer (54), ulcerative colitis (55, 56), acne (57), and psoriasis (58). These IAFs are now understood to be heterogeneous, with distinct and overlapping immune roles across tissues and disease conditions (20, 59). Additional studies have revealed that IAFs can be regulated by leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) (60), Oncostatin-M (61), IL-36 (62), IL-1 (63), IL-4/13 (64), IL-17A (65), transforming growth factor–β (66), TNF (67), and interferon-α2 (68). Despite these recent advances, the upstream triggers of IAF activation remain incompletely defined, underscoring the need for continued investigation to characterize the roles of FBs in skin disease.

An improved understanding of IAFs will demand placing FBs into a clinical context of inflammatory disease, barrier dysfunction, and microbial dysregulation. In atopic dermatitis, filaggrin deficiency is strongly associated with impaired epidermal barrier integrity, dysbiosis, and chronic inflammation, with evidence linking keratinocyte IL-1α to disease-associated immune activation (69, 70). IL-1α is also elevated in diabetic wounds, where a keratinocyte-macrophage IL-1α–IL-1R1 axis contributes to a persistent, nonhealing inflammatory environment (71). Beyond physical barrier disruption, environmental triggers such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation induce rapid release of IL-1α from keratinocytes, which can promote inflammation and tissue remodeling through activation of FBs (72, 73). These UV-driven pathways may be implicated in diseases such as lupus erythematosus, where flares are closely linked to UV exposure and dysregulated innate immune signaling (74). Together, these observations support a broader framework in which keratinocyte-derived IL-1α functions as an early epithelial alarm signal and engages stromal cells to amplify immune responses. Further exploration of the roles of IAFs in these disease contexts may uncover novel therapeutic targets.

Several unanswered questions remain about this keratinocyte-FB IL-1α/IL-1R1 axis of communication. Which downstream signaling pathways mediate the IAF program and its effects on neutrophil recruitment? How do FBs influence keratinocyte antimicrobial responses, including the regulation of CAMP observed here? What is the nature of communication between IAFs and macrophages, and how does FB IL-1R1 signaling help shape myeloid populations during early infection? Can these pathways be therapeutically targeted to improve outcomes of S. aureus skin infection? Last, how does this signaling axis play a role in other IL-1α–driven disease contexts? By establishing FBs as early responders to keratinocyte-derived IL-1α and coordinators of neutrophil recruitment, our study reframes their role in cutaneous defense and suggests avenues to prevent the transition of S. aureus from benign colonizer to invasive threat.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

The aim of this study was to define the cellular networks that prevent cutaneous infection by superficial SA. Sample sizes for in vivo experiments were determined on the basis of preliminary studies and feasibility, including at least four to six mice for SA-treated groups and 2–4 for vehicle controls. Vehicle mice were not included at the 48-hour time point, as local lesions could be compared to off-site skin as background. Inclusion criteria were prespecified: All mice in good health at baseline were included; samples were only excluded in the case of technical failure or clear confounding effect (e.g., mouse successfully removed Tegaderm and gauze and on-site hair cycling). Animals were randomly assigned to sample groups with sex- and age-matched controls. Lesion severity scores were assigned by investigators blind to group. No outliers were removed. Fourteen-hour and 48-hour time points were chosen on the basis of results of time course, with feasibility considerations. Three biological replicates were used for all in vitro assays, except in specified cases for which the experimental goal was exploratory. All experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and relevant controls were included for each experiment and treated consistently. Primary endpoints were neutrophil recruitment and bacterial burden, with secondary endpoints of FB transcriptional and proteomic responses. This study combined unbiased transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic analyses with targeted in vitro and in vivo experiments, allowing us to define an Il1r1-dependent communication axis driving FB recruitment of neutrophils.

Animals and animal care

All animal experiments were approved by the University of California, San Diego (UCSD) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (#S09074). Wild-type, Pdgfra-Cre, Il1r1-fl/fl, and Il1r1−/− mice on C57BL/6 background were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and bred and maintained at UCSD. Mice were housed under a specific pathogen–free condition with 12-hour light and dark cycle at 20° to 22°C and 30 to 70% humidity. Experimental and littermate control animals were age- and sex-matched 8- to 11-week-old males and females.

Mouse models

The reanalyzed scRNA-seq dataset used a mouse model described in Nakatsuji et al. (21). All experiments involving live animals performed for this publication used the following model, with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Guidelines of UCSD (#S09074). Twenty-four hours ahead of bacterial application, back skin of C57BL/6 mice was shaved, treated with depilatory cream (Nair), and thoroughly cleaned with alcohol swabs. Meanwhile, a glycerol stock containing 3 × 107 CFU S. aureus was cultured in 25 ml of tryptic soy broth (TSB) in a 250-ml flask at 37°C with 200 rpm for 9 to 9.5 hours. S. aureus was then pelleted and resuspended in room temperature (RT) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before dilution to 1 × 109 CFU/ml. Vehicle was prepared as PBS with a corresponding volume of TSB (∼5 to 10%). Eight-ply gauze was cut and separated into two-ply, 1-cm2 squares. Mouse back skin was cleaned by alcohol swab and dried by Kim wipe, and then gauze was placed in the central lower back region. A total of 50 μl S. aureus (or vehicle) was applied over the gauze for a dose of 5 × 107 CFU, and back skin was covered by Tegaderm wound dressing film. A total of 200 μg/mouse in 100 μl of PBS of the neutralizing antibody, monoclonal Armenian hamster anti-mouse IL-1α (BE0243, BioXCell), or polyclonal Armenian hamster IgG (BE0091, BioXCell) was intraperitoneally injected 24 hours ahead of S. aureus application.

Bacterial strain and culture

S. aureus (USA300) was grown on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates at 37°C under aerobic conditions overnight. A single colony was isolated and grown in TSB at 37°C at 100 rpm with aeration overnight before pelleting, resuspending in TSB, diluting to 3 × 107 CFU/ml in 50% glycerol, and storing at −80°C.

Quantification of bacterial load on skin surface

Immediately after euthanasia, Tegaderm and gauze were removed from the mouse. For live bacteria collection, swab was soaked in 1 ml of glycerol stock (66.7% TSB, 16.7% glycerol, and 16.7% water) and then passed over the application site 20× with constant pressure. After collection, swab was returned to glycerol stock and frozen on dry ice before storing at −80°C. To quantify bacteria, samples were serially diluted (100 – 10−5) in PBS and grown on TSA plates overnight at 37°C. The following day, isolated colonies were counted, multiplied by appropriate dilution factors, divided by the area of the gauze footprint, and averaged across technical replicates (N = 2 to 3), resulting in a value of 1 CFU/cm2 per mouse.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Skin biopsies were embedded fresh in OCT (23-730-571, Thermo Fisher Scientific), immediately frozen on dry ice, stored at −80°C, and later sectioned to 20 μm at −20°C using a Leica CM1860 cryostat. Skin sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (28908, Thermo Fisher Scientific) before either hematoxylin (MER 47991L, Mercedes Scientific) and eosin (HT110116-500, Sigma-Aldrich) staining (H&E) or immunohistochemistry (IHC). For IHC, sections were then permeabilized with saponin (1 mg/ml; A18820.22, Thermo Fisher Scientific) (except in the case of IL-1α staining), blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA; 30 mg/ml) and glycine (15 mg/ml), and stained with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at RT, nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (P36935, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and sections were stored at 4°C until visualization. Antibodies included: monoclonal Armenian hamster anti-mouse IL-1α (11 μg/ml; BE0243, BioXCell), monoclonal rat anti-mouse Ly6G (1:200; 127602, BioLegend), polyclonal rabbit anti-CAMP [produced in-house as described previously (75)], polyclonal rabbit anti-SA (1:1000; NB100-64499, Bio-Techne), Alexa Fluor 594–goat anti-Armenian hamster IgG (1:1000; A78966, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Cy3–goat anti-rat IgG (1:500; 405408, BioLegend), and Cy3–donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; 406402, BioLegend).

Fluorescence microscopy and quantification

Epifluorescence images of H&E staining were taken using an EVOS4000, with brightness and contrast adjustments made in FIJI (76) and applied across all images. IHC staining was visualized by confocal microscopy using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a white light laser. Z-stacks were processed as maximum intensity projections in FIJI, with subsequent color balance adjustments made equally across all images.

FIJI was also used to calculate the epidermal thickness by tracing the epidermal area of H&E images of perilesional sections and dividing that area by the length of the epidermis. Ly6G signal of 14-hour samples was the average pixel intensity in the red channel of an region of interest (ROI) covering the upper dermal region of IHC images. CAMP and IL-1α signal were measured similarly, but with the ROI for CAMP including the epidermis as well, and the ROI for IL-1α tracing the borders of the epidermis only. Because the large areas with minimal signal, S. aureus signal was calculated differently. A small ROI was defined to collect six background intensity measurements in unstained areas of the red channel each IHC image. A large ROI encompassing the full epidermis and dermis was then used to obtain total integrated density (IntDen) for each image. Background IntDen values were averaged and scaled to the area of the main ROI and then subtracted from the main ROI IntDen for a background-corrected intensity. These values were normalized to the mean of images from control mice to yield fold change.

Lesion quantification

Photos of skin lesions were taken with mice placed on graph paper and then scaled to superimpose the grid before cropping to a standard size. FIJI was used to trace the outline of each lesion and quantify the area, using the grid as a reference to convert to square millimeters. Lesion redness index was derived by splitting the image into RBG channels, quantifying the pixel intensity of a 4-mm2 ROI placed at the most intensely affected area, as well as at an unaffected area of skin for background, and calculating for each mouseR−0.5×(G+B)lesional−R−0.5×(G+B)background

To quantify lesion severity, images were arranged in random order and assessed blindly for four parameters: (i) peak intensity of erythema within the footprint of the gauze where SA was applied (0: indistinguishable from background, 4: deep, dark red); (ii) proportion of gauze footprint with moderate-to-high erythema (0: no high intensity areas, 3: >75% intensely red); (iii) spread of erythema beyond gauze footprint (0: redness completely contained, 3: affected area > 2× gauze footprint); (iv) surface damage (0: no damage, 3: significant peeling and necrosis). The sum of the four scores was used as the lesion severity index.

scRNA-seq analysis

Methods for the WT topical infection scRNA-seq were previously published, including the mouse model, sample collection and processing, and single-cell RNA library construction (21). The 10x Genomics Cell Ranger software pipeline (77) was used with parameters to perform sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, alignment to the mm10 reference genome, and single-cell gene counting. Using Seurat (78), data were filtered (1600 < unique molecular indicators < 35000, 600 < features < 6000, <15% mitochondrial genes, and <0.1% hemoglobin genes), processed, and analyzed. DoubletFinder (79) was used with default settings, and cells with pANN ≥0.35 were removed. Data were further processed in Seurat with standard parameters of NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, ScaleData, and RunPCA. Clusters were identified using FindNeighbors with 20 principal components (PCs), FindClusters (resolution = 2), and RunUMAP (20 PCs). Expression of canonical skin cell lineage markers was used to annotate cell types for each cluster, and CellChat (80) was used with default parameters for network analysis between cell types. For visualization of communication in SA-treated skin, netVisual_circle was used. Edge width was defined as calculated interaction weights multiplied by the square root of the cell count in the sender population, while vertex size was the square root of the cell count.

Pseudobulk analysis was performed on FBs by summing Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts per gene for each sample with AggregateExpression, and then edgeR::cpm (81) was used to convert to counts per million (CPM). Fold changes were calculated (SA versus V) with 0.001 added to avoid dividing by 0, and top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with fold change > 2 and average CPM > 10. This list of FB DEGs was used for pathway enrichment with clusterProfiler (82) and ReactomePA (q < 0.04) (83). FBs were reclustered with 16 PCs and resolution = 2, and then clusters were assigned to 14 subsets based on marker expression. Pseudobulk analysis was repeated on the FB subsets, and pseudobulked counts were used to create a Seurat object for PCA by subset. IAF score was calculated for each cell with AddModuleScore using these genes: Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Csf3, Il6, Ccl2, Ptgs2, Ptges, Ptx3, and Has1.

Selection of IAF gene panel

A panel of nine genes was selected to represent and quantify the IL-1R1–dependent immune activity of a given FB. Four independent transcriptional datasets were compared: (i) pseudobulked FBs from scRNA-seq of SA- versus vehicle-treated skin (see Fig. 1); (ii) RNA-seq of KC-CM-treated versus control FBs (see Fig. 2); (iii) RNA-seq of KC-CM-treated FBs with or without anakinra (see Fig. 4); (iv) Pseudobulked FBs from scRNA-seq of Il1r1fl/fl control versus PdgfraΔIl1r1 with SA (see Fig. 5).

Across datasets, 18 DEGs were shared (see fig. S1C). Of these, nine (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Csf3, Il6, Ccl2, Ptgs2, Ptges, Ptx3, and Has1) were chosen for their established roles in neutrophil recruitment and host defense (22–29). The remaining shared DEGs (Cxcl3, Cxcl5, Dio2, Ereg, Hbegf, Lif, Ngf, Rnd1, and Serpinb2) were excluded from composite IAF scores due to either redundancy or limited evidence linking them to neutrophils.

scRNA-seq analysis

For the 14-hour scRNA-seq experiment, either Il1r1fl/fl control or PdgfraΔIl1r1 was topically treated with either vehicle or SA [Il1r1fl/fl Control Vehicle (CV), PdgfraΔIl1r1Vehicle (PV), CS, and PS]. Four skin biopsies (10 mm) were collected from either two (two each CV and PV) or four (one each CS and PS) ∼9-week-old females at the site of application (N = 4) and pooled together by group before processing. Skin was kept on ice and cut into small pieces before digestion for 1 hour on ice and another at 37°C and 250 rpm in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; 14175095, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with BSA (20 mg/ml), antibiotic-antimycotic (1×;s 15240062, Thermo Fisher Scientific), deoxyribonuclease I (40 U/ml; 4716728001, Sigma-Aldrich), Liberase TL (100 μg/ml; 5401020001, Sigma-Aldrich), Hepes (20 mM; 15630080, Thermo Fisher Scientific), sodium pyruvate (2 mM; 11360070, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and collagenase type IV (1 mg/ml; 17104019, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tissue dissociation was terminated by addition of 5 mM EDTA and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Samples were passed 2× through a 40-μm filter, pelleted at 400 Relative Centrifugal Force (rcf) for 10 min, and incubated in red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer (11814389001, Roche) before terminating lysis with 8% FBS in HBSS. Dead cells were removed with the Dead Cell Removal Kit (130-090-101, Miltenyi Biotec), counted, and diluted to 1000 cells/μl in 0.04% ultrapure BSA (AM2618, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS.

Libraries were generated using the 10× Genomics Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits V4 protocol and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Cell Ranger was used with default parameters for sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, alignment to the mm10 reference genome, and single-cell gene counting. Barcodes with ≥1000 UMIs were retained directly, while low-UMI barcodes (10 to 999 UMIs) were evaluated by DropletUtils::EmptyDrops (84) with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.01. Barcodes that passed EmptyDrops were then recombined with high-UMI barcodes for a Seurat object, which was then filtered (150 < UMIs < 48,000, 80 < features < 7000, <12% mitochondrial genes, and <1% hemoglobin genes). DoubletFinder was used, removing cells with pANN ≥ 0.45, followed by NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, ScaleData, and RunPCA. For clustering with FindNeighbors, FindClusters, and RunUMAP, nine PCs were used, with resolution = 3. Clusters were assigned to cell type lineages based on known marker genes, and FBs were subset for further analysis.

Pseudobulk analysis was performed on FBs as described above, and pseudobulked counts were used to create a Seurat object for PCA by sample. FBs of PS versus CS skin were compared, and genes with fold change < −2 and average CPM > 10 were used for Reactome pathway enrichment analysis. FBs were also reclustered, with 24 PCs and resolution = 3, and resulting clusters were assigned to 16 subsets based on marker gene expression. PCA was performed by subset as described above, and the IAF scores were assigned to each FB as described.

Pseudobulk analysis was also performed on myeloid cells myeloid cells as described above, and myeloid cells of PS versus CS were compared. Genes with fold change > 2 and average CPM > 10 were used for Reactome pathway enrichment analysis. Myeloid cells were reclustered with 30 PCs and resolution = 3, and then clusters were assigned to 14 subsets based on marker gene expression.

Cell culture

All cells were grown in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2 and 37°C under sterile conditions. Primary dermal FBs were isolated as previously described (85) from neonatal or adult WT, Il1r1−/−, Il1r1fl/fl control, or PdgfraΔIl1r1 mice. Primary dermal FBs and HPAds (802s-05a, Cell Applications) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; 11995073, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated (56°C for 30 min) FBS (SH3091003HI; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% GlutaMAX (35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (15240062, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Human Adult Low-Calcium High-Temperature Keratinocytes (HaCaTs) were grown in the same medium, absent the GlutaMAX. NHEKs (C0015C, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were grown in EpiLife medium containing 60 μM of CaCl2 (MEPI500CA, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1% human keratinocyte growth supplement (S0015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic. All KC-CM was collected from confluent cells 48 hours after changing medium and between passages 4 and 7. All conditioned medium experiments were performed on FBs 48 hours postconfluency and before passage 10. FBs were treated with KC-CM for 4 hours unless otherwise stated. Anakinra was applied to FBs 24 hours before treatment and again with KC-CM, while neutralizing antibodies were added to KC-CM 24 hours ahead of treatment. Recombinant human proteins used included: IL-1α (500 pg/ml; 200-LA-002/CF, R&D), IL-1β (500 pg/ml; 201-LB-005, R&D), IL-6 (10 ng/ml; 7270-IL-025, R&D), IL-33 (1 ng/ml; 3625-IL-010, R&D), IL-36γ (50 ng/ml; 6835-IL-010, R&D), and TNF (40 ng/ml; 210-TA-020, R&D). IL-1 signaling inhibitors used included anakinra (10 μg/ml; IL-1R1, kindly provided by H.M.M.), anti–IL-1α (1 μg/ml; AF-200-SP, R&D), and canakinumab (10 μg/ml; anti–IL-1β, kindly provided by H.M.M.).

Conditioned medium preparation

KC-CM was collected and passed through a 0.22-μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane to remove cellular debris. Except where specified (Figs. 2B and 3, E and J, and fig. S2, B and C), all KC-CM was dialyzed before use. For dialysis, KC-CM was passed through an Amicon Ultra-15 10-kDa column (UFC901024, Sigma-Aldrich) and washed 3× in serum-free DMEM. Retained liquid was brought to 1% of the initial supernatant volume with DMEM, aliquoted, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C before use at 300× dilution in FB medium (33.3% dose equivalent).

For SA-stimulated KC-CM, USA300 was cultured in TSB for 18 hours before pelleting and collecting the supernatant. The SA-CM (or TSB control) was passed through a 0.22-μm filter and applied to HaCaTs (or media control) for 24 hours as 5% of medium. After 24 hours, KC-CM was collected, filtered again, and applied to HPAds as 20% of medium for 4 hours before lysis.

Treatments of undialyzed KC-CM were diluted to 20% of FB medium and compared to FB with untreated 20% KC-CM or media control, including the following: heat (70°C for 30 min), proteinase K (50 μg/ml for 2 hours at 55°C and 5 min at 95°C; 25-530-049, Thermo Fisher Scientific), precipitation (24 hours in 60% ammonium sulfate at 4°C, pellet, reconstituted in original volume serum-free DMEM; 1702-10, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and size-based fractionation (Amicon Ultra-0.5 10- or 50-kDa columns, flow-through and retained portions each reconstituted to original volume in serum-free DMEM; UFC501024 and UFC505024, Sigma-Aldrich).

RNA isolation, cDNA library preparation, and qPCR

RNA from cells and tissues were preserved with RNAlater (AM7020, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were lysed, and RNA was isolated with PureLink Isolation kit (12183025; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA libraries were prepared with Verso cDNA synthesis kit (AB1453/B, Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed using the CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green Mix (QP1311, Biomiga). Housekeeping genes Tbp and GAPDH were used to normalize expression for mice and humans, respectively. Specific primer sequences (IDT) are shown in table S1. Relative expression was calculated as 2[Cq(housekeeper)-Cq(gene)].

Bulk RNA-seq

Isolated RNA passing quality control (RNA integrity number > 9) underwent stranded mRNA sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), yielding an average of 28 million stranded 100–base pair paired-end reads per sample. Partek Flow and Partek Genomics Suite software was used for subsequent analysis (with default parameters unless otherwise noted) to determine transcript abundance and differential expression between KC-CM and control and between KC-CM + anakinra and KC-CM. Reads were trimmed (Phred > 20), aligned to human reference genome hg38, quantified, filtered, and normalized. Differential expression was computed with gene specific analysis, resulting in the fold changes and P values used for data visualization.

Multiplex immunoassay

The multiplex immunoassay was performed on 0.22-μm-filtered FB-CM 24 hours after treatment with KC-CM using LEGENDplex Human Proinflammatory Chemokine Panel 1 (13-plex) with Filter Plate (740984; BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were read using a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer and analyzed with the Data Analysis Software Suite for LEGENDplex, with concentrations calculated from standard curves.

Migration assays

On ice, bone marrow was flushed from femurs and tibias of WT mice with Magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS) buffer [0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA in PBS (pH 7.2)]. Cells were pelleted at 300 rcf for 10 min at 4°C and then resuspended gently in RBC lysis buffer for 1 min and washed in MACS buffer. Bone marrow cells were then passed through a 40-μm cell strainer, pelleted, and resuspended at 4 × 106 cells/ml in DMEM with 0.5% FBS. Spleen was directly pushed through a 40-μm cell strainer, pelleted, resuspended in RBC lysis buffer, washed with MACS buffer, pelleted again, and resuspended to match the bone marrow. Meanwhile, mouse dermal FBs were treated with KC-CM or control for 24 hours (in DMEM with 0.5% FBS to reduce FBS-driven leukocyte migration), and FB-CM was collected alongside media controls. Each sample was passed through a 22-μm filter to remove cells and debris, and then 600 μl was loaded into the lower chambers of 3-μm 24-well transwell plate. A total of 100 μl (4 × 105 cells) of either bone marrow or spleen isolate was loaded into the upper chambers, and leukocytes were allowed 3 hours to migrate at 37°C before 60 μl of 0.5 M EDTA was added to lower chambers and plates were incubated at 4°C for 15 min. Alongside these samples, input controls contained the leukocytes in DMEM without a transwell, thus modeling 100% diffusion. The lower portion, containing migrated cells, was collected and pelleted, and nonspecific binding was blocked with rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (1:80; 101302, BioLegend). Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies, fixed with Stabilizing Fixative (1×; 338036, BD), and stored at 4°C. Samples were read using a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo (BD). Antibodies included (all 1:80) the following: Live/Dead Fixable Aqua Stain (L34965, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CD45-PerCP (103129, BioLegend), CD11b-PE/Cy7 (101216, BioLegend), Ly6G-PE (127608, BioLegend), Ly6C-BV711 (128037, BioLegend), CD11c-APC (17-0114-81, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CD3e-BV421 (100341, BioLegend), and CD8a-BV605 (100744, BioLegend).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

IL-1α present in KC-CM was quantified using a human IL-1α DuoSet enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DY200, R&D). KC-CM was collected from HaCaT cells after 24-hour treatment with 5% SA-CM or 5% TSB in 95% medium. The SA-CM had been previously passed through a 0.22-μm PVDF membrane after 18-hour culture of USA300 in TSB.

Phosphoproteomic sample collection and processing

To account for the quantity of protein needed for phosphoproteomics, a fresh batch of KC-CM (NHEK) was dialyzed alongside EpiLife medium. HPAds were grown in 15-cm plates with eight plates per condition. Dialyzed KC-CM was diluted 300× in 10 ml of FB medium per plate and applied to cells for 10 min, 30 min, or 2 hours, while dialyzed EpiLife was similarly diluted and applied for 10 min. For sample collection, cells were gently washed in PBS on ice and scraped in 10 ml of PBS before pooling two plates to each tube for four final biological replicates. Cells were pelleted at 1000 rpm at 4°C for 5 min and washed twice in PBS before flash freezing cell pellets in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80°C.

Samples were processed as previously described (86). Each pellet was thawed on ice, and 500 μl of lysis buffer was added, containing 6 M urea, 7% SDS, 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate, pH adjusted to 8.1, a cOmplete ULTRA EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablet (06538282001, Roche), and a PhosSTOP EASYpack phosphatase inhibitor tablet (04906837001, Roche). Samples were vortexed for 20 min and then pushed through a 22-gauge needle 20 times to ensure complete homogenization. Lysates were probe sonicated for 5 s on ice, using a Q500 QSonica sonicator with a 1.6-mm microtip horn and a pulse setting at 20% amplitude, followed by water bath sonication for 5 min. Sonicated samples were clarified by centrifuging at 16,000 rcf for 5 min at RT, and supernatants were transferred to a fresh Eppendorf 2.0-ml LoBind tube (EP022431102, Sigma-Aldrich) for reduction. Reduction was performed on protein samples with 10 μl of 500 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for a final concentration of 5 mM for 30 min at 47°C. Following reduction, samples were alkylated using 30 μl of 500 mM iodoacetamide for a final concentration of 15 mM and were incubated at RT for 45 min in the dark. Methylation was quenched using 10 μl of 500 mM DTT for a final concentration of 5 mM and incubated at RT for an additional 15 min. Samples were stored at −80°C until extraction.

Samples then underwent chloroform-methanol extraction to prepare for protein digestion, as previously described (87). Four times the volume of methanol was added to each sample, and the resulting mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at maximum speed (3000 rpm) for 2 min. Following centrifugation, 1× the original volume of chloroform was added, and the protein mixture was vortexed, followed by centrifugation at maximum speed for 2 min. Following the addition of chloroform, 3× the original volume of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)–grade water was added to each sample, and the resulting mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at max speed for 2 min. The resulting mixture contained a protein precipitated pellet at the interphase of the aqueous and an organic layer, and all solvents were removed so that only protein pellets were kept. Last, 4× the original volume of methanol was added to the protein pellets and vortexed, followed by centrifugation. Methanol was removed, and samples were cooled on ice before proceeding to the next step. Protein pellets were washed twice with 600 μl of ice-cold acetone and centrifuged at max speed for 5 min at 4°C between each wash. Pellets were dried on a heat block for 20 min at 56°C and stored at −20°C before protein digestion.

Protein pellet digestion was performed by thawing and resuspending protein pellets in 300 μl of 1 M urea in 50 mM Hepes (pH 8.5). Each sample was digested with 50 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin (PRV5117, Promega) in 1 M urea in 50 mM Hepes (pH 8.5) by adding 200 μl of trypsin suspension to each sample. All samples were digested for 6 hours at 37°C, and the reaction was quenched by adding 50 μl of 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Samples containing peptides were frozen at −80°C and lyophilized before sample cleanup via desalting. Desalting was conducted by resuspending lyophilized samples in 2 ml of 0.1% TFA and transferring to C18 resin Sep-Pak 500-mg columns (186004620, Waters). Desalted peptides were eluted using 40% acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.5% acetic acid (AA) and 80% ACN and 0.5% AA, frozen at −80°C, and lyophilized. Cleaned peptides were next resuspended in 50% ACN and were quantified using Thermo’s Pierce Quantitative Assay Kit (23275, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For global proteomics, 30 μg of peptides was aliquoted, and 900 μg of peptides was aliquoted for each sample for phosphopeptide enrichment. Aliquoted samples were frozen at −80°C and lyophilized before both tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling and phosphopeptide enrichment.

Phosphopeptide enrichment and TMT labeling

For phosphoproteomic analysis, samples were enriched with TiO2. Briefly, lyophilized samples were resuspended in 1.8 ml of binding buffer (2 M lactic acid and 50% ACN) and vortexed for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. Clarified supernatant was then transferred to a 2.0-ml Protein LoBind Eppendorf tube containing (4:1 bead-to-sample ratio) Titansphere TiO2 5-μm beads (5020-75000, GL Sciences Inc.) previously washed 1× with binding buffer, 1× with elution buffer (50 mM KH2PO4), and again 2× with binding buffer. Samples combined with TiO2 beads were subjected to incubation at RT with vertexing at the lowest setting for 1 hour. After incubation, bound phosphopeptides were washed 3× with binding buffer and 3× with wash buffer (50% ACN/0.1% TFA). Phosphopeptides were eluted with 2× 200 liters of elution buffer by centrifugation at ×3000 rpm for 2 min. Resulting enriched phosphopeptides were acidified with 200 μl of 1% TFA, frozen at −80°C, and lyophilized. After enrichment, all samples were desalted once more as described above, frozen at −80°C, and lyophilized before TMT-labeling.

Both peptides for proteomics and phosphopeptide-enriched peptides were resuspended in 50 μl of 30% dry ACN in 200 mM Hepes (pH 8.5) in preparation for TMT labeling. A TMTpro 16-plex labeling kit, lot number ZE391936 (A44520, Thermo Fisher Scientific) resuspended in 30% dry ACN in 200 mM Hepes (pH 8.5) was used for labeling of all samples. Sample labeling was conducted by adding 7 μl of each TMT label to prerandomized sample set. Labeling was performed for 1 hour at RT, and reaction was quenched by adding 8 μl of 5% hydroxylamine and incubating for an additional 15 min at RT. Labeled samples were acidified by 50 μl of 1% TFA before pooling each 16-plex together. Pooled reactions were frozen at −80°C and lyophilized. Resulting 16-plex lyophilized reactions were desalted using 50-mg Sep-Pak columns (WAT054955, Waters) as described above. Desalted TMT-plexed reactions were fractioned using a Thermo Fisher Scientific UltiMate 3000 reversed-phase HPLC. Briefly, lyophilized reactions were resuspended in 115 μl of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution and fractioned with increasing concentrations of acetonitrile. A total of 12 fractions were collected, frozen at −80°C, and lyophilized.

Mass spectrometry

The lyophilized, fractionated samples were resuspended in 30 μl of 5% ACN/5% Formic Acid (FA) for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Mass spectrometry–based proteomic analysis was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer containing an in-line EASY-nLC 1000 System. LC–tandem MS (LC-MS/MS) was performed using an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer adapted to an in-line EASY-nLC 1000. Fraction samples were run on a 180-min gradient with a 3% ACN/0.125% FA elution solution. The gradient progressed to end with a 100% ACN/0.125% FA elution solution. Eluted peptides were separated using an EASY-Spray PepMap Neo C18 column with a length of 150 mm and an inner diameter of 75 μm (ES65150PN, Thermo Fisher Scientific). We performed source ionization by applying 2000 V of electricity.

Proteomic spectral data processing

Data were processed using Proteome Discover 2.5.0.400 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tandem Mass Spectrometry (MS2) data were searched against the human reference proteome (UP000005640) using the Sequest algorithm (88). A decoy search was also conducted within sequences in reverse order (89–91). A precursor mass tolerance of 10 parts per million (92, 93) was specified and 0.6-Da tolerance was applied for MS2 fragments with TMT 16-plex. Static modification of TMTpro 16-plex tags on lysine and peptide N termini (+304.207 Da) and carbamidomethylation of cysteines (+57.02146 Da) were specified, and variable oxidation of methionine (+15.99492 Da) was also included within our search parameters. Data were filtered using the percolator built-in node to 1% peptide and protein level FDRs using the target-decoy strategy (94, 95).

The TMTpro reporter ion intensities were gathered from the MS3 spectra for quantitative analysis, and signal-to-noise values were used for downstream quantitation. All spectra were filtered and summed as previously described (96, 97). An average of these normalizations was used for the next steps. To account for slight differences in amounts of protein labeled, these values were then normalized to the median of the entire dataset and reported as final normalized summed signal-to-noise ratios per protein per sample. For any missing values, the median of the entire dataset was acquired and used for downstream binary comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10 and are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, or n.s. (not significant). Where error bars are included, they reflect 1 SD from the arithmetic mean. Binary comparisons were assessed by unpaired, two-tailed Welch’s t tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way Welch’s analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test (adjusted P-values) was used to correct for unequal variances and multiple groups, except where a two-way ANOVA is specified (Fig. 4B). Experimental details, including the number of biological replicates (N) and thresholds for significance, are specified in the figure legends.
