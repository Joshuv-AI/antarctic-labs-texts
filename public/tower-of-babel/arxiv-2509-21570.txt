Breaking 1 / ϵ 1/\epsilon Barrier in Quantum Zero-Sum Games: Generalizing Metric Subregularity for Spectraplexes
Yiheng Su, Emmanouil-Vasileios Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis, Pucheng Xiong

Abstract
Long studied as a toy model, quantum zero-sum games have recently resurfaced as a canonical playground for modern areas such as non-local games, quantum interactive proofs, and quantum machine learning. In this simple yet fundamental setting, two competing quantum players send iteratively mixed quantum states to a referee, who performs a joint measurement to determine their payoffs. In 2025, Vasconcelos et al. 2025 connected quantum communication channels with a hierarchy of quantum optimization algorithms that generalize Matrix Multiplicative Weights Update ( MMWU ) through extra-gradient mechanisms, establishing an average-iterate convergence rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) \bigoh(1/\epsilon) iterations to ϵ \epsilon -Nash equilibria. While a long line of work has shown that bilinear games over polyhedral domains admit gradient methods with linear last-iterate convergence rates of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \bigoh(\log(1/\epsilon)) , it has been conjectured that a fundamental performance gap must persist between quantum feasible sets (spectraplexes) and classical polyhedral sets (simplices). We resolve this conjecture in the negative. We prove that matrix variants of Nesterov’s iterative smoothing ( IterSmooth ) and Optimistic Gradient Descent–Ascent ( OGDA ) achieve last-iterate convergence at a linear rate in quantum zero-sum games, thereby matching the classical polyhedral case. Our analysis relies on a new generalization of error bounds in semidefinite programming geometry, establishing that SP-MS ( SP-MS ) holds for monotone operators over spectrahedra, despite their uncountably many extreme points. Finally, as a byproduct, we obtain an exponential speed-up over the classical Jain–Watrous [ 22 ] method for parallel approximation of strictly positive semidefinite programs.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Min–max optimization lies at the intersection of optimization, game theory, and machine learning. In its most general form, given an objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, one seeks to solve

(\chi^{*},\psi^{*})\;=\;\arg\min_{\chi\in X}\;\arg\max_{\psi\in\Psi}f(\chi,\psi).

The origins of this problem trace back to young John von Neumann, who in December 1926 presented at the Göttingen mathematics seminar his seminal work on
Gesellschaftsspiele

[
49
]

min–max points

(X,\Psi)
are simplices
(\Delta^{n},\Delta^{m})

Nash equilibria
in two-player zero-sum games.

Nearly a century after von Neumann’s minimax theorem, attention has shifted to settings where players’ strategies are quantum states. In such regimes, classical game theory no longer suffices, giving rise to the study of
quantum games
—a framework that captures the exchange and processing of quantum information with implications for complexity, cryptography, and quantum computation. Foundational motivations stem from the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox

Beyond non-locality, subsequent developments revealed deep links to quantum interactive proofs and cryptography. In particular, non-local games can be compiled into single-prover interactive protocols

1.2
Prior Work

Our work builds directly on the influential results of Jain and Watrous
[
23
]
and
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
on non-interactive quantum refereed zero-sum games. Jain and Watrous re-introduced through the nomeclature of quantum channeling the
Matrix Multiplicative Weights Update
(
MMWU
) algorithm, a quantum analogue of the classical matrix exponentiated gradient method of Tsuda et al.
[
47
]
, itself a special case of Mirror Descent
[
38
,
6
]
.
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
extended this framework, showing that a hierarchy of first-order methods can be adapted to quantum zero-sum games; to the best of our knowledge, the
\bigoh(1/\epsilon)
rate achieved by the
Optimistic Matrix Multiplicative Weights Update
(
OMMWU
) algorithm remains the state of the art for computing
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibria.

To obtain sharper guarantees, we revisit the classical literature on finite zero-sum games. By linear programming duality, a folklore result states that
\textsc{Zero-SumNash}\leq_{P}\textsc{LP}
; namely,

computing Nash equilibria reduces to solving a linear program,
which in principle provides a
\bigoh(\log(1/\epsilon))
algorithm.
In practice, however, even in classical setting, LP-based techniques such as the ellipsoid method and interior-point methods are not the algorithms of choice in high dimensions: their per-iteration cost and memory requirements scale poorly. This motivated a shift towards iterative gradient-based algorithms, which feature very low per-iteration complexity and have become the dominant approach.

From an optimization perspective, solving a zero-sum game

\min_{x\in\Delta^{n}}\;\max_{y\in\Delta^{m}}\;\langle x,Uy\rangle

is equivalent to solving a variational inequality for a monotone operator over the polyhedral constraint set

\Delta^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}x=1\},\Delta^{m}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}y=1\}.

Indeed, a pair
(x^{*},y^{*})\in\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{m}
is a Nash equilibrium if and only if

\begin{cases}\langle x^{*},Uy^{*}\rangle\;\leq\;\langle x,Uy^{*}\rangle&\forall\,x\in\Delta^{n},\\[6.0pt]
\langle x^{*},Uy^{*}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle x^{*},Uy\rangle&\forall\,y\in\Delta^{m},\end{cases}\equiv\langle F(z^{*}),\,z-z^{*}\rangle\;\geq\;0\quad\forall\,z\in Z,

where

F(z):=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}\langle x,Uy\rangle,-\,\nabla_{y}\langle x,Uy\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}Uy,-U^{\top}x\end{pmatrix},Z:=\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{m},\quad z:=(x,y).

In a line of work pioneered by Tseng
[
46
]
, and later developed by Nemirovski
[
37
]
and Auslender–Teboulle
[
5
]
, the
mirror-prox
family of algorithms was shown to achieve an iteration complexity of
\bigoh(1/\epsilon)
, which Ouyang and Xu
[
41
]
recently proved to be optimal in the general convex-concave setting.

To obtain sharper rates, additional structure is required. For example, in strongly convex–concave problems, or more generally for strongly monotone variational inequalities, gradient descent–ascent achieves linear convergence
\bigoh(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
, where
\kappa
is the condition number (the ratio of smoothness to strong convexity modulus). A classical approach to enforce such structure is to add quadratic regularization (Tikhonov smoothing). However, if the regularization parameters are chosen on the order of
\epsilon
, the condition number scales as
\bigoh(1/\epsilon)
, leading again to
\bigoh((1/\epsilon)\log(1/\epsilon))
complexity. This drawback motivated
Nesterov’s iterative smoothing
(
IterSmooth
)
[
40
,
14
]
, where the game is first regularized with a constant magnitude and the regularization is gradually reduced, so that the algorithm starts from a well-conditioned surrogate and converges arbitrarily close to the original game.
Gilpin et al. 2012
showed that this decaying-regularization scheme succeeds for polyhedral feasible sets, yielding iteration complexity
\bigoh(\log(1/\epsilon))
.

A well-known limitation of
IterSmooth
is that it operates as a restarting, double-loop
procedure, a design that tends to be viewed as impractical in applied settings.
In contrast, polyhedrality has recently been identified as a particularly powerful structural property.
Advances on optimistic algorithms, including projected

Optimistic Gradient Descent–Ascent
(
OGDA
) and

Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Update
(
OMWU
)
[
13
,
50
,
3
]
,
demonstrate that, for bilinear games over simplices, polyhedral constraints act as an implicit
strong convexifier. This structural advantage yields exponentially fast last-iterate convergence
to Nash equilibria. Conversely, when the feasible region is specified by higher-order polynomial
constraints, such acceleration is no longer achievable, delineating a sharp boundary on the power
of first-order methods
[
50
]
.

Key Ingredient:
Error bounds
.

The structural property that enables gradient-based algorithms to achieve
linear convergence is the existence of an
error bound
, which we will formalize below.
Before doing so, it is useful to recall the distinction between
weak
and
strong

approximations of Nash equilibria—a distinction that parallels the complexity-theoretic separation
between the
PPAD
and
FIXP
classes:

•

An
\epsilon
-weak approximation of a Nash equilibrium

z_{\mathrm{weak}}:=(x_{\mathrm{weak}},y_{\mathrm{weak}})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\langle x_{\mathrm{weak}},Uy_{\mathrm{weak}}\rangle\;\leq\;\langle x,Uy_{\mathrm{weak}}\rangle+\epsilon&\forall x\in\Delta^{n},\\[6.0pt]
\langle x_{\mathrm{weak}},Uy_{\mathrm{weak}}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle x_{\mathrm{weak}},Uy\rangle-\epsilon&\forall y\in\Delta^{m},\end{cases}

which can be measured by the
duality gap

\mathcal{G}(z_{\mathrm{weak}}):=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\Delta^{m}}\langle x_{\mathrm{weak}},Uy^{\prime}\rangle-\min_{x^{\prime}\in\Delta^{n}}\langle x^{\prime},Uy_{\mathrm{weak}}\rangle.

Intuitively, an
\epsilon
-weak equilibrium is simply an
\epsilon
-best response to the opponent’s strategy.
Note that the minimizers of the duality gap coincide exactly with the set of Nash equilibria, i.e.,

\arg\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{G}(z)=\mathcal{N}\!ash
.

•

By contrast, an
\epsilon
-strong approximation of a Nash equilibrium is any strategy profile that lies within

\epsilon
of an exact Nash equilibrium
z^{*}
:

z_{\mathrm{strong}}:=(x_{\mathrm{strong}},y_{\mathrm{strong}}),\qquad\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{N}\!ash}(z_{\mathrm{strong}}):=\min_{z^{*}\in\mathcal{N}\!ash}\|z_{\mathrm{strong}}-z^{*}\|\leq\epsilon.

If the utilities are Lipschitz continuous, then strong approximation implies weak approximation up to constants.
The role of error bounds is to establish the converse: namely, that weak approximations also yield strong ones.
Informally, we say that the zero-sum game satisfies an
error bound
with modulus

\kappa>0

if

\mathcal{G}(z)\;\geq\;\kappa\,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{N}\!ash}(z)\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{Z}.

(Error Bound)

It has been known since the work of Tseng
[
46
]
that affine variational
inequalities satisfy such a bound. With this framework in place,
the central question
(\ast)
regarding
\bigoh(\log(1/\epsilon))
iteration complexity
reduces to understanding whether error bounds continue to hold when one replaces
polyhedral constraint sets with spectraplexes:

What geometric obstacles prevent the derivation of error bounds

over spectraplexes, in contrast to the classical polyhedral case?

1.3
Challenges in Error Bounds over Spectraplexes

For the sake of readability, we revisit below the central argument in the derivation of error bound for bilinear zero-sum games over simplices or even more general polyhedral constrained sets. Then we sketch the main challenges that are raised in generalizing in more smooth domains like spectraplexes. Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
denote the players’ feasible sets and

\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
When
\mathcal{X}=\Delta^{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\Delta^{m}
, the epigraph of the map

z\mapsto\mathcal{G}(z)
is polyhedral. Consequently, it can be represented as the convex
hull of finitely many vertices
\{(z_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
with
t_{i}=\mathcal{G}(z_{i})
.
The proof strategy in
[
14
]
exploits a
finite
vertex separation
: there exists a smallest positive vertex height

t_{\min}:=\min\{t_{i}:\ t_{i}>0\}
, and the sets

S_{>0}:=\{(z_{i},t_{i}):\ t_{i}>0\},\qquad S_{\leq 0}:=\{(z_{i},t_{i}):\ t_{i}\leq 0\}

are separated by a nonzero vertical gap.
A Hoffman-type argument then yields (
Error Bound
) with

\kappa\ =\ \frac{t_{\min}}{\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\|z^{\prime}-z\|:\ (z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in S_{>0},\ (z,t)\in S_{\leq 0}\bigr\}},

a constant depending only on the game instance.
However, for quantum games, the feasible sets are spectraplexes

\mathsf{Spec}_{d_{x}}:=\bigl\{X\succeq 0:\ \operatorname{Tr}\left[X\right]=1\bigr\},\;\mathsf{Spec}_{d_{y}}:=\bigl\{Y\succeq 0:\ \operatorname{Tr}\left[Y\right]=1\bigr\},

whose extreme points (rank-one projectors) form an
uncountable
set. Hence, two geometric effects invalidate the polyhedral argument:

•

Accumulation of extreme points at zero gap.

Because there are infinitely many extreme points, one can construct a sequence of
extreme profiles
\{z_{k}\}
with
\mathcal{G}(z_{k})\downarrow 0
.
Equivalently, the set of “vertex heights” of the epigraph has
0
as an
accumulation point:

\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset\{\mathcal{G}(z):\ z\ \text{extreme in }\mathcal{Z}\},\qquad\sup_{k}t_{k}=0.

Hence there is no instance-dependent
t_{\min}>0
separating
S_{>0}
from
S_{\leq 0}
.
The denominator in the simplex proof remains bounded, but the numerator
t_{\min}
collapses,
forcing
\delta\to 0
and destroying (
Error Bound
).

•

Curved (non-polyhedral) boundary and infinitely many supporting planes.

The boundary of the spectraplex is smooth/curved (semi-algebraic), not polyhedral.
Thus the epigraph of
\mathcal{G}
admits
infinitely many
supporting hyperplanes, and
standard Hoffman-type constants for linear systems do not apply. In particular,
local normal cones can vary continuously along rank-deficient strata, which allows
approach directions with arbitrarily small
\mathcal{G}
at non-negligible Euclidean distance.

A tempting but inadequate fix.

One might attempt to replace
t_{\min}
by a manually chosen threshold
\omega>0

and work with the truncated sets
S_{>\omega}:=\{(z,t):\ t>\omega\}
.
However, the resulting constant

\kappa(\omega)\ =\ \frac{\omega}{\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\|z^{\prime}-z\|:\ (z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in S_{>\omega},\ (z,t)\in S_{\leq 0}\bigr\}}

now depends on the
user-chosen

\omega
rather than solely on the game instance,
and
\kappa(\omega)\to 0
as
\omega\downarrow 0
. This fails to deliver an instance-wise
error bound of the form (
Error Bound
).

Takeaway.

The simplex proof hinges on a finite vertex gap and polyhedral geometry. Spectraplexes
lack both: (i) zero-gap accumulation along infinitely many extreme points, and
(ii) non-polyhedral curvature with infinitely many supporting hyperplanes.
Any direct transposition of the polyhedral argument therefore collapses, and proving
error bounds over spectraplexes requires new ideas that respect the semidefinite geometry
(e.g., rank–stratification-sensitive bounds or metric subregularity tailored to spectrahedra).

1.4
Our Contributions and Methodology

Having established the context of prior work and the challenges in extending error bounds from polyhedral sets to spectraplexes, we answer in a positive way our central question
(\ast)
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to demonstrate that gradient-based methods achieve
linear convergence
in quantum zero-sum games, thereby surpassing the long-standing barrier of linear-constrained domains.

Main Result 1
.

There exists a universal constant
\gamma=\bigoh_{d}(1)
such that the matrix adaptation of
IterSmooth
computes an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium in a refereed quantum zero-sum game, defined by a super-operator
F
, in
\bigoh_{d}(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations.

Methodology.

To obtain this result, we develop a novel analytical tool tailored to the intersection of saddle-point objectives and metric subregularity. Our starting point is a matrix adaptation of a folklore multi-loop method, so-called Nesterov’s Iterative Smoothing. Algorithmically, at the core of
IterSmooth
lies (a) the classical accelerated gradient descent subroutine, whose performance remains optimal even in semidefinite geometry and (b) a deregularizing decaying scheme. On the other hand, the proof proceeds in two main steps:

Main Proof Step 1
.

For a convex function
f
defined over a spectraplex, the matrix analogue of Nesterov’s accelerated method—which we call
q-Smoothing
—computes an
\epsilon
-optimal solution in
\bigoh_{d}({1/\epsilon})
iterations.

More importantly, we establish a new
metric subregularity
condition for the saddle-point operator (SP-MS) defined over spectraplexes:

Main Proof Step 2
.

There exists a constant
c>0
, independent of the approximation accuracy, such that

\displaystyle\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\Bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\val(\superop)\Bigr)\;\geq\;c\,\left\lVert\alpha-\proj_{\mathcal{N}ash|\mathcal{A}^{\ast}}(\alpha)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\forall\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigl(\val(\superop)-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]\Bigr)\;\geq\;c\,\left\lVert\beta-\proj_{\mathcal{N}ash|\mathcal{B}^{\ast}}(\beta)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\forall\,\beta\in\mathcal{B},

where
\superop
is the super-operator defining the payoff gradients of the quantum zero-sum game over the mixed-state strategy sets
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
,
\val(\superop)
denotes the game value, and
\mathcal{A}^{\ast},\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
are the corresponding Nash equilibrium sets.

Building on this, we further show that an even simpler method—a matrix variant of Optimistic Gradient Descent–Ascent (
OGDA
)
[
52
,
48
,
34
,
30
]
—inherits the same linear convergence guarantees. Notably, our result does not contradict the findings of
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
. Specifically, their analysis establishes that the
ergodic average

\bar{z}(T)=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}z_{k}
converges at rate
\bigoh(1/T)
, whereas our result shows that the
iterates themselves
converge geometrically, i.e.,
z_{T}=\bigoh(e^{-\rho T})
.

Main Result 2
.

There exists a constant step-size
\eta=\bigoh_{d}(1)
such that the matrix adaptation of
OGDA
computes an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium in a refereed quantum zero-sum game defined by a super-operator
F
in
\bigoh_{d}(\log(1/\epsilon))

Finally, as a direct corollary of our techniques, we obtain an exponential speed-up over the classical approach of Jain and Watrous
[
22
]
for semidefinite programming:

Corollary
.

There exists a parallel approximation algorithm that computes a

Below, we sketch the main ideas behind the proof of the (SP–MS) error bound over spectraplexes.

1.5
Proof Insights: The SP–MS Error Bound for Spectraplexes

Our approach draws inspiration from the proof of Theorem 5 in
[
51
]
, which establishes related results for the simplex, but we highlight the new difficulties that arise in the semidefinite (spectraplex) setting.
The overall strategy decomposes into five parts.
Before detailing each step, recall that establishing an error bound between the duality gap and the distance from the Nash set reduces to proving that, for some constant
c>0
independent of accuracy,

\displaystyle\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\val(\superop)\bigr)\;\geq\;c\,\left\lVert\alpha-\proj_{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}}(\alpha)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\forall\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigl(\val(\superop)-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]\bigr)\;\geq\;c\,\left\lVert\beta-\proj_{\mathcal{B}^{\ast}}(\beta)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\forall\,\beta\in\mathcal{B}.

Observe these inequalities are symmetric; hence it suffices to prove the first.

(Claim
1
)
. Geometric structure of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

We first establish that the set
\mathcal{A}^{*}
of optimal minimax strategies is convex and compact.
This follows from the convexity and continuity of the payoff function.
Geometrically, this ensures that projections onto
\mathcal{A}^{*}
are well-defined and unique, providing a clean notion of distance from a candidate
\alpha
to the equilibrium set.

(Claim
2
)
. Separation via violated constraints.

Next, we show that for any non-equilibrium strategy
\alpha\notin\mathcal{A}^{*}
, there exists a non-tight optimality constraint that separates
\alpha
from its projection
\alpha^{*}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
Formally, we prove by contradiction that

\max_{\nu\in\mathcal{C}_{o}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\nu(\alpha-\alpha^{*})\right]>0,

where
\mathcal{C}_{o}
is the set of non-tight optimality constraints.
Intuitively, if this inequality failed,
\alpha
would satisfy all the same tight constraints as
\alpha^{*}
, contradicting the fact that
\alpha^{*}
is the closest equilibrium.

(Claim
3
)
. Relating duality gap to constraint violations.

We then establish that the maximum payoff deviation against
\alpha
dominates the maximum violation over non-tight constraints:

\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta)\right]-\val(\superop)\bigr)\;\geq\;\max_{\nu\in\mathcal{C}_{o}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\nu(\alpha-\alpha^{*})\right].

This step connects the duality gap (algorithmic quantity) to geometric constraint violations (structural quantity).

(Claim
4
)
. Decomposition via normal cone.

We show that the deviation
\alpha-\alpha^{*}
can be expressed as a (possibly infinite) conic combination of constraint-defining matrices:

\alpha-\alpha^{*}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}v\;p(v),

where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of constraints and
p
is a probability distribution.
This follows from representing
\alpha-\alpha^{*}
as an element of the normal cone of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
at
\alpha^{*}
.
In other words, the error vector points “outward” in directions defined by violated constraints.

(Claim
5
)
. Bounding the weights and completing the proof.

Finally, we combine the above ingredients to relate the squared distance
\|\alpha-\alpha^{*}\|_{F}^{2}
to constraint violations:

\|\alpha-\alpha^{*}\|_{F}^{2}\;\leq\;\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\nu(\alpha-\alpha^{*})\right]\,\int_{\mathcal{S}}1\;p(v).

By Steps 2–3, the first factor is lower-bounded by the duality gap.
It remains to upper-bound the “total weight”
\int_{\mathcal{S}}1\,p(v)
.
We show that the mapping from normalized deviations
(\alpha-\alpha^{*})/\|\alpha-\alpha^{*}\|_{F}
to their total weight is continuous, and since the domain is compact, this quantity is uniformly bounded by a constant
C
.
This completes the derivation of the desired error bound.

A\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the trace is

\operatorname{Tr}\left[A\right]:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{ii}
.

•

Inner product.

For
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product is

\langle A,B\rangle:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[A^{\dagger}B\right].

•

Frobenius norm.

For
A\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
,

\|A\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left[A^{\dagger}A\right]}=\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|A_{ij}|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}
.

•

Dual norm.

For a normed space
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|)
with dual
\mathcal{X}_{*}
,
the dual norm of
y\in\mathcal{X}_{*}
is

\|y\|_{*}:=\sup\{\langle y,x\rangle:x\in\mathcal{X},\|x\|\leq 1\}
.
The Frobenius norm is self-dual:
\|x\|_{*}=\|x\|_{F}
for all
x
.

•

Adjoint operator.

For a linear map
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}_{*}
, the adjoint

F^{\dagger}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}_{*}
is defined by

\langle F(x),y\rangle=\langle x,F^{\dagger}(y)\rangle
for all
x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

•

Operator norm.

For a linear map
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}_{*}
, the operator norm is

\|F\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{\|x\|=1,\;\|y\|=1}\langle F(x),y\rangle.

Finally, crucial to our analysis are the notions of strong convexity and smoothness, which govern the curvature properties of functions and play a central role in determining algorithmic convergence rates.

Definition 1

(
\mu
-Strong Convexity)
.

A differentiable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-strongly convex
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
if, for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
,

f(X)\;\geq\;f(Y)+\langle\nabla f(Y),X-Y\rangle+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|X-Y\|^{2}.

Definition 2

(
\beta
-Smoothness)
.

A differentiable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\beta
-smooth
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
if, for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
,

f(X)\;\leq\;f(Y)+\langle\nabla f(Y),X-Y\rangle+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\|X-Y\|^{2}.

2.2
Quantum Zero-Sum Games

In this work, we study a restricted but fundamental class of non-interactive refereed quantum zero-sum games. In this setting, two adversarial players, Alice and Bob, independently prepare quantum states and send them to a referee. The referee performs a joint measurement on the received states and, base on a fixed utility function, assigns a payoff to Alice. Since the game is zero-sum, Bob’s payoff is exactly the negative of Alice’s. Thus, any gain by Alice corresponds to an equal loss by Bob.

Formally, Alice selects a quantum state
\alpha
from her set of all possible moves, while Bob chooses a state
\alpha
from his own set. These state spaces are spectraplexes, consisting of density matrices of the appropriate dimension. Such density matrices represent mixed states–the quantum analogue of mixed strategies in classical game theory. Throughout this work, we assume Alice prepares
n
-qubit states within her spectraplex,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{H}^{n}_{+}:\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\right]=1\right\},

while Bob prepares
m
-qubit states within his spectraplex,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\left\{\beta\in\mathcal{H}^{m}_{+}:\operatorname{Tr}\left[\beta\right]=1\right\},

where
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{+}
is the set of
2^{d}\times 2^{d}
-dimensional, positive Hermitian matrices.
We adopt the same game setting and notation as
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
, which builds upon the original framework of
Jain & Watrous 2008
for quantum zero-sum games. We denote the joint state space as

\displaystyle\Psi=(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}.

It is a known fact that all spectraplexes are compact and convex sets and the diameter of joint state space
\mathcal{Z}
is 2. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix C.1 and Propostion 5 of
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
.
Alice and Bob each prepare quantum states, which are jointly measured through a
POVM
(
POVM
) with a finite outcome set
\Omega
. The POVM is defined by

\displaystyle\left\{P_{\omega}\right\}_{\omega\in\Omega},\quad\text{where }\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}P_{\omega}=\mathbb{I}.

Given a joint state
\Psi
, the probability of obtaining outcome
\omega\in\Omega
is defined as

\displaystyle p_{\omega}(\Psi)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{\dagger}\Psi^{\otimes}\right].

Associated with each outcome
\omega\in\Omega
is a real-valued utility
\mathcal{U}(\omega)\in[-1,1]
where we assume the utility function
\mathcal{U}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is finite-valued and normalized. Alice’s payoff observable is defined as

\displaystyle U=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathcal{U}(\omega)P_{\omega}.

The expected payoff
f\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
received by Alice is then

\displaystyle f(\Psi)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[U^{\dagger}\Psi^{\otimes}\right]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathcal{U}(\omega)\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{\dagger}\Psi^{\otimes}\right]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathcal{U}(\omega)p_{\omega}(\Psi).

Since it is a zero-sum game, Bob is utility function is
-\mathcal{U}
, payoff observable is
-U
, and expected payoff is
-f(\Psi)
. Let’s look at the gradient of the payoff function, which we can think as the feedback for Alice and Bob. Alice and Bob’s payoff gradients have been derived in
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
. We restate them here, for Alice’s payoff gradient

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(\beta)=\grad_{\alpha^{\top}}f(\alpha,\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[U^{\dagger}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\beta\right)\right],

and for Bob’s payoff gradient

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(\alpha)=\grad_{\beta^{\top}}-f(\alpha,\beta)=-\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}\left[U^{\dagger}\left(\alpha\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right],

We define the following superoperator
\superop\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
to capture Alice’s payoff gradient

\displaystyle\superop(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[U^{\dagger}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\beta^{\top}\right)\right],

(1)

and define the adjoint superoperator
\superop^{\dagger}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
to capture Bob’s gradient

\displaystyle\superop^{\dagger}(\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{A}}\left[U^{\dagger}\left(\alpha^{\top}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\beta}\right)\right],

which are same as the original work from
Jain & Watrous 2008
.
Accordingly, Alice and Bob’s expected payoff can be expressed as

\displaystyle f(\Psi)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[U^{\dagger}(\alpha\otimes\beta)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha F_{\alpha}(\beta)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta)\right]

(2)

\displaystyle-f(\Psi)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Tr}\left[U^{\dagger}(\alpha\otimes\beta)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[F_{\beta}(\alpha)\beta\right]=-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\superop^{\dagger}(\alpha)\beta\right].

(3)

The gradient-based perspective, introduced by
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
, shifts the focus from the quantum channel-based formulation of
Jain & Watrous 2008
to the geometry of the payoff landscape over the spectraplexes. Equation (
2
) and (
3
) show that Alice’s and Bob’s payoffs are precisely the directional derivatives of
f
with respect to their states
\alpha
and
\beta
. This gradient-based view allows us transfer gradient-based techniques from classical game theory and optimization into the quantum setting. We adopt this viewpoint throughout using the joint payoff gradient

\displaystyle F(\Psi)=(F_{\alpha}(\beta),F_{\beta}(\alpha))=(\superop(\beta),-\superop^{\dagger}(\alpha)).

(4)

2.3
Min-Max Optimization for Quantum Zero-Sum Games

In a quantum zero-sum game defined above, we consider the following constrained min-max optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}f(\alpha,\beta).

(QZS-MinMax)

The corresponding max-min problem is

\displaystyle\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}f(\alpha,\beta).

(QZS-MaxMin)

The compactness of spectraplexes guarantees the existence of optimal states and convexity ensures that mix states are themselves valid quantum states. Since payoff function
f
is bilinear, von Neumann’s Min-Max Theorem implies that the following equality holds

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}f(\alpha,\beta)=\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}f(\alpha,\beta).

(5)

We refer to the common value in (
5
) as the value of the game, denoted by
\val(\superop)
.

Denote
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
as the set of min-max and max-min optimal state respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}f(\alpha,\beta)

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}f(\alpha,\beta)

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\oplus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}

A joint state
\Psi^{\ast}=(\alpha^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}
is called a Nash equilibrium if

\displaystyle f(\alpha^{\ast},\beta)\leq f(\alpha^{\ast},\beta^{\ast})\leq f(\alpha,\beta^{\ast})\quad\forall\;(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{Z}.

We associate to the game the upper and lower value functions

\displaystyle\primal(\alpha)\coloneqq\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}f(\alpha,\beta{{}^{\prime}})=\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]

\displaystyle\dual(\beta)\coloneqq\min_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}f(\alpha{{}^{\prime}},\beta)=\min_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]

Then, problem
QZS-MinMax
and
QZS-MaxMin
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\primal(\alpha)\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\dual(\beta).

Define the duality gap at the joint state
\Psi
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\Psi):=\primal(\alpha)-\dual(\beta)=\max_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]\right\}.

(Duality gap)

Observe that
\mathcal{G}(\Psi)
is always nonnegative, and by von Neumann’s Min-Max Theorem we have
\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{G}(\Psi)=0
.
Define the optimal solution set as

\displaystyle\Opt\coloneqq\argmin_{\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}}\{\mathcal{G}(\Psi)\}.

(Optimal set)

Let
\dist
denote the distance to
\Opt
under the Frobenius norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
. For any
\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\displaystyle\dist(\Psi)\coloneqq\min_{\Psi^{\ast}\in\Opt}\left\{\lVert\Psi-\Psi^{\ast}\rVert_{F}\right\}.

(Distance to
\Opt
)

As outlined in the introduction, in Section
3
, we derive an iterative smoothing scheme inspired by Nesterov’s framework. To prepare for this, let
\mu\geq 0
denote a smoothing parameter and fix
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\mathcal{Z}
.
We introduce a regularizer
\reg\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

\displaystyle\reg(\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\Psi-\widetilde{\Psi}\rVert_{F}^{2},\quad D=\max_{\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}}\reg(\Psi).

(8)

We define the smoothed duality gap as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)=\max_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]-\mu\reg(\Psi^{\prime})\right\}.

(9)

Here, the regularizer
\reg
penalizes deviations from the reference joint state
\widetilde{\Psi}
, and the parameter
\mu\geq 0
controls the strength of smoothing.
Let
\maximizer(\Psi)
denote the maximizer in Equation (
9
)

\displaystyle\maximizer(\Psi)=\argmax_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]-\mu\reg(\Psi^{\prime})\right\}.

The objective inside the braces is the sum of a linear term in
\Psi^{\prime}
and the negative of a
\mu
-scaled strongly convex function
\reg
.
Hence it is strictly concave in
\Psi^{\prime}
, which guarantees that
\maximizer(\Psi)
is uniquely defined. We now formalize the smoothness properties of the smoothed duality gap.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose the regularizer
\reg
is
\sigma_{\reg}
-strongly convex. Then,

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)
is smooth with gradient

\displaystyle\grad_{\Psi}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)=F(\maximizer(\Psi)),

(10)

and
\grad_{\Psi}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu\sigma_{\reg}}\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}

Proof.

We know the joint payoff gradient is in Equation
(
4
)
and its adjoint

\displaystyle F(\Psi)=(F_{\alpha}(\beta),F_{\beta}(\alpha))=(\superop(\beta),-\superop^{\dagger}(\alpha))

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(\Psi)=\left(-\superop(\beta),\,\superop^{\dagger}(\alpha)\right).

We can rewrite the
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)
in Equation (
9
) using gradient

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)=\max_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\Psi^{\prime})\Psi\right]-\mu\reg(\Psi^{\prime})\right\}.

(11)

Then, the gradient of
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)
in
\Psi
is

\displaystyle\grad_{\Psi}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi)=F(\maximizer(\Psi)).

To establish Lipschitz continuity of the gradient, we adapt Nesterov’s technique from the proof of Theorem 1 in
Nesterov 2005a
. Suppose we have two arbitrary joint states
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
. By the first-order optimality condition, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{\dagger}(\Psi_{1})-\mu\grad\reg(\maximizer(\Psi_{1}))(\maximizer(\Psi_{2})-\maximizer(\Psi_{1}))\right]\leq 0,

(12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{\dagger}(\Psi_{2})-\mu\grad\reg(\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))\right]\leq 0.

(13)

Adding them and applying the strong convexity of
\reg
and the adjoint identity, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{\dagger}(\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2})(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mu\left(\grad\reg(\maximizer(\Psi_{1}))-\grad\reg(\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))\right)\left(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mu\sigma_{\reg}\left\lVert\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

We apply the definition of operator norm

\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}=\max_{\Psi\in\mathcal{Z},\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\Psi)^{\top}\Psi^{\prime}\right]:\lVert\Psi\rVert_{F}=1,\lVert\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{F}=1\}.

(14)

Therefore, for any
\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\displaystyle\lVert F(\Psi)\rVert_{\ast}\leq\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{F}

Following the argument of Nesterov,

\displaystyle\lVert F(\maximizer(\Psi_{1}))-F(\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))\rVert_{\ast}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\cdot\lVert\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2})\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu\sigma_{\reg}}\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\cdot\operatorname{Tr}\left[F^{\dagger}(\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2})(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu\sigma_{\reg}}\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\cdot\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2}))(\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu\sigma_{\reg}}\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\cdot\lVert F(\maximizer(\Psi_{1})-\maximizer(\Psi_{2})\rVert_{\ast}\cdot\lVert\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}\rVert_{F},

which proves the smoothness of
\mathcal{G}
. The Lipschitz constant is

\displaystyle L_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu\sigma_{\reg}}\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}.

(15)

∎

Observe that the regularizer
\reg
is
1
-strongly convex with respect to the Frobenius norm, and denote its modulus by
\sigma_{\reg}=1
.

3
First-Order Algorithms for Quantum Zero-Sum Games

As discussed earlier, our aim is to efficiently approximate Nash equilibria

\Psi^{*}=(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
. Additionally, we say that a joint state
\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate equilibrium
if
\mathcal{G}(\Psi)\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
.
In this section, we introduce two matrix-adapted algorithms for computing

\epsilon
-approximate equilibria, while Section
4

analyzes their convergence and shows that our approach achieves
an exponential speedup over existing methods.

3.1
Nesterov’s Iterative Smoothing Method

In 1983, Nesterov introduced an accelerated gradient method that achieves an iteration complexity of
\bigoh(1/\sqrt{\epsilon})
for minimizing smooth convex functions
\phi
with
L
-Lipschitz gradients. After
\bigoh(1/\sqrt{\epsilon})
steps, the method returns
x\in X
such that
\phi(x)\leq\min_{x^{\prime}\in X}\phi(x^{\prime})+\epsilon
. We refer to this as
Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method
. In 2005, Nesterov extended this framework to certain non-smooth objectives. By applying a smoothing technique, one constructs a smooth approximation

\phi_{\mu}
and then applies the accelerated gradient method to
\phi_{\mu}
.
This yields an
\bigoh(1/\epsilon)
convergence rate for computing an

\epsilon
-approximate minimizer of
\phi
. This approach is known as
Nesterov’s iterative smoothing method
. These smoothing-based methods have been successful for problems with polyhedral feasible regions.

In our setting, we apply Nesterov’s smoothing method to the duality gap
\mathcal{G}
arising in quantum zero-sum games with spectraplex feasible sets. Let
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
denote its smoothed version with Lipschitz constant
L_{\mu}
as established in Section
2.3
. Optimizing over
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
then produces an
\epsilon
-approximate equilibrium to the problem

\displaystyle\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(\Psi).

Building on this, we consider the iterative smoothing method, which integrates decaying-regularization scheme for spectraplexes. The game is first regularized with a constant magnitude to create a well-conditioned surrogate problem, and then the regularization is gradually decreased. In practice, we adopt a geometric schedule: letting
\mu_{i}
denote the
smoothing level
at iteration
i
and
\gamma>1
the reduction factor,

\displaystyle\mu_{i+1}=\mu_{i}/\gamma.

As the regularization vanishes, the method converges to a solution arbitrarily close to that of the original game.
Gilpin et al. 2012
established that this decaying-regularization scheme succeeds for polyhedral feasible sets, achieving iteration complexity
\bigoh(\kappa\ln(1/\epsilon))
, where
\kappa
is some condition measure. In Section
4.3
we prove that the same iteration complexity holds for the case of spectraplexes.

3.2

Beyond smoothing-based approaches, another powerful technique for saddle-point problems is the

Optimistic Gradient Descent–Ascent (
OGDA
)
method. Originally proposed by Popov and later
formalized in online learning and game-theoretic contexts

[
42

52

3

13

modifies the classical extragradient method of Korpelevich by reusing past gradient information
as a predictive correction.

Concretely, at each step

performs a standard gradient descent–ascent update but then
“corrects” this step using an extrapolation of the previous gradient. Intuitively, this
optimistic correction anticipates the opponent’s move, stabilizing the dynamics while
avoiding the need for an extra gradient evaluation. As a result,
OGDA
retains the robustness
of extragradient methods but requires only one gradient computation per iteration, making it
computationally attractive.

From a theoretical perspective,
OGDA
is known to converge linearly in monotone variational
inequalities under appropriate structural conditions. In our setting, we establish that the
matrix-adapted version of

\epsilon

4.2
Saddle-Point Metric Subregularity for Spectraplexes

Before proving SP-MS for spectraplexes, we first formally define SP-MS as follows.

Definition 3
.

(SP-MS for spectraplexes)
For any

\Psi^{\ast}
denote its projection onto the optimal joint state set, defined as
\Psi^{\ast}=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}}(z)\coloneqq\argmin_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}}\left\lVert z-z^{\prime}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\Psi)\,(\Psi-\Psi^{\prime})\right]}{\lVert\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{F}}\geq C\left\lVert\Psi-\Psi^{\ast}\right\rVert_{F}^{s+1}

(20)

holding for some parameter
s\geq 0
and constant
C>0
.

With the definition of SP-MS, we are ready to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

A quantum zero-sum game with bilinear payoff function
f(\alpha,\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta)\right]
defined above satisfies SP-MS with
s=0

Proof.

We aim to prove the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\val(\superop)\right)\geq c\left\lVert\alpha-\proj_{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}}(\alpha)\right\rVert_{F}\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in\mathcal{A}

(21)

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\val(\superop)-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]\right)\geq c\left\lVert\beta-\proj_{\mathcal{B}^{\ast}}(\beta)\right\rVert_{F}\quad\text{for all }\beta\in\mathcal{B}.

(22)

With these two inequalities and the diameter of joint state
\left\lVert\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{F}\leq 2
, we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\Psi)\,(\Psi-\Psi^{\prime})\right]}{\lVert\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{F}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[F(\Psi)\,(\Psi-\Psi^{\prime})\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}})\right]-\min_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{2}\left(\left\lVert\beta-\proj_{\mathcal{B}^{\ast}}(\beta)\right\rVert_{F}+\left\lVert\alpha-\proj_{\mathcal{A}^{\ast}}(\alpha)\right\rVert_{F}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{2}\left\lVert\Psi-\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}}(\Psi)\right\rVert_{F}.

4.3
Convergence Rates for
IterSmooth

In this section, we show how SP-MS for spectraplexes implies the error bound for spectraplexes. Using the error bound, we then prove the total number of iterations required for the

algorithm to compute an
\epsilon
-equilibrium.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\superop:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
be the superoperator defined by POVM and
\mathcal{U}
as in (
1

Duality gap
). Then there exists

\displaystyle\dist(\Psi)\leq\frac{\mathcal{G}(\Psi)}{\delta}.

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\Psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}F(\Psi)^{\top}(\Psi-\Psi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{\beta{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha\superop(\beta{{}^{\prime}}^{\top})\right]-\min_{\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha{{}^{\prime}}\superop(\beta^{\top})\right]\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}(\Psi)\geq\frac{c}{2}\left\lVert\Psi-\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}}(\Psi)\right\rVert_{F}=\frac{c}{2}\dist(\Psi),\quad\forall\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}.

(32)

Therefore, we have

\dist(\Psi)\leq\frac{\mathcal{G}(\Psi)}{c},
so one may take

, completing the proof.
∎

Having established the error bound, we now define the condition measure of a superoperator
\superop

\displaystyle\delta(\superop)=\sup_{\delta}\left\{\delta:\dist(\Psi)\leq\frac{\mathcal{G}(\Psi)}{\delta}\;\forall\Psi\in\mathcal{Z}\right\}.

(33)

algorithm.

algorithm requires at most

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{2}\cdot\gamma\cdot\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}\cdot\ln{(\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}/\epsilon)}\cdot\sqrt{D}}{\ln{(\gamma)}\cdot\delta(\superop)}

first-order iterations to compute an
\epsilon
-equilibrium.

The proof of Theorem
4

IterSmooth
algorithm consists of an inner loop, which invokes
q-Smoothing
at a fixed smoothing level, and an outer loop, which progressively decreases the smoothing parameter. In Step 1, we bound the number of iterations required for
q-Smoothing
to terminate within each inner loop. In Step 2, we bound the number of outer-loop stages before the algorithm terminates. These two steps are formalized in the following claims.

Claim 6
.

Each call to
q-Smoothing
at smoothing level
\mu_{i}
terminates after at most

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{2}\cdot\gamma\cdot\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}\cdot\sqrt{D}}{\delta(\superop)}

iterations.

.

The
IterSmooth
algorithm invokes
q-Smoothing
at most

\frac{\ln(\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}/\epsilon)}{\ln(\gamma)}
times.

Proof of Claim

For each

\displaystyle\dist(\Psi_{i})\leq\frac{\epsilon_{i}}{\delta(\superop)}=\frac{\gamma\cdot\epsilon_{i+1}}{\delta(\superop)}

The
i

5
Parallel Approximation of Strictly
Positive Semidefinite Programs

From the seminal work of
Jain & Watrous 2008
, a fundamental connection was established between equilibrium states of quantum zero-sum games and positive semidefinite programs (SDPs). In particular, their parallel algorithm computes
\epsilon
-approximate equilibria in quantum zero-sum games with iteration complexity
\bigoh_{d}(1/\epsilon^{2})
, and these equilibria can in turn be leveraged to approximate solutions of certain SDPs. Building on this connection, we show that the
\bigoh_{d}(\log(1/\epsilon))
convergence-rate algorithms developed in Section
3
yield an exponential improvement:
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate solutions to SDPs can be obtained with logarithmic iteration complexity, thereby exponentially speeding up the bound of prior parallel approaches. To set the stage for the main result of the section, we first collect the key definitions required for its formulation.

Superoperator Semidefinite Programs.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
be Hermitian matrices, let
\superop\colon\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a linear superoperator that preserves Hermiticity. Given an instance tuple
(\alpha,\beta,\superop)
, the
superoperator form
of the primal/dual semidefinite program is

Primal

\displaystyle\quad\max_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\beta y\right]\qquad\text{subject to }\superop(y)\preccurlyeq\alpha,\quad y\succcurlyeq 0

(34)

Dual

\displaystyle\quad\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\alpha x\right]\qquad\text{subject to }\superop^{\dagger}(x)\succcurlyeq\beta,\quad x\succcurlyeq 0.

(35)

This form is equivalent to the
standard form
of semidefinite programs. We refer to it as the
superoperator semidefinite program
. We use superoperator form to connects quantum zero-sum game with semidefinite programs.

Strong Duality via Slater’s Condition.

In contrast with LP, strong duality always holds whenever the primal (or dual) problem is feasible for the case of SDP, it does not always hold automatically.
Slater’s condition
is a standard constraint qualification in convex optimization which ensures
strong duality
. It requires the existence of a strictly feasible solution that satisfies all inequality constraints with strict inequalities. Applied to our setting, if either the primal or the dual program admits a strictly feasible state, then by Slater’s condition, strong duality holds: the optimal values of (
34
) and (
35
) coincide and are attained. In particular, for the primal–dual pair (
34
)–(
35
), strong duality holds if at least one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

there exists
Y\succ 0
with
\superop(Y)\prec A
,

(ii)

there exists
X\succ 0
with
\superop^{\dagger}(X)\succ B
.

Positive and Strictly Positive Instances.

We call an SDP instance
(\alpha,\beta,\superop)
positive if
\alpha\succcurlyeq 0
,
\beta\succcurlyeq 0
, and
\superop
is a positive superoperator, meaning that whenever
x\succcurlyeq 0
, we also have
\superop(x)\succcurlyeq 0
. We call the instance strictly positive if
\alpha\succ 0
,
\beta\succ 0
, and
\superop
is strictly positive, which is defined by the condition
\superop(\mathbb{I})\succ 0
.

Observe that every strictly positive instance satisfies Slater’s condition. Indeed, for the primal program, pick
t>0
small enough that
\superop(t\mathbb{I})=t\superop(\mathbb{I})\prec\alpha
yielding strictly feasibility for (
34
). Similarly, for the dual program, pick
s>0
large enough that
\superop^{\dagger}(s\mathbb{I})=s\superop^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I})\succ\beta
yielding strictly feasibility for (
35
). Therefore, strong duality holds for all strictly positive superoperator semidefinite program.

With strong duality for strictly positive SDPs in place, we next present the reduction to
trace form
and show how the methods of Section
3
can be deployed.

From Strictly Positive Instances to Trace Form.

The
IterSmooth
algorithm
1
applies once any strictly positive superoperator semidefinite programs with instance
(\alpha,\beta,\superopalt)
has been normalized into the following
trace form

Primal

\displaystyle\quad\max_{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[y\right]\qquad\text{subject to }\superop(y)\preccurlyeq\mathbb{I},\quad y\succcurlyeq 0

(36)

Dual

\displaystyle\quad\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[x\right]\qquad\text{subject to }\superop^{\dagger}(x)\succcurlyeq\mathbb{I},\quad x\succcurlyeq 0

(37)

with

\displaystyle\superop(y)=\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\superopalt(\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}y\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}})\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
\superopalt
is strictly positive, we have
\superop(\mathbb{I})\succ 0
, and hence
\superop
is strictly positive as well. By Slater’s condition, strong duality holds: the common optimal value
\opt(\superop)
of the primal and dual programs is attained and satisfies
\opt(\superop)>0
.

Connection between Quantum Zero-Sum Games and Superoperator SDPs.

The following proposition from
Jain & Watrous 2008
establishes the connection between quantum zero-sum games and strictly positive superoperator semidefinite programs. In particular, it shows how an
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibrium can be transformed into an approximate solution for this program. The connection is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 4

(Proposition 5 in
Jain & Watrous 2008
)
.

For any strictly positive superoperator, we have
\val(\superop)=1/\opt(\superop)
.

At the end of
IterSmooth
algorithm, we have
\mathcal{G}(\Psi)<\epsilon
, which implies that

\primal(\alpha)\leq\val(\superop)+\epsilon,\dual(\beta)\geq\val(\superop)-\epsilon.

Suppose the approximate value of the game
\widetilde{\val}(\superop)=\frac{\primal(\alpha)+\dual(\beta)}{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\lvert\widetilde{\val}(\superop)-\val(\superop)\rvert\leq\frac{\primal(\alpha)-\dual(\beta)}{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Then, the approximated value of the game is between

\displaystyle\widetilde{\val}(\superop)\in\left[\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2\val(\superop)}\right)\val(\superop),\;\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2\val(\superop)}\right)\val(\superop)\right]

Using Proposition
4
, let
\xi=\frac{\epsilon}{2\val(\superop)}
the approximated optimal value falls between

\displaystyle\widetilde{\opt}(\superop)\in\left[\left(1+\xi\right)^{-1}\opt(\superop),\;\left(1-\xi\right)^{-1}\opt(\superop)\right]

Complexity Comparison.

The
OMMP
algorithm of
Vasconcelos et al. 2025
achieves a convergence rate of
\bigoh_{d}(1/\epsilon)
for computing an
\epsilon
-approximate Nash equilibrium, leading to a total complexity of

\displaystyle\bigoh_{d}(\frac{1}{2\val(\superop)}\cdot\frac{1}{\xi}),

for obtaining an approximation of strictly positive semidefinite programs

The
IterSmooth
algorithm attains a convergence rate of
\bigoh(\kappa(\superop)\ln(1/\epsilon))
, which yields a total complexity of

\displaystyle\bigoh\left(\kappa(\superop)\left(\ln(\frac{1}{2\val(\superop)})+\ln(1/\xi)\right)\right),

and the
OGDA
algorithm attains a convergence rate of
\bigoh_{d}(\log(1/\epsilon))
yielding a total complexity of

\displaystyle\bigoh_{d}\left(\log(\frac{1}{2\val(\superop)})+\log(1/\xi)\right)

which establishes an exponential speedup in iteration complexity.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we advanced the study of algorithms for computing equilibria in quantum zero-sum games.
We showed that two of the most effective gradient-based methods from the classical setting—Nesterov’s Iterative Smoothing (
IterSmooth
) and the matrix variant of Optimistic Gradient Descent–Ascent (
OGDA
) of
Wei et al. 2021b
—extend naturally to the quantum domain in the high-precision regime.
Our analysis establishes quantum adaptations of
IterSmooth
and
OGDA
as new benchmarks, achieving an exponential speed-up over
OMMWU
while requiring only a single gradient evaluation per iteration.
This demonstrates that gradient-based methods are both theoretically optimal and practically efficient when precision is the dominant complexity parameter.

A central contribution of this work is the identification of error bounds for quantum zero-sum games over spectraplexes.
While in general optimistic methods suffer from a minimax lower bound of order
\bigoh(1/\epsilon^{2})

[
15
]
, we show that by leveraging Bauer’s maximum principle and tools from convex geometry (e.g., infinite mixtures of normal cones), one can establish succinct error bounds that yield iteration complexity
\bigoh_{d}(\log(1/\epsilon))
.
This is remarkable given that
pure Nash equilibria
in the quantum setting correspond to rank-one density matrices—an uncountable set—yet still admit tractable approximation guarantees.

A natural caveat lies in the modulus
\kappa
of the error bound,

\mathcal{G}(z)\;\geq\;\kappa\,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{N}\!ash}(z).

In the worst case,
\kappa
may scale exponentially in the game description, threatening the practical efficiency of error-bound based analyses.
Recent work
Anagnostides & Sandholm 2024
has addressed this issue via the celebrated framework of
smoothed analysis
, pioneered by Spielman and Teng
[
45
]
.
Specifically, it was shown that when the payoff matrix is subject to small random perturbations,
\kappa
is polynomially bounded with high probability, leading to polynomial iteration complexity guarantees.
Experimentally, similar behavior appears to hold in semidefinite games, suggesting that a formal smoothed-analysis theorem for spectraplexes would be both natural and impactful.

Several challenges and open problems remain:

1.

Alternative Methods.

We primarily compared
IterSmooth
,
OGDA
.
It would be valuable to investigate whether other semidefinite programming techniques (e.g., interior-point relaxations or first-order primal-dual methods) can yield comparable or complementary performance for approximating quantum Nash equilibria.

2.

Beyond Two-Player Zero-Sum.

Our analysis is limited to the two-player zero-sum setting.
Recent progress on quantum potential games
[
30
]
and quantum polymatrix games
[
31
]
suggests intriguing directions for extending gradient-based techniques to richer models of interaction.

3.

Tightness of Convergence Rates.

For classical zero-sum games,
OMWU
achieves optimal constants under
\bigoh(\log(1/\epsilon))
iteration complexity when the equilibrium is unique.
An important open question is whether analogous optimality results can be established in the quantum case, especially for general monotone variational inequalities over spectraplexes.
Clarifying this tightness would sharpen our understanding of the true limits of first-order methods in quantum optimization.

In summary, our work closes a long-standing conjecture on the performance of gradient-based methods in quantum zero-sum games and highlights error bounds as a unifying tool for analyzing first-order algorithms.
At the same time, it opens several promising avenues—from smoothed analysis of spectraplexes to new models of quantum interaction—that we hope future work will explore.
