Macroecological assessment of invasive species impact on Japanese lake fish communities using eDNA metabarcoding
Authors: Yurika Oba, Hideyuki Doi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological invasion has negative effects on ecological interaction such as competition and predation. The ecological impacts on native communities may also affect two processes that are important in determining community structures, namely, the environmental and spatial processes. Here, we used lake fish species compositions estimated using environmental DNA metabarcoding to investigate the effects of invasive species composition on the native community and on the processes that determine it. The results of PERMANOVA indicated that the native fish assemblage patterns significantly changed along invasive species compositions. Furthermore, it was observed that the diversity of invasive species acted as a potent ecological filter, driving native fish communities towards biological homogenisation. Distance-based Redundancy Analysis revealed that invasive species alter the relationship between ecological communities and their environment. The study suggests invasive species may obscure distinct environmental signals, potentially by having broader environmental tolerances than native species. A comparison of the relative importance of environmental factors and spatial structure in determining species composition between communities, fish species composition in lakes is more strongly influenced by environmental factors than spatial structure, although the predictive power of these factors varies with the presence of invasive species and is most evident in native-only communities. Our results suggest that the need to understand the impacts of invasive species on native species not only through direct effects, but also the complex effects involving the diversity level of invasive species.

Introduction

Biological invasions can cause fundamental changes in ecological communities, biodiversity, and ecosystem functioning, processes, and services1–3. In aquatic ecosystems, biological invasions often alter interspecific interactions, such as competition and predation, which can lead to a decline in native biodiversity and an increased risk of species extirpation4,5. With regard to closed ecosystems such as lakes, fish communities may produce particularly pronounced responses to stressors such as biological invasion6. Invasive species with competitive traits can affect native communities in lakes and connected watersheds throughout the various stages of the invasion process, including introduction, establishment, and spatial expansion.

Environmental conditions of habitats and their spatial structures are important factors governing the mechanisms that determine community structures7. In the case of lake ecosystems, lake morphometrics (area and depth) and geographic location are strongly associated with water quality, the availability of ecological niches, and dispersal potential and thus have a profound impact on fish community composition and size8–12. The ecological traits of the constituent species determine the relative influence of environmental filtering and spatial processes on community assembly. For instance, traits such as dispersal ability and environmental tolerance determine how much local environmental conditions versus spatial connectivity shape fish communities13,14. Introduction and establishment of invasive species that possess ecological traits different from those of native communities can potentially interact with the environmental and spatial factors that affect fish community structures in lakes, potentially affecting community structure and species diversity patterns. For example, invasive species introduction can cause a decrease in alpha and beta diversity, potentially leading to the biological homogenization of fish communities15,16. However, some studies have shown that even when changes in community structure due to population changes caused by the introduction of invasive species are observed, these changes are not necessarily reflected in local species diversity17. Consequently, it remains difficult to predict how invasive species will affect ecosystem structure across different environments. In particular, there is still a lack of understanding regarding how the presence of invasive species reconfigures the community-environment relationships (i.e., the environmental filtering process) across broad latitudinal gradients.

Large-spatial scale studies covering extensive latitudinal gradients are essential to understand the general rules of community assembly, as environmental filtering—primarily driven by climate—varies significantly across latitudes. In Japan, which spans a wide range of climatic zones, lake fish communities are expected to show distinct latitudinal patterns in response to thermal gradients. However, conventional monitoring of such broad scales is often hindered by the logistical constraints of traditional sampling methods. Environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding has emerged as a powerful tool to overcome these limitations. eDNA metabarcoding has been successfully applied in various aquatic environments, including oceans, rivers, and lakes, to study organism abundances and community structures in aquatic ecosystems18–20. The eDNA methods can effectively detect rare species owing to their high sensitivity21, and they are also suitable for detecting invasive species in the early stages of introduction22.

In this study, we used fish community data collected from 18 lakes covering a wide latitudinal gradient in Japan to assess the effects of invasive species composition on the variation of native fish community structure. To address these objectives, we characterized the fish community using eDNA metabarcoding. In this study, we defined ‘species composition’ based on both species membership (presence/absence) and their respective read counts. First, we examined how the explanatory power of environmental factors for variations in lake fish community structure changed in native and invasive fish communities, using data on fish species composition, lake morphology, and local climate. Second, we assessed the relative importance of environmental factors and spatial structure on community composition for both native and invasive fish. Based on the general understanding, native communities are shaped by long-term environmental filtering while invasive communities are often driven by propagule pressure. Therefore, we expected that native fish would show a stronger correlation with environmental variables than invasive species. Third, we investigated the potential impact of invasive fish diversity on the species composition of native species. This allowed us to evaluate whether interspecific interactions with invasive taxa further constrain native biodiversity beyond the influence of abiotic environmental and spatial factors. We hypothesized that the species composition of native fish in lakes is significantly influenced by the invasive species diversity. Specifically, we expected that a higher invasive species diversity would lead to the biotic homogenization of native communities. Clearly articulating this relationship is crucial for developing new management indicators; by monitoring invasive species composition as a proxy for the risk of native community homogenization, managers can better prioritize intervention strategies for lake ecosystems before regional biodiversity is irreparably lost.

Results

Fish species diversity

The number of fish species in the fish community detected using eDNA metabarcoding in the 18 lakes ranged from 8 to 37 species per lake (Fig. 1). The minimum number of foreign invasive species detected per lake was 0, and the maximum was 11. The number of native and invasive fish species (richness and DNA reads) in all 18 lakes is shown in Fig. 2. Invasive species were detected in 17 of the 18 lakes. A non-significant and weak positive correlation was observed between the number of native species and that of invasive species (Spearman’s ρ = 0.21, p = 0.4056). A non-significant and weak correlation was observed between alpha diversity (inverse Simpson index) of native and invasive species in each lake (Spearman’s ρ = 0.18, p = 0.5235).

Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA)

We performed a db-RDA of the total fish assemblages identified in the 18 lakes. In this study, we analyzed the fish assemblage data using three subsets: (1) the all-species community, which includes all detected fish species; (2) the native community, consisting only of species indigenous to the region; and (3) the invasive community, comprising only non-native species. By comparing these three patterns, we aimed to isolate the specific influence of invasive species on the overall species composition. The db-RDA revealed distinct environmental drivers for the three community patterns (Fig. 3). For the all-species community, the six environmental variables significantly explained 37.4% of the total variation (Permutation test, p = 0.001), with air temperature (p = 0.010) and water type (p = 0.002) identified as the primary drivers. When analyzed separately, the native and invasive communities exhibited different responses to the environmental gradient. For the native community, the environmental variables significantly explained 37.6% of the total variation (Permutation test, p = 0.001), whereas for the invasive community, they explained 28.6% of the variation (p = 0.021). The native community was significantly correlated with air temperature (p = 0.024) and water type (p = 0.003), while the invasive community was primarily influenced by air temperature alone (p = 0.016). The first db-RDA axis (RDA1) explained 27.7% of the fitted variation in the native community, compared to 25.8% in the invasive community. This suggests that the environmental factors evaluated in this study provided a weaker explanation for the invasive species distributions compared to native species ones.

Variation partitioning

Variation partitioning analysis was conducted to distinguish the relative influence of environmental and spatial factors on the community structure of the three groups (Fig. 4). For the all-species community, the total variation explained was 55.8%, with environmental factors uniquely accounting for 30.2%, and spatial factors uniquely explaining 18.4%. In the native community, the total variation explained was 54.3%, the unique contribution of environmental factors was 33%, which was higher than that of spatial factors (16.7%), indicating a predominant role of environmental filtering in structuring native assemblages. In contrast, for the invasive community, the total variation explained was 49.6%, the unique environmental fraction decreased to 22.7%, while the residual (unexplained) variation was the highest among the three groups at 50.4%. The shared fraction between environment and space accounted for 7.2%, 4.6%, and 5.9% for all-species, native, and invasive communities, respectively. These results suggest that while environmental gradients primarily drive native fish distributions, the distribution of invasive species is less predictable by the current environmental and spatial models, potentially reflecting stochastic colonization or human-mediated dispersal.

Fish species composition visualized by NMDS and tested by PERMANOVA

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination reflected the differences in fish species composition among lakes (Fig. 5a). The stress value for the NMDS ordination was 0.149, which is below the threshold of 0.2, indicating that the two-dimensional ordination space adequately represented the native species composition. The PERMANOVA results revealed that the fish community composition differed significantly along the invasive diversity levels (p = 0.033). Specifically, this indicates a significant shift in the group centroids, representing changes in the average species composition associated with the invasive diversity levels. Furthermore, PERMDISP showed that there were significant differences in multivariate dispersion among the groups (p = 0.006) (Fig. 5b). Notably, lakes with higher invasive species diversity exhibited lower community heterogeneity (i.e., smaller multivariate dispersion) compared to other groups. This suggests that the fish species composition in highly invaded lakes is more similar to one another, potentially indicating a process of biotic homogenization driven by invasive species.

Discussion

Our results supported the initial expectation that native and invasive communities are structured by different primary drivers. The strong influence of environmental factors on native species likely reflects their long-term adaptation to historical environmental conditions in this region. In contrast, the relatively weaker effect of the environment on invasive communities suggests that their distribution might be more heavily influenced by dispersal processes or propagule pressure, rather than local environmental filtering. The most striking finding was that increasing invasive species diversity systematically reduces the distinctiveness of native community compositions across lakes. Our study reveals that the invasive fish species acts as a critical driver of biotic homogenization in lake ecosystems, shifting the focus from individual species impacts to broader community-level transformations.

We observed a non-significant and weak positive correlation between the inverse Simpson index of native and invasive species in each lake. This indicates that the introduction of invasive species does not necessarily lead to a decrease in species diversity within these lakes25. However, at some study lakes, approximately 60% of the total species count and 80% of the total DNA reads were attributed to invasive species. Some lakes had a low percentage of invasive species in their fish community richness, but a larger percentage of total DNA reads was detected compared to other study sites. DNA read counts have been reported to be correlated with biomass26,27. Increased abundance and biomass of invasive species can reduce the abundance of native species through competition and predation28. In addition, it has been suggested that there may be a time lag in the impact of the introduction of invasive species on habitats17, it is important to follow up the phenomenon occurring in such lakes and predict future changes.

Our results demonstrate that invasive species diversity acts as a powerful ecological filter that reconfigures native fish assemblages. The significant PERMANOVA results (p = 0.033) indicate a directional shift in community centroids, meaning that the increase in invasive taxa fundamentally alters the average species composition of native communities. More importantly, PERMDISP revealed a significant decrease in multivariate dispersion in lakes with high invasive diversity (p = 0.006). This reduction in community heterogeneity provides strong evidence for ‘biotic homogenization’ mediated by invasive species15. While native communities in low-invasion lakes maintain high variability (beta diversity) reflecting unique local environmental conditions, highly invaded lakes converge toward a simplified and uniform community structure. Interestingly, the NMDS analysis suggests that this homogenization process is non-linear. The dense clustering of sites and the abrupt bending of diversity contours in the ordination space indicate the existence of an ecological threshold. Once invasive species diversity reaches a certain level, the native community may undergo a rapid transition toward a homogenized state. This pattern implies that invasive species can erode the ecological resilience of native assemblages, pushing them beyond a tipping point where local environmental filtering is overwritten by invasive-driven pressures. The mechanistic explanation for this homogenization may lie in the ‘blurring effect’ of invasive taxa on environmental filtering. Highly diverse invasive assemblages often consist of generalist species with broad environmental tolerances. These species may monopolize resources or alter habitats, thereby decoupling native species from their specific environmental niches. Consequently, the distinct community signatures typically shaped by latitudinal or morphological gradients become obscured, resulting in the uniform assemblages observed in our high-diversity group. These findings provide crucial insights for ecosystem management. The identification of a diversity threshold suggests that conservation efforts should focus on preventing invasive species from reaching the levels that trigger rapid homogenization. By monitoring invasive diversity as a proxy for homogenization risk, managers can implement Early Detection and Rapid Response (EDRR) programs more effectively, prioritizing interventions in lakes that are approaching these identified ecological tipping points before regional biodiversity is irreversibly lost.

Our db-RDA revealed that environmental drivers act differently across the three community patterns. The observed shifts in lake positions along the ordination axes among three models suggest that invasive species alter the community-environment relationship. A potential mechanism is that the integration of invasive species “blurs” the environmental signals that are more distinct in native-only communities. This could occur if invasive species have broader niche breadths or are less sensitive to the specific environmental filters that traditionally structure native assemblages in these lakes. Alternatively, since invasive species communities are still somewhat influenced by the environment, albeit differently from native species, it implies that invasion reconfigures the niche space rather than merely adding noise. A previous study of various European lakes showed that environmental characteristics, such as size, turbidity, nutrient enrichment, connectivity, and the presence of non-native fish, can affect the rate at which fish assemblages change across a latitude gradient29. This conclusion was reinforced by the slight differences in the placement of each lake relative to the first axis (representing air temperature) across the three community patterns. A potential mechanistic explanation for this shift is that invasive species may alter the collective thermal niche of the community. Since many successful invaders exhibit broad environmental tolerances or different temperature-response curves compared to native assemblages, their presence can obscure or shift the community-level response to the latitudinal temperature gradient. For instance, if invasive species are less sensitive to temperature changes or dominate across a wider range of the gradient, they would effectively “blur” the environmental filtering effect that typically structures native communities along latitude. Consequently, the relationship between community structure and climatic variables becomes modified, leading to the observed shifts in lake positions along the temperature-related axis. A positive correlation between air temperature and lake area was observed exclusively in the invasive species model. This suggests that the structure of invasive assemblages is uniquely shaped by the convergence of thermal suitability and habitat availability. In our study area, larger lakes at lower latitudes may serve as “optimal colonization sites” for invasive species, offering ideal temperatures for establishment and larger surfaces that often correspond with higher human-mediated propagule pressure. The absence of this correlation in the native-only model highlights that invasive species respond to distinct environmental and anthropogenic filters rather than merely following native distribution patterns.

The impact of environmental factors and spatial structure on the variance in fish species composition among the lakes displayed fluctuations contingent on the existence of invasive species. The most prominent divergence in explanatory power occurred among communities composed solely of native or invasive species, with the greatest explanatory power observed in the native species communities. Notably, environmental factors consistently had more explanatory power than spatial structure, regardless of the presence of invasive species. The contrast in the relative importance of environmental factors and spatial structure was most significant in communities consisting solely of native species; this significance was reduced in communities containing only invasive species. These results highlight the complexity of fish species compositions in response to invasion, emphasizing the importance of environmental factors, specifically among communities of native species. Recent studies have suggested that aquatic habitats have undergone changes owing to anthropogenic stress, with pronounced benefits for highly competitive invasive species30,31. The stressors include eutrophication, pollution, climate change, and introduction of invasive species. Some surveyed lakes in densely populated areas may expect to experience high anthropogenic stress. Overall, our findings elucidate the present conditions of several lakes with high potential for invasive species domination in the future. These insights bear significant implications for ecosystem management and conservation efforts, emphasizing the necessity of strategies that focus on fish community compositions of such lakes. Specifically, we recommend prioritizing these high-risk lakes for early detection and rapid response (EDRR) programs, utilizing high-sensitivity tools such as eDNA metabarcoding. Furthermore, management actions should focus on controlling nutrient runoff to prevent the turbid conditions that favor many invasive generalists. Identifying the most vulnerable lakes based on their environmental and community profiles allows managers to allocate limited resources more effectively to prevent further homogenization of fish assemblages.

In conclusion, we evaluated the impact of invasive species on lake fish communities using eDNA metabarcoding data. Understanding the interactions between species composition dynamics, environmental factors, spatial structure, and ecological characteristics is essential to effectively conserving and managing ecosystems. This study serves as a case study to determine the effectiveness of using the data from eDNA metabarcoding to monitor and model the fundamental processes by which invasive species regulate lake fish communities. While phenomena such as homogenization and latitude effects have been reported previously, the combined use of eDNA data and specific composition analysis in this study was highly effective in ensuring the robustness of the findings. This approach has the potential to predict future changes in fish communities, highlighting its value as a complementary research tool. For example, research combining the eDNA approach and stable isotope analysis is rapidly developing as a powerful tool for integrated ecosystem monitoring32. The eDNA approach has overwhelming value in characterizing species compositions because it enables wide-area and high-frequency sampling that is difficult with conventional capture surveys, dramatically improves the comprehensive detection power of the entire community including rare and invasive species, and allows for the acquisition of standardized large-scale data with minimal ecological impact through non-invasive methods. By integrating high-resolution species composition and spatial distribution data obtained via eDNA metabarcoding with long-term energy flow and trophic level data provided by stable isotope analysis, it is possible to comprehensively and quantitatively elucidate the functional impacts of invasive species on the trophic niche of native species and the overall food web structure of a lake.

The study sheds light on the intricate processes shaping fish species composition, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of aquatic ecosystem dynamics. However, the data used in this study are a snapshot from a sampling period so we could not consider temporal changes in the communities, such as details of when invasive species were introduced. Therefore, long-term datasets and surveys are needed to understand changes in community dynamics over time. While our study focused on how the overall diversity of invasive species leads to biotic homogenization, we acknowledge that specific species identities (compositional effects) might also play a role. Future studies incorporating species-specific traits or identity-based metrics could further refine our understanding of these ecological shifts. Since eDNA samples were collected exclusively from the shoreline, species primarily inhabiting open waters may have been underestimated. Future studies incorporating offshore sampling are needed to provide a more comprehensive characterization of the entire lake community. Local-scale factors such as vegetation cover, substrate composition, and productivity might also play crucial roles in determining the distribution of both invasive and native species. The lack of these local variables in our analysis may limit the explanatory power of our models, and future studies should integrate these micro-habitat characteristics to better understand the mechanisms of invasion.

Methods

Fish community data

We used an eDNA metabarcoding dataset of fish communities from 18 lakes in Japan as previously reported33. Details of the study sites, sampling, sample treatments, and DNA sequencing have been described by Doi et al. (2023)33. Briefly, 18 lakes across Japan were selected as study sites (Fig. 1), and six sampling sites were selected along the shores of each lake with equal distances between them. From 14-July to 4-Nov-2015, water samples were collected at each of the six sites. To obtain water samples, 1 L of surface water was collected in a bottle. The samples were vacuum-filtered through 47-mm GF/F glass fiber filters (nominal pore size of 0.7 μm, manufactured by GE Healthcare) in the laboratory. eDNA was extracted from water filters as described by Uchii et al. (2016)34 and was subsequently purified using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The two-step PCR procedure used for Illumina MiSeq sequencing (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was performed as described by Fujii et al. (2019)35. The first PCR was carried out using MiFish-U-F and MiFish-U-R primers, followed by a second PCR of 12 cycles with a 12-µL reaction volume. Purified PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and paired-end sequenced on a MiSeq platform (Illumina). The bioinformatics analysis procedure was described in detail by Fujii et al. (2019)35, including the removal of primer sequences and merging of identical sequences using UCLUST (usearch 7.0.1001). Taxonomic annotation was performed using a local BLASTN search in BLAST 2.2.29, based on an established reference database of fish species for processed reads36. The identified species and genera were determined using previously described methods37,38. Note that this study did not conduct any new sequences.

Invasive fish species data

A list of invasive fish species was extracted from the Invasive Species of Japan database provided by the National Institute for Environmental Studies (https://www.nies.go.jp/biodiversity/invasive/DB/etoc5_fishes.html, accessed July 2023). The database was based on the invasive state of fish in Japan.

Environmental data

Environmental data on lake morphology as used by Doi et al. (2023)33 were used in the present study. Lake morphology data (surface area [km2], maximum water depth [m], altitude [m], and perimeter [km]) and lake type (trophic status and water type [brackish or freshwater]) were obtained from Tanaka (1992)38. An open climatic dataset from CRUTEM4.6.0.0-2019-12, which is based on Google Earth (https://earth.google.com/web/) on a 0.5° latitude × 0.5° longitude grid, was used to obtain data on average monthly temperatures39,40. For the analysis, we utilized time-series data pertaining to the target lakes, specifically focusing on the monthly mean temperature groups averaged from 1990 to the most recently recorded data.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 4.3.041. To evaluate the alpha diversity of fish communities, we calculated the inverse Simpson’s diversity index based on the eDNA sequencing data. This index represents the ‘effective number of species’ and is particularly sensitive to the relative abundance of dominant species rather than species richness alone. We selected this specific metric because it is more robust against the presence of rare taxa, which in eDNA studies may represent transient individuals or potential sequencing artifacts. By prioritizing the contribution of more abundant species, the inverse Simpson index provides a reliable measure of the community structure that is likely to be functionally important in these lake ecosystems. We estimated the relationship between the inverse Simpson index values of native and invasive species using Spearman’s correlation test with the “cor.test” function of R.

To statistically evaluate the differences in fish community structure, we performed two complementary analyses. To account for varying sequencing depths and to stabilize the influence of highly abundant taxa, we applied the Hellinger transformation to the read count data prior to multivariate analyses. First, distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA), as discussed by Legendre and Anderson (1999)42, was used to assess how environmental gradients correlated with the variation in fish community composition. Analyses were conducted using three groups: one containing all observed fish species, one excluding invasive species (i.e., only native species), and one excluding native species (i.e., only invasive species). The environmental variables for the db-RDA included monthly mean temperature, lake morphology data (surface area [km2], maximum water depth [m], and altitude [m]), and lake type (trophic status and water type [brackish or fresh water]). Bray-Curtis distances were calculated using the transformed eDNA sequencing data (relative abundance) of fish communities to perform db-RDA. The environmental variables for the db-RDA were standardized to remove arbitrariness in the units of measurement. In addition, collinearity was tested to select independent explanatory variables for statistical analyses using the variance inflation factor (VIF). For all explanatory variables, the VIF values ranged from 1.27 to 44.48. Therefore, one variable (lake perimeter) was excluded, and the VIF values were subsequently below five for all variables. Variation partitioning43,44 was applied to disentangle the relative contributions of environmental and spatial processes to the lake fish community composition. The spatial structure of sampling sites is typically described using Moran’s eigenvector maps45, which produce a set of orthogonal spatial variables derived from the geographical coordinates of the study sites. These orthogonal spatial variables were used as explanatory variables to detect spatial signals. The same set of environmental variables for db-RDA was used for this analysis. db-RDA and variation partitioning analyses were performed using R package vegan ver. 2.6.446; the functions “dbrda” and “varpart” of the vegan package were used for these analyses, respectively. PERMANOVA was performed using the continuous values of the Simpson index (1-D) of invasive species to examine whether the average native species composition (group centroids) shifted along the diversity levels of invasive species. We performed PERMANOVA using the adonis2 function in the vegan package of R. Second, the ‘betadisper’ function was used PERMDISP to compare multivariate dispersion (community heterogeneity) between groups of lakes categorized by the quartiles of the invasive species diversity index (e.g., comparing the top 25% ‘High’ group and bottom 25% ‘Low’ group). While PERMANOVA identifies directional shifts in composition, PERMDISP measures the average dissimilarity of individual lakes from their group centroid. By combining these methods, we could distinguish whether the impact of invasive species was driven by a consistent shift in species composition or by an increase in the variability of community structures. In this study, to represent the intensity and complexity of invasion as a stressor, we used the Simpson index. This metric was selected because it effectively captures both the variety and the dominance of invasive species, which are expected to collectively drive the reorganization and homogenization of native communities. The goodness of fit for the NMDS ordination was evaluated using the stress value; a value of less than 0.2 is generally considered to provide a reliable representation of the community data in a reduced-dimensional space47.
