Interrelationships of stress, burnout, anxiety, depression, quality of life and suicidality among Chinese residents under Standardized Residency Training: a network analysis
Authors: Zejun Li, Min Wu, Xiaoyu Zhang, Kewen Yan, Xin Wang, Huixue Xu, Peizhen Li, Yueheng Liu, Qijian Deng, Xueyi Li, Qianjin Wang, Manyun Li, Yunfei Wang, Yuzhu Hao, Li He, Yi-Yuan Tang, Tieqiao Liu, Pu Peng, Qiuxia Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChinese resident physicians confront challenges such as staff shortages and heavy workloads, leading to a heightened prevalence of mental distress. This study aims to investigate the symptom network of stress, burnout, anxiety, depression (SBAD) and adverse personal outcomes in this cohort.MethodsFrom October 2020 to April 2022, 994 physicians were recruited across China through snowball sampling. Stress, burnout, anxiety and depression were assessed using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale, a two-item burnout questionnaire, Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, respectively. Data on adverse personal outcomes (low quality of life [QOL] and suicidal ideation) were collected. We constructed and visualized two networks, calculating expected influence (EI) and bridge EI indices to identify central and bridge symptoms.ResultsIn the SBAD network, perceived helplessness was the most central and critical bridge symptom connecting stress and mental distress, with emotional exhaustion identified as the secondary bridge symptom. Perceived helplessness, perceived self-efficacy and emotional exhaustion exhibited the highest negative correlations with QOL. Worthless (PHQ6) and motor (PHQ8) symptoms were strongly correlated with suicidal ideation. The estimated SBAD network showed excellent stability and accuracy.ConclusionsOur study emphasizes that perceived helplessness may be a high-priority target for preventing and intervening in mental distress and improving QOL among residents. Burnout transcends workplace problems and is widely connected to depression, anxiety and QOL. Implementing early detection and intervention measures at three levels—the individual physician, health system and professional colleges, and external regulators—is crucial for preventing and alleviating stress and mental distress among residents.Keywords: Stress, burnout, mental distress, quality of life, resident physicians, network analysis

Introduction

The mental health of physicians is a significant public health concern globally [1–5]. Approximately one-third of resident physicians experience symptoms of depression and anxiety [5, 6]. A meta-analysis revealed that the global prevalence of burnout among medical and surgical residents is as high as 51% [7]. In China, these issues are exacerbated by the low physician-to-population ratio and strained doctor–patient relationships. Since the launch of the nationwide Standardized Residency Training (SRT) program in 2014, resident physicians have shouldered a major burden of daily medical duties [2]. In addition to challenges common to all physicians, residents may face unique stressors during their SRT phase, such as excessive working hours, academic and examination stress, financial pressures and job insecurity [1, 8]. These factors make residents particularly vulnerable to mental distress, including burnout, anxiety and depression [2–6]. Such distress could lead to adverse personal outcomes, including diminished quality of life [QOL] and suicidal ideation, and even jeopardize patient care [9–11]. Studies have shown that depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation are more prevalent among physicians in training and residency than among their senior counterparts [1, 12]. The well-being of residents is a benchmark for the sustainability of healthcare systems [4, 13]. Therefore, it is crucial to better understand how stress, burnout, anxiety and depression interrelate and affect the personal outcomes among residents.

It is worth noting that most previous studies on the associations among stress, mental distress and personal outcomes have utilized syndrome-level analyses (based on total scale score) [1, 10, 14–23]. Network analysis (NA), a symptom-based approach, provides novel insights into these complex relationships [24]. NA enables us to clarify how specific symptoms (based on individual item scores or dimension scores) of stress, burnout, anxiety and depression symptoms correlate, interact and reinforce each other and how they impact personal outcomes in resident physicians [25]. This method quantifies the importance of each symptom and identifies a central symptom that is most closely linked to and has the most significant impact on other symptoms in the network [25–27]. By mapping these symptoms into the network, NA pinpoints the ‘bridge symptoms’ connecting these four constructs and playing critical roles in their comorbidity [28, 29]. Moreover, NA highlights which symptoms exhibit the strongest connections to specific adverse outcomes through visualization. These central and bridge symptoms, as well as those most strongly connected to adverse outcomes, could serve as pivotal targets for prevention and intervention [30].

To our knowledge, only two studies have documented the intricate network of burnout, anxiety and depression among non-Chinese physicians [31, 32]. However, these studies did not investigate the relationships between mental distress and stress among resident physicians despite the substantial professional pressures they face. Hence, to address these gaps, we employed NA to quantify the relationships between stress and mental distress and explore their associations with adverse outcomes. The purposes of our study were (1) to explore the network structure of perceived stress-burnout-anxiety-depression (SBAD), (2) to determine the central and bridge symptoms across the network and (3) to identify which symptoms are uniquely associated with adverse personal outcomes, including low QOL and suicidal ideation.

Methods

Study procedure and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional online survey from October 2020 to April 2022 using the Wenjuanxing platform (www.wjx.cn), a well-known Chinese online questionnaire website. During this period, China had entered the controlled stage of the COVID-19 pandemic. Participants meeting the following criteria were recruited through snowball sampling: (1) Chinese, age 20–50 years old; (2) being able to understand Chinese and willing to participate in the survey and (3) resident physicians undergoing SRT in Chinese hospitals, regardless of their specialty. All participants provided written informed consent through an online survey platform (www.wjx.cn). Participation was anonymous and voluntary, and they could withdraw from the study at any time. However, participants were required to complete all questions before submitting the questionnaire. The ethics committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University approved the study (JY20200326).

Measurements

Demographic information

We collected basic information through a self-designed questionnaire, including age, gender, marital status, education level, history of mental illness, training mode and training stage. Additionally, we added a trap question, ‘When is the Chinese national day?’ to identify and exclude invalid responses. Participants who answered incorrectly were excluded from the final analysis.

Perceived stress

We used the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) to evaluate perceived stress. Previous studies have indicated that the PSS-10 comprises two factors: perceived helplessness (PHS) and perceived self-efficacy (PSES) [33]. The total PSS-10 score ranges from 0 to 40, with higher scores suggesting higher levels of perceived stress. A cutoff point of 19 was used to identify high perceived stress [34].

Burnout

To measure burnout levels, we utilized the two-item burnout questionnaire, which includes emotional exhaustion (EX) (‘I feel burned out from my job’) and depersonalization (DP) (‘I have become more callous towards persons since I started this work’). Participants rated the frequency of these symptoms on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (every day). Individuals who responded ‘once a week’ or more frequently to either item were classified as experiencing burnout. These two single items have been validated and widely used in examining burnout among medical staff [35–37].

Depression and anxiety

The 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) were used to evaluate depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively. Both scales have been validated among Chinese healthcare workers [38–40]. Participants rated their depressive and anxiety symptoms on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), with higher scores indicating more severe mental distress. A cutoff point of 10 was used to screen clinically relevant depressive and anxiety symptoms [34].

Quality of life

We assessed the quality of life (QOL) using a standardized linear analogue scale, with scores ranging from 0 (as bad as it can be) to 10 (as good as it can be) [41]. In alignment with previous studies [34, 41], we defined low QOL as scores ≥ 1/2 standard deviation (SD) below the gender-matched mean score.

Suicidal ideation

To identify current suicidal ideation (SI), we utilized the ninth item of PHQ-9 (‘Thoughts that you would be better off dead, or thoughts of hurting yourself in some way?’). This single item has been extensively used to assess suicidal ideation in previous epidemiological studies [42]. Participants who scored one or above on this item were classified into the SI group [43].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

We used the Shapiro–Wilks test to check the normality of the continuous data and observed a non-normal distribution. Therefore, continuous data were presented as the median and inter-quartile range (IQR). Categorical data were expressed as percentages and frequencies. We performed all the statistical analyses using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing version 4.3.2, Vienna, Austria). We constructed two networks. In network 1, we analysed the interactions among perceived stress, burnout, anxiety and depression symptoms and further assessed the association of these symptoms with suicidal ideation. In network 2, we explored the associations between these mental distress symptoms and QOL.

Network estimation and visualization

We modelled and visualized the SBAD network of residents in SRT using the ‘estimateNetwork’ function in the R package ‘bootnet’ [44]. The Gaussian graphical model was selected with the default setting of EBICglasso and the ‘spearman’ method [45]. The network consisted of ‘nodes’ (i.e. the items of PSS-10, two-item burnout questionnaire, PHQ-9 and GAD-7) and ‘edges’ (i.e. the partial correlations between two nodes after controlling for other variables in the network). Thicker edges indicate stronger associations, with red edges representing negative associations and blue edges denoting positive associations [46].

Central and bridge symptoms

We used the R package ‘qgraph’ to estimate each node’s expected influence (EI) within the network, thus providing a quantitative measure of the importance of individual nodes in the overall network structure [47, 48]. Nodes with higher EI were identified as central symptoms, which may have a crucial role in initiating and maintaining the SBAD network. We also utilized the R package ‘mgm’ to assess the node predictability in the network [49]. Predictability, defined as the variance in a node that is explained by all other nodes in the network, serves as a crucial indicator of network controllability. Nodes exhibiting high predictability could potentially be influenced or controlled through modifications in their neighbouring nodes [50]. In addition, we computed the bridge expected influence (BEI) index to identify symptoms acting as bridges across the SBAD network [28]. Nodes with higher BEI exhibited stronger associations with symptoms in other distress domains. The selection of bridge symptoms was determined using the 80th percentile BEI threshold [28, 51].

Network stability and accuracy

We applied the bootstrapping method to assess the accuracy and robustness of the network estimate utilizing the ‘bootnet’ package [52]. The stability of both node and BEI was evaluated using the case-dropping procedure. The correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) quantifies the stability of the network, with a CS-C value greater than 0.5 indicating a high level of network stability. To verify the accuracy of estimated edge weights in the network, we employed a nonparametric bootstrapping procedure with 1000 bootstrap samples. Additionally, we performed bootstrapping on the paired differences of edge weights and the paired differences of centrality of different nodes to examine whether edges or nodes differed significantly from one another.

The association of SBAD with adverse outcomes

Finally, we incorporated the variable ‘quality of life’ into network 1 (SBAD network), generating new network 2. Subsequently, the ‘flow’ function was utilized to visualize symptoms directly associated with low QOL as well as suicidal ideation [53].

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 994 resident physicians in SRT completed the online questionnaire, and after the screening, 927 (96.9%) of them were available for further analysis (Table 1). The median age of participants was 25 years old (IQR, 24–27), and most were female (n = 672, 72%), unmarried (n = 786, 85%) and held a bachelor’s degree (n = 734, 79%). Nearly half of the residents (n = 423, 46%) were concurrently enrolled in graduate school alongside their SRT. There were 51 (5.5%) individuals with a history of mental illness. Participants in the three grades of SRT were evenly distributed, with approximately one-third in each grade: 306 (33%) in the 1st year, 286 (31%) in the 2nd year and 335 (36%) in the 3rd year. The prevalence rates of high perceived stress, depression, anxiety, burnout, low QOL and suicidal ideation were 43% (n = 403), 36% (n = 336), 20% (n = 184), 43% (n = 401), 36% (n = 338) and 27% (n = 247), respectively.

Other central symptoms included Uncontrollable worry (GAD2), Trouble relaxing (GAD4) and Nervous (GAD1). Figure 2(B) presents the bridge expected influence of each node. Stability and accuracy of the network

The network exhibited excellent stability and accuracy. The CS-C of EI and BEI was 0.75 (Figure 3(A)), suggesting that the network remained highly correlated with the original data after dropping out 75% of the data. Figure 3(B) displays the results of the nonparametric bootstrapping procedure. The grey area represents the bootstrap 95% confidence interval, which was narrow and indicated high accuracy of the estimated edges.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study provided the first network analysis of stress, burnout, anxiety and depression symptoms and their associations with adverse personal outcomes (low QOL and suicidal ideation) among physicians in SRT. The major findings included the following: (1) the SBAD network showed a dense structure, indicating that stress, burnout, anxiety and depression were highly correlated at the symptom level. (2) Perceived helplessness was the most central and bridge symptom, while Emotional exhaustion was the second bridge symptom. (3) SBAD symptoms displayed varied levels of association with adverse outcomes. PHS, PSES and EX had the strongest negative correlations with QOL, while Worthless (PHQ6) and Motor (PHQ8) were most strongly associated with suicidal ideation. These results provide possible targets for preventing mental distress, reducing suicide and improving the quality of life among residents.

Our study first identified Perceived helplessness as both the most central and bridge symptom in the SBAD network of physicians in SRT. Our results emphasize the importance of PHS in initiating and maintaining the stress and mental distress network. Consistent with our findings, previous studies using non-network analysis have shown that stress was strongly correlated with burnout, anxiety and depressive symptoms [15–17]. Numerous studies have revealed that stress is a major contributor to both physical and mental illnesses [54, 55]. According to the transactional concept of stress, they become stressors when an individual perceives environmental influences as threats and as something that exceeds their capacity to deal with [56]. Exposure to persistent stressors could lead to prolonged, excessive or insufficient activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA), which may subsequently result in the development of psychiatric disorders, including depression and anxiety [55, 57, 58]. When subdividing the dimensions of stress, helplessness is perceived as an essential component of psychopathological symptoms [59]. The PHS dimension assesses how individuals perceive situations in their lives as unpredictable, uncontrollable and overloaded, reflecting the lack of control and negative emotions [60]. It is a belief of powerlessness that someone’s actions do not influence the action’s outcomes, known as learned helplessness [61]. Learned helplessness is a maladaptive response to uncontrollable stress characterized by impaired motor escape responses, reduced motivation and learning deficits [59, 62], which could lead to emotional distress and adverse outcomes in the long term. Moreover, research has shown that helplessness was a strong predictor of emotional exhaustion [63]. This emotional exhaustion could lead to emotional and cognitive distancing, thus negatively impacting physicians’ mental health [64].

QOL includes various aspects of a person’s physical condition, psychological state, level of independence, social relationships, environment and spirituality [65]. The results of this study revealed that PHS and PSES were significantly and negatively correlated with quality of life. This finding is consistent with previous studies that showed a significant association between stress and poor QOL [21–23]. Prioritizing work over personal life or lacking a work–life balance could lead to high levels of stress, further increasing the risk of mental distress and decreasing overall well-being [66–68]. Therefore, how to identify and reduce helplessness among residents in SRT is an urgent issue for researchers and healthcare administrators. Improving self-efficacy and individual interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy and mindfulness-based techniques have proven effective and essential in alleviating PHS and stress [69–73]. At the same time, healthcare facility administrators must implement organizational strategies to manage workload or schedules to alleviate stress, prevent mental distress and mitigate adverse outcomes [71, 72, 74].

Our research also underscored the significance of burnout in the network of mental distress and QOL. This study revealed that burnout symptoms exhibited diverse and extensive connections with depressive and anxiety symptoms, with emotional exhaustion serving as the bridge symptom connecting the SBAD network. There has been an ongoing debate regarding the overlap between burnout and depression constructs [1, 18–20]. Specifically, recent studies have increasingly highlighted a substantial overlap between burnout and depression [75–78], suggesting that burnout might be considered as one dimension of depression rather than a distinct psychological phenomenon [79]. However, other studies argued for the distinctiveness of depression and burnout as ‘two different robust constructs’ [32, 80]. Therefore, further studies are urgently needed to provide a deeper understanding of the intricate relationship between burnout and depression [81]. In addition, we also found that EX significantly negatively impacted the quality of life. These findings emphasize that burnout goes beyond an ‘occupational phenomenon’ and is highly correlated with mental distress and poor QOL, with profound effects on individuals. Consequently, routine screenings for burnout become imperative, particularly highlighting the identification of symptoms of depression, anxiety and diminished quality of life among residency training physicians experiencing burnout. Furthermore, healthcare organizations should develop a well-established support system and implement both individual-focused and structural or organizational strategies to reduce burnout and promote the overall mental health of residents [1, 82, 83].

The network analysis reveals several noteworthy implications. Firstly, our study highlights the critical need to consistently and systematically screen stress, burnout, anxiety, depression and adverse outcomes in physicians during SRT. Secondly, our study emphasizes perceived helplessness as the most central and bridge symptom, standing out as the most influential predictor for low QOL. Thus, PHS may be a promising target for the prevention and intervention of stress and mental distress among residents in SRT. Finally, our findings illustrated that burnout extends beyond the general occupational phenomenon, correlating closely with mental distress and QOL. These findings offer essential guidance for healthcare organizations and residency training administrators to enhance the mental health and well-being of their residents in SRT.

Despite the valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the representativeness of our findings might be compromised by the online snowball sampling method, potentially introducing selection bias. Additionally, the relatively modest sample size might not capture the full diversity of China’s residency-training physician population. Nevertheless, the incidence of mental distress and negative consequences align closely with other large-scale studies among Chinese physicians, suggesting that these biases might be relatively minor [53, 84]. Second, the cross-sectional nature of this study inhibited us from drawing causal relationships. Third, our evaluation of burnout utilized only two items, overlooking the dimension of low personal accomplishment. Utilizing more comprehensive assessments for these key variables might help better understand the association between stress, burnout, anxiety, depression and negative consequences. Lastly, the measurement of burnout in this study did not consider the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic has also been recognized as a significant contributor to burnout, characterized by symptoms such as overwhelming exhaustion, reduced personal accomplishment, emotional drainage and decreased motivation [85]. Further longitudinal research with comprehensive assessments is essential to validate and build upon our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes the first network linking stress, burnout, depression, anxiety, quality of life and suicidal ideation among Chinese resident physicians. Our findings emphasize the important role of perceived helplessness in sustaining this network, driving comorbidity and impacting quality of life. Perceived helplessness should thus be a major prioritized target for prevention and intervention. Additionally, our study indicates that burnout transcends workplace problems and is deeply connected to depression, anxiety and quality of life. Implementing early detection and intervention measures at three levels—the individual physician, health system and professional colleges, and external regulators—is crucial for preventing and alleviating stress and mental distress among resident physicians.
