Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the oral cavity: a narrative review of clinicopathological features and management
Authors: Paola Marino, Desirèe Speranza, Enrica Toscano, Mariacarmela Santarpia, Andrea Ballini, Danila De Vito, Nicola Fazio, Massimiliano Berretta, Nicola Silvestris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the oral cavity are extremely rare, accounting for less than 1% of all head and neck malignancies. Their diagnosis is challenging due to nonspecific clinical features and significant histopathologic overlap with other intraoral tumors. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and management of head and neck NENs in general, with particular attention on the oral cavity side, encompassing both well- and poorly differentiated forms. Reported cases indicate the gingiva and buccal mucosa as the most frequent primary sites. Clinically, lesions usually present as painless nodules or ulcerated masses. Definitive diagnosis requires integration of morphologic and immunohistochemical findings, typically demonstrating positivity for synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56. In the absence of specific international guidelines for the oral cavity, given the limited availability of data in the literature, the most appropriate approach currently follows the guidelines for managing head and neck NENs, which consists of surgical excision, which remains the treatment of choice for localized disease and is often followed by adjuvant chemoradiotherapy. In advanced or metastatic stages, platinum-based chemotherapy may provide transient disease control but offers limited survival benefit. Novel therapeutic modalities, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, show preliminary promise but lack robust clinical validation. Given the rarity and heterogeneity of these tumors, multidisciplinary management and the establishment of multicenter registries are crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy, standardizing treatment strategies, and enhancing clinical outcomes.Keywords: Neuroendocrine neoplasm, Oral cavity, Target therapy, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the oral cavity are sporadic tumors of the head and neck region, accounting for less than 1% of all malignancies [1].

Available reports suggest that oral cavity NENs predominantly affect middle-aged to older adults, with a mean age at diagnosis of approximately 56 years and a slight male predominance, although current evidence remains limited to small case series and isolated reports [2].

According to the 2022 WHO Classification of Neuroendocrine Neoplasms, NENs are categorized into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), based on their morphology and proliferative activity, which are essential for an accurate diagnosis and have significant prognostic implications. In addition, the WHO recognizes mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs), defined as lesions containing both neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components [1].

NECs are subdivided into two main histological variants: small-cell NEC (SCNEC) and large-cell NEC (LCNEC). Both subtypes are characterized by marked cytological atypia, extensive necrosis, and exceptionally high proliferative indices, often with Ki-67 labeling values exceeding 50–90% [3]. SCNECs typically consist of small, round to oval cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm, often displaying nuclear molding and a high mitotic rate. LCNECs are composed of larger polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and a distinct trabecular, nested, or rosette-like architectural arrangement [4].

Although oral cavity NENs are currently classified and graded according to general neuroendocrine tumor criteria, there is increasing recognition that their biology may be shaped by the unique characteristics of the oral microenvironment [5]. The oral mucosa is composed of stratified squamous epithelium, is highly vascularized, and is densely innervated, with a resident lymphoid and microbial milieu that can influence tumor–host interactions [6]. These site-specific factors may impact tumor differentiation, proliferation, hormone secretion, and immune evasion, potentially distinguishing oral NENs from their laryngeal, pulmonary, or gastrointestinal counterparts [6].

This narrative review aims to provide an updated overview of these rare tumors by collecting and synthesizing the limited data currently available in the literature on NENs of the oral cavity, an area in which comprehensive studies attempting to organize existing evidence, propose standardized classifications, or define shared treatment guidelines are currently lacking. By highlighting their distinctive clinical, morphological, and biomolecular characteristics, this review seeks to offer preliminary insights that may help structure future research efforts and contribute to the development of more consistent diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Furthermore, it emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary management strategy that integrates pathological, surgical, oncological, and nuclear medicine expertise to ensure optimal patient care.

Risk factors

Tobacco and alcohol exposure account for approximately 75% of head and neck malignancies, with a well-documented synergistic effect on oral carcinogenesis [7]. Additional factors, including unhealthy dietary habits, poor oral hygiene, overweight, and obesity, contribute to oncogenesis by promoting chronic inflammation, increased cellular proliferation, and impaired apoptosis, thereby favoring the development of precancerous lesions [7]. While these conditions may also facilitate the emergence of NENs in the oral cavity, current evidence is insufficient to define specific risk factors directly associated with NEN development in this anatomical site.

Evidence from other anatomical sites suggests that tobacco may play a pathogenetic role in certain NENs, particularly pulmonary NEC, where smoking represents a major etiologic factor [8]. In contrast, the association between alcohol consumption and gastrointestinal NENs remains less clearly defined and appears weaker compared with its established role in conventional epithelial cancers [9].

In addition to environmental exposures, hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes, most notably multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), are established risk factors for NENs of the pancreas and gastrointestinal tract. To date, however, no consistent association has been demonstrated between these inherited conditions and primary NENs of the oral cavity [10].

In summary, although risk factors for NENs developing from other organs have been widely characterized, specific etiologic determinants for oral cavity NENs remain largely unknown.

Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) infection

Infection with HPV, particularly genotype 16 and 18, is likewise implicated in a significant subset of oropharyngeal carcinomas, even in the absence of other conventional risk factors [11].

Compared with squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), the etiopathogenetic role of HPV in oropharyngeal NENs remains less clearly defined. Although the oncogenic activity of HPV is classically mediated by the E6 and E7 proteins, which inactivate p53 and Rb, respectively, it is uncertain whether these mechanisms represent the main molecular drivers in NECs [12].

In a systematic review of 94 patients with oropharyngeal NECs, Froehlich et al. reported a high proportion of HPV-related marker positivity among evaluable cases (85.1% for p16 and 85.0% for HPV-DNA by in situ hybridization), suggesting a possible viral association in a subset of tumors [13]. However, Yuan et al. demonstrated that HPV-positive cervical NEC cells may rely on Myc-driven proliferation rather than on E6/E7 activity, supporting the existence of distinct molecular mechanisms compared with SCCs [14]. A small case series also reported improved survival in HPV-positive head and neck NECs [15], and more recent studies by Jo et al. [16] and Allison et al. [17] described the clinicopathologic features of HPV-positive NECs of the head and neck, most of which were SCNEC.

Overall, current evidence does not establish a definitive causal or prognostic role for HPV in head and neck NENs. Importantly, the available data predominantly concern tumors arising in the oropharynx, whereas specific evidence addressing primary tumors of the oral cavity is limited and currently insufficient to draw firm conclusions. Therefore, routine HPV testing is not recommended in standard diagnostic practice for NENs [18]. In selected cases, particularly metastatic NENs of unknown primary site, HPV positivity may support an oropharyngeal rather than oral cavity origin, consistent with the established epidemiology of HPV-related malignancies [18]. However, this interpretation should be applied cautiously, given the lack of robust data for oral cavity primaries, and findings should always be integrated within the appropriate clinical, morphologic, and immunohistochemical context.

Clinical and radiological features of oral NENs

NENs of the oral cavity often exhibit atypical and subtle clinical features, frequently remaining asymptomatic in the early stages or mimicking benign conditions, which can delay diagnosis. Clinically, they usually present as persistent, painless, slow-growing nodular lesions, occasionally ulcerated, with symptoms varying according to tumor location and extent, including dysphagia, dyspnea, hoarseness, local irritation or dull pain, bleeding, trismus, and odynophagia [2].

Owing to their nonspecific macroscopic appearance, these lesions may resemble other intraoral malignancies, such as Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC), or benign entities including traumatic fibroma, focal fibrous hyperplasia, and pyogenic granuloma, emphasizing the importance of an accurate differential diagnosis [19, 20].

From a diagnostic standpoint, a structured algorithm is advisable. In patients with suspected oral NEN, initial staging should include contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the total body, with the maxillofacial study to assess bone infiltration, and potential locoregional extension [21]. Clinical evaluation should be systematically complemented by rhinoscopy and/or otoscopy to exclude synchronous lesions or contiguous spread within the upper aerodigestive tract [19].

Positron emission tomography using ^18F-FDG or somatostatin receptor–based tracers (e.g., ^68Ga-DOTATATE) should be performed subsequently to improve detection of metabolically active lesions and occult lymph node metastases [22].

Histopathological features of oral NENs

NENs are an uncommon category of tumors arising in the oral cavity and frequently represent a diagnostic pitfall when evaluated by routine light microscopy alone. Evidence from the literature suggests that, before the widespread adoption of molecular and advanced immunopathologic techniques in biopsy assessment, a substantial proportion of previously reported cases were likely misclassified as poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas or SCCs [23].

Histological distinction between SCNEC and LCNEC primarily relies on the size of the tumor cells relative to lymphocytes. In SCNEC, neoplastic cells are smaller than three lymphocytes in diameter; in LCNEC, they exceed this size threshold [2].

A population-based analysis of SCNEC of the head and neck using the SEER database from 1973 to 2013 reported 609 cases, representing only ~ 0.3% of all head and neck malignancies, including tumors in the oral cavity and oropharynx, and confirmed a markedly poorer prognosis compared to the most common SCC [24].

SCNEC typically shows a submucosal proliferation of small to medium-sized cells with scant cytoplasm, hyperchromatic oval to spindle-shaped nuclei, absent nucleoli, and frequent nuclear molding. The growth pattern typically features solid nests, sheets, or ribbons, often lacking a prominent fibrovascular stroma and frequently associated with surface ulceration [2].

LCNEC is characterized by its distinctive neuroendocrine architecture, including organoid nesting, trabecular arrangements, rosettes, and peripheral palisading, as well as medium to large tumor cells with moderate to abundant cytoplasm. Additional features include marked pleomorphism, vesicular chromatin, and small to prominent nucleoli, aiding in its differentiation from other poorly differentiated neoplasms [2].

Routine histomorphological evaluation alone is insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis of NENs of the oral cavity. Immunohistochemistry is therefore essential for accurate diagnosis and requires a comprehensive marker panel to confirm both epithelial and neuroendocrine differentiation while excluding other entities [19]. Epithelial differentiation is typically demonstrated using cytokeratin markers such as AE1/AE3, CAM5.2, EMA, MFN116, CK19, CK7, and CK20, supporting the carcinomatous nature of the neoplastic cells. Neuroendocrine differentiation is assessed using markers such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, neuron-specific enolase, CD56, INSM1, and TTF-1, which are more consistently expressed in SCNEC but may also be variably expressed in large-cell variants [1]. The use of this integrated immunohistochemical panel enables accurate histopathological characterization and reliable distinction between NEC subtypes [2].

In contrast, regarding specific NETs of the oral cavity, very few data are available in the literature. NETs are well-differentiated NENs and are potentially malignant; they may be functional, causing hormone-related syndromes, or nonfunctional and asymptomatic, which can delay diagnosis, particularly for small lesions. Histologically, NETs typically show an organoid growth pattern (nests/trabeculae/rosettes) with granular cytoplasm and variable nuclear atypia, composed of relatively monomorphic cells with finely granular “salt-and-pepper” chromatin, usually without the marked pleomorphism and extensive necrosis that characterize poorly differentiated NECs [25]. Immunohistochemically, these tumors typically demonstrate neuroendocrine differentiation with synaptophysin and chromogranin A positivity, and may also be positive for INSM1while the broader panels reported across cases may include cytokeratins and ancillary markers (e.g., CAM5.2, S100, vimentin) and support grading through proliferative assessment (e.g., Ki-67 around 10%) and, in selected reports, somatostatin receptor 2 A expression; vascular and myogenic markers (e.g., CD34, factor VIII, αSMA) are generally negative, helping refine the differential diagnosis [25].

Although extremely rare, oral MCC should be included in the differential diagnosis of primary NENs of the oral mucosa due to its morphological and immunophenotypic overlap with SCNEC. CK20 positivity with a characteristic paranuclear dot-like pattern and TTF-1 negativity are key features for distinguishing oral MCC from pulmonary or metastatic NECs, underscoring the importance of an extended immunohistochemical panel for accurate diagnosis [23].

MiNENs of the oral cavity

MiNENs are rare tumors composed of both neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine elements within the same tissue. Although recognized by the WHO classification across various organs, MiNENs in the head and neck region are exceptionally rare. This classification was introduced to encompass the wide spectrum of possible combinations and morphological variations, largely determined by the tumor’s site of origin [26].

Previously, few cases of these tumors of the oral cavity were described in the literature that were difficult to identify, especially before the introduction of the 2017 WHO classification, which coined the term “MiNENs.” Previously, terms such as coexistence, combined, collision, and composite were used, which did not allow for an accurate review of the literature [27].

These tumors consistently demonstrate a dual cellular composition, typically combining a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine component, often SCNEC or LCNEC, more rarely NET, with a non-neuroendocrine component, most frequently SCC [27, 28, 29]. The coexistence of distinct neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components represents the defining feature of oral cavity MiNENs and constitutes their main diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Accurate histopathological recognition is essential, as the neuroendocrine component frequently drives tumor aggressiveness and may influence the choice between surgery alone or combined chemoradiotherapy approaches.

Overall, the available literature highlights the extreme rarity and marked clinicopathological heterogeneity of these tumors, as well as the historical challenges in their classification. The consistent application of the WHO MiNEN framework and standardized reporting are crucial for improving diagnostic reproducibility, enabling meaningful comparisons across published cases, and supporting more evidence-based therapeutic decision-making in this uncommon entity.

Treatments

Locoregional NENs

In head and neck NENs, distinguishing between localized and locally advanced disease is essential for prognostic evaluation and treatment planning. In the absence of a specific staging system, oral cavity NENs are staged according to the TNM classification [30], which is used for other solid head and neck tumors. Localized disease is limited to the primary site without regional lymph node or distant metastatic involvement. In contrast, locally advanced disease is characterized by invasion of adjacent structures and/or regional lymph nodes in the absence of distant metastases, often requiring multimodal treatment [31].

Epidemiological data and available case series consistently indicate that head and neck NENs frequently present at an advanced locoregional stage, yet may still benefit from aggressive multimodal strategies in carefully selected patients [20]. In a cohort of 27 patients, 81% presented with locally advanced disease at diagnosis, confirming the tendency toward extensive regional involvement [32]. Despite this unfavorable presentation, a 3-year overall survival (OS) rate of 39% was observed in the overall population, increasing to 53% among the 17 patients with locally advanced disease treated with curative-intent multimodal approaches. Histologically, SCNEC represented the predominant subtype (58%) [32], supporting the view that high-grade morphology is common in this setting. Importantly, complex surgical management, including vascular resection and reconstruction in cases with carotid invasion, has been reported [33], underscoring how accurate assessment of tumor extent is critical for treatment planning and for determining resectability.

Across published reports, a recurring theme is the potential benefit of integrated locoregional treatment combining surgery, platinum-based chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. For example, aggressive surgical resection followed by adjuvant cisplatin–etoposide chemotherapy achieved durable disease control in a patient with localized SCNEC of the lower gingiva [34], while a multimodal strategy including surgery and chemoradiotherapy provided short-term disease control in primary SCNEC of the buccal mucosa [35]. Rather than representing isolated successes, these experiences collectively support the rationale for combining radical surgery with systemic therapy in fit patients with resectable disease, particularly in high-grade histologies.

This integrative perspective is reinforced by the systematic review by Froehlich et al., which analyzed 94 patients with available treatment data [13]. The most frequently adopted strategies were chemoradiotherapy (30.0%) and surgery, followed by platinum-based chemotherapy (16.7%), whereas locoregional treatment alone was used in 26.7% of cases. Among patients with follow-up data, sustained locoregional control was achieved in 87.5% (28/32), with only 12.5% experiencing local or regional recurrence [13]. These findings suggest that current locoregional approaches are generally effective in controlling disease at the primary site. However, this benefit does not necessarily translate into improved OS, as distant metastases remained common; notably, 40.6% of patients who achieved locoregional control subsequently developed distant spread [13]. Therefore, while aggressive local treatment is justified to prevent uncontrolled regional progression, systemic disease remains the principal determinant of long-term outcome.

Finally, even tumors with apparently indolent biological features may require careful multidisciplinary evaluation. A recent report of a low-grade NET of the tongue base with calcific features, initially suspected to be benign, demonstrated the need for complete excision and margin reassessment despite a low Ki-67 index and limited marker expression [21]. Although not classifiable as high-grade NET, this case highlights that biological behavior cannot be inferred solely from initial clinical impression, further supporting the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making.

Given the anatomical complexity of the oral cavity and the inherent risk of local recurrence, complete surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment [33]. Adjuvant therapies are tailored according to tumor grade, margin status, and lymphovascular invasion, with postoperative chemoradiotherapy recommended for high-grade or biologically aggressive NECs [31], while definitive chemoradiotherapy is reserved for unresectable cases [36]. The rarity of these tumors and the lack of standardized guidelines highlight the need for collaborative registries and prospective studies to establish evidence-based treatment strategies.

Metastatic NENs

Because of the rarity of oral cavity NENs, reports in the literature concerning cases of metastatic disease are exceedingly scarce. Although most reported oral NECs are considered primary tumors, clinicians should be aware that the oral cavity may also represent a metastatic site. As highlighted by Toshinori et al. [37], a lesion involving the mandibular gingiva was ultimately identified as a metastasis from a primary tumor of the duodenal papilla, with imaging demonstrating multiple lymph node metastases and confirming the secondary nature of the oral involvement.

More broadly, metastases to the oral and maxillofacial region are uncommon, accounting for approximately 1–1.5% of all oral malignancies, and most frequently involve the mandible (44.9–51.3%) and gingival soft tissues (16.8–23.3%) [37]. These data underscore the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic work-up, including systemic imaging, when evaluating NENs of the oral cavity, as an accurate distinction between primary and metastatic disease has major implications for staging, prognosis, and therapeutic strategy.

The management of metastatic primitive NEC of the oral cavity is even more challenging than locoregional disease, primarily due to the high-grade, poorly differentiated nature of many of these tumors and their marked tendency for early hematogenous and lymphatic spread [21]. SCNECs exhibit aggressive behavior that closely resembles their pulmonary counterparts, characterized by rapid progression and widespread dissemination [38]. Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of management in these cases, typically following protocols established for small-cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) [36], . Early treatment regimens included combinations such as cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and vincristine [39]. However, current clinical practice favors etoposide with cisplatin or carboplatin, which is now considered the preferred systemic treatment for NECs of the head and neck, including the oral cavity [40]. These treatment recommendations are extrapolated from metastatic NENs of the head and neck and SCLC, as no guideline-based, site-specific recommendations exist for metastatic NENs of the oral cavity due to their extreme rarity.

In cases where metastases are already present at diagnosis or develop during follow-up, chemotherapy often serves a palliative rather than curative strategy. Renner [41] reported that median survival for untreated patients with metastatic NEC of the head and neck is often less than one year, whereas systemic chemotherapy can extend survival to 18–24 months, although this time can vary depending on disease burden and patient condition.

These outcomes align with broader literature reporting poor prognosis even when aggressive multimodal strategies are employed [42, 43, 44]. Prognosis also appears to depend on the tumor site. Indeed, NECs originating in the salivary glands generally demonstrate better outcomes than those from other head and neck regions [45].

Emerging new therapies

While chemotherapy remains the primary systemic treatment, the role of immunotherapy and targeted therapies in oral NENs is not yet defined. Nevertheless, the positive results of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in pulmonary SCNECs, both in localized [46] and advanced disease [47], suggest that inclusion of oral NECs in broader head and neck trials may be essential to explore these novel options.

For instance, durable responses have been observed with the addition of atezolizumab to platinum–etoposide in sinonasal SCNEC, including a reported complete response with overall survival exceeding 27 months despite manageable immune-related toxicity [48]. Similarly, retrospective molecular analyses have highlighted that a subset of NECs may harbor actionable immunologic features [49]. In one cohort including both well-differentiated NETs (67%) and poorly differentiated NECs (33%), immunotherapy demonstrated meaningful activity in selected cases; notably, a patient with head and neck NEC (Ki-67 50%) who had progressed after multiple chemotherapy lines achieved a marked response to durvalumab following identification of high tumor mutational burden (TMB) on repeat biopsy [49].

Collectively, these findings suggest that immunotherapy may be most effective in biologically aggressive NECs, particularly when supported by predictive biomarkers such as elevated TMB. In contrast, comparable results have not yet been consistently observed in well-differentiated NETs, where available data remain sparse, and responses to ICIs appear limited [50].

In this context, combined or sequential strategies integrating conventional chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy warrant further investigation. Chemotherapy-induced increases in TMB or radiotherapy-mediated enhancement of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes may act synergistically to potentiate ICI efficacy [49]. Overall, while current evidence does not yet support routine use of ICIs across all oral and head and neck NENs, a biomarker-driven, histology-specific approach represents a promising direction for future research.

A further area of clinical and research interest involves the potential application of peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), which has demonstrated notable efficacy and durable disease control in well-differentiated NENs from other anatomical sites [51]. PRRT represents a potentially promising systemic option for oral cavity NENs; however, its application in this context remains highly uncertain. PRRT efficacy relies on high somatostatin receptor expression, a hallmark of well-differentiated NETs [52], which is seldom documented in the limited reports of oral NENs. Consequently, while PRRT may offer a future therapeutic avenue, robust preclinical and clinical evidence is still lacking to establish its feasibility, safety, and effectiveness in this rare and poorly characterized patient population.

The identification of predictive biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, TMB, and microsatellite instability, together with comprehensive genomic profiling, is essential for precise patient selection and optimization of emerging therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, there is a critical need to expand prospective studies specifically focused on NENs across different anatomical sites, including the head and neck region, to generate robust evidence guiding tailored treatment approaches.

Simultaneous care

Currently, no disease-specific recommendations are available for supportive care in NENs of the oral cavity. Consequently, the supportive care measures are guided by general head and neck or oral cavity cancer recommendations, based on the treatments applied.

In patients with head and neck cancers, locoregional treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, and concurrent chemoradiotherapy, are associated with a high burden of acute and chronic toxicities that negatively affect quality of life and may compromise treatment adherence [11]. Combined modalities, particularly radiotherapy administered with cisplatin, are linked to grade ≥ 3 toxicities in more than 80% of cases [54], underscoring the clinical relevance of supportive care strategies [53].

The spectrum of adverse effects is broad and interrelated. Acute toxicities such as mucositis, xerostomia, dysphagia, taste alterations, and radiation dermatitis frequently evolve into chronic sequelae, including soft-tissue fibrosis, osteoradionecrosis, and persistent salivary dysfunction [54]. These complications impair oral intake, speech, and swallowing, and are closely associated with both nociceptive and neuropathic pain syndromes [54]. Consequently, pain management and nutritional support should not be viewed as separate issues but as interconnected components of comprehensive care.

Effective pain control requires a structured, multimodal, and interdisciplinary approach. Systematic pain assessment using validated scales should guide prompt initiation of analgesic therapy according to the WHO analgesic ladder [55], progressing from non-opioids to weak and strong opioids based on pain intensity. Given the frequency of neuropathic pain, often related to cisplatin-based chemotherapy, neuromodulatory agents such as anticonvulsants and antidepressants are frequently necessary [56]. More recently, multimodal regimens combining acetaminophen, naproxen, pregabalin, and opioids have demonstrated good efficacy and tolerability in the management of radiotherapy-induced mucositis pain, to reduce opioid requirements and related adverse effects [57]. These strategies highlight the importance of tailoring analgesia to the underlying pathophysiology of treatment-related toxicity.

Supportive care must also systematically address nutritional impairment, which is both a consequence of treatment toxicity and a determinant of clinical outcomes. Tumors involving anatomical structures essential for chewing and swallowing, together with therapy-related side effects such as dysphagia, odynophagia, dysgeusia, xerostomia, mucositis, nausea, and vomiting, markedly reduce oral intake [58].

Malnutrition is common and is associated with increased morbidity, treatment interruptions, poorer oncologic outcomes, and reduced quality of life; notably, even in overweight or obese patients, unintentional weight loss during therapy correlates with worse prognosis and higher mortality [59]. Early nutritional risk assessment individualized dietary counseling, and timely initiation of enteral nutrition when oral intake is inadequate are therefore crucial to preserve body weight and lean mass, improve treatment tolerance, and reduce complications [58].

The integration of optimized analgesic strategies, non-pharmacological interventions (including physiotherapy and psychological support), and proactive nutritional management within a multidisciplinary framework is essential to enhance adherence, maintain quality of life, and ultimately improve overall outcomes in this patient population [11].

Conclusions

NENs of the oral cavity are rare and heterogeneous tumors that remain poorly characterized because the available evidence is limited to isolated case reports and small case series. Although their clinical presentation is often nonspecific, accurate diagnosis requires an integrated approach based on morphology, immunohistochemistry, and appropriate imaging, particularly to distinguish primary oral tumors from metastatic disease and other high-grade malignancies. Current evidence suggests that poorly differentiated NECs represent the predominant and clinically most aggressive forms.

At present, no specific guidelines or formal consensus recommendations are available for oral cavity NENs. As a result, clinical management is largely extrapolated from broader head and neck NEN recommendations and from experience with other high-grade NECs. In this setting, surgery remains the main option for localized disease, whereas platinum-based systemic therapy is generally used in advanced stages. Given the rarity of these tumors and the absence of site-specific evidence, multidisciplinary evaluation and more systematic reporting of clinicopathological features, treatment strategies, and outcomes are essential to support more consistent and tailored management in the future.
