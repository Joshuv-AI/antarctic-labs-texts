Automated Quantification of Shedding Hairs from Smartphone Photographs Using an AI Tool for Alopecia Monitoring
Authors: Shiwen Zhang, Yujing Zhang, Xiangqian Li, Pingxin Han, Xinfeng Li, Cheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObjective shed-hair counts can support clinical assessment, longitudinal monitoring, and evaluation of treatment response. Manual counting is time-consuming, whereas visual estimates vary between observers. Images were assigned by participant into training/internal validation (24 volunteers, 4509 images) and independent testing (6 volunteers, 1121 images). A weakly supervised density-regression model was trained with image-level counts. A blinded reader study compared AI with visual estimates from 15 readers across 56 paired image sets. Median total AI workflow time was 24.5 seconds versus 123.0 seconds for manual counting. Introduction

Hair shedding is a common concern in dermatology and a clinically relevant feature of androgenetic alopecia (AGA) and telogen effluvium (TE).1–3 Objective counts of shedding hairs can support differential assessment, longitudinal monitoring, treatment counselling, and evaluation of treatment response.4,5 Reliable measurement also matters because perceived hair loss is closely associated with anxiety, altered self-image, impaired coping, and reduced quality of life.6–11

Several methods are currently available for assessing hair shedding. Quantitative methods, including the refined wash test (RWT), can quantify daily shedding and help distinguish AGA from TE.3,4 However, they require patients or dermatologists to separate and count every individual hair. This labor burden limits adherence and routine use, especially when repeated counts are needed to monitor disease course, treatment response, or early treatment-related shedding. Visual shedding scales are faster and easier to use, but they provide only subjective, semi-quantitative estimates.12,13 Variation between observers can complicate longitudinal comparison, communication with patients, and interpretation of apparent changes in shedding.

Photographs provide a simple record of shedding hairs that can be reviewed or analyzed after collection in outpatient assessment, wash tests, longitudinal follow-up, or clinical studies. Smartphone images are already used in teledermatology, where image quality and clinical usefulness have been evaluated.14–16 However, although artificial intelligence performs well in some dermatological tasks, such as dermoscopy image classification,17,18 clinical use depends on generalizability and on whether the output addresses a defined need.19–21 Because shedding hairs are fine, curved, overlapping, and often tangled, automated counting still faces unique and unresolved challenges.

We therefore developed a weakly supervised density-regression model to estimate shedding hairs from smartphone photographs using image-level counts rather than individual-hair annotations. We tested the model on independent test set and compared AI with visual estimates from patients, healthy participants, and dermatologists. We also compared usable RWT data between a historical manual cohort and a subsequent AI-assisted photograph-upload cohort. Our aim was to assess a photograph-based method that could support objective hair counting in clinical care and research.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This study evaluated model performance and RWT workflow feasibility at the Department of Dermatology, Peking University People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2026PHB168-001). It included model development and independent testing, a blinded reader study, and a non-randomized sequential comparison of two RWT workflows. Participants collected and photographed shedding hairs at home using their own smartphones. The protocol did not require a fixed smartphone model, standardized lighting, a dedicated camera, or an imaging box.

Image Dataset and Reference Standard

Thirty volunteers collected naturally shedding hairs and photographed them from above with personal smartphones in home settings. Eligible images showed dark brown or black hairs on white paper or another light-colored background. Participants were asked to keep all hairs in view and avoid dense clumps when feasible. Smartphone model, image resolution, and ambient lighting were otherwise unrestricted.

Each participant completed at least two separate collection sessions at different time points, with newly collected shedding hairs used in each session and no reuse of hair sets across sessions. Hair length for each collection was estimated as the mean length of five randomly selected hairs. Within each session, an incremental sequence was constructed starting from one hair, with one hair added at each step. After each addition, two counters independently counted the hairs. Concordant counts defined the reference label, whereas disagreements were resolved by joint recount to establish the final verified reference label. The hairs were randomly redistributed before each subsequent photograph. Both initial counts and final verified reference labels were retained for assessment of reference-count agreement.

The dataset contained 5630 images, with 1 to 400 hairs per image and hair lengths ranging approximately from 5 to 60 cm. Images were partitioned by participant into training (18 participants, 3396 images), internal validation (6 participants, 1113 images), and independent testing (6 participants, 1121 images). No participant contributed images to more than one subset.

AI Model Development and Training

Images were converted to grayscale, normalized for intensity, resized to 256×256 pixels, and duplicated across channels. The model combined an EfficientNet-B0 encoder,22 a U-Net-style decoder,23 and a feature module based on strip pooling and deformable convolution.24,25 These operations were used to represent long, thin, curved, and tangled hair structures. The network generated a non-negative density map, which was integrated to obtain the predicted hair count.

Training used image-level counts and required no bounding boxes, point labels, pixel masks, or tracing of individual hairs. The loss function combined Smooth L1 count loss, background sparsity regularization, and anisotropic total variation regularization. Optimization used AdamW with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4, weight decay of 1 × 10−4, and a batch size of 16. The gradient norm was clipped at 1.0, and training continued for up to 150 epochs. A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler halved the learning rate after 10 epochs without improvement in validation loss. Early stopping was applied after 25 epochs without improvement in validation MAE, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation MAE was retained. Training and inference were performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (Figure 1).

Blinded Reader Study

Ten additional Asian women who did not contribute to training, internal validation, or independent testing provided 56 naturally collected hair samples that were not constructed by sequential hair addition. Each participant invited one other person and counted each example independently. Disagreements were resolved by joint recount, and the adjudicated count became the final reference count. Both initial counts and the final reference count were retained.

Each sample was photographed once with the hairs dispersed and once with the same hairs clustered. AI analyzed only the dispersed-hair photograph. The 15 visual-estimation readers comprised 5 patients reporting increased shedding, 5 healthy participants, and 5 dermatologists specializing in hair disorders. Readers estimated dispersed hairs directly and assessed clustered hairs with the Hair Shedding Visual Scale for Asian women.13 They were blinded to reference counts, AI outputs, and other readers’ estimates. The primary outcome was counting error. Manual counting time and total AI workflow time, including photography, upload, inference, and result display, were also recorded.

RWT Workflow Feasibility Analysis

We compared a historical manual-counting cohort enrolled from April 1 to April 30, 2026, with a subsequent AI-assisted photograph-upload cohort enrolled from May 1 to May 31, 2026. The cohorts were consecutive, non-overlapping, and non-randomized. The endpoints were RWT completion and provision of usable quantitative data. Usable quantitative data required an explicit numerical hair count, while approximate estimates, ranges, and non-numerical descriptions did not meet this endpoint.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

Performance measures included mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), signed bias, overestimation rate, total workflow time, and Pearson correlation between model predictions and final reference counts. Independent-test performance was stratified by reference-count range (1–50, 51–100, 101–200, and >200 hairs). Bland–Altman analysis assessed model–reference agreement, with 95% limits of agreement calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations. For the 56 naturally collected samples, agreement between the two initial counts was quantified using ICC (1,1), and the mean absolute paired difference was summarized descriptively.

Inter-reader ICC (2,1) was calculated for each reader group and image format. Continuous measures were reported as means with 95% t-distribution confidence intervals unless stated otherwise. Paired AI–human comparisons and bias-versus-zero tests used sample-level errors averaged across the five readers within each of the 56 samples, with sign-flip permutation tests and Bonferroni adjustment. Reader-level overestimation rates were compared between groups with exact permutation tests. Manual counting and total AI workflow times were compared with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Associations between processing time and hair count were examined with linear regression, and paired slope differences were tested using linear regression of within-sample manual-minus-AI time differences. RWT workflow proportions were compared between cohorts with Fisher’s exact tests. All analyses were performed using Python 3.10, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Reference Standard Assessment and Independent Test Set Evaluation

Across the 5630 sequence-constructed images, the two initial manual counts were identical in 88.6%, differed by no more than one hair in 97.7%, and differed by no more than two hairs in all images. 643 images required joint recounting to finalize the reference label.

The independent test set comprised 1121 photographs from 6 volunteers whose images were excluded from training and internal validation. Photographs were acquired with multiple smartphone models, and reference counts ranged from 1 to 400 hairs. The model achieved an MAE of 5.4 hairs and an MAPE of 7.0%. Predicted and reference counts were strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.917; P < 0.001), and absolute errors were concentrated near zero (Figure 2A and B). Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean bias of −0.8 hairs (95% LoA, −17.0 to 15.4 hairs; Figure 2C). In stratified performance analysis, MAE increased from 1.9 hairs for counts of 1–50 to 10.6 hairs for counts >200, whereas MAPE decreased from 10.4% to 3.6% (Table 1).

Model Visualization and Qualitative Error Analysis

To examine the spatial features learned by the model, we reviewed representative visualizations and two illustrative high-error images from the independent test set. In two low-error examples, reference counts of 123 and 197 corresponded to predictions of 123.0 and 196.8, respectively (Figure 3A). The activated regions of the density maps closely matched the spatial distribution of visible hairs. The two selected high-error images contained dense central clumps with extensive strand overlap (Figure 3B). The model predicted 174.8 hairs for a reference count of 134 (absolute error, 40.8; 30.4%) and 97.0 hairs for a reference count of 149 (absolute error, 52.0; 34.9%).

Reader Study Comparison of AI and Visual Estimation

The reader study included 56 samples with reference counts of 22 to 403 hairs (median, 105.0; IQR, 50.0–245.5). For the reference standard, the two initial manual counts showed an ICC (1,1) of 0.999 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.998–0.999), with a mean absolute paired difference of 3.8 hairs. AI had an MAE of 6.99 hairs (95% CI, 5.75–8.23) and an MAPE of 6.15% (95% CI, 5.18–7.12). After adjustment for multiple comparisons, every visual estimation method had greater MAE and MAPE than AI (P < 0.001). Dermatologists had lower absolute error than patients and healthy participants (Table 2; Figure 4). Inter-reader reliability was moderate to good and was higher for clustered images, particularly among healthy participants and dermatologists (Table 3).

Reader Bias and Overestimation Patterns

Error direction and magnitude varied by reader group and image format (Table 2; Figure 5A). Healthy participants underestimated dispersed hairs (bias, −33.08 hairs; 95% CI, −45.12 to −21.05). Patients overestimated hair numbers in both dispersed and clustered images (bias, 38.14 and 49.16 hairs, respectively). Dermatologists tended to underestimate dispersed hairs and overestimate clustered hairs. Across readers, overestimation was more common among patients than among healthy participants or dermatologists (P = 0.008; Figure 5B).

Processing Time and RWT Workflow Feasibility

Manual counting took a mean of 188.8 seconds and a median of 123.0 seconds. Total AI workflow time was shorter, with a mean of 25.3 seconds and a median of 24.5 seconds (P < 0.001; Figure 6A). Manual counting time increased with reference hair count (slope, 1.357 seconds per hair), whereas total AI workflow time increased more slowly (slope, 0.057 seconds per hair). The paired difference in slopes was 1.301 seconds per hair (95% CI, 1.173–1.429; P < 0.001; Figure 6B).

In the historical manual cohort, 26 of 48 patients completed the RWT (54.2%), and 14 of 48 (29.2%) provided usable quantitative data (Table 4; Figure 6C). Discussion

This study showed that shedding hairs could be quantified rapidly from ordinary smartphone photographs with low error under the evaluated conditions. The model retained low error in the independent test set and had lower error than human visual estimates in the reader study. The close spatial correspondence between density-map activation and visible hairs was consistent with the model using spatial features relevant to hair counting. The subsequent AI-assisted RWT cohort was also associated with a higher proportion of usable quantitative data than the historical manual cohort.

The reader study illustrates the potential clinical value of objective counts. Visual estimates varied by reader group and image format, including among dermatologists. Patients reporting increased hair loss tended to overestimate hair numbers, possibly reflecting the psychosocial burden of alopecia.6,8,10 Automated counting may therefore provide a more consistent reference for consultations in which perceived and measured shedding diverge.

The RWT analysis provided a preliminary assessment of automated counting within a clinical collection workflow. Usable quantitative data were more frequent in the subsequent AI-assisted cohort than in the historical manual cohort (76.7% versus 29.2%), while RWT completion was 22.5 percentage points higher, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.056). Given the sequential, non-randomized, and non-concurrent design, temporal or other unmeasured factors may have contributed to the observed between-cohort differences, which should therefore be examined further in prospective studies.

The density maps provide insight into what the model learned. Their activated regions closely followed the distribution of visible hairs in low-error examples, showing that the model extracted spatial features directly relevant to hair counting. Dense aggregation emerged as the main failure mode in the illustrative high-error cases. Extensive overlap can disperse density responses across a tangled region or merge signals from several strands, resulting in either overestimation or underestimation. Image-acquisition instructions should therefore encourage users to spread hairs before photography. Higher-resolution or multiscale models may further improve performance in densely aggregated samples.

The model MAE of 5.4 hairs was small relative to both population-level differences reported in previous RWT studies3,4 and the count ranges represented by existing semi-quantitative visual shedding scales,12,13 while remaining close in magnitude to the mean absolute disagreement of 3.8 hairs between independent manual counters. Although no validated individual-level threshold for clinically acceptable counting error has yet been established, these comparisons provide clinically relevant context for the observed accuracy and support further outcome-anchored validation.

This study has several limitations. First, participant recruitment, model development, and RWT evaluation were conducted at a single academic dermatology department, although participants used their own smartphones and no fixed imaging environment was required. The sequence-constructed images were not equivalent to independent, naturally collected shedding events, and the independent test set, although comprising 1121 images, included only six held-out participants. Repeated time-separated collection sessions and paired adjudicated counts strengthened the reference standard, while the 56 naturally collected samples from 10 additional participants provided complementary evaluation outside the sequence-construction paradigm and broadened participant representation, thus validation remains limited in scale and diversity. Hair morphology was not systematically characterized, and evaluation was limited to dark hair on light-colored backgrounds and to Asian women in the visual-scale comparison. Further validation in larger and more diverse populations, hair phenotypes, and imaging conditions is needed to establish generalizability. Finally, the RWT cohorts were sequential, non-randomized, and non-concurrent, and the study did not assess diagnostic accuracy, treatment decisions, treatment response, or patient outcomes. Prospective clinical studies are therefore needed to determine the clinical utility of automated hair counting.

Conclusions

AI analysis of ordinary smartphone photographs quantified dark brown or black shedding hairs on white or light-colored backgrounds rapidly and with low error under the evaluated conditions. It had lower error than human visual estimation and was associated with a higher proportion of usable quantitative RWT data in the subsequent AI-assisted cohort. This photograph-based method has the potential to support objective hair-shedding assessment in clinical care and research, with further validation needed across broader populations and settings.
