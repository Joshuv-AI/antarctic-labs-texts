Quantum Hamming metrics
Marc A. Rieffel

Abstract
. Given the set of words of a given length for a given alphabet, the Hamming metric between two such words is the number of positions where the two words differ. A quantum version of the corresponding Kantorovich-Wasserstein metric on states was introduced in 2021 by De Palma, Marvian, Trevisan and Lloyd. For the quantum version the alphabet is replaced by a full matrix algebra, and the set of words is replaced by the tensor product of a corresponding number of copies of that full matrix algebra. While De Palma et al. work primarily at the level of states, they do obtain the corresponding seminorm (the quantum Hamming metric) on the algebra of observables that plays the role of assigning Lipschitz constants to functions. A suitable such seminorm on a unital C*-algebra is the current common method for defining a quantum metric on a C*-algebra. In this paper we will reverse the process, by first expressing the Hamming metric in terms of the C*-algebra of functions on the set of words, and then dropping the requirement that the algebra be commutative so as to obtain the quantum Hamming metric. From that we obtain the corresponding Kantorovich-Wasserstein metric on states. Along the way we show that many of the steps can be put in more general forms of some interest, notably for infinite-dimensional C*-algebras.

In 2021 De Palma et al. published a paper

They present many basic properties of these quantum versions, and indicate strong connections with a variety of important topics in quantum information theory. In the course of their paper the authors obtain a quantum version of the Hamming metrics themselves.

In the present paper we reverse the process, and start with
Hamming metrics themselves, and provide a natural path
that leads to the definition of quantum Hamming metrics.
With this definition in hand, one is led quite naturally to the
corresponding quantum Kantorovich-Wasserstein metrics on
the state spaces of the underlying quantum systems,
which in

7

“The quantum Wasserstein distance of order 1”).
In the present paper we put
various of the steps into suitable more general contexts,
which provide more perspective for the steps. In particular,
we show that many (but not all) of the steps work well
for arbitrary, possibly infinite-dimensional, unital C*-algebras.
We also show how the
quantum Hamming metrics can be expressed in terms of
Dirac operator type structures.

Here is a quick outline of our approach. We note first that the Hamming
metric comes from first putting the complete-graph metric on each
alphabet in use, and then using the corresponding sum-metric on
the cartesian product of the alphabets.
We then show how to express these two steps in terms of
the corresponding algebras
of functions (the algebras of “observables”), so that the Hamming metric
is expressed entirely in terms of these algebras. Since this reformulation
does not mention points of spaces, nor use the commutativity of the algebras,
we can replace the algebras with ones that are not commutative, such as full
matrix algebras, to obtain our quantum version of the Hamming metrics. The
metric data is then given by a seminorm that plays the role of attaching the
Lipschitz constants to functions on a metric space. We also show
how to express the metric data in terms of “Dirac operators”, from which
a few additional properties become evident. From the
formulas for the quantum Hamming metrics we then obtain the formulas
for the corresponding Kantorovich-Wasserstein metrics on states,
that in
[
7
]
are taken as the
definition
of the quantum
Wasserstein-1 metrics on states.

Necessarily many of the results in the present paper are

2.
Hamming metrics

In this section we begin the discussion of ordinary
Hamming metrics, aiming to put them in
a form suitable for suggesting the quantum versions
that we will discuss later.
The setting is as follows.
We are given a finite set with
n
elements,
I
,
whose elements we use as indices.
For each
i\in I
we are given a finite set,
X_{i}
,
called the alphabet at position
i
.
(For the quantum Hamming metric, for each
i

our alphabet can be a quantum
finite set, that is, a full matrix algebra

{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=M_{m_{i}}({\mathbb{C}})
with its structure as a C*-algebra.)
Often it is assumed that all
these alphabets are the same set, but we do not need
to make this assumption. We then consider “words”
of length
n
with the
i
th entry taken from
X_{i}

for each
i
. The set of these words can be
identified with the product set
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
.
(In the quantum setting there are no words, but
since
C(X)=\otimes C(X_{i})
,
in the quantum setting we will use
the C*-algebra
\otimes{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.) The
Hamming metric on
X
is the metric that sets
the distance between two words to be the
number of indices
i
for which the
i
-th entry of
the two words is different.
This suggests that on each
X_{i}
we let
d_{i}

be the metric that gives distance 1 to all pairs
of distinct elements of
X_{i}
.

Given metrics on several sets, there is a large
number of ways of using them to define a metric
on the product of those sets. The definition of
the Hamming metric suggests that we use
the “sum-metric”, that is
the sum of the metrics. Thus we define
the metric
d_{H}
on
X
by

d_{H}(a,b)=\sum_{i}d_{i}(a_{i},b_{i})

for all
a,b\in X
. It is easily checked that
this is indeed the Hamming metric. For the more general
case in which the
X_{i}
’s are general metric
spaces (usually compact in later sections), with arbitrary metrics

d_{i}
, we will denote the sum-metric on their
product by
d_{S}
.

To try to see what the quantum version of the
Hamming metrics might be, we follow the often-used
path of expressing the situation in
terms of the of the algebra of functions on

X
, and then dropping the requirement that
the algebra be commutative.

For any compact metric space
(X,d)
we denote
the algebra of continuous
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued functions
on
X
by
C(X)
. There are quantum physics experiments
which are considered to indicate that nature knows
about
i=\sqrt{-1}
and that

[
25
]

and the references in
[
20
]
.) For this reason
we will usually use
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued functions, and
matrix algebras over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
For any

L^{d}(f)
be its Lipschitz constant, defined by

(2.1)

L^{d}(f)=\sup\{|f(x)-f(y)|/d(x,y):x,y\in X,\hskip 11.74988ptx\neq y\}

(which may be
+\infty
).
We let
Lip_{d}(X)
be the dense sub-algebra of

consisting of functions

such that
L^{d}(f)<\infty
, that is, are
Lipschitz for
d
. Thus
L^{d}
, as defined in equation

2.1
, restricts to a finite-valued seminorm on

.
But aside from the fact that

can take value

on

,
it has in general quite nice properties

[
26
,
27
,

]
. These properties

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a unital C*-algebra,
and let
L
be a seminorm on
{\mathcal{A}}

that may take the value
+\infty
. We say that

L
is a

if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

For any

we have

L(a)=0
if and only if
a\in{\mathbb{C}}1_{\mathcal{A}}
, and

L
is a
*
-seminorm in the sense that

L(a^{*})=L(a)
.

(2)

{\mathcal{L}}_{L}:=\{a:L(a)<+\infty\}

{\mathcal{A}}

(and so is a dense
*
-subspace of
{\mathcal{A}}
).

(3)

L
satisfies the Leibniz inequality

L(ab)\leq L(a)\|b\|+\|a\|L(b)

for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
. (So
{\mathcal{L}}_{L}
is a
*
-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
.)

(4)

Define a metric,

, on the state space

of
{\mathcal{A}}
by

(2.2)

d^{L}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\{|\mu(a)-\nu(a)|:a\in{\mathcal{A}},\ \ L(a)\leq 1\}.

We require that the topology
on
S({\mathcal{A}})
determined by this metric agrees with the
weak-
*
topology (so it is compact).

(5)

L
is lower semi-continuous with respect to the
operator norm.

We will call a pair
({\mathcal{A}},L)
with
{\mathcal{A}}
a unital C*-algebra
and

a C*-metric on
{\mathcal{A}}

.
If a Leibniz seminorm also satisfied the property that if

is invertible in
{\mathcal{A}}

then

, as discussed
in sections 1 and 2 of
[
29
]
), then we say that

is

strongly Leibniz
.

Remark 2.2
.

A priori

can take the value
+\infty
.
But that can not happen if property (4) holds.
Also, because
L
is a
*
-seminorm,
it suffices to take the supremum in property (4)
only over self-adjoint

’s. To see this, note that
if we let
\phi=\mu-\nu
then
\phi(a^{*})=\overline{\phi(a)}
for
all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Then for any given
a
, we can multiply it
by a complex number of modulus 1 in such a way that

\phi(a)\geq 0
and

is unchanged. Then

\phi((a+a^{*})/2)=\phi(a)
.

One of the consequences of Theorem
5.2
below
is a proof of the well-known fact that
for any compact
metric space
(X,d)
, the pair
(C(X),L^{d})

is a compact C*-metric space.

Accordingly, for
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
with its
Hamming metric
d_{H}
as defined earlier, we will
provisionally take
L_{H}
,
as defined by equation

, to

.
But we will see later that when working with
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued

We need to explore some of the properties of Hamming metrics.
We begin with one simple property. For any
finite set
X
let

be the metric that gives distance 1

L^{1}
be the
corresponding C*-metric on

.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X=\Pi_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
as before, where the
X_{i}
’s
are finite sets.
Then the diameter of
(X,d_{H})
is no more than
n
, and

L^{1}\leq nL_{H}\leq nL^{1}
. If each

contains at

A moment’s thought shows that the metric

takes values between

and

. Thus for
any

and any

|f(x)-f(y)|\geq|f(x)-f(y)|/d_{H}(x,y)\geq(1/n)|f(x)-f(y)|,

from which the first statement follows immediately. For
the second statement, if each
X_{i}
contains at least 2
points, then there are at least two elements of

that
differ from each other in

places, and so have

n
.

3.
Sum-metrics

We examine first
sum-metrics on products of compact metric spaces.
So for each
i\in I
let
(X_{i},d_{i})
be a compact
metric space, let
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
with the product topology,
and let
d_{S}

be the sum-metric on
X
. For each
i\in I

define
L_{i}
on
C(X)
by

(3.1)

L_{i}(f)=\sup\{|f(x)-f(y)|/d_{i}(x_{i},y_{i}):x\neq y\in X,\ \ x_{j}=y_{j}\ \mathrm{for}\ j\neq i\}.

This can be considered to be a rough analog of the partial
derivative of
f
in the
i
direction. As usual,
L_{i}(f)

can be
+\infty
. We record here an easily verified fact that
will be quite important later:

Proposition 3.1
.

With notation as above, let
\tilde{X}_{i}=\Pi\{X_{j}:j\neq i\}
,
so that
X=X_{i}\times\tilde{X}_{i}
.
Then the null-space of
L_{i}

consists of the functions that are constant
in the
i^{th}
variable, and so this null-space is
C(\tilde{X}_{i})
,
where
C(\tilde{X}_{i})
is
viewed as a subalgebra of
C(X)
via the natural projection
from
X
onto
\tilde{X}_{i}
.

The following result, and later its quantum version,
are central to this paper.

Theorem 3.2
.

For each
i\in I
let
(X_{i},d_{i})
be a compact
metric space, let
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
, and let
d_{S}

be the sum-metric on
X
. Let
L^{d_{S}}
be the
Lipschitz seminorm for
d_{S}
, and for each
i

let
L_{i}
be as defined in equation (
3.1
). Then

L^{d_{S}}(f)=\max\{L_{i}(f):i\in I\}

for all
f\in C(X)
. In particular,
f\in Lip_{d_{S}}(X)
exactly if

L_{i}(f)<\infty
for each
i\in I
.

Proof.

We treat first the case in which
I
contains only two elements, 1 and 2,
and so
f
is a function of two variables.
For any
s,t,u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
with
u,v
strictly positive we have
the inequality

\frac{s+t}{u+v}\leq\frac{s}{u}\vee\frac{t}{v},

where “
\vee
” means “maximum”.
The proof of this inequality is straight-forward, especially if one begins by
multiplying the inequality by
v/t
.

Let
f\in C(X_{1}\times X_{2})
.
With evident notation,
for all
a,b\in X_{1}
and
p,q\in X_{2}
with
a\neq b
and
p\neq q
we have

\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,q)|}{d_{S}((a,p),(b,q))}\leq\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,p)|+|f(b,p)-f(b,q)|}{d_{1}(a,b)+d_{2}(p,q)},

which by the above inequality is

\leq\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,p)|}{d_{1}(a,b)}\vee\frac{|f(b,p)-f(b,q)|}{d_{2}(p,q)}.

If instead
p=q
then we have an evident simplified version of the inequality,
and similarly if
a=b
.
Since the term after the last inequality is clearly no bigger than

L^{d_{S}}(f)
, the proof for two spaces is complete.

We now deal with the general case by induction on the
number,
n
, of elements in
I
.
We have just given
a proof for the case of
n=2
. We now assume that
n\geq 3
,
and that the conclusion holds for
n-1
. Choose an index
i_{0}
,
and let
J=I\setminus\{i_{0}\}
.
Let
Y=\Pi\{X_{i}:i\in J\}
, with corresponding sum-metric
d_{S}^{J}
.
Then
X=Y\times X_{i_{0}}
, and, importantly,
d_{S}
is the sum-metric
of
d_{S}^{J}
and
d_{i_{0}}
. Thus from the case for
n=2
treated above,
we see that for any
f\in C(X)
we have

L^{d_{S}}(f)=\sup\{\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,p)|}{d_{S}^{J}(a,b)}\}\vee\sup\{\frac{|f(b,p)-f(b,q)|}{d_{i_{0}}(p,q)}\}

where “sup” is over all
a,b\in Y
and all
p,q\in X_{i_{0}}
.
But from the induction hypothesis that the proposition is true
for
n-1
, it is easily seen that the term

\sup{\{\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,p)|}{d_{S}^{J}(a,b)}:a,b\in Y,p\in X_{i_{0}}\}}

is equal to
\max\{L_{j}(f):j\in J\}
,
while the term

\sup\{\frac{|f(b,p)-f(b,q)|}{d_{i_{0}}(p,q)}\}:b\in Y,\ \ p,q\in X_{i_{0}}\}

is easily seen to be
L_{i_{0}}(f)
. Thus the “max” of these two terms is

\max\{L_{i}(f):i\in I\}
as desired
∎

somewhere in the huge literature about metric spaces, but so far

our later results about metric spaces.

Notice that when

is finite,
calculating

by calculating the

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\{(X_{i},d_{i})\}
and

Let

be the sum-metric on

for

and

Let

, equipped with the sum metric

for

(Z,d^{Z}_{S})=(X\times X^{\prime},d_{S}^{\prime\prime})

4.
Diameters of compact C*-metric spaces

Quantum physicists are very interested in states, which are the generalization
of probability measures to the setting of non-commutative C*-algebras. That is, states
are the positive linear functionals of norm 1 on a C*-algebra.
For mathematical models of quantum systems, the mathematical states
usually correspond to the possible physical (mixed)
states of the physical quantum system
being modeled. Consequently
physicists are very interested in various notions of distance between states.
In the setting of compact metric spaces one has the
well-known definition of the Monge-Kantorovich-Wasserstein metric
on the set of probability measures on a given compact metric space.
It is also variously called the “earth-mover metric” or the “transportation-cost
metric”
[
34
]
. We will be exploring that metric for the case
of sum-metrics, and then in our discussion of quantum Hamming metrics.

But in several places in the next sections we will be concerned with
the radius and diameter of state spaces for various metrics defined
by seminorms.
As a convenience to the reader, we recall now some of
the discussion in section 2 of
[
27
]
concerning the
diameter and radius of
a state space, and we relate it to compact metric spaces.
Here, and throughout
this paper,
for any C*-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
we set
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}={\mathcal{A}}/{\mathbb{C}}1_{\mathcal{A}}
, and
we let
L^{0}
be the seminorm on

{\mathcal{A}}
that is the pull-back to
{\mathcal{A}}
of the quotient norm
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
, that is;

Notation 4.1
.

L^{0}(a)=\inf\{\|a-z1_{\mathcal{A}}\|:z\in{\mathbb{C}}\}

for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

We will use the well-known fact (see lemma 2.1
of
[
27
]
, a consequence of the Jordan decomposition
for self-adjoint linear functionals on
{\mathcal{A}}

[
23
]
)
that the set
\{\mu-\nu:\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})\}

coincides with the the ball
D_{2}
of radius 2 in
the annihilator of
1_{\mathcal{A}}
in the
dual of the space of self-adjoint elements of
{\mathcal{A}}
. We do not
require here that the seminorms
L
satisfy conditions (3), (4) or (5)
of Definition
2.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a unital C*-algebra, and let
L
be
a seminorm on
{\mathcal{A}}
that satisfies properties
(1) and (2) of Definition
2.1
. Then it determines a metric
d^{L}

on
S({\mathcal{A}})
exactly as in the formula
2.2
, except
that the metric may take the value
+\infty
. Then the following
statements are equivalent for any given
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
:

(1)

For all
\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
we have
d^{L}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2r
.

(2)

For all self-adjoint
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have
L^{0}(a)\leq rL(a)
.

Proof.

Suppose that statement (1) holds. Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
a^{*}=a
,
and let
\phi\in D_{2}

so that
\phi=\mu-\nu
for some
\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
. Then

|\phi(a)|=|(\mu-\nu)(a)|\leq d^{L}(\mu,\nu)L(a)\leq 2rL(a).

Since

\phi(1_{\mathcal{A}})=0
, this inequality holds whenever
a
is replaced
by
a-s1_{\mathcal{A}}
for some
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
this is true for all
\phi\in D_{2}
and since
a^{*}=a
, it follows that
statement (2) holds.

Conversely, suppose that statement (2) holds.
Then for any
\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
and any self-adjoint

a\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
L(a)\leq 1
we have

|\mu(a)-\nu(a)|=|(\mu-\nu)(a-s1_{\mathcal{A}})|\leq 2\|a-s1_{\mathcal{A}}\|

for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
a^{*}=a
, it follows that
|\mu(a)-\nu(a)|\leq 2L^{0}(a)
.
Statement (1) follows from this.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

The smallest
r
for which statements (1) and (2)
just above hold is
called the
radius
of
({\mathcal{A}},L)
, and

is
called its

Proposition 4.4
.

If
(X,d)
is a compact metric space whose radius
is

, then the radius, as
defined above, of the compact C*-metric
space
({\mathcal{A}}=C(X),L^{d})

, but only for finite metric spaces, where there
is no distinction between the dual and the predual
of
Lip^{0}(X,d_{S})
. Thus we view
M^{0}_{d}(X)
as the
dual of
Lip^{0}(X,d_{S})
, with dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
, much as
defined in equation (
5.1
). We let
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
, where
each
(X_{i},d_{i})
is a finite
metric space, and we let
d_{S}

be the sum-metric on
X
. Let
L^{d_{S}}
be the
Lipschitz seminorm for
d_{S}
, viewed as a
seminorm on
Lip(X,d_{S})
, so that according to
Theorem
3.2
we have

L^{d_{S}}(f)=\max\{L_{i}(f):i\in I\}

for all
f\in Lip(X,d_{S})
. To apply Proposition
6.1

we let
W=Lip^{0}(X,d_{S})
and we view
L^{d_{S}}
as a norm
on
W
. We also view each of the
L_{i}
’s as a seminorm on

W
(which works since
X
is finite).
From Proposition
3.1
we see that the null-space,
N_{i}
,
of each
L_{i}
is, as vector space, the image in
W
of

C(\tilde{X}_{i})
, where

\tilde{X}_{i}=\Pi\{X_{j}:j\neq i\}
,
and where
C(\tilde{X}_{i})
is
viewed as a subalgebra of
C(X)
. Then
L_{i}
can be viewed
as a norm on the quotient space
C(X)/C(\tilde{X}_{i})
.
We then let
N_{i}^{\perp}
be the annihilator of
N_{i}

in
W^{\prime}
. As notation for the norm on
N_{i}^{\perp}
dual to
L_{i}

we use
\|\cdot\|_{i}
instead of
\rho^{*}_{i}
.
We are then exactly in the situation of Proposition
6.1
,
and it tells us that:

Corollary 6.2
.

With
Lip^{0}(X,d_{S})
and
M^{0}_{d}(X)
as above for finite
X
, and
with notation as just above,
for each
\phi\in M^{0}_{d}(X)
we have

\|\phi\|_{d}=\inf\{\sum\|\phi_{i}\|_{i}:\phi=\sum\phi_{i}\ \ \ \mathrm{with}\ \ \phi_{i}\in N_{i}^{\perp},\ \ i\in I\},

for
N_{i}^{\perp}=C(\tilde{X}_{i})^{\perp}\subseteq M^{0}_{d}(X)
.
Thus if

\mu
and
\nu
are probability measures on
X
, then

d^{L^{d_{S}}}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\{\sum\|\phi_{i}\|_{i}:\mu-\nu=\sum\phi_{i}\ \ \ \mathrm{with}\ \ \phi_{i}\in N_{i}^{\perp},\ \ i\in I\}.

By the argument in Remark
2.2
one can require all the
\phi_{i}
’s
to be self-adjoint.

For specific examples and
\mu
and
\nu
’s, it can be an interesting challenge to calculate

d^{L^{d_{S}}}(\mu,\nu)
, especially if the index set
I
is large.

7.
Differential calculi and Dirac-type operators for sum-metrics

In this section we will see how to express the seminorms
L_{j}

defined above
in terms of first-order differential
calculi, and then in terms of Dirac-type operators,
so that they can be combined to apply to
L^{d_{S}}
.
(By “Dirac-type operator” we are really referring to
how

an operator is used, namely to provide a metric, along the lines
described below. Any non-zero self-adjoint operator
can serve as a Dirac-type operator in many ways.)

Let us first recall (e.g. page 321 of
[
14
]
) the
construction of the universal first-order
differential calculus for any unital algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
over
{\mathbb{C}}
, since
we will somewhat imitate this construction here.
Let
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}
, viewed as an
{\mathcal{A}}
-bimodule
in the evident way. We define a derivation,
\delta
, on
{\mathcal{A}}

with values in
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}
by

\delta a=1\otimes a-a\otimes 1.

The fact that
\delta
is a derivation means that it satisfies
the Leibnitz equality

\delta(ab)=\delta(a)b+a\delta(b)

for
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Then let
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}
be
the sub-bimodule generated by
the range of
d
, spanned by elements of the
form
a\delta(b)
(“first-order differential forms”). The pair
(\Omega_{\mathcal{A}},d)

is the universal first-order differential calculus for
{\mathcal{A}}
.

When
X
is a compact space and
{\mathcal{A}}=C(X)
,
then
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}=C(X\times X)
(we use the completion
of the tensor product), and then
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}
can be
identified with
C_{\infty}(E)
where

E=\{(x,y):x,y\in X,\ x\neq y\}
. Indeed, for any unital
algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
there is a natural identification of
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}

with the kernel of the multiplication map from

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{A}}
. But for
{\mathcal{A}}=C(X)
, when
the algebraic tensor product
C(X)\otimes_{alg}C(X)

is viewed as a
(dense) subalgebra of
C(X\times X)
, the multiplication
map is the same as restriction of functions to the diagonal,
\Delta
,
of
X\otimes X
. That map is onto
C(X)
, and continuous.
When extended to all of
C(X\times X)
, its kernel is exactly
the space of functions in
C(X\times X)
that vanish on
\Delta
,
that is,
C_{\infty}(E)
. (See the “important example”
shortly after exercise 8.3 of
[
14
]
.)
Notice that if
X

is considered to be just a set, then
E
can be viewed
as the set of edges of the complete graph with
X

as the set of vertices.

Returning to the setting of the previous section, we
treat first the case in which
I
contains just two elements. To
ease the notation, we will denote the two compact spaces by

X
and
Y
, with metrics
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
. We will set

Z=X\times Y
, and let
d_{S}
be the sum-metric on
Z

using
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
.
For
f\in Lip(Z,d_{S})
we set, much as earlier,

L_{1}(f)=\sup\{\frac{|f(a,p)-f(b,p)|}{d_{X}(a,b)}:a,b\in X,p\in Y\}.

Note that
L_{1}(f)
is finite because
f\in Lip(Z,d_{S})
,
as seen from Theorem
3.2
.
To define a corresponding bimodule we first set

E=\{(a,p,b)\in X\times Y\times X:a\neq b\}.

Notice that
E
is an open subset of
X\times Y\times X
and
so may not be compact. We let
\Omega=C_{b}(E)
,
consisting of the bounded continuous functions on
E
,
equipped with the supremum norm.
Note that
\Omega
has an
evident
C(X)
-bimodule structure coming from the two
copies of
X
in
E
, and thus it also has a
Lip(Z,d_{S})
-bimodule
structure. We define a derivation,
\delta_{1}
, on
Lip(Z,d_{S})

with values in
\Omega
by

\delta_{1}f(a,p,b)=(f(a,p)-f(b,p))/d_{X}(a,b).

We then see that

(7.1)

L_{1}(f)=\|\delta f\|_{\infty}

for all
f\in Lip(Z,d_{S})
.

More generally, suppose that
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
, where each

(X_{i},d_{i})
is a compact
metric space,
and we let
d_{S}

be the sum-metric on
X
. Then for each
i
we have

X=X_{i}\times\tilde{X}_{i}
where
\tilde{X}_{i}=\Pi\{X_{j}:j\neq i\}

as in the previous section. We can then apply the results just above
to obtain for each
i
a normed bimodule over
C(X)
and a
derivation of
C(X)
into it. We can then form the direct sum
of these bimodules, with supremum norm, and the direct sum
of the derivations, to obtain an expression for
L^{d_{S}}
like
equation
7.1
.

Alternatively, we can express all of this in terms of a Dirac-type
operator. Choose (finite) Borel measures on
X
and
Y

of full measure,
and let
\mu
be the corresponding product measure
on
X\times Y\times X
, restricted to
E
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}(E,\mu)
, and let
\pi
be the (faithful) representation
of
C(Z)
(and so of
Lip(Z,d_{S})
)
on
{\mathcal{H}}
defined by
(\pi_{f}\xi)(a,p,b)=f(a,p)\xi(a,p,b)

for all
\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Define a (possibly unbounded) operator,
D_{1}
,
( for “Dirac”) on
{\mathcal{H}}
by

(D_{1}\xi)(a,p,b)=\xi(b,p,a)/d_{X}(a,b)

(with domain consisting of those
\xi
for which
D_{1}\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}
).
A quick calculation shows that for any

f\in C(Z)
the formal commutator
[D_{1},\pi_{f}]

is given by

([D_{1},\pi_{f}]\xi)(a,p,b)=((f(b,p)-f(a,p))/d_{X}(a,b))\xi(b,p,a)

for any
\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}
. From this we easily obtain:

Proposition 7.1
.

For notation as above,
the functions
f
for which

[D_{1},\pi_{f}]
is a bounded operator on its (dense) domain are exactly
the
f
’s for which
L_{1}(f)<+\infty
, and for these
f
’s we have

\|[D_{1},\pi_{f}]\|=L_{1}(f).

We remark that often
D_{1}
does not have compact resolvent, and
thus often
(Lip(Z,d_{S}),{\mathcal{H}},D_{1})
is not a “spectral triple” as
defined by Connes.

We can now treat the general case in which
X=\Pi_{i}X_{i}
and

d_{S}
is the sum-metric of the
d_{i}
’s. For each
i
let

\tilde{X}_{i}=\Pi\{X_{j}:j\neq i\}
as done earlier,
so that
X=X_{i}\times\tilde{X}_{i}
.
We let
\tilde{d}_{i}
be the metric on
\tilde{X}_{i}
that is the sum-metric
of the
d_{j}
’s for which
j\neq i
. Then

d_{S}
is, according to Proposition
3.3
, the sum-metric
of
d_{i}
and
\tilde{d}_{i}
. Let
L_{i}
be the
seminorm on
C(X)
for the decomposition
X=X_{i}\times\tilde{X}_{i}

using equation
3.1
and
d_{i}
, and
let
({\mathcal{H}}_{i},\pi^{i})

be the representation of
C(X)
constructed as above, with
Dirac operator
D_{i}
, so that

L_{i}(f)=\|[D_{i},\pi^{i}f]\|.

It is easily seen that
this
L_{i}
coincides with the
L_{i}
of equation
3.1
,
so that

L^{d_{S}}(f)=\max_{i}\{L_{i}(f)\}.

Now let
{\mathcal{H}}=\bigoplus{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, let
\pi=\bigoplus\pi_{i}
,
and
D=\bigoplus D_{i}
. It is then clear that

L^{d_{S}}(f)=\|[D,\pi(f)]\|

for every
f\in Lip(X,d_{S})
.

To proceed further we now need to specialize somewhat
by using another aspect of the Hamming metric.

8.
Complete-graph metrics, and their Dirac operators

The Hamming metric involves setting the distance between
the different letters of any given one of its
alphabets to always be 1. This is
the metric on a complete graph for which all edges have
length 1. If
X
is a finite set and
d
is the corresponding
complete-graph metric, then the
corresponding Lipschitz seminorm,
L_{c}
(“c” for “complete”),
is defined by

L_{c}(f)=\max\{|f(x)-f(y)|:x\neq y\}.

for all
f\in C(X)
. But this has meaning whenever
(X,d_{X})

is a compact metric space, although then this
L_{c}
will not
be a C*-metric if
X
is not finite, because requirement (4) of
Definition
2.1
will fail.
The seminorm
L_{c}
is now given exactly by
he universal differential
calculus for the algebra
C(X)
, with
\Omega=C_{\infty}(E)
for

E=\{(x,y);x\neq y\}.

(When
X
is finite this is the edge-set of the complete graph.)
As before, the derivation,
\delta
,
from
C(X)
into
\Omega
is defined by

\delta f(x,y)=f(y)-f(x)

for any
(x,y)\in E
.
It is clear that

L_{c}(f)=\|\delta f\|_{\infty}

for every
f\in C(X)
, as desired.

As before,
L_{c}
can also
be represented by a Dirac-type operator, which
will be bounded in this case. For
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}(E,\mu)

where
\mu
is the product with itself of
a finite Borel measure of full support on
X
, the
Dirac operator,
D
, is defined by

D\xi(x,y)=\xi(y,x)
.

But there is another way of viewing this situation, which we will see
is more useful for obtaining the quantum Hamming metric.
Let
f\in C(X)
be
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued, let
x_{M}
and
x_{m}
be points
where
f
takes its maximum and minimum values,
and let
s=(f(x_{M})+f(x_{m}))/2
. Then one sees quickly that

L_{c}(f)=2\|f-s1_{X}\|_{\infty}.

Even more, one sees easily that
s
is the constant for which
the expression on the right takes its minimum value. That
is

(8.1)

L_{c}(f)=2\inf\{\|f-t1_{X}\|_{\infty}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},

or,
s1_{X}
is the closest function in
{\mathbb{C}}1_{X}
to
f
.
But this latter expression makes sense for
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued
functions, where we also allow
t\in{\mathbb{C}}
. That is,
we can define
L_{q}
(“q” for “quotient”) on
C(X)
by

L_{q}(f)=\inf\{\|f-z1_{X}\|_{\infty}:z\in{\mathbb{C}}\},

so that
L_{q}
is the quotient norm on
C(X)/{\mathbb{C}}1_{X}

pulled back to
C(X)
. But this is exactly the seminorm
L^{0}

that we have been using earlier. Thus

L_{c}=2L^{0}
on real-valued functions. However,
these two
*
-seminorms,
L_{c}
and
2L^{0}
,
can differ on
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued
functions. Here is an example, well-known for other
purposes (see example 1.35 of
[
34
]
).

Example 8.1
.

Let
X
be a set with 3 points, all at
distance 1 from each other, and let
f
be the
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued
function that sends the 3 point to the 3 cube-roots of
1. Then the constant function which is closest to
f
is easily
seen to be the constant function 0, so that

2L^{0}(f)=2\inf\{\|f-t1_{X}\|_{\infty}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}=2
, whereas
L_{c}(f)=\sqrt{3}
.

We can also find a Dirac operator for
L^{0}
(and so for
2L^{0}
).
The construction is somewhat more complicated
than that for
L_{c}
,
but it is suggestive of what one might do in the
quantum case.
For simplicity we will discuss here only the case when

X
is finite. But the discussion we give has a good
generalization to the case when
C(X)
is replaced
by any unital (possibly non-commutative and
infinite-dimensional) C*-algebra – see theorem 3.2
of
[
28
]
and the constructions in its proof.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
X
be a finite set, and let
{\mathcal{H}}=\ell^{2}(X)
, with
C(X)

represented on
{\mathcal{H}}
as pointwise multiplication operators.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be the set of all rank-one projections on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Then for every
f\in C(X)
we have

L^{0}(f)=\sup\{\|[P,\ f]\|\}:P\in{\mathcal{P}}\}.

Proof.

Note that for any
g\in C(X)
we have

\|[P,\ g]\|=\|[P-(1/2)1_{X},\ g]\|\leq\|g\|_{\infty}
,
since
\|P-(1/2)1_{X}\|=1/2
.
Then for any
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have

\|[P,\ g]\|=\|[P,\ g-z1_{X}]\|\leq\|g-z1_{X}\|_{\infty}
, and so

\sup\{\|[P,\ g]\|\}:P\in{\mathcal{P}}\}\leq L^{0}(g)
.
Now fix
f\in C(X)
.
For ease of bookkeeping in obtaining the reverse inequality,
let us assume that
L^{0}(f)=1
. The first basic result of linear
approximation theory tells us that then there is a linear
functional,
\phi
, on
C(X)
such that
\|\phi\|=1=\phi(f)

and
\phi(1)=0
. (Just consider the image of
f
in
C(X)/{\mathbb{C}}1
,
whose norm there is
L^{0}(f)
, so that by the Hahn-Banach theorem
there is a linear functional on
C(X)/{\mathbb{C}}1
of norm 1 whose value
on the image of
f
is
L^{0}(f)
, and pull this linear functional
back to
C(X)
.) Because
X
is finite,
\phi
can be viewed as
an element of
\ell^{1}(X)
with
\|\phi\|_{1}=1
.
Let
\xi=v|\phi|
be the polar
decomposition of
\phi
, and then set
\xi=|\phi|^{1/2}

and
\eta=v|\phi|^{1/2}
. Then
\xi,\eta\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and

\|\xi\|_{2}=1=\|\eta\|_{2}
, and

\phi(g)=\langle g\xi,\eta\rangle
for every
g\in C(X)
. From the
fact that
\phi(1)=0
we see that
\xi\perp\eta
. Let

P
be the rank-one projection along
\eta
, and notice
that
P\xi=0
. Then

\langle[P,g]\xi,\eta\rangle=\langle Pg\xi,\eta\rangle-\langle gP\xi,\eta\rangle=\langle g\xi,\eta\rangle=\phi(g)

for every
g\in C(X)
. In particular,

\langle[P,f]\xi,\eta\rangle=\phi(f)=1
, so that

\|[P,f]\|\geq 1=L^{0}(f)
, as needed.
∎

The rank-1 projections on
{\mathcal{H}}
constitute the projective
space,
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}})
, over
{\mathcal{H}}
. It is compact for its natural
topology. Choose a Borel measure of full support on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}})

and form the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}}),{\mathcal{H}})
of

{\mathcal{H}}
-valued functions on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}})
.
Let
C(X)
act on
L^{2}({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}}),{\mathcal{H}})
by letting each

f\in C(X)
act on the copy of
{\mathcal{H}}
over each point

P
in its usual way.
Let
D
be
the tautological function on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}})
that to each
point
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}})
assigns the operator
P
acting
on the copy of
{\mathcal{H}}
over
P
, and view
D
as
the operator on
L^{2}({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}}),{\mathcal{H}})

of pointwise application of the operators
P
.
From Proposition
8.2
we see
that

L^{0}(f)=\|[D,f]\|

for each
f\in C(X)
. Thus
D
serves as a Dirac operator
for
L^{0}
.

We remark that when
X
is a finite set we saw that the
Dirac operator for
L_{c}
acts on a finite-dimensional
Hilbert space, while the Dirac operator for
L^{0}

given just above still
acts of a Hilbert space of infinite dimension. It is an
interesting question as to whether for finite
X

there is in general
a Dirac operator for
L^{0}
that acts on a Hilbert
space that is finite-dimensional.

9.
The definition of the quantum Hamming metrics

We now combine some of the results obtained above to
reformulate the Hamming metric in terms of the algebra

C(X)
without mentioning points, so that if we drop
the requirement that the algebra be commutative, we
obtain the quantum version, which provides our
definition of the quantum Hamming metric. But then
we will notice that the definition makes sense in a
considerably broader context.

In this
section sets like
X
are finite.
It is helpful to begin by considering the case in which
our index set
I
contains only two elements,
1
and
2
.
We set
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=C(X_{i})
for
i=1,2
,
so that
{\mathcal{A}}=C(X_{1}\times X_{2})=C(X_{1})\otimes C(X_{2})={\mathcal{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
.
Then

L_{1}(f)=\sup\{|f(v_{1},v_{2})-f(w_{1},v_{2})|\}

as
v_{1},w_{1}
range over
X_{1}
and
v_{2}
ranges over
X_{2}
.
Let
f
be
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued. Then by the argument preceding equation
8.1
,
for each
v_{2}
there is an
r_{v_{2}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\sup\{|f(v_{1},v_{2})-f(w_{1},v_{2})|:v_{1},w_{1}\in X_{1}\}=2\|f(\cdot,v_{2})-r_{v_{2}}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\|_{\infty}.

Define
h\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}=C(X_{2})
by
h(v_{2})=r_{v_{2}}
for each
v_{2}\in X_{2}
.
Then
we see that

L_{1}(f)=2\inf\{\|f-1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\otimes k\|:k\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\}=2\|f\|_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}

(because we can use
k=h
),
where
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}
is the pull-back to

{\mathcal{A}}
of the quotient norm on
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
.

Recall that
L_{H}
was provisionally defined shortly
after remark
2.2
.
A proof by induction on the size of
I
then gives:

Theorem 9.1
.

For each
i\in I
let
X_{i}
be a finite set, and let
X=\prod_{i}X_{i}
,
so that
C(X)=\otimes_{i}C(X_{i})
.
For each
i\in I

let
\tilde{X}_{i}=\prod\{X_{j}:j\neq i\}
,
so that
C(X)=C(X_{i})\otimes C(\tilde{X}_{i})
.
Define a seminorm
L_{i}
on
C(X)
by

L_{i}(f)=2\|f\|_{C(X)/C(\tilde{X}_{i})}

for any
f\in C(X)
.
Then for any
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued
f
we have

L_{H}(f)=\max\{L_{i}(f):i\in I\}.

Notice that the
L_{i}
’s above are defined just in terms of the algebras,
with no mention of points. Thus we can drop the assumption that
the algebras are commutative, and take the above formulation as
the
definition
of the quantum Hamming metric.

Definition 9.2
.

For each
i\in I
let
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
be a
matrix algebra
M_{n_{i}}({\mathbb{C}})
, and let
{\mathcal{A}}=\otimes_{i}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
For each
i

let
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\otimes\{{\mathcal{A}}_{j}:j\neq i\}
,
so that
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}_{i}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}

when
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
are viewed as subalgebras
of
{\mathcal{A}}
in the evident way.
Define a
*
-seminorm,
L_{i}
, on
{\mathcal{A}}
by

(9.1)

L_{i}(a)=2\|a\|_{{\mathcal{A}}/\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}=2\inf\{\|a-c\|:c\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\}

for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
Then define a
*
-seminorm,
L_{qH}
, on
{\mathcal{A}}
by

(9.2)

L_{qH}(a)=\max\{L_{i}(a):i\in I\}.

This
L_{qH}
is our
quantum Hamming metric
on
{\mathcal{A}}=\otimes_{i}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.

This definition corresponds to proposition 8 in
[
7
]
, except that
we do not restrict
a
to being self-adjoint. The above definition immediately
makes sense when the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s are arbitrary finite-dimensional
C*-algebras. Even in this slightly more general case
we will see in Section
10
that
L_{qH}
is a C*-metric that
is strongly Leibniz.

But on examining the above definition, we see that it has meaning even
when the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s are arbitrary, perhaps not finite-dimensional,
unital C*-algebras, once we clarify that we
will always use the minimal C*-tensor product
[
17
]
. Without
further comment we will use the fact that the minimal C*-tensor product
is associative and respects sub-algebras and product states
in the ways one would expect, as explained in
section 11.3 of
[
17
]
. We will never need to take quotients
by 2-sided
ideals
, and so we need not be concerned with the
fact that the minimal C*-tensor product does not always work for quotients by ideals
in the ways one would expect. See section 3.7 of
[
4
]
.

It is clear that in this general case
L_{qH}
will
still be a bounded seminorm on
{\mathcal{A}}
. We will see that it
produces a finite metric on
S({\mathcal{A}})
. But unless all the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s
are finite-dimensional, the topology on
S({\mathcal{A}})
from this metric will not
agree with the weak-
*
topology, and will not be compact.
Thus in this setting
we will call
L_{qH}
the “quantum Hamming seminorm”,
reserving the term “quantum Hamming metric” for the case
in which the C*-algebras are finite-dimensional (but not
necessarily just full matrix algebras) so that
L_{qH}
is
a C*-metric, as we will see in the next section.

10.
The diameter of a quantum Hamming seminorm

We seek now a generalization of Proposition
2.3
.
In the process we will obtain further properties of
L_{qH}
.
We continue assuming that the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s
are arbitrary unital C*-algebras,
and that
{\mathcal{A}}
is their tensor product. We define the subalgebras

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
exactly as in
9.1
, and then define the
seminorms
L_{i}
exactly as above. Thus we set

L_{i}(a)=2\|a\|_{{\mathcal{A}}/\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}.

We then define the seminorm

L_{qH}
exactly as above in
9.2
, and we refer to it as a
quantum Hamming seminorm. We need to show that
L_{qH}

is actually a norm not just a seminorm, on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}={\mathcal{A}}/{\mathbb{C}}1_{\mathcal{A}}
,
and that it is equivalent to the norm
L^{0}
.
Since each of the
L_{i}
’s is strongly Leibniz (by theorem 3.2
of
[
28
]
), so is
L_{qH}
(by proposition 1.2 of
[
29
]
).

Our generalization of Proposition
2.3
.
is more complicated than in the commutative case, and the
proof we give is strongly motivated by the proof of proposition 2 of
[
7
]
.
(I thank Eleanor Rieffel for deciphering for me equation 17 of that
proof for
n=2

I
, so we now
take it to be the set of integers between
1
and
n
.
For any

k\geq 2
we set

B_{k}=\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988ptC_{k}=\otimes_{i=k}^{n}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\ ,

so that for each

we have

{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}_{k}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
.
For any linear functional on
{\mathcal{A}}
, say
\psi
,
and for any unital C*-subalgebra
{\mathcal{D}}
of

{\mathcal{A}}
, we denote the restriction of
\psi
to
{\mathcal{D}}
by
\psi_{\mathcal{D}}
,
which is a linear functional on
{\mathcal{D}}
.
To simplify notation we make the following conventions. For each

let

be the identity element of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
, whereas if
K
is a subinterval of
I

of length
p

will denote the tensor product of the

’s for

i\in K
. Whenever it is evident from the context, we will omit mentioning

,
and whenever it should not cause confusion, we will denote the C*-subalgebra

I_{k-1}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\otimes I_{n-k}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
simply by
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
. Then

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=1_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}={\mathcal{B}}_{n}\otimes 1_{n}
,
while
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{k}={\mathcal{B}}_{k}\otimes 1_{k}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}
for

.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be states on
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
\phi=\mu-\nu
.
For each
k
with
k\geq 2

\rho_{k}=\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}\otimes\mu_{{\mathcal{C}}_{k}},

which is a state on
{\mathcal{A}}
,
and for each
k
with
2\leq k\leq n-1
set

\phi_{k}=\rho_{k}-\rho_{k+1}.

Then
\phi_{k}
has the following properties for
each
k
with
2\leq k\leq n-1
:

(1)

The restriction of
\phi_{k}
to

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{k}={\mathcal{B}}_{k}\otimes 1_{k}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}
is the
0 functional.

(2)

The restriction of
\phi_{k}
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}=I_{k-1}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\otimes I_{n-k}
coincides
with
\phi_{{\mathcal{A}}_{k}}
.

Set
\phi_{1}=\mu-\rho_{2}
. Then

(1)

The restriction of
\phi_{1}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=1_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is the
0 functional.

(2)

The restriction of
\phi_{1}
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}={\mathcal{A}}_{1}\otimes I_{n-1}
coincides
with
\phi_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}
.

Set
\phi_{n}=\rho_{n}-\nu
. Then

(1)

The restriction of
\phi_{n}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}={\mathcal{B}}_{n}\otimes 1_{n}
is the
0 functional.

(2)

The restriction of
\phi_{n}
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}=I_{n-1}\otimes{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
coincides
with
\phi_{{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}
.

Furthermore,
\phi=\sum\phi_{k}
(a “telescoping sum”).

Proof.

Let
k
with
2\leq k\leq n-1
be given. Then for any
b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}

and
c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}
we have

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(b\otimes 1_{k}\otimes c)

\displaystyle=\rho_{k}(b\otimes 1_{k}\otimes c)-\rho_{k+1}(b\otimes 1_{k}\otimes c)

\displaystyle=\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}(b)\mu_{{\mathcal{C}}_{k}}(1_{k}\otimes c)-\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}}(b\otimes 1_{k})\mu_{{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}}(c)

\displaystyle=0.

But for any

we have

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(I_{k-1}\otimes a

\displaystyle\otimes I_{n-k})=\rho_{k}(I_{k-1}\otimes a\otimes I_{n-k})-\rho_{k+1}(I_{k-1}\otimes a\otimes I_{n-k})

\displaystyle=\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}(I_{k-1})\mu_{{\mathcal{C}}_{k}}(a\otimes I_{n-k})-\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}}(I_{k-1}\otimes a)\mu_{{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}}(I_{n-k})

\displaystyle=\phi(a).

The proofs for the other two cases are similar.
∎

Much as earlier,
for each
a\in{\mathcal{A}}

we set
L^{0}(a)=\inf\|a-z1_{\mathcal{A}}\|
, where

z
ranges over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then note that just from its definition,

L_{i}(a)\leq 2L^{0}(a)
for each

, so that
L_{qH}(a)\leq 2L^{0}(a)
.

Theorem 10.2
.

Let notation be as above. Then for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

|\phi_{k}(a)|\leq L_{k}(a)
. If also
a^{*}=a
, then

2L^{0}(a)\leq\sum_{1}^{n}L_{k}(a)\leq nL_{qH}(a)\leq 2nL^{0}(a).

In particular, it follows that
L_{qH}
is a norm on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
,
and that the norms
L_{qH}
and
L^{0}
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}

are equivalent. Furthermore, the diameter of
({\mathcal{A}},L_{qH})
is

\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be states of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let

\phi=\mu-\nu
. We apply Lemma
10.1

to this
\phi
.
Notice that from the definition of the
\phi_{k}
’s in
Lemma
10.1
,
\|\phi_{k}\|\leq 2
for each
k
.
We also see that

\phi_{k}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{k})=0
for each
k
.
Thus for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, if
d\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
then

|\phi_{k}(a)|=|\phi_{k}(a-d)|\leq 2\|a-d\|.

On taking the infimum over
d
’s on the right side, we find that

|\phi_{k}(a)|\leq L_{k}(a)
, as desired.
Since also
L_{k}(a)\leq L_{qH}(a)\leq 2L^{0}(a)
, we obtain

\sum_{1}^{n}|\phi_{k}(a)|\leq\sum_{1}^{n}L_{k}(a)\leq nL_{qH}(a)\leq 2nL^{0}(a).

Now assume that
a^{*}=a
, and let
\mu
and
\nu

be states of
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(a)

and
\nu(a)
are the maximum and minimum values of
the spectrum of
a
, so that
2L^{0}(a)=(\mu-\nu)(a)
.
Then for
\phi=\mu-\nu
, we
have
\phi(a)=2L^{0}(a)
. Since
\phi=\sum\phi_{k}
, it
follows that
2L^{0}(a)\leq\sum_{1}^{n}|\phi_{k}(a)|
,
so that
2L^{0}(a)\leq\sum_{1}^{n}L_{k}(a)

as needed.

We see that the norms
L_{qH}
and
L^{0}
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}

are equivalent on the self-adjoint part of
{\mathcal{A}}
, but this inplies
that they are equivalent on all of
{\mathcal{A}}
(with slightly different
constants). The fact that
2L^{0}(a)\leq nL_{qH}(a)
when
a^{*}=a

says that the radius of
L_{qH}
is no more than
n/2
,
according to Definitiion
4.3
. Thus
the diameter of
({\mathcal{A}},L_{qH})
is no more than
n
, generalizing what
happens in the commutative case as seen from Propositions

2.3
and
4.4
.
∎

The following theorem also generalizes what
happens in the commutative case.

Theorem 10.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
as above, but assume that none
of the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s are 1-dimensional, i.e. isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
the diameter of
({\mathcal{A}},L_{qH})
is
n
.

Proof.

Since none of the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s is 1-dimensional, we can choose for
each
i
a self-adjoint element
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}

\|a_{i}\|=1
,
and both +1 and -1 are in its spectrum. Let
\mu_{i}
and
\nu_{i}

be states on
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
such that
\mu_{i}(a_{i})=1
and
\nu(a_{i})=-1
.
Let

c_{1}=a_{1}\otimes I_{n-1},\ \ c_{2}=I_{1}\otimes a_{2}\otimes I_{n-2},\ \ \dots,\ \ c_{n}=I_{n-1}\otimes a_{n},

so that each

is in the subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Notice that
L_{i}(c_{i})\leq 1
for each

since
\|c_{i}\|=1
.
Let
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
. Then
L_{i}(a)\leq 1
for each

since
each

is in the subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Thus
L_{qH}(a)\leq 1
.

Let
\mu=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}
and
\nu=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}
,

{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
\mu_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}=\mu_{i}
and

\nu_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}=\nu_{i}
, so that
\mu_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}(a_{i})=1
and

\nu_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}(a_{i})=-1
, for each

.
Set
\phi=\mu-\nu
, and define
the

’s as in the above Lemma. Then by the properties stated

so that

. Since

, and

,

. Then from Propositiion

11.
The Kantorovich-Wasserstein metric on quantum Hamming states

We continue to take the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s to be
arbitrary unital C*-algebras, and as above we set

{\mathcal{A}}=\otimes_{i}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
We equip
{\mathcal{A}}
with its quantum Hamming seminorm

L_{qH}
defined above.
We will now use Proposition
6.1
to
express the corresponding ordinary metric on
S({\mathcal{A}})

from
L_{qH}
in terms of the
L_{i}
’s, where
L_{i}
is still defined
by equation
9.1
.
View
L_{qH}
as a norm
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
.
On
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
we also have the norm
L^{0}
, but as seen in
Theorem
10.2
these two norms are equivalent, and so define
the same topology on
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
.
Recall that we denote the dual space of

{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
by
M^{0}({\mathcal{A}})
.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{qH}
be the dual
norm on
M^{0}({\mathcal{A}})
determined by
L_{qH}

much as in equation (
5.1
), that is

\|\phi\|_{qH}=\sup\{|\phi(a)|:L_{qH}(a)\leq 1\},

where our notation does not indicate whether we view

a
as an element of
{\mathcal{A}}
or of
{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
.
For every
i\in I

we can view
L_{i}
as a norm on
{\mathcal{A}}/\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
The dual space of
{\mathcal{A}}/\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
can be identified with
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp}
,
which is just the subspace of
M^{0}({\mathcal{A}})
consisting of functionals
that take value 0 on
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
.
We denote the norm on
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp}
that is
dual to
L_{i}
by
\|\cdot\|_{i}
.
Since
L_{qH}(a)=\max\{L_{i}(a):i\in I\}
by definition,
we are in position to apply Proposition
6.1

to obtain:

Theorem 11.1
.

Let notation be as above. Then for any
\phi\in M^{0}({\mathcal{A}})
we have

\|\phi\|_{qH}=\inf\{\sum_{i}\|\phi_{i}\|_{i}:\phi_{i}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp}\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \phi=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}\}.

For any
\phi\in M^{0}({\mathcal{A}})
define
\phi^{*}
by
\phi^{*}(a)=\overline{\phi(a^{*})}
.
Note that
\|\phi^{*}\|_{qH}=\|\phi\|_{qH}
,
so that
\|(\phi+\phi^{*})/2\|_{qH}\leq\|\phi\|_{qH}
.
Also, if
\phi\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp}

then
\phi^{*}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp}
.
Furthermore, if
\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
and we set

\phi=\mu-\nu
then
\phi^{*}=\phi
.
Consequently, we obtain:

Corollary 11.2
.

Let notation be as above. Then
for any
\mu,\nu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
we have

d_{qH}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\{\sum_{i}\|\phi_{i}\|_{i}:\phi_{i}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{\perp},\ \phi_{i}^{*}=\phi_{i},\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \mu-\nu=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}\}.

For the case in which the
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
’s are full matrix algebras
this is basically the expression that De Palma et al. take, in definition 7
of
[
7
]
, as their
definition
of their
Wasserstein-1
metric on states, which corresponds to the
the quantum Hamming distance,
though they express it in terms of density matrices.

It is an interesting challenge to find efficient methods for calculating
this distance between states. The most common situation in which
this distance is
used is that in which each
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
is
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
(a “qbit”) but the size
of
I
can be quite large, e.g.
|I|=500
.

12.
Dirac operators for quantum Hamming seminorms

In this section we will assume that the C*-algebras that
we deal with are finite-dimensional. There are generalizations
of some of the results we give here to the case of infinite dimensional
C*-algebras
[
28
]
, but they are more technical,
and so we do not include them here.

Thus let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a finite dimensional C*-algebra, necessarily
unital, and let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a C*-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
with

1_{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Let
L_{q}
be the pull-back to
{\mathcal{A}}
of
the quotient norm on
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{B}}
. We seek to represent

L_{q}
by a Dirac-type operator. We can construct one
by generalizing the construction used for Propositiion

8.2
.

Let
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
be a finite dimensional faithful
*
-representation
of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and view
{\mathcal{A}}
as a subalgebra of the algebra

L^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}})
of all operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
, with the operator norm.
Notice that the distance from an element
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}

is independent of whether
a
is viewed as an element of

{\mathcal{A}}
or of
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}})
. Thus we can, and we will, take

{\mathcal{A}}
to be all of
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}})
.

Then we let
\tau
be the unique
tracial state on
{\mathcal{A}}=L^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}})
, and in the usual way
we define an inner product,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\tau}
, on
{\mathcal{A}}
,
taken to be linear in the second variable, so that

\langle\xi,\eta\rangle_{\tau}=\tau(\xi^{*}\eta\rangle
for all
\xi,\eta\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We denote the corresponding Hilbert-space
norm by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.
(With more complicated bookkeeping one
can obtain versions of many of the results below if

\tau
is replaced by an arbitrary faithful state on
{\mathcal{A}}
.)

Let
a
be any element of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Much as in the proof
of Proposition
8.2
, the first basic result of linear
approximation theory tells us that there is a linear
functional,
\phi
, on
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\|\phi\|=1

and
L_{q}(a)=\phi(a)

while
\phi(b)=0
for all
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
.
Since
{\mathcal{A}}
is finite dimensional,
\phi
is
continuous for the trace-norm, and so is represented
by an element of
{\mathcal{A}}
, which we will denote
by
f_{\phi}
. With that understanding,

\phi(c)=\langle f_{\phi},c\rangle_{\tau}=\tau(f_{\phi}^{*}c)

for all
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Since
\|\phi\|=1
, we have
\|f_{\phi}\|_{\tau}=1
,
where
\|\cdot\|_{\tau}
is the trace-norm. Let
f_{\phi}=u|f_{\phi}|
be
the polar decomposition of
f_{\phi}
, so that
u
is a partial isometry
on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Let
\xi=|f_{\phi}|^{1/2}
and
\eta=u|f_{\phi}|^{1/2}
, and notice
that
\xi^{*}=\xi
. Notice also that
\|\xi\|_{2}=1=\|\eta\|_{2}
.
Most important, for any
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

\phi(c)=\tau(f_{\phi}^{*}c)=\tau(\xi\eta^{*}c)=\langle\eta,c\xi\rangle_{\tau}.

Since
\phi(b)=0
for all
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
, we have

\langle\eta,b\xi\rangle_{\tau}=0
for all
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Let
P

be the projection onto the subspace
{\mathcal{B}}\eta
. Since this
subspace is clearly invariant under the (left) action of

{\mathcal{B}}
, the projection
P
commutes with this action.
That is,
P
is in the commutant of the action by
{\mathcal{B}}
.
It is also clear that we have
P\eta=\eta
while
P\xi=0
.
Consequently, for any
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

\langle\eta,[P,c]\xi\rangle_{\tau}=\langle\eta,Pc\xi\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\eta,cP\xi\rangle_{\tau}=\langle\eta,c\xi\rangle_{\tau}=\phi(c).

In particular, we have

L_{q}(a)=\phi(a)=\langle\eta,[P,a]\xi\rangle_{\tau}.

Consequently,
L_{q}(a)\leq\|[P,a]\|
. But for any
c\in{\mathcal{A}}

and
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
we have, since
P
commutes with the
action of
{\mathcal{B}}
,

\|[P,c]\|=\|[P,c-b]\|=\|[P-(1/2)I,c-b]\|\leq\|c-b\|.

It follows that
\|[P,c]\|\leq L_{q}(c)
for all
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
and so, in particular,

L_{q}(a)=\|[P,a]\|.

We see that we have obtained the following non-commutative
version of Proposition
8.2
.

Theorem 12.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=L^{\infty}({\mathcal{H}})
for a finite-dimensional Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}
, and let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a C*-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
that contains

1_{\mathcal{A}}
. Let
L_{q}
be the pull-back to
{\mathcal{A}}
of the quotient norm
on
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{B}}
. Then for every
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

L_{q}(a)=\sup\{\|[P,a]\|:P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}\}

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the set of projections in the
commutant of
{\mathcal{B}}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
of
rank no greater than
\dim({\mathcal{B}})
.

This theorem is related to proposition 2.1 of
[
5
]
,
which, as explained there, is related to the Arveson
distance formula for nest algebras in
[
2
]
.

To obtain a single Dirac-type operator for the situation,
for each
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}
let
{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
be a copy of

{\mathcal{H}}
, with the same representation of
{\mathcal{A}}
on
it, and let
{\mathcal{K}}=\oplus\{{\mathcal{H}}_{P}:P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}\}
, with
corresponding direct-sum representation
\pi
of
{\mathcal{A}}

on
{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
D=\oplus\{P:P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}\}
,
a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{K}}
. Then we clearly have

L_{q}(a)=\|[D,\pi(a)]\|

for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Alternatively,
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{B}}
, with metric from
the operator norm, is a compact space, and we can choose
a Borel measure of full support on it, and, much as in
the comments after the proof of Proposition
8.2
, we can
then form the Hilbert space of
{\mathcal{H}}
-valued functions on
it. We then let
D
