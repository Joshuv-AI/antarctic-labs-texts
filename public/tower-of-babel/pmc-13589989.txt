Burden of Treatment and Disease on People with Hemophilia: Global Real-World Survey
Authors: Giancarlo Castaman, Cléa Percier, Naveen Shridhar, Neil Reynolds, Olivera Rajkovic-Hooley, Thomas Dewar, Victor Jiménez-Yuste
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlthough prophylactic treatments can reduce bleeding risk for people with hemophilia (PwH), burdens associated with treatment and physical and psychological symptoms may persist.AimTo determine treatment and disease burden associated with hemophilia from a global perspective.MethodsThis cross-sectional patient survey captured patient-reported outcomes, experiences and clinical data from PwH in eight countries between December 2023 and March 2024. Treatment burden was measured using the child/adult Hemophilia Treatment Experience Measure (Hemo-TEM), validated questionnaires covering five domains. Disease burden was measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29+2 version 2.1 (PROMIS-29+2)/PROMIS Parent Proxy, validated questionnaires comprising eight domains. Here we report data on treatment, physical and psychological burdens for people with moderate and severe hemophilia without inhibitors by treatment class: standard half-life and extended half-life factor replacement for hemophilia A (HA) and hemophilia B (HB), and non-factor therapy for HA.ResultsData from 495 PwH and parents/guardians of PwH were analyzed. Across the five Hemo-TEM domains, adults with HA experienced similar degrees of treatment burden regardless of treatment class, with comparable results observed for adults with HB. Around one-third of PwH experienced soreness (33% HA; 31% HB) and pain (31% HA; 26% HB) due to their current treatment at least sometimes, and one in five experienced some level of treatment bother. PROMIS-29+2 scores indicated that people with HA and HB experienced similar degrees of physical and psychological burden regardless of treatment class. Anxiety and worry were prevalent among PwH.ConclusionPwH continue to experience treatment- and disease-related burdens. Managing hemophilia still requires a balance between bleed protection, quality of life and ease of treatment administration.

Introduction

Treatment advances have improved bleeding management, but people with hemophilia (PwH) continue to experience burdens associated with the disease and treatment administration, affecting joint health, physical well-being, and emotional, psychological and social well-being.1–4 The chronic nature of hemophilia, fear of spontaneous bleeds and lifelong management contribute to mental health challenges.2 Pain, anxiety and depression are common with PwH and physicians reporting negative effects on quality of life (QoL) of PwH.5

Current therapies for hemophilia include clotting factor replacement therapy available as standard half-life (SHL) and extended half-life (EHL) products.6,7 Effective factor replacement does present challenges, with some PwH developing inhibitory antibodies that neutralize hemostatic efficacy.7,8 The need for frequent intravenous injections can damage blood vessels, leading to venous access issues and poor adherence, further contributing to the overall burden of living with hemophilia.8 Newer non-factor therapies (NFT) offer advantages over traditional factor replacement by means of subcutaneous administration with reduced dosing frequency; however, syringe and vial formulations can make injection preparation and administration laborious.9 PwH favor treatments that enable a more active lifestyle and seek therapies that are not only effective but also convenient and less intrusive,10,11 highlighting an ongoing need for treatments that align with their daily lives.

Multinational real-world studies have documented persistent treatment-related and broader clinical and humanistic burdens among PwH receiving contemporary care, including prophylaxis.12,13 However, direct patient-centered evaluations of burden across SHL, EHL and non-factor prophylaxis using common validated measures remain limited.14,15 Treatment-specific measures such as Hemophilia Treatment Experience Measure (Hemo-TEM) and generic health-status measures such as Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29+2 version 2.1 (PROMIS-29+2) provide complementary insights into treatment experience and the wider physical, psychological and social effects reported by patients, capturing aspects of burden that may not be reflected by conventional clinical outcomes such as bleeding rates alone.14,16

To assess the global burden of hemophilia A (HA) and hemophilia B (HB) on PwH receiving prophylactic treatment, this multinational study used a patient survey, incorporating validated patient-reported outcome (PRO) scales to evaluate both treatment burden and disease burden experienced by patients with moderate and severe hemophilia without inhibitors, and their parents/guardians.

Methods

Study Design

This non-interventional, cross-sectional, multinational, real-world patient survey captured PROs, patient experiences, and clinical data directly from PwH and parents/guardians of PwH. Participants were recruited using a mixed approach: referrals from treating healthcare professionals, social media, online panels and, in some countries, patient advocacy groups (PAGs).

PwH and parents/guardians of PwH from eight countries – Canada, France, Germany, India, Italy, Saudi Arabia, Spain and the USA – completed a 30-minute online survey between December 2023 and March 2024. Surveys were distributed via third-party processes to preserve participant anonymity and prevent direct contact with the research organization. Data were collected via online patient survey links, and were self-reported or collected by proxy, dependent on patient age, with proxies being the parent/guardian for those younger than 18 years. Respondent data (gender, location, age) were cross-referenced to prevent duplication. Surveys were administered in the local language of each participating country. Validated local language versions of the Hemo-TEM and PROMIS-29+2/PROMIS Parent Proxy instruments were used.

Participants were asked to report baseline hemophilia severity at diagnosis per International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis definitions:7 mild: >5% to <40% factor level, moderate: 1–5% factor level, and severe: <1% factor level.

Participation was voluntary with modest compensation, and participants could withdraw consent at any time. All patients provided informed consent. Participants younger than 18 years provided assent following informed consent from their parent/guardian. For those below the age of adolescence, proxy consent and survey were completed by their parent/guardian. Parents/guardians provided information for only one associated participant younger than 18 years included in the survey.

Population and Sample

For participation in the survey, participants were required to have a confirmed diagnosis of HA or HB, with or without inhibitors. Physician-recruited participants were assumed to have a confirmed hemophilia diagnosis. Participants recruited outside physician referral pathways (eg, PAGs) were required to provide evidence supporting their diagnosis (eg, medication packaging, prescription documentation or clinic appointment records) or complete structured confirmation questions during screening. The third alternative was that the participant did not have a confirmed diagnosis of hemophilia, at which point the potential participant was screened out and did not complete the questionnaire.

Patients with HA were required to be receiving prophylactic treatment for at least 6 months. Patients with HB could receive prophylactic treatment and/or on-demand treatment as part of core treatment regimen. All participants had to live in one of the eight survey countries.

Exclusion criteria included previous participation in this study, mental incapacity, mental unwillingness or language barrier. PwH currently participating in a clinical trial, or receiving an investigative treatment, immune tolerance induction or compassionate use were excluded. Informed consent was obtained electronically through a secure e-consent process before participants accessed the online survey.

Outcome Measures

Treatment burden was measured using the child or adult Hemo-TEM, validated questionnaires comprising five domains:14 injection difficulties, physical impact, treatment bother, interference and emotional impact (Supplementary Figure 1). Scores ranged from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating greater treatment burden.

Disease burden was measured using the PROMIS-29+2 or PROMIS Parent Proxy tool, validated questionnaires comprising eight domains:16 physical function, fatigue, anxiety, social roles and activities participation, sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms, pain interference and pain intensity, and two questions on cognitive function (Supplementary Figure 2). Adults aged 18 years and older completed PROMIS-29+2, whereas parents/guardians completed PROMIS Parent Proxy Profile-25 for younger participants. Scores for each domain ranged from 0 to 100. Pain intensity was measured using a separate 0–10-point scale. Greater disease burden is indicated by lower scores for physical, social and cognitive domains, and higher scores for fatigue, anxiety, sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms, pain interference and pain intensity. Participants were also asked two independent questions on anxiety and worry regarding bleeding while on treatment (Supplementary Figure 3). Frequency of bleeds were self-reported by PwH or their parent/guardian.

Statistical Methods

Patients with inhibitors were included in the survey; however, because of their small numbers and differing treatment pathways, this manuscript reports analyses and results only for patients without inhibitors. Similarly, although patients with HB receiving on-demand treatment were eligible for recruitment into this study to reflect routine clinical practice, the present analysis was restricted to participants receiving prophylaxis. The number of participants receiving on-demand treatment was small and therefore insufficient to support meaningful subgroup analyses. As this was an exploratory cross-sectional analysis designed to describe treatment and disease burden in a real-world population, no formal sample size calculation or statistical power analysis was undertaken.

Results are reported for PwH without inhibitors by disease severity at diagnosis (moderate and severe) and treatment class: SHL and EHL replacement therapies for factor VIII in HA or factor IX in HB, and NFT in HA. Hemo-TEM and PROMIS-29+2 scores are reported using median and interquartile range for each domain. PROMIS-29+2 scores were transformed into T-scores using a reference sample from the general population with a mean of 50. Specific individual questions within the Hemo-TEM treatment bother and physical impact domains and the most affected PROMIS-29+2 domains overall – physical functioning, sleep disturbance and pain interference – were also assessed. Individual domain questions and independent questions on anxiety and worry are reported as proportions of participants with specified responses. All data were processed using IBM® SPSS® Data Collection Survey Reporter v7.5 software.

Subgroup age split for Hemo-TEM and PROMIS for “child” was: 1–6, 7–12, 13–17 and “adult” 18–25, 26–35, 36–45, 46–55, 56+ years; for treatment anxiety and worry, subgroup age split was 0–12, 13–17, 18+, and for education hindrance/progression 0–6, 7–12, 13–17, 18–30, 31–50, 51+ years.

No inferential statistical comparisons were conducted. Continuous variables are summarized using medians and interquartile ranges, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Demographics

From a total of 495 PwH (390 with HA and 105 with HB) who met the inclusion criteria for the survey (Figure 1), there were 410 PwH without inhibitors (HA=320; HB=90), of whom 389 received prophylaxis (HA=320; HB=69). Among those with HA who were on prophylaxis (Table 1), 315 had known clinical severity at diagnosis, 229 (73%) of whom had severe disease, 70 (22%) had moderate disease, and 16 (5%) had mild disease. Of the 69 people with HB who were on prophylaxis, 68 had known clinical severity, 46 (68%) of whom had severe disease, 15 (22%) had moderate disease and 7 (10%) had mild disease. For HA, 305 people reported taking known prophylactic treatments: 108/305 (35%) used SHL products, 88/305 (29%) used EHL products and 102/305 (33%) used NFT. For HB, 67 people reported taking known prophylactic treatments: 18/67 (27%) used SHL products and 49/67 (73%) used EHL products. For the treatment burden, physical and psychological burden, and frequency of bleeds analyses, only patients with moderate and severe disease were included, due to the low numbers of patients with mild disease. Treatment Burden

Across the five Hemo-TEM domains, treatment burden was comparable among adults with HA, regardless of moderate or severe disease severity and treatment class (Figure 2A). For adults with severe HA, burden appeared to be greatest among people taking SHL products and lowest among those taking NFT, particularly for the interference domain. Treatment burden was also comparable between SHL and EHL products for adults with severe HB (Figure 2B). When median Hemo-TEM scores were assessed by age, treatment burden appeared to be greater for young adults aged 18–25 years with moderate/severe HA, compared with other age categories, across injection difficulties, physical impact, treatment bother and pain interference domains. Emotional impact was reported by all age groups. Results were similar for people with HB (Supplementary Figure 4).

Physical and Psychological Burden

PROMIS-29+2 scores indicated that disease burden was comparable for adults with moderate and severe HA and by treatment class (Figure 5A). Adults with moderate and severe HA reported the greatest burden for the domains of physical functioning, sleep disturbance and pain interference. Among adults with severe HA, 42/144 (29%) reported at least some difficulty (some difficulty, much difficulty or unable to do) when undertaking household chores, 53/144 (37%) reported at least some problems (somewhat, quite a bit or very much) with sleep, and 64/144 (44%) reported that pain interfered with day-to-day activities (somewhat, quite a bit or very much). Among adults with moderate HA, 13/42 (31%) reported difficulty with household chores, 9/42 (21%) reported problems with sleep and 13/42 (31%) reported that pain interfered with day-to-day activities. Like adults with HA, adults with severe HB reported the greatest degree of burden in the domains of physical functioning, sleep disturbance and pain interference (Figure 5B).

Among all people with severe HA taking SHL products, 23/59 (39%) reported feeling anxious (sometimes, often or always) and 20/59 (34%) reported feeling worried that their treatment might not adequately protect them from bleeds. Protection from bleeding while on treatment remained a concern among patients with moderate/severe HB: 7/15 (47%) of those on SHL and 10/44 (23%) of those on EHL reported being anxious, and 7/15 (47%) and 13/44 (30%), respectively, reported being worried at least sometimes.

When median PROMIS-29 scores were assessed by age, disease burden was comparable across all age groups for adults with moderate/severe HA and HB (Supplementary Figure 5). The greatest burden on physical functioning appeared to be experienced by older participants (36–45, 45–55 and ≥56 years).

Frequency of Bleeds Reported by PwH or Their Parent/Guardian

Bleed data were self-reported by PwH or parents/guardians of PwH, with only 61% of participants keeping a bleed diary. Mean numbers of bleeds in the last 12 months reported by people with severe HA were 6.8 (standard deviation [SD] 6.1) for those on SHL products, 6.6 (SD 9.7) for those on EHL products and 2.3 (SD 4.2) for those on NFT, and for people with moderate HA were 7.4 (SD 6.3), 7.7 (SD 10.3) and 2.7 (SD 1.7), respectively. The mean proportion of bleeds not reported to physicians by patients with severe HA on SHL products (n=54) was 20%, EHL products (n=52) was 17% and NFT (n=57) was 17%. For patients with moderate HA, this was 14% (n=35), 5% (n=22) and 35% (n=6), respectively.

Mean numbers of bleeds in the last 12 months reported by people with severe HB were 3.2 (SD 2.8) for those on SHL products and 4.8 (SD 5.8) for those on EHL products, and for people with moderate HB were 16.2 (SD 19.7) and 2.4 (SD 2.5), respectively. The mean proportion of bleeds not reported to physicians by patients with severe HB on SHL products (n=7) was 1% and EHL products (n=31) was 21%. For those with moderate HB this was 11% for both SHL products (n=5) and EHL products (n=6).

Discussion

This international real-world survey indicates that individuals with HA and HB without inhibitors experience treatment burden associated with prophylactic therapies, particularly with older SHL factor replacement therapies. Participants receiving NFT generally reported lower burden scores than those receiving factor replacement therapies. Despite prophylaxis, PwH still reported disease-related challenges, including impaired physical function, sleep disturbance, pain interference, and anxiety and worry about bleed protection. Observations based on the findings of this analysis are descriptive and should not be interpreted as evidence of comparative treatment effectiveness. However, our findings, from a survey of participants from eight countries, are consistent with recent real-world evidence from the United States, which similarly demonstrated that PwH experience persistent treatment and disease burden across multiple domains.4

Treatment Burden

Survey responses indicated treatment burden across all Hemo-TEM domains, including injection difficulties, physical impact of the injection, treatment bother, interference with activities and emotional impact.

Despite reduced infusion frequency with EHL factor replacement therapy, this remains burdensome for those with severe HA, with almost one in five patients reporting soreness and pain associated with injections. Even among patients receiving NFT, involving treatment that is administered subcutaneously every 1–4 weeks, burdens persist, including painful injections, bothersome storage and complicated preparation steps for dosing regimens.

In the smaller cohort of people with HB, treatment burden persisted with both SHL and EHL factor therapy. EHL products may be less burdensome for people with moderate disease owing to reduced infusion frequency and appeared to interfere less with daily activities than SHL products. Other than Canada, at the time that the survey was conducted, prophylactic treatment for patients with HB was limited to factor replacement. The availability of newer therapies for HB may provide opportunities to reduce treatment burden through less frequent administration; however, further research is required to determine whether this translates into improvements in patient activity and quality of life.

These findings suggest that many participants continue to balance the perceived benefits of prophylaxis against the practical demands associated with long-term treatment administration. This includes limitations on physical activity, restriction of daily activities, and interference with work or education. Patients express a preference for treatments that support a more active lifestyle, highlighting the necessity for patient-friendly options that minimize restrictions on daily activities.10 Younger adults aged 18–24 years reported higher levels of treatment burden, suggesting that current treatments may not always align with the lifestyle demands of this age group.

The psychological toll of treatment, including anxiety about bleeding while on treatment and concerns about treatment efficacy, also plays a role. PwH and their caregivers may accept clinical risks, such as spontaneous bleeding or hospitalization, in exchange for improvements in lifestyle,10 emphasizing the importance of patient-centered treatment options to avoid compromises associated with long-term prophylaxis.

Burden of Disease

Hemophilia was reported to impact physical and psychological aspects of life, including physical functioning, social roles and activities, cognitive function, anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and pain interference and intensity. The impact of the disease – particularly impaired physical functioning and pain that interfered with the ability to carry out day-to-day activities and likely contributed to the reported sleep disturbances – is notable given that participants were receiving prophylactic treatment. Although adults reported similar disease burden across treatment types, those older than 45 years experienced greater physical impairment, likely due to inadequate treatment access earlier in life. This highlights the importance of early initiation of effective therapy to prevent long-term complications such as joint damage.

Although PwH receiving NFT reported fewer bleeds than those taking factor therapies, some remained anxious or worried about therapy effectiveness for preventing bleeds. This indicates that newer, more effective therapies do not fully eliminate emotional and psychological burden about bleed protection. Enhancing patients’ overall QoL requires a holistic approach that not only reduces bleeding episodes and alleviates anxiety, but also strives for the ideal of zero bleeds, giving patients the confidence to lead a more active lifestyle.

Limitations

Although this real-world study included broad inclusion criteria to recruit a representative cohort of PwH by age and disease severity, due to low patient numbers particularly for HB, which is less prevalent than HA, subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. Patients with mild disease, those with inhibitors, and patients receiving on-demand treatment were not included in the analyses, which may limit the generalizability of findings to the broader hemophilia population. The population is reflective of real-world consulting patterns from patients under routine clinical care. The multinational design included patients from eight countries with differing healthcare systems and treatment access; as heterogeneity across countries was not formally accounted for, results should not be interpreted as reflecting uniform standards of care. Analyses were descriptive in nature and did not adjust for potential confounding factors including age, country, treatment duration or socioeconomic characteristics; therefore, observed differences between treatment groups should be interpreted with caution. For participants recruited via patients’ organizations, social media and online panels, hemophilia diagnosis was self-reported but supported by screening evidence to avoid enrolment of people without hemophilia. The proportion of participants verified by providing evidence or completing structured confirmation questions was not collected. This mixed recruitment strategy, including physician referral, PAGs, social media and online panels, may have introduced selection bias and may not fully represent the wider hemophilia population. Annual bleeding rates reported in this study are not comparable with those typically observed in clinical trials and standard studies utilizing electronic medical records, as bleeds were self-reported, the need for treatment was unconfirmed, and they may be subject to recall bias or incomplete reporting. As with all surveys, limitations associated with data collection include the potential for responder bias, reliance on accurate recall and survey completion, interpretation of questions and accuracy of translations. Due to the cross-sectional study design, temporal changes in treatment burden and causal relationships between treatment and PROs cannot be inferred.

Conclusions

In PwH, treatment- and disease-related burden persisted across treatment classes despite prophylaxis, highlighting ongoing unmet patient needs. Although they are effective, current treatments still do not meet all the needs of PwH and not all patients have access to newer treatments. Future research should focus on innovative therapies that simplify treatment regimens, which can be easily incorporated into the daily routines of PwH, enabling them to lead more active and fulfilling lives. In addition to developing innovative therapies, increasing access to currently available optimal treatment options will help ensure that patients can avoid long-term complications of hemophilia.
