Cutoff for geodesic paths on hyperbolic manifolds
Charles Bordenave Joffrey Mathien

Abstract
We establish new instances of the cutoff phenomenon for geodesic paths and for the Brownian motion on compact hyperbolic manifolds. We prove that for any fixed compact hyperbolic manifold, the geodesic path started on a spatially localized initial condition exhibits cutoff. Our work also extends results obtained by Golubev and Kamber on hyperbolic surfaces of large volume to any dimension. Our proof relies on a spectral strategy introduced by Lubetzky and Peres for Ramanujan graphs and on a detailed spectral analysis of the spherical mean operator.

Introduction

Cutoff Phenomenon

For an ergodic dynamical system, the cutoff describes an abrupt transition to equilibrium. Historically, it was introduced in seminal works of Diaconis, Shahshahani and Aldous
[
DS81
,
Ald83
,
AD86
]
for card shuffling and other random walks on finite groups.
There are now numerous examples of Markov chains and Markov processes where cutoff has been established, see
[
Dia96
,
LP17
]
but despite some recent efforts such as
[
CSC08
,
BHP17
,
BL22
,
Sal24
]
, a satisfactory theory of cutoff is however still missing. Also, most of current examples are on finite spaces with some exceptions such as
[
CSC08
,
Mél14
,
GK19
,
BP20
,
BCL22
]
. In this paper, we study cutoff for classical processes on compact hyperbolic manifolds and we extend a spectral strategy which has been introduced in
[
LP16
]
and further developed in
[
GK19
,
LLP20
,
BL22
,
GK22
]
.

The formal definition of cutoff that we shall use is as follows. Let
(X^{\delta},\mathcal{B}^{\delta},\pi^{\delta})
be a sequence of probability spaces indexed by a parameter
\delta\in\mathcal{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathds{R}
. Let
\delta_{\star}
be an accumulation point of
\mathcal{D}
. For example, for a card shuffling problem, we may take
\delta=n
where
n
is the number of cards in the deck,
\delta_{\star}=\infty
,
X^{\delta}
is the symmetric group of order
n
and
\pi^{\delta}
is the uniform (or Haar) probability measure on
X^{\delta}
. Next, for each
\delta\in\mathcal{D}
, we consider a sequence of probability measures
(\mu^{\delta}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, indexed by a real or integer
t\geq 0
. In the card shuffling example, for integer
t\geq 0
,
\mu^{\delta}_{t}
would be the law of the deck of cards after
t
shuffles. We assume that
(\mu^{\delta}_{t})
converges to equilibrium, that is for all
\delta\in\mathcal{D}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}^{\delta}=\pi^{\delta},

(1.1)

where the convergence is in total variation : for any probability measures
\mu,\nu
on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{B})
, their distance in total variation is

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|\mu(B)-\nu(B)|=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}(x)-1\right|d\nu(x)\in[0,1].

(1.2)

We have
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=1
if and only
\mu
and
\nu
are mutually singular. To define cutoff, we consider an initial condition
\mu^{\delta}_{0}
which is nearly singular with respect to
\pi^{\delta}
:

\lim_{\delta\to\delta_{\star}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{0},\pi^{\delta})=1.

In the card shuffling example, the initial condition will usually be deterministic :
\mu_{0}^{\delta}
is a Dirac mass at a given permutation of cards. Then, the cutoff occurs when the map
t\to\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{t},\pi^{\delta})
becomes close to a step function as
\delta\to\delta_{\star}
.

Definition 1.1
.

(Cutoff)
The sequence of measures
\mu_{t}^{\delta}
exhibits cutoff if there exists a sequence
(t_{\star}^{\delta})_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}}
in
\mathds{R}^{+}
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{\delta\to\delta_{\star}}\inf_{t\leq(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}^{\delta}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{t},\pi^{\delta})=1\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\lim_{\delta\to\delta_{\star}}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}^{\delta}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{t},\pi^{\delta})=0.

We then say that the cutoff occurs at time
t_{\star}^{\delta}
.

For Markov chains, this definition of cutoff turns out to be equivalent to the usual definition of cutoff with mixing times (which we have skipped here), see
[
LP17
]
. However, we retain the name
mixing time
for
t_{\star}^{\delta}
. The choice of the total variation distance is the most natural from a probabilistic perspective and our definition is tuned for this distance. It is also possible to define cutoff for other distances as in
[
CSC08
,
LP16
]
.

We shall write
\mathcal{L}(Z)
for the law of a random variable
Z
. With a little abuse, we shall say that
Z_{t}^{\delta}
exhibits cutoff if
\mathcal{L}(Z_{t}^{\delta})
does.

Geodesic process and spherical means on hyperbolic manifolds

Let
M
be a compact connected oriented manifold without boundary of constant sectional curvature
-1
– hereafter simply called
compact hyperbolic manifold
– of dimension
d\geq 2
. We denote by
T_{x}M
the tangent space at
x\in M
,
T^{1}_{x}\hskip-1.0ptM
the unit
(d-1)
-sphere of
T_{x}M
and by
TM
,
T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM
the tangent and unit tangent bundles over
M
. The volume form associated with the Riemannian metric on
M
is denoted
\Vol
and
\pi\coloneqq\Vol/\Vol(M)
is the probability measure associated with
\Vol
. We denote by
\sigma_{x}
the uniform probability measure on
T^{1}_{x}\hskip-1.0ptM
so that the Liouville probability measure on
T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM
can written as
dL(z)=d\pi(x)d\sigma_{x}(v)
, with
z=(x,v)\in T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM
.

In this paper, we consider the geodesic flow
\gamma(z,t)
for
z\in T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM,t\in\mathds{R}^{+}
, and our goal is to show that, from a probabilistic point of view, it exhibits some cutoff phenomenon. More precisely, we study the following
geodesic process
. Pick a point
Z_{0}=(X_{0},v_{0})\in T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM
at random where
X_{0}\in M
has law
\mu_{0}
, and, given
X_{0}
,
v_{0}\in T^{1}_{X_{0}}M
has conditional law
\sigma_{X_{0}}
. Then define

Z_{t}=(X_{t},v_{t})=\gamma(Z_{0},t),\quad\mu_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(X_{t}).

Note that our geodesic process it is not what is generally called the geodesic random walk, studied for example in
[
Sun84
,
GK19
]
. Here, the randomness of the process
Z_{t}
is only through the initial condition
Z_{0}
.

The process
X_{t}
is as associated with a family of bounded operators
A_{t}
acting on
L^{2}(M)
:

A_{t}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{T^{1}_{x}M}f(\gamma((x,v),t))d\sigma_{x}(v).

The operator
A_{t}
is called the
spherical mean of radius
t
. If
f_{0}
is the density of
\mu_{0}
with respect to
\pi
and
f_{0}\in L^{2}(M)
, then
\mu_{t}
is also absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
, and its density is given by
A_{t}f_{0}
.

Geometrically, our geodesic process can also be described as follows. The universal cover of
M
is the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathds{H}_{d}
which is naturally endowed with the hyperbolic measure
\ell_{d}
.
When
(M,X_{0})
is lifted to
(\mathds{H}_{d},\tilde{X}_{0})
, the lift of
X_{t}
, say
\tilde{X}_{t}
, is sampled according to the projection of
\ell_{d}
to the sphere in
\mathds{H}_{d}
of radius
t
and center
\tilde{X}_{0}
.

It is a general fact that
\mu_{t}
converges to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
, see
[
Gün66
,
Kni94
,
PSS95
]
. In this work, we choose
\mu_{0}
to be uniform on a small ball around a fixed point
x\in M
. This choice is motivated by the fact that on the one hand we need a measure which is continuous with respect to
\pi
to have convergence in total variation (which would not have been the case with a Dirac mass). On the other hand, this choice still describes well what happens around
x
. We prove that
\mu_{t}
exhibits cutoff when
\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}^{\delta}
is parametrized according to two settings which we describe below. Finally, we observe that whereas
\mu_{t}
converges in total variation, this is not the case for
\mathcal{L}(Z_{t})
toward the Liouville measure
L
. In fact, due to the
L
-invariance and invertibility of the geodesic flow, we have
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(Z_{t}),L)=\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(Z_{0}),L)
. We refer to
[
Rat87
,
NZ15
]
and references therein for studies of the mixing rate of the geodesic flow.

Cutoff with a peaked initial condition

For
\delta>0
and
x\in M
, let
\mu_{0}^{\delta}
be the uniform probability measure on the ball of radius
\delta
around
x
. As above, let
X_{t}^{\delta}
be the geodesic process with initial probability measure
\mu_{0}^{\delta}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be a compact connected hyperbolic manifold of dimension
d
. For almost all
x\in M
, as
\delta\to 0
, the geodesic process
X^{\delta}_{t}
exhibits cutoff at time
t^{\delta}_{\star}=-\frac{\ln\delta}{d-1}
.

In plain words, this result asserts that the position of the geodesic flow on
T^{1}\hskip-2.0ptM
starting from a peaked initial condition on
M
and uniform on the tangent sphere satisfies the cutoff at a time which depends only on the dimension of
M
. The proof will also show that the cutoff is uniform over the initial condition
x\in M
.

From a probabilistic perspective, an interest of Theorem
1.2
is that it is an example of cutoff where the underlying probability space
(M,\pi)
does not depend on
\delta
. The only other example known to the authors is
[
BP20
]
for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process driven by a slowed down Brownian motion (or Lévy process). Here the mixing mechanism seems very different.

Laplace-Beltrami operator

Before introducing our next results, we recall some basic facts on
\Delta=-\mathrm{div}(\nabla)
, (minus) the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the manifold
M
. We refer to
[
Str83
,
Bus92
,
Cha84
]
. On a compact connected hyperbolic manifold (without boundary),
\Delta
extends to a positive self-adjoint operator with discrete spectrum
\mathfrak{S}(\Delta)
with countably many eigenvalues which we denote, counting multiplicities,

0=\lambda_{0}(M)<\lambda_{1}(M)\leq\lambda_{2}(M)\leq\cdots,

see Subsection
2.2
below.
On
\mathds{H}_{d}
, the Laplace-Beltrami operator has continuous
L^{2}
-spectrum on the half-line

\left[\sigma_{d},\infty\right)
with

\sigma_{d}=\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2},

(1.3)

see
[
McK70
]
.

If
M_{n}
is a sequence of compact hyperbolic manifolds of common dimension
d
and with
\Vol(M_{n})\to\infty
, it is known that

\limsup_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})\leq\sigma_{d},

(1.4)

see
[
Che75
, Corollary 2.3]
(or
[
Hub74
]
for the special case of surface).
In the same spirit as
[
LP16
,
GK19
,
LLP20
,
BL22
,
GK22
]
, we shall now prove that sequence of hyperbolic manifolds of increasing volume and with few Laplacian eigenvalues in the interval
[0,\sigma_{d})
, the geodesic process exhibits cutoff. In view of (
1.4
) such manifolds have nearly-optimal spectral edge behavior.

Cutoff for hyperbolic manifolds with a near optimal spectral gap

We consider sequences
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of compact connected hyperbolic manifolds without boundary all of fixed dimension
d\geq 2
. We denote by
\pi_{n}
the normalized volume measure. We fix
\delta>0
. For
x\in M_{n}
, we let
\mu^{n}_{x,0}
be the uniform probability measure on the ball of radius
\delta
and radius
x
. We denote by
Z^{n}_{x,t}=(X^{n}_{x,t},v^{n}_{x,t})
the geodesic process with initial position
X^{n}_{x,0}
sampled according to
\mu^{n}_{x,0}
. Finally, let
\mu^{n}_{x,t}
be the law of
X^{n}_{x,t}
.

We set
V_{n}=\Vol(M_{n})
. In particular if
d=2
, we have
V_{n}=4\pi(g-1)
where
g=g_{n}
is the genus of the surface
M_{n}
. We denote by
\rho(x)
the injectivity radius at
x\in M_{n}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(M_{n})
be a sequence of compact hyperbolic
d
-dimensional manifolds such that
\lim_{n}V_{n}=\infty
and
\liminf_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})\geq\sigma_{d}
. Let
x_{n}\in M_{n}
be a sequence of points such that
\liminf_{n}\frac{\ln\rho(x_{n})}{\ln V_{n}}=0
.
Then the process
X^{n}_{x_{n},t}
exhibits cutoff at time
t^{n}_{\star}=\frac{\ln V_{n}}{(d-1)}
when
n\to\infty
.

The analog of Theorem
1.3
for the Brownian motion and the geodesic random walk is proven in
[
GK19
]
for compact hyperbolic surfaces (that is
d=2
). The proof of Theorem
1.3
will be proven with a similar strategy. To our knowledge, there is currently no example of applications of Theorem
1.3
in dimension
d\geq 3
. In dimension
d=2
, it was recently proven in
[
HM23
]
that there exists a sequence of compact hyperbolic surfaces
M_{n}
of increasing volume such that
\lim\lambda_{1}(M_{n})=\sigma_{2}
. This is actually a generic assumption: it was announced in
[
AM24
]
that a random hyperbolic surface of genus
g
(and volume
V_{n}=4\pi(g-1)
) sampled according to the Weil-Petersson measure on the moduli space of Riemannian metrics satisfies
\lim\lambda_{1}(M_{n})=\sigma_{2}
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Cutoff for hyperbolic manifolds with a Sarnak-Xue spectral density property

The hypothesis
\liminf_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})\geq\sigma_{d}
is very strong. However, it is possible to relax this spectral assumption as observed in related context in
[
GK19
,
BL22
,
GK22
]
. This relaxed spectral assumption is called the
Sarnak-Xue spectral density property
in
[
GK22
]
after
[
SX91
]
. More precisely, let
(M_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of hyperbolic
d
-dimensional manifolds as above with
\lim_{n}V_{n}=\infty
. For any
s\in[0,1)
, set

I(s)\coloneqq\limsup_{n}\frac{\ln\#\left\{k:\lambda_{k}(M_{n})\leq\sigma_{d}(1-s^{2})\right\}}{\ln V_{n}}.

We say that
(M_{n})_{n}
satisfies the
Sarnak-Xue density property
if for all
s\in(0,1)
,
I(s)\leq 1-s
.

In dimension
d=2
, there are a few examples of hyperbolic surfaces satisfying the Sarnak-Xue property. For arithmetic modular surfaces
M_{q}=\Gamma(q)\backslash\mathds{H}_{2}
,
[
SX91
, Corollary 1]
implies that the Sarnak-Xue property holds as
q\to\infty
. For a random hyperbolic surface sampled according to the Weil-Petersson measure,
[
WX22
, Theorem 2]
implies that the Sarnak-Xue density property holds with high probability as the genus
g
goes to infinity (this could also be extracted from the estimates in
[
LW24
,
AM23
]
). Similarly, for random
n
-cover of an hyperbolic surface,
[
MNP22
, Theorem 1.8]
implies that the Sarnak-Xue density property holds with high probability. In all these examples, this property is proved as a consequence of Selberg trace formula and a sharp upper bound on the number of closed geodesic paths of length
L\leq A\ln V_{n}
with
A\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(M_{n})
be a sequence of hyperbolic
d
-dimensional manifolds such that
\lim_{n}V_{n}=\infty
,
\liminf_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})>0
and the Sarnak-Xue density property holds. Suppose further that there exists a measurable set
T_{n}\subset M_{n}
such that
\lim_{n}\inf_{x\in T_{n}}\frac{\ln\rho(x)}{\ln V_{n}}=0
and
\lim_{n}\pi_{n}(T_{n})=1
. Then there exists a measurable subset
S_{n}
such that
\lim_{n}\pi_{n}(S_{n})=1
and for any sequence
x_{n}\in S_{n}
, the process
X^{n}_{x_{n},t}
exhibits cutoff at time
t^{n}_{\star}=\frac{\ln V_{n}}{d-1}
when
n\to\infty
.

We note that a more quantitative upper bound on
1-\pi_{n}(S_{n})
could be extracted from the proof of Theorem
1.4
Also, it is not complicated to combine the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.2
to get a statement when
n\to\infty
and
\delta\to 0
simultaneously. For the sake of clarity and readability we will not do it here.

Application to the Brownian motion

The Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a manifold
M
can be defined as the process with infinitesimal generator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator. There are analogs of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
for the Brownian motions on a sequence of hyperbolic manifolds
(M_{n})
. For
x\in M_{n}
, we denote below by
W^{n}_{x,t}
the Brownian motion on
M_{n}
started at
x
at time
t=0
. The statements are read as follows.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
(M_{n})
be a sequence of compact hyperbolic
d
-dimensional manifolds such that
\lim_{n}V_{n}=\infty
and
\liminf_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})\geq\sigma_{d}
. Let
x_{n}\in M_{n}
be a sequence of points such that
\liminf_{n}\frac{\ln\rho(x_{n})}{\ln V_{n}}=0
.
Then the process
W^{n}_{x_{n},t}
exhibits cutoff at time
2t^{n}_{\star}/(d-1)=2\ln V_{n}
when
n\to\infty
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
(M_{n})
a sequence of hyperbolic
d
-dimensional manifolds such that
\lim_{n}V_{n}=\infty
,
\liminf_{n}\lambda_{1}(M_{n})>0
and the Sarnak-Xue density property holds. Suppose further that there exists a measurable set
T_{n}\subset M_{n}
such that
\lim_{n}\inf_{x\in T_{n}}\frac{\ln\rho(x)}{\ln V_{n}}=0
and
\lim_{n}\pi_{n}(T_{n})=1
. Then there exists a measurable subset
S_{n}\subset M_{n}
such that
\lim_{n}\pi_{n}(S_{n})=1
and for any sequence
x_{n}\in S_{n}
, the process
W^{n}_{x_{n},t}
exhibits cutoff at time
2t^{n}_{\star}/(d-1)=2\ln V_{n}
when
n\to\infty
.

For hyperbolic surfaces, Corollary
1.5
was proven in
[
GK19
]
. For
n
-covers of an hyperbolic surface, Corollary
1.6
is also proven in
[
GK19
]
.

Idea of the proof

To prove Theorem
1.2
-
1.4
, we aim to find more tractable quantities that give us good estimates of the total variation distance.
Since the geodesic flow is of unit speed, it is easy to obtain a crude upper bound on the volume of the support of the process
X_{n}
, which leads to a lower bound on
t_{\star}
. Note that this bound does not require any assumption on the manifolds, which tells us in particular that the mixing time we find is minimal.
On the other hand, we prove a kind of variance contraction for the density
f_{t}
of
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
to derive an upper bound on the mixing time. More precisely, we use the spectral decomposition of the operator
A_{t}
, obtained from that of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, to show an exponential decay of the total variation distance between
\mu_{t}
and
\pi
.

For Brownian motion (Corollaries
1.5
and
1.6
), the general strategy is quite similar.
We can no longer bound the support of the process, but we can still use the drift of the hyperbolic Brownian motion to know that it is concentrated in a precise region of small volume with respect to
V_{n}
.
For the upper bound, we note that the rotational invariance of the law of the Brownian motion ensures that, conditionally on the distance
D_{t}
between the starting point and
W_{t}
, its law is the same as the geodesic process at time
D_{t}
. This allows us to refer back to the previous results.

Some perspectives

Theorem
1.2
gives an example of the cutoff phenomenon for a mixing dynamical system on a compact space started from an initial condition whose distance to equilibrium is large (here a spatially localized initial condition). It would be interesting to investigate the universality of this phenomenon for a large class of mixing dynamical systems. Other examples include baker’s maps or Arnold’s cat map. Geodesic paths on a manifold of non-constant non-negative curvature or chaotic billiards would be natural subjects of investigation.

1.3
and Theorem
1.4
. In Section
3
, we study the Brownian motion and prove Corollary
1.5
and Corollary
1.6
.

2
Study of the geodesic flow

This section is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
, which consist in studying the behavior of the geodesic flow and its mixing properties from a probabilistic point of view.
The proofs of these three theorems are quite similar, and so we will summarize the common elements, before going into the computationnal details of each one separately. Our reasoning is based on two different arguments, one for the lower bound of the mixing time, and one for the upper bound. Only the latter raquires the spectral estimates assumed in Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
.

As explained earlier, we recall that the process is associated with a family of bounded operators
A_{t}
acting on
L^{2}(M)
:

A_{t}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{T^{1}_{x}M}f(\gamma((x,v),t))d\sigma_{x}(v).

When necessary, we will use the exponent
A_{t}^{M}
to make clear on which surface we are working.

In the following, we try to understand the process by studying
A_{t}
. For
x
in a manifold
M
and
\rho>0
, we write
B(x,\rho)
for the ball of radius
\rho
centered at
x
, and
S(x,\rho)
for the corresponding sphere.

2.1
Lower bound

In this section, we provide the tools to obtain the lower bound on the mixing time, that is the following property:

Proposition 2.1
.

We have that

•

under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{t\leq(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}^{\delta}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{t},\pi)=1.

•

under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.3
or Theorem
1.4
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{t\leq(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}^{n}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{n}_{t},\pi^{n})=1.

The argument required is quite elementary, since it comes from a volume argument and does not rely on any spectral assumptions. In particular, it shows that the mixing time
t_{\star}
is "optimal". It is based on the following lemma, which translates the fact that the geodesic flow has unit speed, and so the support of functions or measures does not grow too fast.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider
f\in L^{2}(M)
such that
\Diam\Supp(f)\leq\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Then

\Vol\Supp A_{t}f\leq Ce^{(d-1)t}\sinh\left((d-1)\delta\right),

where
C>0
depends only on the dimension
d
of the manifold.

Proof.

The situation can be lifted in
\mathds{H}_{d}
. Let us fix
p\colon\mathds{H}_{d}\mapsto M
a covering map. Take a fundamental Dirichlet domain (or any nice enough fundamental domain) for the manifold
M
, and define a lift of
f_{0}
by

\forall x\in\mathds{H}_{d},\quad\widetilde{f_{0}}(x)=\begin{cases}f_{0}(x)&\text{if $x\in\mathcal{D}$},\\
0&\text{if $x\notin\mathcal{D}$.}\end{cases}

Then
\tilde{f_{t}}\coloneqq A_{t}^{\mathds{H}_{d}}\tilde{f}_{0}
is a lift of
f_{t}
in the sense that

f_{t}(x)=\overset{}{\underset{y\in p^{-1}(x)}{\sum}}\widetilde{f_{t}}(y).

In particular, since
p
is a local isometry,
\Vol\Supp f_{t}\leq\ell_{d}\Supp\tilde{f_{t}}
. When
f_{0}
is supported inside a ball of radius
\delta
, then so is
\tilde{f_{0}}
. As a consequence,
\tilde{f_{t}}
is supported in a
d
-dimensional spherical shell delimitated by spheres of radiuses
t-\delta,t+\delta
. Let
\Gamma_{d}
be the volume of the
d-1
-dimensional Euclidean sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
. Then the volume of any ball
B(y,r)
of radius
r
in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is given by

\Vol B(y,r)=\Gamma_{d}\int_{0}^{r}\sinh^{d-1}u\ du.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\ell_{d}\Supp\tilde{f}_{t}\leq\Gamma_{d}\int_{t-\delta}^{t+\delta}\sinh^{d-1}u\ du

\displaystyle\leq{\Gamma_{d}}{}\int_{t-\delta}^{t+\delta}e^{(d-1)u}\ du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Gamma_{d}}{(d-1)}e^{(d-1)t}\sinh\left((d-1)\delta\right),

and so

\Vol\Supp f_{t}\leq\frac{2\Gamma_{d}}{(d-1)}e^{(d-1)t}\sinh\left((d-1)\delta\right),

as desired.
∎

This inequality is sufficient to get what we want:

Proof of Property
2.1
.

Consider the support
\Supp f_{t}
of the density
f_{t}
. While
\Vol\Supp f_{t}\leq\eta V_{n}
, for
0<\eta<1
, we have

\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)\geq\mu_{t}(\Supp f_{t})-\pi(\Supp f_{t})\geq 1-\eta.

According to Proposition
2.2
,

\Vol\Supp f_{t}\leq Ce^{(d-1)t}\sinh\left((d-1)\delta\right).

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and take
t=(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}
. Then

\Vol\Supp f_{\left(1-\varepsilon\right)t_{\star}}\ll V_{n},

where the last inequality holds in the three situations presented in Theorems
1.2
-
1.4
. It follows that

\lim\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}},\pi\right)=1,

when
\delta\to 0
for Theorem
1.2
and when
n\to\infty
for Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
∎

As we will confirm in the next subsection, this lower bound is the good estimate for the mixing time: this is in fact not very surprising : indeed, when looking at the behaviour of
\widetilde{f_{t_{\star}}}
on
\mathds{H}^{d}
, we can already see that it is approximately uniform on its support. As a consequence, it only suffices that this support covers "quite uniformly" the surface to get the good estimate. This will not be exactly the approach we choose to get the cutoff, but is still an interesting heuristic.

-

by showing the upper bound on the mixing time :

•

, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}^{\delta}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu^{\delta}_{t},\pi)=1.

•

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.3
or Theorem

, for any

2.2.1
Spectral decomposition of
A_{t}

The main step is to study of
A_{t}
from a spectral point of view. Unless otherwise stated, we will work on
L^{2}(M,\pi)
, abbreviated
L^{2}
, for the rest of the section. Using the fact that
\gamma(v,t)
=
\gamma(-v,-t)
, it is not difficult to show that for any
t\geq 0
,
A_{t}
is a self-adjoint operator which ensures that the spectrum is contained in
\mathds{R}
. The aim of this part is to get the following propositions, which describe the spectrum of
A_{t}
much more precisely. They will be at the core of the end of the proofs of Theorems
1.2
-
1.4
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\phi_{k}
be an eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
. Then,
\phi_{k}
is also an eigenfunction of
A_{t}
, and there exists a function
\nu_{t}\colon\mathds{R}^{+}\to\mathds{R}
such that

A_{t}\phi_{k}=\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})\phi_{k}.

If
\lambda=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}+u^{2}
,
u\in\mathds{R}\cup i\left[-\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right]
,
we have

\nu_{t}(\lambda)=c_{d}\int_{-1}^{1}(\cosh t+x\sinh t)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx,

where
c_{d}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}
. In particular, for
d=3

\nu_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{\sin(ut)}{2u\sinh(t)}.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\lambda=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}+u^{2}
with
u\in\mathds{R}_{+}\cup i\left[0,\frac{d-1}{2}\right]
. Let
t_{0}>0
. There exists a constant
C=C(d,t_{0})
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
, if
u\in i\left[0,\frac{d-1}{2}\right]
, we have

|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|\leq C\min\left(t,\frac{1}{\Im(u)}\right)e^{\Im(u)t-\frac{d-1}{2}t},

and if
u\in\mathds{R}_{+}
,

|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|\leq C\min\left(t,\frac{1}{u^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right)e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}t}.

Note that we have given the closed-form expression for
\nu_{t}(\lambda)
for
d=3
, but more generally for any odd
d\geq 3
, the integral in the definition of
\nu_{t}(\lambda)
could be computed explicitly by integration by parts. For even
d\geq 2
, however, we do not know if it is possible to write a closed-form expression for
\nu_{t}(\lambda)
(but see Lemma
2.4
below for quantitative estimates).
Note also that in the case
d=2
, Lemma
2.4
improves on the estimate made in
[
GK19
, Proposition 2.3]
when
\lambda
is large. This improvement will be crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Before proving the theorems, let us recall the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. The expression of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
in coordinates for
C^{\infty}(M)
functions is as follows:

\forall f\in C^{\infty}(M),\quad\Delta f=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\overset{}{\underset{i,j}{\sum}}\partial_{i}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\partial_{j}f\right).

As an
L^{2}
-unbounded operator,
\Delta
is symmetric, non-negative, and admits a unique self-adjoint extension (
[
Str83
]
). The spectrum
\mathfrak{S}(\Delta)
of the Laplacian operator (or of its extension) is well-understood. In the compact case, it is only pure point, and we have the following theorem, as reminded in the introduction:

Theorem 2.5
.

(
[
Cha84
]
)
Let
M
be an compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary. Then there exists a complete orthonormal system
(\phi_{i})_{i}\geq 0
of
L^{2}(M)
composed of
C^{\infty}
-eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator with eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots,\lambda_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

As a consequence, any
f\in L^{2}
can be decomposed as

f=\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}(\Delta)}\langle f,\phi_{k}\rangle\phi_{k}.

(2.1)

Combined with Lemma
2.4
, this last expression is quite convenient for our problem, as we will see later on.

As explained in
[
GK19
]
for the special case of surfaces, the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the manifold
M
can be parametrized by its unitary dual. More precisely, to describe the unitary dual of
M
, we usually use the parametrisation by
\frac{d-1}{2}+iu\in\mathds{C}
. This parametrization can be divided in three parts :

•

the principal series containing all the parameters in
\left\{\frac{d-1}{2}+iu|u\in\mathds{R}\right\}
, and corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2}+u^{2}\geq\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2}
. In the following, we write
\mathfrak{S}_{P}(\Delta)
the set of eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator coming from the principal series.

•

the complementary series containing all the parameters in
\left\{\frac{d-1}{2}+iu|iu\in\left(-\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\right\}
, and corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2}+u^{2}<\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2}
. We write
\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)
the eigenvalues coming from the complementary series.

•

the discrete series corresponding to the parameters
\{0,d-1\}
, that is
iu\in\left\{-\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right\}
, and to the eigenvalue
0
, associated with the constant eigenfunctions.

As we can see in Lemma
2.4
, this trichotomy is of interest to us, because the behavior of the eigenvalues of
A_{t}
depends strongly on which part of the spectrum they belong to. In particular, the behavior of the geodesic flow is controlled by how many eigenvalues belongs to the complementary series. Our assumptions about the spectrum exactly tell us that we can reasonably control their number. Note that choosing
u
or
-u
in the previous case does not change the associated eigenvalue.

Let us know prove Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

It follows from the fact that
A_{t}
is a radial operator.

To prove it in the special case of
A_{t}
, we refer to
[
Bou22
, Proposition A.1]
. In his case, Boulanger considers the semi-group of operators
\left(\mathcal{O}^{\rho}\right)_{\rho}
, where
\mathcal{O}^{\rho}
does the mean on the hyperbolic ball of radius
\rho
instead of the mean on the hyperbolic sphere of radius
\rho
. He proves what is known as Delsarte’s formula, which states that

\mathcal{O}^{\rho}(\phi_{k})=\frac{1}{\Vol{B_{o}(\rho)}}\int_{B_{o}(\rho)}{y^{s}}{\frac{dx_{1}\cdots dx_{d-1}dy}{y^{d}}}\ \phi_{k},

(2.2)

where
s(d-1-s)=\lambda_{k}=\lambda
,
o=(0,0...,1)
in the half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
and
B_{o}(t)
is hyperbolic ball of radius
t
. Note that the value of the integral depends only on the original eigenvalue, and not of the eigenfunction nor the choice of
s
between the two solutions of the equation. Now, we write
\lambda_{k}=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}+u_{k}^{2}
and
s=s_{k}=\frac{d-1}{2}\pm iu_{k}
.

A direct consequence of
(
2.2
)
for us is that

A_{t}\phi_{k}=\left(\frac{1}{\Vol{S_{o}(t)}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\rho=t}\int_{B_{o}(\rho)}{y^{s}}{\frac{dx_{1}\cdots dx_{d-1}dy}{y^{d}}}\right)\phi_{k},

where
S_{o}(t)
is the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperbolic sphere of radius
t
. We deduce that

\nu_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\Vol{S_{o}(t)}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\rho=t}\int_{B_{o}(\rho)}{y^{s}}{\frac{dx_{1}\cdots dx_{d-1}dy}{y^{d}}}.

In the model of hyperbolic half-space model, the ball
B_{o}(t)
corresponds to the Euclidean ball of center
\cosh(t)\cdot o
and radius
\sinh t
. We use spherical coordinates centered in
\cosh t\cdot o

\begin{cases}r&=d_{h}(x,o)\\
x_{1}&=\sinh r\cos\theta_{1}\\
x_{2}&=\sinh r\sin\theta_{1}\cos{\theta_{2}}\\
\dots\\
x_{d-1}&=\sinh r\sin\theta_{1}\ldots\cos\theta_{d-1}\\
y&=\cosh r+\sinh r\sin\theta_{1}\ldots\sin\theta_{d-1}\end{cases}\quad\text{so that }\begin{cases}r\in[0,t]\\
\ \theta_{j}\in[0,\pi]&\text{ for }1\leq j\leq d-2,\\
\ \theta_{d-1}\in(-\pi,\pi)\end{cases}

to transform the integral. The Jacobian of this diffeomorphism is
y\sinh^{d-1}r\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\sin^{d-1-j}\theta_{j}
. Let us write
d\theta=\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}d\theta_{j}
for the Lebesgue measure on
\mathds{R}^{d-1}
. We find

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Vol S_{o}(t)}\sinh^{d-1}t\int_{[0,\pi]^{d-2}\times(-\pi,\pi)}\left(\cosh t+\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\sin\theta_{j}\right)^{s-d+1}\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\sin^{d-1-j}\theta_{j}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Vol S_{o}(t)}\sinh^{d-1}t\int_{[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]^{d-2}\times(-\pi,\pi)}\left(\cosh t+\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos\theta_{j}\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos^{d-1-j}\theta_{j}d\theta.

Because
\Vol S_{o}(t)=\Gamma_{d}\sinh^{d-1}t
where
\Gamma_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
is the volume of the
(d-1)
-dimensional Euclidean sphere, we get

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{d}}\int_{(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})^{d-2}\times(-\pi,\pi)}\left(\cosh t+\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos\theta_{j}\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos^{d-1-j}\theta_{j}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}\int_{[0,\frac{\pi}{2})^{d-2}\times(-\pi,\pi)}\left(\cosh t+\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos\theta_{j}\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}\cos^{d-1-j}\theta_{j}d\theta.

It remains to simplify the above integral. We perform a first change of variables from
[0,\frac{\pi}{2})^{d-2}\times(-\pi,\pi)
to
[0,1)^{d-2}\times(-1,1)
:

J(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d-1})=(\cos(\theta_{1}),\ldots,\cos(\theta_{d-1})).

We get:

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}\int_{[0,1)^{d-2}\times(-1,1)}\left(\cosh t+\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}x_{j}\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}^{d-1-j}dx}{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}\left(I_{+}+I_{-}\right),

where
dx=\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}dx_{j}
and

I_{\pm}=\int_{[0,1)^{d-1}}\left(\cosh t\pm\sinh t\overset{d-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}x_{j}\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}^{d-1-j}dx}{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}.

If
d=2
, it concludes the proof. We now assume
d\geq 3
. We perform the change of variable from
(0,1)^{d-1}
to
\mathcal{D}=\left\{x\in(0,1)^{d-1}:x_{d-1}<x_{d-2}\cdots<x_{1}\right\}
defined by:

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1})=K(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})=(x_{1},x_{1}x_{2},\ldots,x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{d-1}).

Its inverse
K^{-1}
is given by
K^{-1}(y)_{j}=y_{j}/y_{j-1}
with the convention that
y_{0}=1
. In particular,
DK^{-1}(y)
is an upper triangular matrix and
\det(DK^{-1}(y))=\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}y_{j}^{-1}
. Since
\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}^{d-1-j}=\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}y_{j}
, we deduce that, with
dy=\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}dy_{j}
,

\displaystyle I_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\cosh t\pm y_{d-1}\sinh t\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{dy}{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sqrt{1-y_{j}^{2}/y_{j-1}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\cosh t\pm y_{d-1}\sinh t\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}y_{j}dy}{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sqrt{y_{j-1}^{2}-y_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2^{-d+2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\cosh t\pm x\sinh t\right)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\varphi_{d}(x^{2})dx,

with, for
x\geq 0
,

\varphi_{d}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{d-2}(x)}\frac{dz_{1}\cdots dz_{d-2}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sqrt{z_{j-1}-z_{j}}},

where we have set
z_{0}=1
,
z_{d-1}=x
and
\mathcal{D}_{d-2}(x)=\left\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d-2}):1>z_{1}>\cdots>z_{d-2}>x\right\}
(we have performed the change of variable
z_{j}=y_{j}^{2}
at the last step).
We check by recursion that
\varphi_{d}(x)=u_{d}(1-x)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}
for some real
u_{d}>0
. For
d=2
, we have seen that
u_{2}=1
. We use the recursion:

\varphi_{d+1}(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\frac{\varphi_{d}(y)}{\sqrt{y-x}}dy.

By the recursion hypothesis, we get

\displaystyle\varphi_{d+1}(1-x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\varphi_{d}(1-y)}{\sqrt{x-y}}dy

\displaystyle=u_{d}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{y^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{\sqrt{x-y}}dy

\displaystyle=u_{d}x^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{\sqrt{t}}dt.

Hence
u_{d+1}=u_{d}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}t^{-1/2}dt
. We finally remark that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{k/2}}{\sqrt{t}}dt=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{k/2}dt=2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{k+1}(\theta)d\theta.

We recognize the Wallis integral
W_{k+1}
and the conclusion easily follows from the formulas

W_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k+2}{2}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma\left(d/2\right)}.

The integral formula for
\nu_{t}(\lambda)
follows. For
d=3
, it is obvious to integrate. ∎

Proposition

can be used to obtain the quantitative estimates on

of Proposition

. We denote by
\Im(z)
the imaginary part of a complex number

.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

For
d=3
, the lemma is clear from the explicit expression given in Proposition
2.3
. For general

, we start by proving that all
u\in\mathds{R}_{+}\cup i\left[0,\frac{d-1}{2}\right]

|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|\leq C\min\left(t,\frac{1}{\Im(u)}\right)e^{\Im(u)t-\frac{d-1}{2}t}.

(2.3)

Let
v=\Im(u)\geq 0
and
a=\tanh t\in[\tanh t_{0},1)
. By Proposition
2.3
(applied to
-u

\displaystyle|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle=c_{d}(\cosh t)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}\left|\int_{-1}^{1}(1+ax)^{-iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}(\cosh t)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+ax)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}e^{vt-\frac{d-1}{2}t}\left(I_{+}+I_{-}\right),

where

I_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{1}(1\pm ax)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx

\displaystyle I_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(1-a(1-x))^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}(x(2-x))^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\delta^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1+bx)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}(x(2-x))^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1+bx)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}x^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx,

where at the last line, we have used that for all

I_{-}\leq Ca^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\delta^{v}\int_{0}^{b}(1+y)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}y^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dy.

\displaystyle\delta^{v}\int_{0}^{b}(1+y)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}y^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dy

Note that in a neighborhood of

\delta^{v}\int_{0}^{b_{0}}(1+y)^{v-\frac{d-1}{2}}y^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dy\leq C

for some constant

. We finally use that

\int_{b_{0}}^{b}y^{v-1}dy=\frac{1}{v}(b^{v}-b_{0}^{v})=\frac{b^{v}}{v}\left(1-\frac{b_{0}^{v}}{b^{v}}\right)\leq b^{v}\min\left(\frac{1}{v},\ln\frac{b}{b_{0}}\right),

Using again

It remains to prove that if

|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|\leq\frac{C}{u^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}t}.

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=c_{d}(\cosh t)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+ax)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}dx.

For

.
To this end, we fix
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
. For integers

\mathcal{P}_{k,n}\subset\mathbb{C}[X]

By integration by parts, if

\displaystyle I_{k}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a(\beta-k)}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+ax)^{\beta-k}\left(-2\alpha\frac{xP(x)}{1-x^{2}}-P^{\prime}(x)\right)(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a(\beta-k)}I_{k-1}(Q_{1}),

where

I_{k}(P)=\frac{I_{0}(Q_{k})}{a^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(\beta-k+j)}.

Note that

We apply this observation to

(so that
\frac{d-3}{2}=k-\alpha
,
\frac{d-1}{2}=k-\alpha+1
). We deduce that for some polynomial
Q
of degree at most
k
we have:

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{d}(\cosh t)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}}{a^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(iu-\frac{d-3}{2}+j)}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+ax)^{iu-1+\alpha}Q(x)(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{d}(\cosh t)^{iu-\frac{d-1}{2}}}{a^{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(iu-\frac{d-3}{2}+j)}\left(J_{+}+J_{-}\right),

with

J_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{1}(1\pm ax)^{iu-1+\alpha}Q(x)(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha}dx.

The proof of (
2.4
) will be complete if we prove that

|J_{\pm}|\leq\frac{C}{u^{1-\alpha}}.

(2.5)

We start with
J_{-}
and set
b=a/\delta
as above. Since
((1-x)^{l})_{l\geq 0}
is a basis of
\mathbb{C}[X]
, by linearity, we may assume that
Q(x)=(1-x)^{l}
for some integer
0\leq l\leq k
. From what precedes, we get

\displaystyle J_{-}

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu-1+\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}(1+bx)^{iu-1+\alpha}x^{l}(x(2-x))^{-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu}u^{-1}(\delta b)^{-1+\alpha}b^{-l}\int_{0}^{u\ln(1+b)}e^{iy}e^{y\alpha/u}(e^{y/u}-1)^{l-\alpha}(2-(e^{y/u}-1)/b)^{-\alpha}dy,

where we have performed the change of variable
y=u\ln(1+bx)
, that is
x=(e^{y/u}-1)/b
. Since
\delta b=a
, we find

\displaystyle J_{-}

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu}u^{-1+\alpha}a^{-1+\alpha}b^{-l}\int_{0}^{u\ln(1+b)}e^{iy}y^{-\alpha}f_{b}\left(\frac{y}{u}\right)dy,

where we have set

f_{b}(x)=\frac{(e^{x}-1)^{l}}{\left(g(x)h_{b}(x)\right)^{\alpha}},\quad g(x)=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x},\quad h_{b}(x)=2-\frac{e^{x}-1}{b}.

The functions
g,h_{b}
and
x\mapsto(e^{x}-1)^{l}
are analytic on
\mathbb{C}
. If
\alpha=0
, we deduce that
f_{b}(x)
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}
. If
\alpha=1/2
, consider the principal branch of the function
z\mapsto z^{-\alpha}
analytic on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathds{R}^{-}
with
\mathds{R}^{-}=(-\infty,0]
. For
\theta\geq 0
, let
C_{\theta}=\{x\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{arg}(x)\in[-\theta,\theta]\}
where
\mathrm{arg}(x)\in[-\pi,\pi)
is the argument of a complex number
x
. Let
C^{*}_{\theta}=\mathds{C}_{\theta}\backslash\{0\}
and
C_{\theta}(r)=C_{\theta}\cap B(r)
where
B(r)=\{x:|x|\leq r\}
. We claim that the image of
C_{\pi/8}(\ln(1+b))
by
x\mapsto g(x)h_{b}(x)
is contained in
C^{*}_{7\pi/8}
. This claim implies that
f_{b}
is analytic on
C_{\pi/8}(\ln(1+b))
.

Let us prove the claim. First, for all
x\in C_{\pi/2}(\ln(1+b))
, it is easy to check that
|e^{x}-1|/b\leq(e^{|x|}-1)/b\leq 1
. Thus, for all
x\in C_{\pi/2}(\ln(1+b))
,
h_{b}(x)
is in a ball of radius
1
centered at
2
. In particular, we have
h_{b}(x)\in C^{*}_{\pi/4}
. Note also that
g(0)=1
and
g(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
. Also, for
x\in C^{*}_{\theta}
with
\theta\leq\pi/2
, we have
|\mathrm{arg}(g(x))|\leq|\mathrm{arg}(1-e^{-x})|+|\mathrm{arg}(x^{-1})|\leq\pi/2+\theta
because
\Re(1-e^{-x})=1-e^{-|x|\cos(\theta)}\cos(|x|\sin(\theta))>0
. So we have checked that for all
x\in C^{*}_{\theta}
, we have
g(x)h_{b}(x)\in C^{*}_{\pi/2+\pi/4+\theta}
. Since
g(0)h_{b}(0)=2
, the latter is true for all
x\in C_{\theta}
. We deduce the claim.

We can now complete the proof of (
2.5
) for
J_{-}
. Since
f_{b}
is analytic on
C_{\pi/8}(\ln(1+b))
, we can apply Cauchy formula around a counterclockwise contour around the set
[0,u\ln(1+b)]\cup\{x:x=u\ln(1+b)e^{i\theta},\theta\in[0,\pi/8]\}\cup e^{i\pi/8}[0,u\ln(1+b)]
. We get, with
r=u\ln(1+b)
,

\displaystyle K_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}e^{iy}y^{-\alpha}f_{b}\left(\frac{y}{u}\right)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}e^{iye^{i\pi/8}}(e^{i\pi/8}y)^{-\alpha}f_{b}\left(\frac{ye^{i\pi/8}}{u}\right)e^{i\pi/8}dy-\int_{0}^{\pi/8}e^{iye^{i\theta}}(re^{i\theta})^{-\alpha}f_{b}\left(\frac{re^{i\theta}}{u}\right)rd\theta.

Taking absolute values, with
c=\sin(\pi/8)>0
, we get

|K_{-}|\leq\int_{0}^{r}e^{-cy}y^{-\alpha}\left|f_{b}\left(\frac{ye^{i\pi/8}}{u}\right)\right|dy+r^{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\pi/8}e^{-r\sin(\theta)}\left|f_{b}\left(\frac{re^{i\theta}}{u}\right)\right|d\theta.

Next we can use
\sin(\theta)\geq 2\theta/\pi
and that for all
x\in C_{\pi/2}(\ln(1+b))
we have
|f_{b}(x)|\leq Cb^{l}
for some constant
C>0
. We get that

|K_{-}|\leq Cb^{l}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cy}y^{-\alpha}dy+Cb^{l}r^{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\pi/8}e^{-2r\theta/\pi}d\theta\leq C^{\prime}b^{l}(1+r^{-\alpha}),

for some new constant
C^{\prime}
. Since
J_{-}=\delta^{iu}u^{-1+\alpha}a^{-1+\alpha}b^{-l}K_{-}
, this cmopletes the proof of (
2.5
) for
J_{-}
.

It remains to prove (
2.4
) for
J_{+}
. The proof is the same as the proof for
J_{-}
so we will use the same notation. Using the same argument as above, we assume that
Q(x)=(1-x)^{l}
. We now set
\delta=(1+a)
and
b=a/(1+a)\in[b_{0},1/2)
with
b_{0}=b(t_{0})\in(0,1/2)
. We write

\displaystyle J_{+}

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu-1+\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}(1-bx)^{iu-1+\alpha}x^{l}(x(2-x))^{-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu}u^{-1}(\delta b)^{-1+\alpha}b^{-l}\int_{0}^{-u\ln(1-b)}e^{-iy}e^{-y\alpha/u}(1-e^{-y/u})^{l-\alpha}(2-(1-e^{-y/u})/b)^{-\alpha}dy,

where we have performed the change of variable
y=-u\ln(1-bx)
, that is
x=(1-e^{-y/u})/b
. Since
\delta b=a
, we find

\displaystyle J_{+}

\displaystyle=\delta^{iu}u^{-1+\alpha}a^{-1+\alpha}b^{-l}\int_{0}^{-u\ln(1-b)}e^{-iy}y^{-\alpha}f_{b}\left(\frac{y}{u}\right)dy,

where now we have

f_{b}(x)=\frac{(1-e^{-x})^{l}}{\left(g(x)h_{b}(x)\right)^{\alpha}},\quad g(x)=\frac{e^{x}-1}{x},\quad h_{b}(x)=2-\frac{1-e^{-x}}{b}.

We can then conclude similarly thanks to a contour integration around the set

(note that in this case, it is easier since

and

are uniformly bounded by some

).
∎

2.2.2
Spectral bound on total variation distance

In this section, we give a spectral bound on the total variation distance using Lemma
2.4
and equation
(
2.1
)
. This, combined with Proposition
2.1
, is essentially sufficient to conclude to Theorem
\ref{th:D1}-\ref{th:S2}
. Then, although the outline of the proof is the same for each of our three frameworks, there are some subtleties when going from one to the other. We will therefore separate the three proofs in three subsections, starting with the case of Theorem
1.3
, which is the most direct. We will then deal with Theorem
1.2
, for which we need a little refinement, and conclude with the proof of Theorem
1.4
, which is the more computational situation.

In the following, for
\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)
, we define
s_{k}\in(0,1)
as the only positive number such that

\lambda_{k}=\sigma_{d}(1-s^{2}_{k})

(
i.e.

s_{k}=\frac{2iu}{d-1}
, where
u
has been chosen as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
,
\Im u<0
), so that the conclusion of Lemma
2.4
can be conveniently rewritten as:

|\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})|=\begin{cases}O\left(te^{-\frac{d-1}{2}t}\right)&\text{if $\lambda_{k}\geq\sigma_{d}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}$}\\
O\left(te^{-\frac{d-1}{2}(1-s_{k})t}\right)&\text{if $\lambda_{k}=\sigma_{d}(1-s_{k}^{2})\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}$}.\end{cases}

(2.6)

Proposition 2.6
.

There exists a numerical constant
C>0
such that for any
f\in L^{2}(M,\pi)
, we have

\lVert A_{t}f-1\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq Ct^{2}\left(\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)}e^{-(1-s_{k})(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}(\Delta)}e^{-(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}\right).

In particular,

\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}\leq Ct^{2}\left(\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)}e^{-(1-s_{k})(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}(\Delta)}e^{-(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}\right).

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{1}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{t}-1\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert A_{t}\cdot\left(f_{0}-1\right)\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

On the other hand, the decomposition
(
2.1
)
leads to

\displaystyle\lVert A_{t}\left(f_{0}-1\right)\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)}\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})^{2}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}(\Delta)}\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})^{2}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}.

It remains to use the upper bound (
2.6
).
∎

2.2.3
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Theorem
1.3
is a simple consequence of Proposition
2.6
. Indeed, it tells that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ct\left(\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}(\Delta)}e^{-(1-s_{k})(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}(\Delta)}e^{-(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cte^{-(1-s_{1})(d-1)t}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ct\frac{V_{n}}{\Vol B(x_{n},\delta)}e^{-(1-s_{1})(d-1)t}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and take
t\geq\left(1+\varepsilon\right)t^{n}_{\star}=\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\frac{\ln V_{n}}{d-1}
. Then,

\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}\leq{\frac{Ct}{\Vol B(x_{n},\delta)}}V_{n}^{s_{1}(1+\varepsilon)-\varepsilon}\leq{\frac{C^{\prime}}{\Vol B(x_{n},\delta)}}V_{n}^{s_{1}(1+\varepsilon)-\varepsilon/2},

where
C^{\prime}
depends on
\varepsilon
is such that
Ct\leq C^{\prime}\exp(t(d-1)\varepsilon/2)
for all
t\geq 0
.

By assumption, when
n
becomes large enough,
s_{1}
becomes small, so
s_{1}(1+\varepsilon)<\varepsilon/4
. Since
\sinh x\geq x
, we get that
\Vol B(x_{n},\delta)\geq C_{d}\min(\delta^{d},\rho(x_{n})^{d})
for some constant
C_{d}
which depends only on the dimension
d
. Therefore,

\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}\leq{\frac{C^{\prime}}{C_{d}\min(\delta,\rho(x_{n}))^{d}}}{V_{n}^{-\varepsilon/4}}.

If
\liminf_{n}\frac{\ln\rho(x_{n})}{\ln V_{n}}=0
, then

\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\ln V_{n}+d\ln\rho=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\ln V_{n}+d\frac{\ln\rho}{\ln V_{n}}\ln V_{n}\overset{}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}{}\infty,

and so
\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}\overset{}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}{}0
as required. ∎

2.2.4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The proof of Theorem
1.2
follows the same lines but is a bit more subtle: at time 0, the density is too concentrated, leading to a
L^{2}
-norm in
\delta^{-d}
for
f_{0}
. We however use that the mass of
f_{0}
is concentrated on high eigenfunctions and take benefit of our estimate on
\nu_{t}(\lambda)
for large
\lambda
in Lemma
2.4
.

We fix
\varepsilon>0
and take
t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}
with
t_{\star}=t_{\star}^{\delta}=-\frac{\ln\delta}{d-1}
. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.6
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\lVert A_{t}(f_{0}-1)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\lVert\sum_{k\geq 1}\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle\phi_{k}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\geq 1}\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})^{2}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2},

where we have used the orthogonality of the basis
(\phi_{k})
at the last line. Fix
k_{0}\geq 1
large enough so that
\lambda_{k}\geq 2\sigma_{d}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Let
k_{\delta}>k_{0}
to be defined later. We decompose the sum into three parts,

\sum_{k\geq 1}\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})^{2}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq k_{0}}\cdots+\sum_{k_{0}<k<k_{\delta}}\cdots+\sum_{k\geq k_{\delta}}\cdots=S_{x,1}(t)+S_{x,2}(t)+S_{x,3}(t),

where we have written down the dependence in the initial condition
x\in M
explicitly. Our goal is to prove that for almost all
x\in M
, we have for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}S_{x,i}(t)=0.

We start with
S_{1}
. As
\delta\to 0
, for any fixed
k
, we have
\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle\to\phi_{k}(x)
. Also, for any
\lambda>0
,
\nu_{t}(\lambda)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. We deduce that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}\sup_{x\in M}S_{x,1}(t)=0.

Next from Proposition
2.4
, for all
\lambda\geq 2\sigma_{d}
and
t\geq t_{0}>0
, we have

|\nu_{t}(\lambda)|\leq C\lambda^{-\frac{d-1}{4}}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}t},

where
C=C(t_{0})
(we have used that
\lambda=\sigma_{d}+u^{2}
is equivalent to
u^{2}
for all large
\lambda
). We deduce the upper bound:

S_{x,3}(t)\leq C^{2}e^{-(d-1)t}\lambda^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}_{k_{\delta}}\sum_{k\geq k_{\delta}}\langle f_{0},\varphi_{k}\rangle^{2}\leq C^{2}e^{-(d-1)t}\lambda^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}_{k_{\delta}}\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

We have
\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=O(\delta^{-d})
. Also by Weyl’s law,
\lambda_{k}\sim ck^{2/d}
as
k\to\infty
for some
c>0
. Hence, for some new
C>0
, for all
k\geq k_{\delta}
,

|\nu_{t}(\lambda_{k})|^{2}\leq Ck^{-1+1/d}e^{-(d-1)t},

Picking
k_{\delta}=\lceil\delta^{-d}\rceil
, we get, for some new
C>0
,

S_{x,3}(t)\leq Ce^{-(d-1)t}\delta^{-1}.

We deduce that

\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}\sup_{x\in M}S_{x,3}(t)\leq C\delta^{\varepsilon}.

This obviously goes
0
as
\delta\to 0
. Similarly, using Weyl’s law, for some new
C>0
,

S_{x,2}(t)\leq Ce^{-(d-1)t}\sum_{k\leq k_{\delta}}\frac{\langle f_{0},\varphi_{k}\rangle^{2}}{k^{1-1/d}}=Ce^{-(d-1)t}Z_{x}^{\delta},

where
Z_{x}^{\delta}
does not depend on
t
and we have explicitly written the dependence in
\delta
. We denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
the expectation with respect to
x
sampled according to
\pi
. We want to put an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}Z_{\delta}^{x}
. To do so, let
\phi\in L^{2}(M)
. In the following we write
f_{x,0}
instead of
f_{0}
to insist on the dependence of
x
. From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we find that for all
\delta
less than the injectivity radius of
M
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\langle f_{x,0},\phi\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in M}\langle f_{x,0},\phi\rangle^{2}d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x\in M}\left(\int_{y\in M}(f_{x,0})^{2}(y)d\pi(y)\int_{y\in M}\phi^{2}(y)\mathds{1}_{B(x,\delta)}(y)d\pi(y)\right)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Vol(M)\int_{x\in M}\int_{y\in M}\frac{\mathds{1}_{B(x,\delta)}(y)}{\Vol B(x,\delta)}\phi^{2}(y)d\pi(y)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Vol(M)\int_{y\in M}\phi^{2}(y)\int_{x\in M}\frac{\mathds{1}_{B(y,\delta)}(x)}{\Vol B(x,\delta)}d\pi(x)d\pi(y)

\displaystyle\leq\Vol(M)\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

where at the last line, we have used that below the injectivity radius,
\Vol B(x,\delta)
does not depend on
x
.

By linearity of the expectation, we deduce that, for some
C,C^{\prime}>0
(depending on
M
),

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}Z_{x}^{\delta}\leq C\sum_{k\leq k_{\delta}}k^{-1+1/d}\leq C^{\prime}k_{\delta}^{1/d}\leq C^{\prime}\delta^{-1}.

Let
T_{\delta}=\{x\in M:Z_{x}^{\delta}\leq\delta^{-1-\varepsilon/2}\}
. From Markov inequality,
\pi(T^{c}_{\delta})\leq C\delta^{\varepsilon/2}
and for all
x\in T_{\delta}
,

\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}S_{x,2}(t)\leq\delta^{\varepsilon/2}.

Consequently, for almost all
x\in M
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}S_{x,2}(t)=0.

Indeed, otherwise, there would exist
\eta>0
and a measurable set
T\subset M
with
\pi(T)>\eta
such that for all
x\in T
,

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}}S_{x,2}(t)>\eta.

In particular,
T\subset T_{\delta}^{c}
along a sequence
\delta\to 0
and the inequality
\eta<C\delta^{\varepsilon/2}
leads to a contradiction for
\delta
small enough. It concludes the proof.
∎

2.2.5
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We first remark that on

\inf_{x\in T_{n}}\Vol B(x,\delta)\geq C_{d}\min(\delta,\rho(x))^{d}\geq V_{n}^{-\eta_{n}}

and prove that there exists a sequence

. Because

, this is sufficient. We denote

.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and take

. Let us estimate
\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)
when the initial condition is centered at

sampled according to

. As before, we start from Proposition

to get that

\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{t},\pi\right)^{2}\leq Ct\left(\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}}e^{-(1-s_{k})(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{P}}e^{-(d-1)t}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}\right).

Note that since

and the right-hand side of the above expression is decreasing in

for

is decreasing for

). As a consequence, remembering that

and

depend on the point

where the ball is centered, we get that, for

sampled according to
\tilde{\pi}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}}\sup_{s\geq t}\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{s},\pi\right)^{2}\leq Cte^{-(d-1)t}\left(\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}}e^{s_{k}(d-1)t}\int_{x\in M}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}d\tilde{\pi}(x)+\int_{x\in M}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}d\tilde{\pi}(x)\right).

(2.7)

For
n
large enough,
\pi(T_{n})\geq 1/2
and the second term in parentheses in (

) can be bounded as

\int_{x\in M}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}d\tilde{\pi}(x)\leq\frac{V_{n}}{\pi(T_{n})\inf_{x\in T_{n}}\Vol B(x,\delta)}\leq 2V_{n}^{1+\eta_{n}},

(2.8)

where
\eta_{n}\to 0
was defined at the beginning of the proof. Next, consider the first term in parentheses in (
2.7
) for a given
\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}
. We argue as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
: using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
\pi(T_{n})\geq 1/2

large enough,

\displaystyle\int_{x\in M}\langle f_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle^{2}d\tilde{\pi}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{x\in T_{n}}\left(\int_{y\in M}f_{0}^{2}(y)d\pi(y)\int_{y\in M}\phi^{2}_{k}(y)\mathds{1}_{B(x,\delta)}(y)d\pi(y)\right)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2V_{n}\int_{x\in T_{n}}\int_{y\in M}\frac{\mathds{1}_{B(x,\delta)}(y)}{\Vol B(x,\delta)}\phi_{k}^{2}(y)d\pi(y)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2V_{n}\int_{y\in M}\phi_{k}^{2}(y)\int_{x\in T_{n}}\frac{\mathds{1}_{B(y,\delta)}(x)}{\Vol B(x,\delta)}d\pi(x)d\pi(y)

\displaystyle\leq 2V_{n}^{\eta_{n}}e^{\delta(d-1)}.

We recall that
t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}
. By assumption, there exists
\gamma>0
such that

for all
n
large enough. We then write

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}}e^{s_{k}(d-1)t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}}(d-1)t\int_{-\infty}^{1-\gamma}\mathds{1}_{u\leq s_{k}}e^{u(d-1)t}du

\displaystyle\leq(d-1)t\int_{-\infty}^{1-\gamma}\#\left\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}|\lambda\leq\sigma_{d}^{2}(1-u^{2})\right\}e^{u(d-1)t}du.

The Sarnak-Xue density property implies that for all
u\in[0,1)
,

\#\left\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}|\lambda\leq\sigma_{d}^{2}(1-u^{2})\right\}\leq V_{n}^{1-u+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}.

where
\eta^{\prime}_{n}
is a vanishing sequence. We deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{C}}e^{s_{k}(d-1)t}

\displaystyle\leq(d-1)t\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}V_{n}^{1+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}e^{u(d-1)t}du+\int_{0}^{1-\gamma}V_{n}^{1-u+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}e^{u(d-1)t}du\right)

\displaystyle=V_{n}^{1+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}+\frac{(d-1)tV_{n}^{1+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}}{(d-1)t-\ln V_{n}}\left(V_{n}^{\gamma-1}e^{(1-\gamma)(d-1)t}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{V_{n}^{\gamma+\eta^{\prime}_{n}}(1+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}e^{(1-\gamma)(d-1)t},

where in the last line, we have used that
\ln V_{n}\leq(d-1)t/(1+\varepsilon)
.
All in all, in (
2.7
), combining our last bound with (
2.8
), we find for some new vanishing sequence
\eta^{\prime\prime}_{n}
(depending on
\varepsilon,d
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}}\sup_{s\geq t}\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{s},\pi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq V_{n}^{1+\eta^{\prime\prime}_{n}}te^{-(d-1)t}+V_{n}^{\gamma+\eta^{\prime\prime}_{n}}te^{-\gamma(d-1)t}

\displaystyle\leq CV_{n}^{\eta^{\prime\prime}_{n}-\gamma\varepsilon/2},

where
C
is such that
2t\leq C\exp(\gamma(d-1)t\varepsilon/2)
for all
t\geq 0
and we have used
t\geq(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}
. Finally, using Markov inequality, we get

\tilde{\pi}\left(\left\{x:\sup_{s\geq t}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mu_{s},\pi)\geq V_{n}^{-\gamma\varepsilon/8}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}}\sup_{s\geq t}\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\mu_{s},\pi\right)^{2}}{V_{n}^{-\gamma\varepsilon/4}}\leq CV_{n}^{\eta^{\prime\prime}_{n}-\gamma\varepsilon/4}.

The right sides goes to
0
. We take
t=(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.4
. ∎

3
Brownian Motion

3.1
Hyperbolic Brownian motion

Let
(\tilde{W}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}^{+}}
be the Brownian motion on the hyperbolic space
\mathds{H}_{d}
starting at
o=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\mathds{H}_{d}
with infinitesimal generator
\Delta_{\mathds{H}_{d}}/2
.
Let
d(x,y)
be the distance in
\mathds{H}_{d}
. It is a basic fact that
\tilde{W}_{t}
is radially symmetric. That is, the density of the law of
\tilde{W}_{t}
with respect to the hyperbolic measure
\ell_{d}
at
x
depends only on
d(x,o)
, see for example
[
Hsu02
, Subsection 3.3]
. One consequence is the following. Let
\tilde{D}_{t}=d(\tilde{W}_{t},o)
and
\tilde{Z}_{t}=(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{v}_{t})=\gamma_{t}(o,v_{0})
,
t\in\mathds{R}^{+}
, be the geodesic process started at
\tilde{X}_{0}=o
, independent of
\tilde{B}
. We have the identity in law for any
t\in\mathds{R}^{+}
:

\tilde{W}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{X}_{\tilde{D}_{t}}.

As explained in the proof of Proposition
2.2
, through the covering map the above equation remains true on any compact hyperbolic manifold
M
: for any
t\in\mathds{R}^{+}
:

W_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{\tilde{D}_{t}},

(3.1)

where
(W_{t})
is the Brownian motion on
M
started at
x
,
(X_{t})
is the position of the geodesic process on
M
started at
x
and
\tilde{D}
is as above and independent of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}^{+}}
. We can also randomly sample the initial condition
x
: if
\mu_{0}
is a probability distribution on
M
, then (
3.1
) holds where
(W_{t})
is the Brownian motion on
M
with initial condition
\mu_{0}
and
(X_{t})
is the position of the geodesic process on
M
started with initial condition
\mu_{0}
.

We shall also use that
\tilde{W}_{t}
has an explicit rate of escape: almost surely,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\tilde{D}_{t}}{t}=\frac{d-1}{2},

(3.2)

see
[
CDGM14
,
Shi17
]
.

3.2
Proof of Corollary
1.5
and Corollary
1.6
.

We set
\beta=\frac{d-1}{2}
and
t_{\star}=t_{\star}^{n}=\frac{\ln V_{n}}{d-1}
. We denote by
W_{t}=W_{t}^{n}
the Brownian motion on
M_{n}
. We start with the upper bound. We fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
s=(1+\varepsilon)2\ln V_{n}=(1+\varepsilon)t_{\star}/\beta
. From the Markov property, it is standard that the map
t\to\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,t}),\pi)
is non-increasing (see for example
[
CSC08
, Proposition 3.3]
). Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, to prove the upper bound of Corollary
1.5
and Corollary
1.6
, it is thus sufficient to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,s}),\pi)=0,

(3.3)

where
x=x_{n}
is as in Theorem
1.3
or as in Theorem
1.4
respectively. Fix some
\delta\in(0,1]
as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
and let

0<\rho\leq\min(\delta,\rho(x)).

(3.4)

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists
c_{0}=c_{0}(\alpha)>0
such that with
t_{0}=c_{0}\rho^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t_{0}}\in B_{\mathds{H}_{d}}(o,\rho))\geq 1-\alpha.

(3.5)

Indeed,
\mathds{H}_{d}
is locally isometric to the Euclidean space
\mathds{R}^{d}
and the above inequality is a basic consequence of scale invariance of the Brownian on
\mathds{R}^{d}
:
W_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sqrt{t}W_{1}
. Let
\tilde{g}_{0}
be the density of
\tilde{W}_{t_{0}}
with respect to the hyperbolic measure
\ell_{d}
. Again, by local isometry with the Euclidean space and scale invariance, we have

\sup_{x\in B_{\mathds{H}_{d}}(o,\rho)}\tilde{g}_{0}(x)\leq c_{1}t_{0}^{-d/2}=c_{2}\rho^{-d},

where
c_{1}=c_{1}(\alpha)
is some constant and
c_{2}=c_{1}c_{0}^{-d/2}
.

Next, in
M_{n}
, let
g_{0}
be the density of
W_{x,t_{0}}
with respect to the volume measure
\Vol
. Since
\rho\leq\rho(x)
, from what precedes, we have

\sup_{y\in B(x,\rho)}g_{0}(x)\leq c_{2}\rho^{-d},

Finally, let
\hat{g}_{0}
be the density of
W_{x,t_{0}}
conditioned on the event

E=\{W_{x,t_{0}}\in B(x,\rho)\}

with respect to the uniform measure on
B(x,\delta)
:
\mathbf{1}(y\in B(x,\delta))/\Vol B(x,\delta)d\Vol(y)
. From what precedes and (
3.5
), we find that

\sup_{y\in B(x,\rho)}\hat{g}_{0}(x)\leq\frac{c_{2}\Vol(B(x,\delta))}{1-\alpha}\rho^{-d}\leq c\rho^{-d},

(3.6)

with
c=c(\alpha)=c_{2}\Vol_{H_{d}}(B(o,\delta))/(1-\alpha)
.

We are ready for the proof of the upper bound. We may find
b<\beta
such that
sb\geq(1+\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}
. For any measurable event
A
, from (
3.5
), we have

\mathbb{P}(W_{x,s+t_{0}}\in A)-\pi(A)\leq\mathbb{P}(E)\left(\mathbb{P}(W_{x,s+t_{0}}\in A|E)-\pi(A)\right)+2\alpha,

(3.7)

where
\mathbb{P}(A|B)=p(A\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B)
denotes the conditional probability. From the Markov property and (
3.6
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{x,s+t_{0}}\in A|E)-\pi(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{n}}\left(\mathbb{P}(W_{y,s}\in A)-\pi(A)\right)\hat{g}_{0}(y)f_{0}(y)d\Vol(y)

\displaystyle\leq c\rho^{-d}\int_{M_{n}}\left|\mathbb{P}(W_{y,s}\in A)-\pi(A)\right|f_{0}(y)d\Vol(y)

\displaystyle=c\rho^{-d}\left|\mathbb{P}(W_{X_{x,0},s}\in A)-\pi(A)\right|,

where
(W_{X_{x,0},t})_{t\geq 0}
is the Brownian motion on
M_{n}
with initial condition
X_{x,0}
uniform on
B(x,\delta)
. As in Theorem
1.3
or Theorem
1.4
, let
(X_{x,t})
be the geodesic process on
M_{n}
with initial condition uniform on
B(x,\delta)
. From (
3.1
), if
\nu_{t}
is the law of
\tilde{D}_{t}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{X_{x,0},s}\in A)-\pi(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{x,t}\in A)\nu_{s}(dt)-\pi(A)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}(\tilde{D}_{s}<sb)+\int_{(1+\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{P}(X_{x,t}\in A)-\pi(A))\nu_{s}(dt)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}(\tilde{D}_{s}<sb)+\sup_{t\geq(1+\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(X_{x,t}),\pi).

If
x=x_{n}
is as in Theorem
1.3
or Theorem
1.4
respectively, we can take in (
3.4
)
\rho=V^{-\eta_{n}}_{n}
for some sequence
\eta_{n}\to 0
. The second term of the last shown inequality goes to
0
while the first term goes to
0
from (
3.2
). Moreover, these terms go to
0
at a rate faster than
\rho^{-d}=V_{n}^{d\eta_{n}}
as can be seen from the proofs of Theorem
1.3
or Theorem
1.4
for the second term and from
[
CDGM14
, Proposition 3.2]
for the first term. Taking the supremum over all sets
A
, from (
3.7
), we thus have checked that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,s+t_{0}}),\pi)\leq 2\alpha.

Since
t_{0}=O(1)
and
\alpha
can be taken arbitrarily small, it concludes the proof of the upper bound (
3.3
).

It remains to prove the corresponding lower bound. We argue as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
. We fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and set
s^{\prime}=(1-\varepsilon)2\ln V_{n}=(1-\varepsilon)t_{\star}/\beta
. There exists
b^{\prime}>\beta
such that
s^{\prime}b^{\prime}\leq(1-\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}
. Since
t\to\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,t}),\pi)
is non-increasing, it suffices to prove that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in M_{n}}\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,s^{\prime}}),\pi)=1.

(3.8)

Take
x\in M_{n}
. For
r>0
, we denote by
B(x,r)
the closed ball in
M_{n}
with radius
r
and center
x
. Similarly, we denote by
\tilde{B}(r)
the closed ball in
\mathds{H}_{d}
with center
o
and radius
r
. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, by using the covering map, we have, for some
C>0
,

\Vol(B(x,r))\leq\ell_{d}(\tilde{B}(r))\leq C\exp((d-1)r).

(3.9)

Furthermore, the distance in
M_{n}
between
x
and
W_{x,t}
is stochastically dominated by
\tilde{D}_{t}=d(\tilde{W}_{t},o)
. In other words, for any
r,t
,

\mathbb{P}(W_{x,t}\in B(x,r))\geq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{D}_{t}\leq r).

In particular,

\mathbb{P}(W_{x,s^{\prime}}\in B(x,(1-\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}))\geq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{D}_{s^{\prime}}\leq b^{\prime}s^{\prime}).

On the other hand, from (
3.9
), we have

\pi(B(x,(1-\varepsilon/2)t^{n}_{\star})=\frac{\Vol(B(x,(1-\varepsilon/2)t_{\star})}{V_{n}}\leq CV_{n}^{-\varepsilon/2}.

From the definition of
\mathrm{d_{TV}}
, it follows that

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\mathcal{L}(W_{x,s^{\prime}}),\pi)\geq\mathbb{P}(W_{x,s^{\prime}}\in B(x,(1-\varepsilon/2)t_{\star}))-\pi(B(x,(1-\varepsilon/2)t_{\star})\geq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{D}_{s^{\prime}}\leq b^{\prime}s^{\prime})-CV_{n}^{-\varepsilon/2}.

Using (
3.2
) and
b^{\prime}>\beta
, it concludes the proof of (
3.8
).

∎
