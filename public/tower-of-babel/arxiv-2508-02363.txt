Transport-Guided Rectified Flow Inversion: Improved Image Editing Using Optimal Transport Theory
Marian Lupaşcu 1 Mihai Sorin Stupariu 1 marianlupascu15@gmail.com stupariu@fmi.unibuc.ro 1 Department of Computer Science, University of Bucharest, Romania

Abstract
Effective image inversion in rectified flow models—mapping real images to editable latent representations—is crucial for practical image editing applications, however achieving optimal balance between reconstruction fidelity and editing flexibility remains a fundamental challenge. In this work, we introduce the Optimal Transport Inversion Pipeline (OTIP), a zero-shot framework that leverages optimal transport theory to guide the inversion process in rectified flow models. Our underlying hypothesis is that incorporating transport-based guidance during the reverse diffusion process can effectively balance reconstruction accuracy and editing controllability through principled trajectory optimization. The method computes optimal transport paths between image and noise distributions while maintaining computational efficiency. Our approach achieves high-fidelity reconstruction with LPIPS scores of 0.001 and SSIM of 0.992 on face editing benchmarks, demonstrating superior preservation of fine-grained details compared to existing methods. We evaluate the framework across multiple editing tasks, observing 7.8% to 12.9% improvements in reconstruction loss over RF-Inversion on the LSUN-Bedroom and LSUN-Church datasets, respectively. For semantic face editing, our method achieves an 11.2% improvement in identity preservation and a 1.6% enhancement in perceptual quality, while maintaining computational efficiency comparable to baseline approaches. Qualitatively, our method produces visually compelling edits with superior semantic consistency and fine-grained detail preservation across diverse editing scenarios. Code is available at https://github.com/marianlupascu/OT-Inversion

Introduction

Rectified flow (RF) models
[
22
,
11
]
achieve high-quality text-to-image generation with computational advantages over diffusion models
[
16
,
37
]
, as demonstrated by systems like FLUX
[
20
]
. However, these models lack effective image inversion methods, limiting their utility for image editing applications that require mapping real images back to editable latent representations.

Image inversion in rectified flows presents unique challenges compared to diffusion models. The deterministic nature of RF trajectories eliminates stochastic regularization that aids diffusion model inversion
[
16
]
, leading to trajectory deviation during the inversion process. Small numerical errors compound across the deterministic flow path, causing inverted latents to drift from semantically meaningful representations
[
38
]
. This creates a fundamental trade-off: methods preserving reconstruction fidelity produce latents resistant to editing, while approaches enabling modification introduce visual artifacts.

Existing RF inversion approaches present challenges. DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) inversion
[
37
]
adapted to rectified flows accumulates discretization errors, resulting in suboptimal reconstruction quality. Optimization-based approaches like Null-Text Inversion
[
27
]
require expensive test-time computation that contradicts RF efficiency advantages. The multimodal Diffusion Transformer architecture
[
30
,
11
]
introduces additional complexity through entangled text-image representations.

Optimal transport integration with generative models has enhanced training stability and sample quality. Wasserstein GANs
[
2
,
12
]
demonstrated OT’s utility in adversarial training, while neural optimal transport
[
19
]
enabled scalable computation using neural networks as transport plan approximators. The Wasserstein distance provides natural alignment with perceptual similarity through geometric structure preservation
[
31
]
, making it well-suited for semantic image editing applications.

We introduce the Optimal Transport Inversion Pipeline (OTIP), which leverages optimal transport theory
[
2
,
39
]
to guide RF inversion. Our key insight is that optimal transport provides principled pathways between image distributions
[
29
,
18
]
that can stabilize inversion trajectories while maintaining editability.

Our main contributions are:

(i)
A theoretical framework that links optimal transport distances to RF inversion quality, providing a mathematical foundation for transport-guided denoising;

(ii)
An adaptive scheduling strategy for transport strength, enabling a dynamic trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and editing flexibility during inversion;

(iii)
Empirical improvements over existing RF inversion methods across multiple editing tasks, including reconstruction, semantic manipulation, and regional editing;

3
Approach

Our key insight is that optimal transport theory can provide principled guidance for rectified flow inversion without requiring model retraining. We propose the Optimal Transport Inversion Pipeline (OTIP), a training-free framework that refines the inversion process by leveraging transport-optimal trajectories during the denoising process.

3.1
Overview

Modern rectified flow (RF) models like FLUX
[
20
]
establish deterministic mappings that transform pure Gaussian noise
z_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
into data samples
z_{0}
via straight-line trajectories in the latent space. While this deterministic framework offers computational advantages, the inverse problem—recovering the noise initialization
z_{T}
from a given sample
z_{0}
—fundamentally suffers from trajectory deviation that corrupts subsequent editing operations. We address this limitation by incorporating optimal transport (OT) theory
[
41
,
2
]
, leveraging optimal transport theory to compute minimal-cost transformations between probability distributions. Our method ensures that inversion trajectories approximate geodesics in this space, thereby preserving both structural coherence and semantic relationships essential for high-fidelity editing. The resulting framework maintains the computational efficiency of rectified flows while achieving principled control over the editing process through transport-theoretic guidance.

3.2
Motivation: Why Optimal Transport?

Standard RF-inversion faces two critical challenges that motivate our approach. Recent theoretical analysis
[
13
,
21
]
reveals that rectified flows do not inherently follow optimal transport paths. The straight trajectories that make RFs efficient for generation are problematic during inversion, where we need paths that preserve semantic structure. In practice, this manifests as trajectory deviation—small errors compound over the denoising process, leading to semantic drift and structural inconsistencies
[
34
,
43
]
.

Optimal transport provides the missing theoretical foundation. By computing transport maps between source and target distributions, we obtain globally optimal paths that minimize displacement while preserving measure
[
40
]
. These maps ensure that transformations follow efficient paths in the latent space while maintaining structural coherence.

3.3
Efficient Transport Direction Computation

Exact optimal transport computation scales poorly with dimensionality, requiring expensive iterative solvers
[
9
]
. However, we can avoid this computational bottleneck by exploiting the Brenier map property
[
6
]
: for quadratic costs between Gaussian-like distributions, optimal transport directions admit closed-form solutions. This assumption is well-justified for rectified flow latent spaces, which exhibit approximately Gaussian structure due to the diffusion process initialization and the central limit behavior of high-dimensional embeddings.

Our key computational insight yields:

d_{\text{OT}}(z_{t},z_{\text{target}},t)=\frac{z_{\text{target}}-z_{t}}{\max(T-t,\delta)},

(1)

where
\delta=0.01
prevents numerical instabilities near the endpoint
t=T
. We use Wasserstein-2 distances as they naturally align with the quadratic cost structure of rectified flows and provide closed-form solutions for Gaussian-like latent distributions. For Wasserstein-2 distances with quadratic cost
c(z_{t},z_{\text{target}})=\|z_{t}-z_{\text{target}}\|^{2}
, the gradient of the transport functional is exactly the displacement vector
z_{\text{target}}-z_{t}

[
6
,
41
]
. This eliminates iterative optimization, reducing computational complexity from
O(n^{3})
for general optimal transport to
O(n)
for our closed-form approach.

The temporal scaling
1/(T-t)
serves two purposes: it intensifies transport guidance as we approach the denoising endpoint where reconstruction fidelity is most critical, and it maintains consistency with geodesic flow theory in Wasserstein space
[
1
]
.

For numerical stability, we apply gradient clipping
\|d_{\text{OT}}\|_{2}\leq\tau
with
\tau=10.0
, chosen to prevent transport directions from overwhelming the rectified flow velocity while preserving meaningful guidance. This clipping threshold ensures that transport corrections remain within reasonable bounds relative to typical latent space magnitudes. While gradient clipping introduces a small approximation error, empirical analysis shows minimal impact on transport optimality for practical editing scenarios.

3.4
Adaptive Transport Scheduling

The denoising process exhibits different requirements across timesteps. Early stages (
t\approx T
) prioritize global structure preservation during noise removal, while later stages (
t\approx 0
) require precise semantic alignment with prompt conditioning. We model this temporal evolution through an adaptive weighting schedule:

\alpha(t)=\beta_{0}\cdot\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{T-t}{T}\right)

(2)

where
\mathcal{S}
implements cosine annealing:

\mathcal{S}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos\left(\min\left(\frac{s}{\phi},1\right)\cdot\pi\right)\right)

(3)

Cosine annealing
[
24
]
provides several advantages over linear or exponential schedules. Its smooth, differentiable profile prevents sudden changes in transport guidance that could destabilize the denoising trajectory. The convex-concave shape naturally concentrates transport influence during early timesteps when structural preservation is most important, then smoothly transitions to prompt-dominated guidance as fine details emerge.

The phase parameter
\phi\in[0,1]
controls the temporal scope of transport intervention. Smaller values (
\phi<0.3
) concentrate transport guidance to early timesteps, while larger values (
\phi>0.7
) extend transport influence throughout the denoising process. The base strength parameter
\beta_{0}
determines the maximum transport influence. Values below 0.1 provide insufficient guidance for trajectory correction, while values above 1.0 can overpower the rectified flow dynamics.

3.5
Integration with Rectified Flow Models

Our transport guidance integrates into existing RF pipelines through velocity field modification during the reverse denoising process. Algorithm
1
details the complete procedure, extending the standard rectified flow ODE with optimal transport corrections. The key insight is that transport guidance operates as an additive correction to the rectified flow velocity field. At each timestep, we compute the optimal transport direction
d_{\text{OT}}
toward the target latent
z_{\text{target}}
, then blend it with the prompt-conditioned velocity
v_{\text{RF}}
using the adaptive weighting
\alpha(t)
.

We consistently set
x_{\text{target}}=x_{0}
to preserve the original content structure while enabling semantic modifications. This choice ensures that the transport map guides the denoising trajectory toward the source image’s latent representation, preventing excessive deviation that could corrupt fine details or introduce artifacts. The transport correction is applied through element-wise addition rather than interpolation, preserving the rectified flow’s deterministic properties. Gradient clipping with threshold
\tau=10.0
prevents transport directions from overwhelming the base velocity field, maintaining numerical stability throughout the denoising process.

3.6
Theoretical Properties

We analyze the theoretical behavior of our transport-guided approach under standard assumptions for rectified flow models. Our method solves the transport-guided ODE:

\frac{dz}{dt}=v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c)+\beta_{0}\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{T-t}{T}\right)\frac{z_{\text{target}}-z_{t}}{T-t}

(4)

using Euler integration with stepsize
\Delta t
. Let
z_{t-\Delta t}^{\text{discrete}}
denote the output from our Algorithm 1 and
z_{t-\Delta t}^{\text{continuous}}
the theoretical continuous solution.

Discretization Error.
Under Lipschitz continuity assumptions on
v_{\theta}
, the discretization error satisfies:

\|z_{t-\Delta t}^{\text{discrete}}-z_{t-\Delta t}^{\text{continuous}}\|_{2}\leq L\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})

(5)

where
L
depends on
\|v_{\theta}\|_{\text{Lip}}
, transport strength
\beta_{0}
, and gradient clipping threshold
\tau
.

Convergence.
When
z_{\text{target}}
lies within the data distribution support, the final output satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[\|z_{0}-z_{\text{target}}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\epsilon_{\text{RF}}+C_{\text{transport}}\cdot\beta_{0}^{2}\cdot T

(6)

where
\epsilon_{\text{RF}}
is the base rectified flow reconstruction error and
C_{\text{transport}}
is a constant determined by the latent space diameter and scheduling function
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
.

Edit Control.
For
z_{\text{target}}=z_{0}
, the edit magnitude is bounded by:

W_{2}^{2}(z_{\text{edit}},z_{\text{source}})\leq\beta_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{S}^{2}\left(\frac{T-t}{T}\right)dt+\epsilon_{\text{schedule}}

(7)

where
\epsilon_{\text{schedule}}
is proportional to
\|\nabla\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}
.

These bounds demonstrate that transport guidance introduces controlled modifications that scale predictably with
\beta_{0}
.

5
Conclusion

We introduced OTIP, a framework that addresses the trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and editability in rectified flow inversion by incorporating optimal transport guidance during denoising. This approach mitigates trajectory deviation issues that typically compromise editing quality in rectified flow models. The method operates without requiring model retraining or test-time optimization, making it compatible with existing rectified flow architectures.

Our experiments demonstrate consistent improvements across multiple benchmarks, particularly in fine-grained detail preservation and semantic consistency. The three-parameter control mechanism enables flexible manipulation of the fidelity-editability trade-off, while maintaining computational efficiency comparable to baseline methods.

The approach has certain limitations. Our formulation relies on Lipschitz continuity assumptions that may not apply to all rectified flow architectures. For extreme semantic transformations, the method’s focus on structural preservation may limit editing flexibility compared to approaches designed specifically for large-scale content modifications.

The experimental results across reconstruction, semantic editing, and stroke-to-image tasks suggest that optimal transport guidance offers a practical approach for improving rectified flow inversion. The framework provides a foundation for further exploration of transport-based methods in flow-guided image editing applications. For comprehensive robustness analysis and additional ablation studies, we refer readers to the appendix where we extensively evaluate the method across diverse scenarios and parameter configurations.
